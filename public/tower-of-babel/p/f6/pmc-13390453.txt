Longitudinal trajectories and predictive factors of depressive symptomatology in Chinese elderly with hearing loss: a growth mixture modeling analysis
Authors: Kuo Wen, Enguang Li, Fangzhu Ai, Ping Tang, Hongjuan Wen, Botang Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify longitudinal trajectories of depressive symptoms and their baseline predictors among Chinese older adults with hearing loss.MethodsThis study analyzed four waves of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) collected in 2011, 2013, 2015, and 2018. Growth mixture modeling was used to identify latent depressive symptom trajectory classes, and multinomial logistic regression was used to examine baseline predictors, including sociodemographic characteristics, health behaviors, health status, functional ability, and psychosocial factors.ResultsAmong 1556 participants, four depressive symptom trajectories were identified: increasing, severe-stable, no depressive symptoms stable, and decreasing. Compared with the no depressive symptoms stable class, female sex, poor self-rated health, and poor life satisfaction were associated with higher odds of membership in symptomatic trajectories. Social inactivity was associated with the severe-stable class, whereas fair life satisfaction was associated with the decreasing class. Longer sleep duration and higher activities of daily living scores were protective factors.ConclusionsDepressive symptom courses among older adults with hearing loss are heterogeneous. Baseline psychosocial resources, sleep duration, and functional ability may help identify high-risk subgroups and inform targeted, hearing-friendly mental health screening and intervention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07687-1.Keywords: Growth Mixture Model, Depressive symptoms, Hearing loss, Older adults, Trajectory

Introduction

Hearing loss has emerged as a major public health challenge in aging societies worldwide. According to the World Health Organization, its prevalence demonstrates a strong age-related pattern, affecting 12.7% of individuals aged 60 years and older and rising sharply to 58.6% among those aged 90 years and above [1]. Notably, more than 58% of global disabling hearing loss occurs in adults aged 60 and above, making it one of the most common chronic conditions in later life [2].

Hearing loss is highly prevalent among older adults and is associated with the onset of depressive symptoms [3]. A commonly proposed pathway is that hearing-related communication difficulties reduce participation in social activities and weaken social support, thereby increasing loneliness and depressive symptoms [4–6]. These pathways may be further shaped by potentially modifiable factors, including social inactivity, functional independence in activities of daily living, and sleep duration.

Depressive symptoms in later life are dynamic and heterogeneous. A systematic review of long-term group-based trajectory studies with follow-up periods of at least five years indicates that most cohorts identify three to four distinct trajectories that differ primarily in symptom severity and temporal pattern, including stable, increasing, and decreasing courses. Most individuals remain in a persistently low-symptom trajectory, whereas a notable minority experience chronically high symptom levels [7]. Similar patterns have been reported in older populations. For instance, a large community-based cohort identified four trajectories, including low stable, moderate stable, increasing, and high stable patterns, which were differentially associated with subsequent health outcomes [8]. Evidence from Mexican American and Chinese older adults further supports this heterogeneity, with studies commonly reporting three to four trajectory classes and showing that less favorable courses are associated with poorer functional status, worse health conditions, and limited psychosocial resources, such as lower social support and reduced social participation [9, 10]. Collectively, these findings underscore substantial inter-individual variability in late-life depressive symptom courses and support the use of person-centered approaches, such as growth mixture modeling, to identify clinically meaningful subgroups.

Recent longitudinal evidence has also examined the association between hearing loss and depressive symptoms over time. Using data from the United States Health and Retirement Study from 1998 to 2022, Li and colleagues applied linear mixed-effects models and found that incident hearing loss was associated with an immediate increase in depressive symptoms, followed by an accelerated long-term worsening after onset, even after accounting for pre-onset symptom trajectories and baseline covariates [11]. In contrast, Brewster and colleagues analyzed data from the Health, Aging and Body Composition (Health ABC) Study and applied latent class analysis to depressive symptoms assessed repeatedly over a 10-year period. They identified three trajectories, including low probability, increasing probability, and high probability patterns, and reported that impaired or worsening hearing predicted higher odds of membership in the increasing and high trajectory groups compared with the low trajectory group. This finding suggests a subgroup-specific, potentially nonlinear association rather than a single average change over time [12].

However, existing studies have largely treated hearing status as an exposure in the general older population and have focused primarily on incident hearing loss. Much less is known about longitudinal heterogeneity in depressive symptom trajectories among older adults with established hearing loss, particularly in China. This gap is important because late-life depressive symptoms are clinically consequential and are associated with functional decline, reduced quality of life, and elevated health risks. Identifying distinct symptom trajectories can clarify inter-individual heterogeneity, support early risk stratification, and inform targeted prevention and intervention strategies.

Multiple domains have been linked to unfavorable depressive symptom courses in older adulthood, including adverse health status, functional limitations, and constrained psychosocial resources. Within stress-process and social engagement frameworks, hearing-related communication barriers may heighten psychological distress by limiting social participation, increasing social inactivity, and diminishing functional reserve [13–15]. Functional independence in activities of daily living is a core indicator of autonomy and resilience and may mitigate vulnerability to persistent depressive symptoms. Sleep duration is also closely connected to affect regulation and overall health, and atypical sleep patterns, including longer sleep duration, may signal dysregulation that accompanies or precedes worsening depressive symptoms. Despite their relevance and potential modifiability, social inactivity, sleep duration, and functional independence in activities of daily living have rarely been examined together as predictors that differentiate long-term depressive symptom trajectories specifically within older adults living with hearing loss.

To address these gaps, this study analyzed four waves of nationally representative China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study data collected from 2011 to 2018 among adults aged 60 years or older who reported hearing loss at baseline. Growth mixture modeling was used to identify latent classes of depressive symptom trajectories. Baseline predictors were evaluated across sociodemographic characteristics, health behaviors, health status, functional independence in activities of daily living, and psychosocial factors including life satisfaction, social participation, social inactivity, and sleep duration.

This study aimed to identify distinct longitudinal trajectories of depressive symptoms among older adults with hearing loss and to determine which baseline factors predict membership in each trajectory class. It was hypothesized that depressive symptoms would follow multiple heterogeneous trajectories, including persistently low, persistently high, increasing, and decreasing patterns. It was further hypothesized that lower life satisfaction, greater social inactivity, poorer self-rated health, a greater burden of chronic conditions, lower functional independence in activities of daily living, and sleep duration would differentiate trajectory class membership, with these factors distinguishing symptomatic trajectories from a stable no depressive symptoms trajectory.

By characterizing trajectory classes and their predictors within the hearing-loss population, this study seeks to support earlier identification of high-risk subgroups and to inform hearing-friendly mental health screening and targeted interventions in community-dwelling older adults.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study utilized data from CHARLS, a nationally representative survey of individuals aged ≥ 45 years and their spouses, conducted by the National School of Development at Peking University [16]. Initiated in 2011, CHARLS is a longitudinal survey with biennial follow-ups, incorporating physical examinations during each wave. Ethical approval was obtained from the Peking University Institutional Review Board (IRB00001052-11015), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. To ensure confidentiality, all personal identifiers, including names and residential addresses, were removed and replaced with anonymized codes. Data were stored in encrypted systems with restricted access, and the de-identified dataset is publicly available online.

For this analysis, four waves of CHARLS data collected in 2011, 2013, 2015, and 2018 were used to characterize long-term trajectories of depressive symptoms. Participants were eligible if they were aged 60 years or older at baseline in 2011, met the study definition of hearing loss at baseline, and had CES-D-10 data available across all four waves for trajectory modeling. Participants with missing or invalid baseline hearing information and those with a baseline diagnosis of severe cognitive impairment or terminal illness were excluded. Missing values in covariates were addressed using multiple imputation with chained equations with five imputations, implemented with the mice package in R. Measurement

Hearing Loss

Hearing loss was assessed using self-reported information from the CHARLS survey, as objective measures such as audiometry were unavailable. Prior studies have demonstrated the reliability of self-reported hearing loss for epidemiological research [17, 18]. Trained interviewers conducted face-to-face assessments, and hearing loss was defined according to three criteria: (1) answering “Yes” to the question “Do you have a hearing problem?”; (2) reporting the use of a hearing aid (“Do you ever wear a hearing aid?”); or (3) rating hearing status as “fair” or “poor” in response to “Would you say your hearing is excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor?” (considering hearing aid use when applicable). These criteria provided a validated and pragmatic approach to identifying hearing impairment in the absence of audiometric data. Importantly, the self-rated hearing item was answered considering hearing aid use when applicable; therefore, participants who used hearing aids but still rated their hearing as “fair” or “poor” were classified as having residual hearing difficulty. Although hearing aids may partially mitigate communication barriers for some individuals, they do not fully restore hearing in all cases, and hearing-related communication challenges may persist; thus, psychosocial pathways such as reduced social participation remain relevant. This study also acknowledges that detailed information on hearing aid fitting, daily use, and objective hearing thresholds was unavailable, which may lead to misclassification and should be addressed in future research.

Outcome: depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10), a validated short form of the original CES-D [19]. The scale comprises 10 items rated on a 4-point frequency scale from 0 (“rarely or none of the time”) to 3 (“most or all of the time”). Eight items are negatively phrased and scored directly, whereas two positively phrased items (hopefulness and happiness) are reverse coded. Total scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. The CES-D-10 has demonstrated good reliability and validity in Chinese older adults, with Cronbach’s α ranging from 0.719 to 0.805 in previous large-scale studies [20, 21].

Predictor variables (baseline covariates)

Predictor variables were selected a priori based on prior studies of depressive symptom trajectories in older adults and on conceptual pathways linking hearing loss to depression, including communication barriers, reduced social engagement, sleep disturbances, and functional limitations [15].

The analysis incorporated fifteen baseline predictors from the 2011 survey. Sociodemographic factors included age (years, continuous), gender (male/female), education (uneducated: no formal schooling; educated: primary school or above), marital status (have spouse/no spouse), and health insurance (yes/no). Health behaviors included current smoking (yes/no) and current alcohol drinking (yes/no). Health status indicators included self-rated health (good: very good/good; fair; poor: poor/very poor), presence of any doctor-diagnosed chronic disease (yes/no), and disability status (yes/no, self-reported). Psychosocial factors included life satisfaction (good/fair/poor) and significant misadventures (yes/no, any major adverse events). Lifestyle measures included social activity participation (yes/no, participation in any CHARLS-listed social activities in the past month) and average nightly sleep duration (hours, self-reported, continuous). Functional status was assessed using the Activities of Daily Living (ADL) scale adapted from the Katz Index [22], covering six domains (personal hygiene, dressing, feeding, continence management, toileting, and indoor mobility). Each domain was scored on a 3-point scale (3 = independent/minimal difficulty, 2 = needs assistance, 1 = dependent), and a composite ADL score was calculated by summing across domains, with higher scores indicating better functional independence.

Statistical analysis

The analysis proceeded in three stages. First, descriptive statistics were performed to characterize participant distributions and calculate CES-D-10 scores across the four survey waves, including measures of central tendency, variability, and correlations of depressive symptoms at each time point. Second, GMM was applied to identify heterogeneous trajectories of depressive symptoms, following the methodological framework for latent class growth analysis and growth mixture modeling described by Jung and Wickrama [23]. Model selection was guided by multiple fit indices: the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and sample-adjusted BIC (SABIC), with preference given to models with lower values to avoid overfitting [24, 25]. Model selection was based on a combination of information criteria (AIC, BIC, and SABIC), likelihood ratio tests (LMR and BLRT; P < 0.05), classification quality (entropy), minimum class size (≥ 5%), and clinical interpretability. Entropy was considered one indicator among several, and values in the 0.70–0.80 range were considered acceptable when supported by fit statistics and interpretable trajectories [26]. The final model was chosen based on both statistical fit and clinical interpretability, requiring distinct and meaningful trajectory patterns, with each trajectory class containing at least 5% of the sample. Finally, predictors of trajectory membership were examined. Between-group comparisons were conducted using χ² tests, Fisher’s exact tests, and Kruskal–Wallis H tests, and multinomial logistic regression was employed to estimate associations between baseline predictors and trajectory classes. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.0 for descriptive and regression analyses, and Mplus version 8 for trajectory modeling.

Participants and CES-D-10 scores

Most participants had a spouse (86.6%), and 75.9% had received formal education at the primary school level or above. At baseline, 45.2% reported current smoking and 33.4% reported current alcohol consumption. Overall, 78.0% reported at least one physician-diagnosed chronic disease. Predictors of trajectory class

Group differences in baseline factors across depressive symptom trajectories were examined using χ² tests and Kruskal–Wallis H tests. As presented in Supplementary Materials 3, all examined factors demonstrated statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) except for major misadventures and age. To examine the predictive utility of significant univariate factors for each trajectory pattern, multinomial logistic regression analyses were conducted with the four class growth mixture model classification serving as the dependent variable (variable coding details are provided in Supplementary Materials 4).

Table 4 presents the multinomial logistic regression results for trajectory class membership, using the no depressive symptoms stable class (Class 3) as the reference. Female sex, poor self-rated health, and poor life satisfaction were associated with higher odds of membership in all three symptomatic classes, including the depressive symptoms increasing class (Class 1), the severe depressive symptoms stable class (Class 2), and the depressive symptoms decreasing class (Class 4). Fair life satisfaction was associated with higher odds of belonging to the depressive symptoms decreasing class, whereas social inactivity was specifically associated with the severe depressive symptoms stable class. In addition, the absence of chronic conditions was associated with lower odds of membership in the depressive symptoms decreasing class. Longer sleep duration and higher activities of daily living scores were protective factors and were associated with lower odds of symptomatic trajectory membership.

Discussion

Using four waves of nationally representative CHARLS data, this study identified four heterogeneous depressive symptom trajectories among Chinese older adults with baseline hearing loss and examined predictors of trajectory membership. The most significant findings were the presence of two clinically concerning courses—an accelerating increase and a persistently severe pattern—and the observation that social inactivity uniquely characterized the persistently severe trajectory, whereas longer sleep duration and better functional independence (ADL) were protective against symptomatic trajectories. The innovative contribution of this study lies in applying a person-centered GMM specifically to older adults living with hearing loss, shifting the analytic focus from conceptualizing hearing loss merely as an exposure in general population samples to characterizing heterogeneity within the hearing-loss population itself, thereby generating class-specific risk profiles that can inform targeted, hearing-friendly mental health screening and intervention.

Trajectories of depressive symptoms

This study identified four distinct depressive symptom trajectories among older adults with hearing loss, reflecting heterogeneous courses over time: (1) increasing (9.4%), (2) severe-stable (10.7%), (3) no depressive symptoms stable (59.7%), and (4) decreasing (20.1%). Similar trajectory shapes, including persistently low or high levels as well as increasing and decreasing courses, have been reported in studies of the general older population [7–10]; however, in this cohort of older adults with hearing loss, a relatively larger share of participants fell into symptomatic trajectories, including the increasing, severe-stable, and decreasing groups, and a notable proportion followed persistently high or worsening courses, suggesting that hearing loss–related communication barriers and consequent social withdrawal may contribute to a more adverse symptom course over time. This hearing loss–specific analysis clarifies the distribution of these courses and highlights subgroups that warrant priority attention. The increasing trajectory showed a steep escalation across waves, suggesting progressive symptom worsening. The severe-stable trajectory remained persistently elevated, representing a chronic high-burden subgroup. The no depressive symptoms stable trajectory reflected sustained psychological resilience, whereas the decreasing trajectory indicated symptom remission over time, potentially reflecting adaptive coping or supportive circumstances.

Predictors of trajectory membership

The heterogeneity of depressive symptom trajectories highlights the importance of identifying predictors of trajectory membership among older adults with hearing loss [27]. This study first compared baseline characteristics across classes using χ² and Kruskal–Wallis H tests and then entered significant factors into multinomial logistic regression models [28]. Compared with the no depressive symptoms stable trajectory, female sex, poorer self-rated health, and poorer life satisfaction were associated with higher odds of belonging to symptomatic trajectories, including increasing, severe-stable, and decreasing courses [29, 30]. Social inactivity was specifically associated with the severe-stable trajectory, whereas moderate life satisfaction was associated with the decreasing trajectory. The absence of chronic conditions was associated with lower odds of the decreasing trajectory, suggesting that remission patterns may occur in the context of greater baseline health burden. Longer sleep duration and higher ADL scores were protective factors, reducing the likelihood of symptomatic trajectory membership.

Social inactivity showed a particularly strong association with the severe-stable trajectory compared with the no depressive symptoms stable group. In CHARLS, social activity participation reflects a structural and behavioral aspect of social connectedness, capturing engagement in social and community activities, rather than subjective feelings of loneliness. From an activity theory and behavioral activation perspective, reduced engagement limits access to rewarding experiences and valued social roles, which may help maintain depressed mood. In addition, consistent with the stress process paradigm, hearing loss-related communication difficulties can function as a chronic stressor that increases interaction strain and erodes social support; withdrawal from activities further removes buffering resources, facilitating persistent severe symptoms [31–33]. Together, these mechanisms suggest that expanding hearing-friendly opportunities for social participation, such as group activities that incorporate communication accommodations, may be especially relevant for preventing or alleviating chronic severe depressive symptom trajectories in older adults with hearing loss.

Sleep duration emerged as a significant protective factor against depressive trajectories in older adults with hearing loss. Longer sleep duration was positively associated with the no depressive symptoms stable trajectory, indicating an inverse relationship between sleep duration and depressive symptom risk. This finding aligns with previous evidence showing a dose–response relationship between sleep disturbances and increased severity of depressive and anxiety symptoms [34]. Several mechanisms may explain this effect. Insufficient sleep can exacerbate physical frailty, which may worsen hearing loss and increase vulnerability to depressive symptoms. Moreover, the frequent use of hypnotic medications among older adults with insomnia, particularly when combined with antihypertensive treatments, may disrupt endocrine balance and heighten depressive risk [35]. These results highlight the importance of comprehensive psychological and sleep quality assessments for hearing-impaired older adults, accompanied by tailored interventions and opportunities for social engagement.

This study also found that women were more prone to depressive symptoms. Several factors may explain this gender difference. Women in the sample were more often widowed and living alone, both of which substantially increase the risk of loneliness and emotional distress, thereby elevating depressive symptoms risk [36]. In addition, women are more likely than men to exhibit atypical depressive symptoms—such as fatigue, appetite loss, and sleep disturbances—which are associated with higher levels of inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, reduced serotonin synthesis, and altered hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis activity [37]. However, not all studies report gender differences; for instance, a Greek study found no significant variation in depressive symptoms between men and women [38]. Further research is therefore needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying gender disparities in depressive symptoms among older adults.

Older adults with hearing loss but without comorbid chronic conditions were more likely to follow the depressive symptom remission trajectory. This may reflect the absence of additional health burdens that could exacerbate depressive symptoms. In contrast, multiple chronic conditions not only worsen physical health but also impose financial strain, thereby increasing depressive symptoms risk through socioeconomic stressors [39]. These findings highlight the importance of strengthening chronic disease management and optimizing medical insurance coverage. Investments in preventive care, treatment accessibility, and financial support for economically vulnerable or functionally limited older adults could substantially improve their psychological well-being and quality of life.

Life satisfaction, defined as an individual’s overall self-assessment of quality of life [40], also showed strong associations with depressive symptoms. Low satisfaction was linked to a reduced likelihood of remaining depressive symptoms-free, whereas moderate satisfaction was associated with a higher chance of remission. This may relate to cognitive patterns observed in depressive symptoms, such as biased attention to negative information and impaired working memory [41]. Evidence consistently shows that older adults with minimal or subsyndromal symptoms maintain higher life satisfaction, even after adjusting for confounders. These findings underscore the need for healthcare providers to incorporate life satisfaction screening and interventions, including cognitive-behavioral strategies and resilience training, into care programs for older adults with hearing loss.

Self-rated health showed a strong inverse association with depressive symptoms among older adults. Those with poor self-rated health were significantly more likely to develop depressive symptoms, with evidence from China indicating a 3.47-fold higher risk compared with peers reporting good health [42]. This association likely reflects the dual role of self-rated health as both an objective proxy for physical status and a subjective measure of psychological well-being. Consequently, interventions should adopt an integrated approach that combines medical management of physical conditions with strategies to enhance positive health perceptions, such as cognitive–behavioral techniques and resilience training.

Similarly, higher ADL functioning was associated with a greater likelihood of belonging to the depressive symptoms-free trajectory. Functional independence may promote social participation and positive emotional experiences, thereby contributing to better life satisfaction. These results highlight the importance of addressing functional limitations through comprehensive geriatric programs that integrate physical rehabilitation, mental health support, and targeted health education.

Beyond differentiating trajectory membership, self-rated health, ADL, and social engagement may also shape the overall course of depressive symptoms over time in older adults with hearing loss. Poorer health perceptions, functional limitations, and social inactivity were associated with trajectory patterns characterized by persistently high or worsening symptoms, whereas better health perceptions, functional independence, and active engagement were more consistent with stable low or improving trajectories. This trajectory-oriented interpretation reinforces the value of interventions that maintain functional ability and support social participation as potential levers to improve long-term symptom course in this population.

Implications for intervention and policy

This study highlights the heterogeneity of depressive symptom trajectories among older adults with hearing loss and proposes targeted strategies at both the clinical and policy levels. Key intervention priorities include social participation, self-rated health, ADL, sleep quality, and life satisfaction.

For individuals with worsening or persistently severe depressive symptoms, community-based social prescribing programs are recommended, offering hearing-friendly activities such as group exercise and intergenerational storytelling, supported by accessible transportation. Audiology clinics should integrate mental health professionals who can provide cognitive–behavioral therapy (CBT) to address both hearing and depressive symptoms. For those with declining symptoms, life satisfaction workshops—incorporating gratitude journaling and goal-setting—may help sustain recovery and promote psychological well-being.

To strengthen social engagement, “hearing-friendly” community centers should be established, hosting regular activities such as monthly coffee mornings. Physiotherapists can design functional fitness programs combining balance training and cognitive tasks to improve ADL capacity and health awareness. For sleep disturbances, sleep kits with vibrating alarms and relaxation aids, alongside wearable devices for monitoring, may provide timely interventions.

In chronic disease management, regular monitoring of hearing aid performance should be incorporated into broader health assessments, particularly for patients with diabetes or hypertension. Gender-sensitive measures are also needed, such as widow support groups combined with art therapy, and occupational therapist–led home visits to reduce fall risks and enhance ADL independence.

At the policy level, mandatory “sensory–psychological” joint screening is recommended, ensuring dual assessments for hearing loss and depressive symptoms with reimbursement through medical insurance. Subsidies for over-the-counter hearing aids and rebate incentives linked to mental health improvements should be considered. In senior housing, installation of sound-absorbing materials and visual alarm systems may reduce social isolation and promote inclusion.

Overall, these strategies are targeted, scalable, and feasible, offering clear pathways to mitigate depressive symptoms in this vulnerable population. Pilot testing in high-risk subgroups is warranted to evaluate effectiveness and inform large-scale implementation.

Limitations and future directions

This study provides meaningful insights into the longitudinal trajectories of depressive symptoms among older adults with hearing loss, yet several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although CHARLS is a national survey, the analytical sample in this study was restricted to older adults with hearing loss at baseline who had complete data across four waves, which may overrepresent certain regions and social contexts and thus restrict generalizability. Moreover, cultural features in China, including family-centered norms such as filial piety and intergenerational co-residence, collective community activities, and differences in stigma and service access related to hearing loss and mental health, may shape social participation patterns and coping resources; therefore, the distribution of trajectory classes and the magnitude of predictor effects may differ in other cultural settings. Second, although longitudinal data were applied, residual confounding remains possible, as some influential factors may not have been fully captured or adjusted for. In particular, although this study adjusted for basic socioeconomic proxies, including educational attainment and health insurance coverage, it did not incorporate detailed economic indicators such as household assets and pension income, which may contribute to residual socioeconomic confounding. Third, CHARLS did not provide information on the duration of hearing loss or on the stage of depressive symptom development before the 2011 baseline. Consequently, some observed trajectories may reflect continuation of pre-existing hearing-related or depressive processes rather than changes that began at baseline, which should be considered when interpreting trajectory onset and progression. Fourth, the observational design limits causal inference, and the absence of interventional elements reduces the ability to evaluate the effectiveness of targeted strategies. In addition, key covariates such as Activities of Daily Living, chronic diseases, and health behaviors were considered only at baseline, rather than dynamically modeled as time-varying variables, which may have underestimated their influence on depressive symptom trajectories. Finally, hearing loss was primarily assessed by self-report and did not include composite subjective-objective measures such as pure-tone audiometry or speech-in-noise recognition, which could have yielded a more accurate evaluation of auditory function.

Future research should therefore seek to broaden sample diversity, employ interventional and experimental designs, adopt multidimensional assessment criteria that integrate WHO hearing-threshold classifications with functional measures, and incorporate time-varying covariates into longitudinal models. These refinements would allow for a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying depressive symptom trajectories in older adults with hearing loss and provide stronger evidence to guide intervention strategies.

Conclusion

This study provides important insights into the longitudinal evolution of depressive symptoms among older adults with hearing loss. Baseline psychosocial resources and behaviors, including social inactivity and social participation, together with sleep duration, self-rated health, and functional independence in activities of daily living, were associated with trajectory class membership and long-term symptom course. Social inactivity was particularly associated with persistently severe symptoms, whereas longer sleep duration and better functional independence were associated with a lower likelihood of symptomatic trajectories. These findings suggest that interventions that promote hearing-accessible social engagement, support healthy sleep, enhance perceived health, and preserve independence in daily living may help prevent symptom escalation and improve mental well-being. At the policy level, integrating routine hearing and mental health screening into community health services, alongside financial support for hearing aids and psychological interventions, may further amplify these benefits.
