HandScan criteria versus ACR/EULAR Boolean remission as treatment target in early rheumatoid arthritis: results of a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Matthijs S van der Leeuw, Paco M J Welsing, Janneke Tekstra, Antonius A A Westgeest, Ruth Klaasen, Mihaela Gamala, Johannes W G Jacobs, Maxime M A Verhoeven, Jacob M van Laar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe HandScan is a novel method to quickly and objectively quantify RA disease activity using optical spectral transmission in hands and wrists. The aim of this study was to investigate whether treat-to-target (T2T) in early RA patients aimed at ‘HandScan’ remission is non-inferior to that aimed at ACR/EULAR 2011 ‘Boolean’ remission.MethodsWe performed a multicentre randomized double-blind, non-inferiority trial. Newly diagnosed RA patients naive to DMARDs were randomized 1:1 to targeting HandScan remission or Boolean remission. At monthly visits, treatment was intensified/continued/deescalated according to the respective target following a predefined treatment schedule. Primary outcome was the HAQ score at 18 months, and non-inferiority was tested using a non-inferiority margin of 0.2 with a one-sided alpha of 0.05.ResultsOf the 56 patients included per arm, a remarkably high number was not able to follow the T2T protocol (HandScan: 95%, Boolean: 80%, P = 0.01). The main reason was that patients and/or rheumatologists felt there was overtreatment (43%, 32%). HAQ at 18 months was 0.21 (95% CI: 0.01–0.40) units lower (i.e. better) in the Boolean arm. Thus, non-inferiority was absent for the HandScan target. Sensitivity analyses confirmed this conclusion. The HandScan target required more intensive treatment than the Boolean target, indicating overtreatment.ConclusionCurrent HandScan remission criteria are not suitable for a T2T strategy in early RA since they may lead to overtreatment and inferior clinical outcomes. Additionally, both treatment targets lead to extraordinarily high non-adherence rates predominantly based on the feeling of overtreatment.Trial registrationInternational Clinical Trials Registry Platform, www.trialsearch.who.int, NTR6388Keywords: early RA, treat-to-target, HandScan, optical spectral transmission

Introduction

It has been well established that treatment of (early) RA should follow the principles of tight control and treat-to-target (T2T). This means disease activity is assessed frequently (every 1–3 months) and treatment is adjusted with the aim to quickly achieve a predefined target: a state of low disease activity and preferably remission [1]. Disease activity is most commonly measured using several assessments, which typically include counts of tenderness and swelling of joints, the patient’s and/or provider’s assessment of pain/disease activity/general health on a visual analogue scale (VAS) and an acute phase reactant in blood [2]. Low disease activity or remission targets are then defined by a cut-off of a composite score or by a Boolean definition based on its components. Since assessment of tender joints and a VAS are inherently subjective, standardization remains an issue, and training is needed to make the assessment as objectively as possible [3]. Moreover, performing joint counts can be time consuming which may limit its use in routine care.

The HandScan is a device that uses red/near-infrared light to measure inflammation in hand and wrist joints, using a modality called optical spectral transmission (OST). Patients place their hands into the device, after which the transmission of red/near-infrared light through the hand joints is measured before and after venous blood flow is temporarily impeded by a cuff around the forearms. The transmission is decreased when vasodilation or neovascularization is present, thereby identifying and scoring inflamed joints [4]. The grade of inflammation is expressed by an OST score per joint, as well as by an overall OST score based on all measured joints: PIP joints, MCP joints and wrist joints. OST scores correlate with ultrasound scores in PIP joints, MCP joints and wrist joints, and on the patient level, overall OST scores correlate with DAS28 [5]. The HandScan has a high test–retest reliability on patient level (Intra Class Correlation (ICC) = 0.86, 95% CI 0.76 to 0.92, P < 0.001) and joint level (ICC = 0.76, 95% CI 0.73 to 0.79, P < 0.001) [6]. A HandScan can be performed within 5 min and does not require a trained health professional. Therefore, it is potentially a quick and objective way of measuring disease activity in RA patients. However, it is unknown whether HandScan measurements can validly replace a composite definition of remission in a T2T treatment strategy.

The aim of this trial was to investigate whether treatment in early RA patients aimed at ‘HandScan remission’ is non-inferior to treatment aimed at ACR/EULAR 2011 Boolean remission.

Methods

Design of the study

We performed a randomized, controlled, double-blind, non-inferiority trial of 18 months duration, in five outpatient rheumatology centres in The Netherlands. We included newly diagnosed (diagnosis < 1 year) RA patients ≥ 18 years satisfying the 2010 RA classification criteria, who were naive to treatment with DMARDs. Use of systemic glucocorticoids was not allowed < 6 weeks prior to baseline. Exclusion criteria were a contraindication for treatment with MTX or prednisone and a history of alcohol or substance abuse in the past 6 months. Patients with significant deformities of the hands, porphyria or using photodynamic medication were excluded since they are not eligible for HandScan analysis.

Sample size was based on establishing non-inferiority of using HandScan remission compared with the Boolean remission definition as a treatment target regarding the HAQ score at 18 months. We used a non-inferiority margin of 0.2 and an standard deviation of 0.4 based on previous research [7]. Using a one-sided alpha level of 0.05 and 1:1 randomization resulted in 51 patients per group, which was increased to 56 (total 112 patients) taking drop-out into account. We recruited patients through the outpatient clinics and obtained written informed consent from all participants.

Patients were randomized to treatment targeting either HandScan remission (the HandScan arm) or the ACR/EULAR Boolean definition of remission (the Boolean arm) using nQuery Advisor [8] in permuted blocks of 4, stratified by treatment centre. At monthly visits, treatment was adjusted based on whether patients were in remission or not. To prevent under- and overtreatment using the novel HandScan treatment target, patients in the HandScan arm switched to the Boolean arm if the Boolean and HandScan remission criteria were discordant at three consecutive visits. To allow for allocation concealment, HandScans and clinical assessments were done in both study arms, and results were entered in a decision-aid software application, which provided the appropriate treatment step without disclosing the allocated treatment arm. Primary outcome was the HAQ score at 18 months, and the HAQ was also assessed at baseline, 3, 6 and 12 months. In case patients and/or rheumatologists were not willing or able to follow the T2T drug treatment protocol, patients were treated in regular care, and no further study assessments were performed, except for collection of HAQ scores and medication data at 18 months. This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethical Committee of the University Medical Center Utrecht, Utrecht, The Netherlands on 6 April 2017 (NL50026.041.14).

Assessments and remission definitions

HandScans, joint count of 44 joints (MCP, PIP, MTP, sternoclavicular, acromioclavicular, shoulder, elbow, wrist, knee and ankle), CRP and visual analogue scale (VAS) for the patients’ assessment of general health, were collected at every monthly visit. We used 44-joint counts assessed by the physician or study nurse to not miss actively involved joints, especially in the feet and ankles. In the HandScan arm, remission was defined as ≤ 1 joint with an OST score > 1 AND an overall OST score ≤ 11. In the Boolean arm remission was based on the ACR/EULAR Boolean remission criteria [9], in which all the following must be met: tender joint count (TJC) of 44 joints ≤ 1; swollen joint count (SJC) of 30 small joints ≤ 1 (MCP, PIP and MTP joints); SJC of 14 large joints = 0 (sternoclavicular, acromioclavicular, shoulder, elbow, wrist, knee and ankle joints); CRP ≤ 10 mg/l; patient global assessment of disease activity ≤ 1 (on a visual analogue scale of 0–10 cm, 10 = worst). For the HandScan, no assessor is needed as it automatically measures OST scores when the patient puts his/her hands in the machine after instruction by the research nurse. To prevent a possible bias caused by knowing the HandScan score, HandScans were always performed after assessment of the joints.

Treatment protocol

At baseline, all patients started treatment with MTX 10 mg orally once weekly and prednisone, 10 mg daily. All patients received comedication: folic acid, calcium and vitamin D suppletion and a bisphosphonate (schemes according to local centre guidelines). At monthly visits, treatment was intensified as predefined by increasing MTX dose, adding HCQ, switching to a TNF inhibitor (TNFi) (Supplementary Table S1, available at Rheumatology online), as long as the allocated remission criteria were not met. Each centre prescribed their own preferred TNFi. If remission was not achieved after 3 months of maximum medication (i.e. MTX 30 mg (or maximum tolerable dose), 10 mg prednisone daily and a TNFi), patients were treated in regular care. In case of adverse reactions to MTX, the highest tolerated dose was continued subcutaneously (sc). If remission was achieved, medication was at first continued unchanged. If remission persisted over 3 consecutive months, prednisone was tapered to 7.5 mg daily. For every following 3-month period of remission, medication was tapered in the following order: TNF inhibitor (TNFi), HCQ, prednisone and lastly MTX. If remission was lost during tapering, patients returned to the previous dosages at which they were in remission for three visits and treatment was intensified monthly, until remission was achieved again. At the start of the COVID-19 pandemic (March 2020), the study was put on hold for 3 months, during which period medication was kept stable unless decided otherwise by the treating physician. A more detailed description of the study protocol and treatment schedule has been published previously [10].

Outcomes and analyses

Primary analysis compared HAQ scores at 18 months (primary endpoint) between arms in the intention-to-treat (ITT) population, controlling for baseline HAQ, baseline DAS28, centre, age, gender and seropositivity for either rheumatoid factor or anti-citrullinated protein antibodies using linear regression analysis. We used multiple imputation by chained equations to account for missing data in this analysis. Supplementary Table S2, available at Rheumatology online, shows the variables included in the imputation model used for the analyses of the primary endpoint.

Non-inferiority was declared based on a one-sided test with α = 0.05 and a non-inferiority margin of 0.2. The ITT population was defined as all patients who were randomized and received study medication.

We analysed HAQ scores at 6 months in the same way. Furthermore, we performed sensitivity analyses in the per protocol (PP) population, which was defined as all patients who followed the study protocol for at least 6 months. We also performed an analysis for the non-imputed data on the ITT and PP population. As stopping T2T protocol occurred more often and earlier in the HandScan arm, we also adjusted the primary analysis model for duration of adherence to the T2T protocol, including those switching target according to protocol, classified as < 6 months, 6–12 months, 13–17 months or 18 months.

Additionally, we evaluated HAQ, DAS28, OST scores, advised treatment steps and actual DMARD use over time in both arms. Because of high non-adherence rates, we assessed reasons for non-adherence to the T2T protocol, disease activity and treatment step at time of stopping T2T protocol, and HAQ scores at 18 months for adherent and non-adherent patients. We evaluated time to non-adherence using a Kaplan Meier curve and tested the difference between arms with a log rank test. Additionally, we used a Cox regression analysis to test whether a higher treatment step (as time varying variable) was associated with T2T non-adherence, correcting for the same covariates as in the primary analysis. To evaluate strictness of different remission criteria, we assessed remission rates over time, also including DAS28 remission (DAS28 < 2.6) and a more lenient cut-off for HandScan remission (≤13 overall OST score and ≤ 1 inflamed joints using an adjusted cut-off of 1.2). All analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0.0.0.

Results

From 4 April 2017 to 24 December 2019, 112 patients were enrolled and 56 randomized to each arm. Baseline characteristics were comparable between arms (Table 1). In the HandScan arm, 26 (46%) patients followed T2T protocol up to 6 months, and 3 (5%) patients up to 18 months (although these all switched to the Boolean arm according to protocol). In total, 11 (20%) patients switched from the HandScan arm to the Boolean arm. In the Boolean arm, 41 (73%) patients followed the T2T protocol up to 6 months, and 11 (20%) patients up to 18 months. Primary endpoint

Mean HAQ score at baseline was 1.0 in both arms and decreased to 0.63 (95% CI: 0.44–0.82) in the HandScan arm and to 0.34 (95% CI: 0.22–0.46) in the Boolean arm at 18 months (ITT population with HAQ scores available for 46 patients in each arm). Results of the primary analysis showed that HAQ scores at 18 months were 0.21 (95% CI: 0.01–0.40, P = 0.04) units higher (i.e. more disability) in the HandScan arm, compared with the Boolean arm, with an upper limit (one-sided alpha of 0.05) of 0.37. Therefore, non-inferiority of targeting HandScan remission could not be demonstrated, and the HandScan can even be regarded inferior, based on the 95% CI (although a secondary superiority analysis was not pre-specified). When also adjusting for duration of adherence to the T2T protocol, HAQ was 0.15 (95% CI: −0.05 – 0.36, one-sided upper limit: 0.32) units higher in the HandScan arm, with similar results in the PP population and when using non-imputed data. Duration of following T2T was not a statistically significant predictor of HAQ at 18 months.

HAQ score at 6 months was 0.11 (95% CI: −0.06 – 0.28, one-sided upper limit: 0.25) higher in the HandScan arm compared with the Boolean arm. In the PP analysis, HAQ was 0.19 (95% CI: −0.06 – 0.43, one-sided upper limit: 0.39) units higher in the HandScan arm at 18 months, and 0.07 (95% CI: −0.12 – 0.26, one-sided upper limit: 0.23) at 6 months compared with in the Boolean arm. Analyses based on non-imputed data were in accordance with these results.

Treatment

Fig. 2 shows that the mean advised treatment step (see Supplementary Table S1, available at Rheumatology online) over time was higher in the HandScan arm, indicating that this arm required more frequent intensification of treatment, with no clear difference in DAS28 and even slightly worse HAQ scores (Fig. 3). OST scores were comparable between the arms (Supplementary Fig. S1, available at Rheumatology online). Since many patients deviated from the T2T protocol and were no longer followed in detail, we assessed actual medication use at 18 months. Medication dosages at 18 months were comparable between arms, except for a slightly lower mean prednisone dose in the HandScan arm (3.1 vs 4.1 mg daily, P = 0.03) and numerically fewer patients on biological DMARDs (bDMARDs). Medication dosages for patients who followed T2T for 18 months were similar for MTX, higher for prednisone and much lower for TNFi compared with patients who stopped T2T protocol (Table 2). Out of the 24 patients who stopped T2T protocol because they were not in remission after 3 months of TNFi use (according to their randomization arm), only 2 switched to a different bDMARD when treated in regular care (both from the HandScan arm), suggesting that most of these 24 patients had sufficiently low disease activity according to their treating physician.

T2T non-adherence

Time to T2T non-adherence was significantly shorter in the HandScan arm (log rank test P = 0.01; Supplementary Fig. S2, available at Rheumatology online). HAQ scores at 18 months were not significantly better in patients following T2T than in patients stopping T2T (0.1 units lower, 95% CI: −0.2 – 0.4). A higher treatment step significantly increased the probability of non-adherence in the Cox regression analysis (HR: 1.3 CI: 1.1–1.5, P = 0.02, see Supplementary Table S3, available at Rheumatology online). Accordingly, the main reason for T2T non-adherence was unwillingness of patients and/or rheumatologists to increase medication because they felt that disease activity was sufficiently low (HandScan: 43%, Boolean: 32% according to either patient or rheumatologist). Patients not willing to increase medication were indeed in DAS28 remission (mean DAS28 2.0 95% CI: 1.6–2.4). In both arms, 21% stopped T2T because they did not achieve the predefined HandScan or Boolean remission criteria at maximum medication, that is, MTX at 30 mg orally (or maximum tolerable dose sc), 10 mg prednisone daily and a TNFi for at least 3 months.

Remission rates

Supplementary Fig. S2, available at Rheumatology online, shows the remission rates over time for all patients according to different remission criteria, demonstrating the highest rates for DAS28 remission (i.e. < 2.6), less for Boolean and even less for HandScan remission. HandScan remission rates barely increased over time, despite intensification of treatment in most patients. We explored a higher cut-off point for HandScan remission (≤13 overall OST score and ≤ 1 inflamed joints using an adjusted cut-off of 1.2), which slightly increased overall remission rates, but still showed little progression over time after month 4.

Discussion

The goal of this trial was to assess whether remission according to the HandScan can be validly used as a treatment target for early RA patients, since it is possibly a quicker and more objective way to assess disease activity compared with a composite measure using joint counts and an acute phase reactant.

Our primary analysis did not prove non-inferiority of using the HandScan remission criteria. Most importantly, our results do not reliably reflect the outcome of strictly following either treatment target, because of high non-adherence rates. If patients in the HandScan arm had strictly adhered to the T2T protocol, most patients would have quickly reached the maximum treatment step, presumably still not attaining HandScan remission, given our finding that HandScan remission rates barely increased over time. In accordance with this result, according to both patients and rheumatologists, HandScan remission required intensive treatment deemed unnecessary. Therefore, our primary conclusion is that HandScan remission as defined in this study is not an adequate treatment target in early RA. The fact that patients in the HandScan arm were initially advised more intensive treatment, without better HAQ scores or DAS28, appears to confirm this. Treatment intensity at 18 months was actually lower in the HandScan arm (ITT population), probably because this group stopped T2T protocol sooner and more often, and the treatment target in routine care tends to be less strict than the Boolean criteria. Lower treatment intensity may explain the slightly worse HAQ scores in the HandScan arm at 18 months. Patients were more willing to follow T2T in the Boolean arm, because treatment advice was less intensive, but presumably also because the Boolean criteria include clinical symptoms, perceived as a better reflection of patients’ disease activity and disease impact than OST scores.

Interestingly, non-adherence rates were remarkably high in the Boolean arm as well, since 32% of patients or rheumatologists were not willing to further increase medication to attain Boolean remission. The number of patients not able to achieve Boolean remission with maximum medication for 3 months was also high (21%), indicating possible overtreatment as well. It has to be noted that we assessed 44 joints, rather than 28, making the criteria slightly stricter.

The high non-adherence rates might be partly due the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have made patients hesitant to increase immunosuppressive medication. Also, the treatment protocol did not allow for tapering of prednisone until sustained remission was achieved, which was a reason for a few patients to drop from the T2T protocol. However, it is more likely that both treatment targets used in this study were too strict. Looking at the proportion of patients that fulfilled HandScan, Boolean and DAS28 remission criteria over time, the Boolean criteria are much stricter than the DAS28 criteria and the HandScan remission criteria are stricter still. Patients who were willing and able to follow T2T for 18 months (all using the Boolean target at that time) appear to be a selection of patients who are relatively easy to treat, as they attained remission quicker and more often, and rarely required TNFi treatment.

A decrease in overall OST scores mainly occurred in the first months in both arms but remained largely stable during the rest of follow up, despite escalation of treatment and decreasing DAS28 in most patients. This appears to confirm that the current cut-off point for HandScan remission (≤11 and ≤1 inflamed joint) is too strict for application in practice. A higher (less strict) cut-off for HandScan remission (as shown in Supplementary Fig. S3, available at Rheumatology online) only slightly increased remission rates, but also misclassified a few patients as being in remission at the start of study. This suggests that HandScan remission is not just too strict but is also not a sufficiently accurate measure of RA inflammation. We recently published an analysis on observational HandScan data from regular care, which showed that OST scores should be used in combination with additional clinical data to accurately estimate disease activity [11]. However, in an additional study, a combination of OST scores, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, a patient’s global assessment of disease activity (PGA) and gender was still inferior to DAS28 in classifying RA patients as having active or inactive disease (using clinical classification as reference standard) [12].

The Boolean criteria have been criticized, among other things because a PGA ≤ 1/10 may be too stringent [13–16]. Indeed, when patients were not in Boolean remission, in 19% of cases the PGA was the only criterion that was not fulfilled. To our knowledge, only one other study has been performed using the Boolean criteria (28 joints) as a treatment target [16], in which around 30% did not adhere to T2T therapy (exact definition and reasons not stated). In line with our findings, the most recent EULAR guidelines for the management of RA have endorsed a raise of the PGA threshold to ≤ 2 [17]. However, since no clinical trials have been performed using this revised version of the Boolean criteria, it remains unclear whether this will be a more feasible treatment target in clinical practice.

Our results are also in line with other studies failing to show benefit of the use of strict treatment targets in a T2T treatment strategy and even possibly harmful effects, such as ter Wee et al. [18] using DAS44 < 1.6 as a treatment target or Haavardsholm et al. [19] using clinical as well as remission on ultrasound. Although the main limitation of our study is the high non-adherence to the treatment strategies, complicating the evaluation of the strategies, this is in itself an outcome related to quality of the treatment target. Furthermore, the post hoc sensitivity analyses we performed did not change the main findings of our study. Another limitation related to this as well as COVID-19 pandemic is attrition of patients and missing data. However, our primary outcome was present for 82% of patients and we used multiple imputation including longitudinal information to adequately address this for the remaining 18%, although this of course does not fully correct for the missing data. Finally, results may be influenced by comorbidity, age and the fact that only hand joints are assessed in the HandScan, and therefore, we performed several ad hoc analyses. We found no evidence for possible effect modification by age (P-value of interaction = 0.0.990). When only including patients with at least any hand involvement at baseline, results were very similar to the main analyses with the HandScan approach leading to an 0.22 units higher HAQ at 18 months (95% CI: 0.02–0.41, P = 0.034).

In conclusion, the current HandScan remission criteria are inappropriate for a T2T strategy in early RA patients since they may lead to overtreatment and inferior clinical outcomes. Additionally, strict adherence to the Boolean target may also lead to high non-adherence rates.
