Integrated multi-omics and single-cell analysis of galectins and immune associations in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Peng Ji, Hongrui Liu, Gen-Hong Di, Zhi-Ming Shao, Yue Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Integrated multi-omics and single-cell atlas of galectins in TNBC.•LGALS2/9/10 expression correlates with immune infiltration and immune checkpoint molecules.•Single-cell analysis localizes galectins and links patterns to neoadjuvant therapy response.•Galectins serve as candidate biomarkers and hypothesis-generating molecules for future studies.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), which accounts for approximately 15% of all breast cancers, is a heterogeneous subtype characterized by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) expression and the lack of human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) amplification [1]. Owing to its aggressive biology, marked clinical and molecular heterogeneity, and limited therapeutic options, TNBC carries the poorest prognosis among breast cancer subtypes [2]. In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors, and antibody–drug conjugates, expanded the therapeutic landscape of TNBC [3,4]. In particular, the addition of ICIs to chemotherapy has improved pathological and survival outcomes in selected patients [5,6]. However, a substantial proportion of patients still fail to respond or eventually develop resistance, highlighting the urgent need to identify biomarkers and microenvironmental mechanisms associated with immune evasion and treatment resistance in TNBC.

Galectins are a family of soluble lectins with affinity for β-galactoside–containing glycans and exert pivotal functions across diverse physiological and pathological contexts [7]. Collectively, these proteins act through both extracellular and intracellular mechanisms, engaging glycan-dependent and glycan-independent pathways to reprogram the fate and function of multiple cell types [8,9]. In mammals, this family comprises 15 members, 11 of which are present in humans (Gal1, Gal2, Gal3, Gal4, Gal7, Gal8, Gal9, Gal10, Gal12, Gal13, and Gal14), all sharing a highly conserved carbohydrate recognition domain (CRD) and typically binding lactosamine-containing structures on glycoconjugates [10]. Galectins have been implicated in tumor progression and in modulating responses to multiple anticancer therapies [11]. Notably, they can undermine immunotherapeutic efficacy by disrupting immune-activating pathways or triggering immune-inhibitory circuits [9]. We previously demonstrated that LGALS2 shaped the immunosuppressive nature of the TNBC microenvironment and served as a potential immunotherapy target in TNBC [12]. However, a systematic characterization of galectin family members across genomic, transcriptomic, immune, and single-cell dimensions in TNBC remains lacking.

In this study, we aim to comprehensively investigate the roles of galectins in TNBC by integrating genomic, transcriptomic, single-cell sequencing and clinical data. We evaluated associations between galectin expression and patient prognosis, as well as relationships with immune-checkpoint molecules and tumor-infiltrating immune cells. Single-cell analyses further revealed that the expression of several galectins correlated with clinical outcomes in patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibitor treatment. Together, these findings nominate galectins as promising molecular biomarkers that could refine current TNBC molecular subtyping and help optimize immunotherapy selection.

Methods

Study cohorts

Our study included multi-omics cohorts of TNBC patients from the Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (FUSCC; n = 465, 100% female, including 360 with bulk RNA-seq data, 401 with copy number alteration [CNA] data and 279 with whole exome sequencing [WES] data) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA; n = 161, 100% female), and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data of 26 TNBC patients from FUSCC (100% female) [[13], [14], [15]]. TCGA TNBC cases were selected according to available clinical receptor annotations, including ER negativity, PR negativity, and HER2 negativity or lack of HER2 amplification, consistent with TNBC definition. All 88 normal samples in the FUSCC cohort and 113 normal samples in the TCGA cohort were paired adjacent tissues and have the corresponding tumor samples from the same patient. All bulk and scRNA-seq samples of FUSCC cohort were obtained from treatment-naïve primary TNBC tumors before systemic therapy. The 26 scRNA-seq patients were independent from the FUSCC bulk cohort. Patients with scRNA-seq data received either neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone (nanoparticle albumin-bound paclitaxel plus carboplatin, followed by epirubicin hydrochloride plus cyclophosphamide, n = 7) or in combination with immunotherapy (camrelizumab, n = 19). Pathological therapeutic response was assessed using ypT0/TisN0, defined as the absence of invasive residual disease in the breast and lymph nodes, allowing for noninvasive residuals, as evaluated after neoadjuvant therapy at the time of definitive surgery by experienced pathologists [15]. Genomic, transcriptomic and clinical data for the TCGA cohort were downloaded from the website (http://www.cbioportal.org/). All patients provided appropriate informed consent for data and tissue use as previously described. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the independent ethics committee/Institutional Review Board of the FUSCC Ethical Committee.

Identification of mRNA subtype of TNBC

To determine the optimal number of TNBC subtypes, consensus cluster analysis was performed with the expression profile. TNBC samples were classified into four distinct subtypes using K-means clustering based on genes with top 2000 standard deviations: luminal androgen receptor (LAR), immunomodulatory (IM), basal-like immune-suppressed (BLIS), and mesenchymal (MES). The detailed identification of FUSCC TNBC mRNA subtype was described in the previous research[13].

Copy number alteration analysis

Segmented copy number data were adjusted using the ASCAT algorithm to estimate tumor purity and ploidy, and gene-level CNA profiles were derived using GISTIC [16,17]. Samples with an ASCAT-evaluated aberrant cell fraction equal to 1 or those for which purity estimation failed were excluded. The copy number status by sample was reported as −2 (deletion), −1 (loss), 0 (diploid status), 1 (gain), and 2 (amplification) levels.

PD-L1 staining and evaluation

PD-L1 expression was assessed using the VENTANA SP142 assay as previously described [18]. TNBC specimens were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 6–72 h, and three serial sections of 4 μm thick formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) were prepared from samples. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was used for tissue quality control, requiring ≥50 viable tumor cells with associated stroma. If the H&E slides were unacceptable, staining was repeated. PD-L1 staining was performed on a VENTANA BenchMark ULTRA system according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The third section was stained with Rabbit Monoclonal Negative Control Ig as a matched negative reagent control. Each section contained positive and negative controls. Human benign tonsil stained with VENTANA PD-L1 (SP142) Assay can serve as both positive and negative tissue controls in each run. Staining reproducibility was confirmed by repetition when necessary. Strict quality control measures and appropriate controls contributed to the accuracy and dependability of the experimental results [19].

PD-L1 expression was evaluated using the immune cell (IC) and tumor cell (TC) scoring algorithm: TC staining was defined as the percentage of tumor cells exhibiting membrane staining of any intensity, while IC staining represented the proportion of tumor area occupied by PD-L1-positive immune cells. The tumor area was defined as the region occupied by TCs and their associated intratumoral stroma and contiguous peritumoral stroma. Two trained breast pathologists independently interpreted all PD-L1 results; discrepancies were resolved through iterative review until consensus was reached.

Estimation of tumor microenvironment cell abundance

Immune score, stromal score and ESTIMATE score were calculated using the ESTIMATE algorithm to analyze the infiltration levels of immune cells and stromal cells [20]. The abundance of 22 types of infiltrating immune cells in the TNBC samples was inferred from bulk transcriptomic profiles using the CIBERSORTx algorithm (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/) [21].

Evaluation of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs)

TILs were quantified on H&E-stained slides following the International TILs Working Group criteria [22]. Intratumoral TILs (iTILs) were defined as lymphocytes in direct contact with tumor cells, while stromal TILs (sTILs) were located in tumor-associated stroma. TIL abundance was recorded in 5% increments. Evaluations were independently performed by two experienced pathologists.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining for CD8 was performed on 4-μm FFPE tissue sections. After deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, slides were incubated with anti-CD8 antibody (clone SP57, Ventana) for 1 hour at room temperature. Signal detection was conducted using the Novolink Polymer Detection System, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Quantification was performed in a blinded manner and adjudicated by two pathologists.

Single-cell RNA-seq data processing

Raw scRNA-seq data were processed using Cell Ranger (version 3.1.0) provided by 10 × Genomics for demultiplexing and alignment. The Seurat package (version 5.3.0) was utilized for downstream analysis [23]. For quality controls, we applied the criteria to filter out cells with unique molecular identifier (UMI)/gene numbers <200 or >8000, percentage of mitochondrial counts > 20%.

We then used Harmony package (version 1.2.4) for batch correction [24]. Raw UMI counts were normalized, and most variable genes were identified by the FindVariableFeatures function. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted based on these variable genes, and FindClusters function which employs a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) approach based on PCA was applied for clustering. The first 100 principal components were used with a resolution of 2.0. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) dimensional reduction analysis was performed to visualize the cellular distribution. Marker genes were identified via the FindAllMarkers function, which used the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to determine significant genes, and assigned to cell types based on the CellMarker database and published literature [25].

Statistical analysis

Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Student’s t-test, Mann-Whitney test and Kruskal-Wallis test were applied to compare continuous variables where appropriate. Survival curves were constructed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate whether galectin expression was independently associated with survival after adjustment for clinicopathologic covariates, including tumor size and nodal status. Correlations were evaluated using Spearman’s correlation. Patients were divided into high- and low-expression groups using the optimal cutoff of each galectin related to survival within each cohort and the same classification was used for all analyses. Results are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise specified. Two-sided p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied for multiple testing to mitigate false-positive rates. All statistical analyses were conducted with R software (http://www.R-project.org, version 4.4.1).

Results

Expression of galectins was dysregulated in TNBC

We first explored the expression of galectins in TNBCs and normal tissues based on the FUSCC and TCGA databases. We found that in contrast to corresponding normal tissues, the expression of LGALS2 and LGALS9 were significantly elevated in TNBC while the expression of LGALS3, LGALS4, LGALS7 and LGALS12 were significantly downregulated in TNBC in both databases (Fig. 1A and B). The expression of LGALS8 was significantly downregulated in TNBC in TCGA cohort and the expression of LGALS10 was significantly upregulated in TNBC in FUSCC cohort. Moreover, the expression of LGALS13 and LGALS14 were extremely low and hard to detect in most samples. Thus, we did not include these two galectins in further analysis. Correlation analysis was further performed between two of galectins at the transcriptional level and revealed that the expression of several galectin pairs was highly correlated, including LGALS1 and LGALS3, LGALS2 and LGALS9 (Fig. 1C and D). We next compared galectin expression across FUSCC TNBC molecular subtypes. Genomic alterations of galectins in TNBC

We then analyzed the types and frequencies of genomic alterations in galectins. Results showed that galectins were rarely mutated. Only 3 samples were found to have mutations in LGALS4 while other galectins had no mutations in the FUSCC cohort. In the TCGA cohort, 1 sample had LGALS4 mutation and 3 samples had LGALS12 mutations. We have also investigated whether galectin expression differed according to common driver mutation status, including TP53 and PIK3CA, in the FUSCC cohort. We found that there was no significant association between galectin expression and TP53 mutation, while samples with PIK3CA mutation had higher expression of LGALS3, LGALS8 and LGALS12 (Figure S3 and S4). As for CNAs, our analysis demonstrated that 30% to 40% samples had normal copy number in galectins (Fig. 2A). Among all galectins, LGALS8 more frequently showed copy-number amplification or gain, and LGALS3 and LGALS9 had higher frequencies of copy number deletion or loss, in both FUSCC and TCGA cohorts. Prognostic potential of galectins in TNBC

We next elucidated the role of the expression of galectins in predicting prognosis of TNBC patients. The impact of galectin expression on survival rates was evaluated using FUSCC and TCGA cohorts (Fig. 3 and Figure S6 and S7). Both cohorts revealed that higher expression of LGALS2 was linked to a significantly increased overall survival (OS) value, while higher expression of LGALS3 showed significantly worse OS. Consistently, patients with higher expression of LGALS2 had significantly longer RFS and patients with high expression of LGALS3 had significantly shorter RFS in the FUSCC cohort. Moreover, in multivariable Cox analysis, LGALS2 remained independently associated with favorable OS and RFS, while LGALS3 remained independently associated with unfavorable OS and RFS after adjustment for tumor size and nodal status (Table S1).Fig. 3The prognostic value of galectins in TNBC. Expression of galectins correlated with immune checkpoint molecules

We then compared the expression levels between immune checkpoint molecules and galectins to investigate whether specific galectin expression patterns were linked to an immune-checkpoint-rich tumor microenvironment. Expression of galectins was associated with immune infiltration

Expression of LGALS2, LGALS9 and LGALS12 was significantly positively associated with immune score and stromal score both in FUSCC and TCGA cohorts. Expression of LGALS8 and LGALS10 was positively correlated with immune score and stromal score and expression of LGALS1 was positively correlated with stromal score in FUSCC cohort. We then evaluated the association of galectins with tumor infiltrating immune cells in TNBC. The FUSCC cohort showed that expression of LGALS2, LGALS9 and LGALS10 positively correlated with effector immune cells, including CD8+ T cells, M1 macrophages and memory activated CD4+ T cells, while expression of LGALS8 and LGALS12 was positively associated with inhibitory or resting immune cells, including naïve B cells, M2 macrophages and resting mast cells (Fig. 5B and Figure S9). Single-cell analysis revealed cellular localization of galectins and relationship with treatment

In order to further precisely analyze the role of galectins in the tumor microenvironment, we applied single-cell analysis of previously established cohort from FUSCC comprised 26 patients diagnosed with early-stage TNBC, with 7 patients receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy and 19 patients receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy plus immunotherapy (Fig. 6A). We observed that LGALS1, LGALS3, LGALS8 and LGALS9 expressed in all cell types including cancer cells, immune cells and stromal cells, while LGALS2 expressed more in the macrophages and LGALS7 expressed more in the cancer cells (Fig. 6B and Figure S12). Discussion

By conducting an integrated multi-omics analysis of galectin family members in TNBC, we explored the molecular features of galectins at the genomic and transcriptomic levels. We found that copy number status of LGALS1, LGALS3, LGALS4, and LGALS8 correlated with their mRNA expression levels, suggesting certain TNBC tumors may genetically augment or attenuate specific galectins. The expression of several galectins was revealed to be associated with patient survival outcomes, and correlation between certain galectins and anti-cancer immune activity in the tumor microenvironment was also unveiled. Furthermore, the single-cell analysis demonstrated that galectins are differentially distributed among different cell types in the TNBC microenvironment and associated with chemo-immunotherapy and chemotherapy responses.

Chemotherapy and immunotherapy have become the recommended treatment for TNBC in the neoadjuvant period or at metastatic stage [26]. However, these treatment strategies have not met satisfactory response and survival rates in specific TNBC patients. Therefore, how to select sensitive patients is a critical issue. Our findings carry significant biological and clinical implications, particularly for immunotherapy in TNBC and the development of novel biomarkers. First, the positive association of LGALS2 and LGALS9 with robust immune infiltration and checkpoint molecule expression suggests that these galectins might mark an immune-infiltrated, checkpoint-rich tumor microenvironment. LGALS2 plays different roles in inflammation, immune response, and arteriogenesis, while its function in cancer has been less studied [[27], [28], [29]]. Previous researches revealed that the prognostic role and function of LGALS2 was tumor type dependent [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. Notably, a recent study identified LGALS2 as a key component of tertiary lymphoid structures within breast tumors, implicating LGALS2 in promoting local immune cell activation (particularly dendritic cell stimulation) and anti-tumor immune pathways [34]. Besides, LGALS2 was reported to facilitate M2-like polarization and proliferation of tumor-associated macrophages through the CSF1/CSF1R axis and served as an immunotherapy target in TNBC [12]. Consistent with this, our analysis revealed that patients with lower LGALS2 had better pathological response to chemo-immunotherapy, implying that LGALS2 could serve as a biomarker to identify TNBC patients who might respond to immunotherapy, and who might benefit from combinatorial strategies that neutralize LGALS2’s immunoregulatory effects.

The role of LGALS9 in cancer has been extensively studied and the expression of LGALS9 in tumor tissues is distinct in different tumor types [35]. LGALS9 has been shown to have anti-metastatic potential in breast cancer, possibly because LGALS9 induced tumor cell aggregation and reduced adhesion of breast cancer cells to the extracellular matrix, thereby preventing metastasis and improving survival [36]. Moreover, LGALS9 could bind to and interact with various receptors, such as TIM-3 and PD-1, in the regulation of immune response and tumor biology [37]. TIM-3 is the most closely related among the multiple receptors of LGALS9. Both LGALS9 and TIM-3 can inhibit anti-cancer immune surveillance and the interaction between LGALS9 and TIM-3 can induce apoptosis of CD4+ and CD8+ TILs and inhibit T-cell immune responses [38]. Breast cancer cells activated the TIM-3-LGALS9 pathway by expressing LPHN1 and its ligand FLRT3, and transferred LGALS9 to the cell surface to protect breast cancer cells against cytotoxic immune attack while inhibiting host anti-cancer immune surveillance [39]. Recent research further demonstrated that anti-LGALS9 therapy selectively expanded intratumoral TIM-3+ cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and immunosuppressive regulatory T cells and highlighted LGALS9 as a target for cancer immunotherapy [40]. In this study, LGALS9 was also found to be positively associated with immune infiltration and immune checkpoint molecules including TIM-3 and PD-1. Moreover, single-cell analysis showed that patients with pCR after neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy had lower expression of LGALS9 while patients with pCR after neoadjuvant chemotherapy had higher expression of LGALS9, implying LGALS9 might play different roles in distinct treatments and could serve as a biomarker for various therapies.

The role of LGALS3 in TNBC also has important clinical ramifications. We found that high LGALS3 correlates with poor survival in TNBC, and although it was associated with worse baseline prognosis, intriguingly it was higher in responders to neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy in our cohort. LGALS3 is well-known to have pro-tumoral functions: it can foster tumor cell motility, metastasis, and angiogenesis [41]. In the immune context, LGALS3 can impair T-cell function by forming lattices with glycosylated receptors on T cells and by promoting the expansion of regulatory T cells and other suppressive elements [42]. Tumor-secreted galectin-3 (Gal-3) promotes IL-10 production in B cells, thus impairing antitumor immunity [43]. These findings justify why LGALS3 is generally associated with a worse prognosis - it enables immune evasion and tumor progression. However, the paradoxical association of high LGALS3 with better pathological response to chemo-immunotherapy in our study suggests a nuanced interpretation. Nonetheless, it highlights that LGALS3 may represent a candidate molecule for future therapeutic investigation in TNBC. In fact, LGALS3 inhibitors (such as carbohydrate-based antagonists) are being explored in clinical trials in combination with immunotherapies in other cancers [41,44].

Our single-cell analyses further underscore the importance of intratumoral heterogeneity in shaping galectin biology and treatment outcomes. We observed that galectins such as LGALS1, LGALS3, LGALS8, and LGALS9 were broadly expressed across malignant, immune, and stromal compartments, whereas others, such as LGALS2 in macrophages and LGALS7 in tumor epithelial cells, displayed more restricted, cell-type-specific patterns. Such distribution suggests that galectins may exert context-dependent roles: for example, tumor cell-derived galectins may promote proliferation and immune escape, while immune cell-derived galectins may regulate T cell exhaustion, dendritic cell activation, or macrophage polarization [45,46]. This heterogeneity may partly explain why certain galectins (e.g., LGALS9) exhibit opposite predictive value in chemotherapy versus chemo-immunotherapy cohorts, reflecting treatment-specific cellular dependencies and microenvironmental dynamics [47].

Our study has several limitations. First, the study is retrospective in nature and relies on observational associations. Although we integrated multi-omics data, confounding factors cannot be excluded. Second, potential cohort biases should also be considered. FUSCC cohort is ethnically Chinese and treated at a single center, which may differ from Western populations (like TCGA) in terms of genetics or treatment patterns. We attempted to validate findings in TCGA, but not all analyses could be cross-validated. Third, although we examined associations with immune infiltration and conducted extensive bioinformatic analyses, we did not perform in-depth mechanistic experiments, including in vitro knockdown, pharmacologic blockade, or in vivo validation experiments, within this study to functionally validate each galectin’s role. Fourth, although the scRNA-seq cohort provides high-resolution cell-type-specific information, treatment-response stratification was based on relatively small subgroups. Therefore, the associations between galectin expression and pCR should be interpreted as exploratory and require validation in larger, prospectively collected cohorts. Last, most analyses in the present study were based on mRNA expression. Given the potential discordance between transcript and protein abundance, especially for secreted or extracellular galectins, future studies should validate key findings at the protein level.

In conclusion, this study suggests that galectins, in particular LGALS2, LGALS3, LGALS8 and LGALS9, are related to prognosis of patients with TNBC and are also promising biomarkers to predict the response to chemo-immunotherapy in TNBC, probably due to their unique expression in the anti-tumor inflammatory microenvironment. The bioinformatic survey provides a basis for future experimental work focusing on these members in TNBC.
