Reversible jumps for the joint modeling of the dimension of pseudo-functional models in chromosomal windows in genome-wide selection
Authors: Ernandes Guedes Moura, Carlos Pereira da Silva, Luciano Antonio de Oliveira, Marcio Balestre, Júlio Sílvio de Sousa Bueno Filho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the advance of large-scale genotyping and the high availability of single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers, genomic selection (GS) has become fundamental for plant and animal breeding. However, this technique faces three classical problems: multicollinearity, high marker dimensionality, and high computational cost. To address these problems, mixed models or Bayesian inference methods are generally used. More recently, bin-based genomic window approaches have been widely used, as they can group redundant markers, reducing dimensionality and mitigating the effects of multicollinearity. However, the performance of these methods depends, among other factors, on the number, size, and location of the bins. In general, existing approaches group markers based on linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns or use fixed-size genome partitions.ResultsA transdimensional method was proposed to jointly infer the dimension, location, and composition of genomic compartments, as well as the parameters associated with the model, through reversible jump Markov chain Monte Carlo (RJ-MCMC) sampling. The method was evaluated in simulated F2 and F10 populations under oligogenic and polygenic architectures, as well as in human SNP data from the HapMap project with simulated phenotypes and real eucalyptus data. In the simulated datasets, the method achieved the best results under oligogenic architectures (F2 and F10). In more complex polygenic scenarios and real data, performance was satisfactory and comparable to that of rr-BLUP, Bayes B, and Hu, considering mean squared error (MSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and coefficient of determination (R2).ConclusionsThe proposed method incorporates model complexity as an inferential component, allowing the automatic estimation of the number, location, and composition of genomic compartments jointly with the model effects. The method demonstrated robust predictive performance, in addition to improving the ability to identify causal regions, which may represent a promising methodological alternative for both genomic selection and QTL mapping.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13073-3.

Introduction

The genomic selection (GS) initially proposed by [1] predicts genotypic values ​​using markers that saturate the genome. The statistical results were used to simplify the genetic aspects of previous regression models of QTL mapping and marker-assisted selection. Due to the technological revolution, which generated the availability of thousands of markers, GS methods began to face two theoretical problems of regression models: high dimensionality and multicollinearity. This has led to the development of statistical techniques that allow saturated linear models to be adjusted. In general, the proposed methods are grouped into two basic strategies: variable selection and shrinkage models [2–4].

Variable selection is a crucial technique for model simplification, but evaluating all possible submodels is often impractical. Therefore, establishing appropriate criteria for this selection presents a significant challenge. Furthermore, Bayesian shrinkage estimators tend to be computationally demanding, which can limit their application in regression analysis. The high dimensionality of regression models remains a significant issue, and enhancing statistical methods is essential to fully explore the potential of genetic markers, which are becoming increasingly dense [5–8].

The functional data analysis (FDA) technique has been applied in association studies by considering the genome as a continuous function of chromosomal positions and capturing patterns of linkage disequilibrium (LD) between genomic regions and QTL [7, 9] In this context, [10]) proposed determining window boundaries through inflection points of smoothed splines, providing a data-driven approach. On the other hand, [6] proposed dividing the genome into high-LD blocks called bins, in which regions with higher LD are represented by a smaller number of bins, whereas regions with lower LD require a greater density of windows. These intervals are known as natural bins and are defined based on LD structure [6, 11, 12].

Although the natural bin method has shown higher accuracy than traditional genomic selection (GS) models, blocks with high linkage disequilibrium may not be directly applicable in some situations, since the number of bins depends, among other factors, on sample size. To overcome this limitation [11], introduced the concept of artificial bins, in which breakpoints are defined a priori by the researcher using genetic criteria, such as genomic annotations from previous studies, without the need for prior establishment of high-LD blocks (natural bins), greatly simplifying the analysis.

As seen above, it is possible to combine several single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the genome into a manageable number of genomic windows called bins [4, 6, 11]. Other teams of researchers have proposed similar techniques of dividing the markers into groups with different names in different contexts; for example [13], conducted a study on the construction of haplotypes in the bovine genome to detect genes that affect traits related to meat quality [14]. proposed another similar technique called compact BLUP (cBLUP) to approximate kinship (or identity in state) among individuals in genome-wide association studies (GWAS). The results of these works show that association based on SPN groups may be more efficient than the single SNP analysis, having a lower computational cost.

In the classic definition of bins, the mean effect of the bins is used as a measure of information. That is, the bins are treated as new “synthetic” markers, and because of this, it is assumed that all markers in the same genomic window should have homogeneous effects. This means that the size of the windows cannot be overly large. If the bins are large, another assumption to counterbalance is necessary, i.e., all markers in the bin must have effects in the same direction [4, 6, 11, 12].

Models such as [15], which group SNPs into fixed windows, have shown gains in the power to detect rare QTL and reductions in computational cost. However, they still depend on the prior definition of window size, which may significantly affect model performance. Similarly [12], proposed a modification of the method of [6], incorporating the central SNP of each bin as a measure of information (BIN-One_Lasso). Although this approach improves type I error control and statistical power, it still requires the prior definition of the number of bins based on LD variance criteria.

To partially overcome these limitations [4], proposed weighting the effects of markers within each genomic window based on the frequency of visits obtained via MCMC (Markov Chain Monte Carlo). The weighting avoids the need to cancel opposing directional effects, making the strategy of dividing the genome into blocks more straightforward. However, although satisfactory results were obtained, in practice, the ideal number or size of the bins is unknown, which is a highly influential parameter in the success of the technique. A promising approach to this type of problem appears to be the adoption of reversible jump (RJ-MCMC), proposed by [16] in the context of regression model selection and subsequently applied to QTL models by several authors [17–22]. Despite these advances, several challenges remain. Most approaches depend on fixed partitions or on population-specific LD structures, which may generate unstable representations of the genomic architecture. In addition, many methods treat bins as synthetic markers based on the mean of SNPs, implying a strong assumption of effect homogeneity within windows. Another limitation is determining the ideal size and location of these bins, which directly affect the predictive ability of the technique [4] These limitations highlight the need for more flexible methods capable of simultaneously inferring bin structure and the associated effects.

The present study proposes a Bayesian model that allows the simultaneous inference of the number of bins and the associated effects, treating model dimension as a random variable. Inference is performed through the reversible jump MCMC algorithm (RJ-MCMC) [16], allowing dynamic adaptation of the genomic partition. Marker effects are estimated using the shrinkage method proposed by [23], due to its computational efficiency and ease of implementation.

Materials and methods

Datasets

Simulated data

In this experiment, three simulated datasets were considered to evaluate the proposed method under different genetic architectures and marker structures. In the first scenario, ten linkage groups were simulated, each spanning 1.20 cM, with an average distance of 0.001 cM between adjacent markers. Recombination fractions were generated using the Haldane mapping function, and meiosis was simulated by a random walk process, resulting in an F2 population composed of 300 individuals and 12,150 SNP markers generated with the QGenes software [24] under a biparental crossing scheme derived from two founder parental lines, in which recombination events across generations produced the segregating population.

Two genetic architectures were investigated: an oligogenic scenario, in which 10 SNPs were assigned as QTL, and a scenario with 60 SNPs considered as QTL. The second scenario consisted of an F10 population generated under the same assumptions as the first scenario, representing advanced generations of recombination, resulting in 320 individuals genotyped for 10,020 SNP markers. Given the high marker density (average spacing of 0.001 cM), local linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns were expected to emerge naturally through recombination events across generations, whereas the third scenario incorporated empirical LD structures using HapMap genotype data.

The third scenario was constructed using genotype data with a natural linkage disequilibrium structure obtained from the HapMap Project, while phenotypes were simulated. Specifically, we used the HapMap dataset distributed with the R package SNPassoc, which comprises 9,307 SNP markers spanning the 22 autosomal chromosomes and includes individuals from two populations: European ancestry (CEU) and Yoruba (YRI) [25]. Additional information on the dataset and package implementation is available in the SNPassoc documentation (https://cran.r-project.org/package=SNPassoc).

Marker-associated genomic information, including SNP identifiers, was retrieved from the HapMap data frame, while corresponding chromosome assignments and physical genomic positions were obtained from the HapMap.SNPs.pos dataset, also provided by the SNPassoc package. To ensure data quality, markers with minor allele frequency lower than 5% were removed, and missing genotypes were imputed using the A.mat() function from the rrBLUP package [26]. The resulting dataset consisted of 7,574 SNPs across 120 individuals. Phenotypes were then simulated following the same procedures adopted in the previous scenarios.

Actual data

To illustrate the proposed methodology using real phenotypic data, a population of Eucalyptus spp. composed of 610 individuals was used. This population originated from crosses involving Eucalyptus grandis, E. urophylla, E. globulus and E. camaldulensis. The phenotypic trait considered in this study was plant height (m), measured at 24 months of age, corresponding to the second evaluation season (ALT2). All individuals were genotyped using the Dart-GBS platform, resulting in a panel of 15,104 DArT-Seq markers [27]. For this dataset, in addition to evaluating predictive accuracy, a comparison of computational time among the evaluated methods was also performed.

Functional model

In this study, the Bayesian functional model developed by [4] was adapted to automatic bin selection using the MCMC method with reversible jump (RJ-MCMC). Given the high density of markers, we used the idea of dividing the genome into windows (analogous to the artificial bin method of [11] as a dimensional reduction strategy. When genetic markers cover the entire genome, the positions of the markers are so close that they can be considered a saturated set of observations in the domain of a continuous function (in the chromosome range), i.e., each point (position) that represents a candidate region can be smoothed by a functional relationship.

Thus, the functional genomic model assumes that the signal function of a gene in the genome can be described by a one-dimensional function [4]. Verifying all the positions in the genome is impossible because it is a continuous variable. Therefore, the positions of the markers (λ) are used as a fine grid of realizations of an unknown function f(λ ). In other words, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f\left(\lambda \right)=\gamma (\lambda )$$\end{document}, in which γ is the functional representation of the effect of gene expression at a given position. The ultimate objective is to estimate γ and predict the genomic genetic value of new individuals based on this estimate.

Adaptive RJ-MCMC sampling algorithm

The main innovation of the proposed method lies in incorporating a Reversible Jump Markov Chain Monte Carlo (RJ-MCMC) step into the genomic binning framework, allowing the number, size, and boundaries of genomic windows to be estimated directly from the data. Unlike fixed-window approaches, the method employs Metropolis–Hastings steps that accept or reject different moves, such as the “birth” or “death” of a bin. These steps allow the current model to transition to higher- or lower-dimensional models [30]. Here, the variable does not need to be changed because the determinant of the Jacobian of the transformation is equal to one since it is the determinant of an identity matrix, similar to in the proposals of [17–19, 31].

Due to the variability of the dimensionality of our problem, we must design types of motion between the K subspaces. For this problem, the possible transitions are:(I) the addition of a bin (a birth stage),(II) the exclusion of a bin (a death stage) and.(III) maintenance of the current number of bins.

The details of the conditional algorithm and the complete RJ-MCMC used in this paper, plus the decision rule to include or exclude a bin in the model, will be presented below.

Addition of bin (birth stage)

In this step, a single bin is drawn from among the existing bins, and a single tag contained in that bin is selected. This tag will be transformed into the limit of two new bins and will also be the representative tag of the new bin in the iteration.

The updating of all parameters except K follows the usual standard MCMC models. The RJ-MCMC results in a joint posterior chain that is formed with variable frequencies of bins and markers. This allows marginal inference on parameters such as the number of bins, position and effects of markers (QTL) and individual breeding values for the GS.

Implementation of the analysis

The RJ-MCMC algorithm, combining Reversible Jump steps with Gibbs and Metropolis–Hastings updates, was implemented in R software [33]. All datasets used in this study, together with the BinRJ R package (v0.1.0), are available in the supplementary material. For both simulated and real data analyses, a total of 5,000 iterations were performed. The first 1,000 iterations were discarded as burn-in to reduce the influence of initial values. A thinning interval of two iterations was applied to reduce autocorrelation among stored samples, resulting in 2,000 posterior samples retained for inference. All analyses were performed on a desktop workstation (Intel Core i7 CPU, 32 GB RAM, Windows OS).

Predictive accuracy

In addition to the proposed model, both simulated and real datasets were analysed using competing genomic prediction approaches. Specifically, the Bayes B model was fitted with the BGLR function from the BGLR package [2], the rr-BLUP model was fitted with the mixed.solve function from the rrBLUP package [26], and the model proposed by Hu was implemented through the PAS package [6]. All analyses were conducted in the R environment [31].

Predictive performance was evaluated using the mean squared error (MSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and coefficient of determination (R2), while computational efficiency was additionally assessed through processing time in seconds for the real-data application. These criteria are defined as follows:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$MSE=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\left({y}_{i}-{\widehat{y}}_{i}\right)}^{2}$$\end{document}25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$MAE=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|{y}_{i}-{\widehat{y}}_{i}\right|$$\end{document}

Mean squared error (MSE) and mean absolute error (MAE) can be complemented by the coefficient of determination, a measure that ranges from 0 to 1, where 0 indicates no predictive ability and 1 indicates perfect prediction. This measure is denoted by:26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}^{2}={\left[cor(y,\widehat{y})\right]}^{2}$$\end{document}where27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widehat{y}}_{i}=\widehat{\mu }+Z\widehat{\gamma }$$\end{document}

A k-fold cross-validation (cv) procedure was conducted to assess the model's predictive capacity on untested genotypes. For this purpose, k=5 partitions were adopted, such that in each of the five rounds, four subsets (80% of individuals) formed the training population and the remaining subset (20% of individuals) constituted the validation population. The performance of the techniques was compared based on the arithmetic mean of the five performance estimates from the validation sets.

Discussion

Advances in state-of-the-art sequencing technologies make it possible to address models that were previously only theorized by [1], as well as to expand on the possibilities of these models. The large volume of sequencing data at high marker density and potentially high LD in sequenced populations also indicates that the vast majority of available markers are redundant or do not provide information on QTL segregation [4, 11, 34]. For GS purposes, several strategies have been used to address the high dimensionality, multicollinearity and potentially high computational costs of these models [2, 12]. The objective, however, seems simpler than associating QTL with genomic regions. Unexpected predictors based on noncausal segregation may, by mere chance, describe very well the traits of interest in the population. On the other hand, if there is any interest in inferring causality, strategies based on dividing the genome into bins seem to be more efficient.

Although marker blocks in high linkage disequilibrium (LD) can be exploited to represent genomic regions more informatively, their direct delimitation (natural bins) depends on factors such as marker density and sample size, which limits their practical applicability [6, 11]. As an alternative, approaches based on artificial bins have been proposed to capture local dependence among markers while simultaneously reducing model dimensionality [11, 35].

However, although artificial bins [4, 11] provide valuable computational simplification, these methods remain limited by the need for prior and arbitrary specification of the number and size of such partitions. Since these parameters strongly influence the success of the analyses, their optimization becomes necessary and is often performed through grid search, thereby increasing computational cost and the risk of bias.

In the present study, a strategy related to previous artificial-bin approaches was developed. However, unlike fixed-bin methods [4, 11] in which the number of partitions (K) is arbitrarily specified or selected through grid search, the proposed methodology removes this source of selection bias. By treating K as a random variable within the Reversible Jump framework, the model autonomously explores the parameter space. This allows the underlying genetic architecture of the population to determine the required model complexity, while directly incorporating structural uncertainty into the posterior distribution.

A similar method called cBLUP (compact BLUP) was proposed by [14] In this method, the bin strategy was used to derive the relationship between individuals in genome-wide association studies (GWAS). In analyses by these authors of simulated and real data, this method showed flexibility for evaluating a variety of characteristics. For traits with low heritability, cBLUP outperformed the genomic best linear unbiased prediction gBLUP [36] and Bayesian LASSO methods [37]. However, to optimize the size and number of bins, they used the maximum likelihood method instead of seeking to infer the size of the model.

Figure 6 shows the results of an analysis with K = 75 (panel A). This configuration produced several unnecessary bins, which only delayed the convergence of the posterior chains and increased the computational time required to complete the analysis. In contrast, K = 10 (panel B) appears to provide a more satisfactory partition of the genome. Under this configuration, predictive ability was substantially improved, and results similar to those obtained with the Reversible Jump approach were achieved. Nevertheless, fixed-bin strategies remain limited because imposing uniformly sized bins may fragment causal regions into adjacent windows, thereby reducing mapping resolution [10].

The Reversible Jump binning methodology outperformed all compared methods in the prediction of genomic values under oligogenic simulated scenarios, and remained competitive in the other simulation scenarios as well as in real eucalyptus data, while presenting a moderate computational cost lower than that observed for Bayes B. These results are consistent with previous studies that explored local dependence among markers, such as BayesN [15], in which SNPs are grouped into non-overlapping genomic windows to capture joint effects and LD patterns. However, the proposed Reversible Jump binning extends this rationale by adaptively estimating genomic partitions.

In summary, this study extends previous bin-based GS models by allowing the breakpoints for genomic partitioning to be learned from the data through the RJ-MCMC algorithm. This feature enables the model complexity to adapt to the underlying genetic architecture, flexibly incorporating the local linkage disequilibrium structure. Based on the results obtained, the methodology demonstrated competitive performance in the prediction of genomic values and provided a high-resolution genomic scan for causal regions, suggesting potential for future applications in genome-wide association studies and QTL mapping.
