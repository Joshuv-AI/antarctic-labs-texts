Transcatheter Tricuspid Valve Replacement for Severe Tricuspid Regurgitation Post–Left Ventricular Assist Device
Authors: Gennaro Giustino, Mostafa Naguib, Chantal Y Asselin, Konstantinos P Koulogiannis, Raul Flores, Saad Ahmad, James Slater, Linda Gillam, Philippe Généreux, Abhishek Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe presence of severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) in patients supported with durable left ventricular assist devices (LVADs) is associated with increased morbidity and mortality. The role of transcatheter interventions remains limited, but represents a promising therapeutic frontier.Case SummaryA 62-year-old woman with chemotherapy-induced cardiomyopathy underwent HeartMate 3 LVAD implantation, complicated by progressive right ventricular (RV) dilation, severe annular dilation, and torrential secondary TR with marked leaflet malcoaptation. Despite guideline-directed medical therapy and chronic inotropic support, she developed refractory RV failure with a ventricularized right atrial waveform on catheterization. Given prohibitive surgical risk, transfemoral transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) with a 56-mm Edwards EVOQUE valve was performed, resulting in near-complete TR elimination, improved RV–pulmonary artery coupling, and clinical recovery.DiscussionThis case illustrates TTVR as a viable therapy for refractory TR and RV failure in select LVAD-supported patients.Take-Home MessageTTVR offers a definitive alternative for post–LVAD severe TR when surgical repair is not feasible.Key words: left ventricular assist device, post–LVAD right ventricular failure, right ventricular failure, tricuspid regurgitation, transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement

History of Presentation

A 62-year-old woman with chemotherapy-induced nonischemic cardiomyopathy and implantable cardiac-defibrillator (ICD) for primary prevention presented with NYHA functional class IV heart failure (left ventricular [LV] ejection fraction: 20%), and cardiogenic shock requiring temporary mechanical circulatory support. At presentation, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated severe LV dysfunction with LV ejection fraction of 20%, and moderate tricuspid regurgitation (TR) with central TR jet, mild tricuspid leaflet tethering, and malcoaptation, but preserved leaflet thickness and mobility (Figure 1, Video 1). The right ventricle (RV) at the time was mildly dilated with preserved function (Figure 1, Video 1).

She was bridged to destination therapy with HeartMate 3 left ventricular assist device (LVAD; Abbot Laboratories). This was complicated by refractory RV failure and severe TR requiring hemodynamic stabilization with chronic milrinone support. She was subsequently monitored with structured longitudinal follow-up, including serial clinical and echocardiographic assessments.

Six months post–LVAD, she developed persistent NYHA functional class IV symptoms characterized by progressive weight gain, lower extremity edema, and increasing abdominal girth despite escalation of her diuretic regimen and continuous inotropic support, necessitating repeated hospital admissions.

Past Medical History

Past medical history was significant for a longstanding history of HER2-positive invasive ductal carcinoma of the breast treated with lumpectomy, adjuvant doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide followed by docetaxel, and radiation therapy, with subsequent paclitaxel, trastuzumab, and pertuzumab for recurrent disease. Additional history included papillary thyroid carcinoma status post total thyroidectomy in 2014, psoriatic arthritis currently managed with apremilast (previously methotrexate and ustekinumab), chronic kidney disease stage III with proteinuria, type 2 diabetes mellitus, obstructive sleep apnea, and obesity.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of severe TR culminating in RV failure post–LVAD is broad and encompassed: 1) primary RV dysfunction from progressive anthracycline-mediated myopathy or LVAD-induced geometric distortion with altered interventricular septal mechanics; 2) primary valve pathology driven by ICD lead–related disruption of coaptation geometry; 3) development of, or unmasked, hemodynamically significant precapillary pulmonary hypertension; 4) pericardial or infiltrative disease, including constrictive physiology or cardiac metastatic involvement given her oncologic history; and 5) refractory volume retention from progression of intrinsic renal disease.

Investigations

Baseline electrocardiogram demonstrated normal sinus rhythm and interrogation of ICD revealed no arrythmias and stable lead parameters. Initial TTE revealed progression to torrential TR and severe RV dilation (Video 2), with a right ventricular outflow tract velocity-time integral (RVOT VTI) of 11 cm. Laboratory evaluation was notable for progressively worsening hepatic and renal dysfunction, consistent with end-organ hypoperfusion in the setting of RV failure. Despite increased intravenous milrinone (0.375 μg/kg/min) and intensive combination diuretic therapy (continuous intravenous furosemide infusion at 20 mg/h, with adjunctive chlorothiazide), right heart catheterization demonstrated persistently elevated right-sided filling pressures with a ventricularized right atrial (RA) waveform (Figure 2).

Management

Because the patient was deemed to be a prohibitive risk for reoperation, and the anatomy was not amenable to transcatheter tricuspid valve clip, she was referred for transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) with EVOQUE (Edwards). Preprocedural planning included multidisciplinary evaluation by our advanced heart failure, structural heart, and electrophysiology teams to assess the feasibility of transcatheter valve deployment in the presence of a transvenous ICD. Device interrogation at the time of admission demonstrated stable lead parameters from pre– and post–LVAD implantation. Multimodality imaging confirmed absence of lead-induced leaflet impingement or primary lead-related TR, and the lead position was deemed compatible with transcatheter valve deployment.

A 56-mm Edwards EVOQUE valve was successfully implanted under general anesthesia and TEE guidance (Figure 3, Videos 3 and 4) resulting in immediate TR resolution (Video 5). Postimplantation, there was increase in systolic arterial amplitude and pulse pressure, with restoration of opening of the aortic valve with every beat, reflecting improved forward flow from combined RA–RV–pulmonary arterial (PA) recoupling (Figure 4). This hemodynamic improvement was corroborated by an increase in RVOT VTI from 11 to 16 cm, reflecting augmentation of effective forward stroke volume. Repeat catheterization demonstrated normalization of right-sided pressures with restoration of a physiological RA waveform (Figure 2). Repeat device interrogation demonstrated stable RV lead parameters. Milrinone was successfully discontinued, and she was discharged on oral torsemide 40 mg daily with improved functional status and recovery of baseline renal function.

Outcome and Follow-Up

During longitudinal follow-up, the patient has remained clinically stable and free from recurrent heart failure hospitalization, stroke, or device-related complications. She remains off inotropic support with stable cardiac and renal function. NYHA functional class improved from IV at presentation to II at follow-up. Serial TTE at 1 and 6 months demonstrated stable trace residual TR and stable RV size and function (Figure 5). Follow-up device interrogations continued to demonstrate preserved lead function without interval change. Overall, the patient has exhibited sustained clinical and echocardiographic stability after TTVR intervention.

Discussion

Severe TR in patients with durable LVADs has emerged as a hemodynamically complex and physiologically challenging pathologic process in the era of durable mechanical circulatory support. Recurrent or progressive TR affects up to one-third of LVAD recipients leading to worsening RV function, systemic congestion, and increased late mortality.1 Although LV unloading via LVAD implantation improves left-sided filling pressures and enhances systemic perfusion, its impact on RV performance is often heterogeneous and can be maladaptive, especially in patients with preexisting RV dysfunction or significant TR.2,3 The interplay of chronic RV dysfunction with RA-RV-PA interactions, combined with the structural and mechanical alterations imposed post–LVAD (ie, decreased LV apical twist, pericardial changes, reduced RV mechanical efficiency because of RV and septal deformation from altered interventricular mechanics, continuous-flow physiology), establishes a hemodynamic milieu in which TR both reflects and perpetuates RV compromise.3,4

The role of transcatheter tricuspid valve intervention (TVI) at the time of LVAD implantation for clinically significant TR remains an area of ongoing debate. Large registry analyses and recent meta-analyses evaluating concomitant TVI in patients with moderate-to-severe TR have yielded equivocal results, with no consistent improvement in survival, postoperative right heart failure, or rehospitalization compared with isolated LVAD implantation.1, 2, 3 In both the Interagency Registry for Mechanically Assisted Circulatory Support (INTERMACS) and European Registry for Patients with Mechanical Circulatory Support (EUROMACS) registries, early postoperative reductions in TR severity failed to confer a mortality benefit, with concomitant repair being associated with longer cardiopulmonary bypass times, higher rates of renal dysfunction, and increased early morbidity.1,5 As a result, guidelines now assign concomitant TVI a class IIb recommendation for patients with more-than-moderate TR undergoing LVAD implantation.5 Consequently, prophylactic surgical repair at the time of LVAD implantation may not address the underlying substrate and, in some patients, could introduce unnecessary operative complexity and risk without durable hemodynamic benefit. Persistent or progressive TR after LVAD support thus represents not a failure of initial surgical correction, but rather a marker of ongoing maladaptive RA-RV-PA and left atrial–LV/LVAD interactions. This conceptual shift underscores the importance of individualized, physiology-guided strategies over routine surgical correction in this complex population.1,5

Emerging transcatheter therapies for severe functional TR have expanded treatment options in patients deemed prohibitive surgical candidates; however, their application in LVAD-supported populations remains largely unstudied.3 Leaflet approximation techniques, such as the TriClip Transcatheter Tricuspid Valve Repair System (Abbott Structural Heart) and the PASCAL Transcatheter Valve Repair System (Edwards Lifesciences), have demonstrated safety and efficacy in patients with severe secondary TR and favorable anatomy—specifically those with small septolateral coaptation gaps (<7 mm) and limited leaflet tethering.3,6,7 Similarly, percutaneous annuloplasty systems such as the Cardioband (Edwards Lifesciences) offer targeted annular reduction and are best suited for patients with functional atrial TR driven by annular dilation.6,7 However, both approaches are limited by technical feasibility in patients with extensive annular dilatation, severe leaflet tethering, or complex RV geometry (frequently encountered in post–LVAD patients), and where adequate device anchoring and durable coaptation cannot be achieved.3,6

In contrast, transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) offers a comprehensive corrective strategy capable of restoring valve competence irrespective of jet location or leaflet morphology, provided that RV function is preserved.7 Contemporary data indicate that TTVR can achieve near-complete TR elimination in >95% of treated patients, resulting in substantial improvements in right-sided hemodynamics and clinical status.7 This is particularly attractive in anatomically complex post–LVAD scenarios, where leaflet-approximation or annuloplasty techniques are less likely to yield durable results. In the present case, the patient demonstrated pronounced annular dilatation, a coaptation gap of 11 mm, and significant leaflet tethering—features predictive of poor outcomes with edge-to-edge or annuloplasty-based interventions.3,6,7 Pulmonary vascular resistance was measured at 1.7 Wood units, with preserved pulmonary vascular compliance, suggesting that progressive RV dysfunction was driven by a combination of the underlying global myocardial injury from prior anthracycline exposure that was compounded by volume loading and geometric distortion because of LVAD-induced changes, rather than maladaptive remodeling from fixed pulmonary vascular disease.8 Given these findings, TTVR was deemed the most physiologically rational and definitive therapy to restore tricuspid competence, decompress the RA and RV, and stabilize LVAD flows without the need for RV assist device support.

An additional procedural consideration was the presence of a preexisting transvenous RV ICD lead in a patient with advanced heart failure and LVAD support undergoing evaluation for TVI. Transvalvular cardiac implantable electronic device (CEID) leads are a recognized cause of TR, resulting from leaflet impingement, restriction of leaflet excursion, disruption of coaptation geometry, and/or progressive annular distortion.3,4,9 The presence of CEID leads in patients with advanced heart failure or severe valvular pathologies introduce potential technical challenges during TTVR, including risk of prosthesis-lead interaction, lead entrapment, displacement, or postprocedural alteration in electrical parameters.9 Moreover, CEID leads can further confound the TR mechanism and influence therapeutic selection.7,9 In this patient, absence of the aforementioned etiologies supports a functional mechanism of TR.

After TTVR, the patient exhibited hemodynamic improvement, with RA pressure decreasing from 30 mm Hg (on inotrope) to 12 mm Hg (off inotrope) and cardiac output increasing from 3.2 L/min (on inotrope) to 4.4 L/min (off inotrope) (Figure 2). At 1- and 6-month follow-up, echocardiography demonstrated only trace TR and improved RV performance reflected by stable RVOT VTI (Figure 5, Video 5). These improvements translated into sustained clinical recovery, aligning with emerging data that restoration of tricuspid competence can reestablish RA-RV-LV interaction, improve ventricular interdependence, and enhance global circulatory efficiency in LVAD-supported patients.

Post–LVAD right ventricular failure is multifactorial and influenced by baseline RV contractility, RV geometry, interventricular interaction, and loading conditions. Pulmonary artery pulsatility index (PAPi) and RA pressure have been identified as predictors of RV failure in LVAD recipients.8,10 In the setting of torrential TR, effective RV forward stroke volume, as captured in the RVOT VTI, is reduced despite preserved or elevated total RV output, leading to systemic venous congestion and impaired pulmonary blood flow. In LVAD-supported physiology, this may result in LV underfilling and decreased systemic circulation. In our patient, PAPi decreased from 2.0 before LVAD implantation to 0.7 after LVAD support, and increased to 1.5 after an inotrope bolus during right heart catheterization, suggestive of RV inotropic reserve and tolerance to TV intervention. In the present case, the combination of elevated RA pressures, ventricularized RA waveform, and reduced RVOT VTI was consistent with ineffective forward flow and impaired LV preload, all of which improved immediately after TR elimination.

We recognize that TTVR in patients with advanced RV dysfunction and severely depressed PAPi may carry risk of acute RV decompensation. Careful preprocedural assessment is therefore essential. In our patient, pulmonary vascular resistance was low and PA compliance preserved, suggesting that RV dysfunction was primarily volume-mediated rather than driven by fixed pulmonary vascular disease. These physiological considerations supported intervention, and the immediate normalization of right-sided pressures and improvement in RVOT VTI after TTVR confirm the hemodynamic reversibility of the TR-mediated dysfunction.

Conclusions

Despite advances in LVAD technology and right-sided support strategies, the optimal approach to significant post–LVAD TR remains poorly defined, and the evidence guiding intervention is limited. In this context, the present case highlights the role of TTVR as a novel and effective rescue therapy for severe TR and refractory RV failure in a patient supported by a durable LVAD. This case underscores the complex interplay between LVAD physiology, RV remodeling post–LVAD, and tricuspid valve regurgitation. In patients with preexisting RV dysfunction, LV unloading can exacerbate right-sided strain, leading to progressive TR that perpetuates venous congestion and compromises device performance. Our experience highlights that in anatomically unfavorable cases—characterized by significant annular dilation, leaflet tethering, and large coaptation gaps—TTVR offers a definitive and physiologically sound solution capable of restoring valvular competence and improving systemic hemodynamics. As the field evolves, prospective studies are warranted to refine patient selection, procedural timing, and long-term outcomes of tricuspid interventions in LVAD-supported populations.
