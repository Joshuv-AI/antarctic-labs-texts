Variation and knowledge translation gaps in day-of-surgery glycaemic management by anaesthesiologists: a qualitative exploration of a quality gap in Canadian hospitals

Published: 2026

Introduction

Day-of-surgery hyperglycaemia is a modifiable risk factor for postoperative adverse events, including surgical site infections,1 readmissions2 and mortality.3 To minimise these harms, international anaesthesia and diabetes society guidelines49 recommend preoperative assessment for diabetes, glucose measurement before, during and after surgery, and to treat hyperglycaemia with insulin, with some variation. For example, the glucose threshold for treatment of inpatient hyperglycaemia differs between guidelines from 10.0 mmol/L (Diabetes Canada,7 the American Diabetes Association9 and the Australian Diabetes Society and the Australian and New Zealand College of Anaesthetists & Faculty of Pain Medicine8) to 12.0 mmol/L (Center for Perioperative Care),5 while guidelines for ambulatory surgery patients permit glucose as high as 14.0 mmol/L, depending on the procedure (Society for Ambulatory Anesthesia4 and the American Society of Anesthesiologists) provides no advice.10 While several guidelines do not specify the frequency of glucose monitoring,4 7 the Centre for Perioperative Care and Australian perioperative guidelines recommend hourly measurement4 8 and the American Diabetes Association suggests measurement every 4–6 hours in fasting patients.9 Only the Australian guidelines specifically suggest preoperative diabetes screening for all patients based on their national screening guidelines,8 while other guidelines only address preoperative diabetes assessment for patients with known diabetes.47

Despite broad consensus among clinical guidelines, and the establishment of quality indicators for day-of-surgery glycaemic management,11 important quality gaps have been documented in many settings. Chart audit studies in the USA and UK identified that fewer than 50% of patients with diabetes had a glucose measurement during surgery.12 13 Administrative data in Alberta, Canada, demonstrated that about 15% of patients with diabetes did not have a single glucose measurement on the day of surgery and more than one-third of patients with diabetes had hyperglycaemia.14

The reasons for these evidence-to-practice gaps are not well understood. Non-peer reviewed survey data suggests that protocol complexity, length of existing guidelines and short procedure lengths were barriers to anaesthesiologists’ management of glucose on the day of surgery15; however, there are no rigorous, theory-informed studies identifying barriers and facilitators to high-quality day-of-surgery glycaemic management by anaesthesiologists. We undertook a large, theory-informed qualitative study to describe barriers to high-quality, day-of-surgery glycaemic management for surgical teams.16 In this manuscript, we describe a subset of results from this larger study that demonstrate between-provider variation in day-of-surgery glycaemic management by anaesthesiologists as an important barrier to high-quality care.

Methods

Based on our previous findings describing local gaps in day-of-surgery glycaemic management,14 we undertook a theory-guided qualitative study using in-depth semi-structured interviews with 13 anaesthesiologists and 13 day-of-surgery nurses to understand current practices and explore barriers and facilitators to day-of-surgery glycaemic management. Results from this work that describe barriers faced by anaesthesiologist-nurse teams will be presented elsewhere.16 This manuscript describes analysis of data from anaesthesiologist participants describing provider-based variation in day-of-surgery glycaemic management as a barrier of high-quality glycaemic management. We used the standards of reporting qualitative research (SRQR) guideline17 in writing this manuscript.

Participants and setting

All anaesthesiologists from eight adult hospitals in Calgary and Edmonton (Alberta, Canada) were invited to participate in interviews through e-mail from their department leaders. Healthcare in Alberta is publicly administered, and at the time of this study, all hospitals were part of a single health system (Alberta Health Services) that used the same policies, electronic medical record, order sets and guidelines. Specific surgical services at six of these hospitals are mid-implementation of a quality improvement project to improve perioperative glycaemic management (NCT05036655)18 and anaesthesiologist contact with this work varies based on their clinical rotation. Participants provided informed consent and were offered $100 compensation for their time, in keeping with local hourly stipends for non-clinical work.

Data collection and analysis

Interview guides were created using the Theoretical Domains Framework19 (TDF) and the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research20 (CFIR) to understand team- and systems-level barriers to day-of-surgery glucose measurement. The TDF (V.2) uses 14 domains to understand individual-level barriers to action: knowledge, skills, social and professional role and identity, beliefs about capabilities, optimism, beliefs about consequences, reinforcement, intentions, environmental context and resources, social influences, emotion, behavioural regulation and goals, memory, attention and decision-making processes. The CFIR uses five domains to categorise 39 constructs that explore contextual implementation factors: innovation domain (eg, relative advantage, adaptability), outer setting domain (eg, local attitudes, partnerships and connections), inner setting domain (eg, structural characteristics, relational connections), implementation process domain (eg, assessing needs, tailoring strategies) and individuals domain (eg, innovation delivers, high-level leaders). The interview guides were co-developed by implementation scientists (LM, RE), frontline healthcare workers (AC, LP) and a patient partner with diabetes (online supplemental appendix 1). The interview guide was pilot-tested with an anaesthesiologist for validity and relevance. Interviews were conducted virtually or in-person, one-on-one by a trained interviewer (AO, LH) from January to March 2025, and were audio recorded and transcribed using artificial intelligence (Rev.com).

Two study team members independently coded de-identified transcripts using deductive thematic analysis21 in NVivo (V.15; AO and SMR). The codebook was based on constructs from the TDF and CFIR (online supplemental appendix 2). All codes were reconciled and any disagreements were resolved by presentation to the entire study team for resolution. Saturation was evaluated using the 10+3 rule22; recruitment was considered complete after the first 10 interviews were analysed and the next three consecutive interviews did not identify new themes or findings.

One researcher (SMR) wrote theme statements based on patterns, differences and outlying statements within each code. Theme statements were presented to all study team members for refinement, reduction and regrouping to create a final set of themes describing the study findings. Presented quotations are edited for grammar and succinctness only. Constructs from TDF and CFIR are presented italicised in brackets where relevant.

Reflexivity

AO (cisgender woman) and LH (cisgender man) are non-clinician research assistants with prior experience conducting in-depth interviews and formal training in qualitative analyses. KCL (endocrinologist), AC (gynaecologic oncology surgeon), and LP (registered nurse) are cisgender women healthcare workers with expertise in implementation science and quality improvement methods. LM and RE are cisgender women and implementation scientists. SMR is a cisgender woman general internist with graduate-level training in quality improvement and qualitative research. AO and LH were selected to conduct interviews because they are non-clinical researchers who would not have presupposed expectations or judgements about the operating room procedures and glycaemic management that may influence participant responses. AO and SMR conducted the primary coding; SMR co-leads a pan-provincial quality improvement project in perioperative glycaemic management. Due to this interest in perioperative glycaemic management, SMR had to avoid judgements about how participants prioritised knowledge and evidence around perioperative glycaemic management related to their other work tasks by focusing on the manifest content of interviews rather than applying her bias in interpreting latent content. This was achieved through discussion of theme statements with non-clinician team members (AO, LH, RE and LM) and team members who work in the operating room (AC). When reviewing theme statements, the study team was instructed to focus on manifest content and CFIR and TDF domains rather than applying judgements to identified quality gaps.

Patient and public involvement

Development of the interview guide was done in collaboration with a patient partner with diabetes.

Results

There were 13 anaesthesiologist participants with at least one participant from four of the eight participating hospitals. Most participants were from a single site (Site A, n=8, 61.5%). No participant had fellowship training in perioperative medicine (table 1). Themes that were predominantly generated from CFIR constructs described teamwork gaps in perioperative glycaemic management and will be presented elsewhere. Themes that described individual variation in glycaemic management practices by anaesthesiologists were mostly described using TDF constructs (denoted by italics) and will be presented here.

There was substantial heterogeneity among participants when comparing quotations coded as memory, attention and decision-making processes and goals. This reflected between-provider variation in multiple aspects of day-of-surgery glycaemic management including differences in whether to administer dexamethasone to patients with dysglycaemia (memory, attention and decision-making processes) and the glucose threshold for treatment (goals, online supplemental appendix 3). To visualise between-provider variation, we created subway maps to depict each participant’s decision-making at different steps in preoperative glycaemic management (figure 1) and management of hyperglycaemia (figure 2). Based on this analysis, patients with the same baseline characteristics (eg, glycated haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and random glucose value) would receive different glycaemic and/or medical management dependent on their assigned anaesthesiologist—ranging from whether their surgery was cancelled, whether they received dexamethasone for postoperative nausea and vomiting prophylaxis and at what glucose value they received treatment with insulin (if they were treated at all). Altogether, this variation is an important barrier to high-quality day-of-surgery glycaemic management.

In general, participants attributed this variation in practice to differences in patient or surgical context (environmental context), lack of evidence and guidelines to direct their management (knowledge), challenges in selecting an insulin dose (skills) and lack of observed harm from day-of-surgery hyperglycaemia (beliefs about consequences). In contrast, nearly all participants agreed that most surgeries should not be cancelled based on preoperative HbA1C due to lack of evidence that this will help patients (knowledge). Similarly, most participants preferred intravenous administration of insulin due to ease of use (memory, attention and decision-making processes) and perceived greater reliability compared with subcutaneous administration (knowledge). These findings provide direction to addressing the variation in day-of-surgery glycaemic management in our setting.

Variation in preoperative glycaemic management

Variation in participants’ processes for preoperative glycaemic management is demonstrated in figure 1. Some participants used the HbA1c while others used the day-of-surgery glucose value (or both) to assess the safety of proceeding with surgery. Further, participants differed in the glycaemic cut-offs for deciding whether to cancel a surgery; this variation was attributed to practitioner differences by one participant who shared, “Everybody’s going to have their own threshold [for] a very high A1c, but 9, 10, 11, 12[%], that’s somebody who’s really not optimally controlled preoperatively,” (P11) while another felt that these differences were context-specific: “I can think of circumstances where somebody’s random glucose is 15 [mmol/L] and we just go ahead [with surgery]. I can think of situations in which the random glucose is 8.5 [mmol/L] and we'd say no,” (P06).

Though participants agreed that cancer and emergent surgeries should proceed regardless of HbA1c or day-of-surgery glucose levels, there was some variation between participants on whether elective surgeries should be postponed or cancelled based on glycaemic status. For example, one participant shared that a “wild HbA1c doesn’t make a difference to me intraoperatively or perioperatively,” (P06) in contrast to another who described how “if you see somebody the day before [surgery who has] grossly elevated A1cs in preadmission clinic, then you're having to talk about canceling their surgery,” (P01). These differences were attributed to uncertainty or unawareness of existing evidence by participants: “We know that [really high sugars] are not good for surgery but we still don't have good evidence to suggest that elective surgery should be canceled,” (P02) and “[I don’t know] of any evidence to say that short-term [hyper]glycemia makes any difference to the anesthetic management or perioperative care,” (P06).

There was a range in approaches to administration of steroids for nausea and vomiting prophylaxis in people with diabetes. One participant made no adjustments to steroid administration based on diabetes (P02), while another participant “do[esn’t] give the Decadron [to patients with diabetes],” (P04). Most other participants considered patient factors when determining whether to administer steroids; for example, several participants reported that they would avoid steroids specifically in patients with “brittle diabetes” (P01, P03, P06), who were “very poorly controlled” (P05), or who are “an insulin requiring diabetic… or has dysglycemia prior to the start of the operation” (P06). Lastly, there were two anaesthesiologists who reported risks of hyperglycaemia could be managed so that patients could still benefit from steroids: “Just because they're diabetic doesn't mean that they don't deserve getting steroid treatment. You just have to mitigate that spike in their sugar by giving them more insulin,” (P09) and emphasising that “we can intensify their diabetes management to account for the fact that we've given them steroids… you have to personalize every single anesthetic,” (P11).

Variation in hyperglycaemia treatment

There was substantial variation in the target glucose values across participants (figure 2). Participants reported that they would treat a patient with insulin if their glucose was higher than 8 mmol/L (P07), 10 mmol/L (P02, P04, P05, P09, P10), 11 mmol/L (P01, P08, P12, P13) or 12 mmol/L (P03, P11), though one participant stated that they would not treat at any glucose level “provided that everything else is working well,” (P06). Participants attributed this variation to a perceived lack of immediate consequences from hyperglycaemia though some were unaware of guidelines or evidence to support a firm glucose range. All participants felt that glucose values were a low priority compared with other aspects of anaesthetic management: “What’s more important than blood sugar? Ensuring you have adequate IV access, securing the airway, hypotension, severe hypertension, intraoperative emergencies, acidosis. I would say all of those are more important than blood sugar,” (P12).

Most participants preferred intravenous over subcutaneous administration of insulin (figure 2). The reasons for these preferences varied. Many participants felt that intravenous administration was more predictable due to potential unknown “perfusion to the site of my injection [of subcutaneous insulin] during a surgery and how much of that insulin is actually going to get absorbed and how much of it’s going to be effective,” (P11). There were also perceived logistic benefits to intravenous administration due to order sets that “automate for the nurses how frequently they’re getting blood glucose measured and then basically how to titrate their insulin drip… based on what the glucose reading is,” (P08). These explanations were not universal; while one participant felt that “every other drug we give in anesthesia is given intravenously… subcutaneous insulin, it’s a little bit of a hassle… you’ve got to roll your eyes. It’s outside the normal workflow,” (P06), another contradicted this view: “We give subcutaneous medications all the time. Like heparin is a common one that we give,” (P10) and disagreed that subcutaneous insulins had unfavourable pharmacokinetics, adding that “rapid-acting [subcutaneous] insulin will work in a good timeframe,” (P10). The automated intravenous insulin order set was seen as a disadvantage by another participant, who “[doesn’t] like the idea of having the [insulin] infusion and then have to stop now that [the patient] is going to the ward [without] something subcutaneous on board,” (P03).

Variation in insulin dosing

Many participants were uncertain on how to select an initial insulin dose to treat hyperglycaemia. In general, most participants selected an initial insulin dose for patients with hyperglycaemia based on previous experience or based on the severity of hyperglycaemia rather than by using patient-related factors. For example, participants shared that “I’ll usually give a unit if it’s kind of in the 11 to 15 [mmol/L] range. If it’s above 15 [mmol/L], probably two units and then check it again in an hour,” (P13) or “I would just give two to six units depending on how hyperglycemic they were,” (P12). However, other participants shared they were “pick[ing] numbers out of the air,” (P11) and that the initial insulin dose was “a bit voodoo-ish” (P03). Selection of an initial insulin dose for patients was “intimidat[ing]” (P02) and two participants stated, “I am not an endocrinologist,” (P08, P11) when describing their lack of comfort in selecting the initial dose.

Discussion

This theory-informed analysis of interview data from anaesthesiologists working at multiple hospitals in a single healthcare system demonstrated important variation in perioperative glycaemic management practices. Variation in care between anaesthesiologists was most notable for preoperative glycaemic management, in particular for decisions about steroid administration for people with diabetes. Anaesthesiologists also differed in the glucose value that they would initiate treatment for hyperglycaemia and to a lesser extent, whether they would cancel an elective surgery based on preoperative glycaemic control or day of surgery glucose values. Participant responses suggested this variation may be predominately from a lack of knowledge about guidelines and/or evidence to guide perioperative glycaemic management and due to differing patient or surgical factors. Altogether, this variation in practice likely contributes to quality gaps in perioperative glycaemic management in our setting. Initiatives to address observed quality gaps should address knowledge, standardise decisions to cancel or postpone surgery, and build capacity through training and skills for treating hyperglycaemia.

Though variation in clinical practice is often used as a signal of a quality gap,23 24 some authors have argued that between-provider variation in anaesthesia care should be expected and allowed.25 Variation in anaesthetic management may reflect differences in surgical or patient context,25 even when a best practice (eg, regional or neuraxial anaesthesia for total knee arthroplasty) is identified.26 This was supported by our findings; several of our participants described contextual factors that influence their decision-making related to perioperative glycaemic management such as timing of surgery or baseline diabetes control. However, a few studies have found that anaesthesiologist preferences explain more of the observed variation in adoption of best practices for anaesthetic management than patient, hospital, and procedure characteristics.27 28 In our work, differing knowledge of guidelines or evidence between providers contributed at least in part to observed variation. Notably, our analysis suggests that context is not the primary driver of variation, as patients with the same presenting characteristics (eg, baseline HbA1c, random glucose measurement, elective surgery) who are treated by different anaesthesiologists would have important differences in their perioperative care.

Our results suggest that the observed variation between anaesthesiologists in our setting is due to evidence-to-practice gaps due to a lack of knowledge about day-of-surgery glycaemic management. Similar to our result, an international cross-sectional survey study identified that anaesthesiologists from different continents reported a range of glucose thresholds for postponement of elective cranial surgeries and had varied approaches to intraoperative glucose measurement.29 Gruenbaum et al also found that 74% of providers were unaware of evidence or guidelines about perioperative glucose management,29 which was a similar explanation given by many of our participants. The greatest variation between providers was in practices for dexamethasone administration for patients with diabetes and/or hyperglycaemia, which has been an area of clinical equipoise30 for many years. However, there are now multiple, contemporary meta-analyses of randomised, placebo-controlled studies examining the safety of a single dose of dexamethasone for prophylaxis for postoperative nausea and vomiting which report that the clinical effect of dexamethasone on postoperative glucose values was low and had no association with surgical complications.3133 This evidence supports the Society for Ambulatory Anesthesia’s 2024 recommendation to administer low-dose dexamethasone to patients with diabetes.4 A lack of best practices also does not explain the variation in glucose threshold for treatment reported in our study. A Delphi study which included surgeons, endocrinologists and anaesthesiologists in establishing quality indicators for perioperative glycaemic management suggested targeting an upper limit of 10 mmol/L during and after surgery.11 Canadian7 and other national diabetes6 34 and anaesthesiology4 guidelines have established a cut-off of 10 mmol/L for treatment of hyperglycaemia, which has been incorporated into our healthcare system’s in-hospital glycaemic management policy. Despite these longstanding recommendations, more than half of anaesthesiologists in our study provided different glucose thresholds for treatment. Further, the majority of anaesthesiologists in this study preferred intravenous over subcutaneous administration of insulin due to concerns about absorption and greater familiarity; however, subcutaneous administration is recommended by some4 (though not all6) guidelines as first-line treatment of perioperative hyperglycaemia due to its safety and longer duration of action. Similarly, recent anaesthesia guidelines recommend against postponing procedures based on HbA1c levels,4 a practice that was described by some anaesthesiologists in our sample.

Altogether, our study emphasises a need for knowledge mobilisation of perioperative glycaemic best practices to anaesthesiologists to bridge the gap between what should be done based on evidence and what is occurring in practice. Due to the wide variation in practice observed in our setting and lack of comfort with individualised dosing of insulin, care pathways that standardise or support decision-making may be helpful.23 Based on the barriers identified in our study, quality improvement teams could consider the need for a policy to standardise surgical cancellations, address lack of awareness of existing guidelines, create clinical decision support for selecting an initial insulin dose, and automated order sets that use subcutaneous insulin. Perioperative glycaemic management pathways, which are also recommended by the Society for Ambulatory Anesthesia4 and the Centre for Perioperative Care,5 may reduce variation in care by emphasising best practices.

There are several limitations to this work. Though our study included providers from multiple sites, the generalisability of these findings to other health systems is not known, in particular because of ongoing quality improvement work in perioperative glycaemic management at most hospitals included in this study. Further, our interview guide was designed to identify barriers to high-quality day-of-surgery glycaemic management rather than to probe variation; it is possible that we would identify additional or different insights using a study designed to directly probe variation. A clinical vignette study may be more appropriate to understand how anaesthesiologists make decisions about glycaemic management and describe potential variation. Lastly, study participants are likely to be more interested in perioperative glycaemic management than non-participants; it is possible that our result overestimates variation by including participants who provided outlier perspectives.

Despite these limitations, these findings likely contribute to the quality gap in day-of-surgery glycaemic management observed in our setting and provide important guidance for the design of future interventions to address this gap. The use of implementation science frameworks to comprehensively understand anaesthesiologist perspectives is a robust methodology that provides important foundational insights in the translation of knowledge related to perioperative glycaemic management.
