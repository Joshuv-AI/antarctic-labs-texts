Nilotinib induces immunogenic cuproptosis to potentiate cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Lei Yao, Deze Zhao, Yu Meng, Yihuang Liu, Hui Su, Danyao Chen, Ziyu Guo, Daishi Li, Linfeng Li, Qian Zhou, Shengchao Xu, Furong Zeng, Xiang Chen, Ledu Zhou, Guangtong Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryImmunotherapy efficacy is limited by poor tumor immunogenicity and insufficient immune infiltration, highlighting the critical role of inducing immunogenic cell death for improved outcomes. Targeting cuproptosis holds promising therapeutic potential, but its immunogenic and capacity to stimulate anti-tumor immunity to potentiate immunotherapy remains unknown. Through screening of 240 Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved anti-tumor drugs, we identify nilotinib as a potent cuproptosis inducer in cancer cells, especially at physiologically relevant copper concentrations. Mechanistically, nilotinib-copper elicits cuproptosis in vitro and in vivo by repressing the copper exporter ATP7A via ETV1 to drive mitochondrial copper accumulation and activating mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway through ERK2 phosphorylation. Preclinically, nilotinib-copper induces immunogenic cuproptosis and enhances anti-PD-1 efficacy across multiple melanoma models, including B16F10 tumor-bearing mice, Braf/Pten-driven spontaneous melanoma mice, and PBMC-humanized mice. Clinically, the ATP7ALow/MAPKHigh signature correlates with improved immunotherapy response and prolonged overall survival. This work establishes nilotinib as a clinically tractable, non-ionophore immunogenic cuproptosis inducer capable of activating anti-tumor immunity to potentiate immunotherapy.

Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), has revolutionized oncology by unleashing cytotoxic T cell responses against malignancies.1 Despite this, the efficacy of ICIs remains limited in many patients, largely due to poor tumor immunogenicity and insufficient immune cell infiltration, highlighting the importance of inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD).2,3,4 ICD is a functionally unique form of regulated cell death that releases danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as ATP, HMGB1, and calreticulin (CRT), activating dendritic cells (DCs) and priming a tumor-specific T cell response.5 While conventional ICD inducers (e.g., certain chemotherapeutics and radiotherapy) can potentiate ICIs, their clinical utility is constrained by systemic toxicity, immunosuppressive backlash, and heterogeneous efficacy.5,6 Thus, novel ICD modalities capable of converting immunologically “cold” tumors to “hot” represent an urgent unmet need.

Cuproptosis, a recently discovered copper-dependent cell death pathway distinct from apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, offers promising therapeutic potential.7,8 This process is initiated by copper binding to lipoylated components of mitochondrial respiration, triggering toxic protein aggregation, loss of iron-sulfur clusters, and fatal metabolic collapse.7,9 Critically, tumors exhibit elevated copper levels and dysregulated copper transporters (e.g., CTR1 and ATP7A), suggesting inherent cuproptosis susceptibility.9,10 However, whether cuproptosis is intrinsically immunogenic and capable of stimulating adaptive anti-tumor immunity remains unknown. Current strategies employing copper ionophores (elesclomol/disulfiram + CuCl2) face translational barriers: dose-limiting systemic copper toxicity and uncharacterized immunogenicity.8 Therefore, identifying clinically tractable, non-ionophore agents that selectively trigger immunogenic cuproptosis is therefore imperative.

Nilotinib is an Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved BCR-ABL/c-KIT inhibitor for chronic myeloid leukemia.11 Beyond its canonical targets, emerging evidence suggests nilotinib possesses “off-target” effects to modulate mitochondrial function,12,13,14 processes intrinsically linked to cuproptosis. However, a direct link between nilotinib, the induction of bona fide cuproptosis, and crucially, the potential immunogenicity of such cell death has never been investigated. In this study, we conducted a drug screening of 240 FDA-approved anti-tumor drugs and identified nilotinib as a potent cuproptosis inducer in cancer cells, especially under physiologically relevant copper concentrations. Mechanistically, nilotinib-copper promotes mitochondrial copper accumulation via repressing the copper exporter ATP7A in an ETV1-dependent manner and activates mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway to potentiate cuproptosis through the phosphorylation of ERK2. We further found that nilotinib-copper elicits immunogenic cuproptosis and activates anti-tumor immunity through remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment. Preclinically, nilotinib-copper enhances anti-programmed cell death 1 (anti-PD-1) immunotherapy across multiple melanoma models, including B16F10-tumor-bearing mice, Braf/Pten-driven spontaneous melanoma mice, and peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC)-humanized mice. Clinically, patients with ATP7ALow/MAPKHigh signature showed improved immunotherapy efficacy and extended overall survival. Our work firstly positions nilotinib, a non-ionophore agent, as an inducer of immunogenic cuproptosis that can enhance cancer immunotherapy.

Results

Identification and verification of nilotinib as a clinically tractable cuproptosis inducer

Copper ionophores, the only known class of potent cuproptosis inducers, face translational barriers.8,15 To identify clinically tractable, non-ionophore cuproptosis inducers, we screened 240 FDA-approved anti-tumor drugs with or without the cuproptosis inhibitor tetrathiomolybdate (TTM; 10 μM) (Figure 1A). Viability assays revealed that TTM markedly attenuated cell death induced by nilotinib, nominating it as a top candidate (Figure 1B), an effect validated over time in melanoma cells (Figure S1A). Given the critical role of copper in cuproptosis and the sub-physiological copper levels in standard culture medium (∼1 μM) compared to serum and tissue (10–25 μM),16,17 we supplemented the medium with incremental copper. As expected, the ionophore elesclomol showed copper-dependent cytotoxicity fully reversed by TTM (Figures 1C and 1D). Intriguingly, TTM only partially rescued nilotinib-induced death at low supplemental copper (≤1 μM) but provided complete rescue at ≥ 2.5 μM (Figures 1C and 1D), a finding corroborated by PI/calcein-AM staining and flow cytometry (Figures 1E, 1F, and S1B). Consistently, the near-complete rescue by TTM of nilotinib-induced cell death was consistent across all nilotinib concentrations and treatment durations with 2.5 μM copper (Figures S1C and S1D). Co-treatment with cell death inhibitors further demonstrated that without supplemental copper, nilotinib-induced cell death was partially rescued by cuproptosis inhibitors (TTM, bathocuproinedisulfonic acid [BCS]) and the apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK (Figures 1G and S1E). To definitively establish cuproptosis as the mechanism underlying nilotinib-copper-induced death, we constructed CRISPR-Cas9 knockout cells targeting key effectors of ferroptosis (ACSL4), apoptosis (BAX/BAK1), autophagy (ATG5/7), pyroptosis (GSDMD/E), and necroptosis (MLKL) (Figure S1F). Cell death induced by nilotinib-copper persisted in all knockout cell lines (Figures 1I and S1G), excluding essential roles for these alternative pathways. We further found that nilotinib-copper specifically triggered oligomerization of lipoylated dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT) and degradation of Fe-S cluster proteins—lipoic acid synthetase (LIAS), ferredoxin 1 (FDX1), procollagen-lysine,2-oxoglutarate 5-dioxygenase 1 (PLOD1), and succinate dehydrogenase complex iron sulfur subunit B (SDHB)—thus defining molecular signatures of cuproptosis (Figures 1J and S1H). Immunofluorescence confirmed prominent DLAT foci formation upon nilotinib-copper treatment, which was markedly suppressed by TTM co-treatment (Figures 1K, 1L, and S1I). Critically, this nilotinib-copper-induced cuproptosis phenotype was consistently observed across diverse tumor cell line but not in normal cells (Figure 1M). Taken together, these findings identify and validate nilotinib, particularly under physiologically relevant copper concentrations, as a clinically tractable cuproptosis inducer in vitro.

Nilotinib-copper drives mitochondrial copper accumulation and cuproptosis via ATP7A suppression

Although nilotinib is a clinically approved BCR-ABL inhibitor, ABL silencing did not abrogate nilotinib-copper-induced cytotoxicity (Figures S2A and S2B). Furthermore, among several BCR-ABL inhibitors tested, only nilotinib and its hydrochloride salt elicited cytotoxicity that was rescued by TTM, indicating a mechanism distinct from BCR-ABL targeting (Figure S2C). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) profiling revealed no transcriptional alterations in key cuproptosis-sensitivity regulatory genes following nilotinib-copper treatment (Figure S2D).7 Crucially, imaging with a Cu(I)-selective probe demonstrated that nilotinib alone promoted cytoplasmic and mitochondrial copper accumulation (Figures 2A, S2E, and S2F). To elucidate the mechanism of nilotinib-induced copper accumulation, we analyzed copper importers SLC30A1 and SLC31A1 and the copper exporters ATP7A and ATP7B. Nilotinib selectively downregulated the copper exporter ATP7A at both mRNA and protein levels, irrespective of supplemental copper, suggesting transcriptional regulation (Figures 2B, 2C, and S2G). ATP7A inhibition induces cuproptosis both in vitro and in vivo with supplemental copper,18,19 supporting our model and prompting us to elucidate its transcriptional regulation. We performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) in A375 melanoma cells treated with nilotinib-copper in the presence or absence of TTM. Integrative transcriptional regulator analysis by cross-referencing hTFtarget-2, human TFDB, and GTRD datasets with our bulk RNA-seq result identified 35 potential transcription factors (Figure 2D), nine of which were downregulated more than 1.5-fold upon nilotinib-copper treatment (Figure 2E). We next evaluated their correlation with ATP7A expression across 33 TCGA cancer types (Figure 2F), narrowing the candidates to four transcription factors. Published chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) data revealed that ETV1 specifically binds to the ATP7A promoter region (Figure 2G), and ChIp-qPCR confirmed this binding and its impairment by nilotinib (Figures 2H and 2I). ETV1 silencing also transcriptionally repressed ATP7A, phenocopying nilotinib treatment (Figures 2J and 2K). The regulatory role of ETV1 on ATP7A expression was further validated using RNA-seq data from our Xiangya melanoma cohort (Figure S2H). When cell lines or tumor samples were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the 40th percentile or more stringent cutoffs, the high-ETV1-expression group consistently showed stronger activation of copper homeostasis pathways (Figure S2I), providing strong evidence that ETV1 acts as a key transcriptional factor of ATP7A.

We next conducted a preliminary investigation into the mechanism by which nilotinib suppresses ETV1 transcription. Co-treatment of A375 cells with nilotinib along with inhibitors of endoplasmic reticulum stress (4-PBA and salubrinal), oxidative stress (NAC), or energy stress (2-DG) failed to restore ETV1 expression, ruling out a non-specific stress response (Figure S2J). Given that nilotinib is a tyrosine kinase inhibitor and that ETV1 is subject to kinase-dependent regulation,20 we treated A375 melanoma cells with a panel of kinase inhibitors. Notably, the selective DYRK1B inhibitor AZ191 significantly reduced ETV1 protein levels (Figure S2K), as well as ETV1 and ATP7A mRNA levels (Figure S2L), indicating that nilotinib may repress ETV1 transcription via a DYRK1B-dependent pathway.

To assess the anti-tumor efficacy of nilotinib-copper-induced cuproptosis in vivo, we established subcutaneous B16-F10 allografts in NCG mice (Figure 2L). Nilotinib-copper treatment significantly reduced tumor volume and tumor weight (Figure 2M and 2N). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that nilotinib-copper specifically decreased expression of lipoylated proteins and ATP7A, without affecting caspase-3 cleavage in tumors (Figures 2O and S2M), consistent with in vitro observations and supporting nilotinib-copper-induced cuproptosis in vivo. Given that physiological copper levels might suffice to support nilotinib-induced cuproptosis, we treated nude mice bearing A375 xenografts with vehicle, nilotinib alone, TTM alone, or nilotinib plus TTM (Figure S2N). Nilotinib monotherapy also significantly inhibited tumor growth and decreased lipoylated proteins, which were reversed by TTM (Figures 2P–2R, S2O, and S2P), indicating that nilotinib alone induces melanoma cuproptosis at least in part in vivo. All together, these findings establish that nilotinib-copper induces cuproptosis by transcriptionally suppressing ATP7A via inhibition of ETV1, thereby disrupting copper homeostasis and triggering mitochondrial copper accumulation.

ERK2 activation is essential for nilotinib-copper-induced cuproptosis

During cuproptosis, excess copper ions bind lipoylated proteins, triggering their aberrant aggregation and the depletion of iron-sulfur cluster proteins, while also activating signaling pathways that regulate cellular susceptibility to this form of death. Specifically, copper facilitates the PDK1-AKT interaction, which activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway to suppress cuproptosis.10,21 Copper also directly binds HIF-1α, inhibiting its ubiquitination and enhancing its stability, thereby further inhibiting cuproptosis.22 To explore the downstream signaling pathways regulated by nilotinib-copper, we analyzed the gene expression alterations in nilotinib-copper-treated cells with or without TTM. Among the 2,088 genes that were commonly altered by these two treatments, 94.7% and 93.2% of the genes were rescued by TTM co-treatment, respectively (Figure 3A), indicating a specific transcriptomic reprogramming. KEGG enrichment analysis based on 1,144 specific up-regulated genes by nilotinib-copper revealed the MAPK signaling pathway as one of the top affected pathways (Figure 3B). Given the three major MAPK cascades in eukaryotes (ERK, JNK/SAPK, and p38 MAPK),23,24 we further performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), finding that ERK1/2 pathway, but not the JNK or p38 MAPK pathways, was significantly activated by nilotinib-copper and rescued by TTM co-treatment (Figures 3C and S3A). Given the unchanged MEK phosphorylation, we tested whether nilotinib-copper activates ERK independently of MEK. The MEK inhibitor trametinib and CRISPR-mediated MEK1/2 deletion failed to reverse nilotinib-copper-induced ERK activation and cuproptosis (Figures S3B–S3E), whereas ERK1/2 deletion, particularly ERK2, significantly attenuated cytotoxicity (Figures 3E and 3F), establishing an MEK-independent ERK activation. Oxidative phosphorylation is usually significantly inhibited in cuproptotic cell due to the oligomerization of lipoylated proteins.7,21 As expected, nilotinib-copper treatment markedly suppressed both basal and maximal oxygen consumption rates, while ERK2 knockout attenuated the suppression induced by nilotinib-copper (Figures 3G–3J), highlighting a critical and specific role for ERK2 in nilotinib-copper-induced cuproptosis. Simultaneously, we performed a targeted kinase inhibitor screen to identify potential inhibitors to nilotinib-copper-induced cuproptosis (Figure 3K). Interestingly, ulixertinib, a potent and selective ERK1/2 inhibitor,25,26 robustly suppressed nilotinib-copper-induced cytotoxicity (Figures 3L, 3M, and S3F), which was further validated by flow cytometry and PI staining (Figures 3N and 3O). Consistently, ulixertinib also suppressed the formation of DLAT foci, a hallmark of cuproptosis (Figures 3P, 3Q, and S3G), and rescued lethality across multiple tumor cell lines treated with nilotinib-copper (Figure 3R). Notably, ulixertinib showed no protective effect in cuproptosis models induced by elesclomol-copper, or mitochondrial copper accumulation (Figures S3H–S3J), underscoring the distinct mechanism of nilotinib as a non-ionophore cuproptosis inducer. Collectively, these results demonstrate that nilotinib-copper-induced ERK2 activation is essential for sustaining heightened mitochondrial respiration and executing cuproptosis.

Nilotinib-copper elicits tumor cell cuproptosis and activates anti-tumor immunity in immunocompetent hosts

We further validate the above findings in other major cancer types, including hepatocellular carcinoma and non-small cell lung cancer (Figures S4A–S4C). However, while these results confirm direct tumoricidal effects via cuproptosis, they do not address contributions from the host immune system. To assess whether adaptive immunity contributes to the therapeutic efficacy of nilotinib-copper, we implanted B16-F10 melanoma cells subcutaneously in immune-competent C57BL/6 mice with or without nilotinib-copper treatment (Figure 4A). As expected, nilotinib-copper treatment significantly reduced tumor volume and weight without altering body weight (Figures 4B, 4C, and S4D). Consistent with cuproptosis induction, nilotinib-copper-treated tumors exhibited diminished lipoylated protein expression and reduced ATP7A levels (Figure 4D). To dissect tumor microenvironmental changes, we performed single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) on nilotinib-copper-treated versus control B16-F10 tumors and analyzed the data using the standard Seurat pipeline. After quality control, we obtained a total of 26,828 cells, which were classified into four major cell types based on characteristic marker genes: melanoma cells, lymphoid cells, myeloid cells, and stromal cells (Figure S4E). To quantify the level of cuproptosis in individual cells, we applied a gene-signature-based scoring approach (see STAR Methods)21,27 and found that cuproptosis predominantly occurred in tumor cells (Figure 4E) and was positively correlated with the activation of the MAPK signaling pathway as well as several canonical immunogenic cell death pathways (Figures 4F and S4F).28,29,30 Next, we further explored the remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. Analysis of cell proportions and Ro/e analysis revealed an increased enrichment of T/natural killer (NK) cells in the nilotinib-copper-treated group (Figures 4G, S4G, and S4H). Detailed annotation and further analysis of T/NK cells showed that both NK cells and effector CD8+ T cells were more enriched in the nilotinib-copper-treated group (Figures 4H and S4I), accompanied by elevated expression of cytotoxic markers such as granzyme B (Gzmb) and Irf8 (Figures S4J and S4K). Moreover, subcluster analysis also revealed increased proportions of M1-like macrophages and N1-like neutrophils (Figures S4L and S4M). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that nilotinib-copper treatment significantly increased the intratumoral infiltration of CD8+ T cells, TNFα+ effector CD8+ T cells, PD-1+ CD8+ T cells, and DCs, with minimal effects on other immune cells, including NK cells, CD4+ T cells, Tregs, macrophages, and neutrophils (Figures 4I and S4N). In addition, analysis of major immune cell populations—including T cells, DCs, NK cells, and CD11b+ F4/80+ macrophages—in the spleen demonstrated that nilotinib-copper selectively promotes the accumulation of CD8+ T cell populations in the spleen (Figure S4O). These results suggested that nilotinib-copper can activate both innate and adaptive immune responses.

Spontaneous tumor models preserve native tumor biology and yield superior pathophysiological and clinical relevance compared to subcutaneous implantation. To further evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of nilotinib-copper treatment, we employed the Tyr::CreER; BRafCA/wt, Ptenlox/lox mice genetically engineered mouse model, wherein 4-hydroxytamoxifen-induced melanocyte-specific BrafV600E expression and Pten deletion drive rapid melanoma development (Figure 4J).31 Consistently, nilotinib-copper significantly delayed tumor growth and extended overall survival of tumor-bearing mice (Figures 4K–4M). Multiplex immunohistochemical staining confirmed reduced protein lipoylation and decreased ATP7A expression in treated tumors, accompanied by marked infiltration of GZMB+ CD8+ T cells (Figures 4N and 4O). These data demonstrate that nilotinib-copper induces cuproptosis in tumor cells while concurrently activating anti-tumor immunity, with the intact host immune system substantially amplifying its therapeutic effect.

To biochemically validate ICD induction, we assessed canonical DAMPs. Nilotinib-copper-treated cells exhibited a significant increase in extracellular ATP release and marked elevation in HMGB1 secretion (Figures 5F and 5G). Surface exposure of CRT, an essential “eat-me” signal for DC activation, was significantly increased upon nilotinib-copper treatment, as quantified by flow cytometry (Figure 5H). Immunofluorescence imaging and quantitative colocalization analysis further confirmed CRT translocation to the plasma membrane (Figure 5I). Furthermore, incubation of bone-marrow-derived DCs (BMDCs) with conditional medium collected from nilotinib-copper-treated tumor cells resulted in upregulated expression of DC maturation markers (CD80 and CD86) and the antigen-presentation marker major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II, as measured by flow cytometry (Figures S5A–S5C). Collectively, these findings establish that nilotinib-copper, but not nilotinib alone, induces bona fide immunogenic cuproptosis capable of generating durable anti-tumor immunity.

To extend these mechanistic insights into clinical relevance, we analyzed publicly available scRNA-seq datasets from patients receiving ICB therapy (see STAR Methods). To minimize potential bias caused by therapy-induced selection, we specifically focused on treatment-naive tumors and lymph node metastases (Figures S7B–S7D). Based on these datasets, we modeled nilotinib-copper activity by integrating ATP7A expression with MAPK signaling patterns and subsequently evaluated their association with ICB therapeutic response. Density distributions and statistical comparisons revealed that melanoma cells of ICB responders exhibited lower ATP7A expression but higher MAPK activity, whereas non-responders displayed the inverse pattern (Figures S7E and S7F). Stacked bar plot analysis of the current dataset revealed that, compared with non-responders, responders exhibited a higher proportion of melanoma cells with low ATP7A and high MAPK expression (Figure 7G). Consistently, ROC curve analysis indicated that a classifier based on ATP7A and MAPK expression features accurately reflected patient response status and showed superior discriminative performance in tumor tissues than in lymph node metastases (Figure 7G). Further immune cell profiling revealed that ATP7A expression levels of patients were negatively correlated with the infiltration of effector and pre-exhausted CD8+ T cells, whereas MAPK activation exhibited a positive association (Figure S7G). Consistently, GSEA analysis aligned with the bulk RNA-seq results, showing that the ATP7ALow/MAPKHigh subgroup was significantly enriched for immune activation pathways, including MAPK signaling and cuproptosis-related signatures (Figure S7H). Moreover, across multiple treatment-naive melanoma cohorts, the ATP7ALow/MAPKHigh signature served as a risk factor that effectively predicted poor survival outcomes (Figure 7H). Collectively, these results identify ATP7A and MAPK as key determinants of ICB responsiveness and prognosis and further support the translational potential of targeting copper homeostasis with nilotinib-copper to enhance immunotherapy efficacy.

Discussion

In this study, through a screen of 240 FDA-approved anti-tumor agents, we identified nilotinib as a potent inducer of cuproptosis both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, nilotinib-copper promotes mitochondrial copper accumulation by suppressing the ETV1-dependent transcriptional regulation of ATP7A and activates the MAPK pathway through ERK2 phosphorylation (Figure 7I). Furthermore, we demonstrated that nilotinib-copper triggers ICD and enhances anti-tumor immunity across multiple preclinical melanoma models. Our work establishes nilotinib—a non-ionophore drug—as a novel inducer of immunogenic cuproptosis with the potential to augment cancer immunotherapy.

Nilotinib, an oral BCR-ABL inhibitor approved for chronic myeloid leukemia,11 primarily induces apoptosis through inhibition of BCR-ABL kinase activity.34,35,36,37,38 Consistent with prior reports,12 nilotinib-triggered cell death was partially rescued by the pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD, confirming the involvement of apoptosis. However, upon copper supplementation (≥2.5 μM), this protective effect was abrogated, and nilotinib instead induced cuproptosis, as evidenced by lipoylated protein oligomerization and Fe-S cluster protein depletion, both of which were fully reversed by the copper chelator TTM,7 indicating a copper-dependent shift from apoptosis to cuproptosis. Given that physiological and tumor-associated copper levels often range between 10 and 25 μM,16,17 our findings suggest that cuproptosis may represent the dominant anti-tumor mechanism of nilotinib in copper-replete contexts—a possibility previously overlooked prior to the formal discovery of cuproptosis.

Disruption of intracellular copper homeostasis is a prerequisite for cuproptosis induction, typically achieved using copper ionophores such as elesclomol or disulfiram. Unlike these traditional copper ionophores, nilotinib-copper induces cuproptosis without acting as an ionophore. Previous studies have highlighted the importance of copper transport regulation in this context. For instance, Wnt/β-catenin-driven ATP7B upregulation confers resistance via enhanced copper export,10 while radiotherapy-induced CTR1 expression promotes mitochondrial copper accumulation and cuproptosis.9 Herein, we reveal that nilotinib transcriptionally represses ATP7A through inhibition of ETV1, thereby impairing copper efflux and leading to toxic intracellular copper accumulation. This identifies a non-ionophoric mechanism for inducing cuproptosis. We further identify MAPK/ERK2 signaling as a key mediator of nilotinib-copper-induced cuproptosis. While nilotinib has been reported to suppress MAPK signaling through ABL1/2 inhibition or interference with JNK/p38 pathways in certain contexts,38,39 we observed selective activation of ERK2 following nilotinib-copper treatment. Thus, ERK2 appears to function as both a metabolic regulator and a copper-responsive kinase that sensitizes cells to cuproptosis.

Nilotinib is primarily used in CML and has been reported to synergize with PD-L1 blockade in BCR-ABL1+ B cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia through reversing CD4+ T cell dysfunction.40 Moreover, copper supplementation has been linked to PD-L1 upregulation and immune evasion through inducing phosphorylation of STAT3 and EGFR to inhibit ubiquitin-mediated degradation of PD-L1.41 These studies provide evidence that the combination of nilotinib-copper and immunotherapy may produce a synergistic effect. Here, we found that nilotinib-copper treatment induces classic hallmarks of immunogenic cell death including ATP and HMGB1 release, CRT exposure, and BMDC maturation, thereby augmenting responses to immunotherapy. These results provide a novel mechanistic rationale for targeting nilotinib and copper to overcome immune resistance in cancer.

Limitations of the study

Despite these advances, several limitations merit consideration. First, the optimal dosing regimen including timing, sequence, and ratio of nilotinib to copper remains to be determined through dedicated pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic studies. Second, the mechanisms by which tumors might develop acquired resistance to this combination therapy, for example through the upregulation of ATP7A or reactivation of the MAPK pathway, were not explored here and warrant future investigation. Third, although we utilized multiple in vivo models to validate the immunogenic and therapeutic effects of nilotinib-copper, each model has inherent constraints, such as murine tumor origin, incomplete human immune reconstitution, or lack of functional immunity. Future studies employing patient-derived organoids and ex vivo tumor-immune coculture systems may better recapitulate human copper metabolism and immunogenic cell death dynamics. Fourth, although nilotinib-copper was well tolerated in our preclinical models, clinical studies report adverse effects such as thrombocytopenia (21%) and neutropenia (14%), primarily grade 3 or 4.42 Careful risk stratification and monitoring will therefore be essential for the safe and effective application of nilotinib in solid tumors.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

All detailed experimental procedures have been approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, Xiangya Hospital, Central South University, China. (No. 202606098). Shanghai Model Organisms Center (Shanghai, China) supplied 4–6-week-old female BALB/c nude mice and female NSG mice for this study. Female C57BL/6 mice of the same age range were purchased from Hunan SLAC Laboratory Animal Center. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. All in vivo experiments were performed using female mice only. This experimental design may preclude assessment of potential sex-specific responses to nilotinib-copper treatment.

Subcutaneous tumor mouse models

For the A375 cell-derived xenograft (CDX) model in BALB/c-nu mice and the B16-F10 cell-derived syngeneic model in NCG or C57BL/6 J mice, experimental procedures were performed as previously described in our earlier study.43 Once tumor volumes reached approximately 50–100 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups. Tumor growth was monitored every three days by measuring the long (L) and short (S) diameters using calipers. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: volume = (L × S2)/2. A maximum tumor diameter exceeding 20 mm was defined as the survival endpoint.

Genetically engineered mouse models

For the genetically engineered mouse model (GEMM) of melanoma, Tyr::CreER+/+; Braf wt/wt; Ptenlox/lox mice and Tyr::CreER−/−; BrafCA/CA; Ptenlox/lox mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. Breeding and genotyping were performed to generate Tyr::CreER+/−; Braf CA/wt; Ptenlox/lox mice. At 3–4 weeks of age, female mice were shaved on the dorsal skin and treated topically with 5 mM 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT, 2 μL in ethanol, Sigma-Aldrich, H6278) at the same site for three consecutive days. Spontaneous tumor formation was typically observed ∼15 days after induction, at which point mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups. Tumor dimensions, including length (L), width (S), and height (H), were measured every three days. Tumor volume was calculated using the ellipsoid formula: volume = (π/6) × L × S × H. Mice were considered to have reached the survival endpoint when the average of the tumor’s length and width exceeded 12 mm.

Humanized mouse models

For the PBMC-humanized xenograft model, A375 cells in logarithmic growth phase were digested, resuspended, and subcutaneously inoculated into the flank at a dose of 2 × 106 cells per NSG mouse. Once tumors reached ∼100 mm3 (approximately one week), mice received a tail vein injection of 1 × 107 human PBMCs. Three days after PBMC engraftment, mice were randomly assigned to experimental treatment groups. Tumor growth was monitored every three days by measuring the long (L) and short (S) diameters using calipers. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: volume = (L × S2)/2. A maximum tumor diameter exceeding 20 mm was defined as the survival endpoint.

Drug administration strategy and routes: Oral nilotinib reaches peak plasma levels within ∼0.5 h and is sustained >24 h,44 whereas intraperitoneal copper peaks within 0.5–2 h45 To coincide their peak exposures, nilotinib was administered immediately before copper injection. This dosage regimen was designed to maximize the simultaneous exposure required to induce cuproptosis in vivo. For the evaluation of tumor suppression by nilotinib-induced cuproptosis, the control group received DMSO, while the treatment group received nilotinib (10 mg/kg, i.g., daily) and CuCl2 (0.25 mg/kg, i.p., daily). In the combination therapy study involving nilotinib-Cu and immune checkpoint blockade (ICI), the control group was administered isotype IgG2a (200 μg/mouse, i.p., every 3 days). The anti-PD-1 monotherapy group received PD-1 monoclonal antibody (200 μg/mouse, i.p., every 3 days). The nilotinib-Cu group was treated with nilotinib (10 mg/kg, i.g., daily) and CuCl2 (0.25 mg/kg, i.p., daily). The combination therapy group received nilotinib (10 mg/kg, i.g., daily), CuCl2 (0.25 mg/kg, i.p., daily), and PD-1 monoclonal antibody (200 μg/mouse, i.p., every 3 days).

Cell lines and cell culture

Human melanoma cell lines A375, SK-MEL-28, and WM35; non-small cell lung cancer cell lines A549, NCI-H1975, and lung squamous cell carcinoma cell line NCI-H52, as well as HEK293T cells, were purchased from ATCC. Human colorectal cancer cell lines HCT116 and SW480, human bladder transitional cell carcinoma cell line UM-UC-3, and human cervical cancer cell lines HeLa and C-33 A were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Human hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines MHCC97-H and MHCC-LM3 were purchased from the Shanghai Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The murine melanoma cell line B16-F10 and hepatocellular carcinoma cell line Hepa 1–6 were obtained from ATCC and the Shanghai Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences, respectively. Specifically, A375, SK-MEL-28, WM35, MHCC97-H, MHCC-LM3, UM-UC-3, HeLa, C-33 A, Hepa 1–6, and HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). NCI-H1975, NCI-H520, and B16-F10 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 with 10% FBS. HCT116 and SW480 cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5 A medium, while A549 cells were maintained in Ham’s F-12 K medium. Complete culture media were prepared by supplementing the respective basal media with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution. All cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling, confirmed mycoplasma-free via routine PCR-based testing, and phenotypically validated through morphological assessment and growth kinetic analysis.

Method details

High-throughput screening strategy

For FDA-approved antitumor agents screening, a total of 5,000 A375 melanoma cells were seeded per well in 96-well plates. After allowing cells to adhere for 24 h, a combinatorial treatment was administered using FDA-approved oncology drugs (240-compound library, 10 μM each, Selleck, full list provided in Table S2 Tab A) along or together with tetrathiomolybdate (TTM, 10 μM). Following 24 h of treatment, cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (Bimake, Cat# B34302). The fold change in viability was calculated as: (CCK-8 value in drugs + TTM group)/(CCK-8 value in drugs alone group).

For kinase inhibitor/activator agents screening, a total of 5,000 A375 and SK-MEL-28 melanoma cells were seeded per well in 96-well plates. After 24 h, a combinatorial treatment was administered using kinase inhibitor/activator agents (166-compound library, 10 μM each, MCE, full list provided in Table S2 Tab B) together with nilotinib (10 μM) and Cu2+ (2.5 μM). Following 24 h of treatment, relative viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (Bimake, Cat# B34302).

To identify kinases that regulate ETV1 expression, A375 cells were seeded at a density of 3×105 cells per well in 6-well plates and treated with kinase inhibitors (79-compound library, 10 μM, MCE; full list provided in Table S2 Tab C) for 12 h. Following treatment, cells were harvested, and lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Whole-cell lysates were then subjected to immunoblot analysis. Protein band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software, and fold changes in ETV1 protein expression relative to the vehicle control group were calculated.

Cell death analysis and cell viability assay

Dead cells were stained with propidium iodide (PI, 5 mg/mL; Invitrogen, P1304MP). Fluorescence imaging was performed using a Nikon fluorescence microscope to capture representative images, and quantitative analysis was conducted via flow cytometry (BD, USA). Additional methodological details were described in our previous study.24 Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.10). Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Bimake, Cat# B34302), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Detailed protocols have been described in our previous study.43

DNA constructs, lentiviral transduction, and RNA interference

Human ABL siRNAs were purchased from AuGCT Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting human genes of interest were cloned into the pLenti-CRISPR-V2 plasmid. For each target gene, a pooled plasmid mixture containing two distinct sgRNAs was used. Transfections were performed using TurboFect (Thermo Fisher Scientific, R0532) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Lentivirus-transduced cells were selected with puromycin (2 μg/mL; S250J0) for 1 week. The sequences of all sgRNAs used in this study are listed in key resources table.

Immunofluorescence (IF), Immunohistochemistry (IHC), and Multiplex fluorescent immunohistochemistry (mIHC)

For IF, 1 × 105 cells were seeded onto coverslips placed in 24-well plates and cultured overnight. Cells were then subjected to the indicated treatments. Where applicable, cells were incubated with MitoTracker Red or Cupros Green fluorescent probes according to the manufacturers’ instructions, followed by two washes with PBS. Cells were subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature, permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS, and blocked with 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. For DLAT single staining, cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-DLAT mouse monoclonal antibody (Santa Cruz, Cat# sc-271534, 1:100). The following day, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature, followed by DAPI counterstaining for 5 min. For co-staining of calreticulin and β-actin, cells were incubated overnight with primary antibodies against Calreticulin (Proteintech, Cat# 10292-1-AP, 1:200) and β-actin (Proteintech, Cat# 66009-1-Ig, 1 ∶ 200), followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. Fluorescent images were captured and merged using a Zeiss laser scanning confocal microscope. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence colocalization was performed using ImageJ software.

For IHC, paraffin-embedded tissue sections were baked for 2 h in a slide oven, followed by deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed using high-pressure heating in EDTA buffer. Sections were then blocked with 5% BSA and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against DLAT (Santa Cruz, Cat# sc-271534, 1∶200), Lipoic Acid (Sigma, Cat# 437695 -100UL, 1 ∶ 600), ATP7A (Affinity, Cat# DF8506, 1∶200), and Cleaved Caspase-3 (CST, Cat# #9664, 1∶400). Signal detection was performed using a universal two-step polymer detection system (Mouse/Rabbit Enhanced Polymer Method; ZSGB-Bio, Cat# PV-9000), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Chromogenic staining was carried out using the DAB Substrate Kit (ZSGB-Bio, Cat# ZLI-9018). Images were captured using a Leica upright microscope, and quantification was performed using ImageJ software.

For mIHC, a commercially available mIHC kit was purchased from AiFang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Tissue sections were subjected to deparaffinization, rehydration, antigen retrieval, and blocking, following the same protocol as conventional IHC. A total of five sequential rounds of staining were conducted using the following primary antibodies: anti-GZMB (Abcam, Cat# ab255598, 1∶3000), anti-CD8α (Abcam, Cat# ab217344, 1∶1000, for Mouse), anti-S100B (Proteintech, Cat# 15146-1-AP, 1∶400), anti-ATP7A (Affinity, Cat# DF8506, 1∶400), anti-lipoic acid (Sigma Cat# 437695 -100UL), anti-CD3 epsilon (Abcam, Cat# ab16669, 1:1000) anti-CD8α (Abcam, Cat# ab245118, 1∶1000, for Human) and anti-CD4 (Abcam, Cat# ab288724, 1:1500). Each staining cycle employed a tyramide signal amplification (TSA) system specific to a distinct fluorophore. Between each TSA cycle, antigen retrieval was performed using high-pressure heating in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0; Servicebio, Cat# G1202) for 3 min to remove any residual antibody and prevent cross-reactivity in subsequent cycles. DAPI (Abcam, Cat# ab104139) was used for nuclear counterstaining. Multispectral images were acquired using the Vectra Polaris Automated Quantitative Pathology Imaging System (Akoya Biosciences), with individual fluorophores visualized and pseudo-colored. Image analysis and region annotation were performed using Phenochart software (version 2.2.0).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells or tissue samples using TRIzol reagent (Takara) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Oxygen consumption rate assay

Cellular OCR was measured using the Seahorse XFe96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with the XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 10000 cells per well were seeded into a Seahorse XF96 cell culture microplate and incubated for 24 h. On the day of measurement, cells were sequentially treated with oligomycin (1 μM), the oxidative phosphorylation uncoupler FCCP (1 μM), and a mixture of rotenone and antimycin A (1 μM each), which inhibit mitochondrial complexes I and III, respectively. The microplate was then loaded into the Seahorse XFe96 analyzer to record real-time OCR. All data were normalized to nuclear counts obtained from Hoechst 33342 (Beyotime, Cat# C1027) staining using segmentation analysis with the BioTek Cytation system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Quantification of extracellular ATP and HMGB1 release

To measure the release of ATP and HMGB1, cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well in 2 mL DMEM with 10% FBS. Following the indicated treatments, culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min to remove cellular debris. ATP levels in the clarified supernatants were determined using the Enhanced ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime, Cat# S0026) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. HMGB1 concentrations were measured using human or mouse-specific ELISA kits (Elabscience, E-EL-M0676/E-EL-H1554).

Murine BMDC isolation, culture, and maturation assay

Six-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were sacrificed, and femurs and tibias were aseptically dissected. Bone marrow cells were isolated by flushing the bone marrow cavity with cold PBS. Following red blood cell lysis, the remaining cells were cultured in complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL recombinant mouse GM-CSF and 10 ng/mL recombinant mouse IL-4 at a density of 1×106 cells/mL. On days 3 and 5, half of the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing the same cytokines. Non-adherent immature BMDCs were harvested on day 7.

To prepare conditioned media, B16-F10 cells were treated with indicated drugs or vehicle control for 24 h. Culture supernatants were collected, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to remove cell debris, filtered through 0.22 μm sterile filters. For BMDC maturation assay, immature BMDCs were seeded at 5×105 cells/mL and co-cultured with an equal volume of conditioned medium for 24 h. Cells were harvested, Fc-blocked with anti-CD16/32 antibody, and stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against PE-Cy7 anti-mouse CD11c (BD Pharmingen, Cat# 558079), FITC anti-mouse CD80 (BD Pharmingen, Cat# 561954) BV421 anti-mouse CD86 (BD Pharmingen, Cat# 564198), and BV 785 anti-mouse I-A/I-E (BioLegend, Cat# 107645). Flow cytometry analysis was performed on a BD LSRFortessa instrument, and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.10). The percentages of mature DCs were determined within the CD11c+ cell population.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

ChIP assays were carried out with Pierce(tm) Magnetic ChIP Kit (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 26157, USA). Cultured cells were fixed with crosslinking reagent, lysed, and subjected to sonication to shear chromatin into appropriate fragments. The fragmented chromatin was immunoprecipitated using antibodies against ETV1 (Thermo Fisher, Cat# MA5-57940) or control normal IgG. Protein-DNA complexes were captured by ChIP-Grade Protein A/G magnetic beads, followed by DNA purification. The recovered DNA was then subjected to quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis with the specific primer sets listed in Table S1.

RNA sequencing and data processing

Bulk RNA sequencing was performed on A375 melanoma cells treated with nilotinib–copper, with or without TTM, in biological replicates. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was carried out on B16F10 tumors treated with nilotinib–copper or vehicle control. Both bulk and scRNA-seq were conducted by Singleron Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China), with all raw reads subjected to rigorous quality control. Publicly available datasets were incorporated, including TCGA melanoma cohort and GEO datasets (GSE19234, GSE98394, GSE54467, GSE59455, and GSE133713). Survival analyses were performed following standard procedures using the R survival package. Cell line transcriptomes were obtained from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE). In addition, scRNA-seq data from 23 melanoma patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) were kindly provided by Prof. Marine’s group. The cohort included treatment-naive stage III/IV melanoma patients receiving either anti-PD-1 monotherapy or combined anti-PD-1/anti-CTLA-4 therapy. Tumor biopsies were collected before treatment initiation and at an early on-treatment time point prior to the second ICB infusion. Clinical, pathological, and treatment information for all patients is available in their published study.46 Downstream analyses were performed in R (v4.2.0) with Bioconductor packages, corresponding to Figures 2, 3, 5, 6, and 7. Single-cell data were processed and analyzed using the Seurat pipeline.

Transcription factor analysis

Candidate transcription factors binding to the ATP7A promoter were predicted using hTFtarget, TFDB, and GTRD databases. Differential expression analysis and heatmap visualization were based on bulk RNA-seq data. Correlation analyses were conducted across multiple datasets, including bulk RNA-seq, TCGA, CCLE, and the Xiangya melanoma cohort. The ChIP-seq data were obtained from the Cistrome DB database and visualized using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) software.47

Pathway and functional analysis

For RNA-seq data analysis, functional enrichment was performed using the clusterProfiler and GSVA R packages, following the standard workflows recommended by the respective packages.48,49

For scRNA-seq data analysis, we adapted the computational strategy described by Ye et al.27 Briefly, normalized expression data from the RNA assay of the Seurat object were extracted, and cells were optionally subsetted based on experimental requirements. For each cell, gene expression values within the copper-death signature were combined by calculating the mean expression across genes. If cell-type or other grouping information was available, mean scores were further computed within each group to provide aggregated assessments. The resulting score represented the relative copper-death activity at the single-cell level, with higher values indicating increased propensity for copper-induced cell death.

Most pathways were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). Gene sets related to immunogenic cell death (ICD) and cuproptosis were curated from Ghiringhelli et al.50 and Yao et al.,21 respectively. All gene sets used in this study are provided in the supplemental information.

Ro/e analysis

The primary objective of the Ro/e (Ratio of observed to expected) analysis is to evaluate whetherthe distribution of different cell clusters across tissues significantly deviates from the random expectation.51,52 While the chi-square test only measures the extent of deviation from the expected distribution, the Ro/e analysis provides additional insight by indicating whether a specific cell cluster is enriched or depleted in a particular tissue. Specifically, a Ro/e value greater than 1 suggests that the number of cells from a given cluster in that tissue exceeds the random expectation (indicating enrichment), whereas a Ro/e value less than 1 indicates that the number of cells is lower than expected (indicating depletion).

ATP7A/MAPK stratification and immunotherapy response analysis in melanoma

Melanoma samples were stratified into ATP7A-high and ATP7A-low groups according to the median ATP7A expression level. Similarly, samples were categorized into MAPK-high and MAPK-low groups based on the median MAPK score. Combined ATP7A/MAPK groups were subsequently generated for analyses of immunotherapy response.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise indicated. Statistical analyses were performed using R software and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0.2). Differential expression analysis of raw count RNA-seq data was conducted using DESeq2 with an adjusted p-value threshold of 0.05. TPM/FPKM expression values were log-transformed prior to downstream statistical analyses. Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, depending on data distribution and sample size, while one-way or two-way ANOVA was applied for comparisons among multiple groups. Correlations were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. Categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test or χ2 test, as appropriate. Survival outcomes were assessed using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis with log rank (Mantel-Cox) testing and Cox proportional hazards regression models fitted by iteratively weighted least squares (IWLS). Results are provided in Tables S3, S4, S5, and S6. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Significance levels are indicated as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and n.s. for not significant.
