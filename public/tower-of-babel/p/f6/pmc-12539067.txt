Multidimensional sleep health linked to quality of life among Chinese adults
Authors: Sha Li, Xingchen Shang, Yanzhe Wang, Daniel Yee Tak Fong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDespite the well-documented prevalence of sleep problems in the general population and their associations with significant health consequences that compromise quality of life, comprehensive investigations of multidimensional sleep health in Hong Kong Chinese adults remain limited. This study aimed to characterize multidimensional sleep health in Chinese adults and evaluate its association with health-related quality of life (HRQL).MethodsData were collected through a cross-sectional study of a population-representative sample in the Hong Kong community. The Short-Form-12 Health Survey version 2 (SF-12v2), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), Nonrestorative Sleep Scale (NRSS), Patient Health Questionnaire, Perceived Stress Scale, and ENRICHD Social Support Instrument were administered. Objective sleep parameters were derived from ActiGraph Link accelerometer data. The composite sleep health score was computed by aggregating the scores of efficiency, regularity, duration, and timing by actigraphy measures and the subjectively reported satisfaction and alertness by the PSQI and NRSS. Group comparison and multiple linear regression were performed to examine the association between sleep health and HRQL.ResultsA total of 500 adults (66.4% female, 39.1 years old on average) completed the survey. Participants scored 51.56 (46.57, 55.09), 49.69 (43.07, 56.72), and 2.67 ± 1.36 on the physical component summary (PCS) and mental component summary (MCS) of the SF-12v2 and the sleep health composite score, respectively. Individuals with better sleep regularity and sleep health composite scores had higher MCS. After adjusting for sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics, somatic symptoms, stress, and social support, sleep regularity demonstrated a statistically significant association with MCS (B = 1.38, P = 0.038), while the composite score of sleep health was significantly associated with role physical of PCS (B = 0.46, P = 0.041).ConclusionsSleep regularity and a composite score of sleep health were associated with HRQL in Chinese adults, suggesting that targeted interventions to enhance overall sleep health and specific dimensions may yield measurable improvements in population HRQL outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-025-02436-1.Keywords: Health-related quality of life, Physical component summary , Mental component summary, Sleep health, Sleep regularity

Introduction

Since 1948, the World Health Organization has conceptualized health as complete physical, mental, and social well-being, beyond just the absence of illness [1]. This shift has made quality of life a central goal in health assessment and improvement. Within this context, health-related quality of life (HRQL) has emerged as a specialized application of quality of life concepts to health and illness contexts, encompassing both physical and mental health domains [2, 3]. HRQL offers critical advantages over traditional clinical metrics by providing multidimensional insights into one’s subjective well-being and functional status. For example, HRQL was found to be a primary measure of the effectiveness of treatments for chronic diseases [4] and a better predictor of survival rates than clinician-assessed performance status in cancer patients [5]. Moreover, even with similar diagnoses and treatments, patients demonstrate diverse functional and emotional outcomes that may influence their work retention capacity [6]. Therefore, HRQL assessment enables comprehensive evaluation of individual health status and its functional consequences, explains individual variations in condition response, and evaluates treatment effectiveness [7].

Sleep is an essential biological process that facilitates energy conservation, physiological and psychological restoration, emotional-cognitive regulation, and neurodevelopmental maturation [8]. It crucially regulates physiological processes, including cortisol secretion, thereby influencing HRQL through both somatic and psychological pathways [9]. For instance, research indicates that insufficient sleep duration and poor sleep quality constitute modifiable risk factors for reduced HRQL [10]. However, previous studies remain limited by their focus on bivariate relationships between individual sleep metrics (e.g., sleep quality) and particular outcomes, neglecting the multidimensional nature of sleep physiology [11]. Over the past decade, sleep research has undergone one paradigm shift from a disorder-focused approach to a health-oriented perspective, with growing recognition of sleep health as a multidimensional construct that positively contributes to overall well-being [12, 13]. This emerging paradigm, exemplified by Buysse et al.’s (2014) RU_SATED model, reconceptualizes sleep through six key dimensions—Regularity, Satisfaction, Alertness, Timing, Efficiency, and Duration—representing a comprehensive framework that moves beyond traditional pathology-based models to emphasize sleep’s active role in health promotion [13]. Buysse defines sleep health as an adaptive, multidimensional sleep-wake pattern promoting wellbeing through individual-environmental alignment [13]. Evidence has demonstrated that sleep health was associated with various health outcomes, including physical functioning, glycaemic target achievement, and depression symptoms [14–16].

Despite growing recognition of sleep health’s multidimensional nature, the relationship between multidimensional sleep health and HRQL has been underexplored. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the multidimensional sleep health status in Hong Kong Chinese adults and examine its relationship with HRQL. We posit that better multidimensional sleep health, as operationalized by the RU_SATED framework, positively correlates with enhanced HRQL.

Methods

Study design

This population-based cross-sectional study employed a stratified, systematic random sampling approach to survey Hong Kong households between February 2018 and September 2019. The sampling framework was derived from the Hong Kong Census and Statistics Department’s quarterly maintained residential database, representing the most comprehensive and authoritative housing registry available in the region. Prior to field visits, all selected households received mailed notification letters containing study particulars, scheduled visitation timeframes, and interviewer identification details. During onsite visits, eligibility screening and written consent procedures were completed before taking study measurements. Consented participants were fitted with ActiGraph GT9X Link accelerometers (ActiGraph, US) on their non-dominant wrist for 7 consecutive days of objective monitoring, while concurrently completing a battery of self-report questionnaires. Upon completion of the monitoring period (7 days), researchers conducted follow-up home visits to retrieve devices and disburse participant honoraria in the form of HK$300 (≈ US$40) shopping vouchers.

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Hong Kong and Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster (Approval Reference: UW17-011), with all procedures adhering to established ethical guidelines.

Participants

Eligibility criteria required participants to be adults aged ≥ 18 years, and be proficient in either written Traditional Chinese or spoken Cantonese communication. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) hearing impairment requiring aids, (2) diagnosed psychiatric disorders, (3) current use of sleep medications, (4) pregnancy, (5) parenting children under 24 months, and (6) inability/unwillingness to comply with ActiGraph GT9X Link monitoring protocols. We screened 1,625 potential participants, of whom 500 (30.8%) provided informed consent and completed the survey. A total of 584 (35.9%) individuals were ineligible, while 227 (14.0%) declined participation. Additionally, 314 (19.3%) were unavailable after five attempted household visits. Following the recommendation of a minimum of 10 cases per independent variable, our sample size adequately powered regression models containing multiple predictors while maintaining statistical reliability [17].

Measures

Short-Form-12 Health Survey version 2 (SF-12v2)

The SF-12v2 Health Survey is a widely validated, multidimensional instrument for assessing HRQL [2, 18, 19]. The SF-12v2 generates eight domain-specific scale scores (PF: physical functioning, RP: role-physical, BP: bodily pain, GH: general health, VT: vitality, SF: social functioning, RE: role-emotional, and MH: mental health) and two composite measures: the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS), which respectively quantify physical and mental health status. Psychometric evaluation of the Chinese SF-12v2 revealed adequate reliability, showing Cronbach’s alpha of 0.62 for the PCS and 0.75 for the MCS [20]. Both composite scores were normalized to a 0–100 scale, where higher scores indicate better health status.

Actigraphy

Objective sleep parameters were quantified using ActiGraph GT9X Link accelerometers, with data processed through ActiLife software (ActiGraph LLC, Pensacola, FL). The Cole-Kripke algorithm, validated in adult populations, was employed to derive four key metrics: sleep onset latency, sleep efficiency, time in bed (TIB), and total sleep time (TST) [21, 22]. Participants were instructed to wear the ActiGraph GT9X Link continuously for seven consecutive days, with valid data defined as containing ≥ 4 weekdays and ≥ 1 weekend day of recordings. To enhance data reliability, accelerometer-derived sleep parameters were cross-validated against a sleep diary.

Sleep health

We computed a composite sleep health score based on the six-dimensional RU_SATED model (Regularity, Satisfaction, Alertness, Timing, Efficiency, Duration). Satisfaction was operationalized using Item 6 of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index [23], which assesses global sleep quality perception. Responses of “very good” or “fairly good” were dichotomized as satisfactory (score = 1), while “fairly bad” or “very bad” responses were classified as unsatisfactory (score = 0) [24, 25]. Alertness was assessed using Item 10 of the Nonrestorative Sleep Scale (Do you usually feel alert during the daytime?) [26]. Responses were dichotomized into poor alertness (score ≤ 6, coded as 0) versus good alertness (score >6, coded as 1). Duration was objectively quantified using actigraphy-derived TST, calculated as the temporal interval scored as “asleep” after accounting for sleep onset latency and wake after sleep onset periods [21]. Durations between 6 and 8 h were classified as optimal (coded as 1), and durations outside this range (< 6 or >8 h) were classified as suboptimal (coded as 0) [13, 25]. Timing was calculated as the time between sleep onset and offset, with consideration of latency, accounting for sleep onset latency. Optimal timing (score = 1) was defined as a midpoint occurring between 02:00 am and 04:00 am, while midpoints before 02:00 am or after 04:00 am were classified as suboptimal (score = 0) [13, 25]. Efficiency was computed as the ratio of TST to TIB, and optimal efficiency (i.e., score = 1) was concluded when the ratio was ≥ 85% [25]. Regularity was calculated by the standard deviation (SD) of timing across the record period and dichotomized to regular (≤ 1 SD, score = 1) and irregular (>1 SD, score = 0) [25]. A multidimensional sleep health index was computed by aggregating binary scores (0/1) from the six RU_SATED domains [27]. The composite score ranged from 0 (poorest sleep health) to 6 (optimal sleep health), providing a quantitative measure of overall sleep health status.

Patient health questionnaire (PHQ-15)

Psychosomatic symptom burden was evaluated using the validated Chinese version of the PHQ-15 [28], a reliable instrument demonstrating good psychometric properties in Chinese populations (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.79) [28]. The questionnaire quantifies symptom severity across 15 physical manifestations (e.g., joint pain, palpitations) using an ordinal scale (0 = not bothered at all to 2 = bothered a lot).

Perceived stress scale (PSS)

The Chinese 10-item PSS was administered to evaluate participants’ stress levels. This version demonstrates superior psychometric properties (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.85) [29]. Compared to both the original 14-item and abbreviated 4-item scales. The 10-item scale comprised six positively-phrased and four negatively-phrased items. Positive items were reverse-coded before summation to yield a total score (range: 0–40) where higher values reflect greater perceived stress [29].

ENRICHD social support instrument (ESSI)

Social support was evaluated using the 6-item ESSI. The validated Chinese adaptation comprises six items rated on a 1 (low support) to 5 (high support) scale, yielding a global score between 6 and 30 [30]. Psychometric evaluation confirmed satisfactory reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.79) [30].

Sociodemographic characteristics and lifestyle

Sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics were collected via face-to-face interviews using a standardized investigator-developed questionnaire. The collected information included age, sex (male, female), marital status (single, married/cohabiting, separated/divorced/widowed), education level (primary school or below, secondary education, bachelor’s degree or higher), occupation (working, not working, retired, students), family income (< HK$35000, ≥ HK$35000), and exercise (No exercise, ≤ 3 h/week, >3 h/week).

Statistical analysis

Data quality was ensured through comprehensive cross-checking against source documents and strict adherence to published scoring guidelines for all assessment instruments. Participants were included in the final analytic sample only if they completed ≥ 75% of the scale items. For scales with < 10% missing data at the item level, missing values were imputed using the mean score of completed items from the same scale [31]. Population characteristics were described using mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables, and group comparisons were performed using independent samples t-tests for normally distributed continuous variables between two groups. For comparisons among more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied under the assumption of homogeneity of variance, with Bonferroni correction for post-hoc pairwise comparisons if a significant overall effect was found. In cases where the assumption of homogeneity of variance was violated, Welch’s ANOVA was conducted, followed by Games-Howell post-hoc tests for pairwise comparisons. Continuous variables violating normality assumptions were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR), and the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test was used for multi-group comparisons, followed by Mann-Whitney U tests for pairwise comparisons with appropriate adjustment for multiple testing. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies with corresponding percentages to characterize their distribution in the study population.

Multivariable linear regression models were constructed to evaluate associations between dimensions of multidimensional sleep health and PCS and MCS with the adjustment of sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics, somatic symptoms, stress, and social support. Subsequently, the association between the composite score of multidimensional sleep health and the PCS, MCS, as well as the eight domain scores of HRQL, was further evaluated with covariate adjustment. We employed a two-stage hierarchical regression to assess the effect of the composite score. Sociodemographic and lifestyle covariates were entered first, while somatic symptoms, stress, and social support were additionally controlled. All the corresponding variables were entered into each model simultaneously to reduce selection bias [32]. We assessed multicollinearity through variance inflation factor (VIF) values with the Package “car” [33], applying a conservative cutoff of 5.0 [34]. Statistical significance was set at 5%, with effect estimates reported alongside their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) where applicable. All data analysis was conducted with R-4.5.1.

The association between multidimensional sleep health and quality of life

No significant association was observed between sleep health and PCS in the adjusted models (Table 3). After adjusting for sociodemographic and lifestyle factors, sleep health demonstrated a significant negative association with MCS (B = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.13, 1.29, P = 0.018) (Table 4). Discussion

To our knowledge, this study represents the first comprehensive investigation of sleep health and quality of life associations in Chinese adults. The findings revealed distinct patterns across health domains: sleep regularity maintained a significant association with MCS even after full covariate adjustment, whereas no such relationship emerged for PCS. The composite score of multidimensional sleep health was associated with MCS and role physical of PCS, but the significance of MCS was attenuated after additionally adjusting for somatic symptoms, perceived stress, and social support. A multidimensional perspective on sleep health might illuminate its association with quality of life in adults, informing the design of more refined health promotion strategies.

The results showed that individuals who had better sleep regularity had higher MCS, and sleep regularity was associated with MCS in the adjusted linear regression. One previous study showed that the school-day sleep regularity demonstrated specific associations with depressive symptoms, whereas weekend regularity showed broader associations with overall psychopathology [35]. Irregular sleep timing may adversely affect mental health through prolonged sleep onset latency. A regular sleep pattern will strengthen the circadian clock and facilitate rapid sleep initiation. Conversely, irregular sleep patterns could increase wakefulness before sleep onset, which could induce more rumination and reinforce depressive thinking styles [36]. Existing literature has predominantly examined sleep duration, consistently demonstrating associations between short sleep duration and adverse physical and mental health outcomes [37]. However, sleep regularity, operationalized as the day-to-day consistency of sleep-wake timing, is a potentially more robust predictor of specific health outcomes [38]. For example, one recent prospective cohort study showed that sleep regularity predicts mortality better than sleep duration, suggesting sleep regularity interventions may offer superior population health benefits [38]. Given the demonstrated mental health benefits of regular sleep patterns, adults should be advised to maintain regular sleeping patterns in addition to obtaining sufficient sleep duration to optimize well-being and mental health. Nonetheless, compensatory sleep extension during weekend/non-work days may partially mitigate the detrimental effects of weekday sleep restriction, though this strategy cannot fully substitute for consistent adequate sleep duration [39].

Comparison results showed that individuals who had the highest sleep health composite score had higher MCS than those who had the lowest sleep health, and the composite score of sleep health was associated with MCS after adjusting for sociodemographic and lifestyle factors. One recent study showed that optimal sleep health in late pregnancy was significantly associated with reduced antenatal anxiety and depression symptoms [40]. Previous studies on sleep identified that sleep and mental health shared neurobiological foundations in grey matter anatomy [41]. For example, sleep duration was associated with local cortical thickness, which was related to depression. Evidence from a recent study indicated that each unit increase in the total RU_SATED score corresponded to an 11–20% increase in the odds of reporting improved mental well-being [42]. Therefore, researchers proposed that by integrating multiple sleep dimensions, the composite metrics could better capture the complex, dose-dependent relationships between sleep health and mental health outcomes than unidimensional measures [43].

The association with role physical of PCS was significant in the fully adjusted models. Despite its abbreviated format, the role physical domain of the SF-12v2 maintains adequate psychometric properties, demonstrating satisfactory internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.81) and robust relationship with the corresponding SF-36v2 domain (r = 0.91) [44]. The role physical domain assesses functional limitations in work or daily activities attributable to physical health impairments, quantifying the extent of activity restriction and productivity loss [45]. The association between sleep health and role physical might be due to the impaired daytime function, whereby suboptimal sleep compromises physical capacity and task performance efficiency [46]. For example, individuals maintaining 6–8 h of sleep had significantly larger grey matter volume in multiple brain regions, such as the hippocampi, precentral gyrus, right frontal pole, orbitofrontal cortex, and cerebellar subfields [47]. The correlation between the composite score of sleep health and quality of life suggests that the observed associations are likely to reflect synergistic interactions among multiple sleep dimensions. Future studies could explore the interactions among distinct sleep health dimensions and their collective impact on physiological and psychological outcomes. Such research would advance theoretical frameworks to guide precision sleep medicine interventions tailored to individual sleep health profiles.

The mean sleep health score of 2.67 (range: 0–6) among participants in this study corresponds to 4.46 when standardized to a 0–10 scale. Previous research employing the SATED scale has proposed a clinical cutoff score of < 8 (range: 0–10) as indicative of suboptimal sleep health in adult populations [48]. Although employing a different methodology (i.e., combining actigraphy data with self-reported measures) than the SATED scale, our composite scoring system reveals substantial sleep health deficits in the Hong Kong Chinese adult population. From multiple perspectives on sleep health, studies consistently reveal that Hong Kong Chinese adults exhibit both quantitative sleep deficits and qualitative impairments [49, 50]. Hong Kong has become known as one of the most sleep-deprived places in the world [51], and one recent study showed that 52.2% of the participants had insomnia symptoms based on an Insomnia Severity Index threshold score of 10 [27]. Using the same threshold (PSQI ≥ 5), epidemiological studies reported a weighted prevalence of poor sleep quality of 39.4% among Chinese adults in Hong Kong [52], compared to 27.2% in Singapore [53]. Moreover, one study revealed that Hong Kong adolescents exhibit significantly shorter sleep duration and later bedtimes than their Shanghai counterparts [54]. Adolescents’ sleep is greatly influenced by their parents. Parents maintaining good sleep hygiene themselves can effectively guide their children’s sleep habits. However, evidence showed that Hong Kong Chinese adults quantified significant sleep procrastination attributable to digital media use, with mean delays of 39.70 min for social media and 40.01 min for other media activities [55]. These data underscore the urgent need for targeted sleep health promotion interventions, such as targeted sleep hygiene interventions, among Hong Kong Chinese adults, and promotion of multidimensional “sleep health” concepts should be prioritized in public health initiatives to address this growing health concern.

The comparison between groups and the linear regression models showed that married or cohabiting individuals had higher scores on sleep health and MCS than singles. Moreover, married or cohabiting, or single individuals had higher scores on PCS than those separated or divorced, or widowed. This might be due to the protective effect of an intimate relationship that promotes better communication, emotional connection, and coping styles [40]. Higher family income was associated with better MCS and PCS, which might be due to that greater financial resources enhance individuals’ capacity to address physical health issues and reduce stress related to problem-solving, thereby lowering the risk of physical and mental illness [56]. Moreover, the group comparison and the linear regression show that those who were not working and retired individuals had worse PCS, which might be due to that people who are retired or unemployed are likely to have a higher susceptibility to chronic diseases and declining physical function, consequently contributing to poorer physical health outcomes [56]. Notably, the associations of stress, social support, and somatic symptoms have been discussed in one previous article [56].

As the first comprehensive investigation of the association between sleep health and quality of life in Chinese adults with an adequate and representative sample in the community, this study combined objective actigraphy data with subjective self-report measures, with the consideration of confounders, providing a robust multidimensional assessment of sleep-wake patterns. However, there are several study limitations worth noting. First, this study employed parameter thresholds (e.g., sleep timing cutoffs) derived from existing literature [13]. Their applicability may be limited by cultural differences in sleep-wake preferences. Second, while revealing significant associations, our cross-sectional approach cannot establish causal interpretations of the sleep health-quality of life relationship [57]. Future longitudinal studies incorporating repeated measures and cross-lagged analyses would better elucidate potential bidirectional associations and their developmental trajectories. Lastly, the exclusion criteria encompassing hearing impairment, psychiatric conditions, sleep medication use, and pregnancy necessitate subsequent research to examine the relationships in these clinically significant subpopulations, thereby evaluating the broader applicability of our findings.

Conclusion

The association between sleep health and HRQL warrants the implementation of comprehensive sleep health promotion strategies to promote the sleep health of Hong Kong Chinese adults. Population-level interventions should incorporate sleep education and behavioral modification strategies to optimize sleep health and enhance overall well-being.
