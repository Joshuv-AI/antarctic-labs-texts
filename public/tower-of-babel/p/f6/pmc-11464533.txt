A compendium of genetic variations associated with promoter usage across 49 human tissues
Authors: Jiapei Yuan, Yang Tong, Le Wang, Xiaoxiao Yang, Xiaochuan Liu, Meng Shu, Zekun Li, Wen Jin, Chenchen Guan, Yuting Wang, Qiang Zhang, Yang Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Promoters play a crucial role in regulating gene transcription. However, our understanding of how genetic variants influence alternative promoter selection is still incomplete. In this study, we implement a framework to identify genetic variants that affect the relative usage of alternative promoters, known as promoter usage quantitative trait loci (puQTLs). By constructing an atlas of human puQTLs across 49 different tissues from 838 individuals, we have identified approximately 76,856 independent loci associated with promoter usage, encompassing 602,009 genetic variants. Our study demonstrates that puQTLs represent a distinct type of molecular quantitative trait loci, effectively uncovering regulatory targets and patterns. Furthermore, puQTLs are regulating in a tissue-specific manner and are enriched with binding sites of epigenetic marks and transcription factors, especially those involved in chromatin architecture formation. Notably, we have also found that puQTLs colocalize with complex traits or diseases and contribute to their heritability. Collectively, our findings underscore the significant role of puQTLs in elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying tissue development and complex diseases.

Introduction

Promoters are regions adjacent to the transcription start site (TSS) and contain specific sequences required for initiating transcription1. Promoters integrate signals from distal regulatory elements and epigenetic modifications, collectively determining the transcriptional level. The majority of human protein-coding genes possess multiple distinct promoters, each equipped with its unique set of regulatory elements. One crucial aspect of promoter regulation is the selection of the most appropriate promoter for a given gene, determining the specific gene isoform that will be expressed. Furthermore, tissue-specific regulation of alternative promoters has been observed, and their dysregulation across different tissues has been documented2. Maintaining a balance of promoter activity is crucial for tissue development. For example, in the case of the gene GJB1, the selection of the 3′-most promoter is specific to central nervous system (CNS) tissue, while the 5′-most promoter is selected in other tissues. It has been recognized that the selection of promoters can offer valuable insights into tissue specificity beyond what can be inferred from gene expression alone. GJB1 shows different promoter choices in the CNS and other tissues, with no notable RNA expression differences2. Moreover, several investigations have elucidated the impact of genetic variations on alternative promoter utilization. Notably, the SNP rs4728142 was recently reported to be interacting with the promoter of IRF5 gene and regulating the promoter usage through chromatin looping3.

Previous studies have identified various genetic variations associated with different molecular quantitative trait loci (QTLs)4, such as gene expression (eQTLs)5,6, splicing patterns (sQTLs)7, DNA methylation (meQTLs)8, chromatin accessibility (caQTLs)9–12, 3′ UTR alternative polyadenylation (3′aQTLs)13, N6-methyladenosine modification (m6AQTLs)14,15, and protein expression (pQTLs)16. More recently, studies utilizing cap analysis of gene expression (CAGE) technology to elucidate the genetic variations linked to promoter activity have been conducted. In these studies, the variations have been identified as TSS-associated QTLs (tssQTLs)17 and promoter usage QTLs (puQTLs)18. The predominant approach in many of these studies involves quantifying the activity of each isoform’s promoter through the absolute expression level of specific promoter regions18,19. However, few studies have investigated promoter usage across various tissues20–22, leaving a gap in our understanding of the detailed biological characteristics of puQTLs. Additionally, there are several issues with the current definition of promoter usage. First, the calculation method often results in a high correlation between promoter activity and mRNA expression of the gene, leading to a significant overlap between puQTLs and eQTLs. Second, current literature indicates that each gene typically possesses a dominant or optimal promoter, with alternative promoters playing a limited and potentially detrimental role23,24. In the case of gene STAU2, one promoter is highly expressed in basal cells, another in other subtypes, while the remaining four are not differentially expressed. Similarly, for gene TTC23, promoters 1 and 2 show opposite associations with genetic variant rs8028374, while promoter 4 is expressed at a low level and its proportion does not change with different genotypes. The examples such as GJB1, STAU2, and TTC23 reveal that while multiple alternative promoters may exist for a gene, typically only one is predominantly utilized in a certain tissue or cell type, and their relative usage follows an inverse pattern.

To address the aforementioned concerns and enhance the comprehension of alternative promoter selection in the context of tissue development, we implement a framework to identify the genetic variants that affect the relative usage of alternative promoters (puQTLs), and apply it to a wide range of tissues, encompassing a total of 49 different tissue types. We seek to define puQTL by considering the fractional usage of the major promoter for each gene, may effectively reduce the dimensionality of promoter selection compared to aggregating the activity of all promoters. In order to differentiate this definition from the traditional puQTL, which primarily focused on promoter activity as measured by expression level, we have designated the traditional puQTL as promoter activity QTL (paQTL)18,19. This distinction enables us to clearly define the specific genetic associations with promoter usage, independent of those associated with promoter activity.

In this study, we utilize genotype and RNA-seq data from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project2,25 to explore the impact of puQTL on gene promoter usage across 49 human tissues. We identify puQTLs that are associated with the usage of gene promoters in particular tissues, indicating puQTLs play a significant role in mediating tissue-specific gene expression patterns. Furthermore, we have shown that puQTL is a unique form of molecular QTL that distinguishes itself from paQTL and eQTL from multiple aspects, including associated genes and regulatory patterns. Our study has provided evidence that puQTLs possess the capability to unveil previously unknown promoters affected by genetic variants. We have buttressed these findings with more evidence derived from three-dimensional chromatin interactions and epigenetic marks. In conclusion, our investigation characterizes puQTLs as a discernible form of molecular QTL and underscores the significance of puQTLs in shaping tissue-specific gene promoter usage.

Results

Identification of puQTLs across 49 human tissues

Genetic variations have been shown to have an impact on promoter activity, which is measured by the abundance of RNA transcripts in the promoter region18–20. To enhance our understanding of this phenomenon, we have modified the calculation method for puQTL by introducing the fractional promoter usage for each gene, with a total usage summing up to 1. We then selected the most commonly used promoter for further analysis. The proActiv method was employed to quantify both relative and absolute promoter activity utilizing junction read counts. This approach exhibited a remarkable degree of precision in promoter activity estimation compared to CAGE data and H3K4me3 histone data2,26. By combining genotype and RNA-seq data from the GTEx project27, we identified 602,009 genetic variants associated with promoter usage, corresponding to 76,856 linkage disequilibrium (LD)-independent loci and 759,936 SNP-gene pairs across 49 different human tissues (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Data 1). The number of genetic variants associated with promoter usage varied across tissues, ranging from 999 in kidney cortex to 155,547 in thyroid tissues, corresponding to 42 and 5328 independent loci in kidney and thyroid, respectively. Interestingly, we observed a strong positive correlation between the number of puQTLs and the sample size (Rs = 0.939, P < 2.2 × 10−16) (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1). Additionally, we found that these variants tend to be located near TSS sites (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 2), and their effects on genes are influenced by their distances from the TSS sites (Fig. 1d). More than half of the promoters in all 49 tissues exhibited association with multiple genetic variants, indicating a complex regulatory network (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Data 2). Notably, tissues with larger sample sizes, such as thyroid, sun-exposed skin, and lung, displayed a high proportion of promoters with multiple independent loci (Supplementary Fig. 1e). To validate our findings, we integrated chromatin interaction data from HiChIPdb28 (Supplementary Data 3). We discovered that when puSNPs were located within 0.1 Mb of a gene, the chromatin interactions between them could only be verified for more than 50% of puQTLs in two tissues (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Data 4). However, for puQTLs located more than 0.1 Mb away from genes, the chromatin interactions could be validated for more than 50% of puQTLs in six tissues, suggesting that puQTLs pairing with genes at a distance of more than 0.1 Mb is a more reliable set for validation. We have implemented a comprehensive model to investigate the impact of different covariates on the promoter usage for the identified puQTLs. Our model takes into account various factors that contribute to the variation in gene promoter usage across different tissues. We conducted a regression analysis on promoter usage, considering factors such as ancestry, age, sex, PEER factors, and the genotypes of the top significant SNPs. Each covariate was added sequentially, and we measured the percentage of variance in promoter usage explained by the inclusion of each additional factor. Our findings revealed that the inclusion of PEER factors in the model resulted in a maximum 7.1% increase in explanatory power compared to the combined influence of ancestry, age, and sex (Supplementary Fig. 3a). The selection of PEER factors was based on the integration of technical, clinical, and epidemiological covariates, taking into account the sample size (Supplementary Fig. 3b, see “Methods” section). Furthermore, the incorporation of the puQTLs in the model enhanced its robustness and accounted for an additional 4%–16% of the variance in promoter usage.

puQTL is a distinctive class of molecular QTL that differs from paQTL

puQTL is a unique form of molecular QTL that distinguishes itself from paQTL in measurement and calculation approaches. Specifically, puQTL focuses on genes, assigning a single promoter usage value to each gene and correlating it with genotypes. In contrast, paQTL operates at the transcript level, assigning a promoter activity value to each transcript and associating it with genotypes (Fig. 2a). Previous studies have classified genes associated with paQTLs into five groups based on the number of associated promoters, the effect direction, and size on promoter activity18. Group 1 includes genes with only one promoter, while Group 2 consists of genes with multiple promoters but significantly associated with only one. Group 3 comprises genes linked to multiple promoters with opposite effect directions, and Group 4 includes genes associated with multiple promoters with the same effect direction but a ratio of effect size <2. Lastly, Group 5 consists of genes with a ratio of effect size not <2 (Supplementary Fig. 4a). The genes belonging to Group 1, which are not associated with promoter selection, were excluded from further analysis. Our investigation reveals that a significant proportion (68.6% on median) of paQTL-associated genes align with Group 2, while a minority proportion (<25%) of genes are distributed across Groups 3, 4, and 5 in 12 out of the 49 examined tissues (Supplementary Fig. 4b and Supplementary Data 5). This finding emphasizes the predominant presence of Group 2 genes in paQTL associations and highlights the limited representation of genes in the other groups. The examination of genes in Group 2, which exhibited significant association with a single promoter, bears resemblance to the notion of puQTLs as defined in this research, which employs the fractional promoter usage of the most frequently utilized promoters. Upon conducting an analysis, we have identified 143,172 paSNP-gene pairs in EBV transformed lymphocytes, a substantial increase compared to the 3004 CAGE-based paSNP-gene pairs18 and 2325 RNA-seq-based paSNP-gene pairs previously published19. Notably, our study revealed that 9.95% of CAGE-based paSNP-gene pairs and 13.1% of RNA-seq-based paSNP-gene pairs were rediscovered, despite the disparate data sources (Supplementary Fig. 4c). This signifies the robustness and comprehensiveness of our findings in relation to previously established research.

In this study, a total of 716,766 puSNP-gene pairs and 3,739,969 paSNP-gene pairs were identified, with 381,202 overlapping in at least one tissue (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Data 6). Approximately 75% of the overlapped pairs demonstrated tissue-specific patterns that were uniquely presented in three or fewer tissues (Fig. 2c). Additionally, around 75% of puSNP-gene pairs were not defined as significant paSNP-gene pairs across 49 tissues, emphasizing the importance of focusing on the relative usage of promoters (Supplementary Fig. 4d). Consistently, it should be noted that genes with only one promoter were excluded from the analysis, resulting in the puQTL-associated genes not being categorized into Group 1. When comparing puQTL-associated genes with paQTL-associated genes across 49 different tissues, it was observed that a majority of the puQTL-associated genes fell into Group 2, representing a median of 61.2% of the genes (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Data 6). puQTL-associated genes in Groups 3, 4, and 5 exhibited a similar ratio as compared to paQTL-associated genes. Additionally, the proportion of puQTL-associated genes in these three groups showed a strong correlation with the total number of puQTLs and promoters, suggesting a more intricate regulatory pattern for tissues with more puQTLs.

In our analysis, we conducted a comparison between puQTLs and paQTLs, yielding intriguing observations. Notably, for the genes overlapped in both puQTLs and paQTLs, we discovered that more than 58% of them exhibited different patterns of promoter associations (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Data 7). An illustrative example is the SNP rs34815476 in the esophagus-mucosa tissue, which was found to be linked to the upregulation of the major promoter 25886 usage of the IRF5 gene in puQTL analysis (FDR = 2.46 × 10−16), while it was associated with the downregulation of the alternative promoter 25887 activity in paQTL analysis (FDR = 8.05 × 10−20) (Fig. 2f). Furthermore, we observed that about 56% of the promoters overlapped in both puQTLs and paQTLs exhibited associations in different directions (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Data 7). For instance, the SNP rs1154329 was associated with the same promoter 25886 of the IRF5 gene in the esophagus-mucosa tissue in both puQTL and paQTL analyses, but rs1154329 significantly downregulated the promoter usage (FDR = 7.29 × 10−9) while it significantly upregulated the promoter activity (FDR = 6.61 × 10−4) (Fig. 2h). The relationship between numerous genetic variants in the IRF5 gene and the susceptibility to systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) has been extensively investigated in prior studies29–31. However, it should be noted that the puSNPs rs34815476 and rs1154329 have not been previously included in these reports. Moreover, SLE, being an autoimmune disorder, has the potential to impact multiple organs, including the esophagus, leading to motility disorders32–34. Our study provides insights into how genetic variants affect the promoter usage of the IRF5 gene, which makes individuals more susceptible to SLE. In conclusion, these findings shed light on the distinct nature of puQTLs compared to paQTLs in terms of the associated promoters and regulatory patterns.

puQTL offers an additional layer of information compared to eQTL

We also conducted a comparative analysis between puQTLs and eQTLs. Among the 12,190 puQTL-associated genes and 34,533 eQTL-associated genes, a total of 9697 genes were found to be common between the two in at least one tissue (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Data 8). Approximately 80% of the overlapped associated genes were only found in five or fewer tissues, indicating a significant tissue-specific pattern (Fig. 3b). Approximately 16% of shared genes were found to be linked to identical SNPs in both puQTLs and eQTLs (Fig. 3c). Some puQTLs may be located in regions with high LD to eQTLs. Additionally, when puQTLs in high LD with eQTLs (r2 ≥ 0.6) are considered, the percentage of shared target genes increases to 22%. This indicates distinctive relationships between these QTL types and their corresponding targets (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Moreover, the lead eQTLs and puQTLs for the overlapped genes were located at a median distance of 137 Kb on the chromosome, and no significant LD was observed (Fig. 3d, e). To evaluate the impact of puQTLs and eQTLs on their associated genes, we performed a comparative analysis of the effect sizes associated with these two types of QTLs. Our investigation unveiled a robust positive correlation between the effect sizes of puQTLs and eQTLs on 2234 genes, suggesting a consistent regulatory pattern between these two QTL types (Rs = 0.86, P value <2.2 × 10−16) (Fig. 3f). Conversely, we also identified 2615 genes where the effect sizes of puQTLs and eQTLs exhibited a significantly negative correlation, indicating the presence of contrasting regulation patterns for these genes (Rs = −0.8, P value <2.2 × 10−16) (Fig. 3g). Overall, the effect size of puQTLs is negatively correlated with the effect size of eQTLs (Rs = −0.46, P value <2.2 × 10−16) (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Moreover, our analysis revealed a substantial overlap between the genes associated with puQTLs and eQTLs across 49 different tissues, ranging from 24.57% in the brain amygdala and 81.13% in the cells cultured fibroblasts, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 6a, upper panel). The degree of overlap is highly correlated with the sample size of respective tissue, with larger sample sizes yielding greater statistical power to identify both eQTLs and puQTLs, thereby resulting in a greater overlap between the two types of QTLs (Supplementary Fig. 6a, middle panel). Upon intersecting puSNP-gene pairs with eSNP-gene pairs, we found that more than 50% of the puSNP-gene pairs exhibited a positive correlation with eSNP-genes in 30 tissues, in which there were a greater number of overlapping genes (Supplementary Fig. 6a, bottom panel).

In general, the majority of eQTLs and puQTLs were identified in noncoding regions, such as introns, UTRs, and intergenic regions. Only about 2.5% of these SNPs were found in the exon regions (Supplementary Fig. 6b). The distribution of these genetic variants in the 3′ and 5′ UTRs, as well as other regions adjacent to genes, suggests their potential regulatory role. To gain a better understanding of the positional preference, we classified puQTLs and eQTLs across 49 tissues based on the genomic regions defined in SnpEff35 (Fig. 3h). As expected, we observed that puQTLs were significantly enriched in 5′UTRs and splice site regions, while eQTLs were significantly enriched in intron and downstream regions. To account for biases introduced by the length and number of different genomic regions, we conducted a permutation test to estimate the significance of overlap between different SNPs with promoter, 3′UTRs and 5′UTRs. Consistently, puQTLs were found to be significantly enriched in promoter region and UTRs (P <2.2 × 10−16) (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). Additionally, puQTLs showed a higher enrichment in these regions compared to eQTLs (Fig. 3j). Despite downsampling, we found consistent results indicating a lower presence of eQTLs in promoter region and UTRs (Fig. 3k). By comparing the number of genes associated with puQTLs, paQTLs, and eQTLs, several findings were made. Specifically, 54 genes were exclusively linked with puQTLs, while 750 genes and 142 genes were found to be overlap with eQTLs and paQTLs, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 6e). Moreover, in terms of unique puSNP-gene pairs, the counts were 249,015, while the overlapping numbers were 37,017 and 63,668 for eSNP-gene pairs and paSNP-gene pairs, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 6f). Overall, puQTLs were classified as a distinct molecular QTL from eQTLs and paQTLs based on various factors, including associated genes, distance between different types of QTLs, pattern of regulation on genes, genome distribution, and overlapping SNP-gene pairs.

puQTL is representing in a tissue-specific manner

To gain insight into the regulation of puQTL in different tissues, a comparative analysis was conducted to determine the extent of puQTL sharing across various tissues. Our results show that over 92.7% of puQTLs and 66% of puSNP-gene pairs are unique to a single tissue, with a small portion (under 10%) present in two tissues (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 7). Notably, the proportion of promoter usage variation explained by shared puQTLs increases as the number of shared tissues increases, suggesting a more pronounced influence (Fig. 4b). Tissue-specific puQTLs were prevalent in most tissues, with 11.45%–51.72% of puQTLs being specific to each tissue (Supplementary Fig. 8a). It is noteworthy that an increased sample size is associated with a greater number of uniquely identified puQTLs in a particular tissue. This observation suggests that the enhanced power of the model to detect puQTLs with a larger sample size leads to the identification of a greater number of tissue-specific puQTLs. These tissue-specific puQTLs, along with the genes they influence, could provide insight into the dynamic changes that occur during tissue development at the pre-transcriptional level. To assess the heterogeneity of cross-tissue effects, multivariate adaptive shrinkage (MASH) analysis was employed, revealing two major clusters: brain-related tissues and non-brain tissues (Fig. 4c, d and Supplementary Fig. 8b–e). For instance, we found that digestive tissues (i.e., colon, stomach, and esophagus) were grouped together, indicating a functional similarity. These findings underscore the potential for identifying similarities in developmental and functional characteristics among different tissues based on promoter usage selection.

The results of our study indicate that puQTLs demonstrate a similar pattern to paQTLs in terms of both magnitude and sign effects. Specifically, around 20% of puQTLs were found to be shared in more than one tissue in terms of magnitude, and over 15% were shared in all 49 tissues by sign (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 8f). Furthermore, when comparing puQTLs to paQTLs or eQTLs shared among tissues, we observed a higher proportion of shared paQTLs and eQTLs in terms of both magnitude and sign (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 8g). These findings suggest that the influence of puQTLs on gene expression is tissue-specific, more prevalent than the effects of paQTLs and eQTLs.

puQTLs exhibit a robust capacity for transcriptional regulation

To delve into the transcriptional regulatory capabilities of puQTLs, we conducted a comprehensive investigation by assessing their enrichment in 15 chromatin markers spanning 14 different tissues. Our thorough analysis unveiled a noteworthy enrichment of puQTLs in active enhancer regions and TSS, underscoring their involvement in transcriptional regulation (Fig. 5a). Notably, we observed a remarkable enrichment of puQTLs in brain-related tissues specifically within enhancer regions distinguished by the H3K27ac mark, as well as in active promoter regions delineated by the H3K4me3 mark (Supplementary Fig. 9a). In addition to scrutinizing the concurrence of puQTLs with histone markers, we also explored their overlap with transcription factor (TF) binding sites. This extensive analysis enabled us to identify significantly enriched TFs, thus providing valuable insights into the impact of puQTLs on gene regulation (Supplementary Data 9, see “Methods” section). Intriguingly, a comparative analysis of the enriched TFs across different tissues exhibited a clustering effect, where tissues with similar functions—for instance, the colon, esophagus, and pancreas—manifested similar enriched TF profiles (Fig. 5b). Among the aforementioned findings, it is worth noting that puQTLs demonstrate substantial enrichment in motifs associated with zinc finger family genes (ZNF), a well-established class known to be intricately involved in the developmental and differentiation processes of numerous tissues36 (Fig. 5a). In the context of puQTLs exhibiting statistical significance in lung tissue, we note a substantial enrichment of ZNF binding motifs (P value < 1.0 × 10−4), such as SP1, KLF3, MAZ, and YY1. puQTLs influence gene promoter usage through long-range chromatin interactions

To further unravel the mechanistic basis underlying these observations, we integrated three-dimensional genomics data from HiChIPdb with histone marked ChIP-seq data from ENCODE. This integration allowed us to uncover the regulatory patterns of puQTLs on gene promoter usage. For example, we identified the SNP rs487174 as a regulatory variant of promoter 21197 in the PRDX1 gene. This gene is known to have a significant impact on the development and spread of lung cancers in humans41,42. In lung tissue, this promoter displayed both H3K4me3 and H3K27ac binding signals, suggesting its role as a potentially active transcriptional regulator (Fig. 5c). The puSNP rs487174 was located within H3K27ac-mediated chromatin interactions with promoter 21197 and had a significant impact on its utilization (Fig. 5d). Additionally, we observed that the SNP rs487174 did not have a significant effect on the activity of promoter 21197 in the PRDX1 gene (Fig. 5e). Further analysis revealed that the paSNP rs487174 was associated with decreased activity of another nearby promoter 21198 in the PRDX1 gene and promoter 113605 in the CCDC163 gene, consistent with the results in previous eQTL analysis43 (Fig. 5f, g). The redundancy observed in the results of paQTL and eQTL analyses suggests that the incorporation of puQTL analysis alongside paQTL analysis has the potential to reveal additional targets.

In this study, we found that the SNP rs7170818 has significant associations with different promoters of the CHSY1 gene in lung tissue through puQTL and paQTL analyses. The promoter 27819 exhibited active signals of H3K4me3 and H3K27ac, indicating its function as an active enhancer and promoter (Fig. 5h). Additionally, H3K27ac-mediated interactions between the promoter 27819 and the SNP rs7170818 were observed. The puSNP rs7170818 is significantly associated with increased usage of promoter 27819 in the CHSY1 gene (Fig. 5i). However, the statistical significance of the association between rs7170818 and the activity of promoter 27819 is not achieved (Fig. 5j). Additionally, our findings demonstrate that, within the scope of paQTLs, rs7170818 shows a significant association with the activity of the alternative promoter 27816 (Fig. 5k). However, we did not detect any chromatin loops between rs7170818 and the promoter 27816, nor did we observe significant binding signals of H3K4me3 and H3K27ac around the promoter region. This example highlights the potential benefit of defining puQTLs for major promoters, as it can aid in the identification of targets with greater confidence, supported by multi-omics data. The integration of these datasets substantiates our hypothesis that puQTLs exert their influence on gene promoter usage through long-range chromatin interactions between gene promoters and enhancers.

Furthermore, we identified a cluster of five SNPs in high LD associated with the promoter 49007 of the DUSP18 gene, an atypical member of the dual-specificity phosphatase family that acts as a critical modulator subsequent to acute lung injury44. These puSNPs were marked by H3K27ac and H3K4me1, suggesting their presence within enhancer regions. Additionally, long-range H3K27ac-mediated chromatin interactions were observed between these SNPs and the promoter 49007 in lung tissue (Supplementary Fig. 10a). These puSNPs exhibited significant associations with the upregulation of promoter 49007 usage. SuSiE fine-mapping analysis identified rs5753260 as the causal variant (Supplementary Fig. 10b, c). Collectively, rs5753260 may influence enhancer activity, therefore affecting the promoter usage of DUSP18 through enhancer–promoter interaction. This underscores the potential of integrating multi-omics data to identify functionally relevant genetic variants marked by epigenetic marks and long-range chromatin interactions.

Discussion

In this study, we have conducted a comprehensive analysis to elucidate the impact of puQTL on promoter usage across various human tissues. Recent research has revealed that the major promoter, often referred to as the dominant promoter, predominantly contributes to tissue-specific pre-transcriptional regulation of gene expression23. Alternative promoters contribute to isoform diversity. Choosing a different promoter can affect the coding sequence and result in changes to protein isoforms. In cancer, promoter-switching events have been found to cause major modifications in gene products. Nonetheless, the influence of genetic variations on major promoters during tissue development remains insufficiently investigated. By incorporating the fractional utilization of major promoters, our methodology provides a more refined and nuanced measure of promoter usage. This innovative approach holds great potential for uncovering deeper insights into the intricate mechanisms that govern gene expression regulation. Previous studies have primarily focused on paQTL analysis, which defines paQTL based on transcript level for each alternative promoter, resulting in increased complexity in the relationship between SNP and gene18,19. However, recent research suggests that the contribution of alternative minor promoters is limited and non-adaptive24. In our study, we have calculated the fractional promoter usage at the gene level, directly considering the major promoter, thus simplifying the SNP-promoter pairing relationship and providing a clearer understanding of the regulatory network.

In contrast to earlier investigations that primarily focused on examining traditional paQTL17,19,20,25, our analysis has unveiled a comprehensive set of distinct associations between SNPs and genes. By studying the associations between puQTL and genes, we have not only identified regulatory targets (Figs. 2e, g and 5d–e), but also revealed previously undiscovered regulatory patterns between SNPs and genes at the pre-transcriptional level (Fig. 5l and Supplementary Fig. 11). It is noteworthy that these associations primarily involve promoters located in transcriptional regulatory active regions and interact with SNPs through chromatin interactions47. This finding aligns with our observation that underscore the pivotal role of promoters in initiating gene transcription.

One notable characteristic of puQTL is its biological significance, as puQTL is associated with promoter selection, indicating involvement in pre-transcriptional processes. In contrast, paQTL, and puQTL focus on distinct aspects of gene regulation, with paQTL primarily concerning promoter expression levels. Notably, paQTL shows a strong association with eQTL. The reason for this correlation is that paQTL identifies genetic variants that impact promoter expression levels, while eQTL captures genetic variants that influence gene expression levels. This similarity in capturing genetic variants affecting gene expression levels establishes the correlation between paQTL and eQTL. These distinct classifications of QTLs have unique definitions and calculation methodologies, resulting in disparate target sets and regulatory patterns. Therefore, it is imperative to recognize that puQTLs and other QTLs are not interchangeable, as each provides additional insights into the functional implications of genetic variants.

Moving forward, we summarized three challenges in utilizing puQTLs to investigate the impact of genetic variants on tissue development. First, it is imperative to conduct further research to elucidate the underlying mechanisms through which puQTLs influence phenotypes. Our analysis has provided evidence of the enrichment of puQTLs in transcriptionally active regions, as indicated by the presence of epigenetic markers, TF binding motifs, and chromatin loops. Notably, we have found a significant number of zinc finger family motifs for puQTLs in lung tissue, which are involved in chromatin architecture formation36. This comprehensive result offers valuable insights into the regulatory mechanisms observed in diverse tissues, particularly in relation to transcriptional regulator binding. Second, it is important to note that puQTLs often exhibit tissue-specific effects, which may have implications for the development of diseases in different tissues. Therefore, a major focus for future research should be to map puQTLs in various disease-related cellular and physiological contexts. Third, as other studies have conducted analyses for cell type-specific QTLs of gene expression and splicing27,48, the cell type-specific puQTLs will be highly valuable for gaining a deeper understanding of the intricate interplay between genetic regulation and transcriptional control.

Methods

This study complies with all relevant ethical regulations. All human genotype and RNA-seq data utilized in our study were sourced from publicly available datasets provided by the GTEx project. The Ethical procedures for data collection, including informed consent and ethics approval, have been described in the original GTEx project documentations and publications49. No additional ethics review was required for our study49.

Genotype data processing

The genotyping data utilized in this study were obtained from the GTEx database of Genotypes and Phenotypes (dbGaP) website (phs000424.v9.p2)50. The raw data consisted of genotypes from 838 individuals and a total of 46,526,292 SNPs. To ensure data quality, certain filtering criteria were applied. Namely, SNPs with a call rate below 95%, a minor allele frequency (MAF) lower than 1%, and a Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium P value < 1 × 10−6 were excluded51. After the application of these filtering steps, a total of 10,127,526 variants remained for subsequent analysis. Additionally, for each tissue analyzed, only biallelic sites with an MAF above 1% and a minimum of 10 counts/allele were retained.

Estimation of promoter activity

Both the absolute and relative promoter activities were estimated using the proActiv tool with the junction read counts method2. The junction read counts data were obtained from the GTEx portal as per the official instructions (https://gen3.theanvil.io/explorer), and we collected junction read files of 15,201 samples in SJ.out.tab format. The genome annotation file utilized by proActiv was derived from GENCODE human release 26 annotation (https://www.gencodegenes.org/human/release_26.html). Following the estimation process, we delineated major promoters as those exhibiting the highest average activity across samples for each gene. The promoter usage in the subsequent analysis will be determined by the relative promoter activity value of the selected major promoter. It is worth noting that while there’s only one major promoter for a given gene, there can be multiple puQTLs associated with the usage of this major promoter.

Promoter usage collection and processing

The annotation of transcripts TSSs was from the GENCODE v26 file. Transcripts with the same or closely located TSSs were categorized as being regulated by the same promoter.

To facilitate further analysis, we prepared a promoter usage matrix to capture the relative usage of promoters across 49 tissues. To ensure data quality in each tissue, we defined a missing value if the promoter usage is absent in a promoter or an individual. First, we selected the major promoter with the highest average relative promoter usage across all samples for subsequent analysis. Then, for each promoter, we filtered out promoters with a missing value in more than 50% of individuals. Next, for each individual, we excluded those with missing values in more than 80% of promoters. We also filtered out promoters with a standard deviation equal to 0 across all samples. Finally, the resulting promoter usage values were scaled and normalized using quantiles for each tissue.

puQTL mapping for each tissue

In order to identify puQTLs in various tissues, we conducted an analysis on samples with both promoter utilization and genotype data. As the association between promoter usage and genotype data can be affected by numerous factors, such as population structure, age, gender, batch effects, sequencing technology, and donor, it is imperative to consider these covariates to enhance the sensitivity of QTL analyses. To eliminate the impact of population structure, we employed principal component analysis (PCA) facilitated by the Plink software (version 1.9)52,53 to assess the principal components of the genotype, determining the top five principal components as covariates. Additionally, latent and unobserved covariates for the calculated promoter usage values were ascertained through the utilization of the PEER software (version 1.0)54. Furthermore, gender and age were incorporated as known covariates. Consistent with the recommendation of the GTEx consortium, the selection of PEER factors was optimized based on sample size. Consequently, for sample size <150, 15 PEER factors were chosen, whereas for sample size ranging from 150 to 250, 30 PEER factors were selected, for sample size ranging from 250 to 350, 45 PEER factors were selected, and for sample size exceeding 350, 60 PEER factors were chosen.

Additionally, we conducted an analysis on cis-puQTLs, referring to SNPs that are located within 1 Mb of the promoters. The annotation coordinates for both promoters and SNPs were derived from the human reference genome GRCh38/hg38. The annotation file was derived from the GENCODE human release 26 annotation (https://www.gencodegenes.org/human/release_26.html). The statistical associations between each autosomal SNP and promoter were evaluated through linear regression analysis, employing the Matrix eQTL tool (version 2.3)55. To reduce potential confounding effects, we accounted for factors such as ancestry bias, sex, age, and other hidden variables that might influence promoter usage. To identify puQTLs, we set a significance threshold of false discovery rates (FDRs) <0.01, as calculated by Matrix eQTL, for each SNP-promoter pair.

LD-independent loci identification

We employed the “ld_clump” function within the R package plinkbinr (version 0.0.0.9000) to identify LD-independent loci. By utilizing PLINK’s “--clump” feature for LD clumping, we applied an LD threshold of r2 = 0.1 within a 2 Mb window to exclude SNPs in strong LD (command: --clump-r2 0.1 --clump-kb 2000). The LD calculations were conducted based on the reference panel of the 1000 Genomes Project.

HiChIP data integration

Incorporating HiChIP data has allowed for the consideration of tissue specificity in chromatin interactions. The manuscript provides detailed information on the tissues of puQTLs and the cell types of HiChIP data, along with the corresponding GEO accession numbers, as presented in Supplementary Data 3.

Classification of paQTL-associated genes

To streamline the paQTL analysis procedures, we have undertaken the task of categorizing paQTL-associated genes into five distinctive groups based on the specific impact the paQTLs have on the promoter region18. In Group 1, we identified paQTL-associated genes that possess only a single promoter. Group 2 comprises paQTL-associated genes that exhibit multiple promoters, but with a noteworthy association observed in only one promoter (FDR < 0.01). In Group 3, we found paQTL-associated genes characterized by significant associations with multiple promoters (FDR < 0.01), albeit with opposite directions of effect size. Moving on to Group 4, we identified paQTL-associated genes that display significant associations with multiple promoters (FDR < 0.01), and exhibit concordant directions of effect size with an effect size ratio <2. Lastly, Group 5 comprises paQTL-associated genes that show significant associations with multiple promoters (FDR < 0.01), and exhibit concordant directions of effect size with an effect size ratio >2.

Comparison between puQTLs and paQTLs or eQTLs

The study compared the lead SNP for the overlapping analysis. It looked at the overlap between puQTL-gene pairs and paQTL-gene pairs at the SNP-gene pair level. The overlap between puQTL and eQTL was evaluated at the gene level by comparing the genes associated with puQTL and eQTL to determine the number of overlapping genes. Additionally, for genes associated with both puQTL and eQTL, the LD r2 between lead eQTLs and puQTLs was calculated.

Enrichment analysis of puQTLs and eQTLs in genomic regions

In order to discern the positional preferences of puQTLs and eQTLs, we employed SnpEff (version 5.0)35 to classify them into functional categories based on the genomic locations. First, we calculated the proportion of genomic regions associated with each tissue. Subsequently, we examined the mean proportions of puQTLs and eQTLs across tissues within different annotated regions. To further validate our findings, we implemented bedtools (version 2.29.0) to randomly shuffle various genomic regions (e.g., promoters, 3′UTRs, and 5′UTRs)56. During the process of shuffling, it was ensured that the peaks obtained after shuffling had the same number and length as the original peaks. For each gene, we implemented a filtering process to selectively preserve transcripts featuring a predominant promoter. This method was employed to guarantee the retention of a singular set of promoters, 3′UTRs, and 5′UTRs for each gene, thereby reducing the potential influence of overlaps between neighboring genomic regions. The effect size, representing the disparity between the observed overlapped SNP number and the expected overlapped SNP number from 10,000 simulations, and empirical test was utilized to calculate the P value.

Tissue-specificity analysis of QTL

The statistical method employed in the tissue-specificity analysis was MASH (version 0.2.49)57. MASH utilizes Empirical Bayes methods to estimate the similarity of QTL effect patterns across different tissues58. To create a comprehensive puQTL list, we extracted the significant puQTLs from each tissue and collected the corresponding effect information of promoter-SNP pairs. It is important to note that only puQTLs with complete data across all tissues were retained for the MASH analysis. In the MASH model, the significance of each puQTL effect was calculated using the posterior distributions of Bayes’ theorem, specifically measured as the local false sign rate (LFSR). If the LFSR of a puQTL was significant (LFSR < 0.05) in at least one tissue, it was included in the subsequent analysis of pairwise tissue sharing based on both sign and magnitude.

Functional enrichment of puQTL

To assess the functional enrichment of puQTL, we acquired chromatin state data from various tissues, such as adrenal gland, artery, brain, colon, esophagus, heart, liver, ovary, pancreas, small intestine, spleen, and stomach, through the Roadmap Epigenomics project59. The chromatin states were categorized into 15 classes, including Active TSS (TssA), Flanking Active TSS (TssAFlnk), Transcribed at gene 5′ and 3′ (TxFlnk), Strong transcription (Tx), Weak transcription (TxWk), Genic enhancers (EnhG), Enhancers (Enh), ZNF genes & repeats (ZNF/Rpts), Heterochromatin (Het), Bivalent/Poised TSS (TssBiv), Flanking Bivalent TSS/Enh (BivFlnk), Bivalent Enhancer (EnhBiv), Repressed PolyComb (ReprPC), Weak Repressed PolyComb (ReprPCWk), and Quiescent/Low (Quies).

We conducted puQTL enrichment analysis using the GREGOR tool (version 2)60 to address potential bias resulting from variants in close LD with the functional variants. Initially, we prepared a bed-formatted input file containing information of significant puQTLs and chromatin states. GREGOR provided the observed and expected SNP counts for each chromatin state in each tissue, along with enrichment P values. To gauge the level of enrichment, we computed the odds ratio by dividing the number of observed SNPs by the number of expected SNPs. Additionally, we sought to validate the reliability of chromatin state enrichment identified by GREGOR by utilizing epigenomic data from ENCODE61. These data encompassed ChIP-seq information for H3K27ac, H3K4me1, H3K27me3, and super-enhancers. Fisher’s exact test was employed to examine enrichment through 2 × 2 contingency tables.

The effect of puQTL on transcription factor binding sites

We utilized motifBreakR (version 2.4.0)62, a tool for annotating the disruptive impact of puQTL on TF binding sites. Through this analysis, motifBreakR generates a comprehensive list of predicted TFs whose DNA sequence motifs have been disrupted by variants. We conducted separate analyses for all available genotypes and significant puQTLs. For each TF, we compared the number of puQTLs and non-puQTLs that disrupted the TF binding. To determine the significance of disruption in TF binding, we employed Fisher’s exact test. Subsequently, we identified the significant TFs in each tissue. To assess the TF binding patterns across tissues, we compared the significantly enriched TFs in pairwise tissues using the Jaccard similarity index.

Motif enrichment analysis

The identification of motif sequences that exhibit enrichment in promoter regions associated with puQTLs was accomplished using homer (version 4.11), employing default parameter settings63. Only motifs displaying significant enrichment (P value < 1.0 × 10−4) were retained, while motifs sharing similar sequence type, such as zinc finger, were chosen and presented in accordance with their ranked P values.

GWAS summary statistics

We compiled comprehensive summary statistics for a repertoire of 44 phenotypes obtained from prior investigations. These phenotypes encompass a wide range of diseases and physiological attributes occurring throughout the human lifespan, which can be readily classified into distinct categories, namely, anthropometric, blood, immune and allergy, cardiometabolic, psychiatric and neurological. The GWAS traits and corresponding references are listed in Supplementary Data 10.

Testing the enrichment of GWAS signals in puQTLs

Quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots were utilized to evaluate the enrichment of GWAS signals within distinct QTL types, including puQTL, paQTL, and eQTL. GWAS SNPs overlapping with puQTLs were identified and the Q–Q plot of GWAS P values for these SNPs were generated. A similar analysis was conducted for GWAS SNPs overlapping with paQTLs and eQTLs. Additionally, Q–Q plots of genome-wide GWAS P values were plotted as a control.

Colocalization of puQTL with GWAS traits

The identification of significant signals in GWAS that exhibit similar genetic effects with puQTLs was accomplished through the utilization of colocalization analysis. Specifically, we employed the approximate Bayes factor test within the Coloc (version 5.2.2) package64. To conduct the colocalization analysis of puQTL-promoter pairs, first we set a region located within 1 Mb proximity of the interested promoter and obtained all SNPs within the region from each GWAS trait and subsequently compared them with the complete puQTL set. The process of colocalization generated five posterior probabilities, consisting of PP0, PP1, PP2, PP3, and PP4. PP0 represents a scenario in which neither the trait-associated GWAS signals nor the puQTLs exhibit a genetic association in the region. PP1 signifies that only the GWAS signals exhibit a genetic association in the region, while PP2 indicates that only the puQTLs display a genetic association. On the other hand, PP3 denotes that both the GWAS signals and the puQTLs are associated, but with two independent SNPs. Lastly, PP4 indicates that both the GWAS signal and the puQTLs are associated with a shared single causal variant. The promoters were defined to be colocalized with trait GWAS signals when the posterior probability (PP4) exceeded 0.65. Subsequently, SNPs with a SNP.PP4 value >0.75 were deemed to be part of the shared causal variant set. To ensure the inclusion of only relevant SNPs, a common set of SNPs present in both puQTLs and GWAS signals was generated prior to LD score calculation. The LD score for each SNP in the shared causal variant set was then computed using LDlinkR (version 1.3.0)65.

Heritability and enrichment of puQTLs for GWAS complex traits

We used LD score regression (LDSC) (version 1.0.1) to assess the proportion and enrichment of heritability explained by puQTLs and eQTLs for complex traits46. For puQTL sets15, we set two thresholds: P value < 10−3 and 10−4. Initially, we generated annotation files for puQTLs and eQTLs using MAF, LD, and genomic element coordinates. We then included the baseline models, specifically the baseline v2.1 provided by LDSC14. To ensure precise and unbiased estimations of enrichment, we adjusted the baseline SNP annotations. Specifically, we excluded coding, UTR, and intron regions that may be correlated with the QTL features of interest. This modification was implemented to minimize potential confounding effects caused by functional annotation overlaps.

Fine-mapping of causal variants to puQTLs

In the first step, Plink software was employed to construct LD matrix for individual promoters containing a minimum of two puQTLs66. Subsequently, the SuSiE R package (version 0.12.10) was utilized to identify the causal variants linked to each promoter by analyzing the summary statistics of puQTLs for individual promoters through the susie_rss function, while explicitly setting estimate_prior_variance to FALSE67,68. Following this, the proportion of puQTLs demonstrating high causal effect (as indicated by a Posterior Inclusion Probability, PIP, of 0.5 or higher) for each promoter was computed across a comprehensive set of 49 tissue types.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine the sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses.
