Beyond initial care: a mixed-methods study of hospital-based health services for women and girls who have experienced sexual violence in Ethiopia
Authors: Sintayehu Abebe Woldie, Karen Block, Gregory Armstrong, Kristin Diemer, Mirgissa Kaba, Fikirte Geremew, Cathy Vaughan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess services provided by, and barriers to, hospital-based one-stop centre (OSC) services for women survivors of sexual violence in Ethiopia.DesignA mixed-methods study integrating quantitative service data with that from qualitative interviews.SettingHospital-based OSCs in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.ParticipantsHospital records of 2283 female survivors, in-depth interviews with 20 survivors and 17 key informants.ResultsQuantitative analysis of hospital records showed the majority (61.9%) of survivors seeking OSC care did so within 72 hours of experiencing sexual violence. Most received diagnostic sexually transmitted infection (STI) testing, emergency contraception and postexposure prophylaxis for HIV. Legal and psychosocial referrals were made for 93.5% and 68.4% of survivors, respectively, with 11.8% referred for antiretroviral therapy. The majority of survivors (79%) did not return for follow-up care, including for repeat STI and pregnancy testing. Qualitative findings highlighted women’s experiences of gaps in psychological support, mistrust in legal systems and structural barriers to ongoing care such as work constraints, safety concerns and transport challenges.ConclusionsWhile initial diagnostic services are largely implemented, significant gaps remain in preventive, therapeutic and follow-up care for survivors of sexual violence. This underscores the need to strengthen hospital-based OSCs in Ethiopia to provide comprehensive, survivor-centred care.Keywords: Gender-Based Violence, Africa South of the Sahara, Health Services, Health Services Accessibility

Background

Sexual violence has profound and multifaceted consequences for women’s physical, mental, sexual and reproductive health.1 These include physical trauma and injuries and sexually transmitted infections (STIs) including HIV.2,4 Survivors of sexual violence report higher rates of unintended pregnancy and greater unmet need for contraception compared with non-survivors.2 5 Sexual violence is also associated with mistimed pregnancies, pregnancy loss and other adverse reproductive outcomes.6 7

In addition to reproductive and physical health outcomes, sexual violence has profound mental health impacts. Survivors commonly report symptoms of psychological distress, including depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and panic episodes.3 8 9 Suicidal behaviours have also been reported among women who have experienced sexual violence.10 There is a bidirectional relationship, with mental disorders both resulting from and increasing vulnerability to sexual violence.11

Given these complex and often intersecting health outcomes, survivors of sexual violence require comprehensive, trauma-informed and integrated healthcare. Immediate care includes assessment of and treatment for physical injuries, provision of emergency contraception for prevention of pregnancy and management of the risk of infections such as tetanus and STIs, including postexposure prophylaxis (PEP) for HIV infection.12 Immediate care may also involve collection of forensic evidence for use in any subsequent legal proceedings, psychological first aid to address the acute emotional impact of the violence and safety planning.13 Follow-up is required to test for pregnancy, HIV and other infections. Long-term management focuses on treating potential sequelae such as infertility, dyspareunia (pain during intercourse), dysmenorrhoea (painful menstruation), providing surgical interventions for anatomical complications and provision of sustained mental healthcare, especially for survivors diagnosed with PTSD, depression, anxiety or suicidal ideation.14 15 Referral to specialist case management, social, protection and legal services may be required at any time from initial presentation.

Despite these significant healthcare needs, many survivors face barriers that deter them from accessing timely and appropriate health services. These barriers occur at individual, community, institutional and systemic levels, including stigma, fear of judgement, lack of trauma-informed providers and limited access to specialised care.16 17 Yet, gaining access to services is only part of the challenge. For many survivors, the care they receive even when services are accessed is often incomplete, inconsistent or misaligned with their actual needs as well as with facilities’ standard operating procedures. However, in many countries, including in sub-Saharan Africa, empirical evidence documenting these mismatches remains limited.

The prevalence of violence against women in Ethiopia is high, with 37% of ever-partnered women aged 15–49 years reporting physical and/or sexual violence from an intimate partner.4 The 2016 Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey also found that 10% of all women aged 15–49 had experienced specifically sexual violence at some point in their lives.18 In Ethiopia, hospital-based one-stop centres (OSCs) are the primary model through which health services for survivors of sexual violence are delivered. These centres are primarily embedded within the public health system and operate through multisectoral collaboration. The OSCs are designed to provide integrated, survivor-centred care—offering medical treatment, psychosocial support, legal referrals and coordination with law enforcement in a single location. Adapted from global models initially developed in Malaysia in 1994 and later expanded across Africa and Latin America,19 OSCs have been endorsed by Ethiopia’s Ministry of Health as a core strategy in the national response to sexual and gender-based violence,20 with guidelines for standardised operating procedures and provision of care developed.21 22 As at the end of 2025, a total of 95 OSCs had been established nationwide in Ethiopia, and 42 878 survivors of gender-based violence accessed services during the year, representing a 26% increase compared with the previous year.23

Despite this commitment to and growth in the use of OSCs in Ethiopia, research examining the alignment between the expressed health service needs of women who have experienced sexual violence and the services actually provided at OSCs in Ethiopia is very limited. This study contributes to addressing this evidence gap by exploring the extent to which holistic and responsive care is delivered to women who have experienced sexual violence, based on survivor narratives and facility records at three OSCs in Addis Ababa. To our knowledge, this is the first mixed-methods study in Ethiopia to systematically examine care provided at OSCs. The findings aim to inform the design of more integrated, accessible and survivor-centred service models.

Methods

Study design

A convergent mixed-methods design was employed for this study.24 Quantitative and qualitative data were collected and analysed separately, and then integrated during interpretation to provide a comprehensive understanding of service delivery. Although data collection occurred sequentially, integration of findings took place after completion of both analyses, consistent with a convergent design. Specifically, we draw on the following data in our analysis:

OSC patient records

Data were extracted from the de-identified records of all female survivors of violence attending any of three hospital-based OSCs in different sub-cities in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. Data analysed were from a 5-year period (January 2019 to December 2023). We developed the data extraction tool based on the study hospitals’ registration forms to capture relevant information including demographic variables, the relationship between victim and perpetrator, the type of health services provided and outcome/diagnosis, the nature of referrals made and follow-up appointments attended. Cross-sectional analysis of these data was used to generate descriptive statistics. Receipt of services was assessed against national standards of care. National guidelines outline a minimum package of care for survivors of sexual violence, which was used as a benchmark for analysis.22 This includes: (1) provision of emergency contraception for women of reproductive age within 72 hours to 5 days of exposure; (2) HIV testing, counselling and provision of PEP for eligible survivors presenting within 72 hours; (3) testing and treatment for STIs; (4) hepatitis B screening and vaccination for eligible survivors; (5) provision of psychological first aid and referral for psychosocial support and (6) routine follow-up to complete testing and care.

Primary qualitative data

Following preliminary analysis of the quantitative data, qualitative in-depth interviews were conducted with 20 women survivors of sexual violence and 17 key informants. The semistructured interview guides were developed based on the study objectives and informed by the Tanahashi framework of health service coverage, with questions mapped to key domains of service availability, accessibility, acceptability and effective coverage across the continuum of care. In addition, interviews examined survivors’ perspectives on the adequacy of services received at OSCs, their unmet health needs and help-seeking behaviours. The interview guides were developed through iterative discussions within the research team, including the primary author (SAW), senior researchers based in Australia (CV, KB, KD and GA) and in Ethiopia (MK), and a female Ethiopian research assistant with extensive experience in interviewing survivors of sexual violence (FG). The guides were reviewed to ensure cultural appropriateness, clarity and relevance to the study context, and were pretested with one participant and refined prior to data collection. The pretest interview was not included in the final analysis. The in-depth and key informant interview guides are provided as online supplemental material.

Survivors were recruited through OSCs. Health professionals at the OSCs were provided with information about the project and asked to share this with survivors over the age of 18 years who were not in active crisis, and for whom they assessed this was appropriate and safe. Interested potential participants could contact the primary interviewer (an experienced female research assistant and fluent speaker of Amharic) who would then obtain informed consent. Survivors were interviewed in person by a female member of the research team (FG), in Amharic, with interviews lasting an average of 50 min. Stakeholders were mapped, and key informants were purposively selected from this matrix to provide diverse perspectives. Key informants included national and subnational policymakers, and representatives from women’s services, UN agencies and non-governmental organisations (NGOs). Potential key informants were contacted via phone call and email. After obtaining informed consent, they were interviewed in person by a male member of the research team (SAW) in Amharic or English, with interviews lasting an average of 60 minutes.

All the interviews were audio recorded with participants’ permission. We conducted interviews until saturation was reached and no new themes were identified. Thematic saturation was assessed iteratively during data collection through repeated review of interview content, recurrence of key themes and the absence of substantially new concepts in later interviews. We acknowledge that saturation is a contested concept, but through repeated review of interview content and assessment of the recurrence of themes we also determined that we had collected sufficient information power to realise the aim of the study.25

Data analysis

The quantitative data were analysed using Stata V.18. Descriptive statistics were computed with results presented in tables and figures. Audio-recordings of the qualitative interviews were translated and transcribed, with transcripts imported into NVivo software. Thematic analysis was employed to identify and analyse patterns within the data.26 This process began with a thorough reading of the transcripts to generate preliminary codes. Initial coding was conducted by SAW, with codes developed both deductively from the study framework and inductively from the data. A subset of transcripts was independently coded by a second researcher MK, and reviewed by CV and KB, to ensure consistency in coding and interpretation. A wide range of codes was created to allow for diverse options in synthesising themes. Following the coding process, codes were sorted to develop overarching themes and subthemes. Coding and emerging themes were discussed iteratively by the entire research team, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. Themes were then reviewed to merge overlapping categories.

The findings from the quantitative and qualitative components of the study were triangulated and are presented together. Integration of quantitative and qualitative data was conducted using a merging approach, whereby findings from both data sources were brought together for comparison after separate analyses. Quantitative data were used to describe patterns of service utilisation, while qualitative data were used to contextualise and explain these patterns. Integration was achieved through a narrative weaving approach, in which quantitative and qualitative findings were presented together by theme to enable interpretation.27

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research.

Result

The results section begins with a summary of the sociodemographic characteristics of participants, followed by quantitative findings on service utilisation. These findings are integrated with qualitative data to explore care-seeking motivations, services received in relation to national standards, survivors’ experiences of care and barriers to the full continuum of care.

Overview of the study participants

Quantitative participants

Hospital records from 2283 female survivors of sexual violence were reviewed across three hospital-based OSCs in Addis Ababa: Gandhi Memorial, Menelik II Referral and Tirunesh Beijing General Hospitals. Approximately half of the records were from Gandhi Memorial Hospital. The majority of survivors (85.7%) resided in Addis Ababa. Ages ranged from 1 to 45 years, with a mean age of 14.6 years (SD 6.8). Survivors under 15 years constituted 43.9% of cases, while 37.3% were aged 15–19 years. Representation declined with increasing age, with only 0.4% of records belonging to women aged 40 or older.

The vast majority of survivors, 94.3%, were unmarried. Over half (52.1%) had completed elementary education, and 22.8% had no formal education (table 1). Most survivors (61.9%) seeking care following sexual violence did so within 72 hours of the incident.

Provision of care across key components of recommended services was incomplete

Analysis of OSC records indicates that diagnostic testing was consistently performed on initial presentation (table 4): 94.3% of survivors received HIV testing, 89.4% received syphilis Venereal Disease Research Laboratory testing (VDRL), and 79.4% received hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) testing. Among women of reproductive age, 94.4% underwent pregnancy testing.

Referrals were provided for 93.5% of survivors for legal support and for 68.4% of survivors for psychosocial services. However, key preventive and therapeutic interventions were delivered inconsistently: only 66.5% of eligible survivors received emergency contraception, 62.1% received HIV PEP within the recommended 72-hour window, and just 3.5% were provided hepatitis B vaccination. Among those eligible, only 11.8% were referred for antiretroviral therapy (ART).

Interpretation of these data should be approached with caution, as the reasons for non-receipt of services remain unclear. It is uncertain whether survivors declined the specific services or if providers failed to offer them. Nevertheless, these findings reveal a significant gap in the delivery of comprehensive service packages for survivors of sexual violence.

Emergency contraception

Hospital records indicate that among eligible women of reproductive age who presented within 5 days of the incident, only 66.5% received emergency contraception, meaning that approximately one-third did not receive this critical intervention. This suggests that timely access to pregnancy prevention is not consistently ensured, despite early presentation of survivors.

Postexposure prophylaxis

This study showed that 327 (37.89%) survivors of penetrative rape who presented within 72 hours and tested HIV-negative did not receive PEP. This indicates a critical gap in the delivery of time-sensitive HIV prevention services.

Sexually transmitted infections

Among survivors with a positive VDRL test, only 38.5% of survivors received treatment. Furthermore, among those who tested negative for HBsAg, only 3.5% received the hepatitis B vaccine. These findings highlight inconsistencies in the provision of essential infection prevention and treatment services.

Follow-up care

Routine follow-up is recommended to complete testing and care. However, only 21% of women returned for subsequent visits. While the majority of survivors received HIV, STI and pregnancy testing at their initial visit, given that most attended the OSC within the first 72 hours of an incident of sexual violence, follow-up testing would be required for conclusive results. This reflects a major gap in continuity of care, limiting the effectiveness of initial testing and treatment.

Psychosocial care and support

Supporting survivors’ emotional and social recovery requires the provision of psychological first aid and timely referral to psychosocial services. However, in this study, only 884 survivors (38.7%) received psychological first aid, and 721 (31.6%) did not receive any referral for psychosocial support. This suggests that the psychosocial needs of survivors are not adequately addressed through current services.

Overall, these findings highlight critical gaps in the implementation of recommended health services for survivors of sexual violence. While initial testing protocols were well adhered to, the data reveal gaps in the delivery of essential preventive and follow-up interventions.

Survivors’ experiences of care suggest considerable unmet needs

This study showed that when survivors accessed OSCs, there were considerable gaps in their experiences of care. Services were not meeting survivors’ comprehensive needs, which is consistent with quantitative findings indicating incomplete provision of key services, including limited follow-up care. Qualitative findings revealed that many survivors were satisfied with initial services, particularly HIV testing, pregnancy prevention and general clinical assessments as this met their motivation for seeking care:

I was given medication within the first 24 hours and received post-exposure HIV prophylaxis every day for 28 days. Yes, my expectation was met; I received everything I needed. (Survivor, late 20s)

I expected to do pregnancy and disease tests, but I received more. For instance, the psychiatrist talked to me properly, and he assured me that I would meet him when I needed. (Survivor, early 20s)

However, several unmet needs were identified, particularly around legal and mental health services.

First, a significant concern was the legal process. Many survivors expressed frustration with law enforcement, noting that a lack of trust in the police discouraged them from pursuing legal action.

The problem is with the police inspector’s legal investigation; she could not trust me and thought I was lying. This issue of not being trusted is driving me to quit the legal case against the offender. (Survivor, early 20s)

In addition, key informants noted that survivors’ experience of co-located legal services could affect their willingness to continue accessing health services:

Maybe some health professionals or police inspectors are being obstinate about the incident and become aggressive, which could make the victims quit the legal process and discontinue the treatments. Even though it is rare, I have observed that the child victims were scared of the police inspector, and they would like to discontinue suing the perpetrator and quit the medications as well. (Service provider)

These experiences suggest that disbelief and insensitive questioning by police personnel could intensify survivors’ distress and discourage continued engagement with co-located health and other support services.

Psychological support emerged as another critical unmet need among survivors, with some survivors reporting no psychosocial care at all on their initial presentation at an OSC.

However, I didn’t receive psychological support because it was at night. (Survivor, early 20s)

Key informants echoed survivors’ concerns, noting the challenges in providing psychological support due to the shortage of full-time psychologists in all the OSCs included in this study. A key informant explained:

Sometimes the victims might receive an appointment to receive mental health counseling if the psychologist who works on call is not available. In addition, the victims might not get counseling if the psychologists are already occupied with other patients. (Service provider)

Follow-up care was limited despite the need for ongoing testing and support

About four out of five survivors (79%) did not return for follow-up appointments, reflecting systemic challenges in care continuity. Qualitative data highlighted intersecting barriers to follow-up, which clustered around three domains: work-related constraints, economic hardship and safety risks.

Occupational constraints

Survivors frequently cited inflexible work schedules and concerns about disclosure to employers as deterrents to attending follow-up appointments. For example, one participant explained:

“There is no different reason other than my fear of telling my employers about my visit to the centre, which could make me skip the appointment date. I worry that when the appointment visits are frequent, my employer might think I have a disease.” (Survivor, late adolescence)

Another participant noted:

“Yes, one time I skipped the HIV/AIDS examination schedule.[I skipped going to the OSC] due to juggling legal process going in the court and concurrent work burden at the factory. Especially, the factory manager was not happy about my repeated absence from work.” (Survivor, early 20s)

Transportation issues

logistical challenges such as long distances, inadequate transportation and financial constraints limited survivors’ ability to attend follow-up appointments. One young woman remarked

Yes, if the victim has no money to pay for transportation, she could miss the health care visit. Additionally, forgetting an appointment date and a lack of support from parents could also make you miss the visit. (Survivor, late adolescence)

Another survivor added:

Distance from the house and the inability to find transportation can be reasons to drop out of services. Moreover, lack of money for transportation, and not knowing about the service benefits. (Survivor, late 20s)

Safety concerns

Fears of retaliation or further harm discouraged survivors from returning for follow-up care. One participant stated:

Other factors, like fear of a second-time attack or harm by the rapist, if he is not imprisoned, and if the perpetrator knows my appointment date, could also make me skip the visit. (Survivor, late adolescence)

A key informant further explained the ways limited services to ensure women’s safety undermined access to health care following sexual violence:

The challenges include a shortage of shelter, a lack of safeguards, changing the house, and not having an attendant, which could make it difficult to follow the treatment. In addition, the victims do not follow up on the appointments due to the fear that the perpetrators will attack them again. (Survivor, late adolescence)

Overall, the integrated findings suggest that OSCs served as important entry points for initial care but continuity of support was affected by gaps in psychological support, mistrust in legal systems and structural barriers to ongoing care including work constraints, safety concerns and transport challenges. Discussion

This mixed-methods study examined what happens within OSCs after survivors arrive, how service delivery processes unfold and why care attrition occurs from diagnostic testing through treatment, prevention, referral and follow-up. Quantitative data about OSC services provided were triangulated with the qualitative perspectives of survivors of sexual violence, service providers and policymakers to provide new evidence on the alignment between the healthcare needs of sexual violence survivors and the health services actually delivered. Despite high coverage of diagnostic services, the study identified significant gaps in preventive and curative care, psychological support, legal assistance and follow-up services.

While diagnostic tests for HIV (94.3%), syphilis (89.4%) and HBsAg (79.4%) were provided to the majority of survivors, the lack of follow-up testing may undermine testing effectiveness and subsequent provision of indicated treatment. Preventive measures such as emergency contraception, PEP and hepatitis B vaccination were provided to only 66.5%, 62.1% and 3.5% of eligible survivors, respectively, suggesting gaps in initial care. Similarly, referrals for psychosocial support and ART were offered to only 68.4% and 11.8% of eligible survivors, respectively. These findings are in line with previous studies in Ethiopia which have shown that while diagnostic services are well implemented, the provision of treatment and comprehensive care remains inadequate.28,31 Our study confirms substantial gaps remain in the delivery of comprehensive care for survivors of sexual violence with OSCs failing to adhere to both national and global recommended care guidelines.13 21 22 These findings are consistent with recent evidence indicating that care for survivors of sexual violence in low-resource settings remains inconsistent and fragmented across key service components.32

The high proportion of survivors receiving initial diagnostic testing is consistent with survivors’ primary motivation for seeking care which was largely to determine whether they had contracted infections following sexual violence. Consequently, follow-up attendance may decline once initial test results are negative, either due to the perception that no further care is needed or because of concerns about privacy and stigma associated with returning for additional services.33 It is unclear whether survivors received follow-up testing at facilities other than OSC though survivor interviews suggest this is unlikely.

Furthermore, the qualitative findings reveal critical unmet needs in both legal and psychological support for survivors of sexual violence. Many survivors expressed frustration with law enforcement and a pervasive lack of trust in the legal process. In a number of instances, this drove survivors to not only quit the legal process but also their health service follow-up. Participants described inadequate psychological support at busy OSCs and a shortage of full-time psychologists. Given the majority of survivors interviewed reported that their experience of sexual violence had led to significant mental health harms including suicidal ideation, there is an urgent need to strengthen legal and psychosocial support within comprehensive care programmes in Ethiopia.31

Although national guidelines mandate follow-up care for all survivors, analysis of OSC patient records shows that nearly 79% do not return after their initial contact with the service. Previous research in low-resource settings similarly highlights persistent deficiencies in follow-up care, underscoring the challenges that undermine implementation of comprehensive sexual violence services despite their essential role in long-term recovery.32 This is in contrast to evidence from upper-middle and high-income countries, where follow-up rates among survivors of sexual violence are substantially higher.34 35 Our findings highlight the ways that survivors face overlapping structural barriers to follow-up in resource-constrained settings such as Ethiopia. Targeted interventions may be required to raise survivors’ awareness of the importance of follow-up (including repeat testing for pregnancy and STIs) and to address structural barriers. Survivors reported being unable to disclose their need for healthcare to employers, in part because of the stigma associated with sexual assault. This meant they were unable to seek healthcare during work hours, frequently resulting in missed appointments. In addition, logistical challenges including long distances, inadequate transportation and financial constraints further hindered survivors' ability to return to OSCs. Safety concerns also played a critical role, with survivors expressing fear of retaliation or further harm from perpetrators if learned of their appointment schedules. These challenges may also reflect broader health system constraints, including limited resources and gaps in provider training, as highlighted in previous research.32 Investment in health systems approaches to maximising follow-up (such as case management, active referral, flexible hours of service delivery including mental health services) is required to ensure health services have the capacity to offer flexible, survivor-centred continuity of care.

Collectively, the results of this study indicate that hospital-based OSCs in Ethiopia are delivering only a partial response to the complex health, legal and psychosocial needs of sexual violence survivors. Survivors interviewed for this study expressed gratitude to OSCs and, in the main, were positive about their interactions with healthcare workers. However, their unmet need for healthcare remains substantial. While initial testing services are robust, the failure to ensure continuity, comprehensiveness and survivor trust limits the long-term effectiveness of the care model.

This interpretation should be considered in light of several methodological strengths and limitations. A key strength of this study is the use of a mixed-methods design, integrating quantitative hospital records with qualitative data from survivors, service providers and stakeholders, with the lived experience of survivors and service providers providing an explanatory narrative to frame findings from the analysis of patient records. The relatively large quantitative sample enhances the robustness of the findings. However, the study was conducted in three urban, hospital-based OSCs, which may limit generalisability to rural or non-tertiary settings. The sample of survivor interviewees was restricted to adult female survivors, excluding adolescent girls and male survivors. Boys and men are also harmed by experiences of sexual violence, but their experiences and needs may differ and were not explored in this study36 . In addition, the reliance on facility-based recruitment means that the perspectives of individuals who did not access services are not represented in this study. Finally, the study design does not permit causal inference.

Conclusions

The findings from our study have important implications for policy and practice. Policy efforts should focus on resourcing OSCs such that they can adhere to standard operating procedures and deliver integrated services that include not only diagnostic but also critical therapeutic services. Practice improvements should involve reorienting OSCs to offer flexible scheduling, transportation support and enhanced safety measures, alongside targeted training for healthcare providers to address legal and psychosocial support needs.

Hospital-based One-Stop Centres are a central component of the response to sexual violence in Ethiopia. Our study demonstrates, however, that they operate in a context of substantial structural barriers to care and in an overall healthcare system experiencing constraints in workforce and supplies. Therefore, substantial gaps persist in the delivery of comprehensive care to women who have survived sexual violence in Ethiopia. In a country with a high prevalence of sexual violence, addressing these gaps is a crucial public health and human rights priority.
