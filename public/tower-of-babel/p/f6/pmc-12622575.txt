Early postoperative factors associated with pseudoarthrosis at 24 months following anterior cervical discectomy and fusion
Authors: Hangeul Park, Yunhee Choi, Woojin Kim, Jungbo Sim, Ho Sung Myeong, Jun-Hoe Kim, Chang-Hyun Lee, Chi Heon Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pseudoarthrosis after anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) may lead to clinical deterioration and revision surgery. This study aimed to evaluate early risk factors to determine their associations with pseudoarthrosis at 24 months following ACDF. Patients who underwent ACDF for degenerative cervical spine disease at a single institution were retrospectively analyzed. Pseudoarthrosis was defined as ISM (interspinous motion) ≥ 1 mm at 24 months after ACDF. Among segments with ISM ≥ 1 mm at 6 months postoperatively, we conducted multivariate logistic regression analysis, including patients’ baseline clinical factors and radiological variables measured at 6 months, to identify those associated with pseudoarthrosis. We also determined the optimal ISM cutoff at 6 months for the identification of pseudoarthrosis at 24 months. Pseudoarthrosis occurred in 21 of 120 segments (17.5%). In multivariate analysis, ISM at 6 months was independently associated with pseudoarthrosis (odds ratio 2.81; 95% confidence interval 1.08–7.28; P = .033). Receiver operating characteristic analysis determined the optimal ISM cutoff at 6 months for identifying pseudoarthrosis is 4.0 mm (sensitivity 74%, specificity 95%). ISM cutoff of 4.0 mm at 6 months postoperatively can effectively identify pseudoarthrosis at 24 months. Early identification of patients at high risk for pseudoarthrosis may facilitate tailored follow-up and timely interventions, potentially optimizing clinical outcomes. Large-scale, prospective validation is warranted.

1. Introduction

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is a widely performed surgical procedure used to treat cervical spondylosis, disc herniation resulting in nerve compression, or cervical instability caused by trauma.[1,2] The primary goals of ACDF are to alleviate neurological symptoms and achieve stable interbody fusion.[3] Fusion following ACDF generally occurs within 24 months, but pseudoarthrosis can still occur.[4–6] Although the fusion rate of ACDF is reported to be relatively high, the incidence of pseudoarthrosis has been variably reported, ranging up to 20% in single-level procedures and up to 50% in multilevel procedures.[7,8] Pseudoarthrosis can lead to severe clinical deterioration in patients and may occasionally require revision surgery.[9] Therefore, if pseudoarthrosis can be identified early, it would allow for more meticulous planning of patient follow-up and therapeutic interventions. In the evaluation of fusion, the use of dynamic X-rays with an interspinous motion (ISM) threshold of <1mm is widely employed due to its simplicity compared with traditional computed tomography (CT) methods while providing reliable sensitivity and specificity comparable to CT-based bone bridging assessments.[10–12] Despite these advantages, the strict criterion of ISM < 1 mm at 6 months postoperatively might lead to false-positive cases of pseudoarthrosis, as fusion maturation can still occur beyond this early postoperative stage.[4,5] Previous studies have mainly focused on identifying demographic and surgical risk factors associated with pseudoarthrosis, such as patient age, smoking status, diabetes, and multilevel surgery, but the relationship between radiographical indicators measured early postoperatively and subsequent pseudoarthrosis remains under-investigated.[13,14] Thus, the purpose of this study was to identify clinical factors and radiological factors measured at 6 months postoperatively that are associated with pseudoarthrosis diagnosed at 24 months following ACDF.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and patient selection

All patients who underwent ACDF at a single tertiary referral center between January 2017 and June 2022 were retrospectively included and analyzed in this study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients aged 18 years or older, patients who underwent ACDF for degenerative cervical spine disease, and patients with a minimum follow-up period of 12 months. The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients who underwent combined posterior approach, patients who underwent revision surgery, patients with tic disorders, and patients without postoperative dynamic X-rays. Ultimately, 86 patients and 120 segments were included in this study (Fig. 1).

2.2. Ethical statement

Seoul National University Hospital institutional review board waived the requirement for informed consent and approved the study protocol and chart review (Approval No. H-2407-101-1553). All investigations were conducted in accordance with our institutional review board of guidelines and regulations.

2.3. Data collection

Through a retrospective review of medical records, data on age at the time of surgery, sex, body mass index, history of smoking, comorbidities, surgery level, materials used for interbody fusion, and use of plates were collected.

2.4. Radiological assessment

The radiographs were magnified 1.5 times, and the C2-7 Cobb angle, segmental Cobb angle, ISM, and T1 slope were measured. Radiological parameters were assessed at 6, 12, and 24 months postoperatively. At 24 months, fusion was defined by an ISM < 1 mm, with all segments failing to meet this criterion classified as pseudoarthrosis (ISM ≥ 1 mm).[15] Importantly, segments that already demonstrated ISM < 1 mm at 12 months were regarded as fused, even if subsequent 24-month X-rays were not available. ISM was assessed on dynamic X-rays magnified by at least 150% to enhance measurement precision. Two reproducible landmarks were placed at the tips of the spinous processes bounding the index level, ensuring that both landmarks were clearly identifiable on the flexion and extension views. The interspinous distance was defined as the linear distance between these 2 landmarks, and ISM was calculated as the difference in interspinous distance between flexion and extension.[11,16] (Fig. S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q664).

2.5. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analyses were conducted, with continuous variables presented as the median and range, and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test. Statistical significance was defined as a P value of <.05. Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with pseudoarthrosis. For multivariate analysis, variables with a P value of <.1 in univariate analysis were selected using a forward selection method. A segment-level analysis was used as the base model. To account for the nonindependence of segments within the same patient due to clustering effects, a generalized estimating equation model was applied to adjust for intra-patient clustering effects. In both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses, statistical significance was defined as a P value of <.05, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) that did not include 1.0. The discriminative ability for pseudoarthrosis was assessed using the receiver operating characteristic curve and the area under the curve. The optimal cutoff value for classifying pseudoarthrosis was determined using Youden index. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary ).

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics

The median age of the patients was 57 years, with 54 males (62.8%). The median body mass index was 25.0, and 16 patients (18.6%) had a history of smoking. Additionally, 16 patients (18.6%) had diabetes mellitus. Single-level surgery was the most common, performed in 58 patients (67.4%). Two-level surgery was performed in 23 patients (26.7%). Three-level and 4-level surgeries were performed in 4 patients (4.7%) and 1 patient (1.2%), respectively. Autograft bone (iliac bone) was used as the fusion material in 32 patients (37.2%), and plate-assisted fusion was performed in 79 patients (91.9%). The median C2-7 Cobb angle was −10.4°, the median segmental angle at the index level was −2.0°, and the median T1 slope was 26.1°. The median follow-up duration was 25.1 months (Table 1).

3.2. Time-course of segmental fusion status

At postoperative 6 months, 66 segments (55%) were identified as ISM ≥ 1 mm. Upon reevaluation at postoperative 12 months, 32 segments (26.7%) remained ISM ≥ 1 mm. Further assessment at postoperative 24 months revealed that 21 segments (17.5%) were classified as pseudoarthrosis (Fig. 2).

Time-course of segmental fusion status. At 6 months, 66 of 120 segments (55%) exhibited intersegmental motion (ISM) ≥ 1 mm; this proportion declined to 32 segments (26.7%) at 12 months, and by 24 months, 21 segments (17.5%) met the criteria for pseudoarthrosis. (PO; postoperative, M; months).

3.3. Radiological findings of ISM ≥ 1 mm segments at 6 months

The 66 segments identified at 6 months were ISM ≥ 1 mm. In both the fusion and pseudoarthrosis groups, the C5-6 level was most frequently involved (53.3% vs 47.6%), followed by the C6-7 level (24.4% vs 33.3%). ISM was significantly larger in pseudoarthrosis, with a median of 5.0 mm, compared with 2.2 mm in the fusion group (P < .001). Similarly, segmental motion was greater in pseudoarthrosis (median 5.8°) than in the fusion group (median 2.2°, P < .001). In contrast, C2-7 Cobb angle and T1 slope showed no significant differences between the 2 groups (Table 2).

3.4. Factors associated with pseudoarthrosis among segments with ISM ≥ 1 mm at 6 months

In the univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses, segments with ISM ≥ 1 mm at 6 months were included, along with demographic and surgical factors, to identify associations with pseudoarthrosis diagnosed at 24 months. In the univariate logistic regression analysis, multilevel surgery (odds ratio [OR] 3.36, 95% CI 1.03–10.95, P = .044), ISM at 6 months (OR 3.72, 95% CI 1.83–7.56, P < .001), and segmental motion at 6 months (OR 1.91, 95% CI 1.37–2.65, P < .001) were identified as significant factors associated with pseudoarthrosis. In the multivariate analysis, ISM at 6 months remained the only statistically significant factor associated with pseudoarthrosis (OR 2.81, 95% CI 1.08–7.28, P = .033) (Table 3).

4. Discussion

In this study, we analyzed clinical and radiological factors associated with pseudoarthrosis in segments that showed ISM ≥ 1 mm at 6 months following ACDF. The overall pseudoarthrosis rate among all segments was 17.5%. ISM at 6 months was identified as an independent risk factor. The optimal ISM cutoff for pseudoarthrosis is 4.0 mm, with a sensitivity of 74% and a specificity of 95%.

Achieving solid fusion following ACDF is a crucial factor in symptom relief and functional recovery after surgery.[3,14] Fusion following ACDF begins immediately after surgery, following a saturation curve, with the fusion rate approaching a plateau by the second postoperative year. As a result, the majority of ACDF fusions are achieved within 2 years after surgery.[4,5] Segments that do not achieve fusion by the second year after ACDF are unlikely to achieve fusion by the fifth postoperative year as well.[9] The incidence of pseudoarthrosis following ACDF varies widely across the literature.[13,14,17] Most cases of pseudoarthrosis following ACDF are asymptomatic; however, symptomatic pseudoarthrosis is typically managed through revision surgery.[13,18] Lee et al[9] reported that in patients who underwent ACDF with a minimum follow-up of 5 years, the nonunion group experienced worsening neck pain, arm pain, and Neck Disability Index starting at postoperative 2 years, resulting in poorer clinical outcomes compared with the union group at the 5-year follow-up.

Various modalities and parameters are used in the radiological diagnosis of pseudoarthrosis, among which X-rays are widely employed as a primary tool due to their ease of access, relatively low cost, and reduced radiation exposure.[19] On plain X-rays, the formation of osseous bridging between the graft and vertebral body can be considered indicative of fusion, whereas the absence of osseous bridging and the presence of a halo or lucency around the graft can be considered indicative of pseudoarthrosis. However, assessing bony morphology in the presence of an implant is less accurate, which is why dynamic X-rays are regarded as a more reliable assessment tool.[3] ISM is widely used to evaluate fusion following ACDF.[12] Song et al[10] reported that defining fusion as ISM <1 mm, measured on dynamic X-rays magnified to 150%, demonstrated high accuracy. Furthermore, the criterion of ISM <1 mm showed a similar accuracy to fusion assessed by bone bridging formation on CT.[11] In contrast, the Cobb angle is less reliable and consistent, making it a limited tool for assessing fusion.[3]

As fusion rarely occurs beyond 2 years after ACDF and pseudoarthrosis can lead to symptom deterioration, the early identification of patients at high risk of pseudoarthrosis is clinically important. According to previous studies, there was a discrepancy between ISM on dynamic X-rays and findings on CT scans when solid fusion was assessed during revision surgeries, and the use of ISM < 1 mm was recommended as a reliable indicator for clinically successful fusion.[10,11] However, applying ISM < 1 mm at the early postoperative time may lead to an overdiagnosis of pseudoarthrosis. In this study, we showed that more conservative criterion of ISM 4.0 mm at 6 months would be acceptable in identifying pseudoarthrosis at 24 months after ACDF. By using lenient ISM criteria at 6 months to identify patients with a high risk of pseudoarthrosis at 24 months following ACDF, additional early intervention can be considered based on the patient’s symptoms, and a more proactive follow-up strategy can be implemented including short-term follow-up with repeated dynamic X-rays, CT to confirm bone bridging or peri-screw halo, early correction of modifiable risk factors.

5. Limitations

This study is a retrospective analysis conducted at a single institution, and thus, several limitations exist. First, the study’s retrospective design, limited sample size, and relatively short follow-up period may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Second, because ISM is quantified at the millimeter scale, the measurements may have been susceptible to human measurement error. Third, locked pseudoarthrosis may be included in the fusion due to the use of ISM criteria. Fourth, the absence of differentiation between symptomatic and asymptomatic pseudoarthrosis, as well as the lack of evaluation of other complications such as adjacent segment disease, may affect the comprehensiveness of the analysis. Fifth, although dynamic X-rays were obtained without a cervical collar in all patients, suboptimal cooperation or neck pain may have limited maximal flexion–extension, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true ISM. Finally, several patient-level factors that could influence fusion, such as overall comorbidity burden, frailty status, and paraspinal muscle quality, were not assessed and thus were not incorporated into the analysis. Future prospective studies with longer follow-up periods and larger sample sizes will be necessary to validate these findings further and to explore additional associated factors for pseudoarthrosis in ACDF.

6. Conclusion

Although an ISM cutoff of <1 mm is commonly used to assess fusion after ACDF, its application in the early postoperative period may result in an overdiagnosis of pseudoarthrosis. Our findings indicate that an ISM cutoff of 4.0 mm at 6 months postoperatively can effectively classify pseudoarthrosis at 24 months, achieving a sensitivity of 74% and a specificity of 95%. Early identification of patients at high risk for pseudoarthrosis may facilitate tailored follow-up and timely interventions, potentially optimizing clinical outcomes. Larger-scale prospective studies are required to further validate these findings.
