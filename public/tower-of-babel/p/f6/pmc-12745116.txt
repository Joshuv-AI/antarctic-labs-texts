Real-world perioperative outcomes of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in non–small cell lung cancer
Authors: Giorgio Caturegli, Michael F Kaminski, Maureen Canavan, Oluwaseun F Ayoade, Benjamin J Resio, Daniel J Boffa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveApproximately 30% of non–small cell lung cancers will recur after surgery, highlighting a need for additional perioperative therapies. Several recent clinical trials demonstrated a reduction in non–small cell lung cancer recurrence with the use of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy. Recognizing that trial results may not always be replicated in the general population, our objective was to evaluate perioperative outcomes of immunotherapy in the real-world setting.MethodsAdult patients diagnosed with clinical stage I to III non–small cell lung cancer in the National Cancer Database between 2018 and 2022 were included. Perioperative outcomes were evaluated in the following groups: neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, neoadjuvant chemotherapy, or neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy.ResultsOverall, 3956 patients were identified, including 1051 treated with neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (33.2%), 1590 treated with chemotherapy (40.1%), and 1315 treated with neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (26.5%). The pneumonectomy rate after induction chemoimmunotherapy was 6.2%, which was lower than after chemotherapy (10.0%, P = .001) but similar to after chemoradiotherapy (7.9%, P = .11). Postoperative 90-day mortality among patients receiving chemoimmunotherapy was less than 1.0%, which was lower than both chemotherapy (2.0%, P < .001) and chemoradiotherapy (3.5%, P < .001). Nodal downstaging was seen in 43.9% of patients receiving chemoimmunotherapy. The pathologic complete response rate was 30.2%, which was higher than chemotherapy (9.1%, P < .001) but comparable to chemoradiotherapy (26.8%, P = .13). Results from adjusted logistic regressions were consistent with findings of unadjusted analyses.ConclusionsReal-world outcomes suggest that the reassuring safety and impressive downstaging effect of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy seen in clinical trials can be reproduced in the general population with non–small cell lung cancer. Further study to understand the impact of perioperative chemoimmunotherapy in the real-world setting is justified.Key Words: chemotherapy, immunotherapy, non–small cell lung cancer, radiation, surgery

Central Message

The favorable safety profile and downstaging effect of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy reported in clinical trials are observed in the real-world NSCLC population.

Perspective

This study investigates the perioperative outcomes of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy for NSCLC because trial outcomes are not always replicated in the general population. We report impressive safety and downstaging outcomes consistent with trial results, emphasizing the need for further study on the long-term impact of perioperative chemoimmunotherapy in the real-world setting.

Lung cancer is a prevalent and aggressive malignancy, with an estimated 226,000 new cases and 125,000 deaths per year in the United States.1 Approximately 25% of newly diagnosed non–small cell lung cancers (NSCLCs) can be surgically resected with curative intent.2,3 Unfortunately, 30% of patients with resected NSCLC have recurrence, most of whom ultimately die of their disease.4, 5, 6, 7 This trend suggests that a significant portion of surgically managed patients with NSCLC harbor subclinical disease that is incompletely addressed by resection.

To address residual postoperative microscopic disease, perioperative cytotoxic chemotherapy regimens have been implemented, historically improving 5-year survival by approximately 5%.8, 9, 10, 11 More recently, neoadjuvant and adjuvant approaches have focused on immunotherapy, based on the impressive success of immune checkpoint inhibitors in the stage IV NSCLC population. Several landmark phase III randomized controlled trials have demonstrated the safety and efficacy of adding immunotherapy to neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimens, with high pathologic complete response (pCR) rates of approximately 20%,12, 13, 14, 15 and a recent meta-analysis of 43 trials reported 5.5-fold odds of pCR with neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy.16 Subsequently, neoadjuvant or perioperative chemoimmunotherapy has become a standard approach to patients with locoregionally confined NSCLC.17

Clinical trial outcomes, however, are not always reproduced in the general population. For example, a real-world analysis of first-line pembrolizumab for stage IV NSCLC demonstrated significantly inferior overall survival in a general population with cancer as compared to a clinical trial cohort.18 There are often differences in the characteristics of patients treated in the general population and those treated in clinical trials, which often include younger and healthier patients with specific tumor attributes. Many patients with NSCLC may not be eligible for trials that define the “best practices” relating to a given clinical scenario.19, 20, 21, 22, 23 Moreover, compliance with intended treatment can be lower in the general population.24,25 As such, it is important to characterize and understand neoadjuvant immunotherapy outcomes in the real world.

The National Cancer Database (NCDB) is one of the largest cancer registries in the world and captures highly detailed patient, tumor, and treatment information for approximately 72% of cancers in the United States.26 The NCDB has been widely leveraged to represent the real-world perspective of cancer care, including the use of immunotherapy in NSCLC.27 In this study, patients with clinical stage I to III NSCLC in the NCDB were studied to assess real-world perioperative outcomes and response rates to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy.

Material and Methods

Data Source and Elements

The NCDB is a joint program of the Commission on Cancer and the American Cancer Society that includes patient demographic, tumor, treatment, and outcome data for 72% of newly diagnosed cancer cases, including 65% of newly diagnosed NSCLC cases, in the United States.28,29 The 2022 Participant User File,30 which contains deidentified patient information, was used for this retrospective cohort study conducted in accordance with STROBE guidelines. This study was approved by the Yale Institutional Review Board with waived patient consent (Clinical and Molecular Predictors of Survival in Thoracic Malignancies, IRB #1103008160, 3/21/2011).

Patient Selection

Eligible patients were aged 18 years or older and diagnosed between 2018 and 2022 with clinical stage I, II, or III NSCLC. Patients receiving any palliative therapies were excluded, as were those receiving both radiation and immunotherapy preoperatively. Eligible patients received neoadjuvant chemotherapy that began in the 20 weeks before definitive surgery (wedge resection, segmentectomy, lobectomy, or pneumonectomy). Because of this defined timeline, patients with less than 20 weeks of follow-up were excluded to avoid immortal time bias. Patients were grouped as follows: chemotherapy, chemoimmunotherapy (started within 1 week of each other), and chemoradiotherapy (started within 1 week of each other, with total radiation dose between 30 and 80 Gy).

Data Elements

Independent covariates of interest included age, sex, race, ethnicity, Charlson-Deyo comorbidity index score (grouped by the NCDB as 0, 1, 2, or ≥3), insurance status, facility type and location, year of diagnosis, clinical TNM staging, histologic subtype, histologic grade, surgical approach, and complexity and extent of resection. A complete list of NCDB variables is available online.31 Clinical and pathologic stages were defined according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer 8th Edition staging system. The primary outcome was pCR, defined as pathologic T0 and N0 after resection. Secondary outcomes included nodal downstaging (defined as pathologic N stage less than clinical N stage), pneumonectomy (as opposed to lobectomy or sublobar resection), R0 resection, 30-day unplanned readmission (to the same facility), and 90-day all-cause mortality.

Statistical Analysis

A bivariate sensitivity analysis between patients with and without missing key covariate data is presented in Table E1. Patient sociodemographic and tumor characteristics were compared by chi-square and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests as appropriate based on variable classification and distribution. Multivariable logistic regression models were created for each of the outcome variables, adjusted for patient age, sex, race, ethnicity, academic facility, year of diagnosis, comorbidity index (Charlson-Deyo score), clinical stage, clinical T stage, clinical N stage, squamous histology, histologic grade, surgical approach, and complexity and extent of resection. For variables with proportions of missing data greater than 5% (histologic grade and surgical approach), a level of missing was included in multivariable analysis. Data analysis was conducted in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 3956 patients were identified, including 1590 treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy (40.1%), 1315 treated with neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (26.5%), and 1051 treated with neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (33.2%, Figure E1). Overall, there were 243 patients (6.1%) with stage I, 1093 patients (27.6%) with stage II, and 2620 patients (66.2%) with stage III (Table 1).

Compared with chemotherapy, patients receiving chemoimmunotherapy were more likely to be diagnosed in 2022 (76.0% vs 10.4%, P < .0001), located in the South (42.8% vs 33.5%, P < .0001), and have a Charlson-Deyo score greater than 0 (38.5% vs 35.0%, P = .02). Compared with chemoradiotherapy, patients receiving chemoimmunotherapy were older (median [interquartile range], 66 years [61-72] vs 63 years [58-70], P < .001), more likely to be on Medicare (56.6% vs 52.6%, P < .001), and more likely to be in a clinical trial (1.0% vs 0%, P < .001). Patients receiving chemoimmunotherapy were also more likely than patients receiving chemoradiotherapy to have clinical T1 tumors (24.4% vs 22.2%, P < .001) or clinical N0 tumors (≥38.2% vs 32.6%, P < .001).

Trends Over Time

Over the course of the study period, chemoimmunotherapy use increased, from 52 patients in 2018 to 799 patients in 2022. During this period, the annual number of patients receiving chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy declined by more than 50% (Figure 1). Similar trends were observed after stratification by clinical stage, race-ethnicity, and histologic subtype (data not shown).

Safety Outcomes

Compared with chemotherapy, patients receiving chemoimmunotherapy had a comparable rate of 30-day readmission (4.6% vs 3.9%, P = .09), but lower pneumonectomy (6.2% vs 10.0%, P = .001) and 90-day mortality (<1.0% vs 2.0%, P < .001, Table 2). No significant differences in the above were noted between chemoimmunotherapy and chemotherapy on multivariable logistic regression adjusted for patient and tumor characteristics (Figure 2, A).

Response to Neoadjuvant Therapy

Compared with chemotherapy, chemoimmunotherapy had higher proportions of R0 resection (93.5% vs 90.9%, P = .047), nodal downstaging (43.9% vs 28.8%, P < .001), and pCR (30.2% vs 9.1%, P < .0001). On multivariable analysis, chemoimmunotherapy had significantly higher likelihoods of nodal downstaging (odds ratio, 2.38, 95% CI, 1.78-3.17, P < .001) and pCR (odds ratio, 3.32, 95% CI, 2.49-4.44, P < .001).

Chemoradiotherapy had a higher proportion of nodal downstaging than chemoimmunotherapy (48.8% vs 43.9%, P < .001), but lower pCR (26.8% vs 30.2%, P < .001); however, no significant differences were observed on multivariable analysis.

Discussion

The rapid expansion of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in the general population has created a pressing need to understand real-world outcomes associated with its use. In the general population, neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy was administered with a safety profile similar to chemotherapy and chemoradiotherapy. In fact, the 90-day mortality rate (<1.0%) after chemoimmunotherapy and lung resection in patients with NCDB was even lower than those reported in major trials (2.4% in CheckMate-816 and 3.3% in KEYNOTE-671).12,13 Compared with neoadjuvant chemotherapy, chemoimmunotherapy did not appear to increase the risk for pneumonectomy and was actually associated with a slight reduction (6.2% vs 10.0%). A reduction in pneumonectomy rate was also reported in the CheckMate-816 trial: 16.8% with neoadjuvant nivolumab and chemotherapy as opposed to 25.2% with neoadjuvant chemotherapy alone.13 Interestingly, the rate of pneumonectomy in the NCDB is substantially lower for both approaches, perhaps reflecting a hesitancy to perform or offer pneumonectomy during these years outside of clinical trials.

The impressive response to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy observed in trial settings appears to be reproducible in the general population. Neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy patients in this study demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of pCR than chemotherapy patients, a trend that was preserved on multivariable analysis. This real-world pCR rate associated with neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (30.2%) is comparable to the results of Checkmate-816 (24.0%)13 and KEYNOTE-671 (22.2%).12 Our findings also align with a considerably smaller real-world report from the United Kingdom (130 patients with neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy stage II and III NSCLC) that found a pCR rate of 31.4%.32 Although a high pCR rate may not necessarily translate to improved long-term outcomes, these clinical trials have reported high 3-year survival (71% in KEYNOTE-67133 and 62% in CheckMate-816).34 The alignment between trial and observed pCR rates suggests that real-world lung cancer patients may derive longer-term benefits of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, although we recognize the importance of allowing real-world data to mature.

When compared with chemoradiotherapy, chemoimmunotherapy exhibited no significant differences in R0 resection, nodal downstaging, or pCR on multivariable analysis. Previous studies have demonstrated high rates of R0 resection (97%) and pCR (28%), although high rates of distant recurrence and a 3-year overall survival 61%.35 Whether the high rates of R0 and pCR in this cohort will translate to increased overall survival remains to be seen and can be subject of future study as follow-up data mature and the use of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy continues to grow.

Limitations

This study has several limitations in addition to those commonly associated with retrospective studies. First, a number of key indicators of response to immunotherapy in NSCLC, such as smoking status,36 PD-L1 expression,37 and tumor mutation burden,38 are not currently recorded in the NCDB, although smoking will be included starting with the 2023 participant user file.39 A number of other variables of interest including intraoperative factors, specific neoadjuvant and adjuvant agents and durations of therapy, and subclassification of N2 disease are not available in the NCDB and thus not included in this analysis. As defined by the NCDB, the immunotherapy variable is not limited to immune checkpoint inhibitors and could include other treatment modalities, such as monoclonal antibodies against vascular endothelial growth factor and epidermal growth factor receptor (but does not include targeted therapies such as erlotinib, osimertinib, or alectinib).40 Therefore, it is possible that some patients coded as receiving immunotherapy received agents other than immune checkpoint inhibitors, which was the therapy of interest. However, the timeframe of this study corresponds with the expansion of immune checkpoint blockade therapies, so most patients coded as receiving immunotherapy likely received therapies targeting the PD-1 pathway. Finally, this study included years 2018 to 2022, which overlaps with randomized clinical trials evaluating neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy such as Checkmate-816, KEYNOTE-671, and AEGEAN. These international trials included subsets of patients that could overlap with the patients included in this NCDB study. Clinical trial participation was less than 5% in the neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy group, but it is possible that the collection of this variable is incomplete, and in fact greater overlap with randomized clinical trial participants may exist.

Conclusions

Real-world outcomes suggest the reassuring safety and impressive downstaging effect of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy seen in clinical trials can be reproduced in the general NSCLC population. Further study to understand the impact of perioperative chemoimmunotherapy in the real-world setting is justified.
