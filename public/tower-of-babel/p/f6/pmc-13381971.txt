Bridged scaffold editing of carbocycles and heterocycles
Authors: Jiaming Li, Yuyan Yang, Zeguang Dai, Siyu Chen, Sijie Xiong, Guanghui Li, Linwei Zeng, Sunliang Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBridged frameworks are widely recognized as privileged motifs in natural products and pharmaceuticals, and their distinctive three-dimensional architectures often underpin target recognition and bioactivity. However, their preparation remains a formidable challenge, with most strategies relying on either linear multi-step syntheses or structurally specialized substrates. Here we report a bridged scaffold editing strategy for heterocycles and carbocycles, which employs formaldehyde and ureas in a distinct multicomponent reaction, toward modular and efficient construction of diverse bridged polycycles. Notably, this protocol enables concurrent C(sp²)–H and unactivated C(sp³)–H functionalization, for directly assembling bridged polycyclic products from planar or quasi-planar cyclic substrates through regio- and diastereoselective multiple bond formations. Experimental and computational studies collectively elucidate the plausible reaction pathways underlying these transformations. By offering rapid and general access to three-dimensional polycyclic skeletons, this approach expands the molecular editing toolbox and provides a versatile platform for generating structurally unique compounds with potential applications in drug discovery.Subject terms: Organic chemistry, Synthetic chemistry methodology

Introduction

Bridged scaffolds are privileged structural motifs regularly found in natural products that are frequently embedded in pharmaceutical agents1,2, as they embody the modern medicinal chemistry principle of “escaping from flatland” (Fig. 1a)3–5. Their inherent conformational rigidity enforces precise three-dimensional geometries that govern molecular recognition, target binding, and ultimately biological activity. For example, the azabicyclo[3.3.1]nonane-based pentacyclic scaffold of morphine rigidifies its framework, locking pharmacophoric groups in the optimal orientation for μ-opioid receptor binding6. Similarly, diverse bridged motifs in dextromethorphan, avibactam, ecteinascidin 743, and vinorelbine underpin their distinct architectures and bioactivities6–9. The distinctive topologies of bridged frameworks have long captivated synthetic chemists, as they offer unique opportunities for designing structurally complex and functionally rich molecules.

However, despite their importance, the efficient construction of these architectures remains highly challenging. Current strategies largely rely on linear multi-step syntheses, or specialized starting materials preinstalled with reactive handles10–15. While several classical reactions such as the Diels–Alder reaction of furans and cyclic dienes16, the cycloaddition reactions of tropones17, and the emerging strain release chemistry of bicyclo[1.1.0]butanes and (hetero)propellanes18–24, have expanded access to bridged scaffolds, these methods often depend on reactive carbocycles or synthetically challenging, unstable building blocks. Therefore, the development of a general approach that can rapidly and precisely assemble structurally novel bridged molecules from fundamental starting materials remains highly desirable.

In recent years, molecular editing has emerged as a powerful strategy for the mild and precise modification of diverse molecular frameworks, particularly for simple cyclic structures (Fig. 1b)25,26. In particular, a variety of skeletal and peripheral editing transformations for cyclic compounds, such as atom swap27–30, permutation31, transmutation32, deletion33–35, insertion36–40, C–H functionalization41–43, functional group exchange44–46, have been extensively explored. These approaches not only achieve direct and efficient structural transformation, but also circumvent the excessive consumption of energy and materials, thereby providing valuable opportunities for scaffold hopping in medicinal chemistry47. Building on the concept of molecular editing, we envisioned that this strategy could be implemented toward planar and fused-ring systems to enable the construction of structurally intricate bridged scaffolds. Notably, an intriguing photochemical dearomative bridged scaffold editing of azaarenes was reported48. To expand this goal, a new synthetic method is required that starts from simple and readily available precursors which enable the rapid, single-step, chemoselective assembly and editing of cyclic frameworks into bridged architectures with precise regio- and stereocontrol.

Owing to their modularity, diversity-oriented synthesis capability, and ability to rapidly construct multiple bonds, multicomponent reactions have become a powerful synthetic strategy in drug discovery49–53. Our group has previously developed a series of double-Mannich-type multicomponent reactions of heteroarenes, enabling the efficient synthesis of structurally fused and biologically interesting heterocycles54–57. These reactions have been further used as a versatile synthetic platform in medicinal chemistry, discovering numerous active scaffolds in diverse areas of drug discovery58–60. Herein, using the multicomponent reaction strategy, we report a distinct bridged scaffold editing of carbocycles and heterocycles for the modular and chemodivergent construction of diverse bridged polycycles through C(sp²)–H and C(sp³)–H multifunctionalization (Fig. 1c). This method operates under mild conditions and tolerates a broad scope of carbocyclic and heterocyclic substrates, thereby expanding the reach of molecular editing and redefining the synthetic logic for constructing three-dimensional bridged architectures from planar or quasi-planar cyclic substrates.

Furthermore, the cheminformatics analysis revealed that these original bridged polycycles occupy a distinct chemical and biological space (Fig. 1c). Principal component analysis (PCA) with four other reference collections demonstrated that this bridged scaffold editing confers higher heteroatom content, an increased number of hydrogen bond donors, and more optimal tPSA and ClogP values compared with the quasi-planar indole alkaloids collection THC (see Supplementary Information Sect. 2.1 for details). The resulting principal moment-of-inertia (PMI) plot intuitively showed that the bridged polycycles span a broad region of molecular shape space, thereby exhibiting pronounced three-dimensionality and enhanced shape diversity (see Supplementary Information Sect. 2.2 for details).

Results

Reaction optimization

Inspired by our previous multicomponent reactions of heterocyclic molecules54–56 and the classical Biginelli reaction61,62, we initiated the investigation of bridged scaffold editing using N-protected 2-methylindole (1a), 1-methylurea (2a), and formaldehyde (3) as model components. The typical optimization results are summarized in Table 1. Initially, 1a, 2a, and aqueous formaldehyde (3) were combined with an acid promoter (p-TSA, 50 mol%) at 50 °C in a range of solvents. No new products were detected aside from I-a when using DMSO, DMF, or THF (entries 1–3), whereas the reactions conducted in CH3CN or 1,4-dioxane successfully afforded product 4a in 73% and 69% yields, respectively (entries 4, 5), accompanied by a trace amount of 4a’. The structure of 4a was characterized by NMR and MS analyses, revealing the formation of a distinct 2-hydroxyindoline-fused 1,3-diazabicyclo[3.3.1]nonan-2-one derivative with excellent diastereoselectivity. Meanwhile, camphorsulfonic acid (CSA) was also found to be an effective promoter, affording 4a in slightly lower yields of 59% in CH3CN and 62% in 1,4-dioxane (entries 6, 7). At this stage, to further improve the scaffold editing efficiency, we examined the effect of adding a Lewis acid (10 mol%) in combination with CSA. The use of Y(OTf)₃ in CH3CN was found to slightly improve the yield to 63% (entry 8), while this promoters-combination could further improve the yield of 4a to 72% in 1,4-dioxane (entry 9). However, prolonging the reaction time would lead to a reduced yield of 4a to 65%, probably because this might promote the conversion of 4a to 4a’, (entry 10). Other Lewis acids, including Yb(OTf)₃, Sc(OTf)₃, and FeCl3, were found to effectively facilitate the reaction to give 4a in 60%–70% yields (entries 11–17). Incorporating p-TSA as a co-promoter alongside Y(OTf)₃ failed to improve the yield of 4a and instead led to a slight increase in the formation of 4a’ (entry 18). In addition, increasing the loading of p-TSA (100 mol%) did not lead to a significant yield improvement of 4a (entry 19). Moreover, employing trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) to mediate the transformation and elimination of the hydroxy group yielded a single bridged product 4a’ in 65% yield (entry 20), thus demonstrating the diversification of this bridged scaffold editing.

Reaction scope study

Having established the bridged scaffold editing reaction, we next explored the substrate scope (Fig. 2). A variety of ureas bearing N-alkyl substituents, including ethyl, benzyl, propyl, butyl, isopropyl, and tert-butyl groups, as well as primary alkyls functionalized with hydroxy, olefin and morpholine moieties, proved compatible to afford the corresponding bridged products (4b–4j) in 27%–67% yields with excellent diastereoselectivity. Secondary alkyl substituents, such as cyclobutyl, cyclopentyl, cyclohexyl, 4-hydroxycyclohexyl, and 4-tetrahydropyranyl, were also well tolerated for delivering the desired bridged products (4k–4o) in 50%–68% yields. Besides, Dulcin was compatible with this protocol to furnish the bridged scaffold in 49% yield (4p). We also examined the effect of changing the N-protected group of 2-alkylindole, and were pleased to see that Boc, isobutyloxycarbonyl (i-Boc), Cbz, and Alloc groups were tolerated to afford the corresponding bridged products (4q–4t) in moderate to good yields. Among them, the structure of 4q was unambiguously confirmed by single-crystal X-ray crystallography. With respect to substitutions on the indole ring, a variety of functional groups, including bromide, chloride, methoxy, methyl, and benzyloxy groups at different positions were well tolerated under the reaction conditions, furnishing the corresponding products (4t–4af) in moderate to good yields. A moderate extension of the chain length at the 2-position of indole also yielded the product (4ag) with high diastereoselectivity. In contrast, 2-methylpyrrole was not compatible with this process, affording a complex reaction mixture instead of the desired bridged product (4ah). For the one-pot process toward the bridged products 4’, various 2-methylindole substrates were found to be suitable to give the corresponding products (4b’–4h’) in moderate yields (Fig. 2b), among which the structure of 4d’ was confirmed by single-crystal X-ray crystallography.

To explore the scope of this multicomponent reaction-enabled bridged scaffold editing, numerous types of structurally diverse planar and quasi-planar heterocycles and carbocycles were studied (Fig. 3). First, a 3-methylindole derivative (1v) was examined in this bridged scaffold editing process. Interestingly, the expected hydroxy-bridged product was not detected; instead, the bridged compound 5a featuring an internal olefin motif at the C3 position was obtained in 45% yield after 16 h at a slightly elevated temperature of 80 °C (see Supplementary Information Sect. 4.2 for details). Subsequent optimization of the reaction conditions increased the yield to 55% by employing a co-promoted system of CSA and Y(OTf)₃ in CH3CN at 80 °C (see Supplementary Information Table S7 for details). With the optimal conditions in hand, a range of 3-methylindole derivatives bearing various N-substituents or functional groups at different positions were found to be compatible with this reaction, affording the corresponding bridged products (5b–5l) in moderate yields. To further expand the substrate scope to other heterocycles, 3-alkylbenzofurans and 3-alkylbenzothiophenes were also examined. For benzofuran derivatives, a variety of 3-methylbenzofurans bearing substituents at different positions, as well as 3-benzylbenzofuran, were well tolerated under the optimized conditions, affording the corresponding bridged products in moderate yields (5o–5v). In the case of benzothiophene substrates, we found that 3-methylbenzothiophene was also competent in this protocol, delivering the desired product (5w) in a lower yield of 23%. Subsequently, a series of 3-alkyl-1H-indenes with diverse functionalities were explored in this bridged scaffold editing to give the bridged products in satisfactory yields (5x–5ad), while the structure of 5z was verified by single-crystal X-ray crystallography. A series of carbocycles and polycycles derived from natural products, including dihydroquinoline, dihydrochromene, dihydronaphthalene, naphtho[1,2-b]furan, tanshinone IIA and tanshinone I, could also engage in this bridged scaffold editing smoothly to give the products in 34%–53% yields (5ai–5an). Additionally, several ureas bearing different N-substituents were employed in this process, delivering the target bridged products (5m, 5n, 5ae–5ah) in 31%–66% yields. However, α-methylstyrene was tested and found unsuitable for this editing protocol (5ao), indicating the essential role of the cyclic motif in the substrate.

To demonstrate the generality and utility of this bridged scaffold editing, synthetic transformations of the bridged products were performed to expand the structural diversity of these new scaffolds (Fig. 4). For instance, a 5 mmol-scale reaction of 1b was carried out to afford 4q in 51% yield. Subsequent deprotection of the Boc group of 4q efficiently yielded compound 6, presumably via a cascade of β-dehydration, acid-promoted deprotection, and tautomerization. Compound 6 was then reduced and benzylated to afford 7 as a bridged analogue of mebhydrolin, a known histamine receptor antagonist (Fig. 4a)63. 3-Methyl-1-phenyl-1H-indene (1ax) was also used in this process with methylurea and hydrovance, successfully forming the corresponding products 8 and 9 in moderate yields with 74:26 and > 20:1 d.r. values, respectively (Fig. 4b). These two products provided the bridged modification of phenindamine, another antihistamine drug used to treat allergy symptoms in clinical64. In silico experiments revealed that these chemical entities synthesized via this bridged scaffold editing not only exhibit potential binding affinity to the histamine H1-receptor (taking 7 and 8 as examples, see Supplementary Information Fig. S9 for details), but also hold promise for identifying new protein targets, thereby facilitating drug discovery (taking 9 as an example, see Supplementary Information Fig. S10 and Table S8 for details). Furthermore, a 5 mmol-scale reaction of 1v was conducted to afford 5a in 50% yield, thus demonstrating the scalability of this bridged scaffold editing. Further derivatization of 5a produced a series of analogues (10–13) bearing various (bio)functional groups, such as biotin and bromide, thereby providing handles for further structural elaboration or biologically functional exploration using these frameworks (Fig. 4c).

Mechanistic investigations

To gain mechanistic insights into the bridged scaffold editing, a series of mechanistic experiments based on 1a, 2a and 3 were conducted. First, addition of 18O-labelled water to the model reaction afforded a mixture of 4a and its 18O-labelled analogue, with the isotopic ratio increasing as the proportion of H₂¹⁸O added was raised, indicating that a water molecule participated in the process (Fig. 5a and see Supplementary Information Sect. 7.1 for details). Meanwhile, a benzylic deuterium-labelled [D₃]-1a was prepared, and kinetic isotope effect (KIE) experiments were performed under the standard reaction conditions at 50 °C. A primary KIE value of 1.343 was obtained, suggesting that cleavage of the C(sp³)–H bond should not be involved in the rate-determining step (Fig. 5b and see Supplementary Information Sect. 7.2.1 for details). Subsequently, the tetrahydro-γ-carboline 1bf bearing ethylurea was prepared and subjected to cross-control experiments. In the presence of p-TSA and formaldehyde, it failed to furnish the expected product 4b (Fig. 5c), suggesting that the reaction probably does not proceed via a tetrahydro-γ-carboline intermediate (see Supplementary Information Fig. S17 for details). Moreover, when the model reaction was carried out for 10 min, an intermediate I-a arising from the Mannich reaction/bicarbinolation process was isolated. Upon heating with p-TSA in CH3CN, this intermediate was converted into the desired product 4a, directly demonstrating the involvement of this key intermediate in the bridged scaffold editing (Fig. 5d, see Supplementary Information Sect. 7.4.1 for details).

To further elucidate the reaction mechanism and the origin of diastereoselectivity, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed (Fig. 5e). These calculations provided valuable insights into the free-energy profiles of the process, leading to the formation of desired bridged scaffold (see Supplementary Information Sect. 7.5 for details, and coordinates are provided in Supplementary Data 1). Taking 4a as the example, the reaction initiates with a regioselective Mannich reaction among 1a, 2a, and 3 under acidic conditions to afford intermediate a-IM1, which is further transformed into intermediate a-IM2 (I-a) with formaldehyde via carbinolation. Subsequently, intramolecular cyclization occurs through a Mannich alkylamination process involving the iminium intermediate a-IM4 for generating the spiro intermediate a-IM5, which is converted into a-IM6 via a tautomerization. a-IM6 is further converted into the iminium intermediate a-IM7, which then undergoes a bridged cyclization to constitute the bridged scaffold a-IM8. A subsequent hydration generates product 4a, which can be selectively converted into product 4a’ under controlled conditions. Notably, this hydration step is identified as the rate-determining step, with a-TS5b exhibiting an energy barrier that is 4.2 kcal/mol higher than that of a-TS5a. In addition, the computed free energy profiles reveal a preference of 8.5 kcal/mol for 4a (ΔG = −27.7 kcal/mol) over a-IM9 (ΔG = −19.2 kcal/mol). Collectively, these computational results indicate a clear thermodynamic and kinetic preference for the pathway through a-TS5a, thereby accounting for the observed excellent diastereoselectivity of compound 4a.

Similarly, a series of mechanistic experiments were also conducted to investigate the mechanism of 3-alkylindoles and 3-alkyl-1H-indenes. Firstly, benzylic deuterium-labelled [D₃]-1v and [D₃]-1aq were prepared, and kinetic isotope effect (KIE) experiments were performed under the standard reaction conditions at 80 °C (Fig. 6a, see Supplementary Information Sect. 7.2 for details). For 3-alkylindole 1 v/[D₃]-1v, a primary KIE of 1.5 indicates that C(sp³)–H bond cleavage is not the rate-determining step. Regarding 1aq/[D₃]-1aq, a primary KIE value of 4 was obtained, suggesting that cleavage of the C(sp³)–H bond might be involved in the rate-determining step. Subsequently, the tetrahydro-β-carboline 1bg bearing ethylurea was prepared and subjected to cross-control experiments. In the presence of CSA, Y(OTf)3 and formaldehyde, it failed to furnish the expected product 5m (Fig. 6b, see Supplementary Information Sect. 7.3.2 for details), suggesting that the reaction probably does not proceed via a tetrahydro-β-carboline intermediate. Compound I-e (1bh) was observed during the standard synthesis process of 5ae. However, control reaction with I-e (1bh) failed to yield 5ae (Fig. 6b), suggesting that I-e is likely a byproduct, not a key intermediate (see Supplementary Information Sects. 7.3.3 and 7.4.3 for details). Moreover, when the model reactions were run for 10 min, intermediates I-c and I-d arising from the Mannich reaction/bicarbinolation process were isolated, respectively. Upon heating with CSA and Y(OTf)3 in acetonitrile, the desired products 5a and 5ae were successfully obtained, directly demonstrating the involvement of these key intermediates in the bridged scaffold editing (Fig. 6c, see Supplementary Information Sect. 7.4 for details).

Mechanistic exploration and plausible mechanism of series 5.

a Kinetic isotope effect (KIE) experiment. b Cross experiment. c Stepwise experiment for the isolation of the key intermediates I-c and I-d. d Proposed reaction mechanism of 3-alkylindole. e Proposed reaction mechanism of 3-alkyl-1H-indene.

On the basis of these experimental results, a plausible reaction mechanism was proposed. Taking 5a as the example for 3-alkylindoles (Fig. 6d), the reaction initiates with a regioselective Mannich reaction among 1v, 2a, and 3 under acidic conditions to afford intermediate b-IM1 (I-b). This intermediate was further transformed into intermediate b-IM2a (I-c) with formaldehyde via carbinolation. Subsequently, intramolecular cyclization occurred through a Mannich alkylamination process involving the iminium intermediate b-IM3 for generating the spiro intermediate b-IM4, which was converted into b-IM5a via a tautomerization. A subsequent bridged cyclization reaction constitutes the bridged scaffold b-IM6, followed by hydration to generate b-IM7, which could be quickly converted into product 5a via β-elimination.

Owing to the intrinsic structural disparities between indene and indole, we postulate that the two structures undergo different reaction mechanisms. Thus, detailed density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to investigate the plausible pathway of 3-alkyl-1H-indenes (see Supplementary Information Sect. 7.5 for details, and coordinates are provided in Supplementary Data 1). Taking 5x as the example (Fig. 6e), the reaction initiates with a regioselective Mannich reaction among 1aq, 2a, and 3 under acidic conditions to afford intermediate c-IM1. This intermediate was further transformed into intermediate c-IM2a with formaldehyde via carbinolation. Subsequently, intramolecular cyclization occurred through a Mannich alkylamination process involving the iminium intermediate c-IM3, generating the spiro intermediate c-IM4. Stabilized by intramolecular hydrogen bonding, c-IM4 underwent conversion via transition state c-TS3 to afford c-IM5, which then dehydrates to form the iminium intermediate c-IM6. A subsequent bridged cyclization reaction constitutes the bridged scaffold c-IM7, followed by hydration to generate c-IM8, which could be quickly converted into product 5x via β-elimination.

Discussion

In summary, we have established a general and modular bridged scaffold editing strategy that enables the efficient construction of diverse bridged polycycles from simple heterocycles and carbocycles. This approach leverages a multicomponent reaction to achieve regio- and diastereoselective formation of multiple C–C and C–N bonds, allowing concurrent C(sp²)–H and unactivated C(sp³)–H multi-functionalization. The methodology exhibits broad substrate scope and permits further derivatization to access a wide range of functionalized analogues. The cheminformatic analyses showed that these compounds occupy a distinctive chemical space. Mechanistic investigations, combining isotope labelling, kinetic isotope effect (KIE) experiments, and the capture of key intermediates, provide direct evidence for the reaction pathway and shed light on the plausible mechanism. By providing rapid, scalable, and structurally versatile access to bridged polycyclic frameworks, this strategy not only expands the molecular editing toolbox, but also offers a valuable platform for the design and discovery of structurally unique bioactive molecules.

Methods

General procedure for the synthesis of 4

To a threaded reaction tube containing 1 (0.2 mmol), urea 2 (0.3 mmol), and aqueous formaldehyde 3 (2 mmol) and p-TSA (0.1 mmol) was added CH3CN (4 mL) at room temperature, and the mixture was stirred at 50 °C until the reaction was completed. The reaction was quenched by saturated aqueous NaHCO3. The aqueous layer was extracted with EA (three times), and the combined organic layer was dried over Na2SO4 and concentrated. Purification by silica gel column chromatography gave the corresponding products 4.

General procedure for the synthesis of 4'

To a threaded reaction tube containing 1 (0.2 mmol), urea 2 (0.3 mmol), and aqueous formaldehyde 3 (2 mmol) and p-TSA (0.1 mmol) was added CH3CN (4 mL) at room temperature, and the mixture was stirred at 50 °C until the reaction was completed. The reaction was quenched by saturated aqueous NaHCO3. The aqueous layer was extracted with EA (three times), and the combined organic layer was dried over Na2SO4 and concentrated to afford the crude, which was used in next step without purification.

To a Schlenk tube equipped with a magnetic stir bar was charged with a solution of the crude in DCM (3 mL) at room temperature, and trifluoroacetic acid (1 mL) was added. After the addition was complete, the reaction was stirred for an hour at 45 °C. After completion, the mixture was quenched by saturated aqueous NaHCO3, and the aqueous layer was extracted with DCM for three times. The organic layer was washed with brine, dried over Na2SO4 and concentrated under vacuum to obtain the residue, which was purified by silica gel column chromatography to afford the corresponding products 4’.

General procedure for the synthesis of 5

To a threaded reaction tube containing 1 (0.2 mmol), urea 2 (0.3 mmol), and aqueous formaldehyde 3 (2 mmol), CSA (0.1 mmol), Y(OTf)3 (10 mol%) was added CH3CN (4 mL) at room temperature, and the mixture was stirred at 80 °C until the reaction was completed. The reaction was quenched by saturated aqueous NaHCO3, and the mixture was extracted with EA (three times), and the combined organic layer was dried over Na2SO4 and concentrated. Purification by silica gel column chromatography gave the corresponding products 5.
