A mixed methods assessment of disaster management perceptions among healthcare practitioners in Qatar
Authors: Sara Elshami, Manar E Abdel-Rahman, Hanan Abdul Rahim, Mohamed Izham Mohamed Ibrahim, Ahmed Awaisu, Abdullatif Al-Khal, Samya Al Abdulla, Mohammed Al-Thani, David Dungan, Walid AbouGalala, Banan Mukhalalati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn response to the growing frequency and complexity of disasters, healthcare practitioners (HCPs) must be proficient in disaster management. Research on HCPs’ perceptions of disaster management often lacks the comprehensive scope needed to address diverse healthcare challenges. This study aimed to assess HCPs’ perceptions of their disaster management capabilities, focusing on their knowledge, attitudes, and practices, as well as their willingness to continue practicing during disasters. Additionally, it assessed organization-based management approaches and explored the opportunities and challenges associated with disaster prevention and control strategies. An explanatory mixed-methods approach guided by the Disaster Management Framework was followed. Quantitative data were collected through a self-reported questionnaire administered to a stratified random sample of 771 HCPs from various health organizations across Qatar. Of these, 436 responded (response rate: 56.5%), with 403 responses valid for the analysis. This sample exceeded the minimum required sample size (n = 379), which ensured adequate statistical power. Qualitative data were obtained through six online focus groups (FGs) conducted via Microsoft Teams with 41 purposively selected HCPs, ensuring representation of those with disaster response experience. Qualitative data were analyzed thematically, and data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached. Integration of qualitative and quantitative findings was conducted at the interpretation and reporting levels using a contiguous narrative approach, enabling complementary insights and enhancing the coherence and validity of findings. Quantitative data indicated good perceptions among HCPs concerning their knowledge (median [IQR] = 41 [34–44], Min–Max = 18–55), attitude (median [IQR] = 46 [43–50], Min–Max = 10–55), willingness to practice (median [IQR] = 23 [1–24], Min–Max = 6–30), and their organization-based management (median [IQR] = 43 [40–50], Min–Max = 10–50). However, they reported less frequent involvement in practical disaster management activities (median [IQR] = 31 [24–39], Min–Max = 2–50). Higher disaster management perceptions were significantly associated with prior disaster training, administrative roles, and experience during national emergencies, gender, years of experience, profession, and worksite (p < 0.05). Qualitative data emphasized the importance of practical training, mental health support, technological innovations, interdisciplinary collaboration, community engagement, and robust planning and leadership to strengthen disaster management and enhance public health outcomes. The findings suggest that healthcare organizations worldwide should prioritize proactive disaster readiness, continuous assessment, comprehensive support systems, adaptable protocols, and public engagement to enhance system-wide resilience, adaptability, and community partnership in disaster management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-35518-1.Keywords: Disaster management, Healthcare team, Preparedness, ReadinessSubject terms: Population screening, Health policy, Health services, Public health

Introduction

Disasters have become increasingly frequent and severe, affecting all countries without exception and presenting substantial challenges to global public health and healthcare systems1,2. Their consequences include loss of life, injuries, physical and mental diseases, damage to property and assets, social and economic disruption, and environmental degradation3. Access to healthcare is recognized as a fundamental human right that underpins community well-being4. Nevertheless, the inherent unpredictability of disasters can severely disrupt healthcare systems and hinder the delivery of essential services5. Disaster management is defined as “the organization, planning, and application of measures preparing for, responding to, and recovering from disasters”6. Effective disaster management is critical to maintaining and strengthening community resilience7. The Disaster (or Emergency) Management Framework provides a structured and comprehensive approach to mitigating disaster impacts, enhancing preparedness, guiding immediate response efforts, and supporting long-term recovery8,9.

Healthcare practitioners (HCPs) play a crucial role in mitigating disaster impacts and providing essential medical services10,11. To effectively handle risks, HCPs should possess adequate knowledge, demonstrate a proactive attitude, and be ready to respond promptly7,12,13. However, numerous studies worldwide have consistently shown gaps in HCPs’ preparedness and readiness for disasters14–18. HCPs’ preparedness was influenced by their prior experience, educational background, and access to training. Additionally, self-efficacy and ongoing education were key to their readiness for disaster response19–23.

In Qatar, some disasters were witnessed over the past decade, including the 2017 Gulf Crisis, which disrupted medical supply chains and caused psychological distress among mixed Qatari-Gulf families24,25. The COVID-19 pandemic further tested the healthcare system through overwhelming patient demand and critical resource shortages26–32. Qatar adopts the International Sendai Framework to guide disaster risk reduction, which aligns with its National Health Strategy (2024–2030) to enhance health system efficiency and resilience33–35. The strategy emphasizes sustainable healthcare and effective emergency management where HCPs play key roles through training, capacity building, and strategic measures35. In Qatar, healthcare services are predominantly provided by the governmental sector, where the Ministry of Public Health (MoPH) plays a central role in shaping national healthcare priorities and regulating the healthcare system36. Under its oversight, Hamad Medical Corporation (HMC) and the Primary HealthCare Corporation (PHCC) are key organizations in the governmental healthcare sector36. HMC manages approximately 13 hospitals, including both specialist and community hospitals, and provides a range of medical services36. PHCC, on the other hand, operates around 31 centers strategically located across the country, ensuring accessible primary healthcare services to meet the needs of the population36.

Studies on HCP preparedness in Qatar have highlighted strengths and areas for improvement in disaster management. For example, ElGeed et al. (2021) found that community pharmacists consistently adhered to hygiene protocols during COVID-19, though their patient care involvement varied37. Farhat et al. (2022) identified knowledge gaps among HMC Ambulance Service personnel regarding HazMat-CBRN incidents, which improved significantly after targeted training38. Isleem et al. (2023) reported that experienced emergency pharmacists showed higher disaster readiness during the FIFA World Cup Qatar 202239. Similarly, Al-Romaihi et al. (2023) observed high levels of awareness and training among HCPs for communicable disease threats during mass gatherings40. These studies, collectively, emphasized the need for tailored training programs and resources that address challenges faced by HCPs.

Nevertheless, similar to other international studies, research in Qatar has predominantly focused on specific disaster contexts37,39,40. These studies often examined the experiences of particular professional groups, often with small sample sizes37,38, while overlooking the roles of other essential first responders and administrative healthcare personnel. Furthermore, much of the research has been confined to single institutional or healthcare settings38–40. These methodological and contextual boundaries reveal broader gaps in disaster management research and preparedness strategies globally. Specifically, the absence of perspectives from a wider array of healthcare professions and care levels may overlook important interprofessional dynamics and system-wide vulnerabilities. Furthermore, the emphasis on single-event or single-setting studies may not fully reflect the range of competencies required to manage diverse disaster scenarios across the four phases of disaster management.

Hence, opportunities remain for more comprehensive investigations that can sufficiently account for the complexity and interprofessional coordination required for effective disaster response. Such studies can support the development of comprehensive and adaptable strategies applicable to a wide range of disaster scenarios across all phases of disaster management. Therefore, the aim of this study was to conduct a comprehensive assessment of disaster management, guided by the Disaster Management Framework, through the perceptions of HCPs in Qatar. The objectives of the study were as follows:

The primary objective was to evaluate the perceived disaster management capabilities of the HCPs in terms of knowledge, attitudes, practices, and willingness to continue practicing during disasters, as well as the organizational disaster management approaches within three major healthcare institutions in Qatar.

The secondary objective was to explore perceived opportunities and challenges related to disaster prevention and control strategies.

This study uniquely extends beyond earlier Qatar-based research by employing a mixed-methods design that systematically integrates data from HCPs across diverse professional roles within multiple healthcare organizations representing various sectors and levels of care. It also addresses a broader spectrum of disaster scenarios, including public health emergencies (e.g., COVID-19), politically induced crises (e.g., the 2017 Gulf Crisis), and mass-gathering events (e.g., the FIFA World Cup). Importantly, this study employed the Disaster Management Assessment Tool for Health Care Practitioners (DMAT_HCP)41, a rigorously validated tool developed and tested in Qatar. It demonstrated strong psychometric properties and was designed to comprehensively assess HCPs’ preparedness across all disaster management phases and healthcare disciplines. Its validation among multidisciplinary practitioners in Qatar ensures cultural and contextual suitability, making it an appropriate and robust tool for evaluating national disaster management capabilities.

This study contributes to the evidence base on disaster management in Qatar, the Gulf region, and comparable health systems globally. It provides timely and policy-relevant insights to inform national strategies and aligns with international disaster preparedness frameworks such as the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction. Specifically, the findings are directed to the MoPH for workforce policy, to hospital and primary-care disaster-training accreditation within HMC and PHCC, and to emergency-management planning units as the primary policy audience. By highlighting workforce development, intersectoral collaboration, and institutional readiness, this study supports strategic planning efforts for managing complex disaster risks regionally and internationally. The comprehensive lens of this study offers practical value and holds relevance for multiple stakeholders involved in disaster preparedness and response. It addresses priorities for policymakers and national health authorities, including evidence-based policy development and integration, strategic alignment with the international framework, and workforce planning. It also reflects the priorities of healthcare organizations, such as institutional readiness, interdepartmental coordination, internal response protocol, and workforce development strategies aligned with national preparedness goals. Emergency management agencies are similarly concerned with integrated response frameworks and system-level vulnerability assessments. In addition, the study aligns with the objectives of academic institutions and professional development bodies focused on curriculum development, scalable strategies for strengthening workforce capability, interdisciplinary training, and competency development. More broadly, it contributes to ongoing efforts aimed at strengthening disaster preparedness and resilience across healthcare systems operating in the face of complex and evolving disaster threats.

Perceptions of disaster management among healthcare practitioners.

HCPs demonstrated good perceptions regarding their knowledge of disaster management (median [IQR] = 41 [34–44], Min–Max = 18–55). More than 50% of participants considered their knowledge adequate in identifying situations that require immediate reporting to health authorities. However, approximately 40% perceived their knowledge as basic when it came to organizing disaster awareness campaigns.

Similarly, HCPs exhibited good perceptions of their attitudes toward disaster preparedness (median [IQR] = 46 [43–50], Min–Max = 10–55). More than half of the HCPs strongly agreed on the necessity of having national and institutional disaster management plans and emphasized the importance of integrating disaster-related training into healthcare education and professional development.

In contrast, perceptions of practice in disaster management activities were fair (median [IQR] = 31 [24–39], Min–Max = 2–50). Approximately 30% of HCPs reported frequent participation in workplace disaster drills, while a similar proportion noted occasional involvement in improving their institution’s disaster plans. Additionally, 23% reported occasional participation in managing and supervising volunteers during disaster situations.

HCPs demonstrated good perceptions regarding their willingness to continue practicing during disasters (median [IQR] = 23 [1–24], Min–Max = 6–30). Half of the respondents indicated they would continue performing their duties during disasters, even when required to work additional hours. However, around 30% were neutral regarding their willingness to work if essential disaster management equipment was not available.

Lastly, perceptions of organization-based management were also good (median [IQR] = 43 [40–50], Min–Max = 10–50). Over 40% of HCPs strongly agreed that their organizations recognized their contributions to disaster response efforts. Additionally, around 50% agreed that their institutions possessed adequate medical supplies to accommodate increased patient loads and maintain a safe working environment during disasters.

The Mann-Whitney U (Table 3) indicated that HCPs who had participated in previous disaster management courses or programs reported significantly higher perceptions of disaster management knowledge (median [IQR] = 42 [8], p < 0.001, r = 0.29), attitude (median [IQR] = 46 [7], p = 0.008, r = 0.14), practice (median [IQR] = 33 [14], p < 0.001, r = 0.24), willingness to practice (median [IQR] = 23 [5], p = 0.037, r = 0.11), and organization-based management (median [IQR] = 44 [10], p = 0.034, r = 0.11) compared to those who had not received such training.

Furthermore, those with administrative or management duties within their current worksite were found to have significantly higher perceptions of knowledge (median [IQR] = 42 [8], p < 0.001, r = 0.18), attitude (median [IQR] = 48 [7], p < 0.001, r = 0.20), practice (median [IQR] = 34 [15], p < 0.001, r = 0.18), and willingness to practice (median [IQR] = 24 [6], p = 0.005, r = 0.14) than those without such responsibilities.

Moreover, previous experience during national emergencies was associated with differences in disaster management perceptions. HCPs who were employed in Qatar during the 2017 Blockade or the COVID-19 pandemic reported significantly higher perceptions of disaster management knowledge (median [IQR] = 41 [10], p < 0.001, r = 0.16), practice (median [IQR] = 31 [15], p = 0.016, r = 0.12), and organization-based management (median [IQR] = 44 [10], p < 0.001, r = 0.17) compared to those who were not working during these events.

Additionally, gender-based differences were observed. Males reported significantly higher perceptions of disaster management attitude (median [IQR] = 47 [7]) compared to females (median [IQR] = 44 [7], p = 0.005, r = 0.14).

The Kruskal-Wallis H test (Table 3) identified additional significant differences across groups with multiple categories. Years of professional experience were significantly associated with perceptions of knowledge (p < 0.001, ε²= 0.07). HCPs with ≤ 5 years of experience had significantly lower perceptions of disaster management knowledge (median [IQR] = 34.0 [12]) than those with 11–15 years (median [IQR] = 41.5 [9]) or more than 20 years of experience (median [IQR] = 42.5 [8]).

Significant differences were also found in HCPs’ perceptions of willingness to practice across professional groups (p = 0.004, ε²=0.02). Allied HCPs reported significantly higher willingness to practice during disasters (median [IQR] = 24.0 [6]) compared to nurses and physicians (median [IQR] = 22.0 [5], p = 0.024; median [IQR] = 22.0 [6], p = 0.008, respectively). Similarly, differences were also found in HCPs’ perceptions of organization-based management across professional groups (p = 0.004, ε²=0.02). Physicians reported statistically significantly lower perceptions of organization-based management (median [IQR] = 41.0 [13]) than nurses (median [IQR] = 44.0 [10], p = 0.003) and other allied HCPs (median [IQR] = 44.0 [9], p = 0.035).

A significant difference in the perceptions of disaster management knowledge was also found across worksites (p = 0.004, ε²= 0.03), with HCPs working at PHCC reporting significantly higher perceptions (median [IQR] = 43.0 [12]) than those at HMC (median [IQR] = 39.0 [11], p = 0.002).

Qualitative phase

Forty-one HCPs participated in the six FGs, with 5, 6, 6, 15, 5, and 4 participants in each FG. The FGs were generally heterogeneous, constituting participants from various professions, except for two FGs at HMC that were profession-specific (i.e., physicians and nurses), given their large population size. The participants represented a range of professions, including medicine, nursing, pharmacy, dentistry, paramedicine, lab technology, respiratory therapy, and dietetics. The thematic analysis of HCPs’ perceptions of disaster management revealed insights related to recent disasters such as the Gulf Crisis 2017, COVID-19, and mass casualty events (FIFA World Cup 2022), across the four key phases of disaster management: prevention/mitigation, preparedness, response, and recovery. Theme 1. Prevention or mitigation of disasters

This theme describes the proactive measures that HCPs take to minimize the vulnerabilities and impacts of potential disasters before they occur. These measures involve establishing comprehensive and strategic plans, conducting risk assessments, enforcing regulations, and educating the community. The theme also emphasizes the need for advanced predictive technologies.

Subtheme 1.1. Prevention and control strategies

HCPs across institutions discussed their efforts to create well-established plans and strategies in anticipation of potential disasters. These plans outlined specific roles, responsibilities, and procedures to follow, in addition to resource management and personnel training. HCPs explained how disaster management was operationalized through both central and facility-level protocols, reflecting an institutional commitment to comprehensive planning.

“At PHCC, we have several Emergency and Disaster Preparedness (EDP) plans that follow our corporation’s guidelines. Each health center also has its own local plan, with clear pathways, assigned personnel, and defined resources.” P11-Nur-PHCC.

However, a discrepancy in HCPs’ awareness and familiarity with disaster management plans was revealed. While some HCPs acknowledged the existence of accessible and updated policies, others reported a lack of effective dissemination and the need for regular training. This indicates a disconnect between policy development and practical implementation.

“We have a policy that is approachable and updated. We just need proper training on how to implement it because without practice, these procedures can be easily forgotten.” P12-Phy-PHCC.

A key feature of the disaster plans was the command structures that were put in place, especially during high-risk events. These structures ensured that key decision-makers could coordinate effectively across departments during a disaster.

“We had a clear command structure assigned to each stadium during the FIFA event. The overall coordination was managed through the National Health Command Center.” P29-Allied-HMC.

The HCPs pointed to the iterative nature of disaster planning, stressing the importance of updating these plans regularly to keep them relevant and aligned with the latest best practices. They also presented actions taken to evaluate the effectiveness of these plans and efforts aimed at enhancing the ability to prevent disasters or minimize their impact, such as using real-world experiences for testing and improvement.

“The Arab Cup served as a trial run to evaluate and refine our deployment strategies for the World Cup.” P29-Allied-HMC.

Subtheme 1.2. Risk assessment and mitigation strategies

Many practitioners, particularly those in laboratory and administrative roles, were directly involved in conducting risk assessments and developing mitigation strategies to minimize the impact of the current and potential future outbreaks. Such practices signify a shift toward data-informed preparedness, where risks are anticipated and addressed systematically.

“We developed a risk registry and implemented risk management procedures.” P21-Allied-HMC.

Implementing preventive measures to prevent the spread of infections was a key focus during the mitigation phase, particularly during COVID-19. Special attention was paid to managing personal protective equipment (PPE) and maintaining sanitation standards, which reinforces the role of daily operational practices in controlling disease transmission.

“I was responsible for ensuring that our ambulances met infection control standards, including performing deep cleaning after transporting each patient.” P29-Allied-HMC.

Pharmacists and other HCPs also took on broader public health roles by educating the community about prevention strategies and the importance of early reporting. This reflects a commitment to disaster risk reduction beyond clinical care.

“I educate my community members on how to respond appropriately during disasters, emphasizing that early reporting can help address issues before they escalate.” P1-Phar-MoPH.

Moreover, HCPs working in the higher healthcare authorities focused on minimizing risks by enforcing regulations, contributing to compliance monitoring, and system accountability.

“During COVID-19, our role involved enforcing regulations set by the MoPH by conducting random inspection visits to ensure compliance with established guidelines.” P4-Phy-MoPH.

Finally, advancements in surveillance and predictive analytics were identified as crucial future tools for risk mitigation. This highlights the growing recognition of digital innovation in enhancing early warning systems and data-driven responses.

“Definitely, 2024 is the year of artificial intelligence in predicting diseases and identifying the locations of focal outbreaks.” P7-Phy-PHCC.

Theme 2. Preparedness for disasters

This theme highlights the individual clinical capabilities, psychological readiness, and willingness to respond during disasters. It emphasizes the importance of structured training initiatives, experiential learning from past crises, adequate mental health support, and the professional commitment of practitioners despite challenges.

Subtheme 2.1. Strengthened capabilities and expertise among HCPs

Educational and training initiatives played a critical role in preparing HCPs for disaster management. These initiatives resulted in equipping practitioners with the knowledge, skills, and confidence necessary to respond effectively to crises.

“HCPs know the methods, possess the skills, and have access to the necessary supplies. I now feel confident that we can respond rapidly and effectively whenever needed.” P6-Phy-PHCC.

Moreover, HCPs consistently highlighted the importance of expertise within their teams as a critical factor in effective disaster response. Experience gained from previous and present events was frequently mentioned as invaluable. Several participants stressed that the disasters reflected an experiential learning process where real-world crisis management provided steep learning curves.

“COVID-19 and the 2017 Gulf Crisis served as critical preparedness experiences for future disasters. We now have multiple sources for medication procurement and are better equipped to manage supply chain disruptions and care for large numbers of patients.” P32-Phar-HMC.

Subtheme 2.2. Educational and training initiatives and preparedness programs

All healthcare institutions implemented comprehensive training programs to enhance HCPs’ preparedness for disaster management (e.g., mass casualty management, infection control, and patient handling). Participants emphasized that education and training were not peripheral to preparedness, but its foundation. However, they also made clear that the effectiveness of training programs depends not simply on their availability, but on their relevance, frequency, and ability to simulate real crisis demands. Role-specific training activities were viewed as foundational for equipping HCPs with relevant skills and ensuring their contributions aligned with their scope of practice.

“We accredited a range of activities to support practitioner engagement, each tailored to specific professional scopes of practice.” P3-Phy-MoPH.

Preparedness initiatives were described as multi-model, combining awareness sessions, face-to-face training, formal online courses, e-learning platforms, and AI-based tools. These platforms provided continuous education to HCPs and allowed them to remain updated on new developments.

“Each health center designates an Emergency and Disaster Preparedness (EDP) champion who conducts awareness sessions…that introduce potential disasters and guide staff on appropriate response strategies.” P10-Phar-PHCC.

Participants from the PHCC and HMC indicated that their institutions conduct mock simulations and practical drills, which enable HCPs to practice decision-making, ensure coordination, and practice patient transfers, infection control measures, and the use of PPE.

“We conduct national-level simulation exercises once or twice a year, involving multiple ministries. These mock crises simulate emergency response efforts from beginning to end.” P19-Allied-HMC.

However, virtual training simulations were recommended because physical drills can interfere with daily healthcare operations and patient care.

“Virtual training is now widely implemented and is nearly as effective as in-person drills. It enables staff to receive necessary training without disrupting daily workflow.” P9-Phy-PHCC.

Gaps in disaster training were acknowledged. Several HCPs acknowledged that while they have expertise in their specific health discipline, there is a gap in training for broader disaster management, such as incident command, surge planning, triage under resource scarcity, and mass casualty logistics. In addition, they expressed concerns about the predominantly theoretical nature of disaster management training. Participants also highlighted that disaster training exercises often center on leadership participation, with limited engagement of frontline staff, which restricts opportunities for comprehensive team-based preparedness.

“Tabletop exercises only involve the leads… the frontliners are nearly never involved.” P9-Phy-PHCC.

Finally, the absence of mandatory disaster management training in some healthcare facilities was perceived as a systemic weakness that undermines national preparedness, as it limits the number of HCPs who are adequately prepared for disaster situations. Hence, the need to institutionalize disaster training as a standard competency across healthcare settings is underscored, so it becomes a baseline expectation rather than an institutional afterthought.

“If disaster management training were made mandatory, it would greatly enhance preparedness.” P3-Phy-MoPH.

Subtheme 2.3. Psychosocial preparedness and mental health support

Participants consistently emphasized that disaster preparedness must extend beyond logistics and clinical protocols to address the emotional and psychological demands placed on them. Emotional exhaustion was often the result of overlapping pressures: professional responsibilities, institutional demands, fear of uncertainty, and concern for loved ones.

“We faced multiple pressures at once: numerous new policies, staff shortages, growing case numbers, and concern for our families. It was difficult to manage everything while trying to follow protocols and ensure personal safety.” P15-Allied-PHCC.

Despite the challenges, participants also spoke about the individual coping mechanisms they developed to manage their fear and stress, including emotional reflection and developing a sense of gratitude. This highlights the importance of internal resilience and the role of mindset in sustaining mental health during disasters.

“I believe the first step is to acknowledge challenges and negative thoughts. From there, we can cultivate gratitude …” P37-Phy-HMC.

Equally important was the presence of empathetic leadership and team cohesion. Supervisors who created safe spaces for open and supportive communication helped foster emotional stability among their teams.

“Teamwork plays a vital role in maintaining a positive spirit. Regular departmental meetings provide valuable support from both supervisors and colleagues.” P31-Phar-HMC.

The establishment of formal support systems was viewed as an essential resource that legitimized mental health care as part of disaster planning. This initiative ensures that staff well-being is systematically addressed rather than left to individual coping alone.

“Designated areas within health centers were established to offer psychological support for staff. Additionally, a 24-hour hotline was made available in both Arabic and English for those experiencing stress or anxiety.” P10-Phar-PHCC.

Subtheme 2.4. Willingness to participate in disasters

HCPs’ willingness to engage actively in disaster management efforts was rooted in a combination of professional commitment, moral obligation, collective responsibility, and a deep understanding of the importance of their roles in protecting public health. HCPs across the different worksites perceived their roles during disasters as extending far beyond their typical job descriptions. This sense of purpose not only sustained engagement but also fostered solidarity across teams, reinforcing a collective identity built on service.

“Understanding your role and its impact on patient care and the wider community creates a shared sense of purpose. Despite the challenges, we were motivated by the knowledge that our efforts served the greater good.” P8-Phy-PHCC.

At the same time, this commitment often came with personal sacrifices. Some HCPs made difficult choices, including distancing themselves from their families to protect them. Such decisions, while professionally justified, carried significant emotional weight and underscored the need for institutional recognition and support to sustain this level of dedication over time.

“I chose to isolate myself from my family … Being alone for such an extended period had a significant emotional impact.” P33-Phy-HMC.

Theme 3. Response in disasters

This theme describes the multifaceted and evolving roles of HCPs during the disaster response phase, including frontline care, diagnostic support, medication management, and volunteer coordination. It reflects their adaptability to changing conditions, their commitment to maintaining professional standards, and their reliance on clear role definitions and technology to ensure an effective and organized response.

Subtheme 3.1. Frontline medical care and patient management

HCPs demonstrated the broadened scope of their duties during the disaster response phase, as well as their commitment to patient safety and service continuity under pressure. Nurses and physicians were involved in triage, patient assessment, and patient treatment. Laboratory technologists’ role focused on providing rapid covid-19 testing, validating laboratory machines, and ensuring accurate results. Pharmacists, nurses, and paramedics were actively involved in managing medication supplies and administering vaccinations.

“During the pandemic, we established a new pharmacy in ras laffan in coordination with the nursing unit to ensure the supply of medications.” P32-Phar-HMC.

HCPs reflected on the dual responsibility they face in ensuring their own safety while caring for patients during disasters. These responsibilities were balanced through the use of simulation training and the implementation of strict safety protocols, enabling HCPs to manage both high-risk exposure and mass casualty situations effectively.

“As respiratory therapists, we practiced on mannequins during covid-19 because of the high risk of viral exposure.” P28-Allied-HMC.

HCPs expressed that automation, such as robotic systems for lab testing, could play a significant role in enhancing infection control measures, protecting HCPs, and maintaining workforce availability during disasters.

“Robotic technology can be used to analyze blood samples, helping to reduce the risk of infection.” P14-Nur-PHCC.

Subtheme 3.2. Adaptation to evolving situations and adherence to the standards

HCPs described the need to continuously adapt their practices in response to the evolving nature of disasters. For example, the regulatory bodies in Qatar implemented flexible licensing processes that allowed HCPs to begin work on short notice without completing the full evaluation procedures. Adaptability in responding to disasters also included adjusting testing protocols, managing the surge in patient volume, and implementing new procedures to meet the changing demands of the situation.

“Each time a new mutation emerges, we not only adjust our strategy but also modify the testing process.” P26-Allied-HMC.

Moreover, the HCPs shared examples of their innovative infection control strategies that were developed at the facility level. They demonstrated how local leadership initiated context-specific interventions to reduce transmission, which shows a culture of continuous problem-solving.

“Managers implemented window swapping, which led to a noticeable decrease in positive cases among nurses in the following weeks.” P14-Nur-PHCC.

Redeployment of staff to unfamiliar roles exposed skill gaps that digital tools could help address. Several participants called for clinical decision-support systems, such as AI tools, to assist with diagnosis and treatment decision-making. This reinforces the growing perception that AI can enhance clinical confidence and effectiveness in situations where rapid upskilling is required, which is a strategic asset in future disaster preparedness frameworks.

“Physicians who were originally working in outpatient departments were reassigned to intensive care units, where they lacked the necessary experience to function effectively. This highlighted the need for smart technologies.” P22-Allied-HMC.

While adaptation to evolving situations was necessary, HCPs shared stories of their collective dedication to upholding stringent standards during disaster response. This underscores a culture of professionalism and reinforces the ethical resilience of the healthcare workforce in Qatar.

“Despite the many challenges faced during the COVID-19 period, we made every effort to comply with the required protocols.” P5-Nur-MoPH.

Subtheme 3.3. Coordinating volunteer efforts

HCPs noted that volunteer recruitment and coordination were central to filling workforce gaps during the disaster response phase. HCPs, particularly those in managerial positions, were heavily involved in coordinating the large number of volunteers recruited during the response phase. Volunteers with clinical experience supported key functions such as laboratory testing, sample processing, and patient registration, while those with non-clinical backgrounds contributed to leadership and logistical coordination, especially in vaccination centers. Technology was used to facilitate volunteer assignments and ensure smooth operations. A flexible strategy also allowed for expedited training and temporary licensing to address workforce gaps efficiently.

“Supervising volunteers required an all-hands-on-deck approach. Physicians and nurses without active licenses received appropriate training and temporary licenses to support the care of patients. Volunteers without healthcare backgrounds contributed in leadership and management roles.” P3-Phys-MoPH.

As HCPs recognized the crucial role of volunteers in disaster situations, they recommended embedding trained volunteer teams into departments as a proactive measure. These groups would receive ongoing training and be ready to mobilize during crises. This forward-looking recommendation suggests a shift toward sustained disaster preparedness.

“Establishing a well-trained volunteer base within each department or facility, equipped to manage emergency situations, would be highly beneficial.” P5-Nur-MoPH.

Theme 4. Recovery from disasters

This theme describes HCPs’ roles in restoring operations, supporting patients’ recovery, and providing psychosocial care. It also reflects their efforts to evaluate response experiences and apply lessons learned to improve future disaster response.

Subtheme 4.1. Psychological support and patient follow-up

In the recovery phase of COVID-19, HCPs focused on offering emotional support to help patients cope with the stress and ensuring continued long-term care for vulnerable patients, particularly those with chronic conditions like cancer. These efforts were crucial in promoting patient recovery after the acute phase of the pandemic, which reflects an awareness among HCPs of the chronic impacts of disaster-related disruptions.

“We provided psychological support and ongoing follow-up for patients, particularly those with cancer who were diagnosed with COVID-19.” P39-Nur-HMC.

Subtheme 4.2. Integrating learning and evaluation for improved disaster management

Participants emphasized the importance of documenting experiences to critically assess their responses and extract valuable lessons that can inform and foster team confidence in future actions. Formal documentation, team debriefings, and structured reviews were seen as essential for transforming experience into institutional knowledge.

One of the most important lessons learned was the need for flexible HR systems to manage staffing shortages during disasters. However, proper and timely training for new staff was equally important to prevent avoidable unpleasant incidents. This highlights the tension between rapid workforce expansion and maintaining quality standards, reinforcing the need for structured onboarding and refresher educational programs.

“A large number of employees were recruited … without adequate training… they might have lost the skills.” P14-Nur-PHCC.

Another lesson learned was the importance of embedding infection control and safety protocols into routine healthcare practices. The normalization of safety and hygiene protocols after the pandemic marks a shift toward sustained institutional preparedness and resilience in routine operations.

“All healthcare facilities in Qatar now adhere to safety protocols and place strong emphasis on training HCPs in infection control and safety measures.” P3-Phy-MoPH.

The pandemic also highlighted the importance of effective coordination between departments as a key part of healthcare delivery success. HCPs highlighted how organizational learning during resource shortages led to systemic improvements in communication and supply chain coordination.

“In the early phase of COVID-19, we faced issues like a shortage of N95 masks and inadequate swab supplies. We created a structured communication system through the Major Incident Command Center and developed a corporate emergency communications plan to ensure effective coordination.” P7-Phy-PHCC.

HCPs also perceived telemedicine and AI-enabled remote monitoring systems as key advancements during recent disasters, as they ensured continuity of care while minimizing disaster impacts. This shift toward hybrid care models was seen as not only reactive but also transformational for future healthcare delivery.

“Telemedicine was introduced in response to limited access to healthcare services. It was definitely an effective alternative for continued care, as it proved highly beneficial for individuals who were unable to leave their homes due to health concerns.” P2-Den-MoPH.

Theme 5. Organizational and governmental roles

This theme describes the multifaceted efforts taken by the different healthcare organizations and the government of Qatar across all stages of disaster management, emphasizing how leadership, planning, and resource allocation contribute to effective preparedness and response. It also highlights the integration of best practices and collaboration with local and international partners that directly influence public health outcomes and system resilience during disasters.

Subtheme 5.1. Leadership and governance

HCPs highlighted the government and healthcare organizations’ responsibility in establishing clear leadership roles, conducting strategic planning and training, and ensuring that all relevant departments are prepared for various disaster scenarios. Importantly, the participants viewed leadership development as a cornerstone of Qatar’s disaster preparedness strategy. Their observations pointed to an intentional investment in cultivating capable leaders across healthcare roles. This reflects a shift toward anticipatory governance where building human capital is prioritized as part of a resilient system.

“There has been significant improvement in leadership, particularly in the area of disaster management, with the introduction of numerous leadership training courses.” P3-Phy-MoPH.

HCPs also emphasized the importance of a shared vision and effective communication and coordination among leaders.

Participants also underscored the importance of unified direction and alignment across teams, enabled by strong leadership coordination. The presence of a clearly communicated strategic vision reduced operational ambiguity and improved the consistency of actions taken across institutions. This highlights how clarity at the leadership level can enhance execution at the frontline, supporting more coherent and timely responses during disasters.

“Once the leaders established a shared vision, they communicated it to their respective teams, which facilitated smooth and effective implementation.” 8-Phy-PHCC.

In addition, HCPs acknowledged the value of leveraging international expertise to inform national planning. The integration of global insights into local preparedness strategies illustrates a commitment to adaptive learning and alignment with global standards. It demonstrates how Qatar’s health system positioned itself to benefit from cross-contextual experiences, enhancing its ability to respond to complex disasters.

“We drew on international resources, such as the 2018 FIFA World Cup in Russia, to guide our planning. The government played a key role in engaging international experts to support these efforts.” P34-Phy-HMC.

Subtheme 5.2. Initiatives in preparedness and resource allocation

HCPs reported a recognition of the proactive and well-coordinated disaster response in Qatar, particularly evident in the rapid expansion of services and the strategic redeployment of staff. These efforts reflect organizational agility and effective resource management, which were perceived to reduce morbidity, prevent loss of life, and ensure continuity of care during crises.

“The preparedness of the MoPH was evident through the rapid expansion of services … and the redeployment of staff …. Resources were effectively distributed to healthcare services throughout Qatar.” P38-Nur-HMC.

However, HCPs raised concerns about over-reliance on centralized facilities, especially in diagnostics and emergency services, as it caused inefficiencies in disaster response. These include delays in accessing critical information, timely testing, reporting, and subsequent clinical and public health actions. The participants’ suggestion for decentralization reflects a call for a strategic shift toward distributed service delivery models, which can enhance flexibility, reduce systemic strain, and enable faster local-level responses in future emergencies.

“We currently rely on a national central laboratory, but I believe that decentralizing testing in future emergencies could help resolve the significant bottlenecks we experienced during the pandemic.” P7-Phy-PHCC.

Theme 6. Communication, collaboration, and multidisciplinary teamwork

This theme explores the perception of HCPs regarding the essential role of effective communication, interprofessional collaboration, and coordinated teamwork in disaster management. It emphasizes the need for structured communication systems, technological integration, and clearly defined roles to support timely information sharing and collaborative response efforts across healthcare disciplines and sectors.

Subtheme 6.1. Communication and information dissemination

HCPs consistently emphasized that effective communication was crucial in disaster management. Intra-organizational communication systems, such as daily coordination meetings and direct reporting, were seen as crucial mechanisms to pre-empt failures and sustain real-time situational awareness. This demonstrates how micro-level communication routines can enhance local preparedness and support organizational resilience.

“During the COVID-19 period, one positive aspect in the laboratory department was the daily meetings held with the chairperson to review the day’s events and identify potential issues to prevent future failures.” P20-Allied-HMC.

At the macro level, coordination between healthcare institutions and ministries was seen as essential for managing the flow of information to the public and supporting inter-agency disaster response.

“We were responsible for providing information to our upper management, which collaborated with the MoPH and the Ministry of Interior to ensure the community received timely and accurate information.” P18-Allied-HMC.

Despite the recognized importance of effective communication, some HCPs noted areas where communication across departments could be strengthened. Instances of inconsistent messaging contributed to confusion and coordination challenges. These observations underscore the need to strengthen communication structures to support clearer and more coordinated disaster response efforts. To address these gaps, participants recommended establishing a national communication framework and forming a high-level disaster committee with representation from all relevant authorities.

Furthermore, HCPs emphasized the importance of effective communication with the community during disasters, stressing the need for timely and accurate information flow. Social media emerged as a powerful tool for real-time updates and public engagement, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic.

“It would be beneficial to have a dedicated platform for providing public updates to help individuals stay informed and better prepared.” P40-Nur-HMC.

Beyond conventional tools, HCPs consistently highlighted the vital role of technology in enhancing communication and public awareness during disasters. Technological tools were seen as essential for streamlining information flow, reducing confusion, and improving coordination and response times. Participants highlighted that the use of digital platforms like the Ehteraz mobile application and widespread public information campaigns played a vital role in keeping the population informed about health measures, isolation protocols, and risk mitigation strategies. In parallel, advanced systems, including mobile command units and satellite-enabled devices, were used to maintain uninterrupted communication across critical response teams.

“We have mobile command units equipped with live streaming to the National Health Command Center, as well as body cameras, drones, and satellite phones to ensure uninterrupted communication during disasters.” P29-Allied-HMC.

Nevertheless, a group of HCPs noted that a lack of technological integration across departments made it difficult for HCPs to share vital information. They recommended the development of centralized communication systems to connect different departments, as well as a standardized communication protocol.

“The[electronic health] systems should be integrated: pharmacists should be aware of relevant lab results, and doctors should have insight into what is happening in the ICU. All departments should be connected through a unified platform.” P22-Allied-HMC.

Subtheme 6.2. Collaboration and coordination

Collaboration among various healthcare disciplines and sectors was identified as a cornerstone of managing the complex needs that arose during disasters.

“During the COVID-19 period, everything was based on collaboration and teamwork among nurses, medical technicians, physicians, pharmacists, and even volunteers from other departments and sectors.” P17-Phar-PHCC.

HCPs also highlighted the significance of their partnerships with other external agencies.

“There was strong collaboration among PHCC, Sidra, Qatar University, Hamad Bin Khalifa University, and our laboratory team.” P19-Allied-HMC.

Moreover, HCPs emphasized the value of collaboration with other countries to learn from their experiences with disasters.

“We can draw valuable lessons from countries that have experienced disasters such as floods or earthquakes, using their experiences to strengthen and refine our own policies and preparedness plans.” P10-Phar-PHCC.

Despite the strong collaborative culture, ambiguity around intra- and inter-professional roles was cited as a barrier to effective collaboration and, hence, disaster response. A few HCPs highlighted the need for role awareness training and cross-disciplinary understanding beyond unit-level roles, which reinforces the value of interprofessional education and systems-wide simulation exercises.

“I was surprised to learn about the roles of some colleagues … multidisciplinary meetings would be more helpful for improving role understanding.” P29-Allied-HMC.

Theme 7. Community engagement

This theme explores the perceptions of HCPs’ community awareness, empowerment, involvement, and responsibility in disaster situations. It highlights the importance of community-focused education initiatives to build disaster response capabilities, as well as recognizing community support for healthcare efforts and addressing occasional resistance to health measures.

Subtheme 7.1. Community awareness

HCPs noted that community awareness of disaster management has generally increased after the COVID-19 pandemic. This suggests that recent health emergencies served as informal public education campaigns, increasing health literacy and potentially aiding future disaster responses.

“The public is now much more aware of basic preventive measures, such as practicing infection control, protecting themselves from illnesses like the flu, and getting vaccinated early.” P3-Phy-MoPH.

Despite this progress, some participants highlighted limited community outreach services. Outreach services were perceived as reactive rather than proactive, with insufficient infrastructure to support consistent community engagement.

“Community outreach remains very limited. While home health services exist, we lack dedicated field staff, and these services do not function as true outreach initiatives.” P7-Phy-PHCC.

To address this, participants suggested implementing widespread community-focused interventions, integrating disaster education into schools, organizing public awareness days, and training non-medical volunteers to assist during crises. These proposals highlight the need for institutional and governmental support to implement community-based preparedness programs that are both recurring and inclusive.

“If disaster preparedness is taught in schools or included in school activities, basic training from an early age would help ensure that the entire community is better equipped to handle disasters.” P14-Nur-PHCC.

A laboratory technologist suggested developing interactive, gamified learning platforms. Such interactive tools can improve retention of disaster preparedness concepts, particularly in populations less likely to engage with traditional health communication. This forward-looking approach reflects the potential of interactive technology in making disaster education more accessible and engaging.

“If we could develop a program, such as a game, that allows people to learn how to protect themselves and respond to crises while playing, it would greatly enhance crisis awareness, especially among young people.” P19-Allied-HMC.

Subtheme 7.2. Community responsibility

Beyond awareness, HCPs highlighted the community’s role in disaster management and noted instances where empowering individuals with responsibility supported healthcare efforts. This demonstrates a shift in disaster management from centralized control to a shared responsibility model, which can alleviate system strain during high-demand periods.

“The experience of COVID-19 showed the value of giving greater responsibility to the community. People were provided with home testing kits and were instructed on how to use them and manage certain symptoms on their own.” P18-Allied-HMC.

Yet, few participants acknowledged that public compliance was inconsistent, which indicates a need for trust-building and culturally sensitive risk communication strategies to bridge gaps between public health policy and community behavior.

“Many individuals in the community were hesitant to take the vaccine, and not everyone perceived COVID-19 with the seriousness it required.” P15-Allied-PHCC.

The study indicated that data from both phases confirmed the critical role of structured training and organizational support in being associated with enhanced disaster management capabilities. Subgroup analyses showed that HCPs who had participated in disaster management training or who had experience working during the 2017 Blockade or the COVID-19 pandemic reported significantly higher perceptions across multiple domains, including knowledge, practice, and organizational preparedness. These findings were reinforced by qualitative accounts underscoring the importance of hands-on learning, realistic simulations, and effective leadership. Moreover, qualitative data revealed adaptive practices not captured in the survey, including expanded clinical roles, logistical coordination, and the use of digital technologies in infection control and remote care. These insights complemented the quantitative assessment by illustrating provider adaptability and system-level responsiveness. Further, qualitative insights clarified the quantitative finding of lower perceptions of disaster management practice despite high reported knowledge and attitude. HCPs indicated challenges, including theoretical rather than practical training approaches, limited frontline staff involvement in training, and the absence of mandatory disaster training, highlighting barriers to effectively translating theoretical knowledge into applied disaster management practices. These insights also contextualized subgroup differences, illustrating how prior experience during events such as the blockade and pandemic was associated with stronger practical skills, resilience, and collaboration, which may help explain higher preparedness perceptions among experienced providers. Despite positive perceptions of organizational support in the survey data, qualitative findings revealed persistent operational challenges, including centralized decision-making, fragmented communication, and inconsistent implementation of protocols. These tensions exposed a gap between perceived preparedness and actual system functionality, underscoring areas for improvement in institutional readiness and coordination.

Discussion

This nationwide study is the first in Qatar to comprehensively assess HCPs’ perceptions of disaster management, including their preparedness, readiness, and views on organizational efforts across all phases of the disaster management cycle. It included a diverse sample of HCPs, representing the major healthcare professions in proportions that closely reflect Qatar’s public healthcare workforce. The explanatory sequential mixed-methods design provided in-depth insights and strengthened the validity of the findings.

Contextual influences on disaster management: lessons from national crises and mass events

Although the primary aim of this study was to elicit general insights into disaster-related roles, institutional practices, and perceived competencies, regardless of the specific type of disaster, participants’ reflections were naturally shaped by the national context, often referencing notable real-life events (i.e., the COVID-19 pandemic, the 2017 Gulf Crisis, and the FIFA World Cup 2022). These diverse events offered a unique opportunity to explore how HCPs understand and operationalize the disaster management cycle in different scenarios.

COVID-19 was a public health emergency characterized by clinical surge capacity, infection control, and mental health stressors. HCPs described front-line roles in triage, care delivery, PPE enforcement, and interdisciplinary collaboration, all supported by national-level coordination and daily operational adjustments42,43. In contrast, the Gulf Crisis was a prolonged geopolitical disruption. It exposed vulnerabilities in supply chains and highlighted the need for administrative resilience, diversified procurement, and high-level planning24,25. HCPs took on more strategic roles, ensuring service continuity under logistical constraints24,25. The FIFA World Cup 2022, while not a disaster, served as a preventive example of deliberate preparedness, system simulation, and risk mitigation. HCPs engaged in proactive planning, inter-agency coordination, and simulation exercises, viewing the event as a stress test for institutional readiness. While the former two events prompted reactive adaptation, the latter reflected anticipatory planning and preparedness.

HCPs’ knowledge, attitudes, and willingness to practice

The integrated findings of this study revealed that HCPs in Qatar demonstrated good knowledge, positive attitudes, and a willingness to continue their duties during disasters. Quantitative data revealed that HCPs were aware of their roles and chain of command, believed in the importance of disaster plans, and were willing to work additional hours during a disaster. Qualitative data offered a more nuanced picture that highlighted professional experience, training, internal motivation, professional ethics, and workplace culture as facilitators. These insights were consistent with findings from low and high-disaster-risk settings such as the United Arab Emirates and Malaysia, where HCPs reported positive attitudes and moderate-to-high knowledge levels, but faced challenges translating these into consistent preparedness due to limited disaster training exposure12,44. In Singapore, a low-risk but well-resourced context, HCPs felt unprepared but willing to respond to disasters as part of their professional competency45. They reported that their individual preparedness depends on previous disaster training and prior experience in disaster response45.

The role of training and professional experience in disaster preparedness

Disaster management training initiatives were key to improving HCPs’ capabilities46–48, and were associated with significantly higher perceptions of disaster management practices in this study. The participants in this study had engaged in prior training and workplace drills, which reflects HCPs’ strong commitment to preparedness. Ongoing, structured education remains essential for developing effective disaster response skills49. Programs like the Emergency Disaster and Major Incidents (EDAMS), Hospital Major Incident Medical Management System (HMIMMS), and other mass casualty management courses play a crucial role50. These training programs, offered by institutions like HMC, PHCC, and MoPH, included hands-on simulations and practical drills that were essential in bridging the gap between theoretical knowledge and real-world application50. The use of simulation-based training, especially in preparation for high-profile events like the FIFA World Cup 2022, helped HCPs refine their skills in communication, coordination, and incident management51–54. Notably, professional experience also appeared to play a significant role in effective disaster management, as HCPs with long experience perceived themselves as more knowledgeable and valued their ability to adapt quickly and implement effective solutions. This was supported by similar findings in Qatar, where experienced pharmacists had higher awareness, attitude, and readiness scores39.

Experiential learning from real-world events

Findings from high-risk countries suggest that prior exposure to disasters is associated with better disaster preparedness and readiness55. A study in Iran showed that previous major incident experience was linked to improved performance, coordination, and policy-making in subsequent events56. In Japan, a country with an advanced disaster preparedness infrastructure, a positive association was found between previous disaster experience, training, and the existence of disaster plans among community nurses57. The context of this study, conducted after significant real-world disasters (COVID-19, the Gulf Crisis, FIFA World Cup 2022), likely influenced the findings. Although these events differed in nature and urgency, they all provided HCPs with firsthand disaster management experience and enhanced their readiness58. Participants reported greater confidence, stronger teamwork, and improved disaster protocols, ranging from digital integration to flexible staffing and policy updates. Quantitative findings supported this, showing that HCPs who had experienced COVID-19 or the Gulf Crisis reported significantly higher perceptions of disaster knowledge, practice, and organizational management. Qualitative insights further revealed that each event shaped distinct competencies (i.e., clinical, strategic, or preventive), highlighting the value of experiential learning in disaster preparedness. Integrating reactive and preventive disaster experiences into planning enables Qatar’s healthcare system to develop adaptive, resilient frameworks aligned with global strategies, such as the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction and the WHO Health Emergency33,59.

Bridging the gap between theoretical preparedness and practical engagement

While the study indicated good perceptions among HCPs concerning their knowledge, attitude, and willingness to practice during disasters, they reported less frequent involvement in practical disaster management activities. The qualitative insights, such as the reliance on senior staff during crises and uncertainty in decision-making during unanticipated events, underscored a gap between theoretical knowledge and practical confidence. Previous research suggests that practical engagement in disaster management is influenced not only by training and experience but also by participation in past disaster responses12. In this study, involvement in events like the 2017 Blockade or the COVID-19 pandemic was associated with higher perceptions of practices. This is possibly due to Qatar’s low-risk classification for disasters60, which limits real-world disaster exposure. The study noted limited engagement from HCPs in improving institutional disaster plans, a trend also observed during Canada’s COVID-19 response61. This is partly due to the fact that 65% of participants were not in managerial roles. Those in managerial positions had higher engagement due to their broader responsibilities, strategic planning roles, and access to specialized training, which increased their confidence in disaster management62,63. These findings highlight the need to involve frontline HCPs in disaster planning to strengthen preparedness and response efforts61.

Adaptive practices and collaborative strategies for effective disaster management

This study underscores that effective disaster management extends beyond technical skills. It also depends on adaptive practices such as role flexibility, rapid redeployment, digital technology utilization, mental health support, and interprofessional collaboration. These findings align with Al Thobaity (2024), who emphasized the global relevance of investing in technologies, psychological support systems, and team-based training to build resilient disaster frameworks64. Role flexibility enables professionals to assume expanded or shifted responsibilities as needed65, while rapid redeployment, as demonstrated in the UK during COVID-19, was essential for maintaining service delivery under pressure66. Digital solutions like telemedicine and electronic triage tools support continuity of care and enable real-time coordination when standard systems are disrupted. Additionally, the psychological burden on frontline staff highlights the need for institutionalized mental health support and resilience-building interventions67,68. Interprofessional collaboration enhances communication and reduces inefficiencies, making it a critical component of effective disaster response64.

Institutional and governmental effectiveness in disaster preparedness and coordination

The integrated findings revealed that HCPs in Qatar perceived both their organizations and the government to be highly effective in disaster planning, leadership, and resource management. These perceptions were evident in the high levels of agreement across items assessing the existence of disaster plans, periodic updates, availability of medical resources, and inter-agency coordination. Qualitative data confirmed the operationalization of these systems through regular disaster drills, the establishment of structured command centers, and rapid deployment strategies led by national authorities. Despite some operational gaps, such as centralized decision-making, fragmented communication, unclear role distribution, and variable inclusion of frontline staff in planning processes, the overall system was viewed as proactive and well-coordinated. This institutional and governmental effectiveness resulted in better public health outcomes and fostered a sense of pride among HCPs, reinforcing their commitment to disaster response. Compared to high-risk settings like Iran and Lebanon, where recurrent crises have often exposed weaknesses in coordination and resource allocation, Qatar’s structured and anticipatory disaster preparedness approach demonstrates a more proactive and systematized model. Studies from Iran and Lebanon noted persistent challenges in inter-agency coordination, stakeholder involvement, and implementation of disaster management protocols, often hindering timely responses and undermining workforce readiness69,70. In contrast, Singapore, a similarly low-risk yet high-capacity setting, mirrors Qatar’s emphasis on multi-agency planning, leadership engagement, and comprehensive preparedness strategies, illustrating how proactive governance can compensate for limited direct disaster exposure45. Qatar thus offers an example of how robust disaster preparedness can be achieved through policy commitment and institutional investment, aligning closely with international standards.

Integrating local efforts with international priorities

These efforts occurred in a low-risk context like Qatar but remain closely aligned with the priorities outlined in the International Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction. The framework emphasizes cohesive disaster planning and strategic leadership to strengthen risk governance (Sendai Priority 2), and proactive resource allocation to build resilience (Sendai Priority 3)33,71. Initiatives such as Qatar’s National Health Emergency Management Plan exemplify these principles71. They also demonstrate Qatar’s commitment to enhancing disaster readiness (Sendai Priority 4). Lessons from the COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the need for comprehensive preparedness, which reinforces the value of these strategies72.

Limitations

Nevertheless, the findings should be interpreted with caution due to some limitations. Response bias represents a methodological concern in this study, manifesting primarily through social desirability bias and recall bias. Social desirability bias may have prompted HCPs to report higher ratings of their perceptions related to disaster management, as respondents may have felt compelled to provide answers aligned with perceived professional norms rather than their actual capabilities73. This concern is particularly relevant given that the DMAT_HCP questionnaire measures self-reported perceptions of disaster management knowledge, attitudes, and practices rather than objective performance indicators. This can produce overly optimistic assessments of disaster preparedness and response abilities among HCPs. Additionally, recall bias poses a challenge, especially in reporting past practices. The retrospective nature of practice reporting, combined with the tendency to recall performance more favorably over time, could result in significant overestimation of compliance with disaster protocols and actual response effectiveness during emergency situations. The gap between perceived and actual disaster management practices may lead to overestimation of both individual and organizational readiness levels. Nonetheless, this risk is expected to be minimal, given the inclusion of the ‘not sure’ option, which allowed participants to avoid potentially inaccurate self-assessments when uncertain about their actual performance or organizational capabilities. Furthermore, because the study employed a cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be established. Missing data were handled using pairwise deletion, which may have resulted in minor variations in sample size across subgroup analyses and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Moreover, the focus on the governmental sector limited the comprehensive view of disaster management, which requires cooperation across all healthcare sectors, including private healthcare organizations. However, this does not pose a major concern since healthcare services are predominantly provided by the governmental sector.

Implications for practice and policymaking

The findings of this study generate several actionable implications for policymakers, healthcare organizations, emergency management agencies, and academic institutions working to strengthen disaster preparedness and resilience, particularly in low-disaster-frequency but high-capacity settings such as those in the Gulf region. The key recommendations with responsible actors and implementation timelines for strengthening disaster management in healthcare systems are highlighted in Box 1.

Priority policy actions for healthcare disaster management.

For policymakers, the results highlight the importance of integrating disaster preparedness more explicitly into national health strategies and regulatory frameworks. This includes developing mandates for healthcare workforce training, formalizing the role of frontline professionals in disaster planning, and incentivizing innovation in emergency response technologies. Policymakers can also use these findings to align national preparedness strategies with global frameworks such as the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction. The findings further demonstrate the value of fostering public engagement in preparedness efforts, which can bolster community resilience and strengthen cooperative responses during emergencies. For healthcare organizations, the study underscores the need to actively involve HCPs in the design and periodic review of institutional disaster plans. This participatory approach can strengthen frontline engagement and foster a culture of preparedness across departments. The study suggests that healthcare organizations should prioritize proactive disaster readiness through continuous assessments and comprehensive support systems that address both the physical and mental demands placed on HCPs during crises. The need for adaptability in disaster protocols is also emphasized, enabling healthcare teams to respond to dynamic and evolving emergency conditions effectively. Based on the integrated findings, the study advocates for the institutionalization of scenario-based disaster training as a core workforce development priority. This includes making such training mandatory, interdisciplinary, and context-specific, with certification tied to measurable competencies. Periodic training needs assessments should guide these programs to ensure relevance and effectiveness. In addition, the establishment of a national accreditation system for disaster preparedness competencies would ensure standardization, accountability, and sustainability of these training initiatives across healthcare settings. At the operational level, the study recommends decentralizing key management and diagnostic functions to promote agility in emergency response. Additionally, the creation of unified communication platforms across healthcare institutions is essential to address fragmentation and improve inter-agency coordination. For emergency management agencies, the findings support investments in system-level vulnerability assessments, integrated response planning, and technology adoption. Leveraging digital platforms, such as AI-powered tools for real-time data integration and decision support, can significantly improve situational awareness and rapid response capabilities. Lastly, academic institutions and professional development bodies can utilize these insights to strengthen educational programs by embedding disaster preparedness into curricula and offering interdisciplinary simulations that reflect real-world challenges. These reforms help prepare future professionals with the applied skills and collaborative mindset necessary for effective disaster response.

Collectively, these insights reinforce the importance of adopting a disaster management approach that enhances resilience, adaptability, and community partnership across health systems. These implications reinforce the study’s relevance across sectors and offer a model for anticipatory and coordinated disaster preparedness that can be adapted to similar health systems globally.

Future research

Future research is warranted to employ a longitudinal or comparative design that involves HCPs from private and semi-private sectors to deepen insights into national preparedness and to enhance disaster management strategies across all healthcare settings. Exploring the long-term effects of disaster management preparedness initiatives on HCPs and public resilience could provide valuable insights into how sustained training and preparedness efforts enhance both professional performance and community response during future disasters.

Conclusions

This study offers a comprehensive evaluation of HCPs’ disaster preparedness and organization-based management, identifying both strengths and critical areas requiring attention. HCPs reported high levels of perceived knowledge, positive attitudes, and willingness to respond during disasters, particularly among those with prior training, leadership responsibilities, or firsthand experience with previous crises. The presence of institutional disaster plans and perceived organizational support further reflects a foundational commitment to preparedness within Qatar’s healthcare system. Nevertheless, the findings reveal a persistent disconnect between perceived readiness and actual practice. Insufficient hands-on training, limited engagement of frontline staff in preparedness activities, and the predominantly theoretical nature of existing programs undermined the effective application of disaster competencies. Organizational limitations, such as centralized decision-making, inconsistent communication, and inadequate dissemination of operational protocols, further constrained preparedness efforts. Notably, the study highlights the importance of effective communication, interdisciplinary collaboration, and the integration of technological innovations as key facilitators of disaster response. These factors enhanced coordination, adaptability, and situational awareness during emergencies. In addition, the effectiveness of healthcare institutions and governmental leadership in disaster planning and coordination contributed significantly to improved public health outcomes and reinforced the confidence of the health workforce. In sum, while key structural elements of disaster preparedness are in place, their impact is constrained by operational and systemic gaps. Advancing preparedness efforts requires a sustained emphasis on experiential learning, inclusive planning, and system-wide alignment to ensure that both individual competencies and institutional mechanisms are optimally equipped to respond to future public health emergencies. However, the conclusions should be interpreted with caution, given the reliance on self-reported perceptions and the focus on the public healthcare sector. These factors may limit the generalizability of the findings, particularly to private and semi-private healthcare settings in Qatar and other Gulf countries, where organizational structures and capacities may differ. Nonetheless, the study provides transferable insights that can inform disaster management policies and contribute to frameworks adaptable to comparable international contexts.

Methods

Study design and setting

An explanatory sequential mixed-methods design was utilized, whereby quantitative and qualitative data were collected and analyzed separately, and the results were then integrated, compared, and interpreted in combination74. Figure 3 illustrates a description of the study design, data collection, and analysis. In the quantitative phase, a self-administered questionnaire, DMAT_HCP, was developed, evaluated, and utilized in a cross-sectional observational study design to assess HCPs’ disaster management knowledge, attitude, practice, willingness to continue practicing duties, and their organization-based disaster management. In the qualitative phase, a series of online focus group (FG) discussions were conducted to gain a deeper understanding of HCPs’ perceptions of their overall readiness and preparedness for disaster management and the opportunities and challenges related to disaster prevention and control strategies at different healthcare settings in Qatar. This study was guided by the Disaster Management Framework, which provides a comprehensive strategy that guides the systematic approach to addressing disasters or emergencies through a cycle of four stages: prevention or mitigation, preparedness, response, and recovery8,9. The qualitative and quantitative data obtained were integrated at the interpretation and reporting levels through a narrative, contiguous approach75. In this approach, the findings from each phase were presented in distinct sections: quantitative findings were reported first, followed by the qualitative findings. This approach preserves the methodological integrity of each data type while enabling a coherent synthesis of results. It also facilitates a comprehensive understanding of the topic, consistent with recommended practices for rigorous mixed-methods research in health services research75. Triangulation was employed during the interpretation phase to enhance the validity and depth of findings by comparing and integrating results from both datasets. This process enabled the identification of converging patterns as well as meaningful divergences, thereby supporting a more comprehensive understanding of HCPs’ disaster management perceptions.

A description of the study design.

Previous Gulf-based studies have offered useful insights but were constrained in scope and methodology. Nofal et al. (2021) conducted a cross-sectional survey of emergency HCPs in Saudi Arabia that focused exclusively on bioterrorism preparedness23. Similarly, Al-Hunaishi et al. (2019) examined willingness to participate in disasters among doctors and nurses in three hospitals in Yemen, thereby excluding other key professional groups such as paramedics, pharmacists, and allied health practitioners21. A UAE-based study surveyed HCPs to assess individual knowledge, attitudes, and training in disaster preparedness, but the relatively small sample size limited representativeness, and the study relied solely on quantitative methods without examining organizational preparedness or system-level management44. In contrast, the present study employs a nationally representative explanatory sequential mixed-methods design, integrating quantitative and qualitative data from diverse healthcare professions and institutions, and uniquely applying the newly validated DMAT_HCP instrument. This broader scope enables a more comprehensive assessment of disaster management perceptions at both the individual and organizational levels within Qatar’s healthcare system.

The study was conducted virtually using online platforms from November 2023 to June 2024. The ethical approval was obtained from the Qatar University Institutional Review Board (QU-IRB) [approval number: QU-IRB 1759-EA/22], HMC-IRB [approval number: MRC-03–22–392], and PHCC-IRB [approval number: PHCC/DCR/2022/06/041].

Quantitative phase

Participants, sample size, and sampling

The sampling frame constituted all HCPs from different health disciplines (physicians, nurses, pharmacists, dentists, and allied health professionals) working in different clinical settings (i.e., HMC, PHCC, and MoPH). The eligibility criteria included HCPs who were: (1) above 18 years old, (2) licensed as a physician, a nurse, or an allied healthcare professional, and (3) practicing in PHCC, HMC, or MoPH. All eligible HCPs practicing at PHCC, HMC, or MoPH constituted the sampling frame of this study (n = 25,686). The minimum sample size required for the study was determined based on a 5% margin of error, a 95% confidence level, and a 50% response distribution. To account for potential non-response, the estimated sample size (n = 379) was doubled, resulting in a final target sample of approximately 758 HCPs. HCPs were selected using a stratified random sampling method by healthcare professions/disciplines. A total of 771 HCPs were randomly selected using STATA®. A detailed breakdown of the stratified sample of HCPs and the strata weights applied is provided in the Supplementary Material 2.

Data collection

The quantitative phase utilized DMAT_HCP, a self-administered questionnaire that was developed and validated in a previous study41. The full set of DMAT_HCP items is available in the original development and validation study41 and is fully presented in the current study (Table 2). DMAT_HCP questionnaire assessed HCPs’ perceptions of their disaster management: (A) knowledge [11 items], (B) attitude [11 items], (C) practice [10 items], (D) willingness to continue practicing duties [6 items], and (E) organization-based disaster management [10 items]. Each section comprised five Likert scales, with non-ordinal response options ‘not sure’ and ‘not applicable’ assigned to the ‘practice’ and ‘organization-based disaster management’ sections, respectively.

The DMAT_HCP had several unique features in both its conceptual design and validation process, contributing meaningfully to the field of disaster management assessment. It was developed through a deductive item generation process, guided by a comprehensive scoping review76 and existing validated tools, ensuring theoretical alignment and methodological rigor. DMAT_HCP was intentionally designed to evaluate HCPs’ preparedness across all four phases of disaster management, thereby enabling a comprehensive and structured assessment of capabilities and gaps throughout the entire disaster management cycle. Importantly, the tool was designed to be applicable to a broad range of healthcare professions, including medicine, nursing, pharmacy, dentistry, paramedicine, and allied health, and adaptable to various clinical settings and levels of the healthcare system. The tool captured HCPs’ perceptions of disaster management at both individual and organizational levels. This design enhanced the tool’s utility for both operational assessments and policy planning. The tool was also designed to support its use across a spectrum of disaster types, from natural to complex emergencies. DMAT_HCP content validity was rigorously established through two rounds of expert review, and face validity was confirmed through feedback from HCPs representing the target population. The tool underwent iterative refinement to improve item clarity, eliminate redundancy, and enhance usability through context-appropriate response formats. Psychometric evaluation confirmed a robust factor structure via exploratory factor analysis, supported by evidence of convergent and divergent validity. The tool also demonstrated high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha ranging from 0.89 to 0.97 across subscales and 0.90 overall, affirming its reliability as a comprehensive measure of disaster management in healthcare.

The selected HCPs were invited via email to take part in this study. A link to the self-administered DMAT_HCP questionnaire at SurveyMonkey® (SurveyMonkey Inc., San Mateo, California, USA) with informed consent was included in the invitation emails. A detailed description of the study, including the study purpose, the process of data collection, the assurance of anonymity and confidentiality, and the voluntary nature of involvement, was provided. The survey link remained open from November 27th, 2023, to February 11th, 2024, and follow-up reminders were sent to HCPs every two weeks to improve the response rate.

Data analysis

The quantitative analysis was performed using a standard software package STATA® version 18.1 (StataCorp LLC, Texas 77845 USA). Participants who completed only the demographic section were excluded from the analysis, as these data were not central to the study’s objectives. A descriptive analysis was conducted to understand the distributions of DMAT_HCP items. A per-item approach was adopted for the remaining respondents, whereby all available responses to substantive questionnaire items were retained. For inferential analyses, pairwise deletion was applied, allowing each analysis to include all cases with available data for the relevant variables. Pairwise deletion was selected because missingness across the DMAT_HCP sections was low (Knowledge: 0.00%; Attitude: 0.74%; Practice: 2.73%; Willingness: 5.46%; Organization-based disaster management: 5.96%). This approach allowed maximal retention of available data without introducing the assumptions required for other approaches. The sample size used for each inferential test is reported alongside the corresponding results (Table 3).

The demographics and professional characteristics of HCPs were presented as proportions (%) for categorical data and median (IQR) for continuous data, because the normality assessments demonstrated non-normality. The perceptions of disaster management among HCPs were summarized as proportions (%), total sum scores, and median (IQR) of the total sum scores since the normality assessments of the sum scores demonstrated non-normality. In general, the higher scores in each section denoted better perceptions of disaster management. However, for a more meaningful interpretation, the total sum score of each section was divided into tertiles reflecting poor, fair, and good perceptions as illustrated in Supplementary Material 3. The questionnaire scales were tested for internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s alpha), considering α ≥ 0.7 as the minimum satisfactory value77. An inferential analysis using non-parametric statistics was conducted to compare different subgroups with respect to HCPs’ perceptions of disaster management. The dependent variables were the total sum scores for perceptions of (1) knowledge, (2) attitudes, (3) practices, (4) willingness to continue practicing duties during disasters, and (5) organization-based management. The independent variables were demographic and professional characteristics of HCPs. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the total sum scores of each dependent variable across two-category independent variables, including gender, administrative position, experience working during the 2017 Blockade or the COVID-19 pandemic, and participation in disaster management courses or programs. The Kruskal-Wallis H test was applied to compare scores across independent variables with more than two categories, namely profession, worksite, and years of experience. Significant Kruskal-Wallis tests were followed by Dunn’s post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction for multiple testing. The correlation coefficient effect size (r) was used as a measure of effect size for Mann-Whitney U tests, while epsilon squared (ε²) was used for Kruskal-Wallis tests. The level of statistical significance was set a priori at p < 0.05.

Quality measures

The validity and reliability of the DMAT_HCP used in the quantitative phase were comprehensively established, as outlined above and further detailed in a separate study41. Content validity of the DMAT_HCP was assessed through two rounds of expert review involving national and international experts in disaster preparedness, healthcare, and instrument development. Face validity was also assessed through feedback from a diverse sample of HCPs representing the target population. Structural validity was assessed using exploratory factor analysis, while construct validity was supported by evidence from convergent and divergent validity testing. Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, with all domains exceeding the acceptable threshold.

Qualitative phase

Participants and sampling

A purposive sampling technique was applied to select key informant HCPs with prior experience in disaster situations, drawn from the same sampling frame used in the quantitative phase. An email invitation was sent to the selected HCPs to indicate the voluntary nature of participation in FGs and to provide them with participant information leaflets and consent forms.

Data collection

A topic guide for the FGs was developed based on an extensive literature review pertinent to the research objectives and the findings of the quantitative phase, aligned with the four phases of the Disaster Management Framework. The topic guide consisted of eleven open-ended questions with probes to allow participants to comfortably express their opinions (Supplementary Material 4). The primary aim was to elicit general insights into disaster-related roles, institutional practices, and perceived competencies, regardless of the specific type of disaster. However, to situate participants’ reflections within the national context and enhance recall of real-life experiences, the COVID-19 pandemic and the 2017 Gulf Crisis were introduced as contextual examples during the discussions. Additionally, the FIFA World Cup 2022 was occasionally raised as an example of large-scale event planning and risk mitigation. Six FGs were conducted in June 2024 among HCPs from the three institutions (n [HMC] = 3, n [PHCC] = 2, and n [MoPH] = 1). FGs were conducted online using Microsoft Teams® through synchronous meetings. Before initiating the FGs, participants consented using IRB-approved forms and returned signed consent forms to the researchers. FGs were facilitated by one research assistant (SE) who had prior experience with conducting FGs and, hence, was able to encourage open discussion and make it easier for participants to share their perceptions. Participants were engaged in concurrent discussions and were encouraged to use the “raise hand” feature on Microsoft Teams to mimic face-to-face interactions. Each FG lasted around 90 min, was conducted in English, and was recorded and transcribed verbatim. After the sixth FG, the researchers confirmed that a saturation point was reached and that conducting further FGs was unnecessary. Saturation was independently judged by two researchers (SE and BM) who reviewed the transcripts after each FG. It was determined using the criterion that no new codes, themes, or substantive insights were emerging from the discussions. In particular, the last two FGs yielded only repetitive information that reinforced previously identified themes without adding new insights. Consensus between the two researchers was used to confirm that saturation had been achieved. Anonymity was ensured by using an alphanumeric code with “participant’s number, participant’s profession, and his/her affiliation”. The professions coded were pharmacist, dentist, physician, nurse, and allied healthcare professionals. All research documents were kept in a password-protected laptop.

Data analysis

The transcripts and manually written responses were thematically analyzed using inductive and deductive coding approaches. The deductive approach involved organizing the data into pre-determined categories based on the four phases of the Disaster Management Framework. Two researchers (SE and BM) were involved in the analysis process, and the disparity was resolved through consensus. The coding software, NVIVO® version 14 (QRS International Pty Ltd), was employed to perform the thematic analysis.

Quality measures

To ensure the trustworthiness of the qualitative data, the study followed Lincoln and Guba’s criteria, addressing credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability78. Credibility was enhanced through the use of multiple sources of data in six FGs involving HCPs from three major institutions. Transcripts were verified through cross-checking by two researchers (SE and BM), and the disparity was resolved through consensus. Reaching data saturation after the sixth FG confirmed that the data adequately captured participants’ perspectives. Dependability was supported by using a standardized topic guide, grounded in literature and quantitative findings, which ensured consistent data collection across all FGs. Inter-coder and intra-coder reliability testing confirmed the stability of the coding process, with matched results across independent and repeated analyses. Confirmability was established through transparent documentation of methodological procedures and decisions, and their oversight by a research supervisor. Transferability was facilitated by offering thick descriptions of the study context, settings, and procedures, enabling readers to assess the relevance of the findings to other contexts.
