HMGB1 impairs nasal epithelial barrier via regulating autophagy in the pathogenesis of allergic rhinitis☆
Authors: Yuhao Fan, Miaomiao Xu, Liya Ren, Congcong Lu, Yilin Li, Fuhai Chen, Zhiqi Ma, Yong Li, Xiaolin Cao, Jing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNumerous studies indicate that the epithelial barrier was disrupted in allergic rhinitis (AR). High-mobility group box 1(HMGB1) plays a significant role in activating inflammation. We aimed to explore whether HMGB1 could impair the epithelial barrier in AR and investigate its underlying mechanism.MethodsNasal secretions and nasal specimens were collected. Expression of HMGB1 and tight junction (TJ) proteins were measured by RT-qPCR, western blotting and immunofluorescence in control subjects and AR patients. Hematoxylin and eosin staining was used for histological examination. Cell proliferation and migration were assessed by CCK-8 and wound healing assays. Primary human nasal epithelial cells (pHNECs) were processed by air-liquid interface (ALI) technique and treated with recombinant HMGB1 protein. Lentivirus mRFP-sGFP-LC3 was transfected to detect autophagy flux. An OVA-induced mouse model was used to investigate the role of HMGB1 in vivo.ResultsThe level of HMGB1 was significantly elevated in the nasal secretions of AR patients (P < 0.001) and positively correlated with symptom severity (VAS scores) (r = 0.4863, P = 0.0064). Both ex vivo nasal tissues and in vitro air-liquid interface (ALI) cultures from AR patients showed reduced expression of tight junction proteins (Occludin, Claudin-1) (P < 0.05). In pHNECs, HMGB1 treatment further decreased the expression of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1, while promoting cell proliferation and migration (P < 0.01). Mechanistically, HMGB1 blocked the autophagosome degradation, as evidenced via increased LC3-II and P62 expression by HMGB1; restoration of autophagy with rapamycin reversed these effects and rescued barrier function. In an OVA-induced mouse model of AR, pharmacological inhibition of HMGB1 with glycyrrhizin (GLY) or ethyl pyruvate (EP) alleviated sneezing and nasal rubbing symptoms (P < 0.05) and improved epithelial barrier integrity.ConclusionHMGB1 was highly expressed in AR patients and correlated with disease severity. It impaired the integrity of the epithelial barrier by inhibiting the degradation of autophagosomes. Both GLY and EP treatments restored impaired epithelial barrier by inhibiting HMGB1.

Introduction

Allergic rhinitis (AR) is an Immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated chronic disease due to the aberrant type I hypersensitivity response in nasal mucosa.1 As a global disease, the incidence rate of AR has steadily increased since the 20th century. AR is associated with a range of symptoms, including sneezing, nasal obstruction, nasal itching, and rhinorrhea, as well as systemic symptoms such as fatigue, sleep disturbances, depression, and anxiety. These symptoms significantly diminish the quality of life and impose a considerable socioeconomic burden.2 Despite therapeutic interventions such as intranasal corticosteroids (INS) that provide symptomatic relief, targeted therapy strategies are in urgent need due to the current recurrence and incurability of AR. In recent decades, the epithelial barrier hypothesis has gained traction as it provides an explanation for allergic diseases.3 The nasal epithelial barrier is considered the first line of defense of the upper respiratory tract against large molecular pollutants, pathogens, and allergens. In brief, the epithelial barrier consist of a physical barrier, a chemical barrier, and the innate immune responses of the epithelium.4 Recently, the impairment of the physical barrier has been widely investigated in allergic diseases.5 Physical epithelial barrier mainly depends on the tight junction (TJ) proteins which consist of more than 60 members including Occludin, ZO family, Claudin family, JAM and so on.6 These tight junction proteins connect individual scattered cells to form a complete physical barrier that resists the invasion of external pathogens and allergens, thereby playing an essential role in maintaining normal epithelial function. Many studies have demonstrated that in AR patients, the epithelial barrier is impaired by decreased TJ protein expression, thereby allowing pathogens and allergens to access the subepithelial space and trigger local inflammation.6, 7, 8 During the procedure, epithelial cells undergo cell death, and type 2 mucosal immune response get triggered potentially.9 Thus, preserving the integrity of the epithelial barrier is vital for maintaining mucosa health and could potentially offer a novel therapeutic approach for the management of AR.

High mobility group box 1 protein (HMGB1), a kind of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), is a highly conserved nuclear protein with multifunctionality. HMGB1 alters its functions depending on its position. In the nucleus, HMGB1 binds to chromatin and is crucial for several cellular processes, including gene transcription and DNA repair.10 When released, HMGB1 initiates its proinflammatory effects. On 1 side, inflammatory cells are recruited by HMGB1 to trigger immune response; on the other side, HMGB1 is known to bind to its classical receptors (like Receptor for Advanced Glycation Endproducts) on the cell membrane. Upon binding to these receptors, the MyD88-dependent NF-κB pathway, which is a well-recognized key regulator of inflammation, is activated, leading to the production of a wide range of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and TNF-α. This cascade of events enhances the inflammatory response and subsequently promotes the release of HMGB1, thereby establishing a positive feedback loop.11 Numerous studies have indicated that HMGB1 plays a role in the pathogenesis of various diseases including neuroinflammation, acute kidney injury, and certain cancers.12, 13, 14 Nowadays, HMGB1 is increasingly being acknowledged as a novel potential therapeutic target.15 However, the relationship between HMGB1 and AR has not been extensively investigated.

Previous research has identified the accumulation of autophagosomes in AR, indicating that autophagy may be involved in its pathogenesis.16 Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that autophagy can influence the integrity of the epithelial barrier in both the intestinal tract and retinal pigment epithelial layer.17, 18, 19 However, the relationship between autophagy and the airway epithelial barrier remains unclear. Given that autophagy is a key cellular homeostatic process and has been linked to barrier function in other epithelia, we hypothesized that HMGB1 might disrupt the nasal epithelial barrier by dysregulating autophagy.20

Based on these findings, we explored whether HMGB1 could impair the integrity of the epithelial barrier in AR patients. Primary human nasal epithelial cells (pHNECs) from control subjects and AR patients were isolated for air-liquid interface (ALI) culture. Furthermore, we investigated how HMGB1-regulated autophagy affects the epithelial barrier in the ALI model of pHNECs. Glycyrrhizin (GLY) and ethyl pyruvate (EP), as inhibitors of HMGB1, were used to investigate the role of HMGB1 in an OVA-induced AR mouse model.

Materials and methods

Patients

Control subjects (n = 24) and AR patients (n = 36) were recruited in this study. For ex vivo analysis, nasal biopsy specimens were obtained from the inferior turbinate during aesthetic and/or functional rhinoplasty (information can be found in Table 1). The diagnosis criterion of AR patients was based on the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Allergic Rhinitis (2022, revision).21 A diagnosis required at least 1 allergen with positive serum-specific IgE accompanied by an elevation in total serum IgE in serum allergen testing. Participants with conditions such as eosinophilic esophagitis, aspirin triad, cystic fibrosis, tumors, acute respiratory infection, and autoimmune disorders were excluded from the study. The use of any corticosteroids or antibiotics was prohibited for at least 4 weeks prior to the study, and all participants were instructed to record their AR symptoms scores (including obstruction, sneezing, itching, rhinorrhea and eye symptoms) on a visual analogue scale (score 0–10). For in vitro analysis, pHNECs were isolated from control subjects (n = 6) and AR patients (n = 6) for ALI cultures (information can be found in Table 2). Nasal secretions

Nasal secretions were obtained from a portion of the participants (13 control subjects and 17 AR patients) using a method referred to in the article by Steelant et al.8 A nasal sponge (Geling medical products, Jiangsu, China) was cut into a size of 2.5 ∗ 0.4 ∗ 0.5 cm3 and inserted into nasal cavity. After 10 min, sponge was removed and soaked in 2.5 mL PBS for repeated squeezing. The liquid was centrifuged at 1500g for 20 min. Supernatant was stored at −20 °C for further analysis.

Isolation of primary human nasal epithelial cells (pHNECs)

The method of pHNECs isolation was referred to in articles by Steelant et al.7 Mucosal specimens from the inferior turbinate were collected and subjected to ice-cold RPMI-1640 medium containing 0.1% protease XIV (Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, US) for enzymatic digestion. After overnight incubation at 4°C, the mucosa tissue was ground thoroughly in a sterile laminar flow hood. The isolated cells were collected using 1 ml PBS twice, followed by filtration on a 40 μm cell filter (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Filtered cells were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min and resuspended in 5 mL medium for subsequent incubation.

Air-liquid interface (ALI) cultures

The ALI culture protocol was referred to in articles by Steelant et al and Tian et al.6,22 Isolated primary nasal epithelial cells from control subjects and AR patients were cultured with PneumaCult™-Ex Plus Medium (Stemcell, Vancouver, US) in a T25 culture flask at 37 °C. After approximately 3–5 days, cells were transferred into a 0.4 μm, 0.33-cm2 polyester transwell inserts (Costar, Corning, New York, US) when they reached 70% confluency, at a density of 50,000 cells per well. Once the 100% confluency reached, the medium on the apical side was removed to promote the development of the air-liquid interface (ALI) condition for further cell differentiation. Meanwhile, the basal medium was changed to PneumaCult™-ALI Medium (Stemcell, Vancouver, US), a specific culture medium for differentiation phase, and this medium was subsequently replaced every other day. Approximately 21 days later, the differentiated epithelial cells with biting cilia observed under microscope were treated for further experiments.

Cell culture and reagents

pHNECs were isolated and cultured in RMPI1640 medium (GIBCO, Grand Island, US) with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (biological industries, Haemek, Israel) at 37 °C in 5% CO2. pHNECs were cultured with recombinant HMGB1 protein (R&D systems, Minnesota, US) at the concentration of 0, 100, 200 or 400 ng/mL for 48 h and 400 ng/mL for 0, 2, 6, 12, 24, or 48 h. FPS-ZM1 (20 μM) (MCE, New Jersey, US) and Rapamycin (200 nM) (MCE, New Jersey, US) were used to block the RAGE receptor and facilitate the autophagy flux 2 h before treatment. A lentivirus expressing mRFP-sGFP-LC3 was purchased from Genechem (Shanghai, China) (The multiplicity of infection (MOI) was 80). The following antibodies were used: ZO-1 (ab276131), Occlduin (ab216327), Claudin-1 (ab211737), P62 (ab109012), LC3 (ab192890) were from Abcam (Cambridge, UK), AC-Tubulin (53355) was from Cell Signaling Technology (CST, US). Beta actin (20536-1-AP) was from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). All experiments were conducted in triplicate using cells from at least 3 different subjects.

ELISA

The ELISA kit (ELK Biotechnology, Wuhan, China) was used to measure the concentration of HMGB1 in human nasal secretions according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

pHNECs were planted in a 96-well plate (6 × 103/well) and cultured with serum-free RPMI-1640 medium containing HMGB1 at concentrations of 0, 100, 200, 400, 800, 1000 ng/mL. After 24 or 48 h incubation, CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was added to the cells, which were then incubated for 2 h at 37°C. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, Vermont, US).

Wound-healing assay

pHNECs were planted in a 6-wells plate (5 × 105/well) and incubated at least 12 h until 100% confluence was reached. A wound was created in each well using a 200 μL sterile pipette tip, followed by washing each well twice with 2 mL PBS. pHNECs were then cultured in serum-free RPMI-1640 medium with HMGB1 at concentrations of 0, 100, 200, 400 ng/mL. The progress of the wound-healing was imaged by microscope-mounted camera (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) at 0, 24, and 48 h post-wounding. ImageJ was used for further digital processing of the images.

Western blot

A RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing protease inhibitor (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used to lyse nasal specimens and pHNECs. The lysis was performed for 20 min on ice. Protein samples (20 μg each lane) were loaded onto a 4–12% gradient gel for SDS/PAGE (ACE Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and then transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore, Billerica, US). Blocking was carried out using 5% skimmed milk. The membranes were then incubated with specific antibodies. Quantitative analysis of Western blot band intensity was performed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA) software.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

The RNA extraction was performed with RNA-Quick Purification Kit (ESScience, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Nanodrop 8000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, US) was used to analyze the concentration of RNA. 500 ng RNA was used for reverse transcription by Evo M-MLV RT Premix for qPCR (AG11706) (Accurate Biotechnology, Hunan, China). cDNA was used for RT-qPCR by SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (Rox Plus) (AG11718) (Accurate Biotechnology, Hunan, China) and QuantStudio5 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, US). The results were normalized with GAPDH and analyzed with 2–ΔΔCT method. The primer sequence was listed as follow:

GAPDH:F:TGTGGGCATCAATGGATTTGG;

R: ACACCATGTATTCCGGGTCAAT.

HMGB1: F: CCTCCTTCGGCCTTCTTCCTCT;

R: GTCATCTGCAGCAGTGTTATTCCACA.

TEER measurement

Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) was measured to analyze the integrity of epithelial barrier using a R/V Meter of Epithelium (KINGTECH Technology, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The transwell inserts were washed with DPBS twice, each wash lasting for 3 min. After the final wash, both apical and basal chambers were covered with DPBS. An unused transwell insert was used as a blank group. Each insert was measured in triplicate.

Histopathological examination & immunofluorescence staining

Nasal mucosa specimens and ALI cultures were fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 24 h and subsequently processed into 5-mm paraffin-embedded sections. The sections were placed in a dry oven at 65 °C for 1.5 h for deparaffinization, followed by an antigen retrieval process using Sodium Citrate Solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The sections were then stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining solutions (HaoKebio, Hangzhou, China). For immunofluorescence staining, sections were blocked with 5% BSA (biofroxx, Einhausen, Germany) for 1 h, and incubated with antibody overnight at 4°C. Samples were mounted in DAPI (ABclonal, Wuhan, China) for 15 min before being observed under a microscope. ZEISS confocal-microscope LSM 900 (Carl Zeiss Microscopy, Jena, Germany) was used for the image capture.

Ovalbumin-induced AR mouse model

Female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks) were obtained from Hangzhou Medical University and were kept under the same condition. Mice were divided into 4 groups randomly (n = 10 each group) as follow: control group (CON), OVA-induced AR group (OVA), OVA groups with the treatment of 50 mg/kg glycyrrhizin (GLY), and OVA groups with the treatment of 80 mg/kg ethyl pyruvate (EP). On the days 1, 8, 15, the mice were intraperitoneally injected with 200 ul PBS containing 25 ug OVA (MCE, New Jersey, US) and 2 mg Aluminum hydroxide (MCE, New Jersey, US) except control group. On days 21–28, mice were intranasal challenged with 20 ul OVA solution containing 400 ug OVA or 20 ul PBS daily. The treatment groups were intraperitoneally injected with GLY (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) or EP (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) solution daily before the OVA sensitization on days 22–28. The nasal symptoms, sneezing and nasal rubbing, were counted in 3 min after last challenge. On day 29, all the mice were sacrificed.

Statistical analysis

All the data were analyzed by GraphPad Prism 7 (La Jolla, Calif, USA) and exhibited as means ± standard error (SEM). Difference between 2 groups was analyzed by unpaired t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Difference among multiple groups was analyzed by One-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test. Correlations were determined by Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients/tests. All quantitative data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For data that did not follow a normal distribution, non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U, Kruskal–Wallis, Spearman correlation coefficients) were used. The results were considered statistically significant when the P value was less than 0.05.

Results

Expression of HMGB1 was increased in nasal secretions of AR patients and correlated with AR symptoms

To compare the expression of HMGB1, nasal mucosa specimens were collected from control subjects (n = 24) and AR patients (n = 36) for RT-PCR and western blotting analysis (information of the subjects can be found in Table 1). As the results showed, no significant differences were observed in the protein expression level of HMGB1 between control subjects and AR patients (Fig. 1A and B). However, the expression level of HMGB1 mRNA was statistically significantly upregulated in AR patients (Fig. 1C). Given that nuclear HMGB1 is known to be translocated to extracellular space during inflammation response as mentioned in Introduction, we analyzed HMGB1 level in nasal secretions of the participants. As expected, ELISA analysis showed the concentration of HMGB1 in AR patients was significantly higher than that of control subjects (Fig. 1D). In addition, the relationship between Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores of AR symptoms and HMGB1 level in nasal secretions was investigated by correlation analysis. The results showed a significant positive correlation between VAS scores and HMGB1 level, indicating HMGB1 was related to the severity of AR (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, the concentration of HMGB1 was positively correlated with scores of obstruction, sneeze, itching, and rhinorrhea, especially with obstruction, but not with eye symptoms (Fig. 1F). HMGB1 enhanced the capacity of proliferation and migration of pHNECs

Having established the elevated expression of HMGB1 in AR patients and its clinical correlation with disease severity, we next sought to investigate its functional impact on nasal epithelial cells. In the CCK-8 assay, no significant changes in cell viability were detected within 24 h of HMGB1 treatment. However, after 48 h, cell viability increased with rising HMGB1 concentrations up to 400 ng/mL, with a significant difference observed at this concentration. Cell viability showed a decreasing trend at the concentration of 1000 ng/mL of HMGB1 at 48 h (Fig. S1A). In the wound-healing assay, the migration rate increased with rising concentrations of HMGB1 at both 24 h and 48 h (Fig. S1B and C). Altogether, these results demonstrated that pHNECs exhibited enhanced proliferation and migration capacities under HMGB1 treatment.

HMGB1 impaired the integrity of nasal epithelial barrier

To compare the integrity of epithelial barrier between control subjects and AR patients, Western blot and Immunofluorescence was used to detect the protein expressions of nasal epithelial barrier. H&E staining was used to observe the morphology of the nasal mucosa and Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurement was used to assess the integrity of epithelial barrier of ALI model between different groups.

Western blot revealed that the protein expressions of Occludin and Claudin-1 in nasal specimens were significantly decreased in AR patients, while ZO-1 did not show a significant change (Fig. 2A). The results of immunofluorescence demonstrated the fluorescence intensity of TJ protein, except for ZO-1, and Tubulin were decreased in the nasal specimens of AR patients (Fig. 2B). pHNECs were isolated from 6 healthy control subjects and 6 AR patients for ALI culture (the information of subjects can be found in Table 2). Compared with control subjects, H&E staining showed an increase in nasal mucosa thickness and disordered arrangement of the epithelial cells of the nasal mucosa from AR patients (Fig. 2C). After 21 days, TEER measurements revealed a significant decrease in the ALI cultures of AR patients (Fig. 2D). Facilitating the formation of autophagolysosome reconstituted epithelial barrier integrity impaired by HMGB1

Some studies have demonstrated the structural integrity of the epithelial barrier could be influenced by autophagy level to some extent.18 Our results showed that HMGB1 impaired integrity of epithelial barrier as well as the degradation stage of autophagy. And then the relationship between autophagy and epithelial barrier was investigated in the subsequent experiments. Rapamycin (Rapa), an activator which promotes the formation of autophagolysosomes, was utilized in ALI model.24

pHNECs were treated with Rapamycin or FPS-ZM1 under 400 ng/mL HMGB1 for 48 h. Furthermore, we demonstrated that rapamycin restored the TEER in the ALI cultures of AR patients (Fig. 5C). Immunofluorescence results revealed that the ALI culture of AR patients treated with HMGB1 showed low expressions of ZO-1, Occludin, Claudin-1 and the disordered arrangement of Tubulin, whereas rapamycin restored the expressions of these proteins (Fig. 5D). Inhibition of HMGB1 by GLY and EP treatment reconstituted impaired epithelial barrier in AR mice model

To elucidate the effect of HMGB1 on AR in vivo, 2 small molecule antagonists of HMGB1, glycyrrhizin (GLY) and ethyl pyruvate (EP), were used in this part.15 Forty mice were divided into 4 groups as follow: control group (CON), OVA-induced AR group (OVA), OVA-induced AR treated with GLY group (OVA + GLY), and OVA-induced AR treated with EP group (OVA + EP) (details can be found in Method and Fig. 6A).

Nasal secretions from mice were collected for ELISA analysis. The results showed the concentration of HMGB1 in the nasal secretions of the OVA group was significantly higher than that of the control group. In contrast, the GLY and EP groups exhibited a significant decrease in HMGB1 levels compared to the OVA group (Fig. 6B). Nasal rubbing and sneezing are 2 critical behaviors of allergy in mice; therefore, we recorded the numbers of nasal rubbing and sneezing for 3 min, following the last administration. The results showed that the numbers of nasal rubbing and sneezing were significantly increased in OVA group compared to control group, whereas numbers were getting decreased in GLY and EP groups, and the treatment of EP has demonstrated a remarkable anti-sneezing effect, particularly (Fig. 6C). Interestingly, we also found that the concentration of HMGB1 in nasal secretions positively correlated with the numbers of nasal rubbing and sneezing in mice, which are consistent with our previous results in clinical subjects (Fig. 6D and E). Mouse nasal tissues were processed for H&E staining. The results showed the ciliary structure in the OVA group exhibited disorganization, accompanied by significant infiltration of inflammatory cells compared to control group. Remarkably, treatment of GLY and EP restored the inflammatory change in a histopathological level. (Fig. 6F). We further investigated the effects of GLY and EP treatment on the epithelial barrier. Immunofluorescence staining showed the fluorescence intensity of Occludin and Claudin-1 were decreased in OVA group with a disrupted cilia structure, while the treatment of GLY and EP could increase the fluorescence intensity of tight junction proteins and restore the continuity of cilia epithelium (Fig. 6G).

After OVA sensation, the expression of HMGB1 was increased in nasal secretions of mice. Following treatment with glycyrrhizin and ethyl pyruvate, the concentration of HMGB1 in nasal secretions significantly decreased, accompanied with the recovery of the inflammatory changes and alleviation of allergic symptoms. In conclusion, our findings indicate that extracellular HMGB1 may get involved in the pathogenesis of AR in OVA-induced mice, and inhibition of HMGB1 by GLY and EP treatment may help reconstitute the impaired epithelial barrier of AR in OVA-induced mice and reduced the allergic symptoms.

Discussion

AR is a global disease in highly industrialized and urbanized regions which affect people's quality of life and impact the growth and development of children. Though not lethal, its treatment imposes a substantial financial burden.1 However, the recurrence and incurability of AR in clinical practice present significant therapeutic challenges. Consequently, over the past few decades, researcher have focused on identifying additional pathogenic factors involved in AR development. Recent studies have established significant associations between AR and multiple factors including genetic variants, microbial imbalances, and metabolic disturbances.25,26 The Epithelial Barrier Hypothesis has been increasingly applied in recent years to research on respiratory, digestive, and skin diseases. Damage to the epithelium facilitates the penetration of pathogenic agents and triggers localized inflammatory responses. In the pathogenesis of AR, the disrupted epithelial barrier facilitates allergen penetration, thereby aggravating clinical nasal symptoms. Concurrently, AR-associated inflammatory cytokines further impair epithelial integrity, establishing a vicious cycle.6,8 Therefore, maintaining epithelial integrity is critically important. In this study, we aim to investigate the role of the epithelial barrier in AR pathogenesis. Currently, AR diagnosis relies on allergen-specific testing and provocation tests. To complement these methods, our research aims to identify and validate molecular biomarkers that correlate with AR disease severity.

HMGB1 is widely considered to be implicated in inflammatory diseases, as numerous studies reported. Min et al demonstrated patients with eosinophilic chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyp (ECRSwNP) exhibit higher expression of HMGB1 in both nasal secretions and nasal polyps compared to those with non-eosinophilic chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyp (NECRSwNP).27 Min et al observed an elevated translocation and increased levels of HMGB1 in the nasal secretions of patients with AR compared to healthy controls.28 Cavone et al reported the HMGB1 level in nasal secretions of AR patients was increased which could be restored by treatment with glycyrrhizin. Also, glycyrrhizin treatment selectively killed eosinophils.29 Based on these studies, HMGB1 may be implicated in the pathogenesis of nasal inflammatory diseases. To investigate the expression of HMGB1 in AR patients, we collected nasal mucosa tissues and nasal secretions from both AR patients and a control group. We found that HMGB1 expression was elevated at the gene level in AR patients compared to controls. However, no significant difference was observed at the protein level within the nasal mucosa. As shown in the figure, HMGB1 levels in the AR group were slightly lower than those in the CON group. To explore the reason for the result, we reviewed the molecular biology of HMGB1 and found that HMGB1 would transfer from nuclear into extracellular space under inflammatory conditions. This extracellular translocation may lead to changes in tissue HMGB1 protein levels, potentially accounting for the findings of this study. Accordingly, we tried to focus to detect extracellular HMGB1 and analyzed the patients' nasal secretions and measured HMGB1 levels. This revealed that HMGB1 was significantly elevated in the nasal secretions of AR patients, in our research, the HMGB1 level in nasal secretions of the AR group was approximately twice that of the control group (P < 0.001). Furthermore, HMGB1 level in nasal secretions correlated positively with VAS scores of AR symptoms, especially with itching scores (r = 0.5149, P < 0.01), suggesting that HMGB1 levels may be associated with disease severity. In the clinical section, we noticed no significant correlation between HMGB1 levels and eye symptom scores. This may be attributed to 2 factors. First, a considerable number of enrolled subjects reported no eye symptoms, in contrast to nasal symptoms. Second, it is possible that some patients with elevated HMGB1 levels do not develop eye symptoms, as could be seen in figure, thereby contributing to the result.

Feng et al demonstrated that HMGB1 promoted the proliferation and migration of pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells, which may contribute to tissue remodeling in respiratory inflammatory diseases.30 Min et al and Liu et al demonstrated HMGB1 increased the expression of mesenchymal markers and decreased the expression of epithelial markers in pHNECs, indicating HMGB1 contributed to epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and the formation of nasal polyps.31,32 In our study, HMGB1 enhanced the capacity of proliferation and migration of pHNECs (P < 0.01), accompanied by the decrease in epithelial markers like ZO-1 and Occludin. These findings suggest a potential role of HMGB1 in promoting the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in AR.

The integrity of the epithelial barrier is crucial for preventing pathogen attack and maintaining the immune function. Tight junction (TJ) proteins are the main components of the physical epithelial barrier. Many studies have reported that disruption of the epithelial barrier contributes to allergic diseases, including AR.3,5 In our study, we demonstrated the expression of Occludin, Claudin-1, and the ciliary marker Tubulin was decreased in the mucosa of AR patients. Furthermore, the expression of ZO-1, Occludin, Claudin-1 and Tubulin was even decreased in ALI cultures of AR patients. It indicates the stem cells of primary AR patients may be influenced by the nasal inflammation. Opening of the epithelial barrier allows allergens and bacteria to penetrate the subepithelial region. Subsequently, these invaders activate immune cells and trigger immune responses.3 Maintaining an intact epithelial barrier plays a crucial role in protecting the nasal cavity.

HMGB1 exerts a disruptive effect on the nasal epithelium. In this study, HMGB1 downregulated the expression levels of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1 in a time and concentration dependent manner. However, our research demonstrated there was no significant change in Tubulin morphology under condition of 400 ng/mL HMGB1. We thought that the treatment time may have been insufficient to effectively destroy nasal ciliary function. During the pathogenesis of AR, HMGB1 is released into the extracellular space. It then binds to pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on the cell membrane, activating the NF-κB inflammatory signaling pathway.15 This activation ultimately leads to the production of numerous inflammatory cytokines, which disrupt the epithelial barrier. In turn, the disrupted epithelial barrier facilitates the invasion of large amounts of allergens, thereby exacerbating allergic symptoms and amplifying the immune-inflammatory response.

Studies have demonstrated the accumulation of autophagosomes in the mucosal tissues of AR patients, suggesting that autophagy, a process involved in the degradation and recycling of cellular components, may play a significant role in the pathogenesis of AR.16 In this study, we found autophagosomes were accumulated in AR patients as HMGB1 inhibited the degradation stage of autophagosome. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies. Zhang et al reported HMGB1 impaired the degradation of mitophagy flux through RAGE receptor in stress-induced hypertension.12 The activation of RAGE receptor may provide explanation for the phenomenon. Many studies have reported the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes could be hindered by the presence of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs). Zhang et al demonstrated the expression of both LC3-II and P62 was increased in a time-dependent manner under 200 ug/mL AGEs, and observed a decrease in the colocalization of LC3-II and LAMP2 in the AGEs-treated group.33 In similar, Barutta et al demonstrated treatment of AGEs induced lysosome dysfunction.34

Nowadays, many researchers hold the view that autophagy may facilitate the integrity of epithelial barrier. This topic has been extensively studied in intestinal tract; however, numerous findings hold differing opinions. Wong et al reported activated autophagy (via rapamycin or Earle's Balanced Salt Solution) enhanced the integrity of epithelial barrier in mice colon by inhibiting Occludin endocytosis.17 Saha et al demonstrated that autophagy may inhibited the endocytosis of Occludin in Caco-2 cells via enhancing the phosphorylation of ERK-1/2. While knockdown of autophagy-related protein 7 (ATG7) increased intestinal permeability in mice, exacerbating the severity of experimental colitis.35 Conversely, Ganapathy et al reported that starvation enhances the endocytosis of Claudin-2, resulting in an increase in epithelial barrier permeability in Caco-2 cells.36 However, there is little research on autophagy and airway epithelial barrier. Jia et al demonstrated the treatment of Bufei Yishen formula increased the expression of LC3-II protein and restored the decreased expression of ZO-1 and Occludin in a COPD mice model.37 Roscioli et al observed that exposure to wildfire smoke extract (WFSE) resulted in an increased expression of LC3-II protein, alongside a reduction in TJ proteins expression in small airway epithelial cells (SAEC).38 In this study, application of rapamycin restored the decreased expression levels of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1 by HMGB1 (P < 0.05). These findings collectively suggest that HMGB1 may disrupt the nasal epithelial barrier by inhibiting the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes. Our research contributes to elucidating the relationship between autophagy and the airway epithelial barrier.

To further validate the detrimental role of HMGB1, we conducted in vivo experiments using OVA-induced AR mouse model. AR mouse model exhibited key clinical features consistent with AR patients, including elevated expression of HMGB1 and severe allergic symptoms A positive correlation was observed between nasal HMGB1 levels and nasal symptom severity, further supporting the association of HMGB1 with disease pathogenesis. Importantly, glycyrrhizin (GLY) and ethyl pyruvate (EP) alleviated nasal scratching and sneezing symptoms, especially sneezing (r = 0.5876, P = 0.0004) and also restored the disrupted epithelial barrier in AR mouse model. These findings contribute to the result that HMGB1 is significantly involved in the pathogenesis of AR, and the application of GLY and EP may neutralize the inflammatory effects of HMGB1. Moreover, a number of studies have demonstrated that treatment with GLY or EP can reverse inflammatory effects in mouse models.13,39, 40, 41 This prompts us to consider the potential of these 2 drug ingredients for the development of innovative nasal spray formulations or oral formulations, which may be realized through further study. Due to the technological limitation, we have not assessed the autophagy status in nasal tissues or analysis directly the relationship between autophagy with epithelial barrier. Regarding this concern, Huang et al reported HMGB1 inhibition help increasing the expression of Occludin, Claudin-1, and Cloudin-2 while decreasing the expression of LC3-II in the mouse pancreas, which may help to support our findings.42

Due to financial and technological limitations, we did not discuss the relationship between HMGB1 and type 2 inflammation; which will be further explored in subsequent research. In this study, we did not conduct a further investigation into how HMGB1 influences the degradation of autophagy and how autophagy level influences the integrity of epithelial barrier. In vivo mice model, we didn't directly proving the epithelial-autophagy link.

In this article, our research demonstrated that HMGB1 is upgraded in the nasal secretions of AR patients. HMGB1 impaired epithelial barrier in AR, and the underlying mechanism may involve the inhibition of degradation stage of autophagy. GLY and EP, 2 antagonists of HMGB1, restored the disrupted epithelial barrier in mice model, indicating HMGB1 may become a therapeutic target for AR. We hope these findings may contribute to the understanding of the relationship between HMGB1 and the integrity of epithelial barrier.

Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrated that the concentration of HMGB1 in nasal secretions was increased in AR patients and was positively correlated to clinical allergic symptoms. In vitro, the HMGB1 contributes to the impairment of the epithelial barrier in both pHNECs and ALI cultures, the underlying mechanism may be HMGB1 impairs the late stage of autophagy. Application of Rapamycin could restore the disrupted epithelial barrier. These findings suggest that HMGB1 may represent a novel target for therapeutic intervention in AR.
