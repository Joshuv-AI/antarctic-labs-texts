Deletion of the Mis12C‐Binding Domain of CENP‐C Promotes Chromosomal Aneuploidy in Cutaneous Squamous Cell Carcinoma
Authors: Megumi Saito, Kazuhiro Okumura, Yurika Tokunaga, Sora Tanaka, Keisuke Otoyama, Yoshinori Hasegawa, Masatoshi Hara, Masakazu Hashimoto, Toshihiko Fujimori, Tatsuo Fukagawa, Yuichi Wakabayashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCENP‐C, an essential component of the kinetochore, connects centromeric chromatin to the outer kinetochore, and thereby ensures accurate chromosome segregation. Although deletion of the Mis12‐binding domain (M12BD) of CENP‐C does not cause developmental disorders in mice, it promotes malignant tumor progression in the two‐stage DMBA/TPA‐induced skin carcinogenesis model. In this study, we have demonstrated that M12BD deletion of CENP‐C enhances proliferation and then promotes abnormal differentiation in DMBA/TPA‐induced carcinomas in mice. To elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, we performed RNA sequencing and found the dysregulated expression of keratinization‐related genes. Intriguingly, elevated chromosomal aneuploidy was detectable in mice with the M12BD deletion of CENP‐C. Among the aneuploidies, trisomies of chromosomes 6 and 10 took place at the highest frequency. These specific chromosomal gains were accompanied by upregulation of genes involved in immune and inflammatory responses. Together, our present findings strongly suggest that M12BD of CENP‐C plays a critical role in the regulation of epithelial differentiation during tumor development in mice.Keywords: aneuploidy, CENP, keratinization, mouse model, squamous cell carcinoma

Introduction

Chromosomal aneuploidy, which often occurs due to a defect in the chromosome segregation process, is a hallmark of cancer [1]. The kinetochore protein CENP‐C connects centromeric chromatin to outer kinetochore complexes such as the Mis12 complex, and thus guarantees accurate chromosome segregation [2, 3]. Therefore, the N‐terminal Mis12 complex‐binding domain (M12BD) of CENP‐C was expected to be essential for chromosome segregation. However, deletion of M12BD did not cause obvious developmental disorders in mice, and the functional role of CENP‐C M12BD remains unclear. Recent findings using the DMBA/TPA‐induced skin carcinogenesis model in mice demonstrated that a M12BD deletion of CENP‐C (Cenpc ΔM12BD) augments tumor progression [4]. In addition, M12BD deletion mice or human cells showed a slight increase in anaphase lagging chromosomes or micronuclei [4]. These findings strongly suggest that the CENP‐C/Mis12C interaction contributes to prevention from aneuploidy‐driven tumorigenesis. The present study demonstrated that M12BD of CENP‐C suppresses malignant transformation, which emphasizes the importance of proper kinetochore assembly and centromere integrity in cancer prevention.

Material and Methods

DMBA/TPA‐Induced Carcinoma Samples

The carcinoma samples used in this study were obtained from a DMBA/TPA two‐stage skin carcinogenesis experiment as previously described [4].

Immunofluorescence

The carcinoma sections were prepared as described in our previous study [5]. The following primary and secondary antibodies used for immunostaining are shown in Table S1. Cell nuclei were counterstained with Hard Set Mounting Medium with DAPI (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA). All fluorescence images were obtained using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope with DMI8i.

RNA‐Sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from DMBA/TPA‐induced carcinoma samples using the Fast Gene RNA Premium Kit (Nippon Genetics). Next‐generation sequencing was conducted by the NextSeq 500 system (Illumina) at the Kazusa DNA Research Institute. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted to assess the enrichment of gene sets located at the chromosomal position (M1) between the two groups, using the GSEA software from the Broad Institute. A false discovery rate (FDR) q value < 0.05, with a p value < 0.05 following 1000 permutations, was considered statistically significant. Basic quality control metrics are shown in Table S2. The genomic coordinates were obtained from the UCSC Table Browser (https://genome.ucsc.edu/cgi‐bin/hgTables).

Digital Karyotyping

Total DNA was extracted from FFPE (formalin fixed paraffin embedded) carcinoma samples using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (QIAGEN). Whole‐genome sequencing was performed using the DNBSEQ‐G400RS (MGI) and DNBSEQ‐G400RS High‐throughput Sequencing Set (App‐A FCL PE100) < 1,000,016,994 > (MGI) at the Kazusa DNA Research Institute, as previously described [6]. Basic quality control metrics are shown in Table S3.

Statistical Analysis

For all data, GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 (GraphPad Software) was used for statistical analysis.

Results and Discussion

M12BD of Cenpc Contributes to Cell Proliferation and Differentiation in DMBA/TPA‐Induced Carcinomas

Recent studies have revealed that Cenpc ΔM12BD/ΔM12BD mice promote the malignant conversion of tumors in the two‐stage DMBA/TPA‐induced skin carcinogenesis model [4]. These findings prompted us to further examine the functional significance of M12BD within Cenpc during skin carcinogenesis. To this end, we performed histological analysis using the DMBA/TPA‐induced carcinomas prepared from Cenpc ΔM12BD/ΔM12BD and Cenpc +/+ mice. As clearly shown in the H&E staining of carcinomas from Cenpc +/+ (Figure 1A, Figure S1A,C) and Cenpc ΔM12BD/ΔM12BD (Figure 1B, Figure S1B,D) mice, Cenpc +/+ carcinomas seemed to show a more differentiated histological appearance, including structures that looked like sweat glands, compared to Cenpc ΔM12BD/ΔM12BD carcinomas. Then, we assessed their proliferation, differentiation, and mesenchymal characteristics by immunohistochemistry for Ki67, Keratin 10 (K10), and vimentin, respectively. As shown in Figure 1C–K, Cenpc ΔM12BD/ΔM12BD mice exhibited significantly more Ki67‐positive cells and fewer K10‐ and vimentin‐positive cells than Cenpc +/+ mice, suggesting increased proliferation and impaired differentiation in the absence of M12BD.

To clarify the molecular mechanisms behind these phenomena, we carried out RNA sequencing on three DMBA/TPA‐induced carcinomas, each prepared from Cenpc +/+ and Cenpc ΔM12BD/ΔM12BD mice. As shown in Figure 2 and Table S4, 603 genes were differentially expressed. GO analysis enriched numerous GO terms, but some showed no direct association with skin tumors. Keratinization was the term most strongly implicated in direct relations with skin tumors. Keratinization is an essential process for terminal keratinocyte differentiation and epidermal barrier function. Dysregulation of keratinization might contribute to skin tumor progression [7], suggesting that the M12BD of Cenpc plays a vital role in the regulation of tumor cell differentiation.

M12BD of Cenpc Is Required for the Maintenance of Chromosomal Integrity

Accurate chromosome segregation is essential for genomic stability, and its failure promotes aneuploidy and cancer development [8]. To ask whether M12BD of Cenpc could participate in the link between tumor progression and aneuploidy, we conducted digital karyotyping on the DMBA/TPA‐induced carcinomas prepared from Cenpc +/+ and Cenpc ΔM12BD/ΔM12BD mice. As previously reported [9], carcinomas from both genotypes frequently exhibited chromosome 7 trisomy. Trisomy of chromosomes 6 (p = 0.0063) and 10 (p = 0.0034) was significantly more common in Cenpc ΔM12BD/ΔM12BD mice (Figure 3). Notably, chromosome 6 aneuploidy was particularly prevalent in DMBA/TPA‐induced tumors [9]. Thus, it is indicative that the deficiency in M12BD of Cenpc selectively causes chromosomes 6 and 10 aneuploidy. Trisomy is known to drive cancer by altering gene dosage and promoting malignancy. In human cancer, the recurrent chromosomes 7 and 12 trisomies were commonly observed in colon and ovarian tumors, implicating oncogene amplification in carcinogenesis [10, 11]. Similarly, chemically induced tumors often show recurrent trisomies of chromosomes 6, 7, and 10, suggesting that specific chromosomal amplifications might promote tumor progression [9, 12].

In support of the recent reports [12], we have observed a significant increase in chromosome 10 trisomy in Cenpc ΔM12BD/ΔM12BD mice. RNA sequencing analysis on the DMBA/TPA‐induced carcinomas revealed that multiple gene sets present on chromosomes 6 and 10, such as CHR6A3 (Chr6) and CHR10D3 (Chr10), are obviously enriched, particularly in Cenpc ΔM12BD/ΔM12BD mice (Figure 4A,B and Table S5). Of note, GO analysis on DEGs (fold change ≥ 2) on chromosome 6 and 10 (Tables S6 and S7) showed a significant enrichment of genes related to inflammation and immune response (Figure 4C).

In this study, we have demonstrated that M12BD deletion of CENP‐C promotes cancer cell proliferation, alters differentiation, and causes chromosomal aneuploidy. In mice and human cells with M12BD deletion, mitotic defects are observed. Although the detailed mechanism remains unclear, it is expected that these initial mitotic defects lead to aneuploidy, which in turn contributes to changes in proliferation and differentiation. Normally, trisomy of chromosome 7 is observed most frequently in DMBA/TPA‐induced mouse tumors, as is also the case in the wild type in this study, while trisomies of chromosomes 6 and 10 is observed less frequently. However, in tumors exhibiting M12BD deletion, trisomies of chromosomes 6, 7, and 10 is detected equally. This suggests that trisomies of chromosome 6 and 10 primarily occur in DMBA/TPA‐induced tumors, and that M12BD deletion enhances their frequency, rather than being the primary cause. In tumors exhibiting trisomies of chromosomes 6 and 10, an elevated expression of gene clusters implicated in immune and inflammatory responses was observed. The underlying mechanism, whether it be an increase in gene copy number or the activation of cellular stress pathways that promote inflammation by chromosomal instability, remains to be elucidated. However, it is established that micronuclei induced by chromosomal instability activate the cGAS‐STING pathway [13]. Furthermore, Irf5 (chromosome 6), which demonstrated increased expression in this study, and Stat2 and Il23a (chromosome 10), which did not show increased expression in this study, may also be the cause of immune and inflammatory responses.

Collectively, these findings strongly suggest that M12BD deficiency in Cenpc induces aneuploidy and participates in carcinogenesis through the regulation of immune‐related genes contributing to malignancy.
