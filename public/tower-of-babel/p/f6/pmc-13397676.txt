Nanoemulsions of 1,8-cineole, and Eucalyptus globulus and Laurus nobilis essential oils: cytotoxic, antibacterial, and antioxidant activities
Authors: Mahsa Shafiei, Elham Zarenezhad, Hiva Alipanah, Homa Karimian, Razieh Ranjbar, Mahmoud Osanloo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntimicrobial and anticancer drug resistance is an increasing concern, driving interest in plant-based nanoformulations. Here, we developed and compared nanoemulsions containing Eucalyptus globulus essential oil (EO), Laurus nobilis EO, and their main constituent, 1,8-cineole. Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) revealed 1,8-cineole content of 49.5% in E. globulus EO and 37.9% in L. nobilis EO. Nanoemulsions were prepared by spontaneous emulsification and characterized for droplet size (dynamic light scattering) and zeta potential. Attenuated Total Reflectance-Fourier Transform Infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy qualitatively confirmed EO encapsulation within the nanoemulsions. Average droplet sizes were 172 ± 9 nm (E. globulus EO), 92 ± 5 nm (L. nobilis EO), 137 ± 7 nm (1,8-cineole), and 70 ± 12 nm (blank nanoemulsion). The corresponding zeta potential values were − 24 ± 1.2 mV, − 34 ± 0.7 mV, − 10 ± 0.7 mV, and − 6 ± 1.5 mV. The L. nobilis EO nanoemulsion had the strongest antioxidant activity (IC₅₀ = 342 µg/mL). The E. globulus EO nanoemulsion showed the greatest cytotoxicity against A-375 human melanoma cells (IC₅₀ = 171 µg/mL) and the most potent antibacterial effects against Escherichia coli (IC₅₀ = 762 µg/mL) and Staphylococcus aureus (IC₅₀ = 205 µg/mL). These results suggest that E. globulus EO nanoemulsions have potential as antimicrobial and cytotoxic agents, pending confirmation in in vivo studies.Keywords: Nanoemulsion, Nanomedicine, Melanoma, Antibacterial, Antioxidant

Introduction

Skin cancer, encompassing melanoma and non-melanoma types, ranks among the most prevalent cancers worldwide [1, 2]. Melanoma, the most aggressive form, is responsible for the majority of skin cancer-related deaths due to its rapid metastasis [3]. Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) caused by bacterial pathogens are another critical concern, especially in patients with compromised skin integrity. Among the most notable bacterial agents involved in such infections are the Gram-negative Escherichia coli and the Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus [4]. While typically commensal in the human body, certain strains of E. coli and S. aureus can become pathogenic, with S. aureus being a leading cause of burn wound infections [5, 6]. Both melanoma and bacterial infections underscore the urgent need for multifunctional therapeutic agents, particularly those effective for topical application.

Given the growing threat of drug resistance, medicinal plants have received increasing attention for their diverse therapeutic potential [7]. Laurus nobilis L. (bay leaf), an evergreen tree of the Lauraceae family native to the Mediterranean, Europe, and the Americas, has long been used in traditional medicine to treat conditions such as dermatitis, rheumatism, and urological disorders [8, 9]. Its essential oil (EO) and aqueous extracts exhibit a broad range of biological activities, including antioxidant, antimicrobial [10, 11], anticancer, and other pharmacological effects [12, 13]. Similarly, Eucalyptus globulus, a member of the Myrtaceae family originally from Australia and now cultivated worldwide, is well known for its medicinal value [14]. Its EO and aqueous extracts possess notable antioxidant, antimicrobial [15], and anti-inflammatory properties [16, 17]. Both L. nobilis and E. globulus EOs share 1,8-cineole (eucalyptol), a bioactive monoterpenoid recognized for its antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and pharmacological effects [18, 19]. Furthermore, these two species are widely available in the country where this research was conducted, and their EOs can be easily obtained from pharmaceutical manufacturers, which ensured reliable access to the materials used in this study.

In response to the increasing problem of drug resistance, there has been growing interest in plant-based nanoformulations as a promising strategy to improve the therapeutic efficacy of natural products, particularly those with low intrinsic activity or limited bioavailability due to their hydrophobic nature [17, 20]. Among these, nanoemulsions, colloidal systems with droplet sizes typically between 20 and 200 nm, offer excellent physical stability and are well-suited for localized drug delivery, especially via dermal routes. This nanotechnology-based approach is especially advantageous for treating skin-related conditions such as melanoma and bacterial infections [21, 22]. However, limited studies have systematically compared the biological activities of L. nobilis and E. globulus EOs, or their major constituent 1,8-cineole, when delivered through nanoemulsion systems, particularly against melanoma and clinically relevant bacterial pathogens.

This study aimed to formulate and compare nanoemulsions prepared from L. nobilis and E. globulus essential oils and their major component, 1,8-cineole. As these EOs are well-characterized and readily available from pharmaceutical manufacturers, they were directly nanoemulsified. The resulting nanoemulsions were evaluated for antioxidant activity, cytotoxicity against A375 melanoma cells, and antibacterial activity against E. coli and S. aureus.

Materials and methods

Materials

E. globulus and L. nobilis EOs extracted from their leaves were procured from Zardband Pharmaceuticals Co. (Iran). The EOs was obtained by hydrodistillation using a Clevenger-type apparatus, following standard protocols to ensure high-quality yields. A375 (ATCC CRL-1619) cell line, Escherichia coli (ATCC 25922), and Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 25923) were obtained from the Pasteur Institute of Iran.

GC-MS procedure

The chemical compositions of the EOs were characterized by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) using an Agilent 6890 gas chromatograph equipped with an HP-5MS column (USA) coupled to an Agilent 5973 mass spectrometer (USA). The EO samples were diluted with n-hexane (HPLC-grade), and 1 µL of the resulting solution was injected into the GC-MS system. The HP-5MS column had a length of 30 m, an internal diameter of 0.25 mm, and a film thickness of 0.25 μm. The temperature program was set as follows: the initial temperature was maintained at 50 °C, followed by a 3 °C/min increase for 5 min. The temperature was then increased to 250 °C at a rate of 15 °C/min to 300 °C, which was held for an additional 3 min. The total analysis time was 52 min. The injection chamber temperature was set to 300 °C, operating in a split mode with a 1:35 ratio. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. Mass spectrometric detection was performed using electron ionization (EI) at 70 eV, with a scan range of 40–500 m/z. The ion source temperature was maintained at 220 °C. Compound identification was done by comparing the obtained mass spectra with reference standards, retention indices from published literature, and spectral data available in computer databases and online libraries. The relative percentage of each compound was calculated by normalizing peak areas in the total ion chromatogram without the use of an internal standard [23, 24].

Preparation and characterization of nanoemulsions

Nanoemulsions were prepared following the spontaneous emulsification method described in our previous work [25]. A preliminary screening was conducted to determine the optimal surfactant concentration for achieving stable nanoemulsions. Three different amounts of the surfactant Tween 20 (0.20%, 0.22%, and 0.25% v/v) were evaluated in combination with a fixed concentration (0.2% v/v) of E. globulus EO, L. nobilis EO, or 1,8-cineole. Based on the screening results (see Table 2), which identified 0.25% v/v Tween 20 as the optimal concentration for producing nanoemulsions with a droplet size below 200 nm and a narrow size distribution (SPAN < 1), this ratio was selected for all subsequent preparations.

The organic phase, consisting of 0.2% v/v of either E. globulus EO, L. nobilis EO, or 1,8-cineole, was mixed with 0.25% v/v of Tween 20 at room temperature and stirred at 2000 rpm for 5 min. Distilled water was then gradually added to the mixture until a final volume of 5000 µL was reached, followed by continuous stirring for 45 min to ensure homogeneity. A control nanoemulsion lacking EOs or 1,8-cineole (designated as NE(-oil)) was prepared using the same procedure. The prepared nanoemulsions were characterized using a DLS instrument (Horiba, SZ100, Japan) to determine the mean droplet size, droplet size distribution (expressed as SPAN value), and zeta potential. The SPAN values were obtained using Eq. 1: (d90 − d10) / d50, where d is the droplet diameter and the subscripts 10, 50, and 90 represent the percentiles of droplets smaller than these diameters [26]. The physical stability of the nanoemulsions was evaluated by visual inspection in accordance with accelerated stability testing guidelines. Formulations were stored at 4 °C and 25 °C for 6 months and monitored for signs of instability such as creaming, cracking, or phase separation [27].

ATR-FTIR was employed to characterize the chemical compatibility and presence of expected functional groups of E. globulus EO, L. nobilis EO, and 1,8-cineole within the selected nanoemulsions. Spectra of all nanoemulsion samples, including the NE(-oil), were recorded in the 400–4000 cm− 1 range using a Tensor II spectrometer (Bruker Co., Germany) [26]. Additionally, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Philips EM208S, Netherlands) operating at an accelerating voltage of 100 kV was used to evaluate the morphology and structural features of the nanoemulsions [26].

DPPH assay

Using the DPPH radical-scavenging assay, the antioxidant potential of nanoemulsions containing E. globulus EO, L. nobilis EO, or 1,8-cineole was investigated. Ethanol dilutions spanning concentrations from 31.25 µg/mL to 1000 µg/mL were tested. Each sample dilution (40 µL) was added to 160 µL of a 0.3 mM ethanolic DPPH solution in a 96-well plate. Plates were shielded from light and incubated at 25 °C for 30 min before absorbance measurement at 517 nm (Synergy HTX Multi-Mode Reader). The percentage inhibition of DPPH radicals (antioxidant activity %) was calculated using Eq. 2: ((Ac − As) / Ac) × 100, where Ac is the absorbance of the control (ethanol with DPPH; n = 6 replicates per plate) and As is the absorbance of the sample [26].

The nanoemulsion samples did not exhibit noticeable turbidity or intrinsic absorbance at the measurement wavelength; therefore, background absorbance of samples without DPPH was considered negligible and was not subtracted.

The MTT assay

Cell viability assessment of A375 melanoma cells following exposure was performed using the MTT colorimetric assay. Cells were seeded at 10,000 cells/well into 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h in complete DMEM medium (10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin) under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂, humidified atmosphere). After this initial incubation, a partial medium was replaced by adding 50 µL of fresh complete medium. The nanoemulsions – containing E. globulus EO, L. nobilis EO, 1,8-cineole, or the oil-free control NE(-oil) – were then applied across a concentration gradient (31.25–1000 µg/mL) for 24 h.

Post-treatment, 100 µL of MTT reagent (0.5 mg/mL in medium) was introduced to each well. After 4 h of incubation at 37 °C, intracellular formazan crystals were solubilized by adding 100 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and agitating continuously for 30 min. Absorbance at 570 nm was quantified using a Synergy HTX Multi-Mode Reader. Percentage cell viability relative to untreated controls was computed according to Eq. 3: (ODs / ODc) × 100, where ODc is the absorbance of control (untreated) cells and ODs is the absorbance of treated cells [26].

The nanoemulsion formulations did not exhibit noticeable turbidity or intrinsic absorbance at 570 nm.

Antibacterial assay

The antibacterial effects of the nanoemulsions against S. aureus and E. coli were evaluated using the broth microdilution method in 96-well plates. Bacterial suspensions were prepared in Mueller–Hinton broth (MHB) and adjusted to 0.5 McFarland turbidity (approximately 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL). Serial dilutions of the nanoemulsion samples were prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then mixed with the bacterial suspension in MHB in the microplate wells (50 µL sample dilution + 50 µL bacterial suspension per well).

Control wells included untreated bacterial cells (growth control) and blank nanoemulsion without essential oil (NE-oil) to evaluate the effect of the formulation matrix. All solutions, glassware, and magnetic stir bars used during nanoemulsion preparation were sterilized prior to the experiments to maintain aseptic conditions.

The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, bacterial growth was quantified by measuring absorbance at 630 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy HTX Multi-Mode Reader, USA). The nanoemulsions showed negligible intrinsic color or turbidity at the tested concentrations; therefore, interference with optical density measurements was minimal.

Bacterial growth at each concentration was calculated using Eq. 4: (ODs / ODc) × 100, where ODc is the absorbance of control (untreated) cells and ODs is the absorbance of treated cells [28].

Statistical analyses

All experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates, and each biological replicate included three technical replicate wells per concentration. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Dose–response curves and IC₅₀ values were calculated using CalcuSyn software (Biosoft, Cambridge, UK). Experimental data were entered into the software to generate dose–response relationships, from which IC₅₀ values and their corresponding upper and lower confidence limits were automatically determined.

Comparisons between formulations were interpreted based on the confidence limits of the IC₅₀ values. Non-overlapping upper and lower confidence limits were considered indicative of a significant difference in potency. This clarification has been added to the revised manuscript.

As demonstrated in Table 2, the concentration of Tween 20 significantly impacted the size characteristics of the nanoemulsions for all bioactive compounds tested. A strong inverse relationship was observed: increasing the Tween 20 concentration from 0.20% to 0.25% v/v consistently decreased droplet size (D50) and the SPAN value. For E. globulus EO, only the formulation with 0.25% Tween 20 met both criteria simultaneously (D50 = 172 nm, SPAN = 0.95). The 0.22% formulation had an acceptable droplet size (190 nm) and a SPAN value (0.99) at the acceptable threshold. For L. nobilis EO, the formulation with 0.25% Tween 20 produced a superior nanoemulsion with the smallest droplet size (92 nm) and a desirable SPAN value (0.97). While the 0.22% formulation had an acceptable SPAN value (1.0), its droplet size (137 nm) was larger than the optimal formulation. For 1,8-cineole, the results were particularly definitive. The formulations containing 0.20% and 0.22% Tween 20 produced nanoemulsions with high SPAN values (1.6 and 1.2, respectively), indicating broad, unstable size distributions. Only the formulation with 0.25% Tween 20 successfully formed a nanoemulsion within our target range (D50 = 137 nm, SPAN = 0.96). Furthermore, the control nanoemulsion prepared without any oil (NE(-oil)) using 0.25% Tween 20 confirmed the effectiveness of the chosen surfactant concentration, yielding a tiny droplet size (70 nm) and a narrow distribution (SPAN = 0.99), which is characteristic of well-formed surfactant micelles. In conclusion, the formulation containing 0.25% v/v Tween 20 was unequivocally selected for all subsequent experiments.

Size characteristics

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the droplet sizes and SPAN values of the selected nanoemulsions were as follows: 172 ± 9 nm and 0.95 for E. globulus nanoemulsion, 92 ± 5 nm and 0.97 for L. nobilis nanoemulsion, 137 ± 7 nm and 0.96 for 1,8-cineole nanoemulsion, and 70 ± 12 nm and 0.99 for the blank formulation (NE(-oil)). All formulations exhibited nanoscale droplet sizes with relatively narrow distributions, indicating good physical stability and homogeneity. According to accepted criteria, droplet sizes < 200 nm and SPAN values < 1 are optimal for nanoemulsion systems [29].

Zeta potential and long-term stability

Zeta potential measurements of the nanoemulsions are presented in Fig. 2. The values were − 24 ± 1.2 mV for E. globulus EO nanoemulsion, -34 ± 0.7 mV for L. nobilis EO nanoemulsion, -10 ± 0.7 mV for 1,8-cineole nanoemulsion, and − 6 ± 1.5 mV for the blank formulation (NE(-oil)). These results reflect the formulations’ colloidal stability; higher negative zeta potentials help prevent droplet aggregation through electrostatic repulsion [30].

Zeta potential profiles of nanoemulsions containing A: E. globulus EO (-24 ± 1.2 mV), B: L. nobilis EO (-34 ± 0.7 mV), C: 1,8-cineole (-10 ± 0.7 mV), and D: NE(-oil) (-6 ± 1.5 mV)

The nanoemulsions were stored for 6 months at 4 °C and 25 °C to assess long-term stability. At the end of this period, no visible signs of phase separation, creaming, or cracking were observed in any of the formulations, confirming their excellent physical stability over time. This robust stability is attributed to the combination of sufficient electrostatic repulsion (as indicated by zeta potential) and the steric stabilization provided by Tween 20 [27, 31].

Morphology

TEM imaging (Fig. 3) was used to investigate the morphology of the nanoemulsions. All droplets exhibited a spherical shape with smooth surfaces, typical of well-formed nanoemulsions.

The droplet sizes observed under TEM were consistent with DLS results, supporting the stability and homogeneity of the nanoemulsion systems—both essential for effective drug delivery.

The ATR-FTIR spectrum of the L. nobilis EO nanoemulsion displayed a broad peak at 3254–3700 cm− 1, indicating O-H stretching from hydrogen bonds formed among the EO, Tween 20, and water. The vibration at 2921 and 2856 cm− 1 corresponded to the C-H stretching. The absorption at 1730 cm− 1 is connected to C = O ν(s) due to the C = O group in Tween 20 and EO. The absorption at 1457 cm− 1 exhibited CH2 bending. The prominent peak at 1088 cm− 1 corresponds to C-O bond stretching vibrations.

The ATR-FTIR spectrum of the 1,8-cineole nanoemulsion exhibited a broad absorption band spanning 3254–3700 cm− 1, characteristic of O–H stretching from hydrogen bonding interactions. The vibration at 2925 cm− 1 is assigned to the C-H ν(s) due to tween 20 and 1, 8 cineole. The band at 1755 cm− 1 is related to C = O ν(s). The spectra at around 1457 cm− 1 showed CH2 bending. The spectra at 1088 cm− 1 are related to C-O stretching due to 1,8 cineole, and Tween 20. Strong hydrogen bonding confirms the interaction between the essential oil (EO) or bioactive compounds with Tween 20 and water through intermolecular hydrogen bonding. This result showed that the EOs and 1,8-cineole are components that remain present within the formulation and within the nanodroplets.

Discussions

This study successfully developed stable nanoemulsions containing L. nobilis and E. globulus EOs and their primary bioactive monoterpene, 1,8-cineole. A systematic optimization was employed to achieve colloidal properties specifically designed for enhanced topical delivery. DLS analysis confirmed the formation of nanoscale droplets with a narrow size distribution, obtaining a mean diameter within the ideal range of 92 to 172 nm. This sub-200 nm size is critical for promoting effective skin permeability and deep tissue penetration, overcoming the natural barrier function of the stratum corneum [30, 33].

Furthermore, the uniformly low SPAN values indicate a monodisperse population, essential for consistent dosage and reproducible biological effects upon application. The significant negative zeta potential values, ranging from − 10 to -34 mV, provide a strong electrostatic repulsion barrier that prevents droplet aggregation and coalescence. This electrostatic stabilization, combined with the steric hindrance afforded by the Tween 20 surfactant, confers excellent long-term colloidal stability, a prerequisite for any viable pharmaceutical formulation [34].

Critically, the potential biological role of the surfactant was taken into account. Tween 20 is known to influence membrane permeability and may exert cytotoxic effects at elevated concentrations; however, the formulation level used in this study (0.25% v/v) was deliberately chosen to remain below such thresholds. This was further verified by the negligible biological activity observed in the blank nanoemulsion (Tween 20 in water), confirming that the potent effects are attributable to the encapsulated essential oils rather than the surfactant vehicle [35, 36].

Among the nanoemulsions tested, L. nobilis EO nanoemulsion demonstrated the most potent antioxidant activity with an IC₅₀ of 342 µg/mL. This result aligns with previous reports indicating that methanolic extracts of L. nobilis exhibit significant free radical scavenging properties [37, 38]. Although 1,8-cineole exhibited limited activity in the DPPH assay as a standalone compound, its interaction with other bioactive compounds within the complex EO mixtures may contribute to the overall antioxidant activity. However, these interactions should be considered putative rather than confirmed synergistic effects. Definitive demonstration of synergy would require further studies using reconstituted oils composed of the major pure compounds mixed at the same ratios as found in the natural EOs. For instance, flavonoids or phenolic acids often found in L. nobilis could interact with 1,8-cineole to produce a more substantial overall effect than individual components. These findings suggest that L. nobilis nanoemulsions have potential for complementary therapies targeting oxidative stress.

The E. globulus EO nanoemulsion showed the most pronounced cytotoxic activity, with an IC₅₀ of 171 µg/mL against A375 melanoma cells. This potency compares favorably with other essential oil-based formulations, such as those derived from Zhumeria majdae (IC₅₀: 624 µg/mL) and Plumbago zeylanica (IC₅₀: 625.60 µg/mL) [39, 40], and even natural borneol (IC50: 300 µg/mL) [41]. However, it was slightly less effective than Origanum majorana EO nanoemulsion (IC50: 139 µg/mL) [42]. These findings align with prior research demonstrating the pro-apoptotic activity of E. globulus EO in colorectal carcinoma cells [43]. Furthermore, triterpenoids from E. globulus leaves have shown moderate cytotoxic effects against breast cancer (MDA-MB-231), gastric adenocarcinoma (SGC-7901), and cervical cancer (HeLa) cell lines [44]. Similarly, laurel oil exhibited cytotoxicity against brain cancer (AMGM-5) cells, with apoptosis confirmed via staining and caspase-3 immunohistochemistry [45]. Overall, the substantial reduction in melanoma cell viability positions the E. globulus nanoemulsion as a promising cytotoxic agent, potentially by inducing apoptosis or inhibiting cell proliferation pathways. However, further mechanistic studies are warranted.

In vitro investigations demonstrated concentration-dependent inhibition of MCF-7 breast cancer cell proliferation by ethanolic leaf extracts of L. nobilis [46]. Additionally, a recent study suggests that chitosan nanodroplets loaded with L. nobilis ethanolic extract suppressed cell proliferation and migration activity in gastric cancer cells [47]. A specific investigation revealed that the combination of 1,8-cineole and simvastatin induced cell cycle arrest and increased apoptosis sensitivity in lung cancer cells [48]. Furthermore, another study investigated the anticancer effects of 1,8-cineole on hepatocellular carcinoma and concluded that 1,8-cineole exerts its antitumor effects by regulating multiple molecular targets [49]. These findings suggest a broad-spectrum of cytotoxic activity for both EOs and 1,8-cineole, indicating their versatility. Nevertheless, further mechanistic studies are required to confirm these hypotheses.

The E. globulus nanoemulsion exhibited robust antibacterial activity against E. coli, with an IC50 of 762 µg/mL, outperforming 1,8-cineole (MIC range: 1.25–2 mg/mL) [50] and extracts of Senna alata and Lantana camara (MIC: 970–1170 µg/mL) [51]. Against S. aureus, E. globulus nanoemulsion exhibited potent antibacterial activity (IC50: 205 µg/mL). It outperformed extracts from Portulaca oleracea (MIC: 512 µg/mL) and Kalanchoe crenata (MIC: 512–1024 µg/mL) [52]. Callistemon citrinus and Vernonia adoensis extracts showed lower activity (IC50: 1440 µg/mL and 1610 µg/mL, respectively) [53], while Sardinian medicinal plants displayed the highest inhibitory effect (IC50: 1.4–153.6 µg/mL) [54]. These results indicate that E. globulus nanoemulsion is a strong candidate for treating S. aureus-induced infections.

The antibacterial properties of L. nobilis and E. globulus EOs, primarily attributed to 1,8-cineole, operate through multiple mechanisms. These include disrupting bacterial membrane integrity, leading to increased permeability, leakage of intracellular contents, and subsequent interference with vital enzymatic and metabolic pathways [55, 56]. Bacterial cell death is further driven by the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative stress. Additionally, interactions between 1,8-cineole and other bioactive constituents within the EO may contribute to the observed antibacterial effects. However, these interactions should be interpreted cautiously, as definitive confirmation of synergistic effects would require experiments using reconstituted oils prepared from pure compounds in proportions equivalent to those in the native essential oil. It may offer a valuable approach for overcoming multidrug resistance, particularly by enhancing the penetration of active compounds into bacterial cells or disrupting efflux pumps [57, 58]. Nevertheless, further mechanistic studies are required to confirm these hypotheses.

It should also be noted that the cytotoxic effects were evaluated only on cancer cell lines in this study. Therefore, a logical next step will be to evaluate the selectivity index by including normal human cell lines to better predict therapeutic safety. The promising in vitro results presented here will require further validation in established animal models to assess the efficacy, biodistribution, and in vivo safety profile of these nanoemulsions before any clinical applications can be considered.

Conclusion

The findings highlight the distinct yet complementary multifunctional potential of nanoemulsions containing L. nobilis, E. globulus, and 1,8-cineole. In vitro assessments revealed that the L. nobilis nanoemulsion exhibits effective antioxidant activity, while the E. globulus nanoemulsion demonstrates notably superior antibacterial and cytotoxic effects. In particular, the significant suppression of melanoma cell proliferation by the E. globulus nanoemulsion supports its potential application in anticancer therapy. These results underscore the therapeutic promise of EO-based nanoemulsions and provide a compelling rationale for further preclinical and clinical investigations to integrate them into modern pharmaceutical applications. Future studies should focus on in vivo efficacy and safety profiles, as well as detailed mechanistic investigations to elucidate the specific molecular pathways these nanoformulations target in combating cancer and bacterial infections.
