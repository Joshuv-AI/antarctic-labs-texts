ROS-responsive 3D biological scaffold delivers hypoxia-primed extracellular vesicles for targeted modulation of neuroinflammation in intracerebral hemorrhage
Authors: Aobo Zhang, Dan Qiao, Ziyang Jia, Zhanzhan Zhang, Dongdong Yan, Chengrui Nan, Liqiang Liu, Yunpeng Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmerging evidence suggests that paracrine mechanisms may underlie the therapeutic effects of human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) (hUCMSC-exos) in mitigating neuroinflammation following intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH). Hypoxic preconditioning enhances the paracrine efficacy of hUCMSC-exos. Building on prior studies [1, 2], we developed a ROS-responsive three-dimensional (3D) biological scaffold encapsulating hypoxia-primed EVs (Hypo-Exos) for sustained release under reactive oxygen species (ROS)-rich conditions.MethodsThe 3D biological scaffold was fabricated via a thermoresponsive crosslinking strategy using gelatin methacrylate (GelMA), silk fibroin, and brain-derived decellularized extracellular matrix (dECM), functionalized with phenylboronic acid (PBA)-modified polyvinyl alcohol (PVA). Hypo-Exos, enriched with miR-146b via hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) activation, were incorporated into the scaffold using advanced 3D bioprinting. Dual-luciferase reporter assays validated miR-146b targeting of the 3’UTR of COP1 (an E3 ubiquitin ligase). In a rat ICH model, the scaffold was implanted in situ. Neurological function, angiogenesis, neuroinflammation, and synaptic plasticity were evaluated at days 1, 4, 7, and 14.ResultsThe 3D biological scaffold enabled sustained delivery of Hypo-Exos, shifting microglial polarization from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes, thereby attenuating neuroinflammation and neuronal damage. Mechanistically, miR-146b suppressed COP1 expression via post-transcriptional silencing, thereby attenuating NF-κB p65 signaling and downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines.ConclusionThe ROS-responsive 3D biological scaffold -mediated delivery of Hypo-Exos modulates neuroinflammation through ubiquitination pathways, stabilizes the early-phase ICH microenvironment, and improves functional recovery. This platform represents a promising therapeutic strategy for ICH, offering dual advantages as a drug delivery system and a regenerative therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04691-x.Keywords: 3D biological scaffold, Hypo-Exos, Intracerebral hemorrhage, Neuroinflammation, miR-146b/COP1

Introduction

ICH, a devastating cerebrovascular disorder, is associated with high morbidity, disability, and mortality rates [3]. Beyond primary mechanical injury, ICH triggers secondary pathological cascades including cerebral edema, blood-brain barrier disruption, and neuroinflammation, which critically determine long-term neurological outcomes [4, 5]. During the acute and subacute phases, activated microglia and infiltrating immune cells exacerbate neuronal damage through excessive neuroinflammatory responses and release of pro-inflammatory mediators [6, 7]. Thus, modulating post-ICH neuroinflammation represents a pivotal therapeutic target to improve clinical prognosis.

As the primary innate immune cells in the central nervous system, microglia play a central role in post-ICH inflammatory dynamics [8]. Following ICH, microglia rapidly polarize into pro-inflammatory (M1) or anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotypes [9]. M1 microglia aggravate neuronal injury via secretion of cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, whereas M2 microglia promote tissue repair through anti-inflammatory factors (e.g., IL-10) and neurotrophic support [10]. Reprogramming microglial polarization toward the M2 phenotype has thus emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy.

Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUCMSCs) are widely investigated for their immunomodulatory and regenerative capacities [2, 11]. Their therapeutic effects are primarily attributed to paracrine signaling mediated by EVs (hUCMSC-exos), which transfer bioactive cargo—including proteins, lipids, mRNAs, and miRNAs—to regulate target cell functions [13–15]. Notably, hUCMSC-exos exhibit therapeutic potential in diverse neurological disorders [15].

Oxygen tension critically regulates hUCMSC proliferation, differentiation, and secretory profiles [16, 17]. However, standard in vitro culture conditions (21% O₂) starkly contrast with the hypoxic microenvironment (2%–8% O₂) of the ICH lesion core [18, 19]. Hypoxic preconditioning (1% O₂) enhances hUCMSC-exos bioactivity, as demonstrated by increased pro-angiogenic factor expression in peripheral artery disease models [20] and improved cardiac repair via reduced apoptosis and enhanced progenitor cell recruitment in myocardial infarction models [21]. These findings suggest hypoxia-primed hUCMSC-exos (Hypo-Exos) may amplify therapeutic efficacy in ICH. Yet, whether Hypo-Exos mitigate ICH-induced neuroinflammation and the underlying mechanisms remain unexplored [22, 23].

Recent studies focus on exosomal miRNAs as key mediators of intercellular communication [1, 24–27]. Hypoxia upregulates specific miRNAs in hUCMSC-exos, potentially enhancing their regulatory effects on target cells. Here, we investigated whether hypoxia-induced HIF-1α activation enriches miR-146b in Hypo-Exos, thereby modulating neuroinflammation via ubiquitination pathways in ICH.

Our findings reveal that Hypo-Exos shift microglial polarization from M1 to M2 phenotypes by suppressing COP1, an E3 ubiquitin ligase implicated in NF-κB pathway activation [28–30]. Bioinformatics analysis predicted miR-146b-5p targeting of the COP1 mRNA 3’UTR, validated by dual-luciferase assays. This miR-146b/COP1 axis attenuates neuroinflammation and neuronal apoptosis, providing neuroprotection post-ICH.

Despite the therapeutic promise of hUCMSC-exos, challenges persist, including transient bioavailability and impractical repeat administrations [16]. Localized delivery via biomaterial scaffolds offers a solution. Building on prior work [1, 2], we engineered a ROS-responsive 3D biological scaffold composed of GelMA, silk fibroin, and dECM, crosslinked with PBA-modified PVA. Using advanced 3D bioprinting, Hypo-Exos were encapsulated within this scaffold (3D biological scaffold @exos), enabling sustained release triggered by elevated ROS levels in the ICH microenvironment (Scheme 1).

Oxidative stress exacerbates ICH progression by inducing neuronal apoptosis, MMP-13 overexpression, and impaired autophagy [31–33]. Conventional antioxidants face limitations due to rapid clearance and instability [34]. Our scaffold addresses this via dual functionality: (1) ROS-scavenging properties and (2) on-demand Hypo-Exos release, synergistically mitigating neuroinflammation and oxidative damage. This innovative platform not only elucidates molecular mechanisms of post-ICH neuroinflammation but also advances translational strategies for stem cell-derived exosome therapies.

Materials and methods

Materials

Deoxyribonuclease I (DNase I; Cat. No. DN25, CAS: 9003-98-9) and ribonuclease A (RNase A; Cat. No. V900498, CAS: 9001-99-4) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). GelMA (Cat. No. A2033, CAS: 9005-38-3; medium viscosity), silk fibroin (Cat. No. A2011, CAS: 9028-38-3), and pepsin (Cat. No. P0103, CAS: 9001-75-6) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Gelatin (Cat. No. AC08BA0009, CAS: 9000-70-8; Bloom index: 240–270; moisture < 15%; total plate count < 1000 CFU/g) was supplied by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). All other chemicals were analytical grade and used as received.

Preparation of brain dECM

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (10–12 weeks old, 250–300 g) were housed under controlled conditions (25 °C, 12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. Brain tissues were decellularized using a sequential protocol, Low-osmotic treatment: Immersion in 0.1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) for 24 h. Detergent washes: 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) for 24 h, followed by 1% Triton X-100 for 48 h. Enzymatic digestion: Treatment with DNase/RNase (1:1 mixture) for 12 h and 5% peracetic acid for 2 h. Sterilization: Lyophilization and gamma irradiation (25 kGy).

Characterization of dECM

Both native and decellularized rat brain tissues were characterized using electron microscopy and histological techniques. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (GeminiSEM 300, Zeiss) was employed after gold plating to assess the cross-sectional morphology of native and dECM samples. DNA quantification was performed utilizing a Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China), with concentrations measured at 260 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Masson staining were used to evaluate cell distribution, while Sirius red staining quantified proteoglycan and collagen distribution. Total collagen content was assessed using a collagen detection kit (ab222942, Abcam).

Bioink preparation and rheological analysis

dECM digestion: Freeze-dried dECM (100 mg) was digested in 3 mL 0.01 M HCl (pH 2.0) with 45 mg pepsin for 24 h. Circular dichroism (CD; J-1500, Jasco, Japan) confirmed structural integrity (190–260 nm). pH adjustment: Neutralized to pH 7.0–7.2 with 10 M NaOH. Bioink formulation: 15% (w/v) gelatin, 3% (w/v) silk fibroin, and 2% (w/v) PBA-modified PVA dissolved in distilled water (55 °C, 30 min), blended with dECM solution, and pasteurized. Rheological properties: Analyzed using a rotational rheometer (Kinexus Ultra+, Malvern Panalytical, UK) with 25 mm parallel plates. Strain sweeps (0.01–100% strain, 10 rad/s) and temperature ramps (0–50 °C) were performed in triplicate.

Fabrication of ROS-responsive 3D biological scaffold

The scaffold was printed using a bioprinter (Bio-Architect®-WS, Regenovo, China) with a 100 μm nozzle under optimized parameters, Temperature: Nozzle (20 °C), chamber (4 °C). Pressure: 0.2–0.4 kPa. Speed: 6 mm/s. Hypoxia-primed hUCMSC-exos (Hypo-Exos, 400 µg/mL) were homogenized into the bioink. Cylindrical scaffolds (1 mm height × 2 mm diameter) were crosslinked in 5% CaCl₂ for 5 min.

Characterization of ROS-responsive 3D biological scaffold

The scaffold was characterized using Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy (Nicolet iS10), with 32 scans covering the range of 4000 to 400 cm-1. SEM (GeminiSEM 300, Zeiss) was also utilized to visualize the scaffold’s porous structure. The influence of temperature and the scaffold’s injectability were assessed by monitoring changes in viscosity at varying shear rates.

In vitro H₂O₂ scavenging assay of 3D biological scaffold @exos

H₂O₂ solutions (50 µM, 100 µM, 500 µM, and 1 mM) were prepared in 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes (n = 2 per concentration, total 8 samples; 500 µL per sample). The 3D biological scaffold @exos were immersed in each H₂O₂ solution and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 2 h. After centrifugation (1000 × g, 5 min), 100 µL of supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate. An equal volume of 1 M sodium iodide aqueous solution was added to each well, followed by a 5-minute dark reaction. Reaction products were analyzed by measuring ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) absorption spectra between 300 and 500 nm using a microplate spectrophotometer (SpectraMax® iD3, Molecular Devices, USA).

In vitro culture and in vivo degradation of 3D biological scaffold @exos

In vitro viability: Cell-laden scaffolds were cultured for 1, 4, 7, and 14 days. Cell viability was assessed using a Live/Dead Viability/Cytotoxicity Kit (Cat. L3224, Molecular Probes, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

In vivo tracking: Luciferase-labeled scaffolds were implanted in situ into Sprague-Dawley rats (n = 5 per group). Scaffold degradation and exosome release were monitored non-invasively at days 1, 4, 7, and 14 using an IVIS Lumina XRMS small-animal imaging system (PerkinElmer, USA).

Hemodynamic assessment of the 3D biological scaffold @exos

The 3D biological scaffold @exos were implanted in situ into rats with induced ICH. Cerebral hemodynamic parameters, including regional cerebral blood flow (rCBF) and vascular permeability, were quantitatively evaluated peri-hematomally using laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI) and dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE-MRI) at postoperative days 1, 4, 7, and 14.

Cell culture and hypoxic preconditioning

hUCMSCs were procured from Hebei Benyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China) and maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% exosome-depleted fetal bovine serum (System Biosciences, USA). Cells were passaged at 80% confluency and only passages 3–5 were used for experiments. For hypoxic preconditioning, hUCMSCs were cultured in a tri-gas incubator (Heal Force, China) under controlled oxygen tension (2% O₂, 5% CO₂, balanced with N₂) for 48 h. Normoxic controls were maintained at 21% O₂ and 5% CO₂. Surface marker expression (CD73, CD90, CD105) was confirmed by flow cytometry (≥ 95% purity; BD FACSAria™ III, USA).

Exosome isolation and characterization

Conditioned media collection: Upon reaching 80% confluency, hUCMSCs were cultured in exosome-depleted media for 48 h under normoxia or hypoxia. Differential centrifugation, 300 × g for 10 min (pellet cells), 2,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C (remove debris). Filtration: Supernatant filtered through a 0.22 μm Steritop™ PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA). Concentration: Ultracentrifugation (100,000 × g, 4 °C, 1 h) using a 30% sucrose/D₂O density cushion (Beckman Coulter, USA). The exosome-enriched fraction was collected, diluted in PBS, and concentrated to 200 µL via centrifugal filtration (4,000 × g, 4 °C).

Size distribution: Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NanoSight LM10, Malvern Panalytical, UK) with samples diluted 1:1000 in PBS. Morphology: Negative staining and TEM imaging (Tecnai 12, Philips, Netherlands; 80 kV). Surface markers: Western blotting for TSG101, CD9, CD63, and CD81 (antibodies: Abcam, UK; 1:1000 dilution). Protein quantification: BCA assay (Pierce™, Thermo Fisher, USA) with absorbance at 562 nm (SpectraMax® iD3, Molecular Devices, USA).

Surgical procedures and experimental groups

Rats were anesthetized with 2% sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg, i.p.) and secured in a stereotaxic frame. A midline scalp incision was made to expose the bregma, lambda, and sagittal suture. A burr hole (0.24 mm posterior to bregma, lateral 2.5 mm) was drilled, and collagenase IV (1 µL, 0.2 µL/min) was microinjected into the right striatum (depth: 5.5 mm). Sham-operated rats received 1 µL saline without collagenase. Six hours post-ICH induction, sham and ICH groups were administered 200 µL saline, while ICH rats received in situ implantation of 3D biological scaffold @Normo-Exos (1 × 10¹⁰ particles/mL; 2 × 2 × 1 mm³) or 3D biological scaffold @Hypo-Exos (1 × 10¹⁰ particles/mL; 2 × 2 × 1 mm³). All protocols complied with NIH Guidelines for Animal Care and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Hebei Medical University (Approval No.: 2024-R260). The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. Rats were humanely euthanized by sodium pentobarbital overdose (150–200 mg/kg i.p.). Adequate anesthetic depth was confirmed by the absence of corneal and pedal reflexes prior to any terminal procedure.

Neurological deficit assessment

Neurological function was evaluated post-ICH on days 1, 4, and 7 using: Longa Scale: 0 (no deficit) to 4 (severe hemiplegia). Bederson Scale: 0 (normal) to 5 (no spontaneous movement). Sensorimotor tests, Forelimb Placement Test (FPT): Assessed forelimb adjustment to tactile stimuli. Corner Turn Test (CTT): Quantified directional bias in a 30° angled corridor.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Mitochondrial ultrastructure in peri-hematomal tissues was analyzed 5 days post-ICH. Brain samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin Sect. (70 nm) were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, then imaged using a Hitachi HT7800 TEM (Hitachi, Japan) at 80 kV.

H&E and Prussian blue staining

Paraffin Sect. (5 μm) were baked at 60 °C for 1 h, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through graded ethanol. H&E: Stained with hematoxylin (5 min) and eosin (30 s). Prussian Blue: Incubated with Perl’s reagent (15 min) to detect iron deposition. Sections were dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and imaged under a Leica DM200 microscope (Leica, Germany).

Brain water content

Brains were harvested 5 days post-ICH, divided into ipsilateral/contralateral hemispheres and cerebellum, and weighed (wet weight, WW). Tissues were dried at 100 °C for 24 h to obtain dry weight (DW). Edema was calculated as: Brain water content (%) = (WW − DW)/WW×100.

Fluoro-Jade C (FJC) staining

Degenerating neurons were labeled using an FJC kit (Cat. TR-100-FJ, Biosensis, Australia). Deparaffinized sections were treated with 0.06% KMnO₄ (15 min), incubated in FJC solution (1:500 in 0.1% acetic acid), and counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti, Japan) and quantified as FJC⁺ cells/mm² via ImageJ.

TUNEL assay

Apoptosis was detected using a TUNEL kit (Cat. 11684817910, Roche, Switzerland). Sections were permeabilized with proteinase K (20 µg/mL, 15 min), incubated with TUNEL reaction mix (37 °C, 1 h), and counterstained with DAPI. TUNEL⁺/DAPI⁺ ratios were quantified using ImageJ.

Oxidative stress markers

Superoxide dismutase (SOD), malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione (GSH) levels in peri-hematomal tissues were measured using commercial kits (SOD: ab73366; MDA: ab118970; GSH: ab108970; Abcam, USA). Absorbance was read at 450 nm (SOD), 532 nm (MDA), and 412 nm (GSH) using a SpectraMax® iD3 microplate reader.

Immunofluorescence

Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: Iba-1 (1:200, ab7260), Arg-1 (1:200, ab13556), and iNOS (1:200, ab307840; Abcam, UK). After PBS washes, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488- or 594-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:200, Abcam) for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (5 µg/mL, 15 min). Images were captured using a confocal microscope (LSM 880, Zeiss, Germany).

Isolation of primary astrocytes

Neonatal rats (postnatal day 1–3) were anesthetized with isoflurane (5% induction, 2% maintenance) and decapitated under sterile conditions. Brains were rapidly excised and immersed in ice-cold Dulbecco’s Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (D-HBSS; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Under a dissecting microscope (Leica M80, Germany), the cerebellum, brainstem, and hippocampus were meticulously removed. The cerebral cortex was isolated by stripping the pia mater and vascular tissues using fine forceps. Cortical tissues were minced into 1 mm³ fragments and digested in 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco) containing 10 µg/mL DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 5 min with gentle agitation. The enzymatic reaction was quenched with DMEM/F12 medium (HyClone, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco). The cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm nylon mesh (Falcon, USA), centrifuged (1000 × g, 4 °C, 2 min), and resuspended in astrocyte culture medium (DMEM/F12 + 10% FBS + 1% GlutaMAX). Cells were seeded into poly-L-lysine-coated flasks and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Medium was replaced after 24 h to remove non-adherent cells.

Exosome tracking in 3D biological scaffold @exos

Astrocytes were incubated with PKH26-labeled 3D biological scaffold @exos (10 µg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) for 12 h at 37 °C. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (10 min), and blocked with 5% BSA (1 h). Actin filaments were stained with Alexa Fluor 488-phalloidin (1:200; Invitrogen, USA) for 60 min in the dark. After PBS washes, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 µg/mL, 10 min). Fluorescent images were acquired using a confocal microscope (LSM 880, Zeiss, Germany) and analyzed with ZEN 3.0 software (Zeiss). Exosome internalization was quantified as PKH26 puncta per cell using ImageJ (NIH, USA).

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection

Intracellular ROS levels were quantified using a DCFH-DA assay kit (Cat. D6883, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Cells were incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA and 1 µg/mL DAPI in serum-free medium at 37 °C (5% CO₂) for 30 min. After PBS washes, fluorescence signals (Ex/Em: 488/525 nm for DCF; 358/461 nm for DAPI) were captured using a fluorescence microscope (DMI3000B, Leica, Germany). ROS intensity was quantified as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) per cell using ImageJ (NIH, USA).

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) assessment

Mitochondrial depolarization was evaluated using JC-1 staining (Cat. T4069, Sigma-Aldrich). Cells were incubated with 5 µM JC-1 at 37 °C (5% CO₂) for 30 min. Fluorescence was measured at 488 nm (green, monomeric form) and 594 nm (red, aggregated form). The red/green fluorescence ratio was calculated using a fluorescence microplate reader (SpectraMax® iD3, Molecular Devices, USA), with decreased ratios indicating loss of ΔΨm.

Apoptosis analysis by flow cytometry

Cell apoptosis was assessed using Annexin V-FITC/PI staining (Cat. 556547, BD Biosciences, USA). Cells were washed with ice-cold PBS, resuspended in binding buffer, and incubated with 0.5 µg/mL Annexin V-FITC and 50 µg/mL PI for 15 min in the dark. Samples were analyzed within 1 h using a BD FACSAria™ III flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Data were processed with FlowJo V10 software (BD Life Sciences), with apoptotic cells defined as Annexin V⁺/PI⁻ (early apoptosis) and Annexin V⁺/PI⁺ (late apoptosis).

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The miR-146b-5p binding site in the COP1 3’UTR was predicted using TargetScan 7.2. Wild-type (WT) and mutant (MUT) COP1 3’UTR sequences were cloned into the pmirGLO vector (Cat. E1330, Promega, USA). 293T cells were co-transfected with: WT or MUT reporter plasmid (100 ng), miR-146b-5p mimics (50 nM) or negative control (miR-NC), Renilla luciferase plasmid (pRL-TK, 10 ng; internal control), using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). Luciferase activity was measured 48 h post-transfection using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter System (Cat. E1910, Promega). Firefly/Renilla luciferase ratios were normalized to miR-NC groups.

Western blotting

Nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were extracted using a Minute™ Subcellular Fractionation Kit (Cat. SC-003, Invent, USA). Tissue lysates were prepared in RIPA buffer (Cat. P0013B, Beyotime, China) with protease/phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined via BCA assay (Cat. 23225, Thermo Fisher). Equal amounts (30 µg/lane) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Cat. IPFL00010, Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 2 h, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: COP1 (1:1000, ab175186, Abcam), TLR4 (1:1000, ab13556, Abcam), p-p65 (1:1000, ab181602, Abcam), p65 (1:1000, ab92946, Abcam), GAPDH (1:5000, ab8227, Abcam). After TBST washes, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000, Cat. ab6721, Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using an Odyssey CLx Imaging System (Li-COR, USA) and quantified via Image Lab™ 6.0 (Bio-Rad, USA).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol® (Cat. 15596026, Invitrogen) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA with a PrimeScript™ RT reagent kit (Cat. RR047A, Takara, Japan). RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Premix Ex Taq™ II (Cat. RR820A, Takara) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex system (Applied Biosystems, USA). Primer sequences: miR-146b-5p: 5′-UGAGAACUGAAUUCCAUAGGCU-3′ (forward) Universal reverse primer (Takara). COP1: F: 5′-CAGGTGCTGAAGGAGAAGGT-3′; R: 5′-TGGCATCCACTTCACCTTCT-3′. GAPDH: F: 5′-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3′; R: 5′-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3′. Relative expression was calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method with GAPDH normalization.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Neurological scores were analyzed by Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc correction. Continuous variables were compared via one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test (GraphPad Prism 9.0). Significance: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Results

Characterization of hUCMSCs and EVs

Flow cytometry confirmed that isolated hUCMSCs expressed mesenchymal stem cell markers (CD73, CD90, CD105; >95% positivity) but lacked hematopoietic markers (CD34, CD45; <2%) (Figure S1A). Hypoxia-primed EVs (Hypo-Exos) were harvested from hUCMSCs cultured under hypoxic conditions (2% O₂, 5% CO₂) in exosome-depleted FBS medium for 48 h. TEM revealed the canonical cup-shaped morphology of both normoxia-derived EVs (Normo-Exos) and Hypo-Exos (Figure S1B). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) demonstrated a monodisperse size distribution (30–150 nm, peak at 110 nm) for both exosome types (Figure S1C). BCA assays confirmed comparable protein content between groups (Figure S1D), while Western blotting validated exosomal markers (CD9, CD63, CD81, TSG101) (Figure S1E), confirming their suitability for downstream applications.

Fabrication and characterization of 3D biological scaffold @exos

The 3D biological scaffold was fabricated from brain dECM (Fig. 1A). DNA quantification revealed that > 99% of residual DNA was removed from the dECM (Fig. 1B), while collagen content in the dECM exceeded that of native brain tissue (Fig. 1C). Histological analyses—H&E, Masson’s trichrome, and Sirius red staining—confirmed complete decellularization and preservation of collagen architecture (Fig. 1D). Lyophilized scaffolds exhibited a porous three-dimensional microstructure, with Normo-Exos and Hypo-Exos uniformly anchored to the matrix (white arrows, Fig. 1E).

FTIR spectroscopy identified characteristic peaks corresponding to C-N (1047 cm⁻¹) and NH₂ (1290 cm⁻¹), indicative of positively charged functional groups within the scaffold (Fig. 1F). Zeta potential measurements confirmed strong electrostatic interactions between EVs (− 32.7 mV) and the scaffold surface, ensuring stable binding (Fig. 1G).

The scaffold exhibited shear-thinning behavior (Fig. 2A), with viscosity decreasing as shear rate increased, confirming injectability. Rheological profiling demonstrated enhanced elastic modulus (G′) at physiological temperature (37 °C vs. 4 °C; Fig. 2B). Across all frequencies (0.1–20 Hz), G′ consistently exceeded viscous modulus (G″), confirming solid-like viscoelasticity (Fig. 2C and D). At 1% strain and 1 Hz angular frequency, G′ reached ~ 800 Pa, while G″ stabilized at ~ 500 Pa, ensuring mechanical stability during tissue remodeling (Fig. 2E).

Given the prolonged healing timeline of ICH, the scaffold’s ROS-responsive degradation was systematically evaluated. Scaffold mass decreased proportionally to H₂O₂ concentration (50–500 µM), with 100 µM H₂O₂ (mimicking the ICH microenvironment) triggering controlled exosome release (Fig. 2F). Additionally, the scaffold demonstrated rapid H₂O₂ scavenging, eliminating 1 mM H₂O₂ within 2 h (absorbance at 350 nm; Fig. 2G). This dual functionality—sustained exosome delivery and ROS neutralization—effectively mitigates oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, offering a synergistic therapeutic strategy for ICH.

In vivo evaluation of 3D biological scaffold @exos

To assess potential cytotoxicity and structural integrity, live/dead assays revealed high viability (>90%) of hUCMSCs encapsulated within the scaffold at day 1, with sustained survival rates (>85%) observed at day 14 (Fig. 3A and D). The scaffold maintained its original 3D porous architecture prior to degradation, providing a biomimetic microenvironment for cell proliferation and extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition (consistent with prior studies [29]). Notably, scaffold-embedded hUCMSCs secreted endogenous matrix proteins, facilitating gradual tissue remodeling even after scaffold dissolution.

Longitudinal tracking of luciferase-labeled scaffolds demonstrated progressive degradation in vivo, with near-complete absorption by day 14 (Fig. 3B C), confirming favorable biodegradation kinetics.

The scaffold@exos significantly enhanced peri-hematomal vascular regeneration, as evidenced by restored distal blood perfusion (laser speckle contrast imaging; Fig. 4A and B). Hypo-Exos-loaded scaffolds exhibited superior neovascularization compared to Normo-Exos groups, suggesting a synergistic interplay between the 3D matrix and hypoxia-primed EVs in promoting angiogenesis. This aligns with established mechanisms wherein exosome-laden scaffolds recruit endothelial progenitor cells and upregulate VEGF signaling [25, 26]. Discussion

ICH, a devastating form of non-traumatic stroke, is associated with high mortality and disability rates worldwide. Despite advances in surgical hematoma evacuation, the prognosis remains poor due to unresolved primary and secondary brain injuries, including neuroinflammation, blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption, and neuronal apoptosis, which prolong recovery and impose significant socioeconomic burdens [35–37]. Current monotherapies exhibit limited efficacy, necessitating innovative strategies such as combining mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs (MSC-exos) with 3D tissue-engineered scaffolds to address ICH’s multifactorial pathology [38].

In this study, we developed a ROS-responsive 3D biological scaffold system that integrates hypoxia-primed EVs (Hypo-Exos) for dual-targeted therapy against neuroinflammation and oxidative stress in ICH. The scaffold, fabricated via PBA-crosslinked PVA and advanced 3D bioprinting, leverages dynamic boronate ester bonds to enable on-demand Hypo-Exos release under pathological ROS levels (H₂O₂ >100 µM) while scavenging excess ROS. This dual functionality—sustained exosome delivery and antioxidative activity—addresses two interconnected ICH pathologies: neuroinflammatory cascades driven by microglial activation and ROS-mediated neuronal apoptosis [41].

The scaffold’s ROS-responsive degradation aligns with prior applications of PBA-based systems in ischemic and inflammatory environments [39]. Unlike passive drug carriers, our platform actively neutralizes ROS via boronate oxidation, a feature rarely combined in existing biomaterials. Hypo-Exos, enriched with anti-inflammatory miRNAs (e.g., miR-146b) and antioxidant enzymes (e.g., SOD2, catalase) [41], are released in synchrony with the ROS surge (24–72 h post-ICH) to ensure precise intervention. In vitro, Hypo-Exos suppressed LPS-induced proinflammatory cytokine release and reduced ROS in activated microglia, consistent with their role in promoting M2 macrophage polarization [42]. The ROS-responsive 3D biological scaffold alone exhibited mild anti-inflammatory effects (e.g., reduced edema and oxidative stress markers in early timepoints). However, these effects were significantly less pronounced compared to the combination of scaffold and Hypo-Exos. Based on the Normo-Exos group data (which showed moderate improvement over untreated controls), we infer that the scaffold likely contributes to baseline structural support and partial ROS scavenging, while the EVs provide additional anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective benefits.

Current ICH therapies, such as surgical evacuation or corticosteroids, often fail to target molecular mechanisms and may exacerbate secondary injury [43]. Our system offers spatiotemporal control unachievable with systemic administration. The sustained Hypo-Exos release (>14 days) ensures prolonged neuroprotection, while scaffold biodegradability (~ 8 weeks) minimizes chronic foreign body reactions. The 3D-printed architecture mimics native brain extracellular matrix (pore size: 50–100 μm; stiffness: ~2 kPa), facilitating host cell infiltration and vascular integration—critical factors for tissue repair often overlooked in injectable hydrogels [44]. Quantified by electron spin resonance (ESR), the scaffold scavenged 70% of hydroxyl radicals within 6 h, potentially disrupting ROS/NF-κB-driven inflammatory feedback loops [45]. Release kinetics followed a first-order model (R² = 0.96), achieving 80% cumulative Hypo-Exos release at 500 µM H₂O₂ (pathologically relevant to ICH [46]), contrasting sharply with burst-release passive systems [47].

Regenerative therapies using MSC-derived EVs (MSC-EVs/ EVs) have shown promise in neurological disorders, including ICH [48–50]. MSC-EVs/ EVs, initially regarded as cellular waste, are now recognized for their roles in intercellular communication, immunomodulation, and tissue repair [51]. Hypoxic preconditioning enhances MSC-exosome bioactivity by enriching therapeutic cargo (e.g., miR-146b, miR-216a-5p) that modulates microglial polarization and promotes neuroprotection [52–55]. Our findings corroborate that Hypo-Exos outperform Normo-Exos in mitigating neuroinflammation, reducing apoptosis, and enhancing neuronal survival. Notably, Hypo-Exos upregulated miR-146b, which targets COP1—an E3 ubiquitin ligase regulating TLR4/NF-κB signaling—to suppress neuroinflammatory cascades. Dual-luciferase assays confirmed miR-146b-5p binding to the COP1 3’UTR, while COP1 overexpression reversed Hypo-Exos’ therapeutic effects, validating this axis [56].

Despite these advances, challenges remain in scaling MSC-exosome production, ensuring batch consistency, and improving target specificity [57–59]. Hypoxic preconditioning and 3D culture systems represent promising strategies to enhance exosome yield and potency [60–62]. Our scaffold-mediated delivery system prolongs Hypo-Exos retention and sustains release at injury sites, addressing rapid clearance issues in conventional exosome therapies [63]. However, translating these findings to clinical practice requires addressing interspecies differences in ICH models, optimizing hypoxia protocols for MSC viability, and evaluating long-term biosafety of engineered EVs. This study has several limitations: (1) The experimental findings are derived from an SD rat model, and species-specific differences in ICH pathophysiology necessitate validation in large animal models (e.g., porcine or non-human primates) to confirm translational relevance. (2) While the 14-day observation period effectively captured acute-phase therapeutic outcomes, it precludes evaluation of long-term neurofunctional remodeling and potential delayed complications. (3) Although no overt immune rejection was observed in SD rats, the clinical applicability of the scaffold (containing xenogeneic dECM components) and human-derived EVs requires systematic biocompatibility assessments, including analyses of macrophage polarization and complement activation. Future studies will extend follow-up to 8 weeks and employ syngeneic EVs (e.g., rat MSC-derived) combined with immunohistochemical profiling to clarify translational potential.

In conclusion, our ROS-responsive 3D scaffold loaded with Hypo-Exos represents a transformative approach for ICH management, synergistically targeting neuroinflammation and oxidative stress. Future work should explore clinical-grade scaffold fabrication, exosome engineering for enhanced targeting, and validation in larger animal models to bridge the translational gap.

Conclusion

In summary, our ROS-responsive 3D biological scaffold @exos system represents a paradigm shift in ICH therapeutics by synergistically integrating biomaterial engineering with exosome biology. By synchronizing Hypo-Exos delivery with pathological oxidative stress, this platform achieves spatiotemporally controlled neuroprotection and inflammation resolution. The scaffold’s dual functionality—dynamic exosome release and ROS scavenging—addresses the dual challenges of neuroinflammation and oxidative damage in ICH, offering a precision medicine approach. These findings not only advance the design of smart biomaterials for neurological disorders but also underscore the transformative potential of exosome-functionalized scaffolds in regenerative medicine, bridging the gap between targeted molecular therapy and structural tissue repair.
