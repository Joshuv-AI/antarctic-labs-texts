Strategies to address implementation barriers in sepsis survivors’ care transitions to home health and outpatient care: a multi-site, type 1 hybrid implementation study
Authors: Kathryn H. Bowles, Karen B. Hirschman, Michael A. Stawnychy, Melissa O’Connor, Mark E. Mikkelsen, Sang Bin You, Elaine Sang, Sungho Oh, Katherine Pitcher, Brittany Newman, Charlotte Weiss, Chaerin Lee, Nancy Hodgson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTimely attention by home health care (HHC) and outpatient providers within one week after hospital discharge is associated with lower readmission rates among sepsis survivors. However, few patients nationwide receive this pattern of care. Improving care transitions for sepsis survivors is inhibited by barriers in sepsis identification, information transfer, care coordination, timely access to care, and patient engagement. The I-TRANSFER study was designed to address these gaps by implementing timely delivery of post-acute care (PAC) for sepsis survivors in real-world health care settings. The study aimed to identify, describe, and map the implementation strategies used to implement the I-TRANSFER care transition protocol.MethodsFive diverse health systems (16 hospitals), partnered with an affiliated HHC agency, participated in a Type 1 hybrid implementation science study. Through thematic inductive and deductive qualitative analysis, implementation determinants—barriers and facilitators—were identified, summarized, and presented back to the sites’ implementation teams. This paper reports on implementation strategies used to address barriers and implement the I-TRANSFER care transition protocol. Strategies are mapped to the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) taxonomy.ResultsImplementors and researchers deployed strategies spanning 62% of the 73 ERIC categories, resulting in hundreds of detailed examples. Strategies aimed at identifying sepsis and referring survivors to HHC emphasized relaying clinical data to providers; adjusting the EHR; and educating staff to improve sepsis recognition, documentation, and referral to HHC. Strategies aimed at information transfer and making outpatient appointments emphasized EHR-based tools, telemedicine workflows, and centralized scheduling to ensure communication to PAC and timely outpatient appointments. Providing timely HHC and outpatient visits required staff and patient education, tailored HHC pathways, patient-focused interventions, and quality monitoring. Cross-cutting strategies reinforced coordination and education across the full continuum of care. Heat maps illustrate which strategies were used most often across objectives and by hospital/HHC dyad.ConclusionsThis study identified, described, and mapped to the ERIC taxonomy more than 450 implementation strategies used by hospital-HHC dyads to implement the I-TRANSFER care transition protocol, providing practical, translatable strategies to support timely post-acute care transitions for sepsis survivors. By identifying the barriers in care transitions, and reporting the strategies to address them, these findings can inform broader efforts to deliver timely, coordinated care and improve outcomes for vulnerable populations across settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15118-5.

Background

Significant barriers exist to achieving high-quality, effective care transitions [1, 2]. Readmission rates remain high, especially for those with chronic and serious illnesses, and challenges with delayed or fragmented care call for new insights supporting continuous improvement [3–5]. Care transitions for sepsis survivors are particularly challenging because it is difficult to: identify sepsis in the acute care setting; document and maintain sepsis as a problem within the electronic health record (EHR); communicate sepsis survivorship to post-acute settings; and activate timely and close post-discharge surveillance [2]. Thirty-day readmission rates for sepsis survivors range between 17.6% and 25.4% [6]; median time to readmission is 11 days [7] and sequelae of sepsis can last for years [8]. Among readmissions from home health care (HHC), 32% occur in the first week [9]. Timely attention by home health care (HHC) and outpatient providers in the first week after hospital discharge was associated with 7% lower readmission rates in an observational study [10]. However, only 28% of sepsis survivors nationwide received this timely pattern of care [9]. The I-TRANSFER study was designed to address these gaps by implementing timely delivery of post-acute care (PAC) for sepsis survivors in real-world health care settings and to share the strategies used during the implementation.

Methods

Study design

Our study, Improving Transitions and Outcomes for Sepsis Survivors (I-TRANSFER) is a type 1 hybrid implementation science study using mixed methods to test the effectiveness (quantitative) and study the implementation (qualitative) of a timely HHC and outpatient protocol in the real world [11]. In this paper we report on the qualitative aim which focused on the I-TRANSFER implementation.

Implementation is the process of putting evidence-based interventions to use in clinical practice [12]. This paper reports the implementation strategies mapped to the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) taxonomy [13] that were existing, and/or developed and executed at the participating sites to support implementation and address barriers during implementation of the I-TRANSFER care transition protocol.

Setting and sample

Implementation sites included five health systems (16 hospitals) each of which partnered with one HHC agency (5 dyads). The five dyads were located across four states in the Northeast, Mountain, and Western regions of the United States. Sites were recruited by the Principal Investigator based on size, location, variation in ownership and profit status, and willingness to participate in a study to improve care for sepsis patients. Implementation team members included hospital, HHC, and outpatient administrators and providers; medical coders; quality or information technology staff; and clinicians and care coordinators involved in sepsis care or care transitions. Human ethics and consent to participate declaration

The study was approved in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki by three Institutional Review Boards (University of Pennsylvania #843958, Lancaster General Hospital #843958, and VNS Health #1736370) and all participants provided verbal informed consent to participate and be recorded. Participation was voluntary, with assurances of confidentiality and all data were de-identified.

Approach

The study started with a needs assessment conducted through qualitative interviews with implementation team members. The needs assessment was guided by the widely used Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) to identify the barriers and facilitators affecting implementation across multiple organizational levels [14]. Barriers and facilitators are implementation determinants that hinder or enable the implementation of an evidence-based practice [15]. The interview guide [11] and results of the needs assessment are published elsewhere [2].

For the planning and implementation phases of the study reported here, we used the ERIC taxonomy to systematically label the concrete implementation strategies used to address the determinants [13]. Strategies are concrete activities taken to implement and sustain evidence into practice [16, 17]. The ERIC taxonomy is a compilation of 73 discrete implementation strategies with definitions based upon a review of the literature [18] and further validated by implementation science experts [19]. While CFIR provided a comprehensive lens for understanding what barriers and facilitators affected implementation, ERIC offered a standardized nomenclature for describing how sites addressed these determinants through specific strategies. This dual-framework approach aligns with recommendations for combining determinant frameworks with strategy taxonomies in implementation research [20, 21].

Data collection

During the planning and implementation phases of the study, the themes and examples of barriers and facilitators derived from data collected in the needs assessment were presented to the implementation team to stimulate discussion and support implementation mapping [22]. The implementation mapping process used for developing strategies is described below. Audio- and video-recorded meetings between the research and implementation teams were used to systematically track and promote progress and address emerging issues.

Throughout the study phases, trained research assistants and investigators attended the implementation meetings, listened to meeting recordings, reviewed transcripts, and summarized each meeting, noting the strategies, level of progress, and any barriers to address before the next meeting. The team created implementation tracking sheets and documented the date of the meeting, the people involved, and a description of the strategies, similar to methods used by Bunger and colleagues [23]. Recordings and transcripts were iteratively reviewed during data preparation as needed to extract strategies.

Data preparation, implementation strategy mapping, and rigor

Research team members KHB, MAS, ES, MOC, SBY, BN, and SO, were highly familiar with the context of each implementation site due to their participation in the needs assessment interviews and analyses, planning, and implementation site meetings. Team members marked the strategies as existing (prior to implementation), accomplished (developed and executed) or abandoned, and mapped each site’s strategies to the ERIC taxonomy. During the mapping process, we also noted which of the 8 I-TRANSFER objectives and determinant themes, identified during the needs assessment [2], were addressed by the strategies. During implementation mapping, the ERIC strategy definitions were referenced frequently and clarifications or mapping rules made by the team were documented for reference, serving as data dictionaries and a procedure manual. Through team review of a random 20% sample, we achieved 100% consensus on ERIC strategy mapping. Results

The research and implementation teams participated in over 50 planning meetings, with a mean of 7 per site (range = 4,18 depending on the size of the health system). There were more than 145 implementation meetings, with an average of 21 (range = 15,32 per site). Implementation strategies were provided either by the research team or by the sites’ implementation teams. Combining the research team and the implementation team strategies, the teams executed strategies from 45 of the 73 unique ERIC strategies available (62%).

Strategies implemented by the sites

ERIC strategies executed by the implementation teams at the study sites are organized by I-TRANSFER objectives and reported below in italics. Across the 5 dyads and 8 objectives, the implementation teams executed strategies within 27 of the 73 ERIC categories (37%), accomplished through over 450 examples. The text below provides several examples of strategies used for each objective. Table 2 summarizes ERIC strategies by objective and barriers addressed. Supplemental Table 1 provides details of the strategies.

Objective 1 focused on identifying and documenting sepsis diagnoses and included 7 ERIC strategies represented by 19 examples. A central strategy for this objective was Facilitate Relay of Clinical Data to Providers, which addressed barriers related to the electronic health record (EHR) and acute care processes regarding documentation. Teams implemented sepsis alert algorithms within the EHR to detect signs and symptoms of sepsis beginning in the emergency department (ED), followed by best-practice alerts, banners, or flags to alert clinicians of sepsis and trigger clinical pathways, whiteboards, and checklists to guide care.

At two sites, teams engaged virtual monitoring units—teams of nurses that monitor remotely for signs of sepsis—reflecting the ERIC strategy Promote Network Weaving by leveraging resources in other departments. Teams also employed the ERIC strategy Change Record Systems by implementing sepsis order sets and dot phrases or smart phrases to support treatment and documentation of sepsis, referral to HHC, and communication of sepsis sequelae in the EHR. One site developed a dashboard to monitor compliance with sepsis core measures, including timely blood culture collection and lactate measurement. Develop Educational Materials and Conduct Educational Meetings supported staff training and uptake for better sepsis identification. One site implemented Mandate Change by adopting Sepsis-3 criteria to define sepsis and requiring system-wide adoption of a sepsis recognition tool.

Objectives 2 and 3 focused on identifying and referring sepsis patients for HHC and included 11 ERIC strategies represented by 42 site-specific examples. These strategies targeted barriers related to care coordination, as well as staff and patient education regarding the value of timely HHC and outpatient care. Patient-focused education included bedside teaching, sepsis resource guides, stoplight tools (available in English and Spanish), letters about sepsis and post-acute care, and templated discharge instructions from the EHR. Teams Conducted Educational Meetings for staff across inpatient, HHC, and outpatient settings on sepsis recognition, I-TRANSFER workflows, documentation expectations, and the importance of timely follow-up care.

To support cross-sector collaboration, teams employed Promote Network Weaving by engaging home health liaisons, ambulatory care coordinators, intake staff, and sepsis coordinators. Teams also used Develop and Implement Tools for Quality Monitoring to track rates of (1) sepsis discharges eligible for HHC (2), referral offers (3), referrals executed, and (4) patient refusal of services; teams used these data iteratively to guide improvement efforts.

Teams deployed Change Record Systems to create EHR flags, smart phrases, and “sticky notes” that alerted clinical teams of patients’ eligibility for HHC referral. Dedicated sepsis coordinators played a key role in interdisciplinary bedside rounds by advocating for sepsis survivors, reflecting the ERIC strategy Revise Professional Roles. Staff also Intervened with Patients to Enhance the Uptake and Adherence of timely care by providing education, encouragement, and care coordination. HHC intake supervisors contacted patients by phone who initially refused HHC to educate them about its benefits. Finally, implementation teams Tailored Strategies by designating hospitalists or a transitional care nurse practitioner to sign HHC orders for patients without established outpatient providers.

Objective 4 focused on transferring clinical information to the next level of care and included 10 ERIC strategies represented by 28 examples. This objective was critical to activating the I-TRANSFER visit pattern by ensuring that HHC and outpatient providers were informed that a patient was a sepsis survivor. Prior work by our team [9] and findings from the needs assessment [2, 24] demonstrated that sepsis was often not clearly documented in the medical record or care transition documents.

The strategies mapped to Change Record Systems were helpful to Facilitate Relay of Clinical Data to Providers regarding how and where sepsis survivorship would be communicated in the medical record. Common documentation targets included progress notes, discharge summaries, referral and care coordination notes, after-visit summaries, and home care intake documents. Strategies to improve documentation included the use of EHR flags, dot phrases, transfer reports, and templated notes. Teams used Audit and Provide Feedback to monitor utilization rates and assess documentation quality over time. For example, they compared inpatient lists of sepsis survivors with HHC referrals and tracked whether sepsis was documented in referral materials and the HHC record. In addition, the research team implemented a strategy to improve recognition of sepsis survivorship during care transitions and post-acute care, which resulted in a successful application for a new ICD-10 code for sepsis survivors [25]. The code Z51.A, Encounter for Sepsis Aftercare, is now available for use in discharge summaries, referral documentation, and post-acute records.

Objective 5 called for scheduling an outpatient follow-up appointment to occur within seven days of hospital discharge and included 10 ERIC strategies represented by 27 examples. Teams implemented these strategies to address barriers related to access to care, staffing, and scheduling. Several sites employed Revise Professional Roles or Tailor Strategies by assigning responsibility for appointment scheduling to existing staff, including unit clerks, sepsis coordinators, and inpatient and outpatient care coordinators. Telemedicine visits were offered when in-person appointments were not feasible. Two teams used Centralized Technical Assistance through centralized scheduling systems to facilitate timely appointment booking.

To promote appointment availability, the strategies Facilitation and Conduct Educational Meetings increased outpatient providers’ awareness of the need to offer timely follow-up appointments. Education also targeted patients and caregivers to emphasize the importance of scheduling and attending the follow-up visit. Teams used Change Record Systems and Facilitate Relay of Clinical Data to Providers to support scheduling by flagging sepsis survivors in the medical record and triggering medical orders for urgent outpatient referrals.

The teams further employed Promote Network Weaving by leveraging an existing hospital-at-home program to expand service to sepsis survivors. At one site, additional staff were hired to make outpatient appointments. Teams monitored appointment scheduling and visit completion using Audit and Provide Feedback.

Objectives 6 and 7 occurred in the HHC setting and had 8 ERIC strategies represented by 26 examples. To achieve timely initiation of HHC, sites adopted Conduct Educational Meetings to train HHC staff. Education emphasized the I-TRANSFER definition of timely HHC initiation as occurring within two days of hospital discharge, rather than within two days of a signed physician-ordered HHC referral, which could be received several days later.

Teams introduced new approaches to Facilitate Relay of Clinical Data to Providers, such as secure messaging platforms, patient flagging within secure chat, and HHC EHR-based indicators of the sepsis diagnosis (care type flag). Over time, teams used Remind Clinicians to reinforce protocol adherence, while Develop and Implement Tools for Quality Monitoring enabled tracking of visit timing for sepsis patients. Case reviews and root cause analyses, mapped to the strategy Audit and Provide Feedback, supported continuous improvement. Intervene with Patients to Enhance Uptake and Adherence occurred by sending patients home with letters about sepsis, reinforced that their monitoring and recovery will continue, and emphasized the importance of timely follow-up care. Care coordinators made post-discharge phone calls to assess patients’ conditions and confirm that post-acute care follow-up had started.

For Objective 8 teams implemented 5 ERIC strategies represented by 13 examples to achieve outpatient-appointment scheduling. Intervene with Patients/Consumers to Enhance Uptake and Adherence and Revise Professional Roles identified transportation-related barriers, and one site offered home health aides as escorts to follow-up appointments. Teams used Promote Network Weaving to address transportation challenges and offer alternative models of outpatient care, including telemedicine visits, home-visiting providers, and visits by transitional care nurse practitioners.

Discussion

The implementation of I-TRANSFER, a protocol requiring identification and referral of sepsis survivors for early attention in HHC and outpatient care, was complex and required multiple and diverse strategies. Over 450 detailed strategies are mapped to the ERIC taxonomy. By providing the details of the strategies used in the implementation of I-TRANSFER, readers may consider the feasibility, importance, and compatibility of these strategies in their sites when implementing similar initiatives. Strategies were largely targeted at barriers to optimal sepsis care identified by our needs assessment [2] and closely parallel findings from a systematic review, which synthesized 50 studies from 14 countries and reported inpatient barriers as difficulty recognizing sepsis, poor communication, and lack of knowledge and experience [4]. Additionally, 9 studies from this review identified additional barriers to high-quality sepsis care transitions, such as poor information flow, fragmented care coordination, and limited access to outpatient care [4].

The complexity of implementing care transition protocols such as I-TRANSFER is reflected in the breadth of strategies required to address implementation barriers. Combining the strategies implemented by the sites and the research team, 62% of the recommended ERIC strategies were implemented in multiple and context-specific ways. This is comparable to a systematic review of 20 care transition interventions that identified 40 distinct strategies [26]. Despite not using the ERIC taxonomy, these studies report similar strategies, such as education and training, EHR-enabled communication, facilitation, stakeholder engagement, role revision, coalition building, tailoring, and audit and feedback [26].

The 2025 update of the German S3 Sepsis Guideline [27], and the 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign: International Guidelines [28] explicitly acknowledge that sepsis care extends beyond hospital discharge, emphasizing education for patients and families, care transitions, discharge planning, and structured post-acute management. New recommendations require clearer discharge documentation, including explicit reference to the risk of post-intensive care syndrome (PICS), and highlight the importance of timely communication with patients, families, and outpatient providers. By embedding follow-up care and survivorship considerations into standard sepsis management, guidelines begin to address gaps in care coordination, information transfer, and continuity of care identified in our analysis. Together, these changes support our assumptions that improving discharge documentation and communication and coordinating timely post-discharge care are critical to reducing fragmentation and improving long-term outcomes after sepsis. Our success in obtaining a new ICD-10 code for sepsis survivors is a significant advancement, ensuring that sepsis survivorship is linked to the index hospitalization and remains visible throughout post-acute care [2, 24]. Broad adoption of this code across health systems is essential to improve healthcare delivery through the recognition of sepsis survivorship, support care coordination during transitions, and enable more accurate tracking of outcomes in research and clinical practice.

Waltz and colleagues [19] used concept mapping with 35 implementation science experts to sort the 73 ERIC implementation strategies into 9 categories and rate them for importance and feasibility. Placing the I-TRANSFER implementation in the context of Waltz’s categories, our most common strategies fall into quadrants I and II, that experts identified as most important and feasible for implementing evidence-based interventions. In quadrant I, there are 28 strategies; I-TRANSFER implementation teams applied 25 of them. One heavily used strategy across all sites was Conduct Educational Meetings, which falls in quadrant II, between “moderately and very important” and “moderately and very feasible” [19]. In our study, educating clinicians and, in particular, patients and their caregivers about the importance of timely HHC and outpatient care was paramount for implementation success. Barriers such as incomplete HHC referrals [29], delaying or refusing home healthcare [30, 31], or difficulty making or attending the outpatient appointment [32, 33] are commonly reported by others and were experienced in our study. Barriers to receiving timely post-acute attention are detailed by Sang [34] and Ruhnke [35]. Interventions are necessary to remind, educate, assist, or physically accompany patients to ensure timely follow-up.

One of the most common strategies for I-TRANSFER was identified as highly important and feasible by Waltz and colleagues [19]-- Develop and Implement Tools for Quality Monitoring. Contrasted with a similarly named strategy, Develop and Organize Quality Monitoring Systems is notable. The former refers to creating specific monitoring instruments such as dashboards and tracking reports, while the latter refers to establishing broader organizational infrastructure. Sites predominantly developed discrete monitoring tools rather than building new systems, likely because quality monitoring infrastructure already existed and required adaptation rather than creation. These strategies reflect how dyads engaged in continuous quality improvement throughout the implementation. Prior to implementation, few sites monitored HHC referrals or timely care as defined by I-TRANSFER. Being able to monitor their progress and success helped us identify areas for improvement and celebrate successes.

Given the heterogeneity of the organizations participating in the I-TRANSFER implementation, implementation teams used a variety of strategies to address each objective. Across the sites, the EHR was interestingly both a common barrier and a facilitator for several I-TRANSFER study objectives [24]. Each site tailored strategies to match available resources and potential. For example, one site with a robust health information technology (IT) team could quickly make changes to the EHR, while another site had to place a request in a queue of all IT tickets for the entire healthcare system. Sites with limited resources were creative in leveraging existing reports or datasets to obtain information on sepsis survivors and monitor the success of implementation. These Change Record System strategies optimized the use of the EHR algorithms, alerts, reminders, and notes to identify sepsis, flag it on the record, and communicate it throughout the transition. They were very important for promoting the I-TRANSFER objectives because clinicians relied heavily on the EHR for information, decision support, and communication, and sepsis identification triggered cascades of actions to meet the study objectives. Waltz’s [19] mapping rated Change Record Systems as somewhat to moderately important and somewhat feasible. For our implementation, making changes to the EHR was critical, but feasibility varied at each site depending on motivation, resources, ability, and time.

Identifying sepsis patients for HHC referral was an important component of I-TRANSFER. HHC referral rates using national Medicare data were reported at only 13.1% with 39.5% of sepsis survivors going home to self- or family-care. Given that 75% of sepsis survivors develop new cognitive, functional, or mental health issues [36], 30-day readmission rates are 17.6% to 25.4% [6], and 32% of sepsis readmissions from HHC occur in the first week [9], we advocate for care transition interventions like I-TRANSFER that draw attention to the needs of sepsis survivors and get them the timely attention needed to improve outcomes.

I-TRANSFER is a complex intervention requiring care coordination across three care settings and involvement of multiple stakeholders and processes. As recommended by others [37, 38] we focused on function versus form during the implementation. We encouraged the sites to achieve the 8 objectives as the functions of I-TRANSFER while allowing flexibility in how they achieved it, or the form the strategies took because our sites were diverse with varied levels of resources, staffing, motivation, and know how to implement various strategies. Taking such an approach may increase the feasibility and scalability of the intervention.

Each site also entered the study with various levels of readiness, with HHC being significantly more ready than acute care [39]. Despite this readiness, strategy distribution differed between settings. Acute care objectives required more strategies related to documentation and communication, while HHC objectives showed greater concentration of quality monitoring and patient engagement strategies. This likely reflects that acute care settings already had existing quality monitoring systems in place (e.g., CMS sepsis indicators), while HHC needed to build new monitoring tools for sepsis survivors from scratch. Sites with sepsis committees, and/or sepsis or transitional care coordinators, had an advantage with many strategies already in place that were refreshed or optimized for the implementation such as improved identification and tracking of sepsis survivors, attending rounds to advocate for post-acute referrals, and monitoring patient education and readmissions. To improve scalability, our study reinforced the value of having dedicated personnel, such as sepsis or care coordinators or home health liaisons, available for continuity of care, quality improvement, and care coordination as reported by others [40–42]. Our implementation workgroups were multi-disciplinary and from different care settings. They worked well together through all phases of the implementation. Having integrated hospitals and home care agencies, and integrated EHRs were facilitators because participants knew each other, had shared missions, and understood each other’s roles. A scoping review of how interdisciplinary teams support care transitions found similar facilitators and strategies across multiple studies [43].

Our study demonstrates the diversity of strategies selected by sites, with many examples under each ERIC category and variations by dyad. The heatmaps illustrate which functions (objectives) may have been the most challenging to implement across different dyads. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as larger sites may have reported more strategies due to having more meetings or implementing different approaches across multiple hospitals. Also, complex interventions may require adaptations [44]. In our case, for example, allowing virtual outpatient visits via telemedicine was an adaptation in form that supported the function of achieving timely outpatient care.

This study was designed and reported in accordance with the STaRI (Standards for Reporting Implementation Studies) checklist, enhancing transparency and reproducibility. A key strength is the inclusion of five diverse health systems across four states, selected to capture variation in size, geographic setting, and organizational affiliation with home health care (HHC) agencies. The study engaged multidisciplinary participants with a range of roles and responsibilities, strengthening the relevance of findings to real-world clinical operations. High levels of site engagement were observed, reflected in consistent participation in implementation activities over the study period. In addition, participating sites were self-selected and motivated, which likely facilitated successful implementation.

The implementation strategies employed—such as patient and staff education, improvements in documentation and communication, and routine quality tracking—align closely with standard healthcare operational practices. This alignment enhances the potential for scalability, as these strategies are generally familiar, feasible, and adaptable across health systems. However, scalability may be constrained by contextual factors. For example, limited access to outpatient care, as observed in some sites, may pose a barrier to broader implementation in resource-constrained settings. Furthermore, variability in organizational resources, infrastructure, and engagement levels may influence the degree to which these strategies can be successfully replicated across different HHC agencies and health systems.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the generalizability of the findings may be limited because participants at the implementation sites were motivated early adopters and may not reflect the experiences of less-engaged organizations. Second, the research team played a substantial role in coordinating and facilitating implementation meetings; thus, it is unclear whether similar levels of effort would be achieved without this level of external support. This highlights the importance of establishing strong local leadership, such as dedicated site champions, to sustain implementation. Finally, reporting of implementation strategies and outcomes may have been influenced by differences in site size, the robustness of local tracking systems, and available resources, potentially affecting consistency across sites.

Conclusion

The implementation of the I-TRANSFER protocol across five diverse health systems demonstrated that improving care transitions for sepsis survivors requires a coordinated, multicomponent, and contextually tailored approach spanning acute, home health, and outpatient settings. By mapping more than 450 operationalized strategies to the ERIC taxonomy, this study provides a detailed, replicable account of how real-world health systems navigated the complexities of care transitions for sepsis survivorship. The breadth and saturation of strategies used—particularly those involving information transfer, clinician and patient education, care coordination, and quality monitoring—underscore the multifaceted nature of transitional care interventions and the critical role of adaptability in achieving core implementation functions. Our findings offer health system leaders pragmatic guidance for selecting, tailoring, and sequencing implementation strategies to strengthen post-sepsis care, and highlight the value of structured needs assessments and ongoing quality monitoring in sustaining improvements. As attention to sepsis survivorship grows, the translatable strategies from I-TRANSFER can inform broader efforts to deliver timely care across settings to improve outcomes for this high-risk and vulnerable population.
