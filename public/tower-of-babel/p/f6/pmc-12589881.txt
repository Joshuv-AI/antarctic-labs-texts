Abnormal myelin could be a mediator of childhood-trauma-induced depression: A quantitative synthetic MRI study
Authors: Junyan Wen, Shuqiong Zheng, Zhimin Chen, Xuecong Lin, Shanshan Yang, Wei Cui, Liaoming Gao, Ziqi Wu, Liya Gong, Zhujia Li, Ying Guo, Yanyu Hao, Mingxuan Gao, Jingwen Luo, Linlin Jing, Honglei Yin, Ge Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Myelin abnormalities are associated with the depression.•Synthetic MRI shows myelin differences in MDD patients.•Myelin volume fraction may serve as a depression biomarker.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a globally prevalent mental illness associated with increasing economic, social, and personal burdens (Marx et al., 2023, Greenberg et al., 2021, Whiteford et al., 2013). Despite its substantial negative impact, the underlying pathophysiology is unclear (Marx et al., 2023, Hack et al., 2023, van Oostrom et al., 2013, Rizvi et al., 2021, Puryear et al., 2020, Cuijpers et al., 2020). Childhood trauma exposure is considered a leading contributor to the development of MDD (Li et al., 2016, LeMoult et al., 2020, McKay et al., 2021), with the associated brain mechanisms being linked to functional alterations in the brain (Gerin et al., 2023, Herringa et al., 2013). However, the understanding of the neurobiology connecting childhood trauma and MDD is still limited.

In addition to psychosocial factors, a growing body of evidence suggests a possible link between myelin abnormalities and depression. Myelin, which is the insulating coating on neuron fibres, plays a critical role in nerve signalling and information exchange (Fields, 2014, Li et al., 2022). Studies have identified cortical demyelination as a potential factor in MDD, as it may impact cognitive processes in affected individuals. Postmortem brain tissue analysis of individuals with depression revealed a decrease in myelin and oligodendrocytes, which are responsible for generating and maintaining myelin (Boda, 2021, Simons and Nave, 2015, Weil et al., 2016, Tham et al., 2011). Another study on treatment-resistant depression revealed a decrease in the magnetization transfer ratio (MTR) of the corpus callosum and the hippocampus, indicating a reduction in myelin levels (Zhang et al., 2009). More recently, a study utilized the T1-weighted/T2-weighted (T1w/T2w) ratio, which reflects the cortical myelin content, to distinguish individuals with unipolar depression from healthy controls, even without considering clinical and demographic variables (Baranger et al., 2021). Additionally, another study utilizing the T1w/T2w ratio reported reduced cortical myelin content in regions associated with the salience network and default mode network in MDD patients (Zhang et al., 2023).

Beyond these microstructural factors, the principle of cerebral lateralization provides a critical framework for understanding neuropsychiatric disorders (Ocklenburg et al., 2024, Mundorf and Ocklenburg, 2025). Although evidence for macroscopic structural asymmetry in MDD is equivocal (Kovel et al., 2019), theories of left-hemispheric vulnerability in emotional regulation remain prominent. A key unanswered question is whether this vulnerability operates at the microstructural level of myelination.

Despite these findings, the relationships among myelination alterations, childhood trauma and depression in MDD patients remain poorly understood. One contributing factor to this issue may be the challenges of mapping cortical myelination in routine clinical scans. A previous study proposed a novel technique, synthetic MRI (SyMRI), which can obtain quantitative T1, T2, proton density (PD), and myelin volume fraction (MVF) mapping images in a single 5–6-minute scan (Warntjes et al., 2016, Warntjes et al., 2008). Although the application of SyMRI in psychiatric disorders is still emerging, a handful of studies have begun to reveal its value in characterizing brain microstructural alterations. For instance, in attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), SyMRI has been used to identify widespread abnormalities in T1 and T2 relaxation times across cerebellar, attentional, and default mode networks (Su et al., 2022), as well as to detect region-specific increases in myelin content within white matter tracts (Lin et al., 2024). These findings provide a compelling precedent for utilizing SyMRI to investigate neuropathology in other psychiatric conditions, such as MDD. Several studies have demonstrated that SyMRI results are highly reproducible and that the quantitative images are comparable to those acquired by conventional MR quantitative approaches in brain examinations (Schmidbauer et al., 2019, Saccenti et al., 2020). Although SyMRI offers efficient quantitative mapping of myelin and other tissue properties, its reproducibility across diverse populations and clinical settings requires further validation (Pires et al., 2025). Variations in acquisition parameters and demographic factors may influence the consistency of SyMRI-derived metrics (Syed Nasser et al., 2024, Bao et al., 2022). It is also worth noting that most SyMRI acquisitions are based on 2D sequences, which do not provide the same spatial resolution as 3D acquisitions. This limitation inherently affects the feasibility of superior anatomical specificity analysis. Thus, while SyMRI shows promise for revealing microstructural alterations, its generalizability and anatomical precision remain areas of ongoing investigation. Measuring cortical myelin volume fraction using SyMRI is challenging due to the relatively low myelin content in gray matter. Nevertheless, the previous study has reported correlations of cortical myelin volume fraction measured by SyMRI, T1w/T2w, and MT methods (Hagiwara et al., 2018). Moreover, the SyMRI study in multiple sclerosis have demonstrated altered cortical myelin volume fraction that was inversely correlated with disease duration (Andica et al., 2019). These findings support the feasibility of using SyMRI to assess cortical myelin content.

Therefore, we aimed to assess microstructural alterations in whole-brain gray matter in individuals with MDD via the SyMRI technique. In this study, we explored differences in quantitative MVF mapping, which may serve as potential markers of underlying pathology, between MDD patients and healthy controls. Additionally, we correlated these significantly altered quantitative parameters with the results of neuropsychological tests to assess any associations between brain alterations, clinical symptoms and childhood trauma.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of NanFang Hospital (Approval No. NFEC-2022–506) and signed informed consent was obtained from subjects and/or caregivers. Thirty-five MDD patients and 49 healthy controls were included as study subjects. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) met the diagnostic criteria for depression in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Version V (DSM-V); 2) were aged 18–60 years; 3) had a 24-item Hamilton Depression Inventory (HAMD-24) score of not less than 20; 4) did not receive systematic antidepressant medication before enrollment; and 5) did not take other psychiatric medications within two weeks before enrollment. Written informed consent was given by the guardians of all the subjects (patients). The exclusion criteria included the following: 1) a combination of schizophrenia and other psychiatric diseases; 2) other pathologies, e.g., small vessel disease, ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke, hydrocephalus or tumours; 3) pregnancy, lactation; 4) contraindications to MRI examination, such as cardiac pacemakers, mechanical valves and other metal implants in the body; and 5) poor or marginal MRI image quality. The age range of the final subjects was 18–35 years, with a mean age of 24 years. There were no significant differences in age or sex between the two groups (as shown in Table 1). The HAMD and Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA) were used to evaluate depressive and anxiety symptoms in patients with MDD. The Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ) was used to evaluate the presence and severity of childhood trauma experienced by individuals as a previous environmental stimulus. MRI acquisition

MRI examinations were performed via a 3.0 T MRI scanner (Signa Architect 3.0 T, GE, USA) with a 48-channel head coil and the same scanning protocol for all participants. A coronal T2-weighted sequence was collected to exclude any intracranial lesions. A sagittal 3D T1-weighted MP-RAGE (Magnetization Prepared Rapid Gradient Echo) sequence with an isotropic resolution of 1.00 mm was scanned. Quantitative MRI parameters (Myelin, T1, T2 and PD mapping ratios) were acquired via the SyMRI technique. SyMRI is based on a two-dimensional multiple-dynamic multiple-echo (MDME) pulse sequence that comprises four automatically calculated saturation delay times and two echo times. The main parameters were as follows: TR = 10,205.0 ms, TE = 13.4 ms, flip angle = 20°, NEX = 1, thickness = 3.5 mm, slice gap = 0.7 mm, ETL = 16, and in-plane pixel size = 2.0 mm × 2.0 mm.

Image analysis

After MR scanning, the MVF, T1, T2 and PD quantitative maps were estimated from the SyMRI data via the postprocessing software SyntheticMR (v11.2.2, SyntheticMR AB, Sweden). The high-resolution 3D T1-weighted images (T1w) were segmented into WM, GM, and CSF. Voxels with GM probability > 0.75 and CSF probability < 0.15 were retained to generate a gray matter mask. To obtain the brain-region-level quantitative values, the T1w was first coregistered to the PD map. The coregistered T1w images were subsequently normalized to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space via Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs). After that, all the MVF, T1, T2 and PD mapping images were transformed into MNI space. Finally, the Brainnetome Atlas that overlapped with the gray matter mask was applied to all the normalized images to extract the mean MVF, T1, T2 and PD values of all 246 grey matter regions (Fan et al., 2016). Mean values for MVF, T1, T2, and PD were successfully extracted for all 246 Gy matter regions in every participant, with no missing data requiring imputation or exclusion. Three examples of the atlas registration on individual myelin and PD maps are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed via GraphPad Prism (version 9.4.1 for Windows; GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA; https://www.graphpad.com). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the data. Independent samples t tests were used to compare quantitative variables, whereas the chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables. A whole-brain, unbiased analysis was performed across all 246 Gy matter regions of the Brainnetome Atlas. Statistical comparisons were conducted for each region, and corrections for multiple comparisons by the false discovery rate (FDR) were applied across the entire brain. No regions were pre-selected based on hemispheric lateralization. Cohen's d was reported as a measure of effect size for the group effect. A post hoc power analysis was performed using Jamovi (version 2.3.28.0, with addon jpower 0.1.2) to estimate the statistical power of our group comparisons for the primary imaging metrics (MVF and PD). Based on the observed effect sizes (Cohen’s d ranging from 0.71 to 0.94) from the significant findings, the sample sizes of N1 = 35 (MDD) and N2 = 49 (HC), and a significance level (α) of 0.05, the achieved statistical power exceeded 0.88 for all reported results. This indicates that despite the modest sample size, the study was adequately powered to detect the moderate-to-large effects of interest. In the MDD group, Pearson correlation analyses between significantly altered brain-regional quantitative variables and clinical scales were performed. A p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Function decoding

To further explain the brain regions that were found to be significantly different between groups and correlated with clinical symptoms, we performed function decoding on the relevant brain regions. Function decoding uses probabilistic (activation) mappings provided by the Neurosynth database (version 0.7, https://github.com/neurosynth/neurosynth-data/), which includes over 14,371 functional neuroimaging studies (Yarkoni et al., 2011). A total of 262 terms that had clear biological significance were selected. The list of selected meta-analytic cognitive terms is listed in Supplementary Table 1. The significance of the associations between the identified ROIs and meta-analytic cognitive terms was determined via the BrainMap approach from the NiMARE Python package (v0.0.11, RRID:SCR_017398) (Salo et al., 2022, Salo et al., 2022). The significant terms were determined if both the pForward and pReverse values were < 0.05 (FDR corrected). In the graphical display, the size of the text for each significant term reflects how strongly it is associated with the ROIs.

Mediation analysis

The GLM Mediation Model in the jamovi Advanced Mediation Models (jAMM) module for data analysis in Jamovi 2.3.28 was used to examine whether and to what extent the myelin content mediated the significant environment-symptom associations. Bootstrapping was selected for its robustness to non-normal distributions and smaller sample sizes, as well as its widespread use in mediation analyses (Hayes and Scharkow, 2013). Our study was based on the mediation method with 10,000 bootstrap bias-corrected 95 % confidence intervals, and the indirect effect can be considered statistically significant if the 95 % confidence interval does not include zero (Hayes, 2017).

Results

Clinical characteristics of patients and controls

No statistically significant difference in age was observed between the MDD patients (median age, 23 years; range, 18–34 years) and the healthy controls (median age, 24 years; range, 21–35 years) (p = 0.759). The difference in sex between the two groups was not significant (p = 0.146) (Table 1). All the subjects were right-handed.

Moreover, the PD values of the left inferior frontal sulcus, left caudal area 45, left medial area 14, left rostral area 7, left lateral area 5, left ventral dysgranular and granular insula, and left nucleus accumbens were greater than those in the HC group. Function decoding results

Functional decoding identified a series of functional terms for each brain region of interest with clear biological significance. The left inferior frontal sulcus and left caudal area 45 are related mainly to language processing. Left lateral area 5 was related mainly to attentive vision. The left ventral dysgranular and granular insula is related to negative perceptions and emotions, such as pain, disgust and fear. The full functional decoding results of the brain regions with significantly different MVF and PD values are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Discussion

In this study, we used a synthetic MRI technique to reveal abnormalities in myelin in multiple gray matter regions in major depressive disorder patients for the first time. We found that MDD patients had significantly lower myelin content and higher proton density than controls did in the left inferior frontal sulcus, left caudal area 45, left medial area 14, left rostral area 7, left lateral area 5, left ventral dysgranular and granular insula, and left nucleus accumbens. These abnormalities are associated with clinical symptoms and childhood trauma exposure. Although statistically significant, the modest correlation strengths indicate that myelin content explains only a limited proportion of variance in depressive and anxiety symptoms, underscoring the multifactorial nature of MDD. These findings provide preliminary evidence that gray matter myelin reduction may play a mediating role in childhood trauma-induced depression. Our results lend mechanistic support to the potential of quantitative synthetic MRI techniques to help elucidate neurostructural abnormalities in MDD, which may eventually contribute to a more nuanced understanding of the disorder and inform future research into targeted interventions.

In our study, gray matter regions with significant MVF alterations did not show corresponding changes in T1 or T2 values. This dissociation suggests that myelin alterations predominated in these regions and the observed effects are specific to the myelin-sensitive metric rather than general tissue properties captured by T1 and T2 relaxometry, strengthening our interpretation. This is consistent with previous methodological comparisons indicating that while T1 and T2 are influenced by myelin, they are also sensitive to other factors such as iron content, axonal density, and water mobility (Ocklenburg et al., 2024, Hagiwara et al., 2018). SyMRI models myelin content based on multi-compartment Bloch simulations that account for magnetization exchange and proton density reduction specifically caused by myelin (Ocklenburg et al., 2024). Therefore, the lack of T1 or T2 difference underscores the specificity of SyMRI-derived MVF as a more direct and specific measure of myelin content, which is particularly valuable in gray matter where myelin is sparse and confounded by other microstructural elements.

Our results can be contextualized by comparing them with the nascent literature on SyMRI in psychiatric populations, particularly in ADHD. A recent SyMRI study in drug-naïve children with ADHD reported significantly prolonged T1 and T2 values in the cerebellum, default mode, and attention networks, which were interpreted as potential indicators of delayed maturation, low myelin content, and iron deficiency (Su et al., 2022). Another SyMRI study in ADHD children found increased—rather than decreased—myelin volume fraction in several crucial white matter tracts, including the frontostriatal pathway and corpus callosum (Lin et al., 2024). While the direction of myelin alteration (increase in ADHD white matter vs. decrease in our MDD gray matter) appears disorder-specific, both patterns aberrantly deviate from typical development and underscore the sensitivity of SyMRI in detecting microstructural pathologies. The convergence of SyMRI-derived abnormalities across these disorders, albeit in different tissues and directions, reinforces the technique's utility in mapping the neurobiological underpinnings of mental illnesses.

The identification of lower myelin content and higher proton density in the left hemisphere of MDD patients sheds light on the potential mechanisms underlying childhood trauma-induced depression. Most previous studies focused on abnormalities in myelin in white matter. Much less is known about myelin in grey matter, but the available data reveal similar mechanisms (McGee et al., 2005, Filbin, 2003, Tomassy et al., 2014, Timmler and Simons, 2019). Unlike increasing the nerve conduction speed to the maximum speed in white matter axons that transmit information along one-way nerve fibre bundles, myelin may play a role in synchronizing the information transmission time of the whole or local network in more complex neural circuits because of its discontinuous distribution pattern in grey matter (Tomassy et al., 2014, Timmler and Simons, 2019). When myelin is reduced, this synchronization can be disrupted, ultimately leading to functional disorders. Another theory to explain differences in myelin content in gray matter is to consider the function of myelin as a growth inhibitor to prevent abnormal axonal sprouting and synapse formation (McGee et al., 2005, Filbin, 2003). Gray matter regions that are more plastic and require more synaptic remodelling are generally less myelinated and take longer to complete myelination. Delayed development or disruption of myelin within gray matter may enhance the effects of environmental factors on neuronal remodelling, thereby increasing their vulnerability to adverse factors. Previous findings in mice exposed to social isolation have shown that the myelin content in the prefrontal cortex is reduced and that increased myelination reverses depressive-like behavior (Liu et al., 2012, Liu et al., 2016). On the other hand, proton density is the most basic magnetic resonance measurement indicator, representing the apparent concentration of water protons in each voxel. In a study that correlated MRI indices with postmortem pathological measurements of myelin content, PD was confirmed to be significantly negatively correlated with myelin content (Mottershead et al., 2003). An abnormal increase in proton density indicates that more neural tissue is replaced by water (Gracien et al., 2016, Neeb et al., 2008). Therefore, lower myelin content and higher proton density may reflect the loss of neural integrity and the increase in water content in grey matter regions. This may affect neural communication and plasticity, leading to cognitive and emotional impairments.

The reduced myelin in the left inferior frontal sulcus (Zhang et al., 2023, Sacchet and Gotlib, 2017), superior parietal lobule (Baranger et al., 2021), left ventral dysgranular and granular insula (Zhang et al., 2023) and left nucleus accumbens (NAcc) (Sacchet and Gotlib, 2017) found in this study was consistent with previous studies using other imaging techniques, suggesting that these regions play important roles in MDD. The abnormalities in myelin and PD found in this study may reflect alterations in neurodevelopmental, neuroplasticity and neuroinflammatory processes in MDD patients, which may affect information transmission and integration between neurons, leading to impairments in emotion regulation, cognitive control and social interaction.

Left lateral area 5 of the superior parietal lobule was identified to be involved in attentive vision via functional decoding. Visual attention is the cognitive process that mediates the selection of important information from the environment (Lockhofen and Mulert, 2021). The stimulating environment during childhood growth has a profound effect on the development of a child's brain (Rakesh et al., 2023, Teicher et al., 2016). In our study, myelin content in left lateral area 5 was demonstrated to play a mediating role in the connection between childhood trauma experience and depressive symptoms. We hypothesized that maltreatment-related childhood experiences affect the typical development of children's brains and, when abnormal development affects the regions associated with visual attention, disrupts the proper function of attentive vision, consequently making it harder to focus on tasks or to perceive positive stimuli in their environment, which implies potential environment-brain-behavior plasticity loops. This finding is consistent with previous research conclusions (Chen et al., 2019). This could cause them to experience negative emotions and frustration and to be unable to experience pleasure, as well as impair their social functioning and quality of life (Wang et al., 2020, Trapp et al., 2018).

Our study investigated more detailed insula subregions on the basis of the Brainnetome atlas and revealed that MDD patients presented significantly reduced myelin content and increased proton density in the left ventral dysgranular and granular insula, which was significantly correlated with the severity of clinical anxiety symptoms. The insula is a crucial brain region that integrates internal and external stimuli, including information from the nervous system, to support cognitive, emotional, and balance processes (Simmons et al., 2013). Anatomically, the insula receives input from thalamic nuclei and has reciprocal connections with the amygdala, limbic system, and cortical structures (Benarroch, 2019, Zhuo, 2016). In previous studies, the ventral dysgranular insula and granular insula were confirmed to have extensive connections with the amygdala, anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), presupplementary motor area (preSMA), portions of the ventral premotor cortex (PMV) and posterior parietal cortex (PPC), ventral striatum, lateral hypothalamus, and periaqueductal grey (Benarroch, 2019, Zhang et al., 2019). In our study, we found that it was associated mainly with pain, disgust and fear, utilizing functional decoding performed with meta-analytic data. Our findings suggest that abnormalities in the left ventral dysgranular and granular insula may play a more important mediating role in the pathophysiology of anxiety symptoms in patients with depression. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have shown that the insula is involved in a variety of cognitive and emotional processes, including emotion regulation, interoception, and pain processing, in which hyperactivation is more frequently observed (Etkin and Wager, 2007, Wilcox et al., 2016, Harrison et al., 2021, Labrakakis, 2023).

An intriguing aspect of our findings is their pronounced left-hemispheric lateralization. This was not a predetermined focus but an objective outcome of our whole-brain analysis. This finding invites consideration within the broader context of cerebral lateralization in depression. An important consideration of our left-lateralized findings is their apparent contrast with the largest mega-analysis of structural asymmetry in MDD by the ENIGMA consortium, which reported no significant case-control differences (Kovel et al., 2019). This discrepancy may be reconciled by several key methodological and conceptual differences. First, and most crucially, the ENIGMA study examined macroscopic asymmetry in cortical thickness and surface area, whereas our study suggests microstructural asymmetry in cortical myelin content in MDD. It is biologically plausible that neuropathological processes in MDD, potentially triggered by adverse experiences like childhood trauma, initially disrupt intracortical myelination—a key determinant of neural circuit efficiency—without immediately altering gross cortical morphology measurable at the macroscopic level. Thus, our findings might reflect an earlier or more subtle pathological change that precedes overt volumetric asymmetry. Second, the ENIGMA cohort encompasses vast clinical heterogeneity. Our sample, comprising mainly young drug-naïve patients might capture a more etiologically distinct subgroup where left-hemisphere microstructural vulnerability may be a defining characteristic. Therefore, rather than contradicting, our results potentially extend the ENIGMA findings by highlighting a specific microstructural phenomenon of asymmetry that may be obscured in large, heterogeneous cohorts but is detectable in a more precise clinical sample.

Despite the significance of our findings, we acknowledge several limitations of our study. One of the main drawbacks is that we used a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability and robustness of our results, particularly given the high inter-individual variability in MDD and the challenges in identifying consistent gray matter microstructure patterns. Furthermore, the sex distribution between groups, while not statistically significantly different, was not perfectly matched, with a lower number of males in the MDD group. This is a common feature in MDD research due to its higher epidemiological prevalence in females, but it introduces the possibility that sex could be a confounding variable. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that the clear group differences survived FDR correction across all 246 regions examined identified in our primary analyses, and the effect sizes for significant findings were moderate to large (Cohen's d ranging from approximately 0.7 to 0.9), suggesting that the results are internally robust and biologically plausible. Even so, the limited sample size remains an important constraint, and we emphasize that our study should be considered hypothesis-generating. However, we acknowledge that a more powered, sex-matched design would be ideal to unequivocally disentangle the effects of diagnosis from sex. We recommend that future research replicate our study with larger and more diverse samples to increase the validity and reliability of the conclusions.

Another possible limitation of our study is the lack of longitudinal data. Thus, we could not examine the causal relationships between the variables of interest. In particular, the cross-sectional nature of our data cannot rule out the possibility of reverse causality. For instance, it is plausible that the observed myelin reductions are a consequence of MDD-related pathophysiological processes, such as chronic stress (Kokkosis et al., 2022) or neuroinflammation (Gorlova et al., 2023), rather than a predisposing cause. This is a significant limitation, as it prevents us from drawing any definitive conclusions about the associations between the Myelin or PD mapping ratio and clinical characteristics.

Furthermore, although we controlled for medication history by including only drug-naïve or minimally medicated patients, we did not exclude individuals based on comorbid anxiety symptoms, which are highly prevalent in MDD. The correlation between myelin content and HAMA scores suggests that anxiety may contribute to the observed neural alterations. Future studies with larger samples should consider stratifying participants by anxiety comorbidity or including anxiety as a covariate to better disentangle these effects. Additionally, other potential confounders such as genetic predispositions, lifestyle factors, and social support were not assessed and should be considered in future longitudinal designs.

Moreover, from a methodological perspective, the relatively low in-plane resolution and slice thickness of our SyMRI acquisition limited the feasibility of a superior anatomical specificity analysis and may amplify partial volume effects. To mitigate potential smoothing and registration inaccuracies, we adopted a more conservative ROI-based approach. We acknowledge this as a methodological limitation and recommend that future studies with higher-resolution imaging data employ surface-based or vertex-wise analyses to enhance anatomical precision.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that reduced myelin content of grey matter in the left lateral area 5 and left ventral dysgranular and granular insula may play a mediating role in childhood trauma-induced depression. However, due to the cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be drawn. Additionally, the potential confounding effect of comorbid anxiety was not controlled for. Future longitudinal studies with larger samples are necessary to confirm these mediating effects and establish causality. If corroborated, these findings could inform novel therapeutic strategies aimed at preserving or promoting myelination to mitigate the neurostructural consequences of childhood adversity.
