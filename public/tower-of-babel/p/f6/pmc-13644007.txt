The impact of spiritual care on gestational diabetes self-management: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Negin Shaterian, Fatemeh Abbaszadeh, Mahboobeh Kafaei-Atrian, Zahra Sooki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a common metabolic disorder in pregnancy requiring effective self-management. Spiritual care has been proposed as a supportive approach for chronic disease management.ObjectiveTo determine the effect of spiritual care on self-management of gestational diabetes.MethodsThis randomized controlled trial included 60 pregnant women with GDM (24–28 weeks gestation) attending two health centers in Kashan, Iran (2024). The intervention group received eight 80 min spiritual care sessions, while the control group received routine prenatal diabetes education. Diabetes self-management was assessed before, immediately after, and four weeks post intervention using a validated questionnaire. Data were analyzed using repeated measures adjusting for baseline differences.ResultsBefore the intervention, the mean self-management score was higher in the intervention group (32.90 ± 9.50) than in the control group (24.56 ± 8.90). After adjusting for baseline differences, the intervention group demonstrated significantly higher self-management scores immediately after the intervention (mean difference ≈ 10.3 points) and at four week follow-up (mean difference ≈ 9.8 points) compared with the control group (P < 0.001). The time effect (P = 0.660) and time × group interaction (P = 0.831) were non-significant, indicating that although scores improved, the pattern of change over time did not differ significantly between groups. ConclusionSpiritual care was associated with significantly higher diabetes self-management scores compared with usual care, even after adjusting for baseline differences. However, no significant time-dependent trends were observed, suggesting that the pattern of change between postintervention assessments did not reach a significant level. Background

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), defined as glucose intolerance with onset or first recognition during pregnancy, is a growing global concern and one of the most common metabolic complications of pregnancy [1, 2]. GDM affects 5–6% to 15–20% of pregnancies worldwide [3]. Its prevalence in Asia varies significantly, ranging from 0.7% to 51.0% [4]. In Iran, the prevalence has been reported to range from 1.3% to 8.9% [5]. Despite its high prevalence, the pathophysiology of GDM remains not fully understood. One suggested mechanism involves the increase in placental hormones during pregnancy, which interfere with insulin function, leading to a physiological rise in insulin resistance during the second and third trimesters. These hormonal changes contribute to reduced insulin sensitivity and increased metabolic stress, which can complicate glycemic control during pregnancy [6].

A review study by Abdelbasset et al. revealed that GDM is associated with numerous maternal complications (preterm labor, polyhydramnios, preeclampsia, and eclampsia) as well as fetal and neonatal complications (prematurity, mortality, macrosomia, fetal distress, and hypoglycemia) [7]. Moreover, GDM can negatively affect women’s quality of life, creating stress and pressure with adverse physical and psychological effects, ultimately influencing the control, management, and treatment of diabetes [8].

One of the non-pharmacological interventions that can be considered for these patients is spiritual care. Spirituality and spiritual health are fundamental concepts in the care of patients with chronic illnesses [9]. Human experience has demonstrated that, in critical situations, individuals often seek support from a sacred and divine source, even when physical illness has significantly progressed [10]. Spiritual care involves providing emotional, existential, and religious support to patients as they cope with illness and its consequences. This support may include counseling, spiritual guidance, reflective discussions, or group‑based activities [11].

Spiritual care impacts an individual’s response to stress, improves interpersonal relationships, accelerates recovery, reduces healthcare costs, and enhances self-management [12]. Self‑management is considered the cornerstone of diabetes treatment, emphasizing problem‑solving, behavioral change, and managing the physical, social, and emotional consequences of diabetes [13]. It includes adherence to medication, dietary control, physical activity, glucose monitoring, and regular medical visits [14]. The goal of self‑management in GDM is to achieve fasting blood glucose levels below 95 mg/dL and postprandial glucose levels below 120 mg/dL within two hours after meals, prevent complications, and improve quality of life [15].

Numerous studies have been conducted on the role of spiritual care in diabetes management, each proposing different approaches. These include spiritual self-care [16], spiritual therapy [17], religious-spiritual coping skills [18], group spiritual therapy [19], spiritual education [20], and spiritual-coping care [21]. However, findings across studies have been inconsistent. For example, a study reported significant improvements in glycemic control and self-management behaviors following a spiritual intervention [22], whereas another found minimal or no effect, often due to methodological limitations, short intervention duration, or cultural differences in spiritual engagement [23]. These contradictory results highlight the need for further research, particularly in populations where spirituality plays a central cultural role.

Theoretical frameworks such as the Stress-Coping Model [24] and Meaning-Making Theory [25, 26] suggest that spiritual care may enhance self-management by reducing psychological distress, increasing perceived control, strengthening motivation, and fostering a sense of purpose. These mechanisms may be especially relevant for pregnant women facing the dual stressors of pregnancy and diabetes.

Despite the growing interest in spiritual care, few studies have specifically examined its impact on behavioral aspects of self-management among women with GDM [27, 28], and most existing research has focused primarily on quality of life rather than self-management behaviors [29–31]. Given the rising prevalence of GDM and its short- and long-term consequences, effective self-management is essential to mitigate adverse outcomes for both mothers and infants.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effect of a structured spiritual care intervention on behavioral self-management among pregnant women with GDM in Kashan, Iran.

Methods

Design and setting

This study was a randomized controlled clinical trial conducted among pregnant women diagnosed with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). Participants were recruited from two comprehensive health centers in Kashan, Iran (Mehraparvar and Fin Kuchak) in 2024. After obtaining ethical approval and institutional permissions, sampling and recruitment were initiated.

Sample size and sampling

In this study, the sample size was determined based on a 95% confidence level (α = 0.05) and a 80% power of the test (β = 0.20) and using the sample size calculation formula to compare the means of two independent groups. For this purpose, the results of the study by Zafar El-Hyari et al. (2022) were used as the baseline study; so that the mean and standard deviation of the self-care score after the intervention in the intervention group was reported as 50.42 ± 12.58 and in the control group as 38.10 ± 11.34 [32]. Based on these values, the initial sample size was estimated to be 23 people in each group. Considering the probability of sample attrition (30%), the final sample size in each group was increased to 30 people.

Participants were selected through convenience sampling from medical records of pregnant women with confirmed GDM. They were then randomly assigned to intervention or control groups using block randomization with equal block sizes. Randomization was performed by an independent researcher not involved in data collection or intervention delivery. The statistician analyzing the data was blinded to group allocation. Moreover, given that the intervention was in the form of spiritual care, it was not possible to blind the researcher implementing the intervention as well as the participants.

Intervention

All intervention sessions were conducted in private counseling rooms within the two participating health centers. Sessions were held once per week over an eight-week period. Each session lasted approximately 80 min.

The intervention was delivered by a certified spiritual-health counselor with formal training in psychological counseling and spiritual therapy. The facilitator had over five years of experience conducting group-based spiritual interventions. Given that this study was conducted during pregnancy and assessed self-management of gestational diabetes, and that gestational diabetes is diagnosed after 24 to 28 weeks of pregnancy, and the duration of our interventions is 4 weeks, it was not possible to follow up for more than 4 weeks due to the time of delivery.

Intervention fidelity and standardization

To ensure intervention fidelity and reproducibility, a standardized intervention manual was developed prior to study implementation. This manual contained detailed objectives, educational content, spiritual exercises, discussion topics, and session procedures for all eight sessions. All sessions were delivered by the same certified spiritual health counselor to minimize inter-provider variability. Before the study, the counselor completed a training and orientation program regarding the intervention protocol and study procedures.

To maintain consistency across sessions, a structured session checklist was used after each meeting to verify that all planned components had been delivered. The principal investigator periodically reviewed completed checklists and session records to ensure adherence to the intervention protocol. Participants’ attendance was documented at every session, and attendance rates were monitored throughout the intervention period. Participants who missed a session were contacted and required to attend a make-up session to receive the missed content.

In addition, session duration, educational materials, sequence of content delivery, and group facilitation procedures were standardized across all intervention groups. No modifications to the intervention protocol were made during the study period. These procedures were implemented to enhance treatment fidelity, ensure consistency of intervention delivery, and facilitate replication of the study in future research.

Tools

In this study, two questionnaires were used to collect data: the Demographic Characteristics Questionnaire and the Diabetes Self-Management Questionnaire. The Demographic Characteristics Questionnaire included 18 questions related to the participant’s age and their spouse’s age, years of marriage, pregnancy desire, history of gestational diabetes in previous pregnancies, family history of diabetes, infertility history, history of miscarriage, number of pregnancies, number of children, education level, employment status of both the participant and their spouse, and economic status.

The Diabetes Self-Management Questionnaire contained 27 questions using a 4-point Likert scale (ranging from 0 to 3). This questionnaire assessed four domains: glucose management, dietary control, physical activity, and medical visits. The validity and reliability of this questionnaire were calculated by Hosseinzadegan et al. in 2021. In the reliability analysis, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was found to be 0.88, indicating excellent internal consistency of the tool. The Cronbach’s alpha values for the domains were as follows: glucose management (9 questions) = 0.85, physical activity (3 questions) = 0.66, dietary control (6 questions) = 0.67, and medical visits (4 questions) = 0.78 [34]. Questions 5, 7, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 18, and 20 had reverse scoring.

Data analysis

The data were collected, entered into SPSS software andchecked for error. To assess the normality of the data, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used. For describing normal quantitative variables, mean and standard deviation were used, median and interquartile ranges were used for describing variables with non-normal distribution and qualitative variables were described using percentage frequency. For examining the equality of the educational groups regarding demographic variables, for continuous variables, the Student’s t-test was applied if the data were normally distributed, and the Mann-Whitney test was used if the distribution was not normal. For categorical variables, the Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were employed. To initially explore differences in diabetes self-management scores across measurement time points, descriptive comparisons were performed. Since the self-management scores at the beginning of the study differed between the groups, baseline self-management score and other variables with differences between the groups (age, years since marriage, family history of diabetes, pregnancy intention, parity, number of children, history of gestational diabetes) were entered as covariates in the mixed repeated-measures ANCOVA model. Importantly, the baseline measurement was not included as a repeated measurement time point in the analysis model. Immediately after intervention and four weeks after intervention measurements were entered to model as the within-subject factor and group (intervention/control) as the between-subject factor. Sphericity was evaluated using Mauchly’s test, and when violated, Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. Since all randomized participants completed the study and were included in the final analysis, the intention-to-treat principle was applied. The statistical calculations for the data obtained in this study were performed using SPSS version 22. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered.

Results

A total of 70 pregnant women with GDM were assessed for eligibility. Ten participants were excluded due to not meeting the inclusion criteria or declining participation. The remaining 60 eligible participants were enrolled and randomly allocated to the intervention group (n = 30) and the control group (n = 30) using a computer-generated random sequence with equal allocation (1:1 ratio). Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by an independent researcher who was not involved in participant recruitment or assessment.

No participants withdrew or were lost to follow-up, and outcome data from all 60 participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). To minimize potential bias, outcome assessors and data analysts were blinded to group allocation, and both groups received routine standard care in addition to the intervention-specific protocol.

Immediately after the intervention, the intervention group showed a marked increase in self-management scores (41.90 ± 7.93), while the control group showed a smaller increase (31.56 ± 12.45). Discussion

This study examined the effect of a structured spiritual care intervention on self-management behaviors among pregnant women with GDM. The findings demonstrated that although both groups showed some improvement over time, the intervention group consistently achieved significantly higher self-management scores immediately after the intervention and at four-week follow-up. These results support the potential value of spiritual care as a complementary strategy for enhancing diabetes self-management during pregnancy.

In the present study, significant baseline differences were observed between the intervention and control groups in variables such as age, years since marriage, history of GDM, family history of diabetes, pregnancy intention, number of pregnancies, and number of children. Although the randomization conditions were met in this study, this significant difference between the groups could create a bias in the final result. These variables were therefore included as covariates in the repeated‑measures analysis. The presence of these baseline differences represents an important methodological limitation, as they may have influenced initial self‑management capacity and responsiveness to the intervention. Nevertheless, after adjusting for these differences, the between‑group effect remained statistically significant, suggesting that the spiritual care intervention contributed meaningfully to improved self‑management.

While covariate adjustment helps reduce confounding, baseline imbalances still raise concerns about internal validity. The possibility that adjusted results are influenced by model specification should be acknowledged more cautiously.

The results of the assessment of gestational diabetes self-management status before the intervention in the intervention and control groups showed that self-management of diabetes before the study was significantly different between the two groups. These findings indicate the need for a deeper understanding of the factors affecting self-management in this group of patients. Given that the intervention group had significantly better self-management at baseline, the superiority after intervention may partly reflect pre-existing differences rather than the true effect of the intervention. According to previous research, self-management of diabetes is recognized as a key factor in disease control and improving health outcomes. Studies indicate that the spiritual and social aspects of patients’ lives can influence motivations and behaviors related to self-management [35]. For example, research has shown that the presence of social and spiritual support can help increase patients’ confidence in managing diabetes, leading to improved self-management performance [36]. These mechanisms may partly explain why participants in the intervention group—who engaged in structured spiritual reflection and coping strategies—showed greater improvements in self‑management. The discussion at this point appears to shift toward explanatory narratives that are not directly supported by the presented data. The causal link between spiritual reflection and improved outcomes remains speculative given baseline differences and non-random imbalance in key variables.

The results of this study align with previous research demonstrating the positive effects of spiritual interventions on diabetes outcomes. For instance, studies have reported that spiritual therapy can reduce stress and increase feelings of life satisfaction, which in turn has a positive impact on self-management behaviors [37]. In contrast to the findings of this study, the results of another study showed no significant difference between the control and intervention groups in terms of diabetes self-management before the intervention [38]. These discrepancies may be due to differences in intervention duration, cultural context, baseline spiritual well‑being, or the degree of participant engagement. In cultures where spirituality is deeply integrated into daily life, such as in Iran, spiritual interventions may have stronger effects due to higher receptivity and cultural congruence. Some of these explanations (e.g., cultural congruence) are plausible but remain speculative unless directly tested. The manuscript should more clearly distinguish between evidence-based interpretation and hypothesis.

These findings are consistent with studies showing that spiritual interventions often produce immediate psychological benefits, but long‑term behavioral change requires sustained engagement [39]. Future interventions may benefit from booster sessions, digital follow-ups, or integration into routine prenatal care to maintain long-term effects. This forward-looking interpretation is reasonable, but it should be framed as a hypothesis rather than a conclusion derived from the current dataset.

The lack of statistical significance for the time effect and the time-by-group interaction indicates that while the intervention group consistently exhibited superior self-management scores, the trajectory of change over the study period did not fundamentally differ between the groups. This plateau may reflect the relatively brief four-week follow-up period, which might have been insufficient to capture longitudinal shifts. Furthermore, it is possible that while spiritual interventions elicit rapid behavioral stabilization and initial emotional uplift, these effects require sustained reinforcement and continued support to prevent stagnation and facilitate ongoing improvement. The explanation of a “plateau” effect is speculative and not directly supported by the non-significant interaction results. Alternative interpretations, including measurement sensitivity and regression to the mean, should also be considered.

In the present study, the intervention focused solely on the spiritual aspect and was implemented generally. However, the concept that attending to health and taking medical actions is a religious and spiritual duty was not emphasized. If religion had been linked to health as a religious duty, the outcome might have been more favorable. This issue could serve as the basis for further research. The suggestion that reframing the intervention as a religious duty would improve outcomes is speculative and not supported by empirical evidence within this study.

Another important consideration is the cultural context. In Iranian society, spirituality and religious practices are deeply embedded in daily life, which may enhance acceptance and effectiveness of spiritual interventions. Additionally, family support plays a central role in health behaviors during pregnancy [40]. Women with strong family support may find it easier to implement self-management behaviors, while those with limited support may struggle despite receiving spiritual guidance [41]. These social factors should be considered in future research. Cultural and social explanations are plausible, but they should be presented cautiously as contextual hypotheses rather than confirmed explanatory factors.

Although the group-by-time interaction was not statistically significant, the adjusted group effect demonstrated consistently higher self-management scores in the intervention group at both post-intervention assessments. The lack of a significant interaction may be related to the relatively short follow-up period and limited sample size, which reduced the ability to detect differences in temporal trends. Notably, self-management scores showed a slight increasing trend in the intervention group and a slight decreasing trend in the control group. Future studies with larger samples and longer follow-up are warranted to determine whether these trends translate into statistically significant differences over time.

The study also highlights the need to consider participants’ baseline spiritual well-being. Because spiritual well-being was not measured, it is unclear whether participants with higher initial spirituality benefited more from the intervention. Future studies should include validated measures of spiritual well-being and examine its moderating role. This is a valid methodological point; however, it also underscores a limitation in attributing observed effects specifically to the intervention rather than unmeasured baseline differences.

Limitations

This study had several limitations, including baseline differences between groups, lack of measurement of spiritual well-being, short follow-up duration that limits the ability to assess long-term sustainability of the intervention, and potential influence of cultural and social factors such as family support and individual acceptance of spiritual interventions. Future studies should include longer follow-up periods, assess baseline spiritual well-being, and explore integrated intervention models combining spiritual, psychological, and educational approaches. Given that studies in this field are limited, there were no studies directly related to gestational diabetes and diabetes self-management, so the most similar studies were used for comparison. Although group assignment was randomised, baseline differences between groups were observed. However, even with statistical adjustment, residual confounding factors may remain. The possibility of clinical interpretation of behavioral changes is limited because no objective clinical indicators such as HbA1c or fasting glucose were assessed. Furthermore, as participants and intervention provider could not be blinded and the primary outcome was self-reported, the possibility of performance and response bias cannot be ruled out. These factors should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial evaluated the impact of a structured spiritual care intervention on self-management behaviors among pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus. After adjusting for baseline differences and demographic confounders, the intervention group demonstrated significantly higher self-management scores immediately after the intervention and at four-week follow-up compared with the control group. Although the time effect and the time × group interaction were not statistically significant, the consistent between-group differences indicate that the intervention was associated with consistently higher self-management scores during the short follow-up period.

The non-significant interaction effect suggests that the intervention did not produce a progressively increasing effect over time; rather, its benefits appeared shortly after implementation and were maintained during the short follow-up period. This pattern may indicate that spiritual care may contribute to early psychological or motivational boost that stabilizes rather than grows over time. Longer follow-up periods are needed to determine whether these effects persist, diminish, or strengthen with continued practice.

Overall, the results suggest that integrating spiritual care into prenatal diabetes management may enhance women’s engagement in self-management behaviors, particularly in cultural contexts where spirituality plays a central role. Future research should explore long-term outcomes, incorporate measures of spiritual well-being, and evaluate combined models that integrate spiritual care with behavioral, nutritional, and psychological interventions.
