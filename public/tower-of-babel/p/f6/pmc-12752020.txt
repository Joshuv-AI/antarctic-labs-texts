Radial astroglia cooperate with microglia to clear neuronal cell bodies during zebrafish optic tectum development
Authors: Heather M. Barber, Cassidy G. Robbins, Zachary Cutler, Robin I. Brown, Lauren E. Jung, Inge Werkman, Sarah Kucenas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYThe clearance of dead cells by phagocytes is an essential component of neural development in many organisms. Microglia are the main phagocytes in the central nervous system (CNS), but the extent of participation by other glial cells remains unclear, especially under homeostatic conditions. During zebrafish optic tectum (OT) development, we observed radial astroglia forming dynamic, spherical projections from their basal processes. These projections, which we call scyllate heads, coincide with a wave of neuronal cell death in the OT. We show that scyllate heads surround the majority of dying neurons soon after phosphatidylserine exposure. However, unlike traditional phagosomes, scyllate heads persist for many hours and are rarely acidified or internalized. Instead, microglia invade scyllate heads and remove their contents for degradation. Our study reveals an active role for radial astroglia in homeostatic cell clearance and cooperation between microglia and radial astroglia during zebrafish OT development.

INTRODUCTION

During neural development, many cells undergo programmed cell death (PCD) and are efficiently cleared away by phagocytes.1,2 The process by which phagocytes recognize, engulf, and degrade dead or abnormal cells is called efferocytosis.2–6 Efferocytosis by professional phagocytes, including macrophages and microglia, has been extensively investigated.2–5 However, studies demonstrate that other cell types can participate in efferocytosis and are called non-professional phagocytes when acting in this context.3,4,7–11 In phagocyte-deficient models, some non-professional phagocytes can increase their phagocytic activity to compensate for the lack of professional phagocytosis.7–9,12 However, it is less understood if and how professional and non-professional phagocytes cooperate to clear debris during physiological development.

One of the most well-known examples of developmental PCD occurs during neurogenesis, when neurons unnecessary for circuit function undergo PCD and are cleared by microglia, the resident phagocytes of the central nervous system (CNS).2,5,13 In the zebrafish optic tectum (OT), the major visual processing center in teleosts, retinal ganglion cell (RGC) axons that form the optic nerve begin to connect to OT targets at approximately 60 h post-fertilization (hpf).14,15 At this stage, there is also a sharp increase in the number of dead and dying OT neurons.2,16,17 Microglia, which originate outside of the CNS, begin infiltrating the OT around 60 hpf and do not reach their stable larval population size until 72–96 hpf.16,18,19 Thus, there are few professional phagocytes compared to the amount of dying cells and debris present during this critical developmental window. Previously, we showed that before colonization of the zebrafish trunk by macrophages and microglia, neural crest cells phagocytose debris.20 Therefore, we hypothesized that another non-professional phagocyte population contributes to efferocytosis in the developing OT.

In the OT, there is a population of glial cells called radial astroglia (or simply astroglia) that exhibit astrocyte-like properties such as blood-brain barrier control and association with synapses.21–23 Astrocytes and astrocyte-like cells can function as non-professional phagocytes.2,12,24,25 In mice, astrocytes assist microglia in clearing neurite debris and respond to dying cells when microglial activity is impaired.26 Therefore, we hypothesized that OT astroglia participate in neuronal clearance during this developmental window of high phagocytic load and low microglial activity.

In this study, we utilized live imaging to investigate the role of OT astroglia in the clearance of dying cells. We demonstrate that during physiological zebrafish development, OT astroglia form large spherical structures from their basal processes that surround and hold cell bodies, particularly those of dying neurons. These structures, which we call scyllate heads, are highly dynamic, often long lasting, and rarely acidic, distinguishing them from classically described phagosomes. Microglia subsequently interact with scyllate heads to remove contents from these compartments and complete the degradation process. Together, our findings uncover a previously undescribed role for OT astroglia and reveal highly coordinated interactions between professional and non-professional phagocytes during efferocytosis.

RESULTS

OT radial astroglia form scyllate heads during a critical developmental stage

In the zebrafish OT, RGC axons begin to innervate at approximately 60 hpf.14,15 Concurrently, there is an increase in OT neuronal death between 60 and 72 hpf.2,16,19 To observe how astroglia behave during this critical point in development, we imaged the OT of Tg (slc1a3b:myrGFP-P2A-H2AmCherry) larvae from a sagittal view between 55 and 84 hpf for 6–12 h (Figures 1A and S1A). This transgene uses regulatory sequences for slc1a3b, which encodes the protein glutamate astrocyte specific transporter (GLAST), an established astroglia marker, to drive the expression of membrane-bound GFP and nuclear-localized mCherry.27 In these time-lapses, we observed OT astroglia form large spherical structures, which emerged from small branches off their basal processes (Figures 1A and 1B; Video S1). In our imaging studies, we observed many of these membranous structures forming en masse, often moving around dynamically at the end of thin, neck-like projections before dissipating. Due to their striking morphology and the consistency with which we observed them in nearly all stage-matched time-lapses, we sought to investigate the nature of these astroglial structures.

To confirm that the emergence of these structures was not due to the transgene itself, we generated three stable slc1a3b-driven transgenic lines: a myristoylated membrane-bound Tg(slc1a3b: myrEGFP), a CAAX-localized membrane-bound Tg(slc1a3b: TagRFP-CAAX), and a cytoplasmic Tg(slc1a3b:mCerulean). We then imaged larvae from each transgenic line in time-lapses between 55 and 84 hpf (Figure S1B). In all these transgenic lines, we observed radial astroglia form similar structures.

Early in development, radial glia act as neural and glial progenitors,17,18 so we considered the possibility that these structures contained recent astroglial progeny. Tg(slc1a3b:myrGFP-P2A-H2AmCherry) labels both membranes (myrGFP) and nuclei (H2AmCherry) of astroglial cells.27 The spherical structures observed in our time-lapses did not contain the mCherry nuclear marker, indicating that they were not astroglial cell bodies (Figure S1C). Additionally, these structures usually emerged from the basal process near the middle of the OT rather than the area near the ventricle, where the astroglial cell bodies reside. Most notably, we did not observe astroglial nuclei dividing during our time-lapses. Therefore, we conclude that these structures are not a result of astroglial proliferation.

Due to the known role of astroglia in blood-brain barrier control,16–18 we also investigated the possibility that these structures interact with blood vessels. We imaged Tg(slc1a3b: TagRFP-CAAX);Tg(fli1a:EGFP) transgenic larvae from 55 to 67 hpf to visualize both astroglial membranes and blood vessels. While astroglial membranes do surround blood vessels in tubelike formations, the spherical structures we observed were not associated with blood vessels (Figure S1D).

After these initial investigations and a thorough examination of existing literature, we conclude that our observations represent a previously undescribed behavior of OT astroglia. To discuss these structures concisely while avoiding incorrectly labeling them, we gave them a new name. Many of the structures form as mouth-like cups on neck-like branches (Figure 1B), so we called them scyllate heads after Scylla, a cliff-dwelling, manyheaded monster in Homer’s “Odyssey” who eats sailors from passing ships. For the purposes of our study, we defined scyllate heads as spherical or hemispherical (cup-like) structures formed by astroglial membranes.

In addition to the OT, we observed scyllate-head-like structures in areas such as the torus semicircularis, cerebellum, and spinal cord (Figure S1E). However, the spontaneous formation of these structures outside of the OT is difficult to predict and investigate. Therefore, we focused our investigation on the developing OT, where scyllate head formation was reproducible and stereotyped.

Scyllate head formation and dissipation are dynamic and highly variable

To characterize scyllate heads, we analyzed Tg(slc1a3b: myrGFP-P2A-H2AmCherry) larvae in 6- or 12-h time-lapses between 54 and 86 hpf. For most of our imaging, we focused on the most lateral 40 μm of the OT, the lateral edge being the first z plane in which we clearly identified astroglial basal processes (Figure S1A). However, we observed scyllate heads deeper in the OT (Figure S1F), indicating that this phenomenon is not limited to a specific OT layer or region.

In our imaging, we observed variation in scyllate head characteristics. For example, the number of scyllate heads present varied with the age of the larvae. To create a timeline of scyllate head formation, we counted the number of scyllate heads at specific stages between 42 and 100 hpf (Figure 1C). We began at 42 hpf because we first observed OT expression of Tg(slc1a3b: myrGFP-P2A-H2AmCherry) at approximately 40 hpf. From this analysis, we found that the peak of scyllate head formation was approximately 68 hpf and gradually decreased over the next 24 h (Figure 1C). By 100 hpf, we rarely observed scyllate heads.

Morphologically, the average diameter of a scyllate head was 5.73 ± 1.27 μm (Figure 1D). This is similar to the average size of a nucleus.28 We also assayed how long scyllate heads were maintained after formation by determining the difference between the time point of a scyllate head’s first appearance and its dissipation, which we call the duration. Occasionally, we observed scyllate heads already present when we began imaging or that were still present by the end of imaging, meaning that the measured duration is shorter than the actual duration for these scyllate heads. From our analysis, the average duration of a scyllate head was 172 ± 146 min (Figure 1E). The duration ranged from 0 (single time point) to 710 (entire time-lapse) min. The median duration was 130 min. We conclude that scyllate heads vary in duration and skew toward shorter durations but can be long lasting.

To better visualize scyllate head dynamics, we mosaically labeled astroglia by injecting embryos with slc1a3b:myrGFP-P2A-H2AmCherry plasmid. We then captured the emergence of scyllate heads from the basal processes of single astroglia (Figure 1F; Video S2). In this representative time-lapse beginning at 57 hpf, we observed small projections emitting from a main astroglial basal process, followed by scyllate head formation at 62 hpf (+4:30). The scyllate head was present for 320 min before suddenly dissipating at 67 hpf (+9:50). Surprisingly, the scyllate head appeared to burst open before reincorporating into the basal process rather than being transported to the cell body or pruned by another cell. We saw this method of dissipation in other examples of scyllate heads, including in stable lines. Some dissipating scyllate heads appeared to open and collapse back into the membrane more gradually, while others suddenly enlarged before similarly collapsing.

Using mosaic labeling of astroglia, we also analyzed the behavior of individual scyllate heads. We saw that some scyllate heads appeared directly in contact with the basal process (Figure 1F), while others formed on the ends of thin, neck-like branches that protruded from the basal process (Figures 1B and S1G; Video S2). We also imaged astroglia that did not form any scyllate heads during the imaging window (data not shown). In contrast, some astroglia formed and maintained multiple scyllate heads simultaneously (Figure S1G). These findings indicate that scyllate head formation is not a one-to-one process and is highly dynamic and variable. This variability in individual behavior but consistent timing of overall formation led us to hypothesize that scyllate heads form in response to an extrinsic cue.

RGC axon infiltration and visual input are dispensable for scyllate head formation

Scyllate head formation occurs in the OT while RGCs are creating synaptic connections with OT neurons. Therefore, we considered the possibility that vision and/or RGC axon infiltration may play a role in scyllate head formation. To determine if the presence of RGC axons (and subsequent visual input) affected scyllate head formation, we utilized lakritz (lak) mutant fish. Lak mutants have a missense mutation in atonal bHLH transcription factor 7 (atoh7, also called ath5), a transcription factor important for RGC differentiation.29,30 Without atoh7 function, RGC axons do not develop or infiltrate the OT. Therefore, lak−/− larvae are completely blind.29 We imaged Tg(atoh7:mRFP);Tg(slc1a3b: myrGFP-P2A-H2AmCherry) larvae from 51 to 67 hpf to capture the stage where RGC axons infiltrate and scyllate heads begin to form. In lak+/− and lak+/+ larvae, we observed RGC axons innervating the OT (Figure 2A; Video S3). In lak−/− larvae, while mRFP was expressed in the retina, we saw no RGC axons in the OT (Figure 2A; Video S3). Despite the lack of RGC axon infiltration and vision, we still observed scyllate head formation in lak−/− larvae (Figure 2A; Video S3), and there was no difference in the appearance, timing, or number of scyllate heads between lak+/? and lak−/− sibling larvae (Figures 2A′ and 2B). Therefore, though RGC axon infiltration and scyllate head formation occur in the same developmental window, we conclude that RGC axon infiltration and visual input are dispensable for scyllate head formation.

Scyllate heads contain the cell bodies of dying neurons

In our imaging, we observed that scyllate heads often began as cup-like structures that eventually became fully enclosed, much like phagocytic cups made by professional phagocytes. Therefore, we hypothesized that scyllate heads surround and perhaps even engulf something in the environment. Because scyllate heads are large enough to accommodate a cell body (Figure 1D), we looked at other cells as the potential contents. Major cell types in the OT between 60 and 72 hpf include oligodendrocyte progenitor cells, neurons, and microglia.31,32 There is a large increase in the PCD of neurons at 60 hpf,16,17,19 the same stage at which scyllate heads begin to form, and previous studies show that astroglia interact with neurons in various ways.21,33,34 Therefore, we hypothesized that scyllate heads contained neurons. To investigate this possibility, we generated the transgenic line Tg(elavl3:H2B-GCaMP6s), in which elavl3+ neurons express the calcium indicator GCaMP6s localized to the nucleus, where fluorescence intensity increases when calcium concentration increases.35 Variation in GCaMP6s fluorescence intensity allowed us to identify individual neuronal nuclei. We also labeled astroglial membranes with Tg(slc1a3b:TagRFP-CAAX) and imaged from 60 to 84 hpf in 6- or 12-h time-lapses. Intriguingly, we observed that scyllate heads often contained bright GCaMP+ neuronal nuclei (87.0% ± 8.4%, n = 6 fish, Figures 3A, 3B, and S2A). Therefore, we conclude that scyllate heads surround and contain OT neuronal soma.

Many scyllate heads in these time-lapses first surrounded neurons with a dim GCaMP signal that eventually became bright while within the scyllate head during imaging (Figure S2B). 55% ± 26% of neurons within scyllate heads shifted from GCaMPdim to GCaMPbright after being surrounded, while the other 45% ± 26% were surrounded when they were already GCaMPbright (n = 6 fish). Due to this variability, it is unlikely that calcium activity alone induces scyllate head formation. Instead, these GCaMPbright neurons may represent influxes of calcium that are associated with cell death.36,37 We also observed scyllate heads surrounding neuronal nuclei that shrank during the imaging window (Figure S2C). We suspected that this could be pyknosis or chromatin condensation, a phenomenon where nuclear contents compact during cell death.36,38,39 During OT development, there is an increase in neuronal PCD2,16 concurrent with the period of scyllate head formation (Figure 1C). Therefore, we hypothesized that the neurons held by scyllate heads were dead or dying.

To confirm that the nuclear shrinkage we observed was indeed pyknosis by apoptotic cells, we live stained Tg (slc1a3b:TagRFP-CAAX) larvae at 72 hpf with the DNA-labeling dye acridine orange (AO) and imaged them immediately following treatment (Figure S2D). AO labels condensed DNA in early-stage apoptotic cells, indicated by bright AO fluorescence.40 In these larvae, we observed that 41.9% ± 16.9% of the scyllate heads present held AO+ contents (n = 12 fish, Figures S2D–S2E). This is consistent with our observations of GCaMP+ neurons undergoing nuclear shrinkage after being surrounded by scyllate heads (Figure S2C) and suggests that scyllate heads may surround cells in response to an early apoptotic cue. Overall, these data show that scyllate heads can contain apoptotic cells but do not confirm that all scyllate head contents are dying.

To directly show that scyllate heads surround dying neurons, we imaged Tg(slc1a3b:TagRFP-CAAX);Et(s1101:Gal4);Tg(UAS: secA5-YFP) larvae from 60 to 84 hpf in 6- or 12 h time-lapses. The s1101 enhancer trap line labels many neurons, including a large subset of OT neurons at 60 hpf.41,42 This line drives the expression of a secreted annexin V (secA5), which binds to phosphatidylserine (PS) exposed on the outer membrane of dying cells,43 effectively labeling dead or dying s1101+ neurons (secA5-YFP+). In our imaging, we observed scyllate heads surrounding secA5-YFP+ neuronal cell bodies (Figure 3C; Video S4). Of the neurons that became secA5-YFP+, 75.7% ± 5.0% were surrounded by scyllate heads during our 12-h time-lapses (n = 15 fish, Figure 3D). These data demonstrate that scyllate heads surround the majority of dying neurons before they are cleared.

To determine how quickly astroglia recognized dying neurons, we quantified the time from when a neuron became secA5-YFP+ to when it was engaged by a scyllate head, which we defined as when a cup-like projection from an astroglia contacted the neuron that it later surrounded. From our imaging, we observed that in most events, neurons were engaged by a scyllate head less than 10 min after becoming secA5-YFP+ (47/94) and in almost all cases (93/94) were engaged within 1 h (n = 94 events from 8 fish; Figures 3E and 3F). Together, these data demonstrate that scyllate heads robustly and quickly surround dying neurons.

Radial astroglia form scyllate heads in response to injury

Scyllate head formation coincided with the onset of developmental cell death, and the number of scyllate heads drastically decreased after the clearance of debris was completed. Therefore, we hypothesized that cell death outside of this physiological window would lead to scyllate head formation and created an injury paradigm to test this hypothesis.

We imaged Tg(slc1a3b:TagRFP-CAAX);Tg(elavl3:H2B-GCa MP6s) larvae for 30 min beginning at 96–100 hpf, after the physiological wave of cell death is cleared and scyllate head formation is rare (Figure 1C). We then performed cavitation injuries using a femtosecond pulse laser. Cavitation bubbles occur when laser ablation evaporates liquid in tissue, creating an air bubble that damages surrounding tissue and causes cell death.44,45 We then imaged for 1 h. Following injury, neurons surrounding the cavitation injury often expressed sustained bright GCaMP6s fluorescence, similar to previously described scyllate head targets (Figure 4A). In most injured larvae, astroglial processes quickly elaborated within the cavitation site (Figure 4A; Video S5). We observed astroglial processes surrounding GCaMPbright neurons in scyllate head-like compartments (Figure 4B). We counted scyllate heads containing GCaMPbright neurons within the OT over the time-lapses pre- and post-injury and then calculated the change in scyllate head number (Figure 4C). We found that the number of scyllate heads increased dramatically (by 5.5 ± 2.7) after injury, significantly higher than the stable number (0.5 ± 1.6 increase) observed in uninjured controls (n = 8 fish per condition, p = 0.0009, Figure 4C). These data show that injury induces OT astroglia to form scyllate heads around neuronal cell bodies.

Scyllate heads rarely exhibit phagosomal characteristics

Our data demonstrate that scyllate heads respond to and surround dying neurons. Therefore, we sought to investigate their involvement in the phagocytic clearance of neurons. In several studies, phagocytic engulfment is defined as the internalization of debris into the cell body.8,46 In time-lapses of Tg(slc1a3b: TagRFP-CAAX);Et(s1101:Gal4);Tg(UAS:secA5-YFP) larvae from 60 to 84 hpf, we tracked secA5-YFP+ neuronal cell bodies surrounded by scyllate heads. We defined scyllate head contents as “internalized” when the secA5-YFP+ debris contacted an astroglial cell body without a boundary formed by the scyllate head (Figure 5A). We found that only 4.8% ± 5.9% of neuronal debris within scyllate heads was transported to the astroglial cell body and internalized (n = 15 fish, Figure 5B). Most scyllate heads remained distant from the cell body.

Another aspect of efferocytosis is the degradation of contents via phagolysosomal pathways.4,39 To determine if scyllate heads proceeded along phagolysosomal pathways, we generated a Tg (slc1a3b:mCherry-2xFYVE) transgenic line. This line drives astroglial expression of mCherry with two FYVE domains, which bind to phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PI(3)P), an early endosome phospholipid transferred to phagolysosomes through fusion events.47,48 We imaged Tg(slc1a3b:myrEGFP);Tg(slc1a3b: mCherry-2xFYVE) larvae from 75 to 87 hpf and assayed whether scyllate heads were labeled by mCherry-2xFYVE. In these studies, only a small subset of scyllate heads became mCherry-2xFYVE+ (16.4% ± 12.6%, n = 20 fish, Figures 5C and 5D).

Another hallmark of degradation is the acidification of phagolysosomes. To determine if scyllate heads acidified, we treated either Tg(slc1a3b:TagRFP-CAAX);Tg(elavl3:H2B-GCaMP6s) or Tg(slc1a3b:myrGFP-P2A-H2AmCherry) larvae at 54 or 72 hpf with 10 μM LysoTracker deep red, a pH indicator used to label lysosomes and phagolysosomes,49 for 1 h and then imaged them for 6 or 12 h. Consistent with our PI(3)P data, we observed that only 17.7% ± 7.2% of scyllate heads became LysoTracker+ (n = 12 fish, Figures 5E and 5F). Together, these data demonstrate that scyllate heads can take on phagolysosomal characteristics but rarely do so.

Since astroglia can phagocytose neurites and other small neuronal debris,26,50 we considered that LysoTracker+ scyllate heads may surround non-soma debris that may be easier to degrade. Contrary to this hypothesis, in our time-lapses of LysoTracker-treated Tg(slc1a3b:TagRFP-CAAX);Tg(elavl3:H2B-GCaMP6s) larvae, all scyllate heads that became LysoTracker+ contained GCaMP+ nuclei (7/7 scyllate heads from 3 fish, Figure 5G). We conclude that scyllate heads can acidify when holding neuronal cell bodies.

To better understand the dynamics and fate of acidified scyllate heads, we further analyzed our time-lapses. Most acidified scyllate heads became and remained acidic while distant from the cell body (Figure 5E). Of these acidified scyllate heads, only a few (9/54 scyllate heads from 12 fish) moved to the astroglial soma for internalization. On average, scyllate heads became LysoTracker+ within 113 ± 97 min of observed formation, with the time to acidification ranging from 0 to 490 min (n = 54 scyllate heads from 12 fish, Figure 5H). Additionally, we observed nonacidified scyllate heads that were long lasting, with durations up to 690 min (Figure 5I). This suggests that acidification does not necessarily depend on how long the scyllate head contains its cargo. On average, the total duration of LysoTracker+ scyllate heads (n = 54) was 279 ± 146 compared to 134 ± 105 min in LysoTracker− scyllate heads (n = 226) in the same fish (Figure 5I). Additionally, a greater proportion of acidified scyllate heads was present at the end of the time-lapse compared to non-acidified scyllate heads (48% vs. 18%, n = 54 and 266 scyllate heads from 12 fish, respectively). Overall, acidified scyllate heads last longer and dissipate less often in our time-lapses, suggesting that scyllate heads are ineffective at degrading contents and do not usually dissipate via this mechanism.

Microglia interact with scyllate heads and remove their contents

Having observed that scyllate heads are infrequently acidic, rarely internalized, and usually dissipate in a “bursting” or “collapsing” manner, we sought to further investigate the fate of scyllate heads and their contents. According to existing literature, most CNS developmental efferocytosis is performed by microglia.16,46,51,52 By the time of robust scyllate head formation at 60 hpf, a relatively low number of microglia have infiltrated the OT.16,18 Therefore, we sought to determine if and how microglia and astroglia interact during debris clearance in this developmental window.

Microglia are extremely motile when responding to debris, so to capture microglial dynamics, we imaged Tg(slc1a3b:myrGFP-P2A-H2AmCherry);Tg(mfap4:mTurquoise) larvae from 77 to 79 hpf in 110-s time steps. In our time-lapses, we observed multiple instances of microglia interacting with scyllate heads. In the representative time-lapse shown, a microglial process contacts a scyllate head and then enters and forms its own engulfment compartment inside the scyllate head (Figures 6A and 6A′). The microglial process then retracts, and the scyllate head soon dissipates. We hypothesize that microglia remove contents from scyllate heads, leading to scyllate head dissipation.

To directly visualize microglia removing neuronal debris from scyllate heads, we used Tg(slc1a3b:TagRFP-CAAX);Tg(elavl3: H2B-GCaMP6s);Tg(mfap4:mTurquoise) larvae to label astroglia, microglia, and neurons simultaneously. We imaged larvae between 73 and 78 hpf in 2-h time-lapses with 90–120 s time steps. In these time-lapses, we observed instances of neuron transfer from scyllate heads to microglia, which we call “transfer events.” In the representative time-lapse shown, a microglial cell contacts a scyllate head that contains a GCaMP+ neuron (Figure 6B). The microglial cell then infiltrates the scyllate head and surrounds the neuron with its own membrane processes. The microglial cell then retreats from the scyllate head, taking the neuron with it. During this transfer event, the scyllate head rapidly dissipates (Figure 6B). Another representative transfer event, shown in Video S6, follows a similar process. On average, we observed 3.6 ± 1.1 transfer events per fish during our 2-h time-lapses (n = 5 fish). Our observations of glial-glial debris transfer events are supported by a recent study of the zebrafish retina, where dead cells undergoing phagocytosis by Müller glia were often transferred to microglia for degradation.46

To characterize the transfer events we observed, we tracked individual scyllate heads contacted by microglia throughout our time-lapses (Figure 6C). The time from scyllate head formation to the point when a microglial cell first contacted the scyllate head, which we called microglial engagement, varied greatly, ranging from 0 to 146 min (median = 21 min, average = 35 ± 39 min, n = 18 scyllate heads from 5 fish). In most cases, the time from microglial engagement to neuron engulfment and subsequent scyllate head dissipation was short (median = 6 min, average = 14 ± 19 min), with a few notable exceptions (Figure 6C). Of the 19 scyllate heads we tracked and observed to dissipate during our time-lapses, 18 dissipated after a microglial cell infiltrated and removed the neuronal contents. Taking these results together, we conclude that most scyllate heads dissipate after microglia invade and remove their contents.

We further investigated the manner of scyllate head dissipation after a transfer event. In our imaging, microglial compartments appeared to form inside scyllate heads and only surrounded the contained neuronal debris rather than forming around the entire scyllate head (Figures 6A′ and 6Bii). We did not observe scyllate head debris within the microglial compartment containing the transferred neuron after removal (Figure 6Biii). We note that in our time-lapse ofa single astroglia, the dissipating scyllate head appeared to burst and be reabsorbed into the basal process (Figure 1F; Video S2). If the scyllate head in this time-lapse were pruned by a microglial cell, we would expect to see scyllate head debris detach and move away from the basal process, which we did not observe. Our data are consistent with the hypothesis that microglia infiltrate scyllate heads to remove their contents and do not prune the entire scyllate head structure.

Interestingly, in our 6- to 12-h time-lapses of 55–84 hpf Tg(slc1a3b:myrGFP-P2A-H2AmCherry);Tg(mfap4:mTurquoise) larvae treated with LysoTracker, we did not observe any transfer events between microglia and LysoTracker+ scyllate heads. This is consistent with our observations that acidified scyllate heads are longer lasting and dissipate less often during our time-lapses (Figures 5H and 5I). However, the temporal resolution of these time-lapses and the rarity of scyllate head acidification limit our ability to assay these events. Further work is needed to determine if microglia interact with acidified scyllate heads. Taking these results together, we conclude that the majority of scyllate heads are contacted by microglia, which infiltrate scyllate heads and remove neuronal debris for terminal degradation, resulting in scyllate head dissipation. These data reveal cooperation between OT astroglia and microglia in the clearance of dying neurons during development.

Depletion of microglia leads to longer-lasting scyllate heads

We hypothesized that microglia remove contents from astroglia that would otherwise not be effectively degraded and that transfer events are important for scyllate head dissipation and dead cell clearance. From this hypothesis, we predicted that loss of microglia would lead to longer-lasting scyllate heads. Therefore, we sought to deplete microglia and observe the effects on scyllate head duration.

To deplete microglia, we utilized the nitroreductase-ronidazole (NTR-RDZ) ablation system, wherein fish are treated with the drug RDZ, which does not cause cell death alone53 but, when combined with a transgenic line that drives expression of NTR, induces cell death.54 We treated Tg(slc1a3b:TagRFP-CAAX);Tg(mpeg1:Gal4);Tg(UAS:YFP-NTR2.0) larvae with 100 μM RDZ for 24 h before and throughout imaging, leading to the depletion of mpeg1+ cells (Figure 7A). Mpeg1 is a marker for macrophages and microglia, so we refer to the RDZ-treated condition as “microglia depleted.” We then imaged RDZ-treated and untreated larvae from 75 to 87 hpf following microglial depletion and tracked individual scyllate heads to determine their duration. In microglia-depleted larvae, the average scyllate head duration was significantly longer than in untreated larvae (286 ± 91 min in RDZ-treated larvae vs. 135 ± 27 min in control larvae, n = 11 fish per condition, p = 0.0002; Figure 7B). Additionally, the distribution of durations was significantly different between the control and microglia-depleted conditions, revealing a population shift toward longer durations (Figure 7C). Our data demonstrate that microglia are important for scyllate head dissipation and that when microglia are depleted, scyllate heads continue to hold their contents. This is consistent with our hypothesis that microglia and astroglia cooperate to clear neuronal debris during OT development.

To determine the impacts of prolonged microglia depletion without inflammation from the NTR-RDZ system, we utilized csf1ra mutant zebrafish. Csf1r (colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor) is necessary for the proliferation and migration of microglia into the CNS,55 with csf1ra being the primary paralog during early developmental stages in zebrafish.55,56 We imaged csf1ra−/− ; Tg(slc1a3b:myrGFP-P2A-H2AmCherry);Tg(mfap4:mTurquoise) larvae and their heterozygous or wild-type (csf1ra+/?) siblings in 6- to 12-h time-lapses and created a timeline of the number of scyllate heads present in the OT from 56 to 108 hpf (Figures 7D–7F). We observed no significant difference in the initial emergence of scyllate heads from 56 to 64 hpf across all genotypes (Figure 7F). However, from 76 hpf onward, csf1ra−/− larvae had significantly more scyllate heads compared to their csf1ra+/? siblings (Figures 7E and 7F). Additionally, in csf1ra+/? larvae, there were very few scyllate heads present at 100 hpf (Figure 7F). However, in csf1ra−/− larvae, the number of scyllate heads remained high (Figures 7E and 7F). This is consistent with Casano et al.’s conclusion that dead cells accumulate in the OT of microglia-depleted larvae after 72 hpf.16

We hypothesized that in the absence of microglia, more scyllate heads would become degradative. To test this hypothesis, we treated larvae with LysoTracker as described above and found a nonsignificant increase in LysoTracker+ scyllate heads in csf1ra−/− larvae compared to csf1ra+/? siblings between 76 and 84 hpf (Figure 7G). We also found that the proportion of LysoTracker+ scyllate heads in csf1ra−/− larvae between 100 and 108 hpf was not significantly different from that in csf1ra+/? siblings between 75 and 87 hpf (data not shown). This suggests that astroglia do not significantly increase their phagocytic capability when microglia are impaired, despite being present at later stages in development.

Finally, we hypothesized that scyllate heads persisted longer because they failed to dissipate without trade events with microglia. Therefore, we calculated the duration of scyllate heads in csf1ra−/− larvae compared to csf1ra+/? siblings in time-lapses from 75 to 87 hpf. Unexpectedly, though the duration average and distribution observed in csf1ra−/− larvae were comparable to those of RDZ-treated larvae (Figures 7B, 7C, 7H–7I), we found a nonsignificant increase in scyllate head duration in csf1ra−/− larvae compared to csf1ra+/? siblings (Figures S3B and S3C). We noticed that the csf1ra+/? larvae had an increased average duration and a shifted duration distribution (Figure S3B) compared to our previous control duration results (Figure 1E). Oosterhof et al. reported that csf1ra+/− larvae have an intermediate number of OT microglia at 5 dpf compared to csf1ra+/+ and csf1ra−/− larvae.55 We therefore sorted our data by csf1ra+/+ and csf1ra+/− genotype. When we analyzed our data this way, we observed a significant difference in the duration distributions of csf1ra+/+ compared to csf1ra+/− and csf1ra−/− siblings but not csf1ra+/− and csf1ra−/− siblings (Figure 7I). The average durations by genotype were not significantly different but trended toward longer durations as the csf1ra mutant copy number increased (Figure 7H). Notably, csf1ra+/− larvae did not have elevated scyllate head numbers at later time points, so any potential phenotype appears to be resolved by 100 hpf, unlike in csf1ra−/− larvae.

Overall, our data show that scyllate heads in microglia-depleted larvae fail to dissipate in a timely manner and do not significantly increase their own phagocytic ability. This leads to an accumulation of scyllate heads in the OT that persist past the physiological window of scyllate head activity.

DISCUSSION

In this study, using in vivo time-lapse imaging, we show that radial astroglia in the developing zebrafish OT respond en masse to dying neurons during a wave of neuronal PCD by surrounding and holding neuronal cell bodies in structures we call scyllate heads, revealing a previously undescribed developmental phenomenon in the OT. We show that scyllate heads are commonly contacted by microglia, leading to the transfer of scyllate head contents to the microglial cell. Our research reveals that astroglia actively participate and coordinate with microglia during the response to OT developmental neuronal cell death.

OT radial astroglia as “first responders”

Previous studies describe astrocytes and astrocyte-like cells as non-professional phagocytes that, in the absence of microglia, will increase their phagocytic capacity to partially compensate.8,12,26 Under homeostasis, mouse astrocytes prune synapses50 and engulf neurite debris from dying neurons.26 However, whether astrocyte-like cells participate in whole-cell clearance under homeostatic conditions is unclear.10,26 In our studies, we observed OT astroglia identify and surround neuronal cell bodies during physiological development, even in the presence of microglia. Occasionally, we observed scyllate heads acidify and/or internalize into the astroglial soma, revealing that OT astroglia can phagocytose neuronal cell bodies. However, despite responding to the majority of dying neurons, most scyllate heads do not terminally degrade contents, leading us to wonder why there is such a robust astroglial response to neuronal cell death.

Most studies on efferocytosis focus on the cells that terminally degrade apoptotic cells. This approach may have limited identification of non-professional phagocyte contributions to homeostatic cell clearance. For example, a study on non-professional phagocytosis briefly described mouse BHK cells in vitro surrounding apoptotic cells by forming “a long process” that “mov[ed the apoptotic cell] about.”8 This description resembles a scyllate head. However, this study did not consider the BHK cells “phagocytic” until internalization hours later, nor did it elaborate on this pre-phagocytic behavior.8 By expanding our understanding of participation in cell clearance, our study and others have begun to see non-professional phagocytes as first responders to cell death rather than bystanders that only react when professional phagocytes fail.

Here, we propose that OT astroglia form scyllate heads as an intermediary compartment to hold neurons until they are cleared by microglia. The purpose of an intermediary compartment, especially one as dynamic and likely metabolically costly as a scyllate head, remains unanswered. We hypothesize that scyllate heads are numerous and long lasting at this stage in OT development because the microglia present are unable to effectively clear the large number of neurons undergoing PCD. Because radial astroglial processes span the OT, they are in close proximity to neurons and thus well poised to quickly respond to neuronal death. Scyllate head formation decreases after 4 dpf, possibly because there are more microglia able to efficiently clear the fewer apoptotic neurons. If dead cells are not cleared efficiently, they begin to secrete inflammatory and cytotoxic cues.57,58 Neurons in particular can become excitotoxic, leading to secondary cell death if not resolved.59 Therefore, we hypothesize that scyllate heads form to sequester dying neurons, preventing dead cells from harming nearby healthy neurons until microglia can remove the debris for degradation.

Besides debris sequestration, there could be other benefits to astroglial involvement in cell clearance. Mouse mammary epithelial cells acting as non-professional phagocytes secrete the anti-inflammatory cytokine transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) after engulfing apoptotic cells,7 and in vitro astrocyte phagocytosis leads to a decrease in proinflammatory cytokine secretion.12 Astrocytes can influence microglial activity, for example, via calcium activity or, in mouse models of Alzheimer’s disease, through secretion of C3.10,60,61 Therefore, it is possible that the containment of apoptotic cells within scyllate heads could stimulate similar effects by astroglia. Additionally, scyllate heads form concurrently with increased PCD, and microglial infiltration into the OT is dependent on the amount of cell death at this time point,16,17,19 so scyllate heads may contribute to microglial invasion. An alternate interpretation of our data is that scyllate head encapsulation may be involved in the initiation or progression of apoptosis, as in phagoptosis.6 Our data show that most scyllate head contents are at a stage where they have exposed PS (secA5-YFP+) but have not undergone chromatin condensation (AO+), which may mean that scyllate heads respond to early signals from apoptotic or even pre-apoptotic cells. Astroglial phagoptosis has been suggested previously12,62 but requires further investigation in this context. While we show that scyllate heads form in response to injury, future work is needed to clarify the link between cell death and astroglial response by specifically targeting otherwise healthy neurons. Additionally, studies investigating if and how scyllate heads protect nearby neurons and if their loss leads to aberrant neural activity, increased death and inflammation, or changes in microglial activity are needed. To answer these questions, the field will need to further explore scyllate head formation and develop tools to specifically perturb them without otherwise impairing OT and astroglial function.

Transfer of cellular debris from radial astroglia to microglia

Recently, investigations into interactions between astroglia and microglia have revealed intimate communication between these two cell types. In the developing brains of both mice and rhesus monkeys, microglia closely contact and survey radial glial projections, though the purpose of the interaction is unclear.63,64 In the mouse hypothalamus, astrocytes and microglia coordinate during clearance of ablated neurons by partitioning the types of neuronal debris each one clears, with astroglia engulfing mostly neurite debris.26

In our study, we utilized in vivo time-lapse imaging in zebrafish to interrogate interactions between OT astroglial scyllate heads and microglia. We show that OT radial astroglia and microglia coordinate during the developmental clearance of apoptotic cells to transfer neuronal debris from scyllate heads to microglial phagosomes. To our knowledge, only one other study has identified a similar phenomenon of debris transfer between non-professional and professional phagocytes.46 In this study, Morales et al. observed the transfer of apoptotic debris from Müller glia to microglia during a wave of apoptosis in the developing zebrafish retina. In our work, we show that transfer events occur in the zebrafish OT and are not unique to Müller glia-microglia interactions. Our imaging studies further elucidate the cellular interactions involved in transfer events, such as the initial contact between the scyllate heads and microglia and subsequent “opening” of scyllate heads, as well as the specific type of debris involved (e.g., neuronal cell bodies). Our high-speed imaging reveals that scyllate heads are infiltrated by microglia rather than being pruned along with their contents, as reported by Morales et al.,46 indicating that microglia-scyllate head transfer events are highly coordinated and may require complex cell-cell communication. Considering that similar phenomena occur in these two different contexts, the brain and retina, we suspect that transfer events between professional and non-professional phagocytes may be a common occurrence during developmental cell clearance.

A model for professional and non-professional phagocyte cooperation

Based on our observations, we propose a model ofin vivo efferocytosis during OT development in which astroglia initially surround apoptotic neuronal targets in a temporary membranous holding compartment, which we call a scyllate head. Occasionally, scyllate heads become degradative, leading to terminal efferocytosis of neuronal cells by astroglia. However, in most cases, microglia contact, invade, and remove contents from scyllate heads for terminal degradation instead. Scyllate head dynamics are closely linked to microglial activity, as depleting microglia leads to longer-lasting scyllate heads. Combined with our observations of scyllate heads in other CNS regions and observations in other non-professional phagocytes such as zebrafish Müller glia46 and mouse BHK cells,8 we hypothesize that this process of initial compartmentalization of apoptotic cells by non-professional phagocytes and later transfer to professional phagocytes could be generalized to other biological contexts.

To better understand the contribution of astroglia to cell clearance, future studies and tools are needed to determine the cellular mechanisms involved in scyllate head formation, target recognition, and interactions with microglia. Known astroglial phagocytic receptors that may be involved in scyllate head target recognition include mertk/axl and megf10.2,25,26,46,50 As intermediate compartments surrounding cell bodies, scyllate heads could utilize different receptors and cues than astroglial phagosomes that engulf synapses and neurites. Microglia have a host of phagocytic receptors and can respond to transient ATP and calcium gradients induced after injury.2,65,66 One study shows microglia temporarily surrounding highly active neurons to modulate their activity.67 While astroglia do not necessarily respond to the same signals as microglia,12 scyllate head targets often exhibit elevated calcium levels, including after injury. This potential relationship to calcium activity requires further investigation. Even more mysterious is cell-cell communication between scyllate heads and microglial processes, including how microglia find and remove apoptotic debris from within a scyllate head. We observed variation in transfer event dynamics, including prolonged microglial-scyllate head contact, suggesting complex interactions. Finally, many studies have sought to identify what cues cause non-professional phagocytes to actively engulf and degrade debris. Parnaik et al. suggest that this switch is time dependent, while Magnus et al. suggest it is based on the amount of debris, but whether the change occurs in the apoptotic cell or the non-professional phagocyte is debated.7,8 Our findings suggest that scyllate head acidification is not wholly dependent on scyllate head duration. However, we observed terminal degradation by astroglia too rarely to make a conclusion. Additionally, LysoTracker+ scyllate heads did not dissipate due to microglial interaction. This could indicate an intrinsic change from an intermediate holding compartment to an acidified degradative compartment that precludes microglial interaction. Our work invites investigation into how non-professional phagocytes shift from first responders to active phagocytes under certain conditions and what cues may cause this change.

Limitations of the study

Here, we focus on describing scyllate heads and their significant characteristics. This broad approach leaves many details unexplored. We are interested in the relationship between scyllate heads and neuronal activity. Since our GCaMP imaging was not optimized for detailed fluorescence quantification, we were conservative in our conclusions. Another study focusing on neuronal activity is warranted. While we show that scyllate head formation is correlated to neuronal death and can be induced by injury, we did not show that it is directly caused by cell death, and we still have many questions about this relationship. Regarding our csf1ra data, we did not expect a heterozygous phenotype when designing the experiment. Further examination revealed that the split genotype data, despite the small sample size and nonsignificant results, were more consistent with those of previous studies and our own depletion data. Therefore, we include both datasets. We hope that our research inspires further exploration of these and other directions.

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Fish husbandry

All animal studies were approved by the University of Virginia Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Animals were housed at 28°C at a density of 8–10 fish/L and exposed to a 14/10 h light-dark cycle. Previously published and newly generated zebrafish strains used in this study are included in Table S1. Embryos were produced by pairwise natural matings and raised in egg water at 28.5°C. Larvae were staged according to hours postfertilization (hpf) and screened for desired transgenic fluorescence prior to use. Sex was not considered as a variable for experiments because sex is indeterminate in zebrafish prior to 25 dpf.

METHOD DETAILS

Generation of transgenic lines

Lines generated in this study include Tg(slc1a3b:mCerulean)uva84, Tg(slc1a3b:myrEGFP)uva74, Tg(elavl3:H2B-GCaMP6s)uva98, and Tg (slc1a3b:mCherry-2xFYVE;cry:mCherry)uva72Tg. Transgenic constructs were created using the Tol2kit Gateway-based cloning system.77 The slc1a3b-p5e plasmid was provided by Kelly Monk.27 The myrEFGP-pMe plasmid was provided by Laura Fontenas and produced from the slc1a3b:myrGFP-P2A-H2AmCherry plasmid provided by Kelly Monk.27 AB* embryos were injected with 20 ng/μL plasmid DNA and 100 ng/μL transposase mRNA at the one-cell stage. Embryos were screened for expression and positive embryos were grown to adulthood. Founders were determined by pairwise individual matings with AB* adults and progeny were screened for desired expression. To generate a stable line, outcrosses were performed for two generations.

Genotyping

Genotype of imaged embryos was determined post-imaging for relevant experiments. After imaging, larvae were individually removed from imaging dishes. DNA was extracted using HotSHOT lysis solutions and protocol.78 PCR amplification was performed using GoTaq Green Master Mix (Fisher) with corresponding primers found in the key resources table. Genotype of lakritz mutants was determined by performing a restriction digest on the PCR product with AcuI restriction enzyme (New England Biolabs). Genotype of csf1ra mutants was determined by performing a restriction digest on the PCR product with BssSI-v2 restriction enzyme (New England Biolabs).

Mosaic labeling of cells

One-cell embryos were injected with slc1a3b:myrGFP-P2A-H2AmCherry plasmid DNA (provided by Kelly Monk) as described in the “generation of transgenic lines” section. Larvae were screened for desired fluorescence and those with labeled astroglia were subsequently imaged.

In vivo imaging

Embryos were treated with 0.003% PTU at 24 hpf to reduce pigment formation and screened for transgenic fluorescence prior to imaging. For time-points before 56 hpf, embryos were manually dechorionated. Larvae were immobilized prior to imaging using diluted Tricaine (MS-222; Syndel/Western Chemical, ordered from The Pond Outlet; concentration varied by age). Larvae were then mounted on their sides in 0.8% low gelling point agarose in 4-well glass bottom 35 mm Petri dishes (Fisher, Greiner Bio-One) and covered with diluted Tricaine (concentration varied by age), unless otherwise noted. Imaging was performed on either Andor CSU-W1 Spinning Disc Confocal Microscope or Andor Dragonfly 200 Spinning Disc Confocal Microscope with iXon Ultra camera with 40×/1.10 objective (Andor/Oxford Instruments). Images were illuminated with 445 nm (mTurquoise, mCerulean), 488 nm (GFP, GCaMP6s, YFP, AO), 561 nm (mCherry, TagRFP), and/or 647 nm (LysoTracker) lasers. Time-lapse length and time-step varied according to the feasibility and needs of the experiment and is stated if relevant. Images were acquired as 40-μm z series with 1-μm step size unless otherwise stated. For some representative images, imaging was performed using frame averaging of 4 (Figure 3; Video S4).

Acridine Orange incorporation

Acridine Orange (AO) treatment was performed as described in Lyons et al., 2005.79 Briefly, larvae were immersed in a solution of 10 μg/mL AO (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) in the dark at 28.5°C for 20 min. Larvae were washed in egg water three times. Larvae were then immobilized and mounted for imaging as previously described. If necessary, embryos were dechorionated prior to treatment.

LysoTracker treatment

LysoTracker treatment was performed as described in Zhu et al. 2019.20 Briefly, larvae were immersed in 10 μM LysoTracker Deep Red (Invitrogen) in the dark at 25°C for 1 h. The larvae were rinsed with egg water three times and then immobilized and mounted for imaging as previously described. If necessary, embryos were dechorionated prior to treatment.

Cavitation injury

For cavitation injuries, larvae between 96 and 100 hpf were mounted for imaging as previously described. Larvae were imaged for 30 min prior to injury. After pre-injury imaging, cavitation injury was induced using the MicroPoint Illumination and Ablation System (Andor/Photonic Instruments) femtosecond pulse laser at 445 nm. Single point ROIs near the center of the optic tectum were selected and subjected to pulses at 50–70 power. ROIs were ablated until a cavitation bubble formed, between 1 and 10 repeats. Cavitation injury was performed on all non-control fish, then post-injury imaging was immediately started. Sibling control larvae were not injured but were mounted and imaged alongside injured fish. When necessary, 10 min between ‘pre-injury’ and ‘post-injury’ was added in control imaging to reflect time for administration of cavitation injury. Injured larvae that did not begin to close the injury site with astroglial membranes were excluded from analysis, as these fish died soon after injury.

Microglial depletion

To ablate microglia via nitroreductase-ronidazole (NTR-RDZ) treatment, Tg(mpeg1:Gal4);Tg(UAS:YFP-NTR2.0) larvae were used. Expression of YFP-NTR2.0 was confirmed prior to treatment. Egg water was replaced with 100 μM RDZ (Sigma) dissolved in PTU approximately 12 or 24 h prior to imaging. Control siblings were kept in PTU only. When it was not possible to screen larvae prior to RDZ treatment, positive larvae were identified on the confocal microscope by the presence of low mpeg1-expressing neurons in the spinal cord. Treated larvae remained in 100 μM RDZ throughout imaging. Ablation efficiency was assessed by counting the number of mpeg1+ cells in the imaging window throughout the time-lapse. The average number of mpeg1+ cells was 20.1 ± 9.8 cells in control larvae and 3.4 ± 2.1 in RDZ-treated larvae (n = 6 per condition). Therefore, ablation efficiency was approximately 83%. To confirm that RDZ treatment alone does not affect scyllate head duration, Tg(slc1a3b:TagRFP-CAAX);Tg(mpeg1:EGFP) larvae were treated with 100 μM RDZ and imaged as described. Average scyllate head duration in treated fish was not significantly different from that of untreated siblings (190.5 ± 48.5 min vs. 199.6 ± 62.6 min respectively, p = 0.75, n = 8 fish per condition; Kolmogorov-Smirnov D = 0.095, p = 0.60).

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Image analysis and quantification

Time-lapse images were analyzed using Imaris software (Version 10.2.1). To standardize analysis, only astroglia in the dorsal anterior side of the OT (i.e., those projecting through the SPV and neuropil, see Figure S1A) were considered for analysis. Scyllate heads were identified by assessing individual z-planes for a spherical or hemi-spherical shape and evaluating nearby time points for characteristic formation and movement. In Tg(slc1a3b:myrGFP-P2A-H2AmCherry) larvae, spherical shapes containing mCherry+ nuclei were not considered scyllate heads regardless of position to avoid confusion with astroglial cell bodies.

For Figures 1C, 2B, and 7F, we examined frames at predetermined time-points from time-lapses and counted the number of scyllate heads visible in that frame. We examined 2–3 time-points per time-lapse, based on which time-points were covered by the timelapse. The n indicated in each of these experiments refers to all fish used for the experiment. The number of fish used for each quantification varies by time-point and condition from 3 to 13 fish.

The “spots” tool was used to track individual scyllate heads manually. For analysis of individual scyllate heads, spot tracks were numbered in order of appearance first and disappearance second. Similar procedures were followed for tracking secA5-YFP+ neurons (Figures 3C, 3D, and 5B).

Scyllate head size (Figures 1D and S2C) was measured in Imaris using the distance tool. Scyllate heads were measured at the timepoint and single z-plane at which they appeared largest by eye.

Scyllate head duration (Figures 1E, 5I, 7B, 7C, 7H, 7I, and S3A–S3C) was calculated as the difference between the first frame and last frame that the scyllate head could be identified multiplied by the time-step between frames. Scyllate heads present at the beginning of the time-lapse were counted as appearing at the first frame and scyllate heads present at the end of the time-lapse were counted as dissipating at the last frame. To determine the time to scyllate head engagement (Figure 3F), we only considered neurons which became secA5-YFP+ during the time-lapse (i.e., did not begin secA5-YFP+) and were at some point surrounded by a scyllate head. The time to engagement was calculated as the difference between the frame in which the neuron became recognizably secA5-YFP+ and the frame in which a cup-like astroglial projection contacted the neuron, multiplied by the time-step. To determine time to acidification (Figure 5H), we only considered scyllate heads which became LysoTracker+ during the time-lapse. The time to acidification was calculated as the difference between the frame at which the scyllate head formed and the frame at which the scyllate head contained LysoTracker+ signal discernable above background, multiplied by the time-step.

In Figure 6C, microglial engagement was defined as the duration of contact between the microglia involved in the transfer event and the scyllate head. Microglial engulfment was defined as the timepoint that the microglia surrounded the neuron in a compartment.

When determining the proportion of scyllate heads that became GCaMP+ (Figure 3B), AO+ (Figure S2E), mCherry-2xFYVE (Figure 5D), and LysoTracker+ (Figure 5F), scyllate heads were tracked for their entire duration and examined for relevant characteristics throughout the time-lapse. Scyllate head contents were considered positive for a marker if fluorescence was discernibly above background. When determining the proportion of secA5-YFP+ neurons that were engaged by scyllate heads (Figure 3D) or internalized (Figure 5B), secA5-YFP+ neuron cell bodies were tracked throughout the entire duration of discernable fluorescence. Neurons were considered engaged by a scyllate head when they were contacted by cup-like astroglial membrane that would surround them later in the time-lapse. Those contained in scyllate heads were considered internalized if they were moved to an astroglial cell body and the scyllate head no longer formed a clear boundary around the secA5-YFP+ debris. To make analysis more feasible when determining the percentage of LysoTracker+ scyllate heads in Figure 5F, in fish with more than 30 scyllate heads, a random number generator was used to choose 30 of the total scyllate heads in that fish to examine for LysoTracker labeling.

In Figure 4C, only scyllate heads surrounding GCaMP+ neuronal nuclei were considered scyllate heads, to avoid confusion with the convoluted astroglial membrane structures that formed in the injury site. All scyllate heads with neuronal contents that formed in the OT were counted, regardless of proximity to the injury site, so that quantifications were comparable to uninjured controls.

Brightness and contrast of images were adjusted uniformly throughout all images, with each channel adjusted individually, to improve image clarity. In the insets in Figure 6A′ and 6Bi–iii, images were adjusted slightly from the original panels to improve clarity of specific features. Original, unprocessed images and images with Imaris analysis are available upon request.

Statistical methods

Prism software was used to perform statistical analyses of quantifications and create graphs. Prism analysis documents and raw quantification data are available upon request. Graph sizing and aesthetic presentation were adjusted in Illustrator without any changes made to the data shown. All individual data points used for graphical generation are shown. Error bars indicate standard deviation unless otherwise stated. Results are presented in mean ± standard deviation format unless otherwise stated. Sample size (n) is reported in the results, the figure, and figure legend where applicable. What n represents is specified in the results and figure legends. On figures where statistical tests were performed, asterisks were used to quickly indicate significance level. These are defined as follows: ns (not significant) for p > 0.05; * for p < 0.05; ** for p < 0.01; *** for p < 0.001; **** for p < 0.0001.

For Figure 2F, to elucidate the margin of error caused by the chosen time-step, data is portrayed as a histogram with 10-min intervals such that events with a time to engagement of 0 min were in the 0–9 min bin.

Unpaired t-tests with Welch’s correction were performed for data shown in Figures 4C, 7F, 7B, 7H, and S3B. In Figure 4C, statistical analysis was performed on the change in scyllate head number from pre-to post-injury, rather than the raw scyllate head numbers. In Figures 7B–7H and S3B, individual data points shown represent the average scyllate head duration of each fish, with the overall average per condition being the average of these averages. For further transparency, Figure S3A shows the individual scyllate head durations for each fish from each RDZ condition. Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests were performed for data shown in Figures 7C, 7I, and S3C.

Figures 7H and 7I shows data split by genotype. This experiment was originally designed without differentiation between csf1ra+/+ or csf1ra+/− larvae. Therefore, the data with the combined csf1ra+/? data is included in Figures S3B and S3C. Two larvae known to be csf1ra+/? due to the presence of RGC axons were unable to be genotyped and were thus excluded from the split data.
