Pregnane X receptor mitigates aristolochic acid–induced acute kidney injury via p53 ubiquitination
Authors: Qianfang Liu, Fei Deng, Anqun Chen, Yu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAristolochic acid (AA), commonly used in Chinese herbal medicine to treat various diseases, can cause acute kidney injury (AKI). The pregnane X receptor (PXR), a nuclear receptor, is involved in drug metabolism, carcinogenesis, inflammation, apoptosis, oxidative stress and energy metabolism. Here, we demonstrate that PXR plays a protective role in AA-induced AKI. First, PXR expression was dramatically decreased in mice and Boston University mouse proximal tubular (BUMPT) cells treated with AA. Overexpression of PXR in BUMPT cells alleviated apoptosis induced by AA in vitro. The specific agonist of PXR pregnenolone carbonitrile (PCN) relieved AA-induced AKI in mice, while the PXR inhibitor ketoconazole exacerbated the damage caused by AA in mice. Mechanistically, PXR bound to p53 in BUMPT cells and led to the ubiquitination and degradation of p53, thereby downregulating its expression. Taken as a whole, our data demonstrate that PXR may protect against AA-induced AKI by suppressing p53 expression.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI), a clinical syndrome characterized by a sudden decline in glomerular filtration rate within a short period, contributes to its high morbidity and mortality [1,2]. Importantly, studies have indicated a relatively high risk of AKI transitioning into chronic kidney disease (CKD)[3]. AKI can be caused by various pathological conditions such as sepsis, trauma, ischemia-reperfusion and nephrotoxic drugs [1,4]. Tubular epithelial cells are highly active in metabolism and are sensitive to hypoxia, sepsis and a variety of toxic compounds, damage to these cells represents the most common pathological feature in AKI caused by various reasons [5,6]. Despite increasing understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms of AKI, its incidence has been on the rise, and thus far there is no effective treatment [7].

Aristolochic acid (AA), found in species of Aristolochia and Asarum, is often used in Chinese herbal medicine to treat various diseases, such as hepatitis, upper respiratory infection, vaginitis, urinary tract infection, dysmenorrhea and eczema [8–10]. However, AA is nephrotoxic and can cause aristolochic acid nephropathy (AAN), leading to AKI or chronic kidney disease (CKD)[9,11]. Animal experiments have shown that a single high-dose AA administration could cause AKI with renal tubular necrosis, while repeated low-dose administration of AA could induce CKD with renal tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis [12,13]. AA primarily acts on renal tubular epithelial cells, facilitating tubular cell death by forming DNA adducts [9]. Previous studies have shown that the nephrotoxicity induced by AA was related to apoptosis, oxidative stress, inflammation and fibrosis [10,14]. Nevertheless, the mechanism by which AA causes nephrotoxicity remains incompletely understood; therefore therapy for AA-induced AKI is also largely limited.

The pregnane X receptor (PXR) belongs to the nuclear receptor superfamily [15]. Meanwhile, PXR is also a ligand-activated transcription factor and participates in metabolism of endogenous and exogenous compounds by regulating drug-metabolizing enzymes [16,17]. Besides, PXR also has a connection with carcinogenesis, apoptosis, inflammation, oxidative stress, energy metabolism [15,18–20]. Recently, PXR has received increasing attention in the context of kidney disease, and some studies have shown that PXR is linked to kidney diseases. Velenosi et al. provided evidence that PXR activity is decreased in a CKD model induced by 5/6 nephrectomy in rats [21]. Watanabe et al. found that the expression of PXR was upregulated in diabetic nephropathy [22]. In addition, PXR could ameliorate AKI caused by cisplatin or ischemia/reperfusion (I/R). While the specific roles and underlying mechanisms of PXR in renal diseases largely remain unclear [23,24].

The tumor suppressor protein p53, a regulator of apoptosis [25], has been widely studied in various diseases. P53 is induced under stress conditions, involved in cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, senescence and DNA repair [26,27]. P53 plays an essential role in acute and chronic kidney disease [28]. Studies have shown that proximal renal tubule p53 knockout mice could attenuate renal pathological damage and cell apoptosis in cisplatin- and I/R- induced AKI [29]. In addition, the suppression of p53 signaling pathway decreases tubular cell apoptosis in septic AKI induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [30]. Moreover, in AA induced AKI, p53 promotes renal tubular cell apoptosis [13]. In chronic kidney disease, Yuan Ying et al. showed p53 deficiency in renal proximal tubule attenuated renal fibrosis after IRI [31]. Furthermore, inhibition of p53 with pifithrin-αor p53-knockout ameliorated fibrosis, inflammation and tubular cell apoptosis in mice with unilateral urethral obstruction (UUO) [32], and a recent study showed that suppression of p53 relieved renal fibrosis and injury after repeated low-dose cisplatin nephrotoxicity [33].

Our research verified the function of PXR in AA-induced AKI through in vitro and in vivo experiments. We found that PXR expression was highly down-regulated in the kidneys of AA-treated mice and AA-treated BUMPT cells. PXR activation significantly attenuated AA-induced renal function impairment, histological lesions, and apoptosis in tubular epithelial cells. Meanwhile, the inhibition of PXR showed the opposite effect. Mechanistically, we demonstrated the interaction of PXR and p53 through a co-immunoprecipitation assay, and found that PXR promoting the ubiquitination and degradation of p53. Hence, PXR may protect against AA-induced AKI by inhibiting p53 through ubiquitination.

Materials and methods

Antibodies and reagents

The primary antibodies utilized in the present research included PXR (Abcam, ab192579), p53 (Cell Signaling Technology, 2524 and proteintech, 10442-1-AP), cleaved caspase 3 (Cell Signaling Technology, 9664), BCL2 (Affinity, AF6139), FLAG (Sigma-Aldrich, F1804), HA (51064-2-AP), IgG (Proteinch, B900620), GAPDH (Proteintech, 10494-1-AP), and tubulin (Proteintech, 66031-1-Ig). The secondary antibodies used were HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG(H + L) (SA00001-2) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG(H + L) (SA00001-1). The TUNEL assay kit (Product number, 12156792910) was purchased from Roche Life Science. ProteinA/G PLUS-Agarose (sc-2003) was obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. Pregnenolone carbonitrile (PCN) (P911021) and ketoconazole (KTZ) (HY-B0105) were purchased from MACKLIN and MCE, respectively.

Cell culture and treatment

The BUMPT cell line (Boston University mouse proximal tubule) used in this research was donated by Professor Dong of the Institute of Nephrology, the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University, and cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were placed in a humidified incubator at 37°C under a 5% CO2 atmosphere. To establish the AA-induced AKI cell model, BUMPT cells were subjected to AA (20 μg/ml) in DMEM medium supplemented with 2% FBS for 24 h.

Plasmid transfection

To overexpress PXR, BUMPT cells were transfected with PXR overexpression plasmid with lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, United States), strictly following the manufacturer’s manual. 12 h later, the culture medium was replaced, and the cells were harvested 36–48 h after transfection.

Animals and treatment

The use of animals in this research was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (No.2022650). Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Slack Jingda laboratory animal company (Hunan, China). The mice were housed in the Laboratory Animal Center of the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University and were given a one-week period of acclimatization feeding. To construct the AA-induced AKI model in vivo, the mice were randomly divided into following two groups (n = 6, sample sizes were chosen based on prior similar studies and feasibility considerations): (1) PBS; (2) AA. Single dose of AA (10 mg/kg) was intraperitoneally injected into mice, and the negative control group was injected with PBS. After four days AA treatment, the mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg) for subsequent analyses. To examine the effect of the PXR specific agonist PCN and the PXR inhibitor KTZ, the mice were randomly divided into following three groups (n = 6): (1) PBS + corn oil; (2) AA + corn oil; (3) AA + PCN or AA+ KTZ. For PCN administration, mice were pretreated with PCN by gavage for consecutive four days, after which they were given a single intraperitoneal injection of AA (10 mg/kg) on the 4th day. Subsequently, PCN treatment was continued for three days, the mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg) four days after AA intervention, and blood and tissues were harvested to be used for subsequent analysis. For KTZ administration, mice were pretreated with KTZ by gavage for consecutive two days, after which they were given a single intraperitoneal injection of AA (10 mg/kg) on the 2nd day. Subsequently, KTZ treatment was continued for three days, the mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg) four days after AA intervention, and blood and tissues were harvested to be used for subsequent analysis.

Renal function analysis

For renal function analysis, a kit procured from the Bioengineering Institute of Nanjing Jiancheng was used to determine the serum creatinine levels. Following the manufacturer’s guidelines, 6 µL of double distilled water (blank group), standards and samples were pipetted into distinct wells of a 96-well plate respectively, 180 µL of enzyme solution A was dispensed into each well, and the samples were incubated at 37°Cfor 5 min in a constant temperature incubator. The absorbance value of A1 was detected by microplate reader. After detection, 60 µL of enzyme solution B was dispensed into each well, the samples were incubated at 37°C for 5 min again in a constant temperature incubator, then the absorbance value of A2 was determined. Creatinine values were calculated for each well based on the measured values and formulas.

Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) and PAS staining

For renal histological analysis, kidney tissues underwent 24h fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde, then were paraffin-embedded, sectioned into 4-µm sections, and paraffin-embedded renal slices were performed HE and PAS staining to assess renal histological damage, which showed the following changes: tubule dilation and vacuolization, naked nuclei, exfoliation of epithelial cells, and cast formation. Tubular damage scores were evaluated using the percentage of tubular damage: 0, no injury; 1, < 25%; 2, 25–50%; 3, 50–75%; 4, > 75%.

TUNEL assay

After the intervention, BUMPT cells were washed in PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature (RT) for 10 min. Subsequently, permeabilization of the cell membrane was performed using a mixture of 0.1% Triton X −100 and 0.1% sodium citrate for 10 min, followed by a 1-h incubation with the TUNEL reaction mixture at 37 °C. After incubation, an anti-fluorescence quench containing DAPI was added to the cells and observed under a fluorescence microscope. For kidney tissues, this method was similar to that used for BUMPT cells.

Western blotting and immuniprecipitation

Kidney tissues or BUMPT cells were lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor. The lysates were subsequently centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C and the protein samples were obtained in the supernatant. The protein concentration of the samples was tested using the BCA Protein Assay Kit. Loading buffer was added to the sample and then denatured by heating for 10 min at 100 °C. The protein samples were subjected to electrophoresis with SDS-PAGE and electrotransferred to PVDF membranes. Then the membranes were incubated with 5% skimmed milk/TBST blocking buffer for 1h at ambient temperature, followed by overnight incubation with primary antibody at 4 °C. On the following day, after washed, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1h at RT, washed again, then were exposed to ECL reagent (WBKLS0500, Millipore). For immuniprecipitation (IP), after transfection of the plasmid for 48 h, BUMPT cells in a 10 cm dish were lysed with 1 mL of IP-lysis buffer containing protease inhibitor, and the lysates were subsequently centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C and the protein samples were obtained in the supernatant. The cell lysates were incubated with anti-flag or anti-p53 and anti-IgG antibodies, overnight at 4 °C in a windmill shaker. The next day, proteinA/G PLUS-Agarose were added to the lysate and incubated for 3h at 4 °C in a windmill shaker. After washed with PIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013D), they were mixed with SDS loading buffer, heated at 100°C for 10 min, and separated using SDS-PAGE for immunoblot analysis. Image J software was utilized to analyze the protein band intensity for semi-quantitative analysis.

RNA extraction and Real-Time PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from renal tissues or BUMPT cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a commercial kit (Takara, Tokyo, Japan). Real-Time qPCR analysis was conducted using SYBR Green reagent (Takara) in a Roche PCR system (Roche Life Science), following the manufacturer’s protocols. Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were displayed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and the statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism8.0.2. T-test was employed to assess intergroup differences for two groups, while one-way ANOVA was applied for comparisons involving three or more groups. In all analyses, a p-value < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

Result

PXR expression is down-regulated in AA-induced AKI

To explore whether PXR participates in AA-induced AKI, an AA-induced AKI mouse model was established. HE and PAS staining showed severe kidney injury in AA-treated mice, presenting as dilation, vacuolization, cast formation, and denuded nuclei in renal tubules, which was confirmed by the tubular damage score (Figure 1A-B). Renal function tests revealed a notable increase in serum creatinine level after AA treatment in mice, confirming the successful establishment of an acute kidney injury model (Figure 1C). We then detected PXR expression in AA-induced AKI mice. RT-PCR analysis revealed that Pxr mRNA levels in the renal tissues of AA-treated mice were significantly lower than those in healthy control mice (Figure 1D). Consistent with this, immunoblot analysis showed a decrease in PXR protein levels in mice treated with AA (Figure 1E), as validated by quantitative analysis (Figure 1F). Similarly, PXR expression was also down-regulated in BUMPT cells with AA treatment, as shown by the reduced PXR mRNA and protein levels (Figure 1G-I). In summary, these results show PXR down-regulation in renal tissues and BUMPT cells following AA treatment, indicating an underlying impact on AA-induced AKI.

Overexpression of PXR attenuates AA induced apoptosis in BUMPT cells

Renal tubular cell apoptosis represents a key pathophysiological feature of AA-induced AKI [13]. To assess whether PXR modulates apoptosis in AA-treated BUMPT cells, we first constructed a PXR-overexpression plasmid with FLAG tag. There was a FLAG band in PXR-overexpressing plasmid-transfected BUMPT cells, suggesting successful transfection of PXR into BUMPT cells (Figure 2C). Subsequently, the BUMPT cells were exposed to 20 μg/ml AA for 24 h. Figure 2A and B demonstrated that AA treatment induced cell death in cells transfected with the empty vector, which was partly prevented by overexpressing PXR. Additionally, we detected the influence of PXR on cleaved caspase-3 and BCL-2 protein levels using Western blotting. As illustrated in Figure 2C–E, AA treatment increased cleaved caspase 3 and decreased BCL-2 levels, which were rescued in PXR-overexpressing cells. In agreement with the above results, the TUNEL assay also revealed that TUNEL-stained positive cells were dramatically increased in empty vector-transfected cells following AA treatment, while PXR overexpression suppressed it (Figure 2F–G). Taken together, these results provide evidence that PXR protects AA-treated renal tubular cells from apoptosis.

Activation of PXR alleviates AA-induced AKI in vivo

Next, we tested the influence of PXR on AA-treated mice. To this end, male C57BL/6 mice received daily oral gavage of the PXR-specific agonist PCN at a dose of 50 mg/kg, and then were intraperitoneally treated with AA. HE and PAS staining revealed severe kidney damage in AA-treated mice, manifesting as dilation, vacuolization, cast formation, and denuded nuclei in renal tubules, which was significantly rescued by PCN (Figure 3A-B). Levels of serum creatinine in mice were measured to evaluate renal function. As shown in Figure 3C, the levels of serum creatinine in mice were conspicuously increased after AA injection and this change could be ameliorated by PCN. The western blot also showed that NGAL expression was markedly increased in mice treated with AA, while PCN weakened its expression (Figure 3D-E). Moreover, Immunoblot analysis revealed that co-treatment with AA and PCN resulted in significant lower cleaved caspase-3 expression than AA monotherapy in mice (Figure 3F-G). Correspondingly, the TUNEL assay also showed that compared with mice treated with AA alone, there were fewer apoptotic cells in those treated with AA and PCN (Figure 3H-I). Collectively, these findings suggest that the activation of PXR confers protection against AA-induced AKI in mice.

Inhibition of PXR aggravates AA-induced AKI in vivo

Additionally, the PXR inhibitor ketoconazole (KTZ) was used to validate PXR’s role in our study. Specifically, male C57BL/6 mice were administered KTZ by oral gavage (200 mg/kg per day) and then treated intraperitoneally with AA. As expected, HE and PAS staining showed obvious kidney damage, which was aggravated by KTZ (Figure 4A-B). In addition, co-treatment with AA and KTZ resulted in significantly elevated serum creatinine levels compared with AA treatment alone in mice. (Figure 4C). The Western blot showed that NGAL expression was further enhanced by KTZ in mice treated with AA (Figure 3D-E). Moreover, immunoblot analysis showed that KTZ treatment increased the level of cleaved caspase3 after AA (Figure 4F-G). In line with the results of immunoblot analysis, TUNEL staining showed that KTZ administration led to an increase in the number of TUNEL-positive cells (Figure 4H-I). Overall, these results imply that inhibition of PXR exacerbates injury after AA treatment in mice.

PXR interacts with p53 and down-regulates p53 through ubiquitination

Next, we investigated the molecular mechanism underlying the protective role of PXR in AA-induced AKI. A previous study demonstrated that PXR interacts with p53 in 293 T cells and regulates p53 in colon cancer cells [34]. We hypothesized that PXR interacts with p53 in BUMPT cells and then regulates the expression of p53, thereby modulating AA-induced AKI. To validate this, we examined the combination of PXR and p53 in BUMPT cells. Co-immunoprecipitation demonstrated that PXR bound to p53 in BUMPT cells (Figure 5A-B). In order to further explore the regulation of p53 by PXR, we evaluated p53 expression under the influence of PXR. Immunoblot analysis revealed that p53 levels were elevated in empty vector cells after AA treatment, which was partly inhibited in PXR-overexpressing cells (Figure 5C-D). Uniformly, in vivo experiments showed that combined treatment with AA and PCN led to reduced p53 expression compared to AA treatment alone (Figure 5E-F). Then, we co-overexpressed PXR and p53 in BUMPT cells to observe the effect on AA-induced apoptosis. We found that overexpression of PXR reduced the expression of cleaved caspase3 in response to AA treatment, which could be rescued by overexpressing p53 (Figure 5G-H). The ubiquitin-proteasome system is one of the main pathways for protein degradation. To explore whether PXR affects the ubiquitination of p53, we examined the ubiquitination of p53 by PXR. As shown in Figure 5I, overexpression of PXR increased the ubiquitination of p53 in BUMPT cells treated with AA, suggesting that PXR may down-regulate the expression of p53 by promoting its ubiquitination and degradation. In short, these data imply that PXR interacts with p53, leading to the ubiquitination and degradation of p53, thereby down-regulating its expression.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrated that PXR exerts renoprotective effects in AA-induced AKI. First, we showed that PXR expression was down-regulated in both renal tissues of AA-treated mice and AA-treated BUMPT cells. Our in vitro experiments revealed that PXR overexpression reduced AA-induced apoptosis in BUMPT cells. Consistently, PCN, a specific agonist of PXR, attenuated tubular apoptosis, renal histological damage, and renal dysfunction in AA-induced AKI mice, while KTZ, an inhibitor of PXR, showed the opposite effects. Finally, we found that PXR interacted with p53 in BUMPT cells, then PXR down-regulated p53 expression in both animal models and cells. And overexpression of PXR increased the ubiquitination of p53. Taken together, these results elucidate that PXR reduce tubular cell apoptosis via promoting the ubiquitination and degradation of p53, thereby alleviating AA-induced AKI.

Nuclear receptors, a group of transcription factors, harbor DNA-binding (DBD) and ligand-binding (LBD) domains and are involved in various biological events, including inflammation, metabolism, homeostasis, circadian rhythm, reproduction, and differentiation [35,36]. PXR is a member of the nuclear receptor superfamily, serves as a ligand-dependent transcription factor with the DBD and LBD. The LBD, a hydrophobic domain with a flexible pocket, is activated by multiple drugs and chemical compounds. In general, PXR heterodimerizes with retinol X receptor α (RXRα) to form transcriptional complex, regulating down-stream gene expression [37,38]. Previous studies have established that PXR exerts significant effects on drug metabolism by regulating metabolic enzymes [16,17,37]. PXR was involved in inflammation, apoptosis, oxidative stress, energy and lipid metabolism in various diseases [15,18,19,39,40].

In recent years, several researches have reported that PXR has an important effect on kidney disease [23,24,41]. The activity of PXR was decreased in 5/6 nephrectomy-induced CKD rats [21]. Another study found that PXR was up-regulated in diabetic nephropathy [22]. However, neither of these two studies examined the specific function of PXR in the kidney. We observed a decrease in PXR expression in AA-induced AKI. These results suggest that PXR expression is altered differently in different diseases, and this divergence might result from distinct changes in the pathophysiology of different diseases. Subsequently, Yu et al. showed that PXR attenuated cisplatin-induced AKI and IRI-induced AKI by targeting AKR1B7 [23]. Luan et al. also found that PXR alleviated renal damage via PI3K/AKT pathway in cisplatin-induced AKI [24]. Besides, traditional Chinese medicine Salvia miltiorrhiza and Tanshinone IIA, its bioactive constituent, exert renoprotective effect against cisplatin-induced AKI via modulation of the PXR/NF-κB pathway [41]. We also found that PXR protects against AA-induced AKI.

Numerous studies have revealed that PXR is involved in apoptosis. Zhou et al. demonstrated that PXR overexpression reduced deoxycholic acid-mediated apoptosis in colon cancer HCT116 cells. In LS180 colon cancer cells, PXR activation attenuated staurosporine induced apoptosis [42]. PXR knockout increased ALT and AST levels and aggravated pathological injury and hepatocyte apoptosis in D-galactose/bacterial lipopolysaccharide (D/L)-induced acute liver injury (ALI) [43]. A study has suggested that non-dioxin-like polychlorinated biphenyls (NDL-PCBs) inhibited UV-induced hepatocyte apoptosis, which was weakened by PXR knockdown, indicating that PXR suppresses Uv-induced hepatocyte apoptosis [44]. Similarly, our study showed the anti-apoptotic function of PXR in both in vivo and in vitro models.

Besides acting as a transcription factor, PXR also modulates cellular functions by engaging in protein-protein interactions and signal crosstalk. For example, a study showed that in colon cancer cells, PXR disrupted the combination of ATF3 and MDM2 by interacting with ATF3 and then increased the stability of ATF3 by suppressing MDM2-mediated ubiquitination degradation of ATF3, promoting radioresistance in colorectal cancer cells [45]. Bakshi et al. found that PXR interacted with Tip60, a histone acetyltransferase, to form a complex that enhanced Tip60 enzymatic activity and regulated the migratory and invasive abilities of colon cancer cells [46]. In addition, studies have shown that PXR interacts with p53 and inhibits the expression of p53 downstream genes in colon cancer cells, thereby regulating tumor transformation [34]. Another study has shown that PXR combines with p53 in hepatocyte and inhibits p53 expression, thereby reducing AMPKβ1 expression and activity regulated by p53 and attenuating ammonia-induced autophagy in hepatocytes [47]. Totally, these results show that PXR is involved in various diseases through protein-protein interactions. And our study shows PXR binds to p53 and inhibits its expression by promoting its ubiquitination and degradation.

The tumor suppressor p53, which is activated in AKI caused by various reasons, has important effects on AKI progression [28]. The relationship between PXR and p53 in the kidney remains unclear, and we speculate that PXR interacts with p53 in renal tubular cells, suppressing p53 expression and alleviating AA-induced AKI. To validate this hypothesis, we detected the binding of PXR to p53 by co-immunoprecipitation. Subsequently, our experiment demonstrated the combination of PXR and p53 in BUMPT cells. Furthermore, we found that overexpression of PXR inhibited p53 levels in BUMPT cells, and activation of PXR by a specific agonist resulted in p53 suppression in mice. Moreover, PXR prompted ubiquitination of p53 in BUMPT cells. Therefore, PXR exerts a protective effect against AA-induced AKI through regulating the p53 pathway.

This study had some limitations. Firstly, we found that AA treatment led to a decrease in PXR expression in mouse kidney tissues and BUMPT cells, but the mechanism is unclear. Future investigations should focus on elucidating the upstream mechanisms responsible for the downregulation of PXR following aristolochic acid exposure, including potential roles of epigenetic regulation, transcriptional repression, and post-translational modifications. Secondly, in our animal experiments, we only used PXR agonist and antagonist to elaborate on the role and mechanism of PXR. In future, the use of kidney-specific PXR knockout or transgenic animal models will be essential to definitively establish the causal role of PXR in AKI and to exclude off-target effects associated with pharmacological agonists or antagonists. Thirdly, AA can cause AKI and CKD, which are usually used in the transition from AKI to CKD [48]. This study was exclusively concerned with the function of PXR in AKI induced by AA, long-term studies are warranted to determine whether PXR-mediated suppression of p53 also mitigates chronic tubular injury, fibrosis, and maladaptive repair following aristolochic acid exposure. Additionally, our research clarified that PXR affects AKI through p53-mediated apoptosis. Exploring the crosstalk between PXR-p53 signaling and other pathogenic pathways, such as oxidative stress, inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and autophagy, may further clarify the integrated network governing tubular cell fate. Finally, our current research focuses on cellular and animal experiments regarding the role and mechanism of PXR in AKI. Translational studies assessing PXR expression and activity in human kidney biopsies, as well as the therapeutic potential and safety of PXR-targeting agents, could pave the way for novel, mechanism-based interventions for toxin-induced kidney injury.

In conclusion, our study clearly validates that PXR exerts a renoprotective role in AA-induced AKI. Mechanistically, our data showed that PXR binds to p53 in renal tubular cells and down-regulates p53 expression by promoting its ubiquitination and degradation, thereby alleviating AA-induced apoptosis in the kidney. Therefore, PXR may be a viable therapeutic target for AKI.
