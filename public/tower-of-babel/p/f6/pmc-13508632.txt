Integrated Genetic Risk Stratification Identifies High Adenoma Detection Rates at Colonoscopy in a Real-World US Health System
Authors: Mitchelle V Zolotarevsky, Keon Karimabady, Raza Muhammad, Annabelle Ashworth, Ashley Mulford, Zhuqing Shi, Huy Tran, Jun Wei, S Lilly Zheng, Brian T Helfand, Alan R Sanders, Andrew Ross, Blake Jones, Jianfeng Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsColonoscopy prevents colorectal cancer through detection and removal of precursor adenomas but remains a resource-intensive for population screening. Inherited genetic risk may improve yield of adenoma detection and prioritization of colonoscopy in higher risk individuals supporting a shift toward genetically informed screening paradigm.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective chart review of 800 adults from a US health-system biobank who underwent colonoscopy between 2010 and 2025, with investigators blinded to genetic risk status. Participants were randomly sampled across 4 predefined genetic risk categories (Low, Intermediate, High, Very High; 200 in each group) using an externally developed and validated genetic probability model (genomic probability of colorectal cancer risk) incorporating polygenic risk scores, family history, and Lynch syndrome pathogenic variants. After applying exclusion criteria, 608 patients were included into the study. The primary outcome was adenoma detection rate (ADR). Multivariable logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, genetic ancestry, colonoscopy indication, and year of colonoscopy. Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).ResultsOverall ADR was 43.59% and increased from 26.27% (Low), 41.38% (Intermediate), and 54.08% (High) to 68.35% (Very High), Ptrend<0.001. Compared with Low-risk, adjusted odds ratios (95% confidence interval) were 1.77 (1.04–3.01), 2.40 (1.48–3.90), and 4.18 (2.21–7.91) for Intermediate-, High-, and Very High-risk groups, respectively. The absolute difference in ADR between the Very High- and Low-risk groups was 42.08 percentage points. Findings were consistent in screening-only and other sensitivity analyses. Genomic probability of colorectal cancer risk improved discrimination beyond baseline predictors (AUC 0.739 vs 0.715; ΔAUC = 0.024; P = .03)ConclusionIntegrated genetic risk stratification was strongly associated with adenoma detection and substantially enriched colonoscopy yield. These findings support incorporating genetic risk to prioritize colonoscopy resources more efficiently.Keywords: Colonoscopy, Lynch Syndrome (LS), Family History (FH), Polygenic Score (PGS), Colorectal Cancer

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the United States overall.1 While CRC incidence and mortality have declined among adults older than 50 years, largely due to widespread screening, the epidemiologic pattern in younger adults has reversed. Incidence of CRC in individuals younger than 50 years has nearly doubled over the past 2 decades, and CRC is now the leading cause of cancer-related death among individuals younger than 50 years.2 These trends underscore the need not only for earlier detection but also for more efficient prevention strategies.

Colonoscopy is uniquely preventive because it enables detection and removal of precursor adenomas, thereby reducing both CRC incidence and mortality. However, colonoscopy is an invasive, resource-intensive procedure requiring specialized personnel, facility capacity, and patient preparation.3,4 In the setting of growing demand, workforce shortages in gastroenterology, and prolonged wait times in many regions, optimizing the allocation of colonoscopy has become increasingly important. Current CRC screening strategies remain largely age-based, with earlier or more intensive screening reserved for individuals with a strong family history (FH) or pathogenic variants (PVs) in Lynch syndrome or other genes.5, 6, 7 Outside of these criteria, a “one-size-fits-all” approach predominates, potentially leading to low-yield procedures in some individuals and missed opportunities for intensified prevention in others.

Polygenic risk scores (PRSs), which aggregate the effects of common single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) across the genome, have emerged as a promising tool for inherited risk stratification.8,9 Furthermore, integration of PRS with FH and Lynch syndrome PV status considerably improves discrimination of CRC risk and identifies substantially more individuals at elevated lifetime risk than traditional high-risk definitions alone.10 Although integrated genetic risk models have been validated for predicting CRC incidence, their relevance to colonoscopy-detected premalignant lesions remains incompletely defined.11,12

Because the preventive benefit of colonoscopy derives primarily from detection and removal of adenomas and other precancerous lesions, identifying individuals more likely to harbor colonic neoplasia has important implications for optimizing colonoscopy use. If genetic risk stratification meaningfully enriches for neoplasia detection, colonoscopy resources could be preferentially directed toward individuals most likely to benefit, thereby improving efficiency and maximizing the preventive impact of this limited and resource-intensive intervention.3,13

In this study, we applied an externally developed and validated integrated genetic probability (genomic probability of colorectal cancer risk [GenProbCRC]) to a real-world health-system cohort to evaluate whether genetic risk stratification identifies subgroups with higher adenoma detection rates (ADRs) at colonoscopy.10

Methods

Study Participants

Participants were selected from the Genomic Health Initiative (GHI), a health-care system–based biobank at Endeavor Health in Illinois.14 Demographic and clinical data were obtained through linkage to electronic health records. Germline DNA was isolated from peripheral blood samples and sequenced using next-generation sequencing, including high-coverage targeted panel sequencing and low-coverage whole-genome sequencing. SNPs were imputed using established reference-based methods.10 All participants provided informed consent for GHI participation. The study was approved by the Endeavor Health Institutional Review Board.

A total of 800 participants were sampled from 4 predefined genetic risk groups (see below) within the GHI using a disproportionate stratified random sampling to ensure representation across risk categories. Eligible participants were adults (≥18 years) with available genetic risk estimates who underwent colonoscopy at any Endeavor Health hospital between 2010 and 2025. Individuals with a prior diagnosis of CRC before the index colonoscopy or with missing or incomplete pathology reports were excluded. No participants carried a documented diagnosis of familial adenomatous polyposis. Individuals with inflammatory bowel disease or other hereditary CRC syndromes were not excluded and were retained to reflect real-world clinical practice.

Data Collection

Chart review of colonoscopy of selected participants was performed by M.Z., K.K., R.M., and A.A., who were blinded to genetic risk group assignment. The earliest colonoscopy during the study period was defined as the index examination. Demographic and clinical data at the index colonoscopy were abstracted through structured chart review of the Endeavor Health electronic health record and recorded in a secure, password-protected REDCap database.

Collected variables included demographics (age, sex, race, ethnicity, body mass index [BMI]), comorbidities associated with CRC risk (hypertension, hyperlipidemia, type 2 diabetes), lifestyle factors (use of alcohol, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug), FH (see below), and personal history of hereditary cancer syndromes (eg, Lynch syndrome, Cowden syndrome).15,16

Colonoscopy indication was categorized based on the procedure report as follows: screening (first-time CRC screening or interval screening after prior negative colonoscopy), surveillance (colonoscopy following prior polyp or neoplasia findings), and diagnostic (colonoscopy performed for evaluation of symptoms, eg, bleeding, abdominal pain or other clinical indications).

For each colonoscopy, the following variables were abstracted from procedure and pathology reports: total number of polyps, size (mm) and location of the largest polyp, and histologic type. Advanced adenomas were defined as adenomas ≥10 mm, with villous or tubulovillous histology, or high-grade dysplasia. Sessile serrated lesions (SSLs), including sessile serrated polyps/adenomas and SSL not otherwise specified, were identified. Advanced SSLs were defined as ≥10 mm, presence of dysplasia, or traditional serrated adenoma. If invasive CRC was detected, tumor stage was recorded.7,17

Genetic Risk Factors

An integrated genetic probability (GenProbCRC) incorporating PRS, FH, and PV carrier status of Lynch syndrome genes and adjusting sex and ancestry principal components was used for genetic risk stratification in this study.10 Briefly, the GenProbCRC was originally developed in the UK Biobank and subsequently validated in the full GHI cohort for prediction of CRC diagnosis. FH of CRC was included regardless of the degree of familial relation. Pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants in MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2 are grouped as PVs.18,19 A previously published PRS for CRC (PGS003852) was selected based on the large sample sizes used for model development and validation, as well as its demonstrated performance across diverse ancestry populations.9,20

Risk group thresholds derived from the UK Biobank were applied to classify GHI participants into 4 predefined categories: Low, Intermediate, High, and Very High CRC genetic risk. For selected analyses, the Intermediate, High, and Very High groups were collectively defined as genetically elevated risk.

Statistical Methods

Baseline characteristics were summarized overall and by genetic risk group. Continuous variables were reported as median (interquartile range) and compared using Kruskal–Wallis tests. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages and compared using chi-square tests.

The primary outcome was ADR, defined as the proportion of colonoscopies with ≥1 colorectal adenoma detected. The primary analysis evaluated the association between GenProbCRC category and ADR using logistic regression in the full cohort. Models were adjusted for age, sex, BMI, genetic ancestry (top 10 principal components), colonoscopy indication (screening, surveillance, diagnostic), and year of colonoscopy. Low genetic risk served as the reference group. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Trend across ordered genetic risk categories was assessed by modeling risk group as an ordinal variable.

To enhance clinical interpretability, ADR was calculated within each genetic risk category. We estimated the number of colonoscopies required to detect 1 adenoma (number needed to scope) within each risk group as the reciprocal of the ADR (1/ADR).

Because adenoma detection varies by indication, a prespecified subgroup analysis was conducted among individuals undergoing screening colonoscopy using the same adjustment strategy.

To evaluate robustness of findings, sensitivity analyses were performed, including (1) excluding diagnostic colonoscopies, (2) restricting to adequate bowel preparation, (3) excluding individuals with monogenic PVs, and (4) restricting to participants of European ancestry.

Discriminative performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Incremental discrimination was evaluated by sequentially comparing nested models as follows: (1) a baseline model (age, sex, BMI, genetic ancestry, colonoscopy indication, and year of colonoscopy); (2) baseline plus FH; (3) baseline plus PV carrier status; and (4) baseline plus integrated genetic risk group (GenProbCRC). Differences in AUC between nested models were tested using DeLong test.

Because this was a retrospective study using a fixed sampling design, an a priori sample size calculation was not performed.

Secondary outcomes included detection of advanced adenomas and SSLs, as defined above.

The absolute difference in ADR between the Very High- and Low-risk groups was 42.08 percentage points. These correspond to approximately 3.81 colonoscopies required to detect 1 adenoma in the Low-risk group compared with 1.46 in the Very High-risk group.

When evaluated separately, traditional genetic risk factors demonstrated weaker associations with adenoma detection. The ADR was 49.7% and 41.65% among participants with and without a FH of CRC; OR (95% CI) was 1.62 (1.07–2.43), P = .02. The ADR was 30.77% and 43.87% among PV carriers and noncarriers of Lynch syndrome genes, OR (95% CI) was 0.41 (0.12–1.44), P = .16. The lower ADR among PV carriers likely reflects the exclusion of individuals with prior CRC or adenomas from this cohort, many of whom may have undergone intensive prior screening.

All models incorporating genetic risk factors demonstrated improved discrimination for adenoma detection compared with the baseline model. The greatest and statistically significant improvement was observed with GenProbCRC, with the AUC increasing from 0.715 to 0.739 (ΔAUC = 0.024, P = .03) (Table 3).

Results were consistent across multiple prespecified sensitivity analyses, including limited to those with adequate bowel preparation (N = 567), without monogenic PVs (N = 595), and European ancestry (N = 487) (Supplementary Table 2). Across all analyses, a progressive increase in ADR was observed with increasing GenProbCRC risk groups, and effect estimates were similar to those observed in the primary analysis (Table 2).

We performed additional analyses evaluating secondary colonoscopy outcomes, including advanced adenomas and SSLs. A consistent stepwise increase in the detection rate of advanced adenomas was observed across higher GenProbCRC risk groups, P for trend <0.001 (Supplementary Table 3). In contrast, no clear trend was observed for SSLs, although higher genetic risk groups had higher ORs.

Discussion

In this real-world health-system cohort, we demonstrated that integrated genetic risk stratification (GenProbCRC) was strongly and progressively associated with adenoma detection at colonoscopy. ADR increased from 26.27% in the Low-risk group to 68.35% in the Very High-risk group, with a more than 4-fold adjusted increase in odds of adenoma detection across categories. Importantly, the absolute difference in ADR between Very High and Low genetic risk groups exceeded 42 percentage points, translating into substantially greater adenoma yield per colonoscopy in genetically high-risk individuals. In contrast, traditional genetic risk markers (FH and monogenic PVs) alone demonstrated weaker associations with adenoma detection and more modest discriminative performance. Models incorporating integrated genetic risk provided the greatest improvement in AUC compared with baseline model.

While numerous studies have evaluated PRS and integrated genetic risk models for predicting CRC incidence, few have examined whether an integrated inherited risk model incorporating PRS, FH, and Lynch syndrome PVs is associated with clinically actionable colonoscopy outcomes. Our study extends prior work in 2 important ways. First, we evaluated an integrated genetic risk model that combines multiple established inherited risk factors rather than PRS alone. Second, we assessed colonoscopy outcomes, including adenoma and advanced adenoma detection, rather than CRC incidence. The observed gradient in adenoma detection across genetic risk groups suggests that inherited genetic risk assessment may have utility beyond disease prediction and could improve the efficiency of preventive colonoscopy.

Although the improvement in discrimination was modest (AUC increase from 0.715 to 0.739), this finding is consistent with many clinically useful risk stratification tools, where meaningful clinical utility may not be fully captured by changes in AUC alone. The primary value of GenProbCRC lies in its ability to identify subgroups with substantially different colonoscopy yield. For example, ADR increased from 26.3% in the Low-risk group to 68.4% in the Very High-risk group, and the odds of advanced adenoma detection increased more than 5-fold. These differences translated into large absolute differences in adenoma detection and substantially lower numbers needed to scope, suggesting that inherited genetic risk may improve the efficiency of preventive colonoscopy. Thus, the clinical utility of GenProbCRC is reflected more by risk stratification and enrichment of actionable findings than by incremental changes in discrimination metrics alone.

The ADR among participants in the Low genetic risk group was consistent with contemporary US practice, particularly in mixed screening and surveillance populations. Reported ADR benchmarks typically range from 25% to 35% in screening populations, with higher rates observed in older cohorts and surveillance examinations.21, 22, 23 The observed ADR in our cohort therefore aligns with expected performance metrics and supports the generalizability of our findings within routine clinical care. Importantly, the stepwise gradient in ADR across genetic risk groups mirrors the magnitude of risk stratification seen in epidemiologic CRC incidence studies but now translated into colonoscopy-detected precursor lesions.

Because participants were sampled across predefined genetic risk strata, the observed gradient in ADR should be interpreted in the context of the underlying population distribution. In the broader GHI population, approximately 60% of individuals fall into the Low genetic risk category, whereas 17%, 21%, and 1.2% are classified as Intermediate, High, and Very High risk, respectively.10 Overall, about 40% of the population carries elevated-genetic risk (Intermediate, High, or Very High). Given the substantially higher ADR observed in these elevated-risk groups, a minority of individuals may account for a disproportionate burden of detectable colonic neoplasia. From a health-system perspective, these findings support a targeted strategy to improve preventive efficiency by aligning colonoscopy allocation with inherited risk.

A key observation is that most individuals classified as having elevated GenProbCRC risk were neither FH–positive nor PV carriers of Lynch syndrome genes. Among the 40% of participants categorized as genetically elevated risk in GHI, only 18.6% had FH alone, 0.6% carried a Lynch PV alone, and 0.2% had both FH and PVs.10 These findings indicate that the discriminatory capacity of the integrated model is driven predominantly by polygenic risk rather than traditional high-risk markers. This observation is consistent with the limited predictive value of FH alone observed in both the present study and prior literature.3,24,25 Together, these findings suggest that polygenic risk substantially expands inherited risk stratification beyond monogenic syndromes and reported FH, identifying a broader segment of the population with clinically meaningful risk enrichment.

Few studies have examined the relationship between inherited genetic risk and colonoscopy-detected adenomas. Our 2021 study demonstrated that a SNP-based genetic risk score (22 SNPs only) was associated with adenomatous polyps in an average-risk screening population of European ancestry, with detection rates rising from 10.8% to 39.7% across genetic risk score categories.26 The present study builds upon and substantially advances that work by using an externally developed and validated integrated genetic probability model, evaluating a broader, real-world health-system cohort (not restricted to screening-only Caucasian participants), demonstrating clinically meaningful absolute differences in ADR and quantifying improvements in discrimination beyond clinical risk factors. Together, these results strengthen the evidence that inherited genetic risk is associated not only with CRC incidence but also with prevalent colonic neoplasia at colonoscopy.

The central implication of our findings is not merely improved risk prediction, but more efficient allocation of colonoscopy. Colonoscopy is invasive, costly, and resource-intensive, and access is constrained by workforce shortages and prolonged wait times. Yet current screening paradigms remain largely age-based, with limited personalization beyond strong FH or monogenic PV. Our results suggest that integrated genetic risk stratification can identify individuals with substantially higher adenoma yield, thereby increasing the number of adenomas detected per colonoscopy and enhancing preventive efficiency. Such stratification may inform decisions regarding earlier or more intensive colonoscopy screening in genetically enriched subgroups, while potentially redirecting lower-risk individuals toward noninvasive modalities.27 Rather than focusing solely on increasing screening uptake, genetic risk stratification offers a strategy to maximize the preventive impact of colonoscopy within a constrained health-care environment.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, this was a retrospective study within a single health system, which may limit generalizability to other practice settings. Additional validation in external and more diverse populations is warranted. Second, approximately 80% of participants were of European ancestry. Although findings were consistent in ancestry-restricted analyses, PRS performance may vary across ancestry groups and further evaluation in diverse populations is needed. Third, sampling across predefined genetic risk categories, while methodologically intentional, does not reflect the natural population distribution. Although this approach enhances internal comparisons, extrapolation to population-level effects requires appropriate weighting. Fourth, operator-level quality metrics, including endoscopist-specific ADR and use of image-enhanced endoscopy, were not consistently available. However, adequate bowel preparation was documented in more than 90% of examinations and cecal intubation was achieved in all included procedures, supporting the overall quality of colonoscopy examinations. Finally, colonoscopy indications varied (screening, surveillance, and diagnostic). Although analyses adjusted for indication and findings were consistent in screening-only analyses, residual confounding cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

In a real-world health-system cohort, our study demonstrates integrated genetic risk stratification (GenProbCRC) was strongly associated with adenoma detection at colonoscopy and substantially enriched adenoma yield across risk categories. Compared with traditional genetic risk factors alone, integrated genetic risk provided greater discrimination and clinically meaningful absolute differences in detection rates. These findings support the potential role of inherited genetic risk in personalizing colonoscopy allocation to improve preventive efficiency and maximize the cancer-prevention impact of a limited and resource-intensive intervention.
