ABSTRACT
PurposeCardiovascular (CV) toxicity is prevalent in cancer survivors due to shared risk factors, direct CV injury from cancer therapy (CT), and development/exacerbation of CV risk factors during treatment. Succinct guidance on the minimum standard of CV care required of cancer care providers (CCPs) can improve outcomes for survivors. This clinical practice statement (CPS) is aimed at CCPs focused on identification, prevention, and management of CVD in individuals with cancer.MethodsThe International Cardio-Oncology Society and Multinational Association of Supportive Care in Cancer identified members with expertise across oncology, cardiology, and cancer survivorship. Key questions/areas of practice recommendations related to CV risk and CVD were identified across the cancer trajectory.Working groups performed targeted literature reviews and consensus was reached for all recommendations.Results5 over-arching clinical practice recommendations for CCPs were identified: (1) perform a CV risk assessment prior to starting CT (2) identify and facilitate CV-related care during CT; (3) ensure measures are taken to mitigate the risk of cancer therapy related CV toxicity (CTR-CVT) prior to and during CT; (4) monitor CV status and risk factors during therapy and refer to the appropriate health care provider if CV risk factors are uncontrolled or new signs/symptoms develop; and (5) re-assess CV risk following completion of CT (periodically for those with advanced disease on life long treatment) and develop a CV surveillance plan.ConclusionsCCPs play a pivotal role in identifying and addressing CTR-CVT. This CPS provides specific, succinct, and actionable behaviours that can be readily implemented in cancer care globally.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Cardiovascular (CV) disease is a major competing cause of mortality and morbidity in cancer survivors [1, 2] given the improvements seen in survivorship largely due to early detection and improved cancer therapies [3, 4]. CV toxicity can occur due to the direct impact of cancer therapy on the CV system or indirectly by negatively impacting risk factors for CVD [5, 6]. Furthermore, identification, attention to, and management of risk factors for cardiovasculasr disease (CVD) may be given less priority during cancer treatment by patients and their health care providers [7]. The combination of direct and indirect CV toxicity and inattention to CVD risk factors leads to increased CV morbidity and premature mortality [3, 8]. In individuals with cancer, management of CV risk factors and CVD is an important aspect of cancer survivorship.

Several professional organizations have published expert opinions and guidelines on the CV care of individuals with cancer, including the European Society of Cardiology, American College of Cardiology, Canadian Cardiovascular Society, American Society of Clinical Oncology, and the European Society of Medical Oncology [3, 4, 6, 9, 10]. However, these documents have largely focused on recommendations for cardiology providers rather than cancer care providers, and they have not considered unique resource constraints with regards to provision of cardio-oncology care around the globe. While comprehensive, the most robust recommendations for cardiovascular care of individuals with cancer may be overlooked given the multitude of recommendations. This may lead to sub-optimal adoption of evidence based recommendations in clinical practice.

While current guidelines in cardio-oncology highlight the importance of collaboration between oncologists, cardiologists and allied health care professionals, there is limited discussion on the specific role of cancer care providers in contributing to the cardiovascular care of individuals with cancer at risk of CV toxicity or with pre-exisiting CVD [6, 11]. Cancer care providers are well positioned to assess individuals at risk of cancer therapy related CV toxicity; however, they often have competing priorities [7]. Oncology visits, from consultation, initiation and follow-up of therapy, and post-treatment visits, all have critical oncology-based priorities centred around staging, prognosis, treatment decisions, toxicity management, and surveillance/survivorship planning. Thus the necessary attention to CVD is likely to be impacted by competing priorities and time constraints. It is therefore critical that cancer care providers are aware of what is a realistic minimal standard of care for assessment and management of CV risk/CVD and what components of care should be appropriately referred to cardiologist/cardio-oncologists or other health care providers with expertise in this field.

This joint effort by two international organizations focused on supportive and clinical care, the International Cardio-Oncology Society (ICOS) and the Multinational Association of Supportive Care in Cancer (MASCC), aims to address this gap by developing a clinical practice statement (CPS) – a brief, clinically focused set of recommendations aimed at cancer care professionals addressing the minimum standard of care for cancer care providers in the prevention and management of CVD in individuals with cancer, taking into account competing priorities and variation in resources globally.

Methods

This CPS was developed collaboratively by members of ICOS and MASCC with expertise in oncology, cardiovascular disease, and cancer survivorship representing diverse professional disciplines and international geographic locations. The authorship group met virtually to identify and agree upon several key questions to address the assessment of cancer therapy related CV toxicity risk and management during an individual’s cancer disease trajectory - initial assessment, during treatment, and in follow-up. Multidisciplinary working groups were formed to work on a targeted literature review and proposed recommendations.The 2022 European Society of Cardiology Cardio-Oncology guideline [6] was used as a comprehensive source for literature review. Additional literature was sourced through searches of the authors’ own files, with the final reference list generated from items most relevant to the broad scope of this review.

The recommendations were reviewed by all authors, and any differences of opinion were resolved by consensus with key practice recommendations summarised as clinical practice statements (CPS). The overarching purpose and principles of the CPS are summarised in Table 1.

Clinical Practice Statement (CPS) Purpose and Principles

Overarching principles: CVD management needs to address an individual’s CV risks in the context of their cancer, their cancer treatment goals, and long term health outcomes. Cancer care professionals are uniquely positioned to identify individuals at risk of CVD and develop and initiate a plan for multidisciplinary management with input from other providers.

The model of care delivery should be evidence based, utilizing resources available in the relevant setting, including supported self management, primary care, community care, and specialist care when required.

CPS 1. Cancer care providers play a critical role in identifying pre-existing CV risk factors and CVD in individuals with cancer. Cancer care providers should be aware of how these may impact the selection of cancer treatment and how cancer treatment may affect underlying CV risk factors/CVD. These considerations should be integrated into the overall cancer care plan, with input from other members of the multidisciplinary team as required

Cancer care is inherently multidisciplinary with input from diverse oncology disciplines, allied health care providers, and other specialised and primary care providers with cancer care providers frequently involved in ensuring coordinated, integrated care. Cancer care providers should be aware of an individual’s pre-exisitng CVD, the presence of CV risk factors, and how these may impact the selection of cancer treatment [3, 6, 11]. It is important to consider that assessment of CVD risk is taking place in the context of broad considerations of an individual’s cancer specific circumstances including assessment of cancer stage, prognosis, treatment options and unique physical, psychosocial and practical supportive care needs in the context of limited time available for a comprehensive assessment.

It is a minimal standard of care that cancer care providers consider pertinent CVD considerations sufficient to identify areas of concerns that (a) may impact selection of cancer therapies, (b) may be impacted by cancer therapies, or (c) may warrant referral to a specialist health care provider for appropriate cardiovascular management. Referrals may include a cardio-oncologist, cardiologist, internist or primary care providers with expertise in chronic disease management or appropriate allied health provider (i.e. exercise physiologist, dietitian, or psychologist) to manage CV risk factors or cardiovascular disease accordingly. The specific thresholds for referral will depend on unique patient considerations and availability of services within each clinical location [3, 6, 11].

Management of risk for cancer therapy related cardiovascular toxicity (CTR-CVT) is only one aspect of the care that needs to be effectively integrated into the overall care of individuals with cancer to optimize their health outcomes and minimize cost, time demands, and duplications. Careful care planning that takes into account individual preferences is key as is effective multidisciplinary management that involves oncology, primary care, pharmacy, allied health providers and specialized services. Shared care models may be beneficial involving primary care providers and specialists [6].

Care planning needs to consider unique resource constraints applicable to cancer and non-cancer workforces around the globe [3]. Access to cancer care is limited in many places around the world as is access to specialized cardiology, cardio-oncology, and primary care resources that are critical in the management of CV risk factors and CVD. Careful consideration of existing resources to plan tailored care delivery is needed. In a setting where resources are limited, care planning should emphasize lifestyle modification to optimize CVD risk factors and utilization of primary care resources and community health workers for risk reduction and follow-up.

CPS 2. Cancer care providers should undertake a CV risk assessment for all individuals prior to commencement of cancer therapy

Prior to commencing treatment, the cancer care team should ensure that all individuals with cancer are assessed for risk of CV toxicity [3, 6, 11]. This includes identifying pre-existing CV disease and CV risk factors, documenting prior exposure to cardiotoxic cancer therapy, performing and/or gathering relevant cardiovascular related investigations, and performing a baseline risk assessment [3, 6, 11].

Cancer care providers should undertake a thorough history, physical examination, and gather/perform relevant cardiac investigations [6] (Table 2). Baseline investigations should include HbA1c and lipids (LDL, HDL, and total cholesterol) in those undergoing systemic therapy [6]. Left ventricular (LV) function assessment should be performed at baseline for all individuals treated with cancer drugs associated with LV dysfunction (e.g. anthracyclines) [6]. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), with global longitudinal strain (GLS) if available, is considered the gold standard [12–14]. An electrocardiogram (ECG/EKG) should be performed prior to initiation of systemic therapy for individuals with pre-existing CV risk factors or CVD and for individuals being considered for treatment with cancer therapies associated with a high risk of arrhythmias or CV toxicity [4, 6, 15, 16]. Pre-treatment (baseline) CV risk assessment

All individuals with cancer should have a baseline assessment for risk of CTR-CVT. While general population CV risk tools may be applicable in individuals with cancer [17], specific tools for this population are available which classify individuals into low, medium, and high-risk groups for CTR-CVT. The HFA-ICOS risk stratification tool (www.mdcalc.com) provides an estimate of risk of cardiotoxicity in the setting of seven different classes of potentially cardidotoxic drugs (e.g. anthracyclines, HER-2 targeted therapies). Applying this tool prior to initiation of cancer therapy allows clinicians to stratify individuals with cancer prior to starting treatment into low risk (< 10%), medium risk (10–19%), or high risk (≥ 20%) of cardiovascular complications [11]. Risk stratification can also help guide CV monitoring and determine which individuals may benefit from a cardiology/cardio-oncology referral [11] (Table 2). Individuals who are considered low and medium risk should be followed by their cancer care providers as per institutional practice and referred to cardiology/cardio-oncology if they develop signs or symptoms of cardiovascular toxicity. For all individuals at high/very high baseline risk, consider the addition of baseline cardiac biomarkers (troponin, B-type natriuretic peptide - BNP) and refer to a cardio-oncologist, cardiologist, or health care provider with experience in cardio-oncology ideally prior to beginning cancer therapy (or as soon as possible at initiation of therapy) [6, 11]. A multidisciplinary discussion to discuss the risk/benefits of the proposed cancer therapy should take place for all individuals who are at high/very high risk of CV toxicity [11]. Individuals with complex care needs (for example those who are pregnant or those with prior exposure to cardiotoxic agents) may benefit from a referral to a specialist cardio-oncologist or other appropriate care provider to ensure appropriate management [6].

CPS 3. Cancer care providers should ensure that measures are taken to mitigate the risk of CTR-CVT prior to and during cancer therapy

Measures should be implemented either by the cancer care team or by an appropriate specialist to mitigate the risk of CTR-CVT (Table 3). This includes recommending and supporting lifestyle modifications, managing comorbid CVD, addressing poorly controlled modifiable CV risk factors, and selecting appropriate cancer therapies to decrease CTR-CVT in high/very-high risk individuals [3, 6, 18]. Immediately prior to and during cancer therapy can be a difficult time to optimize CVD/CV risk factors; however, care should be taken to address any of these poorly controlled co-morbidities to facilitate safe delivery of cancer therapies and to mitigate the risk of future CTR-CVT [18].

Measures to mitigate the risk of CTR-CVT prior to and during cancer therapy

Cancer care providers should counsel all individuals on and/or arrange for an appropriate referral for lifestyle modifications including heart healthy diet, smoking cessation, weight management, and exercise [19, 20]. These lifestyle behaviors are beneficial in improving treatment tolerance and cancer outcomes including prevention of recurrence and prolongation of survival [21, 22]. Further, cancer care providers should ensure an appropriate health care provider is managing comorbid CVD and addressing modifiable CV risk factors such as hypertension [18–20].

The key strategy for reducing risk of CTR-CVT remains minimizing exposure to cardiotoxic agents by limiting cumulative doses of systemic therapy and/or radiation known to cause CV toxicity [23]. Cardioprotective agents such as dexrazoxane should be considered in individuals at high risk of CV toxicity such as those with prior exposure to cardiotoxic agents (e.g. anthracyclines) where further therapy with cardiotoxic agents (e.g. anthracyclines ≥ 250 mg/m2 life time dose) is warranted [23–25]. For individuals at risk of CV toxicity, radiation therapy intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) or proton therapy should be considered if resources are available [6, 26–28]. Optimize the total radiation dose and fractionation schedules for individuals receiving thoracic (including mediastinal, left breast and chest wall) radiation. Reduce mean heart dose through techniques such as deep inspiration breath-hold [29].

Multiple studies have evaluated the cardioprotective potential of neurohormonal antagonists and statins; however, current evidence does not support their routine use for primary prevention of CTR-CVT [23, 30]. These agents may be considered for patients with cancer at high/very-risk of cardiovascular toxicity in consultation with cardiology or cardio-oncology specialists [6]. SGLT-2 inhibitors have demonstrated clinical benefits in heart failure management, but existing data in oncology are largely observational and limited to individuals with diabetes [31].

CPS 4. Cancer care providers should monitor individuals for the development of new symptoms of CV toxicity or poorly controlled CV risk factors with referral to the cardio-oncology/cardiology team as required

The cancer care team should assess patients during active cancer treatment for signs and symptoms concerning for new and/or progressive CVD or poorly controlled CV risk factors, and refer as appropriate [6, 11]. Re-assessment should occur at major transition points in care (new therapies, change in therapy), when new clinical symptoms arise, when there is a sustained change in vital signs, and when drugs that are known to exacerbate CV risk factors are used (i.e. steroids causing hyperglycemia and hypertension) [6, 11]. The cancer care team should follow drug specific recommendations for cardiac monitoring [6, 32, 33]. While the threshold for referral to cardiology may vary according to the capacity and experience of oncology and cardiology/cardio-oncology services in each setting, potential indications for referral should include new cardiovascular disease which has developed while on treatment, deterioration of ejection fraction (e.g. LVEF < 53% and/or global longitudinal strain drop > 15%), development of new onset arrythmias (e.g. atrial fibrillation), poorly controlled or exacerbation of pre-existing CV risk factors (e.g. hypertension), and concern for drug-drug interactions [6].

CPS 5. At completion of treatment, cancer care providers should ensure that individuals have a CV surveillance plan and recommendations for prevention of CTR-CVT

Cancer care providers should reassess CV status and risk at the end of cancer treatment(s), or for those individuals on life-long therapy due to advanced disease, periodically during treatment, based on CV risk (Table 4). There is limited data on the optimal frequency of cardiac imaging in this population [3]. CV toxicity may not develop for 5–10 years following completion of cancer treatment and some toxicities such as valvular disease develop after 10 years [6, 34]. As survivors age, they are also at higher risk of ischaemic heart disease, peripheral vascular disease, and stroke [6, 27, 35, 36]. It is important that cancer care providers educate individuals and their primary health care providers on the potential long term cardiovascular toxicities associated with systemic and/or radiation therapy. Guidance for all cancer survivors

Cancer care providers should provide tailored lifestyle advice to all cancer survivors. This includes healthy diet, regular physical activity, avoidance of tobacco use, reduction in alcohol usage, and maintaining healthy body weight. There is strong evidence to support the benefits of exercise to mitigate the effects of treatment and emerging evidence of cardiovascular benefit [37, 38]. A recent white paper from the International Cardio-Oncology Society (IC-OS) highlights the importance of cardio-oncology rehabilitation and exercise (CORE) to improve the CV health of cancer survivors. The Challenge prospective randomized trial demonstrated improved physical fitness and cancer survival in the exercise group compared to the health-education group [21, 39]. Other complimentary modalities such as nutrition counselling, psychosocial support and rehabilitation referrals may be appropriate for those individuals who are at risk of CV toxicity. In all individuals exposed to cancer therapies (regardless of post-therapy risk), if CV risk factors are present, they should be aggressively managed to recommended targets.

Guidance for surveillance based on risk of CTR-CVT

All individuals who have received cancer therapy should be provided with recommendations for annual CV risk assessment (including history of cardiac symptoms and measurement of CV risk factors) by their routine health care team (e.g. primary care provider, nurse practitioner or oncology provider based on local health care resources) [6, 40, 41]. An echocardiogram at 12 months is recommended for those individuals treated with HER-2 targeted therapy and for indivdiuals with early stage cancer treated with curative intent who have recieved a cumulative dose of ≥ 250 mg/m2 of doxorubucin or equivalent as per the ESC cardio-oncology guidelines (class I recommendation; level of evidence B) [6].

Individuals considered to be at moderate risk of CV toxicity maybe considered for repeat transthoracic echocardiogram every 5 years (class IIb recommendation; level of evidence C); however, these is minimal data to support this recommendation [6]. The health care team should consider referral to a cardiologist/cardio-oncologist if new cardiac symptoms develop. If resources are available, it is reasonable for individuals at high/very high risk to be referred to a cardio-oncologist, cardiologist, or internist with expertise in caring for this population (if not already). Individuals should be followed regularly by a multidisciplinary team for cardiac surveillance and consideration of cardioprotection strategies based on individualized risk [6] (Table 4).

Cardio-oncology or cardiology (if no cardio-oncology expertise available) follow-up is recommended for all patients with CV toxicities that are directly related to cancer therapy such as development of tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI)-mediated hypertension, anthracycline or HER-2 targeted LV dysfunction, vascular toxicities,QT prolongation, or immune checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis. Follow-up may also be appropriate for individuals with complex needs such as those who are planning pregnancy in the setting of prior exposure to cardiotoxic cancer therapy [6].

Cancer survivors who develop heart failure or CVD at any time following a cancer diagnosis should be referred to a cardiac rehabilitation program if the resources are available [42].

Cardiovascular care for individuals with advanced cancer

There is limited evidence to support routine cardiovascular investigations (e.g. echocardiograms) in adults with metastatic cancer who remain on lifelong cancer therapy (unless required for the specific cancer therapy), particularly in the absence of known CVD or CV risk factors; however cardiac surveillance may be reasonable based on need for optimization of cancer treatment and cardiovascular needs.

Conclusion

Cardiovascular disease is a major cause of mortality and morbidity among cancer survivors. Cancer care providers play an important role in cardiovascular risk assessment, risk reduction, and follow-up of cardiovascular health in individuals with cancer as part of a multidisciplinary approach that prioritizes early intervention and behavioural modification that can be beneficial for both CVD and cancer.
