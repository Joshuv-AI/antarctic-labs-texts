Unlocking the acquired ultraviolet-resistance and enhanced antioxidant potential of pterostilbene-loaded pharmaceutical-dots
Authors: Rujing Wang, Lan Zou, Dandan Mi, Zijian Song, Tiantian Liu, Han Yuan, Youbai Chen, Dong Wang, Mengnan Zhao, Sanjun Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ultraviolet (UV) radiation, particularly UVA and UVB, induces skin damage through oxidative stress and inflammation. However, existing UV protection materials fail to simultaneously block UV rays and scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS). To address this dual challenge, we developed pterostilbene (PTE)-loaded Tween 80 micelles, designated Tw80-based pharmaceutical dots (PTE-Tw80@PDs). This nanosystem exploited the chromophore's aggregation induced emission property: it absorbs UV radiation and re-emit it as harmless longer-wavelength light (410 nm), while delivering potent antioxidant activity. Physicochemical characterization confirmed PTE-Tw80@PDs to exhibit a narrow size distribution (∼10 nm), significantly enhanced fluorescence, and broad-spectrum UV attenuation (200–400 nm). In vitro assays demonstrated superior ROS scavenging over free PTE, reduced UV-induced cytotoxicity in HUVEC and HaCaT cells, and excellent biocompatibility based on Tween 80. This nanoplatform integrates UV shielding, ROS neutralization, and safe delivery, offering a promising strategy for comprehensive photoprotection.

Introduction

Ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, the pre-eminent injurious component of the solar spectrum, is taxonomically parsed into UVA (320–400 nm), UVB (280–320 nm) and UVC (200–280 nm) (Chen et al., 2024). Stratospheric ozone quantitatively sequesters UVC, whereas UVA and UVB traverse the atmosphere and precipitate cutaneous and ocular insults of considerable magnitude. UVA, constituting 90–95% of terrestrial UV, penetrates to the dermal stratum, where it orchestrates photoageing by transcriptionally up-regulating matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) that proteolytically dismantle collagenous and elastic fibres (Schneider et al., 2017). UVB, although less abundant (5–10%), is energetically more potent; confined to the epidermis, it engenders cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers and 6–4 photoproducts, while simultaneously unleashing an intense oxidative burst that propels mutagenesis and photocarcinogenesis (Silva et al., 2021). In ocular tissues, UV exposure precipitates photokeratitis, cortical and nuclear cataracts, and macular degeneration—risks that escalate precipitously in high-albedo milieus such as alpine or snowy terrains (Xiong et al., 2019); (Yam and Kwok, 2014). At the dermal level, UV imprints a protracted melanogenic response, accrues insidiously, and culminates in collagen fragmentation, genomic instability and malignant transformation (Li et al., 2023). Convergent pathobiology pathways are driven by the breakdown of redox homeostasis. Specifically, the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and pro-oxidant components overwhelms antioxidant defenses, which in turn induces an NF-κB-centered inflammatory cascade, inhibits DNA repair, and reduces cutaneous Langerhans cells, thereby facilitating apoptotic or oncogenic trajectories (Wang et al., 2024). Consequently, efficacious mitigation of UV injury mandates not only physical interception of incident radiation but also the targeted scavenging of intracellular ROS and the restoration of physiological redox equilibrium.

In recent years, anti-ultraviolet research has achieved decisive advances across materials science, nutrition and drug-delivery disciplines. Physical blockers, typified by TiO2 and ZnO nanoparticles, abrogate UV insult primarily through reflection and scattering, yet their efficacy attenuates steeply toward longer-wavelength UVA and visible light; moreover, the documented percutaneous transit of TiO2 nanocrystals raises credible concerns regarding systemic toxicodynamics (Al-Attafi et al., 2023). Chemical sunscreen products such as oxybenzone operate via photon absorption, but suffer from incomplete spectral coverage (notably in the UVA window) and unresolved ecotoxicological concerns (Araújo et al., 2022). Botanical antioxidants such as ascorbate, polyphenols and congeners undergo rapid clearance (t1/2 < 2 h) and display negligible stratum corneum permeability, curtailing their therapeutic reach (Kasote et al., 2015; Tun et al., 2020). Current paradigms therefore remain dichotomous: they either intercept incident photons or quench reactive oxygen species, rarely both. This mechanistic inadequacy has allowed UV-related morbidity to escalate unabated, underscoring an urgent imperative for next-generation edible or pharmaceutical constructs that co-integrate broad-spectrum photoprotection, potent ROS detoxification, superior biocompatibility and precise tissue tropism.

Free radical scavengers, or antioxidants, constitute a class of compounds that neutralize ROS, thereby preventing or mitigating oxidative damage (Haider et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2019; Yi et al., 2024). Despite being encumbered by sub-optimal bioavailability (Habtemariam, 2020) and rapid metabolic inactivation (Andrade et al., 2023), natural antioxidants remain an indispensable repository for mining potent radical-quenching activity. They have garnered significant attention due to their favorable profiles, including potent redox activity, minimal adverse effects, and tolerability for chronic administration, offering distinct advantages over synthetic counterparts (Abbigeri et al., 2025; Coulombier et al., 2021).

Pterostilbene (PTE, trans-3,5-dimethyoxy-4′-hydroxystilbene), a polyphenolic stilbene abundant in blueberries, grapes and certain medicinal plants, has emerged as a compelling bioactive compound with diverse therapeutic potential (Ding et al., 2024; Dou et al., 2024; Pan et al., 2019). Preclinical studies highlight its multifunctional pharmacological profile, encompassing antioxidant (Lin et al., 2022), anti-inflammatory (Patil et al., 2024), chemopreventive (Surien et al., 2023), and antitumor activities (He et al., 2024). Functioning as a potent radical quencher, its phenolic hydroxyl donates a hydrogen atom to neutralize diverse ROS with exceptional efficacy, rendering it an appealing candidate for systemic redox intervention (Shukla et al., 2024). Its capacity to counteract or attenuate UV-induced injury, however, remains equivocal.

In our preliminary studies, PTE self-assembles upon UV irradiation and spontaneously emits fluorescence, indicating its capacity for ultraviolet absorption, an aggregation-induced emission (AIE) process that dissipates incident photons via radiative decay (Sahana et al., 2023). To augment both colloidal stability and aqueous dispersibility, we encapsulated PTE within polysorbate 80 (Tween 80, Tw80) micelles, generating PTE-Tw80@formulations. Compared with free PTE or unencapsulated aggregates, these micelles exhibit markedly amplified intrinsic fluorescence, implying concomitantly heightened UV attenuation. This non-covalent formulation strategy, which endows the drug molecule with self-illuminating properties, is hereby termed “PTE@pharmaceutical dots” (PTE@PDs) (Zou et al., 2025). Notably, Tween80 is already approved for food use at concentrations up to 1% in products such as ice cream, beverages, and baked goods (Ogulur et al., 2023), providing a regulatory advantage that should streamline the clinical translation of PTE@PDs. Nevertheless, the precise extent to which PTE@PDs can sequester and attenuate UV energy remains to be rigorously quantified.

In this study, a non-covalent supramolecular strategy transformed the individually non-emissive constituents Tween 80 and PTE into a self-illuminating photosensitizing platform, designated PTE-Tw80@PDs. Tween 80 serves dual roles as a hydrophilic stabilizer and bioavailability enhancer, while the nano-confinement architecture of PTE-Tw80@PDs enables sustained radical scavenging activity through spatial restriction of intramolecular motions. The formulation was subjected to comprehensive physicochemical characterization using FTIR, UV–vis, and fluorescence spectroscopy, confirming successful encapsulation and AIE-active photoluminescence. Antioxidant efficacy radical neutralization capacity compared to free PTE. Biocompatibility was validated via MTT assays on HUVEC and HaCaT cells, revealing negligible cytotoxicity at therapeutic concentrations. Intracellular ROS levels, measured using DCFH-DA fluorescence probes, confirming significant attenuation of H2O2-induced oxidative stress following PTE-Tw80@PDs treatment (Sun et al., 2025). UV-protective capacity was evaluated through spectrophotometric absorbance/transmittance profiling and UV-induced cytotoxicity assays in HUVEC and HaCaT cells, with PTE-Tw80@PDs exhibiting broadband UV attenuation (200–400 nm) and reduction in UV-mediated phototoxicity compared to untreated controls. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PTE@PDs function as a topically deliverable platform that simultaneously affords broad-spectrum UV interception (UV photoprotection), potent ROS eradication (antioxidant defense) and uncompromised biocompatibility.

Materials and methods

Materials

Pterostilbene (PTE, >97% purity) was purchased from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Tween 80 (pharmaceutical grade) was acquired from Kelong Chemical Co. Ltd. (Chengdu, China). Thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT, >98%) were supplied by Rhawn Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Calcein/PI cell viability assay kit and ROS detection kit were obtained from UElandy Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). All other chemicals and solvents used were of analytical reagent grade unless otherwise specified.

Preparation of PTE@NPs and PTE-Tw80@PDs

The PTE compound was initially solubilized in 0.3 mL of DMSO through gentle stirring at ambient temperature, facilitating spontaneous self-assembly into colloidal nanoparticles (PTE@NPs). Concurrently, the PTE-Tween 80 composite underwent analogous dissolution in an equivalent DMSO volume (0.3 mL) under identical conditions, yielding surfactant-stabilized polymeric nanodroplets (PTE-Tw80@PDs). Following formulation, both colloidal systems were subjected to ultrasonic dispersion in 2.7 mL of aqueous buffer to achieve homogeneous aqueous suspensions.

Preparation of nanoparticles with various Tween 80 concentration

PTE formulations were prepared by dissolving specified amounts of Tween 80 (4, 5, 6, 8, 9 mg) in 0.3 mL of DMSO alongside PTE, followed by gentle magnetic stirring at room temperature to ensure complete solubilization. Each formulation was then diluted into 2.7 mL of aqueous buffer under ultrasonic agitation to yield stable colloidal suspensions.

Preparation of nanoparticles with various PTE concentration

PTE formulations were prepared by systematically varying PTE loadings (1, 3, 6, 9, 12, or 15 mg) while maintaining a constant Tween 80 content in 0.3 mL of DMSO. Each mixture underwent magnetic stirring at ambient temperature to ensure complete dissolution. Following solubilization, formulations were diluted into 2.7 mL of aqueous buffer under ultrasonic agitation to generate stable colloidal suspensions.

Characterization of test formulations

Particle size distribution and zeta potential analysis

The physicochemical properties of the test formulations, including hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI) and zeta potential, were measured using a dynamic light scattering (DLS) analyzer (BeNano 90 Zeta, Bettersize, China). Three independent measurements were performed for each sample to minimize experimental variability and multiple scattering effects.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis

A droplet of PTE-Tw80@PDs suspension was deposited onto a carbon-coated copper grid to form a thin film. Negative staining was performed using a 2% (w/v) phosphotungstic acid solution for 3 min to enhance contrast for TEM imaging. The grid was subsequently air-dried at room temperature. Morphological characterization of the nanoparticles was conducted using a transmission electron microscope (HT-7800, Hitachi, Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV.

UV–visible (UV–vis) assay

UV–vis absorption spectra were recorded using an Analytik Jena Specord® 200 spectrophotometer (Germany). Following suspension in deionized water, the test formulations were analyzed in triplicate using UV–vis spectroscopy. Absorption spectra were acquired across a wavelength range of 200–700 nm to characterize their optical properties under standardized conditions (temperature: 25 °C; path length: 1 cm).

FT-IR spectroscopy analysis

Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was performed using an Agilent Cary 610 FTIR spectrometer equipped with a diamond ATR accessory. Test powders were uniformly deposited onto a quartz plate and analyzed across a spectral range of 400 to 4000 cm−1 at room temperature. Spectral data were collected in transmittance mode with a resolution of 4 cm−1 to ensure precise characterization of functional group vibrations.

Encapsulation efficacy and drug loading

The free drug was isolated by ultracentrifugation at 36,000 rpm for 2 h following trisodium citrate addition (180 mg) to 1 mL of PTE-Tw80@PDs solution (Han et al., 2019). The supernatant was subsequently mixed with methanol (1:1, v/v), filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter, and analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to determine the free drug content (Wfree). PTE-Tw80@PDs solution (200 μL) was demulsified with methanol (800 μL) under sonication. Following filtration through a 0.45 μm syringe filter, the total drug content (Wtotal) was quantified by HPLC. The encapsulation efficacy (EE%) and drug loading (DL%) were calculated using the following formulas:EE%=Wtotal−Wfree/Wtotal×100%DL%=Wtotal−Wfree/Wall×100%where Wtotal is the total amount of PTE within PTE-Tw80@PDs and Wfree is the amount of unloaded PTE in the liquid supernatant, Wall is the total amounts of PTE and Tween 80 added in the PTE-Tw80@PDs.

In vitro drug release study

Nanoparticles were suspended in 0.9% NaCl solution at 37 °C under controlled conditions. At predetermined time intervals, samples were carefully collected and subsequently resuspended in fresh 0.9% NaCl solution to maintain sink conditions (Chen et al., 2020). The quantity of PTE released into the supernatant was quantified using HPLC spectrophotometer, ensuring high sensitivity and accuracy. The cumulative release percentage (C) was calculated based on the following equation.C=Wr/W1×100%where Wr is the amount of PTE released in the liquid and W1 is the amount of PTE loaded in the PTE-Tw80@PDs.

Optical properties of test formulation

Aggregation induced emission (AIE) optical properties

AIE-related fluorescence assays were performed using PTE-loaded nanoparticles, as detailed below. The nanoparticles were suspended in water/DMSO cosolvent systems with varying water fractions (0–95%, v/v). Fluorescence measurements were conducted using a multimode microplate reader (SpectraMax iD5, Molecular Devices, USA) with emission spectra recorded across a wavelength range of 290–750 nm.

Solvents' impact on the optical characteristics

PTE-loaded nanoparticles were suspended in 0.3 mL of various organic solvents (DMSO, ETOH, MEOH, ACE, THF). The mixtures were stirred gently at room temperature until complete dissolution. Each formulation was subsequently diluted into 2.7 mL of aqueous media to produce homogeneous suspensions. Fluorescence emission spectra of the suspensions were then recorded using a SpectraMax iD5 multimode microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA) at the specified excitation wavelength.

PTE-loaded nanoparticles concentration impact on the optical properties

The nanoparticle dispersions were diluted with deionized water to establish a concentrations gradient spanning 0.025–1 mg/mL. Emission spectra were acquired using a multimode microplate reader (SpectraMax iD5, Molecular Devices, USA) with excitation set at 250 nm. Additionally, fluorescence intensity measurements were performed at defined wavelengths (λex = 350 nm, λem = 400 nm) to quantify emission efficiency under standardized conditions.

Excitation wavelength dependency on the optical characteristics

PTE-loaded nanoparticles were subjected to spectral analysis using a SpectraMax iD5 multimode microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA) across a systematic excitation wavelength scan from 250 to 340 nm. This enabled comprehensive characterization of their photophysical properties under varying excitation conditions.

Fluorescence excitation, emission spectra and decay curves of PTE-Tw80@PDs

Fluorescence excitation and emission spectra of PTE-Tw80@PDs was measured using an FLS 1000 steady-state fluorescence spectrophotometer (Edinburgh, UK) equipped with a 450 W continuous xenon lamp. Spectra were acquired over a wavelength range of 250–850 nm with automated correction for spectral overlap. Fluorescence decay curves were subsequently recorded on the same instrument, utilizing a time-correlated single-photon counting module for lifetime measurements.

Quantum yield (QY)

The fluorescence quantum yield (QY), defined as the ratio of emitted fluorescence photons to absorbed photons, was determined using an FLS1000 spectrofluorimeter (Edinburgh, UK) equipped with an integrating sphere. The system was configured with orthogonal (90°) entrance and exit ports to minimize back-reflection artifacts, and the sample (PTE-Tw80@PDs) was positioned at the sphere's geometric center to ensure uniform illumination. Spectral measurements were corrected for instrument-specific response functions, including those of the integrating sphere, monochromators, and detectors. System calibration was performed using a NIST-traceable calibrated tungsten lamp to establish photon flux detection efficiency across the operational wavelength range. In this context, τ represents the fluorescence lifetime, while Ktotal, Kr and Knr denote the total, radiative and non-radiative decay rate constants, respectively.

Viscosity dependence assay

500 μL of PTE-Tw80@PDs (3 mg/mL, in terms of PTE) was mixed with glycerol (Gly) solution of varying concentrations (0.1–0.5 mg/mL) to create microenvironmental viscosity gradients. Emission spectra were recorded using a multimode microplate reader (SpectraMax iD5, Molecular Devices, USA) with excitation set at 250 nm.

Temperature dependence assay

500 μL of PTE-Tw80@PDs (3 mg/mL, in terms of PTE) was aliquoted into EP tubes and incubated at distinct temperatures (4, 25, 37, and 60 °C) for 1 h to assess thermal effects on emission behavior. Emission spectra were acquired using a multimode microplate reader (SpectraMax iD5, Molecular Devices, USA) with excitation set at 250 nm.

Antioxidant activity analysis

DPPH free radical scavenging ability

The radical scavenging activity of PTE-Tw80@PDs against DPPH was evaluated following a standardized protocol. Briefly, stock solutions of PTE-Tw80@PDs were serially diluted to concentrations of 5, 10, 20, 30, 35, 40, 45 μg/mL. Then 1 mL of diluted sample was mixed with 1 mL DPPH (50 μg/mL in anhydrous ethanol; experimental group). 1 mL of diluted sample was combined with 1 mL of anhydrous ethanol to account for background absorbance (control group). 1 mL of DPPH solution (dissolved in anhydrous ethanol) was added to 1 mL of deionized water to establish the maximum DPPH absorbance (blank group). All mixtures were vortexed thoroughly and incubated for 60 min at room temperature to ensure complete reaction. Absorbance measurements were then recorded at 517 nm. Parallel experiments were conducted for free PTE and the positive control VC. The DPPH radical scavenging capacity (%) was calculated using the formula:DPPH scavenging rate%=1−Asample−AcontrolAblank×100%

ABTS+ free radical scavenging ability

The ABTS+ radical scavenging experiment was conducted following a previously established protocol. In brief, PTE-Tw80@PDs were diluted into various concentrations (1, 5, 25, 100, 125 μg/mL). The ABTS+ solution was prepared by combining a 7 mM ABTS anhydrous ethanol solution with a 2.45 mM potassium persulfate (K2S2O4) solution at 1:1 (v/v) ratio and incubating the mixture at room temperature for 16 h in the dark. For the experimental group, 3 mL of each prepared sample was mixed with 1 mL of the ABTS+ solution (experimental group). Additionally, 3 mL of each prepared sample was mixed with 1 mL of anhydrous ethanol (control group). Furthermore, 3 mL of deionized water was mixed with 1 mL of the ABTS+ solution (blank group). All three groups were then allowed to react at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. Subsequently, the absorbance was measured at 734 nm. The same procedure was repeated for PTE and VC, with VC serving as the reference standard. The ABTS+ radical scavenging capacity (%) was calculated using the following formula:ABTS+scavenging rate%=1−Asample−AcontrolAblank×100%

Biosecurity and cellular antioxidant activity analysis

Cell cytotoxicity assessment

The biocompatibility of PTE-Tw80@PDs was assessed through the MTT assay utilizing HUVEC cells. In brief, HUVEC cells were seeded into a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, free PTE, PTE@NPs and PTE-Tw80@PDs were dissolved in a cell culture medium to attain final PTE concentrations of 0, 0.4, 2, 10, 25, 50, 100, 200 μM. The cells were then exposed to these solutions for an additional 48 h. Following this, 100 μL of a 0.5 mg/mL MTT solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. To solubilize the formazan crystals, 100 μL of DMSO was added to each well after removing the medium. The absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a multimode microplate reader (SpectraMax iD5, USA). Furthermore, cell viability and cytotoxicity were evaluated using the Calcein-AM/PI assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Cellular antioxidant activity assessment

To assess the cellular antioxidant capabilities of PTE-Tw80@PDs, ROS assay was conducted in HUVEC cells. HUVEC cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density a 1 × 105 cells per well and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with VC (5 μM), Tw80 (12.8 μg/mL), free PTE (50 μM) and PTE-Tw80@PDs (50 μM) for an additional 24-h period. Following this, the culture medium was replaced with a fresh one containing 250 μM of H2O2. For the ROS assay, the DCFH-DA probe was added to the medium, and the cells were co-incubated for 30 min. ImageJ software was employed to quantify the intensity of the fluorescence.

Enhancing ultraviolet photoprotection

HUVEC cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density a 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h. Cells were subsequently treated with vitamin C (VC, 5 μM), Tw80 (12.8 μg/mL), free PTE (25 or 50 μM) or PTE-Tw80@PDs (25 or 50 μM). Following a 30 min exposure to UV irradiation (365 nm, 10 J/cm2), cells were co-cultured in formulation for an additional 24-h period. Cell viability was quantified using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a multimode microplate reader (Flexstation 3, USA).

HaCaT cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density a 5 × 104 cells per well and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h. Cells were subsequently treated with PTE-Tw80@PDs (50 μM), TiO2 (5 μg/mL) or ZnO (5 μg/mL). Following a 30 min exposure to UV irradiation (365 nm, 10 J/cm2), cells were co-cultured in formulation for an additional 24-h period. The cells were then exposed to these solutions for an additional 24 h. Following this, 100 μL of a 0.5 mg/mL MTT solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. To solubilize the formazan crystals, 100 μL of DMSO was added to each well after removing the medium. The absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a multimode microplate reader (SpectraMax iD5, USA).

Statistical analysis

Error bars were presented to represent the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between two groups, the Student's t-test was utilized. All statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 9 software.

Results and discussion

Optical properties of PTE@NPs

Beyond its established role as a potent antioxidant, we unexpectedly revealed the photophysical properties of PTE: the self-assembled nanostructuring spontaneously ignites intrinsic fluorescence, thereby redefining its prospective applications. The self-assembled nanostructuring spontaneously elicits intrinsic fluorescence, thereby redefining its prospective applications. Consequently, the emulsifier-free self-assembled nanoparticles formed by PTE were herein designated as PTE@NPs. As depicted in Fig. 1A, the maximum excitation wavelengths for PTE@NPs were determined to be 260 nm and 350 nm, with a corresponding emission wavelength at 410 nm. PTE contains phenyl rings and conjugates double-bond systems, which promote intramolecular electronic resonance and π-conjugation. This delocalized electron system enables efficient charge mobility within the molecule, giving rise to multiple fluorescence emission pathways and ultimately producing a bimodal fluorescence spectrum. Subsequent characterization of PTE@NPs optical properties revealed a striking aggregation-induced emission (AIE) behavior. Briefly, we observed a significant fluorescence enhancement with increasing water content in water/DMSO mixed solvents (Fig. 1B-C). Solvent-dependent fluorescence analysis serves as a critical tool for characterizing the optical properties of AIEgens. The polarity of the solvent environment significantly influences the electronic states or chromophores embedded in the nanostructure, thereby modulating the absorption characteristics and emission behavior of the fluorescent system. PTE@NPs demonstrated pronounced solvent-dependent optical properties, exhibiting maximum fluorescence intensity in DMSO (Fig. 1E). The solvent polarity significantly influenced the emission properties of samples, with the fluorescence maximum exhibiting a distinct blueshift from 400 nm in polar DMSO to 390 nm in less polar THF. The observed hypsochromic shift in emission was accompanied by a concurrent blueshift of the π-π absorption band, indicating stabilization of the excited state in polar solvents through strengthened intramolecular charge transfer interactions. To elucidate the unusual emission characteristics of nanomaterials in aqueous solution, we conducted comprehensive concentration-dependent fluorescence studies. The spectroscopic analysis revealed a striking positive correlation between fluorescence intensity and nanoparticle concentration (Fig. 1F-G). Notably, the emission intensity exhibited a concentration-dependent enhancement, akin to the AIE effect.

Furthermore, we observed a concentration-dependent fluorescence enhancement in nanoscale systems, marked by a 5.3-fold increase in fluorescence intensity at elevated PTE concentrations (Fig. 2C). The concentration-dependent fluorescence enhancement suggests that spatial constraints at the nanoscale alter radiative recombination pathways. Notably, despite this substantial fluorescence enhancement, the emission maximum maintained exceptional stability across all concentration gradients (Fig. 2D), indicating effective surface passivation through our preparation protocol and preservation of quantum confinement characteristics. The nanoparticle assemblies, stabilized by non-covalent intermolecular interactions, exhibited gradual precipitation within 3-h post-preparation, indicative of inherent thermodynamic instability in aqueous media. To address this critical barrier, we developed polymer-based stabilization protocols to enhance nanomaterial integrity while preserving intrinsic photoluminescence intensity.

Tween 80 modulation on nanoparticle physicochemical properties

To simultaneously augment colloidal stability and optical performance, we leveraged the amphiphilic, non-ionic surfactant Tween 80 to sterically coat PTE@NPs, yielding Tw80-stabilized PTE@NPs. A systematic investigation was conducted to elucidate concentration property relationships, evaluating Tw80's impact on multiple nanoparticle parameters, such as hydrodynamic diameter, size distribution, zeta potential, long-term stability, and fluorescence emission properties. As depicted in Fig. 3A, the nanoformulations exhibited uniformly narrow size distributions (4–14 nm) across the entire Tween 80 interval investigated (4–9 mg), with negligible concentration-dependent variations in particle dimensions. To evaluate the physicochemical performance of Tw80-stabilized PTE@NPs, zeta potential analysis was performed to assess surface electrostatic properties (Table S1). As illustrated in Fig. 3C, varying the Tween 80 concentration within the 4–9 mg range resulted in zeta potential values that remained confined to a relatively narrow range of −19.57 to −23.84 mV. This consistency in surface charge across the tested concentrations highlights the formulation's robustness, demonstrating minimal variability in electrostatic surface properties under the evaluated conditions. Subsequent characterization employed fluorescence spectroscopy to investigate the surface properties of Tween 80-modified nanomaterials, providing insights into the surfactant's influence on their optical behavior (Fig. 3D). Notably, the peak emission wavelength remained precisely maintained at 390 nm, underscoring exceptional preservation of optical homeostasis. This striking invariance in photoluminescence characteristics suggests that the surface passivation mechanism mediated by PTE ligands maintains its functional integrity across the evaluated formulation space. Furthermore, fluorescence intensity measurements demonstrated remarkable stability across the concentration gradient (Fig. 3E), with negligible intensity fluctuations observed. Collectively, these findings—integrating with previously characterized stability profiles and size distribution analyses across 4–9 mg Tween 80 formulation range—culminated in the selection of 9 mg as the optimal surfactant concentration. This concentration achieves a thermodynamically favorable equilibrium between steric stabilization and surfactant adsorption kinetics, maximizing surface passivation efficacy while maintaining long-term colloidal stability.

PTE modulation on nanoparticle physicochemical properties

As a pivotal component in the nanomaterial system, the concentration-dependent effects of PTE were systematically investigated to elucidate its structural and functional implications on nanomaterial properties. Initial concentration screening across a 1–15 mg range revealed two critical trends (Table S2). First, DLS analysis (Fig. 4A) demonstrated a concentration-dependent reduction in hydrodynamic diameter, with particle sizes progressively decreasing from 218 nm to 4 nm as PTE loading increased (Fig. 4B). Complementary zeta potential measurements, a critical determinant of colloidal stability and functional performance, revealed a progressive attenuation of surface charge magnitude with increasing PTE concentration beyond 9 mg (Fig. 4C). Characteristics properties of PTE-Tw80@PDs

Building upon these insights, we adopt a rational design strategy to fine-tune the ratio of the nonionic stabilizer Tween 80 to the functional ligand PTE while integrating a photosensitizer. Upon self-assembly, Tw80-stabilized PTE@NPs displayed a pronounced reduction in size and a concomitant surge in fluorescence, transmuting a hitherto non-emissive molecule into an intrinsically luminous construct herein termed PTE-Tw80@PDs. This supramolecular nanocomposite achieved photonic enhancement exclusively through non-covalent interfacial coordination, obviating the need for covalent modification.

As shown in Fig. 5A, PTE@NPs assembled without Tween 80 underwent complete precipitation within 3 h, concomitant with the total loss of fluorescence. However, PTE-Tw80@PDs exhibited superior stability relative to conventional PTE@NPs during 30-day ambient storage. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed monodisperse spherical nanostructures with an average diameter of ∼25 nm (Fig. 5B) and consistent nanoscale dimensional uniformity. The EE and DL were determined to be 91.13% and 26.63% (Fig. S1), respectively. The release profile was characterized by an initial rapid phase within the first 10 h, followed by a sustained release stage (Fig. S2). These observations validate the formulation's capacity to stabilize PTE within a defined nanoarchitecture, ensuring reproducible physicochemical properties essential for advanced biomaterials. Given the established correlation between surface passivation and fluorescence efficiency, we characterized the excitation-dependent optical response of PTE and PTE-Tw80@PDs. In contrast to conventional AIE systems with excitation-wavelength dependent emission shifts, these nanomaterials displayed invariant spectral profiles across the visible spectrum (Fig. 5C-D). As shown in Fig. 5E, PTE-Tw80@PDs demonstrated enhanced fluorescence intensity compared to both PTE@NPs and free PTE, despite maintaining colloidal stability. This phenomenon was also validated via cellular uptake assay (Fig. S3). To elucidate the photophysical mechanisms underlying this enhancement, spectral overlap analysis of absorption and emission profiles was conducted (Fig. 5F). The absorption spectrum exhibits dual peaks at 222 nm and 310 nm, with the emission maximum at 410 nm yielding a Stokes shift of ∼100 nm relative to the primary absorption band. This substantial spectral separation minimizes self-quenching by reducing exciton-phonon coupling and suppresses non-radiative decay pathways. FT-IR spectroscopy (Fig. 5G) revealed characteristic vibrational bands in free PTE: a broad -OH stretch (3345 cm−1), aromatic C=C/C-C stretches (1598/1585 cm−1), C—O stretch (1353 cm−1) and trans-olefinic C—H bending (962 cm−1). While aromatic/olefinic signatures persist in nanocomposites, hydroxyl groups exhibit bathochromic shifts in PTE@NPs and PTE-Tw80@PDs, indicative of intermolecular hydrogen bonding.

Intriguingly, PTE-Tw80@PDs exhibited pronounced fluorescence enhancement. The emission intensity exhibits a positive correlation with both increasing viscosity and elevated temperature (Fig. S4). Consistent with AIE characteristics, this phenomenon arose from the synergistic interplay between molecular aggregation under viscous confinement and thermal activation. This trend, while modest in magnitude, provides empirical validation for the hypothesized viscosity-temperature co-dependence of fluorescence enhancement (Li et al., 2025). Moreover, the stability and optical properties were investigated using emulsion Span 80 (Fig. S5). The emulsion system demonstrated exceptional stability throughout the evaluation period. Fluorescence intensity remained invariant, exhibiting no significant fluctuations over time. Concurrently, DLS measurements revealed consistent particle dimensions (150–160 nm), with a marginal size increase (<7%) observed at day 7. This dual stability profile, comprising both optical and physical attributes, confirms the robustness of the formulation under prolonged evaluation.

As depicted in Fig. 5H, the nanomaterial displays well-defined excitation/emission maxima at 376 nm (excitation) and 421 nm (emission), respectively. Rigorous luminescence characterization induced time-correlated single photon counting (TCSPC), yielding an average lifetime of τ = 2.0497 ns (Fig. 5I) and quantum yield (QY) measurements via integrating sphere (1.61%, Fig. 5J). Photophysical characterization of PTE-Tw80@PDs quantified radiative (Kr) and non-radiative (Knr) decay rate constants (Fig. 5K). The results confirm that non-radiative decay pathways dominate deactivation processes, with Knr values (4.80) exceeding Kr (0.08) by an order of magnitude (60-fold). The exceptional efficiency of non-radiative decay pathways in AIE-active compounds facilitates the dissipation of absorbed ultraviolet energy through innocuous channels (e.g., thermal dissipation), effectively suppressing the formation of detrimental ROS. This inherent energy quenching mechanism confers robust photoprotection, making these materials highly effective for UV-shielding applications (Zayat et al., 2007). Spectroscopic analysis further reveals broad-spectrum UV absorption (200–400 nm), enabling efficient photoprotection against UV radiation. Notably, intrinsic fluorescence facilitates conversion of absorbed UV energy into non-toxic visible light, thereby mitigating UV-induced free radical generation. This dual mechanism enhances photostability while reducing oxidative stress, establishing PTE-Tw80@PDs as a multifunctional additive for formulations requiring antioxidant and UV-shielding capabilities.

Biosecurity and cellular antioxidant activity of PTE-Tw80@PDs

Conventional commercial systems, such as ZnO/TiO₂, raise significant biosafety concerns (Smijs and Pavel, 2011), whereas simpler carriers including PLGA and micelles exhibit limited drug encapsulation efficiency (He et al., 2021; Lefol et al., 2025). In contrast, PTE-Tw80@PDs appears to operate through a fundamentally distinct mechanism, leveraging food-grade Tween 80 as a stabilizer in combination with the bioactive compound pterostilbene, thereby effectively circumventing the aforementioned limitations. The cytotoxicity of Tween 80, free PTE and PTE-Tw80@PDs against HUVEC cells was evaluated using the MTT assay. As shown in Fig. 7A-C, all formulations exhibited negligible cytotoxicity across the tested concentration range (1–50 μM PTE). Notably, PTE-Tw80@PDs demonstrated dose-dependent enhancement of HUVEC viability after 24 h incubation at 37 °C, indicating favorable biocompatibility profiles. UV resistance of PTE-Tw80@PDs in HUVEC cells

Conventional agents that absorb ultraviolet yet fail to dissipate the absorbed energy remain vulnerable to photodamage (Baker et al., 2015). In contrast, PTE-Tw80@PDs exhibited an excitation maximum that falls precisely within the UVA window; upon illumination, they re-emit the captured energy as benign visible fluorescence. This efficient down-conversion suggests that the supramolecular pharmaceutical dots function as a photon sink, intercepting high-energy UVA and rerouting it into non-toxic longer wavelengths, thereby conferring intrinsic UVA resistance. To evaluate the UV-shielding efficacy of Tween 80, free PTE, and PTE-Tw80@PDs, spectral analysis confirmed that PTE-Tw80@PDs exhibit significantly enhanced UV–vis absorption capacity (250–400 nm) compared to both Tween 80 and free PTE (Fig. 9A), indicative of superior UV attenuation performance. Conversely, PTE-Tw80@PDs exhibited a pronounced attenuation in optical transmittance (Fig. 9B), a trend that strictly adheres to the Lambert-Beer law (Mamouei et al., 2021): diminished transmittance within the UV window signifies elevated absorptive capacity. Relative to Tween 80 alone or free PTE, PTE-Tw80@PDs therefore manifested markedly superior ultraviolet absorption. Concentration-dependent UV absorption and transmittance profiles were further analyzed to validate this property. As shown in Fig. 9C-D, absorbance increases with PTE-Tw80@PDs concentration while maintaining decrease light transmittance. Fig. 9E illustrates that, across 1–6 mg/mL, PTE-Tw80@PDs present broad, concentration-proportional UVA absorption; the higher the concentration, the more pronounced the attenuation. This dose-responsive behavior demonstrates that tunable UV-shielding efficacy is achieved by down-converting UV photons into innocuous, longer-wavelength light while preserving optical transparency. To elucidate how the distinctive photophysical behavior of PTE-Tw80@PDs modulates ROS formation relative to conventional UV filters under identical photon flux, we established three experimental groups: control (PBS), UV irradiation alone (UV), and UV with PTE-Tw80@PDs (UV + PDs) (Fig. 10C-D). This design enabled investigation of two interrelated hypotheses: (i) UV-induced ROS generation, to verify whether UV exposure promotes oxidative accumulation in HaCaT cells; and (ii) PTE-Tw80@PDs-mediated ROS suppression, to determine whether this system effectively attenuates UV-triggered oxidative stress. The photoprotective efficacy of PTE-Tw80@PDs was evaluated relative to avobenzone (AVO), a benchmark UV filter (Fig. S7). UV exposure significantly elevated intracellular ROS levels (p < 0.05), whereas pretreatment with either AVO or PTE-Tw80@PDs markedly attenuated this oxidative surge. Quantitative analysis revealed comparable ROS suppression efficacy between the two formulations under identical experimental conditions. These findings are rationalized by the dual mechanistic features of PTE-Tw80@PDs, specifically non-radiative decay-mediated energy dissipation and intrinsic ROS scavenging capacity. This integrated mechanistic framework strengthens the theoretical foundation for designing optimized UV-shielding systems.

The most critical aspect lies in elucidating the cellular mechanisms underlying the UV resistance conferred by PTE-Tw80@PDs. Matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-1, recognized as interstitial collagenase, plays a pivotal role in UV-induced long-term detrimental effects, including tumorigenesis and premature skin aging by cleaving interstitial collagens. UV radiation, a major exogenous genotoxic agent, induces cellular damage and activates the P53 protein, which rapidly co-localizes with phosphorylated-H2AX (γ-H2AX) to form DNA damage. In HaCaT model, we characterized UV-mediated DNA damage responses through immunofluorescence microscopy (Fig. 10E-F). Quantitative analysis revealed that UV exposure significantly upregulates MMP-1 and γ-H2AX expression, indicating both collagen degradation and DNA double-strand breaks. Critically, co-incubation with PTE-Tw80@PDs (50 μM) demonstrated suppression of both MMP-1 and γ-H2AX compared to UV-only controls, establishing its potential protective mechanism against photocarcinogenesis and photoaging.

While in vitro studies confirm the core hypothesis and demonstrate the strong UV-resistance efficacy of PTE-Tw80@PDs, the absence of skin-penetration assays and in vivo UV-exposure data remains a limitation. Future work will therefore focus on in vivo models, specifically: (1) evaluating photoprotection via UVA irradiation protocols in animals, with mechanistic analysis of ROS reduction, DNA damage, and oxidative stress markers in skin tissue; and (2) assessing long-term safety through repeated-dose toxicity and histopathological examination to establish biocompatibility for chronic UV-shielding applications.

Conclusion

This study systematically explored the photophysical properties of PTE and developed a multifunctional delivery system using food-grade Tween 80 as a stabilizer. The resulting PTE-Tw80@PDs exhibit enhanced physicochemical stability and three distinct functionalities: (1) AIE-enhanced photoluminescence, (2) broadband UV-shielding efficacy, and (3) robust antioxidant activity. Key findings include: i) pharmaceutical dot-enhanced UV absorption: The nano-confinement effect in PTE-Tw80@PDs restricts intramolecular rotation, enabling stable UV light absorption (λabs = 350 nm) in aggregated state. ii) Dual-mode UV protection: Beyond direct UV attenuation, PTE-Tw80@PDs convert absorbed UV energy into non-toxic visible light (λem = 410 nm) through non-radiative decay pathways, thereby mitigating UV-induced ROS generation and oxidative stress. iii) Validated bioactivity: Standardized DPPH/ABTS+ assays confirmed superior radical scavenging capacity compared to free PTE. Cellular studies revealed significantly enhanced protection against H2O2-induced cytotoxicity and UV-mediated damage in HUVEC and HaCaT cells, attributable to sustained antioxidant activity and improved bioavailability. The PTE-Tw80@PDs system represents an innovative platform for hydrophobic bioactive delivery, combining synergistic photoprotective and antioxidant effects with tunable physicochemical properties for biomedical and cosmeceutical applications.
