Association Between Donor-recipient Matchability, Access to Transplantation, and Posttransplant Outcomes
Authors: Rowena Lalji, Ryan Gately, Ross Francis, Wai Lim, Scott Campbell, Narelle Watson, Gary Torrens, Anna Francis, Germaine Wong, David W. Johnson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background.Globally, deceased donor kidney allocation algorithms prioritize HLA matching, potentially disadvantaging transplant candidates with less common HLA alleles. This study developed an Australian Matchability score (M-score) to assess access to transplantation and posttransplant outcomes based on HLA match probability.Methods.M-scores were calculated by comparing all recipients and donors with complete HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-DR data from the Australia and New Zealand Dialysis and Transplant Registry (July 1, 2006–December 31, 2023). Multivariable Cox regression was used to analyze associations between M-score quartiles and time to transplantation as well as transplantation outcomes.Results.HLA data from 14 836 recipients and 7708 donors were used to generate M-scores. Of these, 10 760 recipients had available waitlist data and were included in the models. M-scores were normally distributed with a mean ± SD of 11.4 ± 0.9. The proportion of non-European Australians increased significantly with each quartile (ie, more difficult to HLA match), Q1: 16%, Q2: 26%, Q3: 40% Q4: 60% (P < 0.001). Compared with Q1, patients in Q4 were significantly less likely to receive a deceased donor kidney transplant (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 0.56; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.52-0.60; P < 0.001) had the highest risk of death-censored graft loss (aHR 1.39; 95% CI, 1.01-1.91; P = 0.05) and acute rejection (aHR, 1.29; 95% CI, 1.09-1.52; P = 0.002).Conclusions.The M-score identifies transplant recipients with difficult-to-match HLA profiles. Higher M-scores were associated with a lower likelihood of transplantation and an increased risk of death-censored graft loss and acute rejection. These findings highlight significant inequities in the current HLA-based algorithm for deceased donor allocation.

INTRODUCTION

Globally, deceased donor kidney allocation algorithms prioritize favorable HLA matching to reduce the risk of acute rejection, improve longer-term graft and patient survival rates, and reduce the potential for future sensitization.1,2 However, such processes may disadvantage patients with less common HLA alleles, especially in the context of a relatively homogenous donor pool, limiting their likelihood of receiving a closely HLA-matched donor kidney while significantly extending their waiting time.3,4 Although focusing on utility principles through optimal HLA matching may improve graft survival and reduce the risk of acute rejection, equity principles seek to ensure equal access to transplantation across diverse populations, including those with less common HLA profiles, such as those from ethnic minorities and First Nations backgrounds (including Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander, Māori, and Pasifika peoples).5 Balancing these priorities is complex, because preferentially prioritizing allocating donor kidneys to transplant candidates with the lowest HLA incompatibility may theoretically result in longer waiting times on dialysis and/or accepting inferior quality kidneys for people of ethnic minorities and First Nations backgrounds.

Other countries with diverse populations, such as the United Kingdom and France, have recognized the inherent inequities associated with prioritizing the allocation of HLA-compatible donor kidneys and have adapted their kidney allocation algorithms accordingly. In the United Kingdom, transplant candidates are assigned a “matching score” based on their blood group, HLA typing, and unacceptable antigens. Those in the hardest-to-match decile (Decile 10) are granted priority status to reduce the disparity in organ waiting times.6 However, this does not address the equity issues around HLA matching because patients in higher deciles remain more likely to receive more poorly matched kidneys. France uses the “matched donor potential” (MDP), an indicator of low access to well-matched donors, counterbalancing the weight given to HLA matching.7 This metric is incorporated into an overall Unified Allocation Score, which also accounts for other factors such as age compatibility, sensitization, and dialysis duration.7 The introduction of the MDP in 2015 has demonstrably improved access to kidney transplantation for ethnic minority groups while simultaneously enhancing HLA matching for younger recipients across the entire cohort.7,8

Despite recent modifications to the Australian national deceased donor kidney allocation algorithm, including prioritization of highly sensitized patients over HLA matching, significant disparities in access to transplantation persist, particularly among minority populations, including Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples.9 In this study, we developed the Australian “Matchability score (M-score),” which accounts for the probability of receiving an HLA-matched kidney from the Australian donor population. We evaluated whether this score was associated with time to transplantation and posttransplant outcomes, including acute rejection and death-censored graft survival.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Population

Ethical approval for this study was granted by Metro South Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/2023/QMS/92730). Research design and findings are reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology checklist to promote transparency and reproducibility.10

We conducted a cohort analysis using the Australia and New Zealand Dialysis and Transplant (ANZDATA) Registry from July 1, 2006, and December 31, 2023. To maximize HLA diversity in M-score calculations, all available and complete HLA data were used (for HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-DR), including data from potential candidates and donors who underwent tissue typing but did not proceed to transplantation, as well as from recipients and donors from living or deceased donor kidney transplants (see Figure 1A). Since waitlist data were only available for Australian patients, New Zealand patients were excluded from this study. Recipients with generated M-scores and available waitlist data were included in the time-to-transplant analysis, and those who underwent deceased donor transplantation were included in the posttransplant analyses (see Figure 1B). Only the first transplant was included if a patient received multiple kidney transplants during the study period. Multiorgan and living-donor transplant recipients were excluded from the posttransplant analyses.

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow diagrams of study cohort and excluded patients for the creation of M-score and for posttransplant analyses. M-score, Matchability score.

Data Collection

Data were extracted from the ANZDATA Registry and included donor characteristics (self-identified ethnicity, HLA typing, and Kidney Disease Risk Index11), recipient characteristics (sex, age at waitlisting and transplantation, ethnicity, time on waitlist, dialysis duration, blood group, and HLA typing), and transplant-related characteristics (geographical state, era, and percentage measured panel-reactive antibody [mPRA]). mPRA was dichotomized at 80% (ie, <80% and ≥80%) as this cut-point is used in the current kidney transplant allocation algorithm to define highly sensitized patients and provide a priority bonus.12

Conversion of HLA Data

All HLA data used in the analysis were defined by molecular techniques and converted to HLA serotypes for analysis.13 The conversion of HLA alleles to serotypes was based on historical reactivity patterns, structural similarities, and established nomenclature.14 These standardized guidelines are freely available online.15

For HLA alleles with no World Health Organization–assigned serotypes, the serotype was then defined by the related HLA G codes, which group HLA alleles that have identical nucleotide sequences.16 Any serological conversions not available through the above guidelines were derived from published data15 using a computational tool13 for virtual serotype assignments. The final serological assignment reflected HLA matching, which is currently used in OrganMatch, the tissue-typing platform for deceased donor kidney allocation in Australia. The HLA allele-to-serotype conversion data are provided in Table S1 (SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A798).

Creation of M-score

To create M-scores, we compared all recipients to all donors and calculated HLA mismatch scores for each pairing. Mismatch points were assigned as follows: 1 point per HLA-A mismatch, 2 points per HLA-B mismatch, and 4 points per HLA-DR mismatch. The relative weighting of mismatch points was based on the ratio of their hazard ratio (HR) coefficients from a multivariable model of death-censored graft survival, considering only HLA mismatches (Table S2, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A798). This approach aligned with previously published Australian data on HLA mismatch effects17 and the weighting used in the French MDP system.8 Scores ranged from 0 to 14, with a fully mismatched donor-recipient pair (2A, 2B, 2DR MM) receiving the maximum score of 14 and a perfectly matched pair receiving a score of 0.

Once scores were calculated for all donor-recipient pairs, each recipient’s mean score was determined. These scores were then divided into quartiles, similar to the French MDP system7 (Figure 2). Q1, representing the “easiest” group to match, had the lowest average scores, whereas Q4, the “hardest” to match group, had the highest. An individual’s M-score remained constant across all analyses. Blood group compatibility was not included in the M-score calculation.

Continuous distribution of M-scores with quartile markings. M-score, Matchability score.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the time from waitlisting to transplantation. Secondary outcomes included time from transplant to death-censored graft loss, and time from transplant to first rejection (cellular or antibody-mediated). Acute rejection episodes were recorded by clinicians or data managers from each hospital, using ANZDATA’s prespecified definitions. Patients were censored at the end of follow-up, death, or loss to follow-up, whichever occurred first.

Statistical Methods

Results were expressed as percentages for categorical data, mean ± SD for normally distributed continuous data, and median (interquartile range) for nonnormally distributed continuous data. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Three multivariable Cox models were used to analyze time to transplantation, time to death-censored graft loss, and time to first rejection. These models included donor, recipient, and transplant characteristics (as described earlier), with variables selected a priori based on clinical relevance. To assess whether the association between matchability and time to transplantation varied by ethnic group, an interaction term between ethnicity and matchability was included in this model and tested using the likelihood ratio test. Models were stratified by era and state as these 2 variables violated the proportional hazards assumptions, necessitating separate baseline hazard rates to account for these violations. We assessed the 3 key outcomes, time to first transplantation, time to first acute rejection, and death-censored graft loss, using Kaplan-Meier curves categorized by matchability quartiles, and differences between groups were evaluated using log-rank tests. All analyses were undertaken using R version 4.4.0.18

RESULTS

M-scores

From an initial cohort of 16 099 recipients (potential and actual) and 8804 donors (potential and actual) in Australia, a total of 14 836 recipients and 7708 donors were included in M-score calculations after excluding those with insufficient or missing HLA data (Figure 1A). M-scores were normally distributed, with a mean ± SD of 11.4 ± 0.9. The interquartile ranges were as follows: Q1 = 9.3–10.7, Q2 = 10.8–11.3, Q3 = 11.4–12.0, and Q4 = 12.0–13.7. The distribution of M-scores for the recipient cohort is shown in Figure 2.

Baseline Characteristics

Baseline characteristics of the waitlisted cohort are reported in Table 1. Non-European Australian recipients (including Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander, Māori, and Pasifika peoples) were disproportionally represented in higher (worse) matchability quartiles (Q1: 16%, Q2: 26%, Q3: 40%, and Q4: 61%, P < 0.001; Figure 3). A supplementary analysis of incomplete HLA recipient and donor data was also undertaken to ensure that excluded individuals from this study were not enriched for ethnic minorities – they were not (Tables S3 and S4, SDC, https://links.lww.com/TXD/A798). The proportion of sensitized patients (mPRA ≥ 80%) increased with each quartile (Q1: 150 [5.9%]; Q2: 168 [6.4%]; Q3: 224 [8.2%]; Q4: 298 [10%]; P < 0.001). There was also a significant difference in time from first dialysis to waitlisting, with the longest delays in Q4 compared with other quartiles (median, 1.2 y [interquartile range, 0.5–2.9]; P = 0.02). More than one-third of Q4 recipients lived in New South Wales (936 patients, 33% of Q4, P < 0.001). Significant differences were observed in blood group (P < 0.001), sex (P = 0.03), and age at waitlisting (child versus adult, P = 0.02) based on M-score quartiles.

Deceased Donors’ and Candidates’ Ethnicity Over Time

There were notable differences in the relative proportions of donor (P < 0.001) and candidate ethnicity trends across different time periods. The most common ethnicity among all deceased donors was European Australian, but there was a proportional representation decline from 93% in the earliest transplantation era (2006–2011) to 85% in the most recent era (P < 0.001). Concurrently, the proportion of deceased donors from non-European Australian backgrounds increased over time. The proportion of deceased donors of Asian Australian heritage increased from 5% to 8.4%, whereas deceased donors from Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander Peoples increased from 1.3% to 3.7% (Tables 2 and 3; Figure 4).

Donor/recipient ethnicity by era.

Although a proportional increase in deceased donors from non-European Australians was observed over time, the proportion of non-European Australian candidates on the transplant waiting list has also risen. In the most recent era (2018–2023), Asian Australians, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples, and Pasifika candidates comprised 19%, 7.4%, and 3.5%, respectively, the waiting list, compared with 15%, 4.6%, and 2.8% in the earliest era (2006–2011). This highlights the ongoing inequities in transplantation access and organ availability (Tables 2 and 3; Figure 4).

Time to Transplant

The Kaplan-Meier cumulative incidence curve for transplantation is shown in Figure 5. Significant differences were observed between quartiles (log-rank test, P < 0.001), with Q1 having the shortest time from waitlisting to transplant. The median time to transplantation (95% confidence interval [CI]) was 252 d (233–280) in Q1 compared with 743 d (673–812) in Q4. In the multivariable Cox model, higher matchability quartiles were associated with a lower likelihood of transplantation (Table 4; Figure 6). Compared with Q1, the adjusted HR [aHRs] (95% CIs) for transplantation were 0.79 (0.74-0.84) for Q2, 0.66 (0.62-0.70) for Q3, and 0.56 (0.52-0.60) for Q4. The interaction term between ethnicity and matchability scores was tested but was not statistically significant (P = 0.828). As a result, it was not included in the final model.

The Kaplan-Meier survival curve for death-censored graft loss is shown in Figure 7. Significant differences were observed between quartiles (log-rank test, P < 0.001), with Q4 exhibiting the shortest survival time. The 5-y posttransplant survival rates by matchability quartile were: 96.2%, 95.2%, 94.1%, and 92.7% for Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q4, respectively. DISCUSSION

The M-score is a novel metric that incorporates and quantifies the likelihood of finding a compatible HLA match from the existing deceased donor pool in Australia. It highlights the significant inequities in access to transplantation and outcomes for hard-to-match transplant candidates. Despite shifts in the ethnic composition of the donor pool over time, these changes have not kept pace with the increasing ethnic diversity of the candidates on the deceased donor waiting list. As a result, individuals with uncommon HLA alleles face significant difficulties in finding a suitable deceased donor match based on the broad HLA antigen matching. Our results report significant disparities in the time to transplantation, risk of death-censored graft loss, and the risk of acute rejection based on the HLA matching M-score. At every at-risk time point, for all outcomes of interest, patients in the hardest-to-match quartile (Q4; including First Nations, Australian, and non-European Australians) were least likely to receive a kidney transplant offer and had the highest risks of graft loss and acute rejection. On average, Q4 patients waited 3 times longer for a kidney offer, experienced 40% more graft loss, and had almost 30% higher rates of acute rejection than those in the easiest group (Q1). Candidates within Q2 and Q3 experienced intermediate levels of disadvantage compared with those within Q1.

This research adds to the growing body of evidence that HLA matching disproportionately disadvantages patients with rare and complex HLA profiles, unless it is adjusted for within the allocation algorithm. The M-score thus provides a practical and implementable framework for objectively identifying these patients, and has the potential to be incorporated in a future, more equitable allocation algorithm. In this updated version of the system, the inclusion of additional HLA-DQ loci and prioritizing the best available match for each recipient would represent a unique approach that moves beyond the traditional, uniform, and fixed definition of a “well-matched” HLA offer.6,8 Regardless of whether the M-score is used within future allocation algorithms, it has immediate clinical relevance for pediatric and young adults who are likely to require multiple transplants in their lifetime, as it can inform clinicians and guide decision-making regarding offers for deceased donors.

In recent years, countries with diverse populations, such as the United Kingdom, have focused on increasing donor rates, specifically within marginalized ethnic groups, to improve HLA matching (and therefore graft outcomes). This was a direct result of data collected by the National Health Service (NHS) Blood and Transplant, which clearly showed an increase in the number of ethnic minority patients (referred to within the NHS under the acronym of “BAME—Black, Asian, Minority Ethnic”) requiring a kidney transplant during the past decade and dying before receiving a transplant offer.19 Figures from the most recent NHS Blood and Transplant BAME report show a 21% fall in both living and deceased ethnic minority donors during the past 5 y.20 Ethnic minority patients represented 37% of kidney transplants overall and accounted for 35% of the kidney transplant waiting list. Consequently, in 2024, NHS Blood and Transplant and The National BAME Transplant Alliance created a campaign specifically focused on increasing kidney donation rates within BAME groups (both living and deceased donors). By working directly with minority ethnic stakeholders and religious and community groups to promote the principle of organ donation, the campaign aims to break down perceived myths and religious and cultural barriers to organ donation. It is hoped that this will increase organ donation rates (and consequently improve HLA matching) among these minority groups.21 A similar targeted campaign could be of value for ethnic minority groups within the Australian population.

Any discussion regarding the prioritization of HLA matching and equity must, as a counterbalance, consider organ utility. In organ allocation, the utility principle asserts that distribution should maximize the overall “net medical benefit” to achieve the greatest positive impact. Nevertheless, there is strong societal agreement that factors such as social value, worth, or group-level outcome predictions should not influence allocation decisions based on utility.22,23 Utility decisions using the aforementioned factors would perpetuate healthcare disparities for socially disadvantaged groups and contribute to existing inequities for individuals who, through no fault of their own, have been especially vulnerable to the impacts of the social determinants of health.22 Furthermore, a recent ANZDATA study of HLA-matched versus non-HLA-matched transplant recipients by Gramlick et al24 found that HLA-matched transplants had better graft and recipient outcomes, but that this benefit was largely due to a shorter time spent on dialysis (28.1 versus 44.8 mo; P < 0.001) and that this benefit came at the direct expense of the nonmatched cohort, the majority of whom were likely to identify as ethnicities other than European Australian. With the above knowledge, it is then clear to see the fundamental reasons for some of the kidney health disparities that continue to exist for Australia’s First Nations peoples, as well as other vulnerable ethnic minority groups.3,25

This study has several strengths and some potential weaknesses. The introduction of the M-score as a quantifiable measure of HLA matching likelihood provides a systematic strategy to assess the difficulties faced by hard-to-match candidates. This metric enables the development of potential strategies to mitigate disparities in transplantation access, particularly among the “long-waiters” on the waiting list. Our findings provide strong evidence to support advocacy efforts for initiatives, such as community engagement to increase donor rates among minority populations and modifications to the traditional allocation with a specific focus on a more individualized approach to matching within the allocation algorithm. An in-depth discussion on how specifically to address this donor deficit is beyond the scope of this article, but it is of clear relevance, given that better HLA matchability for all candidates is a result of both an improved algorithm and diversification of the donor pool.

Although the ANZDATA Registry is a comprehensive registry that collects data on both candidates’ waitlisting and posttransplant outcomes of almost all Australians treated with kidney replacement therapy, there are inherent limitations. Coding errors may occur, and only recipients with complete HLA data were included in the study. Furthermore, these data cross a national shift in 2016 from physical complement-dependent cytotoxicity-based PRA to Luminex bead calculated PRA. This resulted in an increase in PRA levels for many sensitized patients due to the increased sensitivity of the Luminex-based assay for HLA antibodies. Given that sensitization likely has a nonlinear association with waiting time, we explored a range of functional forms for the mPRA variable; however, none of these approaches improved model fit compared with dichotomization, based on akaike information criteria criteria. We ultimately chose to dichotomize mPRA at the 80% threshold because this cut-point is used in the current kidney transplant allocation algorithm to define highly sensitized patients and provide a priority bonus. It should also be noted that measurement error exists when converting historical serological HLA typing to molecular serotypes using imputation methods, although this applied to <0.1% of our data set. Finally, these outcomes were derived from data that span across multiple decades and use historical algorithms that have changed many times throughout the study period.

CONCLUSIONS

The proposed M-score may help to identify patients with less common HLA alleles who are unlikely to receive well-matched HLA offers from the Australian donor pool. This in addition to improving HLA diversity within the donor pool encourages organ donation from ethnic minority groups. Including the M-score in the algorithm is a critical first step to addressing systemic inequity issues around HLA matching within the current Australian national deceased donor allocation algorithm.
