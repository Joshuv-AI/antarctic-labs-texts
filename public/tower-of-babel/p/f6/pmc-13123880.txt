Prioritising Health Workforce Solutions for Rural and Regional Queensland: A Delphi Study
Authors: Tracy Flenady, Tessa Benveniste, Drew Dawson, Matthew J W Thomas, Matthew McGrail
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPersistent health workforce shortages in regional, rural and remote Australia compromise access to equitable, high‐quality care. Despite multiple initiatives, recruitment and retention challenges remain. Evidence‐informed, context‐specific solutions are needed to address local workforce needs.AimTo identify and prioritise locally responsive health workforce models in Central Queensland and assess their feasibility, acceptability and effectiveness from the perspective of local stakeholders.MethodsA modified Delphi method was used, comprising two rounds of online surveys with health practitioners and administrative staff. In Round One, participants rated 11 workforce models, developed from prior qualitative interviews and expert roundtable discussions, on feasibility and effectiveness using a five‐point Likert scale. Open‐text responses were collected to provide qualitative insights. Models meeting predefined consensus thresholds (≥ 70% feasibility or ≥ 60% effectiveness) progressed to Round Two, where participants, stratified by profession (nursing, medical, allied health, other), re‐evaluated the shortlisted models. Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics, and qualitative responses were analysed using deductive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006).ResultsSeven models met consensus thresholds in Round One: Placement, Career Broker, Transport Partner, Telehealth Blended, Advertisement/Pop Culture, Expatriate and FIFO. In Round Two, the Placement model was consistently rated highly across all professional groups, with strong support also for the Career Broker and Transport Partner models, particularly among allied health and nursing participants. Models requiring significant system reform, such as Expatriate and FIFO, received more variable ratings.ConclusionLocally grounded, system‐supported strategies, especially Placement, Career Broker and Transport Partner models, were favoured over approaches relying on external incentives or structural overhaul. Implementation of these models, supported by pilot testing and collaborative evaluation, offers a promising pathway to strengthening rural and regional workforce sustainability.Keywords: Central Queensland, Delphi study, healthcare workforce, recruitment, retention, rural health, workforce models

Background

Persistent health workforce shortages in regional, rural and remote Australia continue to compromise access to safe, high‐quality and equitable healthcare [1]. Although longstanding, the COVID‐19 pandemic exacerbated pressures on already fragile systems, increasing the urgency to address recruitment and retention in these communities [2]. A range of initiatives including financial incentives and rural training pipelines have been introduced, yet many are inconsistently applied, top‐down and lack sustainability [3]. Evidence suggests that successful strategies must be locally informed, culturally responsive and system‐supported, incorporating the lived realities of clinicians, communities and service managers [4].

Recent national workforce reviews have identified priority models for testing and adaptation in rural and remote areas [2], yet limited research has focused on how these models are perceived at the regional level or how their feasibility aligns with specific local contexts. Central Queensland, characterised by geographic dispersion, workforce maldistribution, high workforce turnover and high service need, offers a valuable case study for examining which workforce solutions are most viable and acceptable to stakeholders. Health services across the region include hospital‐based acute care, community health services, primary care and outreach specialist services delivered across geographically dispersed communities. With a population of ~228 246 [5] across 497 714 km2, the region's challenges reflect those seen more broadly across regional Australia.

To inform the development of contextually appropriate workforce strategies, we conducted a preliminary qualitative phase involving interviews with local health professionals and expert roundtable discussions. Insights from this phase, alongside existing rural health workforce literature, informed the development of 11 potential workforce models aimed at addressing recruitment and retention challenges in Central Queensland. While national workforce reviews have proposed a range of strategies for rural and remote settings, there remains limited evidence regarding how such models are perceived by those working within specific regional contexts or which approaches are considered both feasible and effective by local stakeholders. This study therefore employed a modified Delphi process to identify and prioritise locally responsive workforce models, with the aim of generating stakeholder‐informed solutions to strengthen recruitment and retention in regional Queensland.

Regional and Rural Health Workforce Recruitment and Retention

Decades of investment in rural health workforce strategies, especially for medicine, have yielded limited sustained improvement [6, 7, 8]. A review by Buykx et al. [6] found little evidence supporting the effectiveness of any single approach.

More recent studies highlight the importance of place attachment, showing that a positive rural upbringing or meaningful placement experiences can increase the likelihood of health professionals remaining in rural practice [9, 10]. Similarly, Noya et al. [11] found that rural background, exposure and participation in rural‐focused education programs are key drivers of recruitment and retention. However, most research emphasises recruitment, with less focus on sustained retention [12]. The World Health Organisation endorses strategies like bonded service and education‐linked grants, but these may only achieve short‐term gains [11]. Russell et al. [4] argue that sustained retention requires selecting students from rural backgrounds, training them rurally and explicitly preparing them for rural careers. Intrinsic motivators, especially professional and personal support, were also found to influence retention and may be modifiable [4]. There is broad consensus that successful retention strategies must be multifaceted and context specific. Key considerations include social and family needs, housing, access to professional support and travel ease to urban centres [13].

To design this study, we conducted a preliminary qualitative phase with local health professionals and expert roundtable discussions. These informed the development of 11 potential workforce models, which were then evaluated through a modified Delphi process. This ensured that the proposed solutions were grounded in local knowledge and aligned with region‐specific challenges. The study aimed to: (1) co‐develop innovative, contextually appropriate recruitment and retention strategies and (2) determine which were viewed as most feasible and effective by regional health professionals.

Method

Design

This study followed the Conducting and REporting of DElphi Studies (CREDES) guidelines to ensure transparency and methodological rigour in the design, conduct and reporting of Delphi processes [14]. A completed CREDES checklist is provided as File S1. The Delphi method is a structured, iterative technique used to achieve consensus among a panel of experts on complex problems [15]. The process typically involves multiple rounds of structured questionnaires, where feedback from each round is shared anonymously with an expert panel to refine responses and move toward convergence of opinion. This method is particularly well‐suited to health services research where empirical evidence may be limited, or implementation challenges are context‐specific. In this research, we used a modified Delphi method to identify and prioritise health workforce models perceived as feasible and effective for improving recruitment and retention across Central Queensland. A preliminary qualitative phase comprising interviews and expert roundtable discussions was used to generate an initial list of 11 workforce models. These models formed the basis for two rounds of online surveys. Consensus was defined a priori using context‐specific thresholds. A model was deemed feasible if > 70% of participants agreed it could be realistically implemented within the regional healthcare context, and effective if > 60% of participants rated it as such. These thresholds align with typical Delphi study conventions, where consensus is often defined as 60%–80% agreement [16].

Ethics

This project was approved by the <university> ethics committee, approval number <redacted>. Confidentiality of participants was maintained as no identifying information was collected in the survey dataset. Informed consent was received by participants by providing details on the study at the start of the survey, with a clear statement of consent and agreement that continuing with the survey meant they would have their data collected. Participants were free to withdraw at any time via exiting the survey.

Participants and Recruitment

The scope of future rural retention intervention studies should extend beyond the medical workforce to include nurses, allied health professionals and Indigenous health practitioners, particularly in remote areas, given that nurses represent the largest proportion of the rural healthcare workforce [4, 17]. In recognition of this, the current study intentionally recruited a diverse group of participants to reflect the broader composition of the rural health workforce. A combination of snowball and direct sampling techniques was employed. Snowball sampling was facilitated through the distribution of a recruitment flyer on social media platforms (including paid advertisements on Facebook) and via informal health professional networks. Direct sampling involved targeted email invitations and distribution of recruitment materials through the research team's existing professional networks.

An initial qualitative phase was undertaken to identify region‐specific workforce challenges and inform the development of potential workforce models. Semi‐structured interviews were conducted with 11 key stakeholders across Central Queensland, including health executives, workforce planners and clinicians.

Interviews explored perceived barriers and enablers to rural workforce recruitment and retention, including professional, organisational and community‐level influences. Interview findings were summarised thematically and used to inform the generation of potential workforce intervention models. Phase Two: Model Development

Building on the themes identified during stakeholder interviews, an expert roundtable discussion was convened with researchers and health workforce stakeholders to generate potential intervention models. These discussions drew on both the qualitative findings and existing literature on rural health workforce strategies.

Through this process, 11 preliminary workforce models were developed. Each model was designed to address one or more barriers or enabling factors related to recruitment or retention in rural and regional healthcare contexts. These models formed the basis for the Delphi survey and are summarised in Table 2.

Phase Three: Delphi Survey Rounds

Two rounds of Delphi surveys were conducted using an online questionnaire platform.

In Round One, participants rated each of the 11 proposed workforce models in terms of perceived feasibility and effectiveness using five‐point Likert scales ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Participants were also invited to provide open‐text comments to elaborate on their ratings or suggest additional strategies.

In Round Two, only those models meeting the predefined consensus thresholds in Round One (≥ 70% feasibility or ≥ 60% effectiveness) were retained for further evaluation. Participants who indicated that they were currently working in Central Queensland were invited to participate in this round (n = 32).

To explore variation across professional groups, responses in Round Two were stratified by profession (nursing, medical, allied health and other).

Analysis

Quantitative and qualitative data were analysed separately and then integrated to support interpretation of findings. Quantitative responses were analysed using descriptive statistics, with Likert scale responses collapsed into agreement categories to assess perceived feasibility and effectiveness. Models meeting predefined consensus thresholds (≥ 70% agreement on feasibility or ≥ 60% agreement on effectiveness) were progressed to the second Delphi round, consistent with established Delphi methodological guidance [18]. Qualitative data from open‐text responses were analysed using deductive thematic analysis [19]. Responses were reviewed and coded to identify recurring themes relating to perceived strengths, limitations and contextual considerations associated with each workforce model. The qualitative findings were used to provide explanatory context for the quantitative ratings and to highlight profession‐specific perspectives regarding implementation feasibility and workforce impact.

Results

A total of 112 participants completed Round One of the Delphi survey. In Round Two, 32 participants re‐evaluated the shortlisted workforce models, representing a retention rate of 29% between the two Delphi rounds.

Qualitative responses in Round One revealed that perceptions of feasibility and effectiveness were often shaped by the specific contexts, workflows and challenges experienced within each health profession. For instance, allied health participants frequently commented on the need for flexible models that accommodated part‐time roles and outreach work, while medical professionals highlighted concerns around continuity of care and medico‐legal responsibilities. Nursing participants, by contrast, emphasised the importance of professional development and team integration. These differences underscored the need to tailor implementation strategies to the unique realities of each discipline. As a result, in Delphi Round Two, participants were stratified by profession (nursing, medical, allied health, other) to explore variation in model endorsement and refine consensus within each group. Several professional groups who participated in Round One were not represented in Round Two, which may have influenced the distribution of ratings. The telehealth blended model was supported by all professions except nursing and was notably deemed effective only by allied health respondents. Support for the Career Broker and Transport Partner models was reinforced in Round Two, particularly among allied health and other professions. These results should be interpreted with caution, as the absence of several professional groups in Round Two may have influenced the distribution of ratings. Placement Model

Participants widely supported the placement model as a meaningful way to foster enduring connections between students or early‐career health professionals and rural communities. A medical participant explained, ‘I was a medical student who did multiple rural placements throughout medical school. As a result of the experiences I had and the relationships I made on these placements, I am pursuing a career as a rural generalist.’ Others echoed this view, highlighting that first‐hand exposure often outweighs promotional campaigns. One allied health professional stated, ‘Positive experiences in rural communities is more effective than advertising. Social connection to rural communities is key to keeping people in rural areas.’ However, several participants raised concerns about insufficient support for supervisors and host sites. One nursing participant noted, ‘There is no support in a lot of rural areas for students… Students are not always getting a supported placement.’ Another warned of potential burnout among existing staff: ‘We would burn out our future workforce. There is not the depth of staff in numbers/clinical skills to properly support the novice employee.’ These concerns suggest that for the placement model to be successful, adequate staffing and structural support are essential.

Telehealth Blended Model

The blended telehealth model was perceived as an innovative and flexible approach for certain services and populations. Several respondents supported the model for its capacity to balance rural service delivery with professional flexibility. One allied health professional noted, ‘I feel this model has a good balance between time in rural settings with defined timeframes whilst also providing continued support as required.’ A nurse working in a remote setting remarked, ‘It is very effective from my experience.’

Medical practitioners echoed these sentiments, with one early‐career doctor commenting, ‘It sounds like a great model and would require the current Telehealth services available in CQHHS facilities to be expanded.’ Another reflected on population‐specific applicability, stating, ‘…has worked well with young persons who are more tech savvy.’ However, participants stressed that telehealth is highly context‐ and profession‐specific. A nurse expressed concern, saying, ‘Telehealth is very position specific and doesn't really assist nursing and midwifery workforce who need to be with patients around the clock.’ An allied health manager commented, ‘Much of what we do requires more frequent face‐to‐face contact… It could work for some medical specialties.’ Other comments highlighted barriers including technological infrastructure and the importance of contextual understanding: ‘Telehealth faces a lot of technology difficulties especially in smaller facilities that do not have decent internet,’ and ‘My concern with each model is that the person delivering care is committed to the community they serve, that is, they have an understanding of the health context.’

Career Broker Model

The Career Broker model was viewed by many participants as a supportive strategy to align rural and regional placements with broader career planning and professional development goals. Several respondents, particularly from allied health disciplines, valued the potential for this approach to help individuals ‘realise the opportunities that going regional/rural could afford them’ and to use rural placements as a ‘stepping stone to other opportunities.’ These insights reflect the model's potential to frame rural experience as a meaningful and strategic component of long‐term career trajectories.

Another allied health respondent noted that a key strength of the model was its alignment with intrinsic motivators, stating that it ‘reinforces value‐based work/employment and personal/professional growth, these factors are central to employee internal motivation and productivity and talent attraction and retention.’ This emphasis on personal growth and job satisfaction was viewed as critical to building a sustainable and committed workforce. However, several challenges were identified. One nurse in a management role suggested that effective implementation would require coordination across multiple agencies and roles, highlighting the need for a ‘hospital liaison staff/recruitment, immigration team, logistics travel team, real estate team,’ all working together to facilitate smooth integration into communities. This feedback underscores the significant logistical and structural support needed to operationalise this effectively. Scepticism also emerged among some participants, with one allied health practitioner noting that the model might not address the ‘root causes of people not wanting to move/work regionally,’ and therefore may offer only a limited impact on recruitment outcomes if implemented in isolation.

Transport Partner Model

The Transport Partner model received mixed feedback, with participants acknowledging its potential to alleviate a well‐known barrier to rural and remote healthcare workforce retention, travel burden. One early‐career nurse noted, ‘Definitely needs to be more accessible, for staff and patients, as this would hopefully increase the care patients can receive,’ reflecting support for improved access as a means of strengthening service provision. Participants highlighted the significant personal and operational challenges that travel can pose in rural health work. An allied health professional remarked, ‘Travel time and costs can be a significant burden on individuals and programs. Efficiencies in this area would likely improve service delivery and individual's satisfaction with roles/positions,’ underscoring how logistical support could directly influence both workforce sustainability and quality of care. However, concerns were raised about the practicality and unintended consequences of this model. One allied health respondent cautioned that ‘this seems to me to be a more expensive model that requires staff to travel away from home more frequently, which puts pressure on families and work‐life balance.’ This highlights a key tension: while better transport can improve accessibility and reduce fatigue, it may also disrupt personal lives if not carefully structured.

FIFO Model

Feedback on the Fly‐In–Fly‐Out (FIFO) model was mixed. Some participants appreciated its flexibility, particularly for health professionals who preferred not to relocate permanently. One respondent commented, ‘FIFO could work well for those with strong ties to urban areas, it allows you to serve rural communities without uprooting your life.’ However, many raised concerns about continuity of care and workforce integration. A nurse remarked, ‘Patients don't build trust when staff are constantly rotating in and out. It's not sustainable.’ An allied health participant echoed this sentiment: ‘This might help with short‐term gaps, but it doesn't help communities in the long run.’ Others cited the cost and logistical complexity of maintaining a FIFO workforce and questioned whether the benefits outweighed the challenges for smaller rural services.

Expatriate Model

The Expatriate model, based on adapting overseas deployment approaches to incentivise domestic rural placements, elicited a range of responses. Some respondents saw it as a novel way to attract professionals who value structured contracts and lifestyle benefits. ‘This would appeal to people who want a defined term and tangible benefits, like housing, education, or partner support,’ one participant said.

However, concerns were raised about sustainability and perceived equity. One clinician questioned, ‘Why should someone get all these incentives just for coming to rural areas for a year or two, when others have committed long‐term?’ Another participant worried that such packages might create divisions within teams or undermine broader workforce cohesion.

Advertisement/Pop Culture Model

Participants expressed varied opinions about the Advertisement/Pop Culture model, which proposed using creative media, such as social media campaigns, television and influencer partnerships, to attract health professionals to rural areas. Some respondents believed this approach could modernise recruitment efforts and reach new audiences. ‘It's a great way to show the lifestyle and real stories, something people can relate to,’ one early‐career respondent said. Others, however, were sceptical of its effectiveness in isolation. ‘It might raise awareness, but it doesn't fix the real issues like lack of housing, professional isolation, or poor infrastructure,’ noted a senior clinician. There were also concerns about image vs. reality: ‘If people are sold a dream that doesn't match reality, they'll leave just as quickly.’

Discussion

The prioritised models identified in this study, Placement, Career Broker and Transport Partner, offer a practical, evidence‐informed and policy‐aligned pathway to strengthening the rural and regional health workforce. Each is underpinned by established evidence of effectiveness in improving recruitment and retention, and each aligns with national and state workforce priorities. Well‐supported rural placements have consistently been shown to foster long‐term retention by strengthening place attachment, building local networks and improving rural practice readiness [9, 20, 21]. The Career Broker model complements this by providing structured career navigation and targeted placement matching, addressing the well‐documented need for sustained professional development and strategic career planning to retain health professionals in rural areas [4]. The Transport Partner model directly addresses travel and accessibility barriers, recognised determinants of workforce retention, by reducing professional isolation, increasing access to family and social supports and improving work–life balance [13]. Together, these models align with the Australian Government's National Rural and Remote Health Strategy and the Queensland Government's Rural and Remote Health Workforce Plan, both of which emphasise early exposure, professional support and enabling infrastructure. Their adoption is therefore not only evidence‐based but also strategically positioned for policy uptake.

An important consideration for rural workforce planning in Australia is the health and wellbeing of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, who are disproportionately represented in rural and remote populations and experience significant health inequities compared with non‐Indigenous Australians [22, 23]. Workforce models that strengthen continuity of care, community engagement and culturally responsive practice are therefore particularly relevant in these contexts. Approaches such as rural placements and structured career pathways may also contribute to strengthening the Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health workforce pipeline and improving culturally safe care delivery. While this study did not specifically examine workforce strategies for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health services, the prioritised models identified here may support broader efforts to strengthen culturally responsive healthcare in rural communities.

This study set out to develop and test innovative, context‐responsive solutions to one of Queensland's most persistent health challenges: recruiting and retaining health professionals in rural and regional areas. While the study explored models that extended beyond traditional approaches, the Delphi process revealed a marked conservatism among participants. Of the 11 models presented, those receiving the strongest support, the Placement, Career Broker and Transport Partner models, were already embedded in current practice. In contrast, more novel ideas such as armed forces‐style deployment, social sponsorship and lifestyle brokering gained little traction. This preference for familiar, lower‐risk strategies over system‐level change is well documented in the literature and is often linked to perceived implementation complexity, low trialability and misalignment with professional norms [24, 25]. In rural settings, these barriers are compounded by resource constraints, workforce fatigue and institutional inertia [4], which can limit receptivity to untested interventions.

Several factors may underlie this conservatism. Rural health services operate under sustained strain and staff may lack the capacity to engage with systemic change initiatives. Models requiring substantial community integration, adaptation, or workforce mobility may be viewed as impractical in contexts already characterised by instability. Broader structural issues, including the ongoing centralisation of workforce supply and the persistent pull toward metropolitan areas, further influence practitioner decision‐making [3, 26]. These forces are likely to intensify with future stressors, such as climate change, which may disproportionately affect rural infrastructure and services [27]. Without targeted policy reform and investment in rural workforce infrastructure, the viability of rural health systems could be further compromised.

The findings of this study are consistent with research from other rural jurisdictions, which similarly emphasise the importance of locally grounded workforce strategies and early rural exposure. Studies from other Australian states and internationally, including Canada and parts of the United States, have also demonstrated that rural training pathways, supported professional networks and improved infrastructure are among the most effective strategies for strengthening rural workforce sustainability [3, 4]. These parallels suggest that while the specific workforce challenges of Central Queensland are shaped by local geography and service distribution, the underlying drivers of recruitment and retention may be broadly shared across rural health systems.

The rejection of more creative models highlights a gap between innovation in policy rhetoric and adoption on the ground. Frontline uptake lags when enabling systems are weak, perceived risk is high, or interventions appear misaligned with the lived realities of rural practice. According to Rogers' Diffusion of Innovations framework (2014), adoption is influenced by perceived advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability and observability, criteria that were not clearly met by many of the less favoured models. Recognising this conservatism offers a valuable diagnostic lens for workforce reform. Future interventions should be co‐designed with stakeholders to ensure technical feasibility, cultural readiness and emotional sustainability for rural health professionals.

The results also reinforce the importance of considering the distinct workforce dynamics of different professional groups. While much rural workforce policy has historically focused on medical workforce shortages, nurses and allied health professionals constitute a substantial proportion of the rural health workforce and often experience unique recruitment and retention challenges. Previous research has highlighted that factors such as professional isolation, limited career progression opportunities and access to continuing professional development can disproportionately affect these groups [4, 17]. The strong representation of nurses and allied health professionals in this study therefore provides valuable insight into workforce strategies that extend beyond traditional medical workforce models.

Limitations

One key limitation of this study was the structure of the survey design, which restricted the depth of qualitative exploration. While open‐ended responses provided valuable insight into participants' perspectives on each model, the format did not allow for follow‐up or clarification, limiting the ability to probe more deeply into the reasoning behind participant views or to unpack contextual nuances that may have influenced responses. As a result, some of the complexities underlying feasibility and effectiveness ratings may not have been fully captured. Another identified limitation involved collapsing ‘Agree’ and ‘Strongly Agree’ into a single category. While this approach improved clarity and supported the study's descriptive focus, it may have masked subtle differences in the strength of agreement between groups. Future studies may wish to retain the full Likert scale to allow for more granular interpretation. A further limitation was that several professional groups represented in Round One were not represented in Round Two. This absence reduces the representativeness of the Round Two sample and may have introduced bias into the feasibility and effectiveness ratings, as the perspectives of those missing professions were not captured in the second‐round consensus process. As a result, the Round Two findings may disproportionately reflect the priorities and experiences of the professional groups who did participate. An additional limitation is that the study did not specifically recruit participants from Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health services or organisations. While participants reflected a diverse range of health professions and regional service contexts, the perspectives of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health practitioners and community‐controlled health services may provide additional insights into culturally appropriate workforce strategies for rural and remote communities. Future research should consider targeted engagement with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander stakeholders to inform workforce models that support culturally safe and community‐led healthcare delivery. Future studies should aim to ensure broader professional representation across all Delphi rounds to strengthen generalisability.

Strengths

This study included a strong representation of nurses and allied health professionals, ensuring that the perspectives of these often‐underrepresented groups were captured in a space typically dominated by medical discourse. Participants reflected a wide range of professional backgrounds, career stages and geographic contexts, providing a rich and diverse dataset. This breadth of experience enhances the credibility of the findings and supports the validity of the Delphi process used to identify consensus across disciplines. The study adhered to the CREDES guidelines for conducting and reporting Delphi studies, supporting reproducibility and clarity in the methodological description.

Recommendations

Implementing the prioritised models identified in this study, Placement, Career Broker and Transport Partner, should be a focus for rural and regional health workforce planning. Piloting these models in real‐world settings, accompanied by robust evaluation, will generate the implementation evidence needed to inform broader adoption and guide resource allocation. Such evaluations should assess not only recruitment and retention outcomes but also cost‐effectiveness, workforce satisfaction and community impact.

This study also reinforces the value of existing initiatives, such as the $5000 rural placement incentive, which aligns with strong support for the Placement model. Embedding this financial support within a structured placement and career navigation framework could further enhance its impact. Continued investment in programs that combine early rural exposure, targeted career support and logistical enablers such as transport partnerships will strengthen the rural workforce pipeline. Aligning these efforts with the National Rural and Remote Health Strategy and Queensland's Rural and Remote Health Workforce Plan will ensure that implementation is both evidence‐informed and policy‐ready, increasing the likelihood of sustained change.

Conclusions

This study used a two‐round Delphi process to evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of 11 workforce models aimed at addressing recruitment and retention challenges in rural and regional Queensland. Despite an emphasis on innovation, participants favoured familiar, structured approaches over models requiring major system change. The Placement, Career Broker and Transport Partner models emerged as the most widely supported, reflecting preferences for strategies that provide early exposure, structured career pathways and reduced logistical barriers. These findings have immediate relevance for workforce policy and planning. Well‐supported rural placements are consistently linked to stronger place attachment, professional confidence and long‐term rural practice intentions [9, 20, 21]. The Career Broker model complements this by offering tailored career guidance and strategic rural placement matching, addressing recognised gaps in professional development and career progression support for rural clinicians [4]. The Transport Partner model directly targets a documented retention barrier—travel burden—by enabling access to family, social supports and professional networks [13]. Together, these models align closely with national and state‐level strategies, including the National Rural and Remote Health Strategy and Queensland's Rural and Remote Health Workforce Plan, both of which call for sustainable, locally grounded workforce solutions.

Implementing these models in combination offers a realistic, evidence‐informed and policy‐aligned approach to strengthening the rural and regional health workforce. Future research should focus on piloting and evaluating these models in real‐world settings to generate robust implementation evidence and inform scalable, system‐wide adoption.
