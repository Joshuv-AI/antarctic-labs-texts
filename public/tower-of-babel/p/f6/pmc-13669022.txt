Clinical outcomes of transvaginal fistulectomy and internal orifice closure in rectovaginal fistulas secondary to stapled prolapsectomy: a monocentric case series
Authors: C La Raja, F Lecchi, G La Greca, C Gozzo, C Casiraghi, P Cellerino, A Giori, F Marazzi, P Muselli, P Prestianni, E Francescangeli, A Stuto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRectovaginal fistula (RVF) is a rare but severe complication of stapled prolapsectomy (SP), resulting from inadvertent inclusion of the posterior vaginal wall within the stapler line. Introduction

Rectovaginal fistula (RVF) is a rare but severe complication of stapled prolapsectomy (SP), occurring when the posterior vaginal wall is inadvertently incorporated into the stapler line. These fistulas may be recognized intraoperatively or present days to weeks after surgery with characteristic vaginal passage of flatus or feces. In high-resource settings, iatrogenic causes account for more than 25% of RVFs, with SP-related fistulas representing a distinct clinical entity [1].

RVFs secondary to SP exhibit peculiar anatomical features. Typically the fistulous tract is short, located in the lower third of the rectum, and associated with ischemic damage and fibrosis. Despite these distinctive characteristics, the optimal management of stapler-induced RVFs has not been clearly defined. Current recommendations by the American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons suggest endorectal advancement flap for most RVFs, although reported success rates are highly variable (41–78%) [2]. Transvaginal approaches have shown encouraging results in selected series, while the role of temporary fecal diversion remains controversial and is often extrapolated from heterogeneous RVF populations [2, 3].

The unique features of RVFs following SP raise the question of whether aggressive strategies are always necessary. Transvaginal fistulectomy with internal orifice closure may represent a tailored alternative, allowing direct fistula excision and layered closure without complex reconstruction. However, outcome data for this approach in SP-related RVFs remain limited [4–6]. This study presents a monocentric case series evaluating the clinical outcomes of this technique, aiming to contribute evidence toward a more specific and de-escalated treatment strategy for this rare complication.

Methods

This is a monocentric, retrospective, observational study conducted at IRCCS Policlinico San Donato, San Donato Milanese, Metropolitan City of Milan, Italy. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The data analyzed in this manuscript derive from our institutional RVF study, which was approved by the Regional Ethics Committee Comitato Etico Territoriale Lombardia 1 (approval number CET 371-2025). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

We included all consecutive adult female patients (≥ 18 years) with RVF secondary to SP, confirmed by clinical evaluation, treated between January 2023 and April 2025 at our center. Exclusion criteria were RVF due to other causes, systemic infection at time of surgery, or follow-up < 6 months.

Data were retrospectively extracted from a prospectively maintained institutional database. Variables collected included age, BMI, prior pelvic surgeries, obstetric history, symptom duration, fistula tract location, operative time, clinical healing at 6 and 12 months, persistence, recurrence, adverse events classified using the Clavien–Dindo classification system.

Surgical procedure

All patients were admitted the same day of the procedure. Each patient received a cleansing enema the morning of surgery. All procedures were performed by a joint team of colorectal surgeons and gynecologists under spinal anesthesia and sedation. A urinary catheter was inserted intraoperatively and intravenous cefazolin and metronidazole administered. A transvaginal inverse T-shaped colpotomy was performed to expose the vaginal orifice. The vaginal wall was carefully dissected around the fistula, allowing for the separation of the rectal wall from the posterior vaginal wall. Dissection proceeded laterally to isolate the medial portions of the puborectalis muscle, providing adequate exposure for repair. The surgical staples encountered in the dissection were removed. The rectal defect was closed transvaginally with full-thickness interrupted sutures with Vicryl 2–0 (polyglactin 910; Ethicon, Johnson & Johnson, Somerville, NJ, USA), thereby avoiding a transanal or endorectal approach. The puborectalis muscle bundles were then approximated in the midline with additional Vicryl sutures to reinforce pelvic floor support. Finally, the posterior vaginal wall was closed using interrupted Vicryl 2–0 stitches in a T-shape. A schematic representation of the main steps of the transvaginal repair is provided in Fig. 1. At the end of the procedure, a vaginal tampon was inserted. No diverting stoma was formed in any of the cases.

Postoperative care

Postoperative care was standardized across all patients. A urinary catheter and vaginal tampon were maintained for 24 h following surgery. Oral antibiotic prophylaxis with amoxicillin/clavulanic acid was administered for 48 h postoperatively. Patients were instructed to follow a liquid diet for the first 3 days, then transition to a low-fiber diet for 1 month. The aim was to avoid frequent liquid stools while maintaining a low-volume, formed stool output during the early healing phase. Sexual activity was restricted for a minimum of 40 days post-procedure to allow for adequate healing. Patients were instructed to avoid strenuous physical exercise for 1 month. Follow-up evaluations were scheduled at 2 weeks, and subsequently at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively.

Outcomes and definitions

Healing was defined as the absence of RVF-related symptoms (e.g., passage of gas or stool through the vagina) and clinical closure of the fistulous tract at both the rectal and vaginal ends during physical examination at 6 and 12 months postoperatively. Routine postoperative imaging was not performed in asymptomatic patients with complete clinical closure. Recurrence was defined as reappearance of symptoms and clinical reopening of the tract after prior documented healing. Persistence was defined as continued presence of symptoms and tract patency without interval healing at the 6-month follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were median with range. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

Results

Between January 2023 and April 2025, four consecutive patients were treated for RVF secondary to SP at our tertiary referral center and included in this case series. One patient underwent SP at our center while the other three patients were referred from other hospitals. All patients were initially operated for internal rectal prolapse causing obstructed defecation syndrome (ODS) and underwent transvaginal fistulectomy with internal orifice closure.

The median age at the time of RVF repair was 60 years (range 54–74). One patient had a medical history of arterial hypertension, while the remaining three reported no significant comorbidities or chronic medical therapy. None of the patients had a history of previous anorectal surgery. Median BMI was 23.5 (range 20–25). All fistulas were located above the anal canal and were therefore considered mid-level fistulas according to the American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons Clinical Practice Guidelines [2]. The anal opening was consistently identified 4 cm from the anal verge, with a median diameter of 3 mm (range 2–7 mm), while the vaginal opening was located at a median distance of 4 cm from the vaginal introitus (range 3.5–5 cm) and measured a median of 2 mm in diameter (range 2–5 mm). All patients were symptomatic at presentation, reporting vaginal passage of gas and stool. Diagnosis was established clinically and confirmed during examination under anesthesia at the time of surgical repair. No patient had undergone previous attempts at RVF repair following SP, and none required fecal diversion prior to or during the procedure. The median interval between SP and RVF repair was 18 days (range 15–30 days).

The median operative time was 91 min (range 45–110). No intraoperative adverse events occurred, and no postoperative complications were recorded in the short-term postoperative period (Clavien–Dindo grade 0). All patients were followed clinically, with assessments performed at 6 and 12 months postoperatively. At both follow-up time points, complete fistula healing was observed, with no evidence of persistent or recurrent communication on vaginal or rectal examination. No late complications, including chronic pain, were reported during the follow-up period. No patient spontaneously reported dyspareunia. No recurrences were identified by the end of the study.

Discussion

Rectovaginal fistula following SP represents a rare but clinically significant iatrogenic complication. Current treatment strategies are largely extrapolated from heterogeneous RVF populations, and the optimal timing of repair, the necessity of fecal diversion, and the most appropriate surgical technique for RVFs secondary to SP remain unclear [1, 2, 7, 8]. This study presents a monocentric case series evaluating transvaginal fistulectomy with internal orifice closure performed without fecal diversion in patients with RVF secondary to SP. Despite the limited sample size, our findings suggest that this approach may be safe and effective, achieving fistula healing at 12 months with minimal morbidity.

A particularly important conceptual aspect of transvaginal fistulectomy and internal orifice closure is the deliberate avoidance of a transanal or endorectal approach. Traditional surgical strategies often emphasize identification and closure of the rectal opening, frequently through endorectal advancement flaps [2]. In our experience, focusing on transvaginal closure with full thickness rectal repair without raising an endorectal mucosal flap or performing a transanal mucosal repair resulted in acceptable healing rates. Our intention is not to challenge the current recommendations for the broader RVF population but rather to propose that SP-related RVFs may represent a distinct iatrogenic subtype in which a transvaginal, stoma-sparing strategy deserves consideration in selected cases.

To our knowledge, published reports are limited to isolated cases adopting more aggressive surgical strategies. Liekens et al. reported a case of SP-related RVF managed with temporary ileostomy followed by Martius flap reconstruction, reflecting a traditional paradigm favoring diversion and vascularized tissue interposition to optimize healing [9]. Frigerio et al. described a transvaginal layered repair for RVF following SP; however, a temporary protective stoma was routinely performed [4]. While these approaches achieved satisfactory outcomes, they expose patients to the additional morbidity associated with fecal diversion and subsequent stoma reversal.

In contrast, our series demonstrates that stoma-free repair may be feasible in patients in which a closure attempt is conducted closely to the index surgery. This observation aligns with previously reported data indicating that fecal diversion does not necessarily improve fistula healing rates, but rather facilitates the management of septic complications when present, especially in recurrent cases [10, 11].

An additional aspect that may have contributed to the favorable outcomes observed in this series is the early timing of surgical repair. In all cases, RVF repair was performed as soon as feasible after diagnosis, ideally within 2 weeks and no later than 1 month following recognition of the fistula. While this hypothesis requires confirmation in larger cohorts, our experience suggests that early surgical repair should be considered a key component of a tailored, stoma-sparing strategy for RVFs secondary to SP. It should be emphasized that our results reflect a selected subgroup of early, short, non-septic fistulas with limited tissue destruction and should not be extrapolated to chronic, epithelialized, recurrent, or septic RVFs.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The primary limitations of this study are the small sample size and the lack of a control group. However, RVF secondary to SP is a rare complication, even in high-volume referral centers [12]. Our unit includes eight surgeons independently performing SP. During the study period (January 2023–April 2025) 561 SP were performed and one RVF was registered. Another important limitation is that patient-reported outcomes were not formally assessed because of the retrospective design; in particular, postoperative dyspareunia was not systematically evaluated using validated questionnaires and therefore cannot be reliably quantified. Additionally, while the follow-up duration was sufficient to assess 12-month healing, late recurrences or delayed complications cannot be excluded. Longer-term assessment is needed before the durability of this approach can be established.

Conclusions

With the significant limitation given by the small retrospective single cohort, these results represent preliminary evidence that transvaginal fistulectomy with internal orifice closure without fecal diversion could be a safe and effective surgical option for RVF secondary to SP.
