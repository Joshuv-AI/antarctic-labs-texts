Tip functionalization of anisotropic plasmonic nanoparticles with conductive polymer patches via site-selective micelle intercalation
Authors: Bangshuo Zu, Yinfeng Long, Kaitong Deng, Lin Wang, Wenhui Wang, Mingzhu Liu, Mingjie Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlasmonic patchy nanoparticles (NPs), which consist of polymer domains distributed on metal NP surface, are important for photonics, electronics, and biomedicine applications. However, current synthetic strategies are largely limited to thiol-containing ligands or block copolymers. It remains a challenge to achieve the site-selective positioning of functional conductive polymer patches on a metal NP. Here, we show how micelle intercalation dictates the nucleation sites of polypyrrole (PPy) on anisotropic plasmonic NPs, including Au nanorods (NRs) and triangular nanoprisms. The site-selectivity of the PPy patches is governed by NP local curvature and sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS) concentration. Moreover, we control over the charge transfer at the PPy and Au interface through HCl doping, offering possible stimuli-responsive properties at single-NP level. Furthermore, we extend the structural complexity via seed-mediated overgrowth and self-assembly. These results establish micelle intercalation as a strategy for programming conductive polymer patches on plasmonic NPs with diverse geometries.Subject terms: Soft materials, Nanoparticles

Introduction

Plasmonic patchy nanoparticles (NPs) are an emerging class of materials in which polymer patches are integrated with underlying metal NPs at the nanoscale1,2. This surface patterning provides additional control over chemical and physical properties, including plasmon resonance3, charge transfer4, and mechanical stiffness5. These properties are determined by the composition, spatial distribution and structure of each subunit. Moreover, patch-induced anisotropy enables directional interactions6 during NP self-assembly, leading to structures not available with non-patchy NP7–9. Therefore, the rational design and synthesis of patchy NPs is important not only for achieving synergetic properties, but also for revealing uncharted NP superlattices with favorable functionalities.

Until now, two solution-based strategies have been widely used to synthesize patchy NPs10. The first is solvent- or heat-mediated phase separation1,11. In this method, predesigned polymers first bind uniformly to the NP surface and then undergo phase separation during solvent exchange12 or heating13,14, thereby forming patches. The phase behaviour, including polymer swelling and collapse, is governed by the balance between polymer-solvent interfacial energy and polymer-polymer interactions under different solvent conditions. Although this was among the first routes to patchy NPs, the need for customized polymer synthesis raises the entry barrier for broad use15. The second approach is ligand-islands formation, in which ligands first adsorb site-selectively on the NP surface and direct subsequent polymer patch growth. Site selectivity is determined by how ligand adsorption optimizes entropic and enthalpic contributions on the NP surface16. For example, Chen and co-workers showed that the configurational entropy of 2-naphthalenethiol (2-NAT) ligands is maximized at high-curvature sites of NPs9,17, enabling tip-patched Au triangular nanoprisms through stepwise adsorption of PS-b-PAA onto 2-NAT-coated regions. More recently, by co-adsorption of iodide and 2-NAT on Au NPs, the same group achieved selective surface masking18, producing previously unobserved patterns, including face patches, hybrid face-vertex patches, web-like patches, and symmetry-broken patches. In both cases, however, pre-bound thiol-containing ligand islands are difficult to remove after polymer-patch formation, which can complicate the structure and properties of the polymer–metal interface19,20.

Among the many patchy NP compositions, conductive polymer-metal heterostructures4,21,22 are of particular interest for both fundamental studies of nanoscale charge transport23 and the development of next-generation electronic devices. Unfortunately, both of the above wet-chemical methods failed to synthesize patchy NPs with conductive polymer patches, likely due to their limited solubility24. To date, fabrication of conductive polymer-metal patchy NPs has therefore relied mainly on electrochemical deposition3,22,25, in which NPs are confined to substrates25 or hard template3. This confinement restricts structural tunability and limits downstream applications. Therefore, developing an alternative one-pot approach that enables the site-selective positioning of conductive polymer patches on a metal NP is critically needed.

Tip-selective nucleation and growth on Au NRs26 have been well established for metals26, metal oxides27, and selenide27. In these systems, crystallization follows the classical LaMer model28 and spatial selectivity arises from the non-uniform distribution of surfactant molecules across the Au NR surface as a function of local curvature29. Although polymeric patches with preferential tip localization on Au NRs have also been reported, these systems mainly rely on solvent12 or heat-driven13 phase separation, which is not applicable to conductive polymers, as discussed above. Here we achieve tip-selective patching of conductive polymers on Au NRs using a fundamentally different strategy: kinetic regulation of polymerization from the monomer stage. Specifically, we introduce a micelle intercalation strategy to synthesize patchy NPs with precise control over the position, size, and shape of conductive polymer domains. We propose that monomer-containing micelles selectively intercalate into the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) bilayers on NP surfaces and subsequently polymerize into insoluble patches30–32. Using PPy as a model system, we demonstrate the feasibility of this strategy for conductive polymers undergoing oxidative polymerization. The spatial location and thickness of the PPy patches are tuned by the micelle size and Py volume, respectively. Owing to its simplicity and modularity, this approach provides a practical route for patterning diverse metal nanostructures with conductive polymer patches, enabling patchy NPs with varied geometries and functionalities.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of tip-patched Au NRs

CTAB-capped Au NRs (Supplementary Fig. 1) were prepared following a method reported in ref. 33. In a typical patchy NP synthesis, Au NRs were mixed with an SDS solution containing Py monomers, followed by the addition of ammonium persulfate (APS) as the oxidizer (Fig. 1). In the first step, SDS micelles containing Py (Py–SDS micelles) intercalate into the CTAB bilayer at the Au NR tips29,34, where oxidizer-initiated polymerization occurs in situ to form insoluble PPy patches.

Preparation of tip-patched NPs via Py–SDS micelle intercalation.

a Schematic illustrations showing site-selective intercalation of Py–SDS micelles at the tips of Au NRs for localized polymer patch formation. b Oxidative polymerization of Py into PPy.

The product NPs are collected by centrifugation and characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), where tip patches are clearly seen from the darker SEM contrast of PPy (Fig. 2a). The patch thickness is 13 ± 2 nm and the uncovered length L along the longitudinal axis of the NR decreased to 56 ± 1 nm (Fig. 2c), compared with their initial length (Supplementary Fig. 1, 82 ± 5 nm). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image of a representative tip-patched NR, overlaid by the NR’s contour colored to local curvature, shows that the PPy patches only cover the highly curved tips, leaving most of the flat sides of the NR free of polymer coating (Fig. 2b). The PPy patches originated from selective Py–SDS micelle intercalation in CTAB bilayers on Au NRs, as the polymerization reaction initiated only where Py monomers are present. Such site-selective nucleation and growth is consistent with earlier work on tip-functionalization of Au NR with metal35 or metal oxide36, presumably due to CTAB molecules generally exhibiting sparse coverage at high-curvature sites35–37. Figure. 2d shows that with a fixed SDS concentration (CSDS) of 40 mM, the patchy NPs kept dumbbell shape. When we increase the Py volume (VPy), the uncovered length of the dumbbell NPs first decreases, and then stabilizes (Fig. 2e), indicating that PPy nucleation is restricted to the NR tips. At the same time, the patch thickness gradually increased as a result of growth of polymeric chains at the NR tips (Fig. 2e). The differences in patch thickness were also well-reflected in the UV-vis spectra of the dumbbell NP dispersions3. Figure 2f shows that, compared with the initial NRs, the transverse resonance absorptions remain unchanged, whereas the longitudinal resonance absorptions of dumbbell NPs are red-shifted. These shifts are likely due to the PPy patches (n≈1.5–1.7) having a higher refractive index than H₂O (n=1.3), resulting in a smaller wLSPR according to Eq. (1). The PPy patches act like dielectric caps on the tips of the Au NR, slowing down the plasmon oscillation and hence shifting the longitudinal resonance to longer wavelengths38,39.

Here, wLSPR is the localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) frequency of the patchy NP, wp is the plasma frequency of bulk metal Au, εm is the dielectric constant of local surrounding medium. Note that the red-shifts of the longitudinal resonance absorptions is most significant (≈32 nm) when an 8 nm layer of PPy patch first grows on the NR tips (Fig. 2d, e, VPy=4μL) and the absorption only slightly red-shifted further (≈8 nm) with the patch thickness increased to 15 nm (Fig. 2d, e, VPy=8μL). These results can be explained using a near-field decay model40,41, where the field of longitudinal resonance is strongly confined at the NR tips and decays exponentially with distance into the surrounding media. Hence, for the dumbbell NPs, only PPy patch within a few nm to the tips significantly affects the resonance. The surface composition and molecular structure of the dumbbell NPs were examined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (Supplementary Fig. 11) and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 12). A distinct Br 3 d signal, together with FTIR bands around 2850 cm⁻¹ characteristic of aliphatic C–H stretching, confirms residual CTAB on the NP surface, whereas no detectable S 2p signal indicates that SDS does not persist after polymerization, likely due to micelle disassembly during polymerization. High-resolution TEM reveals compact PPy patches without internal nanopores, indicating that bulky SDS and CTAB are excluded from incorporation into the polymer phase (Supplementary Fig. 13). The absence of a low-contrast gap between the Au core and PPy patches—typically associated with residual surfactant bilayers—confirms direct Au–PPy contact, which is essential for unhindered charge transfer. Notably, the CTAB bilayer exists in a dynamic adsorption–desorption equilibrium42, allowing transient exposure of the underlying Au surface and enabling continuous PPy growth through progressive displacement of CTAB at the interface. Such dynamic surfactant reorganization has been widely reported during the overgrowth of Au43, Ag44, and Pd35 on Au NPs.

Precision control of patch shape and size on Au NRs

To elucidate the intercalation of Py–SDS micelles into the CTAB bilayer, we measured the zeta potentials of Au NRs before and after mixing with Py–SDS micelles at varying SDS concentrations while maintaining a constant Py volume (VPy of 6 μL). The as-prepared Au NRs exhibit a positive surface potential due to the adsorbed CTAB bilayer. Upon introduction of negatively charged Py–SDS micelles, the zeta potential reverses sign immediately, indicating rapid charge inversion at the NP interface driven by electrostatic interactions (Supplementary Fig. 2). Importantly, the negative surface potential persists after centrifugation and washing with H2O (Fig. 3b), excluding weakly adsorbed species and suggesting stable association of the micelles with the CTAB layer. These results support electrostatically driven intercalation of Py–SDS micelles into the CTAB bilayer, enabling effective enrichment of pyrrole monomers at the Au NR surface prior to polymerization. Moreover, the dynamic light scattering result shows that the sizes of Py–SDS micelles decrease with increasing CSDS from 20 to 50 mM, at a fixed VPy of 6 μL (Fig. 3c). As we discussed earlier (Fig. 2), the grafting density of CTAB bilayers varies across the surface of Au NRs, with the lowest CTAB bilayer density on the highest local curvature sites. As a result, high-curvature sites accommodate large Py–SDS micelles, and vice versa for low-curvature sites (Supplementary Fig. 3), which leads to a library of patchy NPs, including dog-bone, dumbbell, and core–shell structures. Specifically, at 20 mM SDS, PPy nucleates and grows after the addition of APS, at two high curvature sites farthest apart at each NR tip (Supplementary Fig. 5), probably due to steric hindrances. The final NP exhibits four symmetric PPy patches (Fig. 3a, and Supplementary Fig. 4), resembling a dog-bone structure. The PPy patches continue to cover more NR surface as CSDS increases from 40 to 50 mM, resulting in structural transformations of a NP from dumbbell (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 6) to core-shell (Fig. 3a, and Supplementary Fig. 7). Figure 3d shows a clear decrease in the uncovered length and the PPy patch thickness for core-shell NPs compared to that of dumbbell NPs as a result of the increase in PPy nucleation sites at a fixed VPy. We have summarized the effect of CSDS and VPy on the shapes of the patchy NP in Fig. 3e and the detailed NP morphologies can be found in Figs. 2, 3 and Supplementary Figs. 4–7, 9. In contrast, introduction of monomer-containing SDS micelles to Au NRs without systematic control results in uniform polymer coatings, consistent with earlier reports45,46. We attribute this distinction to the deliberate modulation of CSDS and VPy in our approach, which governs micelle intercalation and thereby enables site-selective polymer growth on the NR surface. Moreover, unlike previously reported heat- or solvent-induced phase separation strategies12,13 that operate at the level of pre-formed polymer chains, our method achieves enhanced spatial resolution by kinetically regulating radical cation generation, oligomer population, and chain propagation during the early stages of polymerization. To further clarify the role of CTAB in tip-selective polymer growth, we systematically varied the CTAB concentration (Supplementary Fig. 8). DLS measurements show that increasing the CTAB concentration leads to an enlargement of the monomer-containing micelles, likely due to the reconstruction of CTAB-SDS mixed surfactant assemblies47. The change in micelle size directly influences their site-selective intercalation into the CTAB bilayer on the Au NR surface. As a result, the NP morphology evolves from a dumbbell to a dog-bone structure, consistent with our observations in Fig. 3.

Experimental control of NP anisotropy in patch formation

To validate the generality of our synthetic approach, we first replaced the Au NRs with NRs of a smaller aspect ratio (AR ≈ 1.7, Supplementary Fig. 14a) and repeated the synthesis. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 14b, dumbbell NPs were readily obtained, similar to those in Figs. 2, 3, confirming the effectiveness of our method. Moreover, we used Au triangular nanoprisms as seed NPs to fabricate tip-patched NPs. The triangular nanoprisms are geometrically sophisticated, with a drastic variation in local curvature ranging from flat basal planes, beveled side facets, and sharp tips (Supplementary Fig. 15a)48. A representative patchy triangular nanoprism, overlaid by the prism’s contour colored to local curvature shows that the PPy patches covered the highly curved tips (Fig. 4a), demonstrating the site-selective nucleation and growth of PPy. By increasing VPy in the reactant solution, we found the tip-patched NPs transformed into edge fully covered NPs (Fig. 4b). This morphological transition likely results from PPy nucleation at both the nanoprism tips and beveled side facets under CSDS at 30 mM, driving a progressive evolution of patchy NP morphologies. Supplementary Fig. 15b shows lower-magnification SEM images, capturing more tip-patched triangular nanoprisms within the field of view. Note that despite the shape similarities of tip-patched nanoprisms obtained here to ref. 9,17, we present a non-thiol–based synthetic approach for the precise positioning of conductive polymer patches on NP surfaces, which has not been realized before. Additional geometry-dependent patching behaviours of Au NPs are occasionally observed during our synthesis (Supplementary Fig. 16). We further demonstrate tip-selective patching on NPs with compositions beyond Au (Supplementary Fig. 17), highlighting the generality of our approach and providing a platform for engineering patchy NPs with tunable compositions and functionalities.

Mapping charge transfer across polymer patch–Au interface

To probe the possible applications of dumbbell NP in nanoelectronics devices, we inspect the electrical behaviours of the PPy–Au interface at single NP level using Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM)49,50. The band gaps of the PPy patches can be tuned through HCl doping (Fig. 5a)51,52. Figure 5b, d, shows that height of the PPy patch is ≈14.6 nm, positioning at the tips of the NR, and the morphologies of the dumbbell NPs kept unchanged before (Fig. 5b) and after (Fig. 5c) HCl doping. The surface potential mapping of a typical dumbbell NP extracted from KPFM shows uniform contrast indicating no obvious differences in the work functions of the undoped PPy patch and Au (Fig. 5e, g). After doping with HCl (see Methods), the potential distribution on the dumbbell NP became heterogeneous (Fig. 5f) with the Au middle sections revealing brighter contrast (ΔV≅ 270 mV, Fig. 5g), and thus higher work function. The large work function difference between doped PPy patches and the bare Au NR surface suggests the formation of a built-in potential at the PPy–Au interface, driving charge transfer from PPy to Au. As discussed above, no significant accumulation of CTAB or SDS at the PPy–Au interface is observed (Supplementary Figs. 10–13). The potential difference that emerges after HCl doping is therefore attributed to increased carrier density and Fermi- level shift in PPy, enhancing interfacial charge transfer. This intrinsic asymmetry in electrical properties indicates that our dumbbell NP could function as a nanoscale rectifying junction, a fundamental building block for nanoelectronic diodes and related devices.

Patchy NP geometries by overgrowth and self-assembly

Lastly, moving beyond the dumbbell nanostructure, the morphology, as well as the associated electronic and optoelectronic properties can be modulated by tailoring patchy NP geometries through controlled overgrowth and self-assembly. We followed the morphological changes of the dumbbell NPs in the reaction solution containing CTAC, Au3+, Ag+ ions, and ascorbic acid. Figure 6 shows that Au atoms preferentially deposited on the side facets of the dumbbell NP rather than on the tips (Supplementary Fig. 18), forming small irregular NPs (Fig. 6a). With an increase of CAu3+, these small NPs grew into well-defined Au {111} crystal facets (Fig. 6b). Note that we observed a distinct gap between these facets, indicating site-selective Au deposition, likely arising from the surface energy difference between the Au {110} and Au {100} facets (γ{100}<γ{110}, γ is the surface energy.) along the side of the dumbbell NP. A continuous increase in CAu3+ leads to overall nucleation and growth on the exposed Au surfaces of dumbbell NPs, resulting in tip-truncated octahedron nanostructures (Fig. 6c). In addition to the shape evolution of individual NPs, the self-assembly of NPs provides a strategy for broadening structural complexity53,54. For instance, we show that our dumbbell NPs assembled into closed-stacks55 (Supplementary Fig. 19) in a mixed solvent of N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone and H2O at elevated temperature. These NP geometries are expected to influence properties, such as light adsorption and carrier-injection barriers, potentially enhancing their applications in photodetection and chemical sensing.

In summary, we present a one-pot strategy to synthesize patchy NPs via site-selective micelle intercalation, where Au NRs accommodate Py–SDS micelles in a size-dependent manner. This method affords precise control over the spatial distribution, dimensions, and morphology of conductive polymer patches on metal NPs, thereby broadening structural complexity for nanoelectronics and self-assembly. The conceptual platform we provide can be generalized to diverse NP geometries, such as triangular nanoprisms, enabling a versatile library of tip-patched NPs for wide-ranging applications.

Synthesis of Au NRs

Au NRs were prepared according to refs. 33,56 with slight modifications. For the growth of Au NRs, we first prepared a seed solution as follows. 2 mL of 100 mM CTAB and 0.1 mL of 10 mM HAuCl4·3H2O aqueous solutions were mixed together to form solution A. Then, an aqueous solution of 0.2 mL of 10 mM NaBH4 was injected into the solution A, and the mixture turned brown instantly, indicating the formation of small Au NPs. This seed solution was kept at 30 °C for 1 h before use.

Next, we prepared the growth solution by mixing 4 mL of 100 mM CTAB, 0.2 mL of 10 mM HAuCl4·3H2O, 0.05 mL of 10 mM AgNO3, and 0.2 mL of 100 mM hydroquinone together. 0.01 mL of seed solution was injected into the growth solution under rapid stirring. The final solution was kept at 30 °C for 12 h. The resulting Au NR dispersion had a particle concentration of ≈0.6 nM, which was estimated from the absorption spectra assuming a molar extinction coefficient of 5.5 × 109 M−1 cm−1 for Au NRs57. Au NRs with lower aspect ratio (AR ≈ 1.7) were prepared following the same recipe only with less AgNO3 amount (0.037 mL) in the growth solution. Prior to their use, the Au NRs were taken out from the stock solution and washed with 1 mM CTAB by centrifugation (6870 × g) three times.

Synthesis and purification of Au triangular nanoprisms

Au triangular nanoprisms were prepared via seed-mediated growth48,58 with minor modifications. Specifically, we first prepared a seed solution as follows. 4.65 mL of 100 mM CTAC and 0.1 mL of 10 mM HAuCl4·3H2O aqueous solutions were mixed together to form solution A. Then, an aqueous solution of 0.225 mL of 10 mM NaBH4 was injected into the solution A, and the mixture was kept at 30 °C for 2 h. After the reaction, the seed solution was diluted 10 times with 100 mM CTAC before use.

Next, a tube of aqueous solutions containing 3.23 mL of 100 mM CTAC, 0.067 mL of 10 mM HAuCl4·3H2O, 0.008 mL of 10 mM KI, 0.062 mL of 40 mM ascorbic acid was prepared, and the bottle was labelled A. Another bottle of solution containing 9.7 mL of 100 mM CTAC, 0.2 mL of 10 mM HAuCl4·3H2O, 0.03 mL of 10 mM KI, and 0.017 mL of 40 mM ascorbic acid was prepared, and labelled B. Next, 0.12 mL of the diluted seed solution was added into bottle A and mixed. Then, 0.05 mL of the mixture in bottle A was added to B. The solution was kept at room temperature (RT) for 15 min followed by centrifugation (6870× g) and washing with 1 mM CTAC twice. The precipitates were redispersed in 3 mL of 1 mM CTAC for further purification.

The Au triangular nanoprisms underwent two rounds of purification process to remove small polyhedral particles impurities by depletion attraction-induced aggregation, following ref. 59. Specifically, the 3 mL Au triangular nanoprism solution was mixed with an aqueous solution of 780 μL of 780 mM CTAC (a final CTAC concentration of 161 mM). The solution was left undisturbed overnight allowing the triangular nanoprisms to stack into columns by depletion attraction and sediment as black solids, while the small polyhedral particles remained dispersed in the top solution. Next the top solution was removed from the centrifuge tube and the remaining sediment was re-dispersed with 3 mL H2O. The above purification process was repeated once to further remove the small particle impurities. The collected nanoprism sediment was then washed and centrifuged three times with 1 mM CTAC. The final sediment was re-dispersed with 0.3 mL of 1 mM CTAC, and labelled as nanoprism stock solution. The nanoprism stock solution was centrifuged and concentrated 50 times before use.

Synthesis of dumbbell NPs

Patchy NPs were synthesized using a micelle-intercalation approach. Specifically, for typical dumbbell NPs (Fig. 2a), a tube of aqueous solution containing 5 μL Py and 10 mL of 40 mM SDS was prepared, and the bottle was labelled A. Then, 250 μL of solution from bottle A was mixed with 150 μL of ≈6 nM Au NRs (see Methods), and the bottle was labelled B. Finally, 1.5 mL of 2 mM APS solution was added into bottle B and mixed thoroughly. The solution was kept at 30 °C for 12 h. After the reaction, the dumbbell NPs were washed by centrifugation (6870 × g) two times in 1 mM CTAB solution. After the last centrifugation, 150 μL of 1 mM CTAB was added to the precipitate, and the dispersion was stored at RT for further characterization. For the dumbbell NPs with different patch thicknesses (Fig. 2d), the Py volumes in bottle A was increased from 4, 6, to 8 μL, respectively, at a fixed SDS concentration of 40 mM.

Synthesis of dog-bone and core-shell structured patchy NRs

For the synthesis of dog-bone structured patchy NRs (Fig. 3a), a tube of aqueous solution containing 6 μL Py and 10 mL of 20 mM SDS was prepared, and the bottle was labelled A. Then, 250 μL of solution from bottle A was mixed with 150 μL of ≈6 nM Au NRs, and the bottle was labelled B. Finally, 1.5 mL of 2 mM APS aqueous solution was added into bottle B and mixed. The solution was kept at 30 °C for 12 h. After the reaction, the dumbbell NPs were washed by centrifugation (6870 × g) twice using 1 mM CTAB. After the last centrifugation, 150 μL of 1 mM CTAB was added to the precipitate, and the dispersion was stored at RT for further characterization. For the core-shell NPs (Fig. 3a), SDS concentration and Py volume in bottle A was 50 mM and 6 μL, respectively.

Synthesis of tip-patched triangular nanoprisms

For a typical synthesis of tip-patched triangular nanoprisms (Fig. 4a), a tube of aqueous solution containing 4 μL of Py and 10 mL of 30 mM SDS was prepared, and the bottle was labelled A. Then, 50 μL of solution from bottle A was mixed with 30 μL of triangular nanoprism seed solution (see Methods), and the bottle was labelled B. Finally, 300 μL of 2 mM APS aqueous solution was added into bottle B and mixed. The solution was kept at 30 °C for 12 h. After the reaction, the dumbbell NPs were washed by centrifugation (6870 × g) twice in 1 mM CTAB. After the last centrifugation, 30 μL of 1 mM CTAB was added to the precipitate, and the dispersion was stored at RT for further characterization. For the tip-patched nanoprisms with different patch thickness (Fig. 4b), Py amount in bottle A was increased from 2, 4, 6, to 8 μL, at a fixed SDS concentration of 30 mM.

Overgrowth of tip-patched NRs

For the dumbbell NP overgrowth reaction, typically, 1 mL of 100 mM CTAC, 0.05 mL of 10 mM HAuCl4·3H2O, 0.015 mL of 10 mM AgNO3, and 0.02 mL of 1 M HCl were mixed together by stirring. Then, while stirring, 0.015 mL of 100 mM ascorbic acid and 0.1 mL of ≈6 nM dumbbell NPs (calculated based on Au NR content) were added sequentially to the solution. The mixture solution was stirred for additional 2 min and kept at 30 °C for 12 h. After the reaction, the NPs were washed twice by centrifugation (6870 × g) in 1 mM CTAC solution.

Characterization methods

UV-vis spectra of the Au NRs and dumbbell NPs with different patch thickness in Fig. 2f were measured using a Shimadzu UV-2600 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu (Suzhou) Instruments Manufacturing Co., LTD., Suzhou, China) with a plastic cuvette (path length = 1 cm, FisherbrandTM). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements (Fig. 3c) were performed on a Litesizer DLS 501 instrument (Anton Paar GmbH, Graz, Austria) in backscattering mode. The measurements were conducted on the same sample for three times with standard deviation provided in the plots. AFM height and KPFM potential images (Fig. 5) were obtained using Bruker Dimension Icon AFM (Bruker Pty Ltd., Victoria, Australia). The AFM/KPFM sample was prepared by drop-casting 2 µL of dumbbell NP dispersion onto a Si substrate (0.25 cm2) and dried at ambient conditions. HCl-doped AFM samples was prepared by further immersing the Si substrate with NPs dried on the surface into 10 mM HCl for 12 h. The sample was rinsed with H2O and dried at ambient conditions. For the KPFM measurements, an external voltage was applied to the sample with the tip grounded; thus a positive KPFM signal indicates a higher work function of the sample relative to the probe50.

Imaging and data processing

SEM images were acquired using ZEISS Sigma 360 (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany). TEM and STEM images were acquired using a 200 kV FEI Talos F200X G2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Hillsboro, OR, USA) and 300 kV FEI Themis G3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Hillsboro, OR, USA), respectively. The image processing algorithm for analysing the local curvature of the NP was written in Python 3.10 using OpenCV60 library. The local curvature at each coordinate of the NR or triangular nanoprism contour (Figs. 2b, 4a) is defined as the inverse of the radius of the best-fitted circle to the local arc containing the coordinate. The local thickness map of the polymer patches was obtained using the built-in “Local Thickness” function in ImageJ (version: 1.54p). Height and potential line profiles from AFM measurements and the line profile from the TEM image in Supplementary Fig. 13 were plotted after applying a one-dimensional Gaussian filter to the raw data, with σ values of 0.6, 6, and 6, respectively. Both raw and filtered data are provided in the Source Data file.

Statistics and Reproducibility

Experiments shown as representative micrographs and images were independently repeated at least three times with similar results, unless otherwise stated. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD, with sample sizes indicated in the corresponding figure legends.
