Bridging the know-do gap: a cross-sectional assessment of hypertension management amongst primary healthcare workers in Northwestern Nigeria
Authors: Umar Abdullahi, Abdullah Shehu, Sanusi Garba, Bello Alhaji Mohammed, Kabiru Bello Sada, Harrison Sunday Akogwu, Kabiru Muhammad Mande, Jelani Muhammad, Sani Ahmad, Lawan Umar, Mujtaba Bala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension is a leading cause of cardiovascular mortality in Nigeria, with a high burden in the Northwest. Effective management at the primary healthcare (PHC) level is critical, yet data on healthcare workers’ capacity in this security-challenged region are limited.ObjectivesTo assess the knowledge and practice of hypertension management amongst PHC workers in Gusau, Zamfara State, and identify associated factors.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted amongst 320 healthcare workers using a pretested questionnaire. Knowledge and self-reported practice were assessed and categorised as ‘good/adequate’ (score ≥ 75%) or ‘poor/inadequate.’ Data were analysed with SPSS using chi-square tests and multivariable logistic regression (p < 0.05).ResultsRespondents’ mean age was 34.6 ± 7.8 years; 62.8% were male, and 48.4% were Community Health Extension Workers. Overall, 61.9% had good knowledge, but gaps existed in identifying diagnostic thresholds (56.3%) and complications (59.7%). Only 54.7% demonstrated adequate practice, with low follow-up scheduling (48.1%), guideline use (51.6%), and referral (46.9%). Prior training (AOR = 2.6; 95% CI: 1.6–4.3) and > 10 years’ experience (AOR = 1.8; 95% CI: 1.1–3.0) predicted good knowledge. Good knowledge (AOR = 2.1; 95% CI: 1.3–3.5) and on-site guideline availability (AOR = 1.9; 95% CI: 1.2–3.1) predicted adequate practice.ConclusionA significant know-do gap exists in hypertension management. Recurrent in-service training for all PHC cadres—particularly Community Health Extension Workers (CHEWs) and Community Health Officers (CHOs), who constitute the majority of the workforce—alongside accessible point-of-care guidelines, strengthened chronic care systems, and supportive supervision are urgently needed to improve hypertension care in this region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05726-8.Keywords: Hypertension, Knowledge, Practice, Know-Do Gap, Primary Healthcare, Healthcare Workers, Northwestern Nigeria

Introduction

Hypertension is a leading global health challenge and the most common cardiovascular disorder worldwide. It significantly contributes to morbidity and mortality through complications such as stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure, and chronic kidney disease, making it one of the leading causes of premature death globally [1, 2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that more than 1.28 billion adults aged 30–79 years are hypertensive, with the majority living in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where awareness, treatment, and control rates are often low [3].

In sub-Saharan Africa, hypertension prevalence has risen sharply due to rapid urbanisation, sedentary lifestyles, unhealthy dietary habits, and population ageing, posing a significant public health burden [4]. Nigeria, the most populous African country, bears a disproportionately high prevalence of hypertension, ranging between 20% and 46% across different regions [5, 6]. Despite this, awareness, treatment, and control remain inadequate, leading to increased cardiovascular morbidity and premature deaths [7].

Primary healthcare (PHC) centres form the cornerstone of Nigeria’s health system and serve as the first point of contact for most patients, especially in rural and semi-urban communities. They are expected to play a pivotal role in the detection, treatment, and long-term management of hypertension [8]. Nigeria’s PHC system is organised around ward-level facilities. The main clinical cadres include Community Health Extension Workers (CHEWs) and Community Health Officers (CHOs), who perform screening, lifestyle counselling, and uncomplicated management; nurses and midwives, who provide curative and preventive services; and medical officers, who supervise and manage complex cases. The scope of practice for each cadre is defined by the National Primary Health Care Development Agency [8]. However, the capacity of PHC workers to manage hypertension effectively varies widely and is influenced by local training, resources, and context. The quality of hypertension management at this level largely depends on the knowledge and practice of healthcare workers, who are responsible for screening, diagnosis, initiation of treatment, lifestyle counselling, and long-term follow-up [9]. Evidence suggests that gaps in healthcare workers’ knowledge, diagnosis, and treatment practice at the PHC level contribute to underdiagnosis, poor treatment adherence, and inadequate blood pressure control [10, 11].

Zamfara State, located in Northwestern Nigeria, faces unique security challenges and limited health resources. Gusau, the state capital, hosts secondary (general hospital) and tertiary (Federal Medical Centre) facilities, but PHC centres remain the central platform for delivering primary care under constrained conditions for the majority of the population. While the high prevalence of hypertension in the region is documented [12], there is a dearth of specific research evaluating the capacity of PHC workers in Zamfara State to manage this condition effectively. To the best of our knowledge, no study has specifically assessed the knowledge and practice of hypertension management amongst PHC workers in Gusau, Zamfara State—a region facing unique security and resource challenges that likely exacerbate health system weaknesses.

Assessing the knowledge and practice of hypertension management amongst healthcare workers in PHCs within Gusau is therefore vital for identifying context-specific gaps and informing targeted interventions. Furthermore, this research will contribute to the limited literature on hypertension management at the PHC level in Nigeria, where most patients seek care and effective intervention could have the greatest population-level impact on hypertension control and cardiovascular disease prevention. Such evidence can inform the design of targeted interventions, training, and resource allocation to strengthen PHC capacity in tackling the growing hypertension epidemic in security-challenged regions. This study aims to evaluate the knowledge and practice of hypertension management amongst healthcare workers in PHC centres in Gusau, Zamfara State, Northwestern Nigeria.

Methods

Study design

This was a descriptive cross-sectional study conducted to assess the knowledge and practice of hypertension management amongst healthcare workers in primary healthcare (PHC) centres in Gusau, Zamfara State, Northwestern Nigeria. The study was carried out over a three-month period (September 2025 to November 2025).

Study setting

The study was carried out in Gusau, the capital city of Zamfara State, located in Northwestern Nigeria. Zamfara State has a predominantly rural population, with agriculture as the main economic activity. The state has limited health infrastructure and faces significant security challenges that affect healthcare delivery. PHC centres serve as the main entry point into the healthcare system for the majority of the population. Gusau has several functional PHC centres providing preventive, promotive, and curative services, including the management of common non-communicable diseases such as hypertension. According to the Zamfara State Primary Health Care Development Agency, Gusau Local Government Area has 23 functional PHC centres distributed across 12 administrative wards.

Study population

The study population consisted of healthcare workers in PHC centres in Gusau including medical officers, nurses, midwives, community health extension workers (CHEWs), and other cadres directly involved in patient care and hypertension management. All healthcare workers who met the eligibility criteria and provided written informed consent were enrolled. No eligible worker was included without consent. Healthcare workers who were on leave or unavailable during the study period, administrative staff, and those not directly involved in patient care were excluded.

Sample size determination

The minimum sample size was calculated using the Cochran formula for estimating cross-sectional studies: n = z²pq/d². Using z = 1.96, p = 0.753 (75.3%) prevalence of good knowledge from a similar study by Bello et al. in Sokoto [13], q = 1-p (0.247) and d = 0.05, the minimum sample size calculated was 318. After adding 10% for non-response rate, 340 questionnaires were finally distributed.

Sampling technique

A multistage sampling technique was employed.

Stage 1: Six of the 11 wards in Gusau Local Government Area were selected using simple random sampling (balloting).

Stage 2: In each selected ward, a list of all PHC centres was obtained from the local health authority. Two PHC centres per ward were randomly selected (balloting), giving a total of 12 participating PHC centres.

Stage 3: Within each selected PHC centre, all eligible and consenting healthcare workers were recruited consecutively until the required sample size was attained.

Data collection instrument

A pretested, semi-structured, self-administered questionnaire was used to collect data. The instrument was adapted from previously validated tools assessing knowledge and practice of hypertension management in LMICs [13–15]. It consisted of three sections: (1) Sociodemographic characteristics of respondents; (2) Knowledge of hypertension (definition, risk factors, diagnosis, and treatment); (3) Practice of hypertension management (screening, drug prescription, lifestyle advice, follow-up, and adherence to guidelines). A single questionnaire was administered to all cadres because it assessed fundamental hypertension management competencies that are relevant to every health worker involved in direct patient care at the PHC level, irrespective of formal qualifications. The instrument has been used across different cadres in previous Nigerian studies [13, 14] and demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.81). The tool was reviewed by experts and pretested amongst 30 healthcare workers in a non-study location for validity and reliability. Knowledge and practice items were benchmarked against the Nigerian National Guidelines for the Management of Hypertension (Federal Ministry of Health, 2019) and the Seventh Report of the Joint National Committee on Prevention, Detection, Evaluation, and Treatment of High Blood Pressure (JNC 7). Trained research assistants supervised the data collection process, provided clarifications when needed, and obtained written informed consent prior to questionnaire administration. A copy of the questionnaire is available as supplementary material.

Measurement of variables

Knowledge assessment

Knowledge was assessed using 12 items covering four domains: definition of hypertension (1 item), risk factors (5 items), diagnostic thresholds (2 items), and complications (4 items). One point was awarded for each correct answer, yielding a maximum score of 12. A composite knowledge score was calculated, and participants scoring ≥ 9 points (75%) were categorised as having ‘good knowledge,’ while those scoring < 9 points were categorised as having ‘poor knowledge.’

Practice assessment

Practice was assessed using 10 items across five domains: routine blood pressure screening (2 items), lifestyle modification counselling (2 items), regular follow-up scheduling (2 items), use of standard treatment guidelines (2 items), and referral of complicated cases (2 items). Each item was scored on a 3-point scale (always = 2, sometimes = 1, never = 0). The maximum practice score was 20. Participants scoring ≥ 15 points (75%) were categorised as having ‘adequate practice,’ while those scoring < 15 points were categorised as having ‘inadequate practice.’

Data collection procedure

Data were collected by trained research assistants under the supervision of the investigators. Prior to administration, the purpose of the study was explained, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant. The questionnaire was self-administered, and research assistants were available to clarify any questions.

Data analysis

Data were entered into and analysed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics summarised respondent characteristics, and outcome variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations. Knowledge and practice were categorised based on composite scores as described above. Bivariate analysis using chi-square tests was performed to identify variables associated with good knowledge and adequate practice. Variables with p < 0.2 in bivariate analysis were entered into multivariable logistic regression models to identify independent predictors. Results were presented as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Health Research Ethics Committee of Zamfara State Ministry of Health, Gusau (Reference number: ZSHREC26092025/334). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality was maintained throughout the study. All data were anonymised and stored securely.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

Three hundred and forty (340) questionnaires were distributed, 320 were returned, yielding a response rate of 94.1%. The mean age of respondents was 34.6 ± 7.8 years, with the majority (62.8%) being male. The age range was 22–58 years. Community Health Extension Workers (CHEWs) constituted 155 (48.4%), Community Health Officers (CHOs) 103 (32.2%), and other cadres (including nurses, midwives, and medical officers) 62 (19.4%). Most participants had less than 10 years of professional experience (184, 57.5%). A critical finding was that 218 (68.1%) had received no formal in-service training on hypertension management (workshops, seminars, or continuing medical education) in the past two years (Table 1).

Knowledge of hypertension management

Overall, 198 (61.9%) of respondents had good knowledge of hypertension management. While knowledge of basic definitions of hypertension (271, 84.7%) and common risk factors like increased age (254, 79.4%), excessive salt intake (261, 81.6%), and overweight (234, 73.1%) were high, specific clinical knowledge gaps were observed. Only 180 (56.3%) knew the correct diagnostic BP threshold for hypertension (≥ 140/90 mmHg), and 191 (59.7%) could list major long-term complications of uncontrolled hypertension (stroke, heart failure, etc.) (Table 2).

Practice of hypertension management

The overall adequate practice score was 54.7% (175/320). While routine blood pressure screening was reported by 230 (71.9%) and lifestyle counselling by 206 (64.4%), core chronic disease management practices were deficient. Regular follow-up scheduling (154, 48.1%), adherence to standard treatment guidelines (165, 51.6%), and appropriate referral of complicated cases (150, 46.9%) were all below satisfactory levels (Table 3). None of the participating PHC centres had a formal chronic care model or dedicated hypertension registry.

Factors associated with knowledge and practice

Bivariate analysis revealed that professional cadre (p = 0.012), years of professional experience (p = 0.021), and previous training on hypertension management (p = 0.001) were significantly associated with knowledge of hypertension. Adequate hypertension management practice was significantly associated with good knowledge of hypertension (p = 0.003), professional cadre (p = 0.018), and availability of hypertension management guidelines at the PHC (p = 0.009).

Multivariable logistic regression revealed that receiving prior hypertension training was the strongest predictor of good knowledge (AOR = 2.6; 95% CI: 1.6–4.3), and having > 10 years of experience was also significant (AOR = 1.8; 95% CI: 1.1–3.0). Good knowledge was a key independent predictor of better practice (AOR = 2.1; 95% CI: 1.3–3.5). Furthermore, the physical availability of clinical guidelines at the facility significantly increased the odds of adequate practice (AOR = 1.9; 95% CI: 1.2–3.1) (Table 4).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study assessed the knowledge and practice of hypertension management amongst primary healthcare (PHC) workers in Gusau, Zamfara State, Northwestern Nigeria. The findings reveal a moderate level of knowledge (61.9% good knowledge) but suboptimal clinical practice (54.7% adequate practice), underscoring a significant know-do gap in a region marked by resource constraints and security challenges.

Our study indicates that while most healthcare workers understood basic hypertension concepts, critical gaps persisted in identifying diagnostic thresholds (only 56.3% knew the correct BP threshold) and complications (only 59.7% could list major complications). More concerning was the translation of knowledge into practice: fewer than half of respondents consistently scheduled follow-ups (48.1%), adhered to guidelines (51.6%), or made appropriate referrals (46.9%). These findings highlight a disconnect between theoretical knowledge and clinical application, a gap exacerbated by the absence of recent training for 68.1% of workers. Multivariable analysis identified prior training and longer professional experience as key predictors of good knowledge, while both good knowledge and onsite guideline availability independently predicted better practice (participants working in facilities with on-site guidelines had nearly twice the odds of adequate practice: AOR = 1.9; 95% CI: 1.2–3.1) .

The proportion of healthcare workers with good knowledge (61.9%) aligns with findings from Southern Nigeria (65.4%) [16]. However, the depth of knowledge in specific clinical domains—such as diagnostic criteria and complications—was lower than reported in studies from more stable regions [18]. This suggests that aggregate knowledge scores may mask critical competency deficits essential for accurate diagnosis and patient education. The low proportion (59.7%) of respondents who could correctly identify major complications of hypertension is particularly concerning, as this deficit may delay early referral and timely intervention. Strengthening knowledge of complications should therefore be a core component of future training curricula. The observed knowledge-practice gap is consistent with reports from other low-resource settings [17], but appears more pronounced in our study. For example, rates of guideline adherence and follow-up in our sample were substantially lower than those documented in national hypertension programme evaluations in Nigeria [18]. This discrepancy may reflect the unique operational barriers in Zamfara State, where insecurity disrupts supply chains, limits patient mobility, and constrains routine supervision and training [19].

Our finding that guideline availability independently improves practice (AOR = 1.9) supports the concept of point-of-care job aids as cognitive support tools in high-workload, low-training settings [20]. Similarly, the strong link between in-service training and knowledge (AOR = 2.6) corroborates evidence from structured hypertension capacity-building programmes in sub-Saharan Africa [18]. The context of Northwestern Nigeria—characterised by insecurity, limited infrastructure, and a predominantly community-health-worker-driven PHC system—is central to interpreting these findings. The young workforce, largely comprised of CHEWs and CHOs with less than 10 years of experience, reflects the human resource realities of many rural and crisis-affected areas in Nigeria [13, 21, 22]. Their performance under such constraints underscores both resilience and the urgent need for tailored, task-strengthening support [20].

Implications for policy and practice

The practical implications are threefold. For policy, there is a compelling need for state and national policies that mandate the availability of standard treatment guidelines at every PHC facility, guarantee regular competency-based training for all cadres, and establish accountability mechanisms linked to funding and quality improvement. For health systems, institutionalising recurrent, hands-on hypertension training for PHC workers is essential, with curricula targeting identified knowledge gaps (diagnostic thresholds, complications, and guideline use). Concurrently, health agencies should ensure the widespread availability of simplified, pictorial hypertension management guidelines at every PHC facility, particularly in insecure regions.

For clinical practice, facility managers should adopt structured supportive supervision that emphasises not only knowledge retention but also the practical application of guidelines, follow-up systems, and referral protocols. The implementation of simple quality improvement tools—such as patient registers and recall systems—could help address the critically low follow-up rates. None of the facilities we surveyed had such chronic care modules; their introduction should be a priority.

For research, future implementation research should test bundled interventions (combining training, job aids, and supervision) specifically designed for insecure settings. Qualitative studies are also needed to better understand the contextual barriers—including drug stockouts, patient loads, security concerns, and sociocultural factors—that impede optimal practice.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides rare empirical data from an under-researched, crisis-affected region, employing validated tools and multivariable analysis to identify independent predictors of performance. The high response rate (94.1%) and inclusion of multiple cadres strengthen external relevance.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the reliance on self-reported practices introduces the potential for social desirability bias, possibly leading to an overestimation of reported good practices. Second, the cross-sectional design establishes associations but cannot infer causality; observed relationships may be influenced by unmeasured confounders. Third, our knowledge assessment emphasised factual recall over applied clinical reasoning, which may not fully capture practical competence. Fourth, the use of a 75% cutoff to define ‘good knowledge’ and ‘adequate practice’, though consistent with prior studies, is inherently arbitrary and may influence prevalence estimates. Fifth, we did not assess patient outcomes (e.g., blood pressure control), so it remains unknown whether the reported practices translate into improved clinical results. Sixth, the study was conducted in a single, security-challenged state, which may limit generalisability to other regions with different security and resource profiles. Finally, we did not extensively explore facility-level barriers such as consistent drug supply or equipment functionality, which are known to significantly influence practice. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings and designing future interventions.

Conclusion

This study identified a substantial gap between knowledge and practice in hypertension management amongst PHC workers in Northwestern Nigeria. While prior training and access to clinical guidelines were key facilitators of better performance, systemic deficiencies in routine skill updates, chronic care systems, and supportive supervision remain. To improve hypertension control in this vulnerable region, we recommend (i) structured, recurrent in-service training for all PHC cadres; (ii) dissemination of simple, wall-chart style guidelines to every consultation room; and (iii) strengthening of facility-level chronic care systems—including patient registers, follow-up mechanisms, and referral protocols—that support health workers to translate their knowledge into consistent practice. Further research should evaluate the effectiveness of such multicomponent interventions in security-constrained settings.
