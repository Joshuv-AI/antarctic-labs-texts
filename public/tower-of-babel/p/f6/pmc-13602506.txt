Clinical features of nontuberculous mycobacteria and diagnosis with nanopore targeted sequencing: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Xingyu Liu, Tianzhen Wang, Yicheng Guo, Yi Zeng, Weiwei Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiagnosing pulmonary non-tuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) disease remains challenging, not only because its clinical manifestations often overlap with those of tuberculosis (TB), but also because its symptoms and signs—such as chronic cough, sputum production, fatigue, and weight loss—can closely resemble those of other common respiratory conditions, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), recurrent pulmonary infections, and chronic bronchitis. This symptomatic overlap often leads to delayed or misdiagnosis, a problem exacerbated by the time-consuming nature of confirmatory culture, which further impedes timely diagnosis.Against the backdrop of a rising incidence of NTM pulmonary disease, Nanopore Targeted Sequencing (NTS) emerges as a promising solution, enabling rapid and comprehensive pathogen detection. This study aims to evaluate the clinical utility of NTS, with a focus on its diagnostic performance, ability to delineate the pathogen spectrum, and reveal polymicrobial co-infections.MethodsIn this retrospective study, we analyzed 350 patients suspected with pulmonary NTM infection at Nanjing Second Hospital between 2023 and 2025. We evaluated clinical characteristics, pathogen profiles and co-infections. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), sputum, and tissue specimens were subjected to NTS. The diagnostic performance of NTS was assessed by comparing it with traditional methods, including acid-fast bacillus (AFB) smear, mycobacterial species identification, and conventional culture.ResultsAmong 350 enrolled patients, 293 were diagnosed with NTM pulmonary disease. The NTM group was significantly older (median age: 61.0 vs. 43.5 years) compared to the non-NTM group (n = 57). NTM patients demonstrated significant cellular immune dysfunction, including lower CD3⁺, CD4⁺ T-cell levels, and CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio, alongside a more protracted disease course compared to controls. No significant difference in BMI was observed between the two groups.The most common clinical symptom was productive cough, and imaging predominantly showed patchy parenchymal and nodular shadows. Evaluation of diagnostic performance demonstrated that NTS showed the highest sensitivity (95.9%) and the highest AUC (0.892) among the methods evaluated, although its specificity (82.5%) was slightly lower than that of Mycobacterial culture (84.2%). In contrast, traditional methods such as Mycobacterial culture showed critically low sensitivity (50.5%), despite high specificity. Venn diagram analysis confirmed that NTS detected the highest number of positive cases, supporting its role as a comprehensive diagnostic tool. Furthermore, analysis of co-infections revealed a high frequency (56.0%) of polymicrobial infections among NTM patients, with Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Human Herpesvirus being the most common co-pathogens. Other frequent co-infections included Aspergillus, Candida, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Complex polymicrobial combinations were also observed, highlighting the intricate microbial landscape in NTM pulmonary disease and underscoring the need for comprehensive diagnostic strategies.ConclusionNTS is a rapid, highly sensitive tool that may improve the diagnosis of pulmonary NTM by enabling simultaneous species identification and co-infection profiling; however, its optimal application requires integration with clinical, radiological, and immunological findings to accurately distinguish true infection from colonization and guide effective management.

Introduction

Pulmonary disease caused by non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) is an increasingly recognized global health issue, with its incidence surpassing that of tuberculosis (TB) in some regions [1, 2]. In China, a significant upward trend in NTM infections has been observed in major cities including Beijing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou [3–6], particularly among immunocompromised hosts or those with underlying structural lung diseases such as bronchiectasis and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [7–10].

The clinical presentation of NTM disease frequently overlaps with that of tuberculosis (TB) and other pneumonias, often leading to misdiagnosis and delays in appropriate treatment [11]. Furthermore, co-infections between NTM and other pulmonary pathogens are increasingly recognized as clinically significant, associated with distinctive radiological patterns, poorer nutritional status, and reduced survival, especially among elderly patients [12]. Accurate identification of NTM to the species level is also critical, given that different species exhibit varying degrees of pathogenicity and distinct antimicrobial susceptibility profiles, which directly impact therapeutic decisions [13].The diagnosis of NTM lung disease is complex, requiring a combination of clinical, radiological, and microbiological criteria as per established guidelines [14]. Traditional methods, including acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smear and culture, lack speed and sensitivity. Culture, the gold standard, can take weeks to yield results and has suboptimal sensitivity, especially for paucibacillary disease [15] .

Nanopore Targeted Sequencing (NTS) technology, as implemented in clinical diagnostics such as the TBseq® test (Hangzhou ShengTing Medical Technology Co., China), utilizes the Oxford Nanopore MinION or GridION platform to enable real-time, long-read, high-throughput sequencing with a rapid turnaround time. The TBseq® assay is based on targeted NGS that allows simultaneous identification of mycobacterial species—by capturing the 16 S and hsp65 gene regions—and prediction of drug resistance through deep sequencing of 21 Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) genes associated with anti-TB drug resistance. This method is directly applicable to clinical specimens such as sputum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, and integrates automated analysis software (Nano TNGS V1.0) and a secure web application (TBseq® Web App) for result interpretation. This positions NTS as a promising comprehensive diagnostic tool for NTM disease, with potential advantages over some other molecular methods in the setting of complex co-infections. However, the high sensitivity of NTS also necessitates careful differentiation between true pathogens and colonization or residual nucleic acids, a critical consideration in respiratory diagnostics.

We conducted a retrospective analysis of 293 patients with NTM pulmonary disease to evaluate the clinical utility of NTS. The study aimed to: (1) analyze the clinical characteristics of the disease, (2) assess the diagnostic performance of NTS against conventional methods, (3) characterize the NTM species distribution and co-infection profile, and (4) highlight the critical importance of correlating NTS findings with clinical context to differentiate true infection from colonization.

Materials and methods

Study population and sample collection

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nanjing Second Hospital. The study received approval from the Human Research Ethics and System Review Committee of the Second Hospital of Nanjing (ID: 2025-LS-ky112). Between June 2023 and March 2025, 823 patients suspected of having lower respiratory tract infections or NTM pulmonary disease, and who had undergone NTS testing, were initially screened. Of these, 293 patients diagnosed with NTM pulmonary disease were ultimately enrolled. The diagnosis of NTM pulmonary disease was established based on the 2020 Chinese Medical Association guidelines and the 2021 ATS/IDSA guidelines [14], which require a comprehensive assessment integrating clinical, radiological, and microbiological criteria. Specifically, patients must meet all of the following criteria: (1) Clinical Criteria: Presence of persistent or progressive respiratory symptoms (e.g., chronic cough, sputum, hemoptysis), or relevant constitutional symptoms; (2) Radiological Criteria: Chest CT imaging revealing characteristic abnormalities, such as nodular/bronchiectatic changes (especially in the middle lobe and lingula) or cavitary lesions; (3) Microbiological Criteria: Isolation of the same NTM species from at least two separate expectorated sputum samples, or from one bronchial wash/lavage (BALF), or from a lung biopsy specimen.Demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, BMI, T-lymphocyte counts, radiographic findings, sample types (e.g., bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, sputum, tissue), and sequencing reports, were collected from electronic medical records. The detailed screening process is outlined in the figure below(Fig. 1). In addition, for patients who were culture-negative, the diagnosis of NTM pulmonary disease was established through a multidisciplinary approach rather than based on NTS results alone. All such patients fulfilled the following integrated criteria: (1) persistent or progressive respiratory symptoms consistent with NTM disease, such as chronic productive cough or fatigue; (2) characteristic radiographic abnormalities on chest computed tomography, including nodular or bronchiectatic changes (particularly in the middle lobe and lingula), cavitary lesions, or multifocal involvement; and (3) supportive microbiological or histopathological evidence, including a positive acid-fast bacillus smear, species-level identification via NTS, species identification, or granulomatous inflammation on biopsy. A rigorous differential diagnosis was additionally performed to completely rule out other pulmonary diseases with similar clinical and radiological manifestations, and the final diagnosis was further confirmed by combining the patients’ clinical therapeutic response to targeted anti-NTM treatment. All cases were reviewed by a panel of respiratory physicians, radiologists, and microbiologists to differentiate true infection from colonization or contamination.

DNA extraction and library preparation

Genomic DNA was extracted from clinical samples using the standardized protocol and reagents provided in the TBseq® Ultra 4.0 kit (Hangzhou ShengTing Medical Technology Co., China), the TBseq® Ultra 4.0 kit targets the 16 S rRNA and hsp65 genes for mycobacterial species identification and is capable of detecting both Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) and over 30 clinically relevant NTM species, as well as common bacterial, viral, and fungal pathogens, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA libraries for NTS were constructed using the Universal Gene Sequencing Kit (ShengTing), which is based on a multiplex, targeted amplification approach. This method simultaneously targets genomic regions for the identification of mycobacterial species (including the 16 S and hsp65 genes), common bacterial and viral pathogens, fungal elements, and a comprehensive panel of antibiotic resistance genes. The prepared libraries were subsequently sequenced on FLO-MIN106 (R9.4.1) flow cells using the MinION or GridION platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies).

Mycobacterial culture and species identification

Mycobacterial culture was performed using both liquid and solid media to optimize recovery. Respiratory specimens, including bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, sputum, and tissue homogenates, were processed and inoculated into the Mycobacteria Growth Indicator Tube (MGIT) 960 system (BD Diagnostics, USA) for liquid culture, and onto Lowenstein–Jensen (L-J) solid slants. Liquid cultures were incubated at 37 °C for up to 6 weeks, whereas solid cultures were incubated at 37 °C for up to 8 weeks, with weekly inspections for growth. Positive cultures were confirmed by acid-fast bacillus (AFB) staining.

Species identification of positive cultures was conducted using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization–time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; Bruker Daltonics, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For isolates that could not be definitively identified by MALDI-TOF MS with data analyzed using Biotyper 3.1 software (Bruker Daltonics), partial sequencing of the hsp65 or 16 S rRNA gene was performed using Sanger sequencing. The obtained sequences were compared against reference databases (e.g., NCBI GenBank, BLASTn) for final species confirmation. This comprehensive approach ensured accurate speciation, which is essential given the distinct antimicrobial susceptibility profiles associated with different NTM species.

Bioinformatics and data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using appropriate statistical software SPSS version 27, R 4.4.3, and graphical visualizations were generated using GraphPad Prism. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic, clinical, and radiological characteristics of the study participants. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as Mean ± Standard Deviation and were compared between the NTM and non-NTM groups using the Independent Samples t-test. Non-normally distributed data are presented as Median Interquartile Range (IQR)and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are expressed as Numbers (Percentages, n%) and were compared using the Chi-square (χ²) test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.A two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Additionally, the study also calculated the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of the four detection methods by analyzing TP, FP, FN, and TN values. Combined with ROC curves and the Venn diagram, the effectiveness of NTS and other detection methods in diagnosing NTM was compared.

Results

Patient demographics and sample types

A total of 350 patients were included in this study, comprising 293 patients diagnosed with NTM pulmonary disease and 57 patients in the non-NTM group. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of all participants are summarized in Table 1.

The NTM group was significantly older than the non-NTM group, with a median age of 61.0 years versus 43.5 years. Body mass index (BMI) was comparable between the two groups (20.8 ± 4.3 kg/m² in the NTM group vs. 22.3 ± 3.8 kg/m² in the non-NTM group).

In terms of T-lymphocyte immune function, the NTM group demonstrated significantly lower levels of total CD3⁺ and CD4⁺ T cells compared to the non-NTM group, as well as a significantly lower CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio. The disease course was also significantly longer in the NTM group. Regarding imaging characteristics, lesions in the NTM group were predominantly distributed in the right middle lobe, the lingular segment of the left lung, and the basal segments of both lower lobes, with common manifestations including consolidation, nodules, and bronchiectasis. Overall, the NTM group exhibited a more extensive lobar involvement compared to the non-NTM group.

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid was the primary specimen type used for diagnosis in both cohorts. Clinically, cough with sputum was the most frequently reported symptom among all patients. Interestingly, night sweats were more common in non-NTM patients (3.5%) than in the NTM group (2.4%). Among comorbidities, hypertension (16.0%) and a history of tumor (7.8%) were relatively common in the NTM population. Imaging findings in the NTM group revealed a variety of manifestations, with patchy parenchymal shadows and nodular shadows being the most prevalent across different lung lobes. Immunophenotypic analysis based on lymphocyte subsets showed that NTM patients had median CD3⁺, CD4⁺, and CD8⁺ T-cell counts of 934.00, 572.00, and 292.00 cells/µL, respectively, and a CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio of 1.8.

Pathogen spectrum identified by NTS

The most prevalent species was Mycobacterium intracellulare (M. intracellulare), representing a significant portion of the cases with 157 isolates. This was followed by Mycobacterium abscessus (M. abscessus, 48 isolates) and Mycobacterium kansasii (M. kansasii, 20 isolates). Other notable species included Mycobacterium avium (M. avium, 16 isolates) and Mycobacterium gordonae (M. gordonae, 12 isolates).The distribution also comprised several less common species, such as Mycobacterium chelonae (M. chelonae, 10 isolates), Mycobacterium mucogenicum (M. mucogenicum, 9 isolates), and Mycobacterium fortuitum (M. fortuitum, 8 isolates). A group of rare species, including Mycobacterium paragordonae (M. paragordonae, 5 isolates), Mycobacterium xenopi (M. xenopi, 4 isolates) ,M. avium complex (MAC, 4 isolate), Mycobacterium phocaicum (M. phocaicum, 2 isolates), Mycobacterium chimaera (M. chimaera, 1 isolate), Mycobacterium parascrofulaceum (M. parascrofulaceum, 1 isolate), and Mycobacterium europaeum (M. europaeum, 1 isolate), constituted the remainder of the identified specimens see in Fig. 2.

The diagnostic performance of NTS versus established diagnostic methods

The diagnostic performance of NTS was compared against established methods, including acid-fast bacillus (AFB) smear, Mycobacterial culture (MGIT 960 liquid and Lowenstein–Jensen solid media), and species identification by MALDI-TOF MS and Sanger sequencing.As summarized in Table 2; Fig. 3, NTS demonstrated superior sensitivity (95.9%; 95% CI 93.6–98.2) and the highest area under the ROC curve (AUC = 0.892; 95% CI 0.841–0.943) among all tests. While Mycobacterial culture exhibited high specificity (84.2%), its sensitivity was markedly low (50.5%). The AFB smear showed the lowest sensitivity (25.6%) and specificity (NA) .The Venn diagram see Fig. 4. further illustrates that NTS detected the largest number of positive cases, including many that were missed by the other methods, highlighting its comprehensive detection capability.

Dominant role of NTS in solo and combined diagnosis of NTM

Among all NTM-positive patients, NTS, Mycobacterial culturer and Species identification tests yielded positive results in 281, 148, and 98 cases, respectively. A single diagnostic method identified 105 patients: NTS alone accounted for 100 cases, followed by Mycobacterial culture (3 cases) and Species identification (2 cases). Concurrent positivity by two methods was observed in 127 patients. The combinations of NTS with Mycobacterial culture, and Species identification accounted for 87, and 38 cases, respectively, while the remaining two cases were identified by Mycobacterial culture with Species identification. Furthermore, 56 patients tested positive by three methods: the combinations of NTS with Mycobacterial culture and Species identification (Fig. 4).

High prevalence of co-infections

The analysis of pathogen co-infections revealed a complex microbial landscape among patients with NTM pulmonary disease. A significant proportion of patients (164/293, 56.0%)presented with NTM co-infections involving one or more additional pathogens. The number of concomitant pathogens varied, with 94 cases involving one pathogens, 45 cases involving two pathogens, 17 cases involving three, and several cases involving up to six different pathogens. Among the co-infecting organisms, Mycobacterium tuberculosis (TB) (n = 32) and Human Herpesvirus (HHV) (n = 19) were the most frequently identified. Other common co-pathogens included fungi such as Aspergillus (n = 12) and Candida (n = 11), and bacteria like Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA) (n = 3). Specific polymicrobial combinations were also observed, with the most prevalent being Candida and HHV (n = 6), followed by TB and Nocardia farcinica (n = 4), and TB and HHV (n = 4). (Fig. 5)

Comparative analysis of NTS-positive/mycobacterial culture-negative and NTS-positive/mycobacterial culture-positive patients

To further evaluate the diagnostic value of NTS in cases with results discordant from conventional culture, we compared the clinical characteristics of patients who were NTS-positive but Mycobacterial culture-negative (NTS⁺/Mycobacterial culture⁻, n = 138) with those who tested positive by both methods (NTS⁺/Mycobacterial culture⁺, n = 143). The results are summarized in Table 3.

Patients in the NTS⁺/Mycobacterial culture⁻ group were significantly younger (median age: 60.0 years vs. 64.0 years, p = 0.003) and had a slightly higher BMI (20.7 kg/m² vs. 20.0 kg/m², p = 0.018) compared with the NTS⁺/Mycobacterial culture⁺ group. A higher proportion of patients in the NTS⁺/Mycobacterial culture⁻ group were diagnosed via lung pathological tissue Sect. (23.9% vs. 0%, p < 0.001), whereas the NTS⁺/Mycobacterial culture⁺ group was more frequently diagnosed using bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (86.7% vs. 55.1%, p < 0.001). This pattern suggests that the NTS⁺/Mycobacterial culture⁻ group may represent cases with a lower microbiological burden or more localized disease, in which NTS offers a significant diagnostic advantage.

Radiologically, the NTS⁺/Mycobacterial culture⁻ group exhibited less extensive disease, with a lower proportion of patients having five lobes involved (21.0% vs. 50.3%, p < 0.001) and a higher proportion having three to four lobes involved (36.9% vs. 20.3%, p < 0.001). Regarding T-lymphocyte subsets, no significant differences were observed in CD3⁺, CD4⁺, or CD8⁺ counts, nor in the CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio, between the two groups.

Distribution of NTM species in NTS-positive/ mycobacterial culture-negative and NTS-positive/ mycobacterial culture-positive patients

As shown in Fig. 6, the distribution of NTM species differed between the NTS⁺/Mycobacterial culture⁻ and NTS⁺/Mycobacterial culture⁺ groups. In both groups, M. intracellulare was the predominant species, accounting for the majority of cases. However, its relative proportion was higher in the NTS⁺/Mycobacterial culture⁻ group (68.1%) compared with the NTS⁺/Mycobacterial culture⁺ group (58.0%). M. abscessus was the second most common species in both groups, with a slightly higher proportion in the NTS⁺/Mycobacterial culture⁺ group (19.6%) than in the NTS⁺/Mycobacterial culture⁻ group (13.0%). Other species, including M. kansasii, M. avium, and M. gordonae, were observed at low frequencies in both groups, with no significant differences in distribution. These findings suggest that NTS detects NTM species with greater sensitivity, even in cases where culture remains negative, while the overall species composition remains largely consistent across different culture statuses.Notably, NTS identified a broader diversity of NTM species than culture-based methods, including rare species not recovered by conventional culture.

Discussion

The presented findings highlight significant demographic, immunologic, and clinical distinctions between patients with NTM pulmonary disease and the non-NTM control group. The NTM cohort was notably older, which aligns with the established epidemiology of NTM pulmonary disease, as aging is associated with a natural decline in immune competence and an increased prevalence of structural lung damage, both of which are key risk factors for NTM infection [14]. More critically, the NTM group exhibited a profoundly compromised cellular immune profile, characterized by significantly lower levels of total CD3⁺ and CD4⁺ T-cells and a reduced CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio. This finding is consistent with the known pathogenesis of NTM, where an effective T-helper 1 (Th1) cell-mediated immune response, orchestrated by CD4⁺ T-cells, is essential for controlling M. intracellular growth [16]. The observed immunophenotype—with median CD4⁺ T-cell counts of 572.00 cells/µL—suggests a state of relative immunosuppression, even in the absence of a profoundly immunodeficient state, which may predispose individuals to NTM acquisition and persistence [17].

Radiologically, the predilection of NTM lesions for the right middle lobe, lingula, and basal segments of the lower lobes, manifesting as consolidation, nodules, and bronchiectasis, is a classic presentation often referred to as the “Lady Windermere syndrome” pattern. This distribution is thought to result from impaired mucociliary clearance in these dependent lung zones, facilitating the colonization and infection by NTM. The more extensive lobar involvement in the NTM group, coupled with a longer disease course, underscores the chronic and progressive nature of this disease [18]. The low prevalence of classic systemic symptoms like night sweats further distinguishes NTM from tuberculosis and emphasizes its indolent course. The high rate of comorbidities such as hypertension and a history of tumor in the NTM population may reflect the demographic of older adults affected but also suggests that underlying conditions could contribute to immune senescence or dysregulation. Overall, these results reinforce the concept that NTM pulmonary disease is a complex interplay between host immunity (cellular immunodeficiency) and local lung anatomy (structural airway disease), necessitating a high index of suspicion for diagnosis, particularly in older individuals with characteristic imaging findings.

This study demonstrates the exceptional utility of NTS as a comprehensive diagnostic tool for NTM pulmonary disease. Our analysis of 293 clinical samples reveals several key findings with direct clinical relevance, positioning NTS at the forefront of modern infectious disease diagnostics.

First, the high detection rate (281/293 = 95.9%) of NTM suggests the effectiveness of NTS in identifying these pathogens. The species distribution, dominated by M. intracellulare and M. abscessus, aligns with epidemiological studies from East Asia [19–21]. Accurate species-level identification is critical as treatment regimens differ significantly; for example, M. abscessus is notoriously resistant to antibiotics and requires a macrolide-based multidrug regimen [22]. The rapidity of NTS (results within a single work shift) is a potential advantage over culture-based methods, enabling clinicians to initiate species-directed therapy much earlier. This is particularly crucial given the evolving treatment landscape, which includes inhaled amikacin (ALIS) for MAC [23] and novel regimens for M. abscessus involving dual β-lactams [24–26] or oxazolidinones like contezolid [27, 28], where early and correct species identification directly impacts regimen choice.

Second, the high prevalence of co-infections (56.0%) uncovered by NTS represents a notable finding, which may be missed by conventional diagnostic methods. Although the clinical significance remains to be fully elucidated, the detection of fungi like Aspergillus and viruses like EBV suggests these pathogens may modulate the host immune response or exacerbate lung damage, potentially influencing disease progression and outcome. This comprehensive picture may help clinicians to consider all detected pathogens, but clinical relevance must be determined by integrating microbiological, clinical and radiological data. Furthermore, the ability of NTS to provide a holistic pathogen profile echoes the diagnostic philosophy behind other emerging non-culture-based biomarkers, such as urine LAM [29], breath volatiles, and serum cfDNA [30], which aim to capture the complex pathophysiology of infection beyond a single pathogen. NTS uniquely integrates this comprehensive profiling with precise microbial identification. However, interpreting such complex microbial landscapes requires caution. As highlighted in discussions of metagenomic sequencing (mNGS), a core challenge is differentiating true infection from colonization, particularly for organisms known to inhabit the respiratory tract. Clinicians must integrate NTS findings with host immune status, clinical symptoms, and radiological features to ascertain the clinical significance of each detected pathogen.

Third, the detection of MTBC in 20.1%, of patients underscores the diagnostic challenge in differentiating TB from NTM disease. This finding is consistent with recent studies reporting significant rates of TB/NTM co-infection, which is associated with more severe clinical manifestations, including lower BMI, higher rates of cavitation and hilar lymphadenopathy, and worse survival outcomes, especially in the elderly [12].NTS can also detect MTBC in the same test, which may help reduce diagnostic delays, provided that results are interpreted with clinical correlation and that appropriate public health measures are ensured. Besides, the relatively low sensitivity of Mycobacterial culture (50.5%) observed in our study is consistent with previous reports. For instance, using BALF specimens, culture sensitivity for NTM has been reported as 77.0% [31] to 95% [32], while sputum culture sensitivity is considerably lower, ranging from 30% to 44% in certain radiological subtypes [32]. Notably, even under optimized conditions using BAL, approximately 5%–23% of NTM-PD cases remain culture-negative [31, 32] Several factors contribute to this suboptimal sensitivity, including prior antibiotic exposure, low bacterial load (paucibacillary disease), and the fastidious nature of some NTM species, such as slowly growing mycobacteria that require extended incubation periods [33]. This highlights the complementary role of molecular methods like NTS in culture-negative or low-burden disease, a role that has been increasingly recognized in recent studies using nanopore sequencing and other molecular technologies [34].

Forth, NTS also demonstrated particular value in patients with negative Mycobacterial culture, who accounted for nearly half of the NTM cohort in this study. These patients were younger, had less extensive radiological involvement, and were more frequently diagnosed via lung tissue rather than bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, suggesting a more localized or paucibacillary disease pattern in which conventional culture often fails [35, 36]. The species distribution remained largely consistent between culture-positive and culture-negative groups, with M. intracellulare predominating in both, further supporting that NTS detects authentic pathogens rather than nonspecific signals [37]. These findings highlight the complementary role of NTS in culture-negative NTM disease, though integration with clinical, radiological, and host immune assessments remains essential to avoid overdiagnosis, particularly in elderly patients with structural lung disease [14].

Finally, the identification of antibiotic resistance genes provides an early warning for potential treatment failures. While correlation with phenotypic susceptibility testing is needed, the presence of genes like blaNDM or mecA can guide the initial selection of empirical therapy, especially in severe cases. This functional genomic insight may offer valuable insights in the era of precision medicine, especially when considering the use of newer agents like the benzoxaboroles (e.g., epetraborole, MRX-5) [38, 39], where pre-existing resistance could jeopardize treatment success.

A crucial consideration in applying highly sensitive molecular techniques like NTS is the challenge of microbial colonization. Similar to the diagnostic dilemma observed with Pneumocystis jirovecii [38], NTM can exist as a colonizer in individuals with underlying lung diseases such as COPD or bronchiectasis without causing active disease. The mere detection of NTM DNA by NTS, therefore, should not be conflated with a diagnosis of NTM pulmonary disease. Our findings, which report a 95.9% detection rate, underscore the test’s sensitivity but also highlight the imperative for stringent clinical correlation.But a positive NTS result alone should never be used as the sole criterion for diagnosing NTM pulmonary disease. The diagnosis must follow established guidelines that require compatible clinical symptoms and consistent radiological findings. However, the interpretation of such complex microbial landscapes requires caution. The detection of multiple microorganisms, including fungi like Candida or viruses like EBV, does not automatically equate to active disease for each one. As highlighted in discussions of metagenomic sequencing (mNGS), a core challenge is differentiating true infection from colonization, particularly for organisms known to inhabit the respiratory tract [39–42]. Parameters such as NTS read counts and genome coverage may offer supportive evidence, but there are no absolute thresholds to definitively separate colonization from infection. Ultimately, the clinical context—evaluating the host’s immune status, symptom profile, and radiographic findings—remains paramount in accurately interpreting a positive NTS result and avoiding unnecessary treatment for colonization. The diagnosis of NTM lung disease must still adhere to established guidelines [14], which integrate clinical and radiological criteria.

Limitations

This is a single-center retrospective study. It is important to note that the NTS assay used in this study is a targeted sequencing panel, as opposed to metagenomic sequencing (mNGS); consequently, it is unable to detect rare or novel pathogens that fall outside its pre-defined target range. Clinical outcomes and correlation with phenotypic drug susceptibility testing (DST) were not fully analyzed in this dataset, and future prospective studies linking NTS results to treatment outcomes and phenotypic DST are warranted.

A common limitation in studies utilizing highly sensitive nucleic acid detection methods is the lack of systematic data to definitively classify cases as true infection versus colonization. At present, no universal read-count or relative abundance thresholds can definitively distinguish true infection from colonization or background noise using NTS. In this study, differentiation relied on a composite reference standard integrating clinical, radiological, and microbiological data, with semi-quantitative consideration of read abundance and rank. Future prospective studies with quantitative PCR or RNA-based sequencing are needed to establish clinically actionable cut-offs.

Furthermore, the absence of a perfect reference standard introduces incorporation bias. Specifically, the reference standard for NTM pulmonary disease was a composite of clinical, radiological, and microbiological criteria, which in culture-negative patients partially incorporated NTS results. This may have inflated the apparent specificity (82.5%), because NTS findings contributed to defining who was considered disease-free. Consequently, the true specificity of NTS could be lower than reported. Future studies using an independent reference standard (e.g., longitudinal clinical follow-up without NTS-informed diagnosis) are needed to validate the true specificity of NTS.

Finally, cost-effectiveness analyses comparing NTS to standard diagnostic workflows, including emerging biomarker tests, would be highly valuable for guiding its implementation in routine clinical practice.

Conclusion

This study suggests that NTS may be a rapid and sensitive diagnostic tool for pulmonary NTM disease. In this cohort, it showed higher detection efficacy than traditional methods, but its results require clinical correlation; the test also allows simultaneous identification of NTM species, polymicrobial co-infections, and resistance markers — features that could inform targeted therapy. Given the nonspecific clinical presentation of NTM and its frequent occurrence in individuals with compromised immunity or underlying structural lung disease, distinguishing true infection from colonization or mimicking conditions remains a clinical challenge. Therefore, the optimal application of NTS necessitates its integration with a thorough assessment of clinical symptoms, radiographic findings, and host immune status. This integrated diagnostic approach is essential to achieve accurate diagnosis, guide appropriate management, and ultimately improve patient outcomes.
