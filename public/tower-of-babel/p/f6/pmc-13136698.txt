Operative treatment is associated with improved 3-year survival after periprosthetic humerus fracture
Authors: Alyssa R Henriquez, Bryan Crook, Lindsey V Ruderman, Aaron Therien, Eoghan T Hurley, Christopher Klifto, Oke Anakwenze, Michael A Moverman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study examines the survival implications of operative versus nonoperative management of periprosthetic humerus fractures in a large, single-center cohort.MethodsPatients at a single tertiary care center with periprosthetic humerus fractures about a shoulder prosthesis between January 2013 and March 2024 were retrospectively identified. Patients were stratified according to operative versus nonoperative treatment. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. Survival was compared using Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests. Prespecified time horizons were 90 days, 1 year, 2 years, and 3 years.ResultsNinety patients with periprosthetic fractures were identified (23 nonoperative, 67 operative). There were 13 total deaths (8/23 [35%] nonoperative, 5/67 [7.5%] operative). Baseline characteristics were largely similar aside from a higher body mass index in the operative group (32 vs. 29 kg/m2, P = .046). Age (nonoperative: 74 vs. operative: 71 years, P = .30), sex (P = .40), hand dominance (P > .9), race (P = .07), smoking (P = .40), comorbidity burden (Elixhauser weighted and count P = .20 and P = .60, respectively), and Charlson distribution (P = .20) were all similar between groups. On Kaplan–Meier analysis, early mortality was rare: 90-day survival was 95.7% in the nonoperative group versus 100.0% in the operative group (P = .09). Survival differences widened over time, becoming significant at 3 years after the injury: 1 year (nonoperative: 95.7%; operative: 98.5%; P = .42), 2 years (nonoperative: 90%, operative: 98.5%; P = .09), and 3 years (nonoperative: 72.2%; operative: 96.5%; P = .004).ConclusionsSurgical fixation of periprosthetic humerus fractures is associated with lower mortality that becomes apparent in a delayed fashion and grows over time. By 3 years after the injury, the mortality difference approached 25% between operatively and nonoperatively treated patients. These findings should be validated in larger, multicenter studies.Keywords: Periprosthetic fractures, Mortality, Operative management, Nonoperative management, Reverse shoulder arthroplasty, Total shoulder arthroplasty, Hemiarthroplasty

As the incidence of shoulder arthroplasty continues to increase, complications such as periprosthetic fracture are more commonly observed in practice.8,23 Periprosthetic humerus fractures affect an estimated 0.5 to 3% of shoulder arthroplasty patients and pose a distinct clinical challenge, in part, because they tend to arise in patients with existing comorbidities and humeral bone loss.16,18 Currently, optimal treatment pathways for periprosthetic fractures are not well established, specifically the impact of operative versus nonoperative management on mortality.17,21

Operative management of periprosthetic fractures ranges from open reduction and internal fixation to revision shoulder arthroplasty, with the respective strategy chosen according to fracture characteristics and individual patient risk factors.15,17 Alternatively, patients with minimal displacement and no implant loosening may be managed with nonoperative immobilization.6,15 For lower extremity periprosthetic fractures, operative management has been associated with lower mortality, although current evidence is mixed and may depend on fracture pattern.3,9 The survival advantage of operative management may be associated with earlier mobilization and a reduced risk of immobility-related complications.2,19,20 Whether this mortality benefit extends to periprosthetic fractures in the upper extremity is currently unknown.

This study examines the survival implications of operative versus nonoperative management of periprosthetic humerus fractures in a large, single-center cohort. We hypothesized that operative management would be associated with lower mortality compared to nonoperative treatment.

Methods

Patient selection and data collection

Patients with periprosthetic humerus fractures at a single tertiary referral center between January 2013 and March 2024 were retrospectively identified. Treatment was classified as either operative or nonoperative. Periprosthetic humerus fractures were included regardless of whether they occurred intraoperatively or in the postinjury setting, provided the fracture occurred in association with a shoulder arthroplasty implant. The distribution of fracture mechanism did not significantly differ between operative and nonoperative cohorts, with the majority of fractures in both groups resulting from traumatic falls.

The patient demographics collected included age at time of injury, sex, race, body mass index (BMI; kg/m2), Charlson Comorbidity Index, Elixhauser Comorbidity Index, smoking status, and preambulatory injury status. Additionally, the index implant type and injury mechanism were collected. Follow-up was defined as the time from the date of injury to the most recent clinical encounter available in the electronic medical record.

Imaging was reviewed by a single author to categorize each fracture according to the Sanchez-Sotelo classification system. This system groups periprosthetic fractures as either tuberosity fractures (type 1), diaphyseal fractures that overlap the humeral implant (type 2), or fractures completely distal to the humeral implant (type 3). The fractures were then further categorized according to bone stock and stem type.

Statistical analysis

Time zero was specified as the date of injury, and patients were censored at last clinical encounter. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. Continuous variables were compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and categorical variables were compared using Fisher exact tests. Survival was compared using Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves for operative versus nonoperative groups, with differences assessed by log-rank tests. Prespecified time horizons were 90 days, 1 year, 2 years, 3 years, and 5 years; given cohort follow-up of 35 (24–48) months, KM plots were truncated at 3 years for the primary display. Propensity score matching was explored but deemed infeasible due to sample size and limited overlap. Similarly, multivariable survival modeling with Cox proportional hazard models was not pursued to avoid overfitting. Statistics were computed using RStudio (version 2023.12.1 + 402) with R (version 4.3.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Ninety patients with periprosthetic fractures were identified, comprising 23 nonoperative patients and 67 operative patients. The median follow-up was 35 [24, 48] months. Baseline characteristics were largely similar aside from a modestly higher BMI in the operative group (32 vs. 29 kg/m2, P = .046; Table I). Age (nonoperative: 74 vs. operative: 71 years, P = .30), sex (P = .40), race (P = .07), smoking (P = .40), comorbidity burden (Elixhauser weighted and count P = .20 and P = .60, respectively), and Charlson distribution (P = .20) were all statistically similar between groups. Although this did not reach statistical significance, there was a trend toward a higher rate of assistive device utilization during ambulation in the nonoperative group (no assistive device 65% vs. 85%, P = .11), as well as a higher percentage of Caucasian patients in the operative group (91% vs. 78%) (P = .07).

There were 13 total deaths throughout the study duration, which included 8 of 23 (35%) patients in the nonoperative group, and 5 of 67 (7.5%) patients in the operative group (Fig. 1). On KM analysis, early mortality was rare: 90-day survival was 95.7% in the nonoperative group versus 100.0% in the operative group (P = .09; Table II and Fig. 2). Survival differences widened over time, becoming significant at 3 years after the injury. At 1-year, the survival rate was 95.7% for nonoperative patients versus 98.5% for operative patients (P = .42; Fig. 3). At 2 years, the survival rate was 90% for nonoperative patients and 98.5% for operative patients (P = .09; Fig. 4). At 3 years, the difference in survival significantly differed, with a rate of 72.2% for nonoperative patients and 96.5% for operative patients (P = .004; Fig. 5).

The distribution of index implants significantly differed between groups (Table III). The nonoperative cohort had a higher percentage of hemiarthroplasty patients (22% vs. 3%; P = .001), and reverse shoulder arthroplasty was more common in the operative group (76% vs. 39%; P = .001). The majority of patients (87%) sustained their fractures as a result of direct trauma such as falls. The distribution of fracture type did not significantly differ between cohorts: for both the operative and nonoperative cohorts, the majority of patients had type 2 fractures, or diaphyseal fractures overlapping the humeral implant.

Discussion

This study found that surgical fixation of periprosthetic humerus fractures is associated with lower mortality that becomes apparent in a delayed fashion and grows over time. By 3 years after the injury, the mortality difference approached 25% between operatively and nonoperatively treated patients. These findings suggest that the benefits associated with surgical stabilization, such as early restoration of mobility, may have long-term survival implications that compound over time. While this study presents one of the largest single-institution cohorts of periprosthetic fracture patients to date, it is important that these findings are validated in larger, multicenter studies, as the decision to pursue operative versus nonoperative treatment is often patient and fracture specific.

For lower extremity periprosthetic fractures, certain studies have likewise found an association between surgical management and improved survival. Ahmad et al3 found that nonoperative management was linked to higher mortality rates after both hip and knee fractures, regardless of fracture complexity. Farrow et al9 specifically explored treatment delays after periprosthetic hip and knee fractures in a systematic review and found significantly better outcomes for patients who received early surgery. Conversely, Efird et al7 found that certain types of periprosthetic femoral fractures may be adequately treated with nonoperative management, and that 1-year mortality did not differ between operative versus nonoperative patients. Overall, these results underscore the complexity of managing periprosthetic fractures, which should incorporate nuances such as their location, as well as patient factors and goals. Despite the available evidence regarding periprosthetic fractures in the lower limb, little is known regarding the mortality implications of operative versus non operative management of upper extremity periprosthetic fractures.

While prior studies regarding periprosthetic humerus fractures have not directly focused on mortality, there is a growing body of literature on union rates, complications, and surgical technique for these patients.1,21 Tansey et al21 found that nonoperative treatment is associated with significantly higher complication rates and malunion rates than operative management. Cox et al5 noted that 25% of patients in their single-institution study failed initial nonoperative treatment and were converted to operative management, although both groups had a high rate of union. Abaydi et al reported on a retrospective case series, which found that operative patients had more consistent functional recovery according to Constant Murley scores and range of motion measurements.1 Of note, the choice of whether to pursue operative management ultimately depends on a variety of factors, including fracture location, stem fixation, and individual patient risk factors.11 Regardless, the evidence supports operative management in terms of functional outcomes, and union rates represents an important consideration when selecting a treatment plan.

One possible reason for the observed survival benefit after operative management is the effect of early mobilization on recovery and long-term activity. However, patients who receive nonoperative management with a sling or brace may have prolonged functional limitations, which inhibits their ability to be physically active and prevent deconditioning.2,4,19,20 This is especially crucial for elderly patients, who most often receive arthroplasty and are at increased risk for immobility-related complications such as venous thromboembolism, pneumonia, and pressure ulcers.22,25 Of note, this study did not directly assess functional outcomes, and no causal inference can be made. Although preinjury ambulatory status was assessed as a proxy for baseline functional independence and did not significantly differ between groups, this measure does not capture upper extremity-specific function or reliance on the affected limb for transfers or assistive device use. As such, an upper extremity injury may not directly limit ambulation for all patients. Additionally, the nonoperative cohort had a higher prevalence of hemiarthroplasty, which may reflect differences in baseline shoulder pathology, bone quality, or preinjury function that influenced both index arthroplasty selection and subsequent fracture management.10 These factors could not be fully accounted for in this retrospective analysis but are important considerations when counseling patients regarding treatment options.

The mortality difference in this study widened over time, with an 8.5% difference at 2 years that trended toward significance and 24.3% at 3 years that was statistically significant. The fact that this difference increased over time and was not significant until 3 years suggests that it may be associated with downstream effects of prolonged functional limitation. In the orthopedic literature, studies on elderly patients with hip fractures have comparably found that operative management is associated with at 28.6% reduced rate of 1-year mortality on average, in addition to improved 2-year mortality rates.13,14 In combination with existing data on the union and lower complication rates observed in operative patients, the findings of our study likewise suggest that operative management should be considered when feasible and indicated based on fracture characteristics and patient goals.1,5,21

In this study, baseline demographic characteristics, including age, sex, Elixhauser Comorbidity Index and Charlson Comorbidity Index, were statistically similar between cohorts. The median BMI was higher in the operative cohort, which would be expected to predispose this group to worse outcomes, although the opposite trend was observed.12,24 We elected to not use propensity score matching due to the small nonoperative cohort, which would have forced large exclusions and left limited events for time-to-event modeling. In a modestly sized cohort, this would have inflated variance and amplified selection bias, in addition to increasing the risk of overfitting multivariable models. Despite the similar patient characteristics between groups, multicenter studies with larger cohorts of patients and improved matching should be considered, as it is possible that are results are influenced by confounding factors.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. While we present one of the largest single-institution cohorts of periprosthetic fracture patients to date, the patient count is ultimately limited by the rare nature of this condition. Furthermore, the cohort size restricts the statistical analysis that is possible for this cohort without overfitting. This retrospective study is inherently subject to selection bias, as treatment decisions are influenced by patient health status, fracture characteristics, and surgeon judgment. Although baseline demographics, comorbidity indices, and ambulatory status were similar between groups, unmeasured factors such as frailty, cognitive impairment, or social support may have contributed to both treatment selection and long-term mortality risk. Furthermore, the limited cohort size and small number of mortality events may render the observed associations statistically fragile and increase the risk of type II error in comparisons of baseline characteristics. As such, the observed survival differences should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. This study did not include direct measures of post-operative function or activity level, which limits the ability to evaluate functional decline as a mediator of long-term mortality differences. Finally, cause-specific mortality could not be reliably assessed due to incomplete documentation and out of system deaths and therefore was not reported to avoid misclassification or differential ascertainment across treatment groups.

Conclusion

Operative management of periprosthetic humerus fractures is associated with a lower mortality rate that becomes apparent in a delayed fashion and grows over time. By 3 years after injury, the mortality difference approached 25% between operatively and nonoperatively managed patients. These findings should be validated in larger, multicenter studies.
