Low-dose oral minoxidil for the management of vismodegib-induced alopecia
Authors: Jack Newcomer, Nessa Aghazadeh
Published: 2025

Introduction

Vismodegib, an inhibitor of the sonic hedgehog pathway, is a useful treatment for locally advanced or metastatic basal cell carcinoma (BCC) and has also been shown to suppress the development of BCCs in patients with genetic syndromes such as nevoid basal cell carcinoma syndrome (Gorlin syndrome).1 Side effects of vismodegib include muscle spasms, alopecia, dysgeusia, and weight loss. Alopecia, which is seen in 58% of patients on vismodegib,2 is a distressing side effect, which can lead to medication discontinuation. We present a case of a 66 year-old-female with vismodegib-induced alopecia successfully treated with low-dose oral minoxidil.

Case report

A 66-year-old female with a history of nevoid BCC syndrome and a heterozygous PTCH1 gene variant (c.1184T>A) was initially managed with frequent Mohs micrographic surgery and other destructive procedures for numerous skin cancers over 4 decades. For the past 11 years, she had been treated with intermittent courses of vismodegib, which significantly reduced the frequency of new BCCs. However, she developed diffuse, progressive alopecia and muscle spasms that eventually led her to discontinue the medication for the previous 2 months due to negative psychosocial effects.

On physical examination, she had diffuse, non-scarring alopecia throughout the scalp (Fig 1, A, Fig 2, A). She was initiated on low-dose oral minoxidil (0.625 mg daily), and after 3 months had reported subjective improvement in hair growth. Photos taken 1 year later demonstrate a sustained improvement in hair density (Fig 1, B, Fig 2, B). Discussion

The hedgehog signaling pathway plays an important role in hair follicle development and cycling, particularly the transition from the telogen (resting) to anagen (growth) phase.3 Inhibition of this pathway by agents, such as vismodegib, can impair this transition, resulting in alopecia. Hair loss associated with vismodegib is typically gradual, unlike the rapid anagen effluvium seen with chemotherapy, and may involve the scalp, face, and body. It is typically reversible upon discontinuation. Alopecia occurs in up to 58% of patients on vismodegib and 49% of patients on sonidegib, another hedgehog inhibitor.2

Oral minoxidil, a vasodilator originally developed as an antihypertensive, has gained increasing off-label use in treating various types of alopecia including both male and female pattern hair loss. It demonstrates efficacy through a variety of mechanisms including shortening of the telogen phase, prolonging of the anagen phase, reduction of perifollicular microinflammation, and increased blood supply to the hair follicle.4 Minoxidil also exhibits anti-fibrotic properties through inhibition of lysyl hydroxylase, an enzyme necessary for collagen cross-linking, explaining its occasional use in scarring alopecias. Common side effects include hypertrichosis, fluid retention, and hypotension, although these are less common at lower doses.

Low-dose oral minoxidil, typically ranging from 0.25 to 5 mg daily, is an emerging treatment option for various forms of non-scarring alopecia.5 Its favorable side effect profile, ease of administration, and growing evidence base have led to increased use by dermatologists for patients with androgenetic alopecia, telogen effluvium, and alopecia areata. Doses under 1 mg are generally well tolerated and may be especially useful for patients with drug-induced alopecia, as in this case.

This case highlights the potential benefit of low-dose oral minoxidil in hedgehog pathway inhibitor-induced alopecia. The anagen-prolonging effects of minoxidil may help counteract the impaired telogen-anagen transition caused by vismodegib. In addition, switching to sonidegib may have provided an additional benefit to her symptoms, although alopecia is a common side effect with all hedgehog inhibitors. Since alopecia is both common and distressing in patients on these agents, proactive treatment can improve both adherence and patient outcomes. Interruptions in vismodegib therapy can lead to development of new or worsening of preexisting BCCs, highlighting the importance of managing side effects to increase long-term adherence.

Given the high prevalence of alopecia with hedgehog pathway inhibitors, we recommend a low threshold for initiating low-dose oral minoxidil. Early intervention may prevent progression to severe alopecia, reduce psychosocial distress, and improve adherence and disease control. This case supports the use of minoxidil not only as a salvage treatment for drug-induced alopecia, but also as a potential prophylactic option.

In summary, this case highlights significant improvement in alopecia with low-dose oral minoxidil in a patient on vismodegib, allowing her to resume hedgehog pathway inhibition while maintaining satisfactory hair regrowth and disease suppression. Future systematic investigations should evaluate optimal dosing regimens of oral minoxidil for drug-induced alopecia, including comparisons between lower and higher doses in terms of efficacy, durability of response, and side effect profiles. Prospective studies are needed to further define the role of oral minoxidil as both a treatment and preventative strategy for alopecia in patients receiving hedgehog pathway inhibitors.
