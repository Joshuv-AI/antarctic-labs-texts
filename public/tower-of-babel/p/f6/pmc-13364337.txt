Long-term Clinical Outcomes of Radiofrequency Ablation for Pathologically Diagnosed T1a Renal Cell Carcinoma: A 10-Year Single-center Experience
Authors: Masashi Fujimori, Takashi Yamanaka, Naritaka Matsushita, Seiya Kishi, Chisami Nagata, Hiroaki Kato, Hikari Fukui, Yuki Omori, Ryosuke Shima, Kouhei Nishikawa, Hiroshi Kodama, Haruyuki Takaki, Koichiro Yamakado, Hajime Sakuma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose: Radiofrequency ablation is a useful alternative to surgery for small renal cell carcinoma in nonsurgical candidates; however, its long-term clinical outcomes remain controversial. This study evaluated the long-term outcomes of radiofrequency ablation in patients with pathologically diagnosed T1a renal cell carcinoma.Material and Methods: This single-center retrospective study included 60 consecutive patients (median age, 67.0 years) who underwent radiofrequency ablation for 62 pathologically diagnosed T1a renal cell carcinomas between 2002 and 2014, all without prior renal cell carcinoma or other active malignancies. Tumor diameters ranged from 1.0 to 3.9 cm (median, 2.3 cm), and 22 (35.5%) renal cell carcinomas were centrally located. Overall and cancer-specific survival, local tumor control, and complications were evaluated.Results: Complete tumor ablation was achieved in 53 renal cell carcinomas (85.5%) after the initial radiofrequency ablation and in all renal cell carcinomas after the second radiofrequency ablation. The local tumor control rates were 96.3% (95% confidence interval, 91.2%-100%) and 91.6% (95% confidence interval, 83.6%-99.6%) at 5 and 10 years, respectively. During a median follow-up period of 9.0 years (range, 1.3-16.5 years), renal cell carcinoma-related death in only one (1.7%) patient. Overall and cancer-specific survival rates were 98.3% (95% confidence interval, 94.9%-100%) and 100% at 5 years, and 85.7% (95% confidence interval, 75.0%-96.4%) and 97.4% (95% confidence interval, 92.5%-100%) at 10 years. Univariate analysis identified diabetes mellitus (p = 0.007) and chronic kidney disease (p = 0.039) as significant prognostic factors. No procedure-related deaths or major complications were observed. Dialysis was initiated in four (6.7%) patients.Conclusions: Radiofrequency ablation is a safe procedure with strong anticancer effects and contributes to the long-term prognosis of patients with T1a renal cell carcinoma.

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is the most common diagnosis of kidney cancer, and its global incidence is increasing [1]. Most newly diagnosed RCC cases are early-stage T1a tumors [2, 3]. Partial nephrectomy is preferred for patients with small RCC without metastasis based on the treatment guidelines [4-6]. However, some patients are not eligible for surgery because of comorbidities or advanced age. The aforementioned guidelines also suggest the usefulness of thermal ablation as an alternative to surgery in frail patients with small RCCs who have surgical risks, impaired renal function, solitary kidneys, bilateral multiple RCC, or hereditary RCC [4, 5]. The advantages of thermal ablation include a lower complication rate [7, 8] and a reduced risk of renal function decline [7-9].

RFA is a common thermal ablation technique for treating small RCCs in nonsurgical candidates [7, 10-14]. Although some studies have demonstrated clinical outcomes after RFA similar to those after surgery, studies on long-term clinical outcomes after treatment of small RCC via RFA are limited [7, 10-16].

Therefore, this study evaluated the long-term clinical outcomes of RFA in patients with T1a RCC.

Material and Methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study was approved by our Institutional Review Board, and informed consent to treat RCC and use patients’ data for the study was obtained from all patients. Data were obtained from a database in which patients who underwent RFA were prospectively registered.

Patients and tumor characteristics

The indication for RFA for T1a RCC was determined by the cancer board, consisting of urologists and interventional and diagnostic radiologists. Between May 2002 and December 2014, local treatment for RCC was performed in 526 patients at our institution. Of these patients, 327 were surgically treated, and RFA was chosen as an alternative RCC treatment for 199 patients. Of the 199 patients treated with RFA, 142 (71.4%) had tumors measuring ≤4.0 cm without metastasis. Of these 142 patients, 36 (25.4%) were excluded because of the absence of a pathological diagnosis, 24 (16.8%) because of short follow-up periods of <1 year due to follow-up at local hospitals, 3 (2.1%) because of data unavailability, and 19 (13.3%) because they had other cancers. Ultimately, this study enrolled 60 patients (Figure 1) (34 males and 26 females) with a median age of 67.0 years (interquartile range [IQR], 56.0-75.8; range, 28-84 years). Two (3.3%) patients had solitary kidneys. There were three (5.0%) patients with a history of cerebellar hemangioblastoma, and they were diagnosed as von Hippel-Lindau disease (VHL).

Flow diagram of the inclusion process of patients who underwent radiofrequency ablation for T1a renal cell carcinomas.

Forty-six (76.7%) patients were ineligible for surgery due to comorbidities or advanced age (≥80 years). The remaining 14 (23.3%) patients declined surgical intervention. One (1.7%) patient was on dialysis at the time of RFA.

The pathological diagnosis of RCC was clear cell carcinoma in 54 (90.0%) patients, chromophobe cell carcinoma in three (4.9%) patients, and papillary renal cell carcinoma in three (5.0%) patients. T1a RCC was single in 58 (96.7%) patients and double in two (3.3%) patients. Therefore, 62 pathologically diagnosed T1a RCCs were treated with RFA. The maximum tumor diameter ranged from 1.0 to 3.9 cm (median, 2.3 cm; IQR, 1.7-3.1 cm). Tumor location was central in 22 (35.5%) tumors [17]. The RENAL (radius, exophytic/endophytic, nearness to collecting system, anterior/posterior, and location) score ranged from 4 to 10 (median, 7.0; IQR, 5-8). Tumor complexity was classified as low (RENAL score; 4-6) in 29 (46.8%) tumors, moderate (RENAL score; 7-9) in 30 (48.4%) tumors, and high (RENAL score; 10-12) in three (4.8%) tumors [18]. A history of comorbidities was defined as the continued use of medications for these conditions (Table 1).

Pretreatment work-up

In all patients, blood tests, routine physical examination, and chest-to-pelvis CT and/or renal MRI were performed [19, 20], with contrast-enhanced imaging carried out in 55 patients (91.7%) and omitted in five (8.2%) due to impaired renal function (n = 4, 6.7%) or asthma (n = 1, 1.7%).

RFA

All RFA procedures were performed by three board-certified interventional radiologists on an inpatient basis. TAE prior to RFA was performed in five patients (8.3%). While TAE was indicated for RCCs larger than 3 cm in diameter, it was also applied to lesions ≤3 cm during 2002 and 2003. In addition, TAE was omitted in 11 patients with impaired renal function and one patient with asthma. All RCCs were ablated under real-time CT fluoroscopy guidance (X-Vigor, Asterion, or Aquilion, Canon Medical Systems Corporation, Otawara, Japan) with conscious sedation. For local anesthesia, lidocaine (Xylocaine; Astellas Pharma Inc., Tokyo, Japan) was used. For analgesia, fentanyl (Fentanest; Janssen-Kyowa, Tokyo, Japan) was administered during RFA at a dose of 0.1-0.3 mg. RFA was administered using 17-gauge internally cooled electrodes (Cool-Tip RF Ablation System; Radionics, Burlington, MA, or Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) with an impedance control algorithm for 10-12 min at each tumor site. The procedural endpoint was complete coverage of RCC with microbubbles on CT fluoroscopy images during RFA. The electrodes were placed with reference to contrast-enhanced CT/MRI (n = 55), or to non-contrast CT/MRI in patients who did not undergo contrast studies (n = 5). A multiple-electrode switching system (Cool-Tip RF Switching controller, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN), introduced in November 2009, was used mainly for RCCs measuring ≥2 cm and was applied in 38 (61.3%) tumors. For these tumors, two or three electrodes were placed sequentially, and an impedance switching algorithm was used to apply the RF energy [21].

To avoid collateral thermal injury to adjacent critical organs, hydrodissection was performed before RFA in 26 (41.9%) tumors; 5% dextrose in water containing 2% iodinated contrast-enhanced medium was injected into the perirenal space using an 18-gauge needle. Pyeloperfusion was performed by injecting water via a ureter stent for one (1.6%) tumor that was adjacent to the ureter.

Follow-up

The treated patients were followed up by four board-certified interventional radiologists. Blood tests, including renal function, and imaging evaluation with three-phase contrast-enhanced CT or MRI were performed within 1 week after RFA. Complete eradication of tumor enhancement after RFA was defined as the technique’s efficacy [20]. A contrast-enhanced, unablated lesion at the ablative margin was defined as a residual tumor [21]. Two (3.3%) patients with poor renal function and one (1.7%) patient with asthma underwent CT or MRI without contrast enhancement. Tumors not covered by the ablative zone on CT or MRI were considered residual tumors in such patients [22] After RFA, patients were followed up with abdominal CT or MRI evaluations, routine physical examinations, and blood tests at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months, and then every 6 months from 1 to 5 years and every 6 months or annually thereafter. Developed contrast-enhanced tumor foci at the edge of the ablated zone on follow-up CT or MR images, or the appearance of hyperintense soft tissue on T2-weighted MR images after complete tumor ablation was defined as local tumor progression [22]. Distant tumor recurrence was defined as the emergence of a new RCC tumor in the untreated renal parenchyma or extrarenal metastasis.

Follow-up continued until the patient’s death or their last clinical visit, whichever came first, through December 2024.

Renal function

Renal function 1 year after RFA was compared with pre-RFA values using estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [23]. For comparisons at 1, 3, 5, and 10 years after RFA, serum creatinine levels were used instead of eGFR to avoid the effects of age-related changes in eGFR.

Assessment and statistical analyses

Categorical and continuous variables are represented as frequencies with percentages and medians with IQR, respectively. The primary and secondary technique efficacy rates were defined as the percentages of tumors that were completely ablated after the initial RFA sessions and after one or more additional RFA sessions, respectively. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to assess local tumor control and survival rates. Factors affecting overall survival were analyzed using univariate Cox proportional hazards regression models. Because of the limited number of events, multivariate analysis was not performed. Hazard ratios were not estimated with the Cox proportional hazards model for factors in which no events occurred during the follow-up period. Local tumor control was evaluated on a tumor basis, and survival rates were evaluated on a patient basis. To assess renal function decline, statistical analyses were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test on non-hemodialysis patients with renal function data on record. Procedure-related complications were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5 [24] and evaluated on a session basis. Grade 3-5 adverse events were defined as major adverse events, whereas all other complications were defined as minor ones (Grades 1 and 2). IBM SPSS Statistics (ver. 30.0; IBM Co., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for all statistical analyses. p-values < 0.05 were used to denote statistical significance.

Results

Initial therapeutic results and tumor progression

A total of 69 RFA sessions were performed for 62 pathologically diagnosed T1a RCCs. Complete ablation was achieved in 53 RCCs in 51 patients with a single RFA session (Figure 2). Two patients, each with two RCCs, underwent a single RFA session in which both RCCs were treated simultaneously. The primary technique efficacy rate was 85.5% (53/62 RCCs). Of the seven RCCs with residual tumor after initial RFA, five underwent the planned two-session protocol. The remaining two were ≥35 mm and were ablated without TAE due to impaired renal function or asthma. Complete ablation was achieved in all seven after the second session (secondary technique efficacy 100%).

Flowchart of clinical course of included patients with pathologically proven T1a renal cell carcinomas.

Local tumor progression occurred in four (6.5%) tumors during a median follow-up period of 9.0 years (IQR, 4.6-12.0 years; range, 1.3-16.5 years), two occurred in patients with impaired renal function, and one in a patients with VHL-associated RCC who had ≤2-mm margins, and one in a tumor adjacent to the renal pelvis with a minimal pelvic-side margin. The interval from RFA to local progression ranged from 1.0 to 7.6 years (median, 4.6 years; IQR, 1.7-7.0 years). The local tumor control rates were 98.4% (95% CI, 95.3%-100%) at 1 year, 96.3% (95% CI, 91.2%-100%) at 5 years, and 91.6% (95% CI, 83.6%-99.6%) at 10 years after RFA (Figure 3). Of the four locally progressed tumors in four (6.7%) patients, three were treated with RFA or cryoablation and subsequently exhibited no local progression (Figure 2). Therefore, the secondary tumor control rate was 97.9% (95% CI, 93.8%-100%) at 5 and 10 years. The remaining patient developed a locally progressed renal tumor with renal vein invasion and simultaneous distant metastases to the lungs and pancreas 3.9 years after RFA. The patient underwent systemic chemotherapy and died of RCC 7.7 years post-RFA. There was another patient in whom distant metastases to the lungs and bone emerged 6.2 years after RFA; the patient was followed for an additional 2.6 years and was confirmed to be alive with metastatic disease at the time of the final follow-up conducted 8.8 years after renal RFA. New RCCs emerged in three (5.0%) patients with VHL in the untreated renal parenchyma and were treated with cryoablation.

Kaplan-Meier curve of the local tumor control rates of 62 pathologically proven T1a renal cell carcinomas in 60 patients. The primary local tumor control rates at 1, 5, and 10 years after RFA were 98.4% (95% CI, 95.3%-100%), 96.3% (95% CI, 91.2%-100%), and 91.6% (95% CI, 83.6%-99.6%), respectively. The secondary local tumor control rates at 1, 5, and 10 years after RFA were 100%, 97.8% (95% CI, 93.8%-100%), and 97.8% (95% CI, 93.8%-100%), respectively.

The univariate analysis revealed no factors significantly associated with local tumor progression.

Renal function and adverse events

At the time of RFA, there were three (5.0%) patients in CKD stage 4 and three (5.0%) in stage 5. Hemodialysis was initiated in four (6.7%) patients 0.1-2.6 years (median, 1.7 years; IQR, 0.5-2.4 years) after RFA. These four patients were classified as CKD stage 4 and 5 (two patients each), and their median eGFR was 18.7 mL/min/1.73 m2 (range, 4.3-26.6 mL/min/1.73 m2; IQR, 6.1-26.4 mL/min/1.73 m2) at pre-RFA. Among 45 non-hemodialysis patients with available eGFR data at both pre- and 1 year after RFA, median eGFR declined 1 year after RFA (pre-RFA: 65.7 mL/min/1.73 m2, range: 11.4-111.2 mL/min/1.73 m2, IQR; 48.0-85.5 mL/min/1.73 m2; 1 year after RFA: 58.6 mL/min/1.73 m2, range; 7.1-102.6 mL/min/1.73 m2, IQR; 46.8-75.0 mL/min/1.73 m2; p < 0.001). CKD stage progression occurred in 11 patients (24.4%, 11/45), but no patients in CKD stage 1-3 progressed to CKD 4 or 5 1 year after RFA. Serum creatinine levels at 1, 3, 5, and 10 years after RFA were compared with pre-RFA values. While serum creatinine levels increased significantly at 1 and 3 years after RFA, no statistically significant differences were observed at 5 or 10 years compared with pre-RFA values (Table 4).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that RFA was feasible, had strong anticancer effects, and contributed to the long-term prognosis of patients with pathologically proven T1a RCC. A key strength of this study is its long median follow-up period of 9.0 years. Previous studies reporting 7- or 9-year overall survival after RFA had median follow-up periods of less than 4 years [25, 26], and even other large-scale studies with over 100 patients reported follow-up periods of up to 7.5 years [16, 27]. Kim et al. [28] recently reported outcomes with a median follow-up period of 10.9 years; however, more than one quarter of their cohort underwent a laparoscopic approach, therefore, long-term outcomes of percutaneous RFA are still limited. A longer median follow-up enables the detection of events such as recurrence and death that may not be captured in shorter-term studies. This facilitates a more accurate evaluation of the durability of treatment efficacy and the identification of late-onset complications such as ureteral stricture, urinoma formation, and tract seeding, thereby reducing the risk of overestimating treatment effects.

The local tumor control rate at 5 years (96.3%) in this study is comparable to previously reported rates of 92%-96% and to the control rate of 95%-100% after partial nephrectomy for small RCCs [7, 8, 12, 16]. In this study, three of the four locally progressed tumors were retreated with RFA or cryoablation. The feasibility of retreatment is an advantage of ablation therapy [27, 29]. The secondary local tumor control rate improved to as high as 97.9% at 10 years.

The 5-year overall survival after RFA for T1a RCC has been reported to be 72%-97.2% [8, 12, 16, 27]. The slightly higher rate of our results (98.3%) may reflect differences in baseline comorbidities across study cohorts. Partial nephrectomy provides a 10-year overall survival rate ranging from 72.3%-87.6% in patients with T1a RCC [9, 30]. Our 10-year overall survival rate following RFA was 85.7%, which is comparable to these outcomes. Although data on 10-year overall survival after RFA remain limited, existing studies report widely variable results. Chan et al. [8] described a 10-year overall survival rate of 89.0% in T1a RCC patients. The exclusion of patients with multifocal disease or a solitary kidney―subgroups included in our analysis―may have contributed to the more favorable 10-year overall survival observed in their cohort compared to ours (85.7%) [8]. On the other hand, Abdelsalam et al. [31] reported a lower 10-year overall survival rate of 40.7%, potentially influenced by the presence of another primary malignancy in more than half (52.6%) of the study participants. These findings highlight the variability in long-term overall survival outcomes across different patient populations and underscore the need for more consistent long-term evidence and the importance of patient selection criteria in interpreting survival data.

Advanced age and a higher proportion of patients with comorbidities were considered to lower the overall survival rates of patients who undergo RFA relative to those undergoing partial nephrectomy [32]. CKD and diabetes mellitus were reported to affect all-cause and cancer-specific mortality rates in patients with RCC [33, 34]. In this study, CKD and diabetes mellitus were identified as the significant adverse prognostic factors following RFA for T1a RCC. In a large-scale, population-based retrospective cohort study, Lindblad et al. [35] reported that both morbidity and mortality from RCC were higher in patients with diabetes mellitus compared to the general population. Similarly, Labochka et al. [34], in their review article, described that diabetes mellitus negatively impacts the prognosis of patients with RCC. In terms of cancer-specific survival rates, two comparative studies have shown similar cancer-specific survival rates between ablation therapy and partial nephrectomy: one at 7 years (100% vs. 98.8%) [25] and the other at 9 years (96.3% vs. 96.4%) [26]. However, a recent comparative study showed a higher 10-year cancer-specific mortality rate after ablation therapy (8.7%) than after partial nephrectomy (5.5%) [30]. In this study, only one patient (1.7%) died of RCC, and the 5- and 10-year cancer-specific survival rates were 100% and 97.4%, respectively, which are comparable to those reported in previous studies.

Distant metastases in organs other than the kidney were observed in two (3.3%) patients, and new RCCs appeared at sites distant from the original tumors in three (5.0%) patients with VHL disease in this study. Pierorazio et al. [36] prospectively assessed clinical outcomes in patients with small renal masses, and distant metastases were observed in two (0.7%, 2/274) patients, and new RCCs developed in the kidney in five patients (1.8%, 5/274) during a median follow-up period of 2.1 years. Psutka et al. [27] retrospectively assessed the oncological outcomes of patients who underwent RFA for T1a RCC, and distant metastases and new RCCs developed in one (0.7%, 1/143) and four (2.8%, 4/143) patients, respectively, after a median follow-up period of 6.4 years. Although the incidence of distant metastases in the present study was slightly higher than previously reported, the fact that metastases were observed 3.9 and 6.2 years after RFA suggests that the relatively long median follow-up period of this study may have contributed to their detection. Furthermore, considering that local tumor progression occurred 1.0-7.6 years after RFA, long-term follow-up after renal RFA appears essential for accurately assessing patient prognosis.

In this study, CKD stage migration from 1-3 to 4-5 was not observed, but renal function decreased significantly 1- and 3-years after RFA, with a subset of patients with impaired renal function progressing to hemodialysis. Although RFA has been reported to lower the risk of renal function decline compared to surgical treatment [7-9], significant declines in eGFR after RFA have also been reported [8, 9]. These findings underscore the importance of careful renal function monitoring in CKD patients undergoing renal RFA.

In this study, no major complications were observed (0%), which is comparable to reported rates of 0%-2% [7, 8, 37, 38], and all minor complications were self-limiting. RFA has a low complication rate and can be a therapeutic alternative for elderly patients with several comorbidities. Pierorazio et al. [36] reported that 21 of 223 patients (9%) under active surveillance crossed over to delayed interventions because of rapid tumor growth (growth rate: 0.5 cm/year). Since most patients under active surveillance are elderly and have comorbidities, RFA may be considered a suitable option for such patients when the crossover treatment is required.

Although long-term follow-up is a strength of this study, the long study period inevitably introduced temporal heterogeneity in diagnostic and therapeutic practice. Advances in imaging technology during this period may have improved the detection of subtle residual or recurrent diseases. Furthermore, systemic therapies for recurrent RCC, including molecular targeted agents and, more recently, immune checkpoint inhibitors, became available during the follow-up period and may have influenced post-recurrence survival. These temporal changes may have introduced potential biases that should be considered when interpreting the long-term outcomes. This study has several limitations. First, the single-center, single-arm retrospective design may introduce selection bias due to facility-specific patient characteristics and treatment policies. The retrospective design also raises the possibility of information bias from incomplete or inconsistent medical records. The lack of control groups, such as active surveillance, surgical treatment, and radiation treatment, makes it difficult to establish causal relationships between RFA and observed outcomes, and limits conclusions regarding comparative effectiveness. Second, the small sample size may reduce statistical power and reduce the ability to control confounding variables. The number of patients who experienced local tumor progression or died in this study might be too small to statistically identify factors affecting the local control rate or survival. To overcome these limitations, future research should, where feasible, employ large-scale, multicenter, prospective comparative study designs.

In conclusion, RFA is a safe treatment alternative for T1a RCC with favorable local tumor control and can be a promising treatment option, with a positive outlook, owing to its excellent long-term renal cancer-specific survival.
