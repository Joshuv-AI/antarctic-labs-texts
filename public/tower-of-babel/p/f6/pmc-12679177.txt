Multi‐pathway‐driven hepatic protection: Semaglutide combined with HIIT counteracts diabetic liver injury in db/db mice
Authors: Wenjun Yu, Yongfu Liu, Shenglong Le, Yuxin Xiao, Xiaoyan Chen, Junhua Wang, Feng Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveInvestigate the impact and mechanism of semaglutide (Sema) combined with high‐intensity interval training (HIIT) on hepatic glucose/lipid metabolism in db/db mice using untargeted metabolomics.MethodsDb/db mice were divided into model control, Sema alone, and Sema + HIIT groups, with db/m mice as controls. Sema (0.1 mg/kg/week) was administered subcutaneously, and the control group was subcutaneously injected with the same volume of distilled water. Engaging in HIIT swimming occurred for five consecutive days weekly. Weekly fasting plasma glucose (FPG), body mass, and food intake were assessed. After 8 weeks, blood/liver tissues were analyzed; liver metabolomics used LC–MS.ResultsFollowing an 8‐week intervention, it was determined that the weight loss efficacy of Sema alone was suboptimal. The lowering of FPG did not differ significantly between the Sema alone and the combined intervention groups. The combined treatment demonstrated superior efficacy compared with Sema alone in enhancing liver weight, liver index, food intake, TG, TC, hepatic TG, and liver injury biomarkers (AST, ALT; P < 0.05). Histological findings indicated that the combination intervention markedly decreased fat vacuoles and inflammatory infiltration in hepatocytes (P < 0.001). A total of 721 differential metabolites were identified after the combined intervention, with primary alterations in amino acids and their derivatives, lipids, energy metabolism, and bile acids, encompassing key pathways such as the TCA cycle, amino acid metabolism, and bile acid metabolism.ConclusionsSemaglutide combined with HIIT synergistically improves hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism in diabetic mice, providing a new treatment strategy for liver lipid disorders.

INTRODUCTION

The global public health crisis of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is closely connected with the obesity epidemic. Its pathological basis includes insulin resistance (IR), β‐cell dysfunction, and abnormalities in hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism 1 . The liver, as the primary organ for glucose and lipid metabolism, demonstrates hypergluconeogenesis, increased de novo lipogenesis, and diminished mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation during the progression of T2DM. This ultimately results in a detrimental loop of intrahepatic lipid accumulation and non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease (MASLD) 2 , 3 . Semaglutide (Sema), a glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonist (GLP‐1RA), addresses these pathogenic pathways by stimulating central appetite suppression, augmenting insulin production, and directly modulating essential enzymes implicated in hepatic glucose and lipid homeostasis. The SUSTAIN and STEP clinical studies have exhibited remarkable hypoglycemia, weight reduction, and cardiovascular protective effects 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 . Alongside the administration of hypoglycemic and lipid‐modulating medications, exercise therapy is an essential component of T2DM management. The high‐intensity interval training (HIIT) modality has garnered significant interest owing to its brevity and efficacy. Exercise can improve glucose uptake by activating mechanisms such as Irisin/AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) in skeletal muscle, stimulate fatty acid β‐oxidation in the liver, and mitigate lipotoxicity through muscle‐derived substances 8 .

Sema and liraglutide are both GLP‐1RAs. In patients with obesity and T2DM, research indicates that the concurrent application of GLP‐1RA and exercise can synergistically enhance β‐cell secretion function, markedly diminish the severity of metabolic syndrome, reduce abdominal fat percentage and inflammatory markers, and more effectively regulate blood glucose and body weight 9 , 10 , 11 . The overall effectiveness of this combined intervention surpasses that of an individual intervention. Nonetheless, the mechanism driving the synergistic effect of HIIT and Sema on hepatic metabolism remains an unexamined study domain. The necessity for further verification exists to determine if the metabolic advantages of this combination strategy stem from the cross‐regulation of several pathways. This study utilized db/db mice as a diabetic model to examine the dynamic modulation of the hepatic glucose and lipid metabolic network under the combined treatment with Sema and HIIT. The integration of non‐targeted metabolomics facilitated a comprehensive analysis of the alterations in metabolic profiles and related pathways within liver tissues. The objective was to elucidate the metabolic process underlying the synergistic intervention of drug and exercise, thereby offering theoretical justification for cross‐dimensional intervention strategies in the personalized treatment of T2DM.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animal model

The study comprised 24 male mice with an average body weight of 38.14 g and 8 db/m 6‐week‐old males from the same litter with an average body weight of 20.9 g. The mice were acquired from Jiangsu Huachuang Xinnuo Pharmaceutical Science and Technology Co. Ltd. (License No. SCXK (SU) 2020‐009). The animals were male BKS.Cg‐Dock7m+/+ Leprdb/JNju mice, aged 6 weeks, that spontaneously develop T2DM. The animals were bred in the animal facility at the Laboratory Animal Center of Hubei University of Medicine (SYXK (E) 2021‐0031). They were maintained under constant temperature and humidity conditions with a 12‐h light–dark cycle. Sterilized standard rodent diet (XieTong Bio‐Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China; containing 18.5% protein, 4.5% fat, and 4.2% fiber) and autoclaved water were provided ad libitum. Fasting blood glucose (FPG) was assessed in the tails of 24 db/db mice among the experimental animals. Animals with FPG levels >11.1 mmol/L were assigned to the diabetic group (Roche blood glucose meter) 12 . The db/m from the identical litter consisted of 8 in the normal control group (NC), 8 in the model control group (MC), 8 in the model + Sema group (MS), and 8 in the model + Sema + HIIT group (MSH). After the adaptive training, Sema (Novo Nordisk, China) subcutaneous injection was started, and the dose selected was 0.1 mg/kg/week for 8 weeks according to the mouse study by Manuel Soto‐Catalan et al. (24 μg/kg/week for the first 2 weeks and 100 μg/kg/week for the second 2 weeks). 13 The control group was subcutaneously injected with the same volume of distilled water. Upon completion of the intervention, the mice in each group had an 8‐h fasting period. The animals were subsequently sedated with an intraperitoneal injection of 10% sodium pentobarbital. Blood samples and liver tissues were obtained and placed into cryopreservation tubes. The samples were subsequently frozen in liquid nitrogen and preserved in a −80°C freezer.

The intervention of HIIT

After 2 weeks of adaptive feeding, adaptive training was started for 1 week. Swimming was used as the main form of exercise in this study, and the swimming protocol was adapted from the swimming method of Marcel Pimenta et al. 14 . HIIT, where adaptive training was first performed by swimming for 15 min without weight bearing for five consecutive days. Maximal exercise capacity testing was performed on day 6, with 20 s forced swimming and 10 s passive recovery, while the mice were tails bearing 5% of their body weight of lead skin until 6 s was unable to get out of the water. The 20 s forced swimming and the 10 s passive recovery were divided into one group, the maximum number of groups was calculated, and the subsequent HIIT plan was formulated. HIIT intensity in this study was mainly reflected by increasing the number of swimming groups and tail weight bearing. In the MSH group, the exercise intensity was 50% of the maximum number of groups tested in the first week, remained unchanged in the second week, and in the third week, the exercise intensity was increased by two groups in the first week, and so on. Tail loading began at week 1 with a 5% load of lead skin and increased by 1% every 2 weeks while weight adjusted lead skin weight was measured weekly. Swimming workouts were conducted daily at 7:00 p.m. for 5 days each week over a duration of 8 weeks. The water temperature was sustained at 30°C ± 1, and the water depth was 20 cm.

Measurement of body weight, FPG, food intake, liver weight, and liver index

After the weekly exercise session, the mice were given a one‐day rest period. Subsequently, the mice were transferred to new cages. They were then subjected to fasting while having free access to water. After an 8‐h fasting period, blood glucose measurements were taken. A blade was used to form a wound at the end of the tail; the first drop of blood was wiped off, and the measurements were kept quiet to avoid struggling, agitated mice. After FPG was measured, body weight and weekly food intake were measured. At the end of the 8th week of exercise intervention, the mice were tested for fasting 8‐h blood glucose after a day of rest, and then, the samples were taken, and the liver was weighed and recorded. The liver index was calculated as liver weight (g) divided by body weight (g), multiplied by 100%.

Biochemical analysis

Included were triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST). Blood was obtained in a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube and refrigerated for 4 h; thereafter, it was subjected to centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C to isolate the supernatant. Upon configuring the requisite parameters on the automated biochemical instrument, the samples were thereafter loaded and analyzed automatically (Serum detection kit and automated biochemical analyzer: Shenzhen Leidu).

Determination of liver lipids

Including TG and TC, after the mice were sacrificed and the livers were removed and weighed, 100 mg of liver tissue was weighed and ground in a centrifuge tube at 60 Hz for 120 s. The sample tubes were placed in the −80°C refrigerator and frozen for 10 min. After thawing on the ice box at 4°C, the tubes were vortexed to shake evenly and kept frozen. This was repeated five times. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. Protein quantification was performed using the BCA method. The standard curve (0–250 μg/mL) was prepared using Wuhan Borfu BCA kit (item number: BF0026) according to the instructions. After appropriate dilution, 20 μL of the sample was mixed with 200 μL of BCA working solution, and the reaction was carried out at 37°C for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 500 nm, and the protein concentration was calculated according to the standard curve.

HE staining

The specimens were fixed, dehydrated, and immersed in 75% alcohol for 4 h, 85% alcohol for 2 h, 90% alcohol for 2 h, 95% alcohol for 1 h, followed by absolute ethanol, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, dewaxed to water, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and sealed through dehydration.

Oil red O staining

The frozen pieces were thawed, dried, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min before being allowed to dry. Immerse in oil red working solution for 8–10 min (dark); distinguish with 75% alcohol; counterstain nuclei with hematoxylin. The images were converted to 8‐bit grayscale images using ImageJ software, and the same scale was set. The positive areas were segmented by software algorithm and analyzed quantitatively.

Extraction of metabolites

A 25 mg sample was weighed in an EP tube at low temperature, to which two homogenate beads were added, followed by the addition of 500 μL of extraction solution (methanol:acetonitrile:water = 2:2:1 (V/V)) containing an isotope‐labeled internal standard. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s and then homogenized in a homogenizer at 35 Hz for 4 min. Subsequently, it was transferred to an ice water bath and sonicated for 5 min, with this step repeated three times. The cells were then placed at −40°C for 1 h. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm (centrifugal force 13,800 (×g), radius 8.6 cm) for 15 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected into an injection bottle for analysis, and an equivalent volume of supernatant was incorporated into Quality Control (QC) samples.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis employed one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or T‐test for normally distributed data, and Kruskal–Wallis H or Mann–Whitney U test for non‐normally distributed data. The trend of separation across groups was assessed using principal component analysis (PCA), whereas differentiated metabolites (VIP > 1.0 and P < 0.05) were identified by partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS‐DA). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was employed to ascertain the principal metabolic pathways of different metabolites. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.0, with a significance threshold established at P < 0.05.

The NC group displayed a normal structure devoid of discernible pathological changes after HE staining. Conversely, the MC group exhibited disorganized hepatic lobule architecture, abundant lipid vacuoles, and ballooning degeneration. In comparison with the MC group, the degree of cellular fatty infiltration was reduced in the MS group after administration, but the edema of liver cells and lipid deposition were still more obvious. Significantly, in the MSH group, liver lesions were substantially improved with the combination of Sema and HIIT treatment (Figure 4a).

Metabolomics analysis

Correlation of samples

The Pearson correlation coefficient of QC samples was 0.923, which was close to the theoretical maximum value of 1 (threshold value >0.85), indicating that the experimental system was stable and the data quality was reliable (Figure S1). The high correlation further validates that the instrument state is stable and the sample processing is consistent, which meet the quality standards for metabolomics data.

PCA

Samples within each group (NC, MC, MS, MSH) exhibited tight clustering, suggesting homogeneity in metabolite composition and concentration across biological replicates. By contrast, pronounced intergroup separation was observed in PCA, demonstrating distinct metabolic profiles among experimental cohorts. The method had good stability and high data quality. As shown in the PCA plot, significant alterations in hepatic small‐molecule metabolites were observed in the MC group compared with the NC group, indicating metabolic dysregulation during diabetes progression. Furthermore, distinct separation between the MC group and both the MS group and MSH group suggests that either pharmacological intervention alone or combined therapeutic strategies could ameliorate the diabetes‐induced metabolic disturbances observed in the MC group. OPLS‐DA

OPLS‐DA was used to find the true differences between groups and screen effective differential metabolites (Figure 6b). In the figure, the NC group, MC group, MS group, and MSH group can be clearly distinguished, and the corresponding permutation test figure is shown in Figure 6c. By randomly changing the permutation order of the categorical variable Y, the corresponding OPLS‐DA model was established several times to obtain the R 2 and Q 2 values of the random model. Screening of differential metabolites

Differential metabolites between MC and each group

Compounds exhibiting both VIP > 1 and P < 0.05 were identified through pairwise comparison between the MC group and other groups. A total of 572 significant differential metabolites were identified in the MC group compared with the NC group (231 up‐regulated and 341 down‐regulated), and 743 significant differential metabolites were identified in the MC group compared with the MS group (244 up‐regulated and 341 down‐regulated). A total of 721 significant differential metabolites were found in the MC versus MSH group, comprising 118 up‐regulated and 603 down‐regulated metabolites. The differential metabolites were cross‐analyzed

In order to further analyze the differences between combined treatment and drug treatment alone, the overlapping differential metabolites in each group were screened, and the intersection of MC group vs NC group, MC group vs MS group, and MC group vs MSH group was performed by Venn diagram, and 166 common differential metabolites were found (Figure 8). The results showed that the differential metabolites mainly pointed to lipid metabolism disorders, and changes in amino acid metabolism and steroid hormone metabolism were also observed. According to the fold change (FC) between groups <0.67 or >1.50, P < 0.05, and VIP > 1.0, the differential metabolites were screened, and the differential metabolites in different groups were further analyzed (Table 3). Compared with NC group, the significantly different metabolites in MC group were found to be mainly related to amino acids and their derivatives, purine metabolism, lipid metabolism, energy metabolism, and bile acid metabolism (Table 3). Among the above differential metabolites, Serine‐Tyrosine (Ser‐Tyr), Xanthine, Taurocholic acid, Methionyl‐Glycine (Met‐Gly), Alanyl‐Glutamine (Ala‐Gln), Phenylalanine (Phe), and Eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) were significantly up‐regulated in the MC group, while the differential metabolites that caused metabolic disorders were significantly down‐regulated in the MS and MSH groups after intervention. Cis‐aconitic acid, N‐acetyl‐arginine, lysophosphatidylcholine (LysoPC), and phosphatidylcholine (42:7) (PC (42:7)) were significantly down‐regulated in the development of T2DM, which were reversed to varying degrees by MSH treatment. Although the MS group up‐regulated most of the differential metabolites significantly down‐regulated in the MC group mentioned above, the regulation of N‐Acetyl‐arginine was not obvious. Moreover, the reversal degree of the overall differential metabolites in the MSH group was stronger than that in the MS group. Sema combined with HIIT is more effective in reversing metabolic abnormalities in diabetic mice than drug alone.

KEGG enrichment analysis of differential metabolites and metabolic pathways

KEGG enrichment maps of differential metabolites between groups are shown in Figure 9a–c. MC and NC were mainly concentrated in ABC transporters, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and central carbon metabolism in cancer. MC and MS mainly focused on ABC transporters, biosynthesis of amino acids, and protein digestion and absorption. MC and MSH mainly focused on ABC transporters, biosynthesis of cofactors, and biosynthesis of amino acids. ABC transporters play a central role in the enrichment analysis of differential metabolites between groups. The ABC transporter facilitates ATP‐dependent transmembrane transfer of molecules. This encompasses lipids (cholesterol, phospholipids), amino acids, carbohydrates, etc., therefore expediting lipid turnover and glucose metabolism, which aids in sustaining intracellular lipid equilibrium and averting lipid accumulation within the cell.

DISCUSSION

Hepatic steatosis is closely associated with T2DM and dyslipidemia and is one of the major complications in patients with T2DM 15 , 16 . The coexistence of T2DM and hepatic steatosis can significantly accelerate systemic IR 17 . In this study, db/db mice were used as a diabetic model, and their continuous weight gain and abnormal levels of serum ALT, AST, TC, TG, LDL‐C, and HDL‐C not only confirmed the typical phenotype of impaired liver lipid metabolism and dysfunction but also suggested that this model could effectively simulate the clinicopathological features of T2DM combined with hepatic steatosis 18 . Although the efficacy of Sema as a GLP‐1RA in improving glycemic control and MASLD in T2DM patients has been confirmed by SUSTAIN and other clinical trials 6 , 19 , 20 , 21 , drug therapy alone may not be as effective as exercise combined with drug therapy in improving body weight control and lipid metabolism disorders, which is consistent with previous studies 9 , 10 , 11 . In comparison with the medication group, the combined intervention group exhibited synergistic benefits in body weight management, food consumption, serum triglycerides, serum total cholesterol, liver function, and lipid metabolism; however, there was no significant difference in the regulation of fasting plasma glucose.

The dynamic alterations in liver metabolites were examined utilizing a non‐targeted metabolomics approach to elucidate the underlying mechanism. Multidimensional statistical analysis revealed that Sema, both independently and in conjunction with HIIT, dramatically altered the liver metabolite profile in db/db mice, with the combined HIIT group exhibiting distinct metabolic characteristics. Significant alterations in taurocholic acid, EPA, and phospholipids were consistently observed among the differential metabolites between db/db and db/m mice, aligning with the findings of Junyu Zou's team in the MASLD model, indicating that these metabolites may serve as biomarkers in the remission process of liver lipotoxicity 18 .

Chronic hyperglycemia induces mitochondrial metabolic dysregulation in pancreatic β cells, leading to the activation of mTORC1 and the down‐regulation of the AMPK signaling pathway, which therefore diminishes the production of TCA cycle intermediates and progressively impairs insulin synthesis and secretion 22 . The decrease in the production of TCA cycle intermediates may hinder the transamination reaction of phenylalanine, resulting in its aberrant buildup in the liver 23 , 24 . This study identified a significant correlation between mitochondrial membrane potential damage in the livers of T2DM mice and a reduction in TCA cycle flux, evidenced by decreased levels of intermediate metabolites such as cis‐aconitic acid, indicating that the disruption of mitochondrial energy metabolism and hepatic gluconeogenesis created a detrimental cycle 23 . The research findings indicate that following the combined intervention, the expression of cis‐aconitic acid is restored, and the hepatic levels of Phe are markedly diminished. The rationale for this may be that Sema, in conjunction with HIIT, counteracts the aforementioned pathogenic process via a targeted complimentary mechanism. Sema can stimulate the GLP‐1R‐AMPK axis and expedite the transformation of pyruvate to acetyl‐CoA to restore the TCA cycle 25 , 26 , 27 . HIIT enhances mitochondrial biogenesis and the efficiency of the electron transport chain by stimulating PGC‐1α expression in skeletal muscle, thereby synergistically restoring hepatic glucose oxidation capability 28 . Conversely, additional research has demonstrated that Phe can activate calcium‐sensitive receptors present in endocrine cells of the small intestine, hence enhancing the release of GLP‐1 and insulin. Administering exogenous Phe can postpone the onset and progression of T2DM 29 . Consequently, additional investigation is required on the correlation between Phe and T2DM, as well as its underlying mechanisms.

Oxidative stress and the disruption of reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis induce dysfunction by impairing mitochondrial metabolic flexibility, exacerbating the accumulation of misfolded ROS proteins, and perpetuating a detrimental cycle. Concurrently, these factors propel the pathological progression of hepatocyte lipotoxicity and insulin resistance associated with diabetes by inhibiting proteasome activity and autophagy while coordinating endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 . Metabolomics investigation revealed that Sema, in conjunction with HIIT treatment, markedly rectified the aberrant buildup of amino acid derivatives, including Ser‐Tyr, Ala‐Gln, and Met‐Gly, in the livers of db/db mice. Ser‐Tyr is a crucial regulatory molecule for the phosphorylation modification of insulin receptor substrate (IRS), and the dysregulation of Ser and Tyr phosphorylation status in its breakdown products has been established as closely associated with compromised insulin signaling in the peripheral tissues of T2DM patients 35 . Simultaneously, the metabolic remodeling of Ala‐Gln sustains the energy supply of intestinal immune cells via D‐glutamine metabolism and augments α‐ketoglutarate (α‐KG) production through the TCA cycle to regulate nitrogen balance and increase REDOX equilibrium. The catabolism of Met‐Gly (Gly and α‐KG) may have a protective function via a twofold mechanism. Gly functions as a bile acid binder to facilitate lipid emulsification and breakdown. α‐KG enhances the TCA cycle to improve ATP production efficiency. The examination of the study's results indicates that both Sema alone and the combined treatment can break down and down‐regulate the improperly accumulated metabolites, with the combined treatment demonstrating superior efficacy. N‐Acetyl‐arginine, an acetylated derivative of arginine, exhibits metabolic dynamics intricately linked to oxidative stress induced by diabetes, which accelerates arginine metabolism and diminishes its bioavailability, hence establishing a detrimental loop of “arginine deficiency ‐ insulin resistance.” 36 Nevertheless, the results of the present study showed a reversion of Arg expression after combined treatment. In conclusion, it is proposed that exercise and Sema may mitigate oxidative stress and exhibit significant anti‐inflammatory and mitochondrial biogenesis regulating effects 37 , 38 . The observed benefits are mostly associated with the suppression of the pro‐inflammatory transcription factor NF‐κB, the activation of critical mitochondrial components such as uncoupling protein 1, and the stimulation of AMPK and SIRT1 27 , 39 . This study concluded that Sema coupled with HIIT significantly enhances liver metabolism, with the TCA cycle serving as the central nexus between glucose metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and energy metabolism. P. Schrauwen et al. indicated that the TCA cycle significantly contributes to T2DM, evidenced by a decrease in circulating flow 23 . This study demonstrated that the integrated intervention of pharmacological agents and physical exercise improved the TCA cycle, facilitated D‐glutamine and D‐glutamic acid metabolism, reinstated mitochondrial function, and regulated aberrant amino acid expression.

The EPA, a crucial component of ω‐3 polyunsaturated fatty acids, has been documented in studies to exhibit a substantial elevation in liver levels of EPA in db/db mice, leading to atypical lipid accumulation in the liver. This phenomenon aligns with the traits of lipid metabolism problems observed in T2DM models documented in prior research 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 . Furthermore, the insufficiency of phosphatidylcholine in the liver will diminish the secretion of very low‐density lipoprotein, thus resulting in the buildup of fat in the liver. Following the combination intervention in this investigation, the EPA level was down‐regulated, facilitating phospholipid production and diminishing lipid buildup in the liver. Additionally, the elevation of PC restored insulin sensitivity, while inflammation and oxidative stress were mitigated. In the presence of T2DM, intestinal flora dysbiosis enhances the conversion of primary bile acids to taurocholic acid, impedes fatty acid β‐oxidation, and stimulates lipid regeneration through the activation of the liver farnesoid X receptor (FXR) 44 . Current research indicates that Sema mitigates the overactivation of FXR by promoting advantageous bacterial populations such as Akkermansia muciniphila, while HIIT improves intestinal barrier integrity through mechanical stress and diminishes the enterohepatic circulation burden of taurocholic acid 45 , 46 . The results of both intervention groups in this study showed taurocholic acid down‐regulation. Therefore, it is speculated that the combined intervention of Sema and exercise may improve metabolic disorders by regulating intestinal flora and bile acid metabolism and alleviating the excessive activation of the FXR signaling pathway. However, the specific mechanisms still need to be further verified, which is the focus of follow‐up studies in this study.

This study, while yielding fresh results, also presents certain drawbacks. The diabetic model employed in this study was db/db mice, which may not accurately reflect the metabolic markers of diverse diabetic people. Secondly, while the study demonstrated that the TCA cycle can mitigate insulin resistance, restore mitochondrial function, and diminish inflammation through modifications in several metabolic pathways and their associated metabolites, we did not perform an association analysis of target proteins and genetics. In further research, we will perform transcriptome and target pathway analysis to confirm the association.

The combination of Semaglutide and HIIT significantly improves hepatic lipid metabolism and reduces liver injury in db/db mice, outperforming Semaglutide alone. This synergistic effect is likely mediated through modulation of key metabolic pathways involving amino acids, lipids, and bile acids, along with enhanced energy metabolism. The study provides novel insights into a combined therapeutic strategy for diabetes‐related hepatic steatosis and metabolic dysfunction.
