Microbiogeographic insights as keys to understanding personalized gut microbiota responses: the role of Bifidobacterium residing on intestinal starch granules
Authors: Yusuke NAGARA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere is growing optimism regarding the potential therapeutic and preventive benefits of regulating intestinal microbiota for various diseases. Diet is one of the most straightforward and safest methods for modulating the intestinal microbiota; however, considerable individual differences have been observed in the microbiota response to dietary interventions. These individual differences pose substantial challenges in application, which are primarily attributed to variations in the commensal flora and bacterial competition for nutrients. Our previous research indicated that the microscopic localization of bacteria provides valuable insights into the mechanisms by which specific intestinal bacterial species acquire nutrients within a competitive gut environment. Furthermore, our analysis revealed that the combination of bifidobacterial species and the nutrient source found in the localization analysis determined individual differences in microbiota response. These findings suggest that bacterial colonization facilitates the efficient, preferential, and presumably exclusive utilization of solid nutrient sources in the human gut. Moreover, the impact of a single nutrient source on the gut and human body may vary depending on the presence or absence of the primary species colonizing that source. In this review, we examined the micrometer-scale localization of intestinal bacteria and individual variability in microbiota responses to diet, drawing upon the results of our previous studies.Keywords: microbiogeography, competition, human gut microbiota, Bifidobacterium, resistant starch

INTRODUCTION

The response of the gut microbiota to various interventions exhibits inter-individual variability. Recent studies have highlighted the effects of gut bacteria on a wide range of human health parameters. Consequently, there is growing anticipation for the potential treatment and prevention of various diseases through modification and enhancement of the intestinal microbiota. Fecal microbiota transplantation, antibiotics, probiotics, and prebiotics are the primary methods used to alter the composition of the commensal microbiota. Antibiotics are effective for eradicating pathogenic bacteria, but they also disrupt the composition and function of the commensal microbiota. Similarly, fecal microbiota transplantation is associated with safety concerns. Consequently, the safest and most straightforward approach for improving the functionality of the gut microbiota currently appears to be the use of probiotics and prebiotics. However, one of the primary obstacles in applications involving microbiota modification and personalization is the highly individualized response of microbiota. For instance, the increase in bacterial populations in response to the intake of the same type of dietary fiber can vary significantly between individuals [1]. One study provided insight into the possible causes of this individual variation, showing that orally ingested human-derived bifidobacterial strains can persist in the intestine for extended periods only in the absence of endogenous strains competing for nutrients [2]. Another study indicated that whether a specific Bacteroides strain can establish itself at a high abundance in the gut following oral administration depends on the composition of the resident microbiota and the presence of the same species in the gut. However, simultaneous intake of carbohydrates that are specifically utilized by the ingested strain can promote a high level of colonization in the gut, regardless of the composition of the resident microbiota [3]. This suggests that individual differences in the response of intestinal microbiota are attributable, at least in part, to nutrient competition among bacteria and variations in the repertoire of commensal bacteria. Although predicting individual responses remains challenging, efforts to elucidate and anticipate inter-individual variations have increased in recent years. A review by Kok et al. summarizes the studies conducted from this perspective [4]. In individual studies, Button et al., for example, demonstrated that the concurrent administration of Bifidobacterium infantis and human milk oligosaccharides led to a relatively consistent increase in B. infantis across participants [5, 6]. Similarly, Watanabe et al. reported that upon arabinoxylan intake, Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum strains possessing xylanases increased in participants already carrying the strains, whereas xylanase-negative strains did not [7]. Negishi et al. reported that Faecalibacterium prausnitzii abundance increased following maltobionic acid supplementation in participants harboring bacterial taxa capable of converting maltobionic acid into glucuronic acid, which was subsequently utilized by F. prausnitzii [8].

MICROBIOGEOGRAPHICAL CLUES TO THE ADAPTIVE STRATEGIES OF GUT BACTERIA

Gut microbiota ecology can be elucidated using various approaches. One approach is to examine the spatial distribution of the bacteria. Macroscale anatomical regions (e.g., the ileum and descending colon) are commonly considered, where differences in factors, including pH, oxygen, transit speed, and mucus layer thickness, inform bacterial adaptive strategies. In contrast, micrometer-scale spatial distribution, referred to in this review as microbiogeography, has received less attention despite its direct relevance to bacterial ecology. At this scale, the consideration of local substrates and short-range gradients of growth-promoting or growth-inhibitory factors facilitates a more precise causal inference. Uniform bacterial dispersion suggests that survival factors are uniformly distributed or ubiquitously abundant, whereas clustering suggests that resources supporting the clustered bacteria are locally concentrated. Thus, microbiogeography is an important and direct indicator of an organism’s lifestyle and relies on fewer assumptions than other biological measures. For human-associated bacteria, it offers useful insights into the microbial ecology of polymicrobial infections [9], dental plaque [10], and mammalian gut bacteria. In the 2000s, Swidsinski et al. applied fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) to stool sections to investigate disease-associated alterations in bacterial spatial distribution primarily from the perspective of gastroenterology [11, 12]. In recent years, studies using mice have focused on the bacteria present around the colonic mucosa. For example, image-based analyses of bacterial density have demonstrated that the abundance of each individual bacterial species varies with distance from the mucosa [13,14,15,16]. Research on the adhesion and localization of bacteria on food residues was actively conducted using ruminants from the 1960s to the 1990s [17, 18]. Since the 2000s, studies have begun to analyze the microbiota attached to solid matter in human feces [19, 20], as well as those adhering to dietary fiber in fecal culture systems [21, 22]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have specifically identified food residues colonized by bacteria in human feces or examined the nutrient acquisition strategies of the human gut microbiota based on their colonization of food residues.

In this context, to investigate bacterial localization on food residues, we conducted analyses using mice in which we prepared paraffin sections of intestinal contents or feces and observed them using FISH [23]. The use of serial sections facilitated the identification of food residues colonized by bacteria and the determination of the bacterial species present. In mice, Bifidobacterium pseudolongum, a starch-utilizing bacterial species, was found to colonize starch granules in the intestine. This result was also supported by a microbiota analysis of areas surrounding starch granules and granule-free areas on sections collected using laser microdissection (LMD). Moreover, B. pseudolongum strains isolated from these mice showed starch-degrading activity, strongly suggesting that undigested starch granules in the mouse intestine are an important habitat and nutritional source for this species (Fig. 1).

The colonization of starch granules by B. adolescentis was similar to that observed in mice, suggesting that this may be a universal characteristic of bifidobacteria across mammalian host species. Therefore, we focused on the colonization of starch granules by B. adolescentis. The above study did not specify the diet, and only one participant had starch granules in their stool samples. Therefore, a follow-up study was conducted in which multiple participants ingested starch granules that reached their large intestine.

Natural starch granules produced by plants swell and become soluble when heated with water (i.e., cooked), increasing their digestibility; thus, almost all of them are digested and absorbed in the upper digestive tract. Starches commonly consumed in daily dietary components, such as bread, noodles, and rice, undergo cooking. In contrast, the dietary records of a participant who exhibited bifidobacterial colonization included the consumption of raw Japanese yam, a starchy root vegetable frequently eaten raw in Japan, which is presumed to be a source of resistant starch type 2 (RS2), a type of RS that is raw and granular. Therefore, we investigated the frequency of bifidobacterial colonization of starch granules when a small bowl of raw Japanese yam was consumed while restricting the intake of other raw starches (Fig. 4A).

In vitro capacity of B. adolescentis to preferentially utilize starch granules over other bacteria

The major gut bacterial species were isolated from two participants in a Japanese yam intake trial, and their adhesion properties toward starch granules were evaluated using commercially available potato starch. B. adolescentis, Agathobacter rectalis (formerly Eubacterium rectale), and Collinsella aerofaciens exhibited adhesion to starch granules. All tested Bacteroides species, along with B. adolescentis and A. rectalis, were able to utilize autoclaved (solubilized) starch. However, only B. adolescentis could utilize raw starch granules, indicating that fewer gut bacterial species can metabolize starch granules without heat processing (Table 2). Moreover, the number of species possessing both adhesion and degradative capacities for raw starch is limited, with B. adolescentis exhibiting the most robust utilization mechanisms. In another experiment, we confirmed that B. adolescentis outcompeted Ruminococcus bromii on the surface of starch granules. Ruminococcus bromii has been reported to degrade, utilize, and bind to starch granules and release starch breakdown products to the surrounding environment [25].

It is known that the adhesion of B. adolescentis to starch granules is inhibited under highly acidic conditions (pH <3) as well as following protease treatment of bacterial cells [26]. Recently, advances in genomic analysis have provided novel insights, particularly regarding carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs) [27]. For example, purified RSD2, an α-amylase that contains starch-binding CBMs, efficiently degrades an RS2, high-amylose maize starch (HAMS) [28]. Regarding other species, R. bromii employs an enzyme–protein complex, “amylosome”, analogous to the cellulosome involved in cellulose degradation, to mediate adhesion to and utilization of starch granules [29]. For A. rectalis, the starch-binding amylase Amy13K exhibits a high affinity for HAMS and efficiently degrades it, while showing very limited binding or degradation activity toward raw potato starch [30]. Incidentally, although the Sus system of Bacteroides species is well characterized, it is primarily specialized for the utilization of soluble starch and contributes only minimally to the degradation of starch granules [27], which is consistent with our findings. These facts—that enzymatic activity on soluble starch alone is insufficient to degrade granules and that granule-degrading capacity varies with the starch’s botanical origin—indicate that gut bacteria have adapted and evolved to exploit naturally occurring dietary substrates. Notably, a human intervention study showed contrasting microbiota responses to potato starch and HAMS, suggesting key substrate-specific mechanisms [31]. For RS types other than RS2, differences in the degradation capacities of B. adolescentis and R. bromii have also been reported [32].

B. adolescentis determines the intestinal microbiota response to starch granule ingestion

Raw starch granules, or RS2, alter the human gut microbiota when consumed in appropriate amounts [33]. We investigated how the presence or absence of B. adolescentis affects changes in the microbiota upon consumption of sufficient amounts of raw starch granules (Fig. 5A). During the potato starch intake period, B. adolescentis markedly increased and aggregated around fecal starch granules in all participants (Fig. 5B, 5C). A. rectalis, a known starch-utilizing species [34], also showed a substantial increase in some participants, second to B. adolescentis. Although A. rectalis was present in all participants, its increase was observed only in those who did not harbor B. adolescentis. Similarly, R. bromii increased only in B. adolescentis-negative individuals (Fig. 5B). These findings suggest that starch granules reaching the colon can substantially promote the growth of multiple bacterial species, but a hierarchical order exists: when B. adolescentis is present, it preferentially expands, whereas other species proliferate only in its absence.

We observed differences in starch granule metabolism between B. adolescentis carriers and non-carriers. First, the amount of starch granules excreted in feces was lower in B. adolescentis carriers than in non-carriers, suggesting that starch granules were more efficiently degraded and fermented in the presence of B. adolescentis (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, during starch granule intake, B. adolescentis carriers exhibited increased levels of lactate and acetate, which are known metabolites of Bifidobacterium, whereas these metabolites remained unchanged in non-carriers; instead, an increase in butyrate, a known metabolite of A. rectalis, was observed (Fig. 6B). Positive correlations were detected between B. adolescentis and lactate and acetate levels, as well as between A. rectalis and butyrate, indicating that these two bacterial species were the primary producers of their respective organic acids (Fig. 6C).

An additional correlation analysis revealed that B. adolescentis was negatively correlated with A. rectalis and positively correlated with Bifidobacterium longum, while A. rectalis was positively correlated with R. bromii (Fig. 7). These results suggest that B. adolescentis inhibits the growth of A. rectalis while promoting a favorable environment for other bifidobacterial species and that A. rectalis proliferates by utilizing starch degradation products provided by R. bromii [25].

The concurrent growth of A. rectalis and R. bromii in the same participants was consistent with the previously reported role of R. bromii as a keystone species [25]. However, in contrast to previous findings, B. adolescentis competed with A. rectalis and R. bromii, and its apparent impact overshadowed that of R. bromii in our study. Based on these results, to understand and predict in vivo dynamics, factors beyond nutrient acquisition might also need to be considered, such as the acidic environment resulting from carbohydrate fermentation, which favors acid-tolerant species such as bifidobacteria. Notably, the growth of Megasphaera, which utilizes lactate under strongly acidic conditions, has been observed in B. adolescentis carriers (Fig. 5B), a finding recently corroborated by other studies [35]. From a different yet important perspective, the RS analyzed in the aforementioned R. bromii study was RS3 or HAMS, and the results may have differed if potato starch had been used. Given that microbiota responses can differ substantially depending on the type of RS [31], previous findings should be interpreted with caution, although the bacterial species involved in degradation are often similar among RS types.

Owing to their strong adherence to starch granules, colonizing bifidobacterial species are considered capable of preferentially or almost exclusively utilizing starch granules. Following starch granule ingestion, B. adolescentis markedly increases in abundance and becomes the most abundant species in the human gut microbiota, thereby promoting the overall production of acetate and lactate.

SIGNIFICANCE OF BACTERIAL LOCALIZATION ANALYSIS

As described above, the series of studies introduced here revealed a unique relationship between starch granules, a familiar dietary component that differs somewhat from the commonly consumed cooked starch, and B. adolescentis, their primary colonizing bacterium, through the analysis of bacterial microscale biogeography. Building on the identification of B. pseudolongum colonization of starch granules in mice, a remarkable colonization of B. adolescentis on starch granules in the human gut was discovered. This finding focused subsequent research on the ecological significance of B. adolescentis as a preferential user of starch granules. This led to the discovery that the overall gut microbiota response to starch granule intake differs substantially, depending on the presence or absence of this species.

Consistent with initial expectations, this colonization was not a random co-occurrence of unrelated substrates and microbes but rather a specific relationship between a distinct nutrient source and its consumer that leads to community-wide changes characterized by selective alterations in specific bacteria. Bifidobacteria are known for their various health benefits, and B. adolescentis is one of the most frequently detected bifidobacteria in adults. Moreover, Bifidobacterium colonization of starch granules is common in mice and humans, suggesting that it is an important phenomenon conserved across many mammalian species. Thus, identifying bacterial microscale habitats in the gut may contribute to understanding the evolution of the mechanisms that maintain host health.

In recent years, omics approaches have greatly facilitated the acquisition of large-scale datasets on the human gut microbiota. However, extracting biologically meaningful insights from such data can be difficult and requires appropriate conceptual frameworks. Localization analysis, which allows the direct observation of human-derived samples, offers a valuable perspective and practical entry point for microbiome studies. In particular, when the target nutrient is predetermined and consists of poorly digestible solid materials, the associated bacterial groups can be readily identified using FISH or by analyzing samples collected from sections using LMD. If colonization is observed on unidentified structures, identification of the colonized structures will be a major challenge; however, such findings may lead to novel discoveries. Potential candidates for such structures may include a wide range of biological sources, and their shapes and properties could be influenced by cooking. Morphology, various staining methods, dietary surveys, and dietary controls can help identify these structures.

In this review, we primarily introduced findings that originated from simple observations using FISH and iodine staining; however, the field of microscale microbiogeography has seen the development of more advanced analytical approaches [36]. First, high-phylogenetic-resolution microbiome mapping by FISH (HiPR-FISH), which encodes combinations of fluorescent probes to visualize numerous targets (bacterial taxa) simultaneously, removes the limitation of conventional FISH, which only allows three to four probes to be used at once [37]. Analysis of the local microbiota community has been extended by a method called SAMPL-seq. In SAMPL-seq, samples are fixed and fragmented into micro-pieces, followed by a microbiome analysis for each fragment. Unlike LMD, predefined target regions are not required, allowing non-targeted analysis and comprehensive identification of the local co-occurrence patterns of bacteria [38]. Efforts to integrate spatial information on host tissues and microbial localization are also advancing. For example, combining tissue-clearing techniques with FISH allows for the clear visualization of bacterial positioning within intestinal crypts [39]. Spatial transcriptomics approaches that tag nucleic acids with positional information have also been applied to sections containing both gut microbes and host tissue [40]. Although still limited in number, technologies aimed at mapping bacterial activity and function in situ are emerging. These include matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry imaging (MALDI-MSI), cell wall synthesis probes, and pH-sensitive probes, each of which is integrated with FISH [41,42,43].

There remains considerable scope for innovation in methods that reveal the spatial distribution of microbial activity and function. Likewise, as highlighted above, the spatial relationship between dietary components and bacteria remains poorly understood. Many cutting-edge spatial analysis techniques require advanced computational resources and expensive reagents or instruments. Nevertheless, when specific bacterial taxa or regions of interest are defined, useful spatial information can be obtained using simple tools. Therefore, in addition to the development of advanced technologies, it is equally important to raise awareness among biologists about scenarios where spatial analysis can be particularly informative, to promote accessible methods for researchers without specialized facilities, and to encourage hypothesis generation and study design through the mining of existing spatial datasets. Ultimately, these efforts should lead not only to technical progress but also to fundamental insights into microbial ecology and host physiology.

ADVANCING UNDERSTANDING OFINDIVIDUAL DIFFERENCES ININTESTINAL MICROBIOTA RESPONSES

The observation that either B. adolescentis or A. rectalis responds to potato starch granules in each individual exemplifies the inter-individual variation in gut microbiota responses to prebiotics. This variation is influenced by the resident microbial repertoire and competition for nutrient acquisition. Similar to the findings regarding starch granules, investigations into the colonization of bacteria on other solid nutrient sources in the gut may reveal highly selective and dominant consumers that contribute to inter-individual variation as a consequence of competition between species.

Moreover, given the strong selectivity of B. adolescentis for starch granules, the microbiota response to starch granule intake in carriers can be reliably predicted. Although reliably inducing targeted microbial changes across all individuals is currently difficult, these findings may guide the design of foods or pharmaceuticals aimed at specific outcomes, such as the promotion of acetate and lactate production or, alternatively, the promotion of butyrate production. Although prebiotics can induce substantial shifts in the overall composition of the gut microbiota, their effects often differ between individuals, potentially complicating the elucidation of their true efficacy. Future research, including studies using bacterial localization analysis, should clarify individual differences, leading to a deeper understanding and better utilization of the true effects of our daily diets.
