Optical force brush enabled free-space painting of 4D functional structures
Authors: Chenqi Yi, Shuyuan Qu, Yaoyu Wang, Haoning Qi, Yufeng Zhang, Gary J. Cheng
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Femtosecond laser–based technique called two-photon polymerization (TPP) has emerged as a powerful tool for nanofabrication and integrating nanomaterials. However, challenges persist in existing three-dimensional (3D) nanoprinting methods, such as slow layer-by-layer printing and limited material options due to laser-matter interactions. Here, we present an approach to 3D nanoprinting called free-space nanopainting, using an optical force brush (OFB). OFB enables precise spatial writing paths, instantaneous adjustment of linewidths and concentrations, and unrestricted resolution beyond optical limits. OFB allows rapid aggregation and solidification of radicals, resulting in narrower lines at lower polymerization thresholds and enhanced sensitivity to laser energy. This advancement enables high-accuracy free-space painting, analogous to Chinese brush painting on paper. The printing speed is increased substantially compared to layer-by-layer methods, from 100 to 1000 times faster. We successfully printed various bionic muscle models derived from 4D nanostructures with tunable mechanical properties, responsive to electrical signals, and excellent biocompatibility.

INTRODUCTION

Nanodevices and nanostructures manufactured at high resolution and speed are critical for next-generation products. The semiconductor industry has extensively used lithography, deposition, and etching to create three-dimensional (3D) structures from various materials; however, the high processing cost and limited selection of materials prevent the flexible fabrication of 3D structures of functional materials (1–3). Two-photon polymerization (TPP)–based femtosecond laser direct writing has been widely used for fabricating complex 3D nanostructures from micro/nano polymer (4–6) for photonic quasicrystals (7), metamaterials (8, 9), micro-/nanomechanical devices (10–12), polymer matrix nanocomposites with azobenzene derivatives (13) and nanoparticles [CdS (14), gold (15), and Fe3O4 (16)], and polymer-ceramics/carbon laminate 3D nanoarchitecture for mechanical and thermal metamaterials. Two material-integrated 3D micro/nanostructures have been fabricated via layer-by-layer TPP for microrobots in drug delivery (17). However, TPP-based 3D nanoprinting still shares common problems with other layered generation techniques, such as slow printing speed, stairwise surface texture, and limited photocurable materials.

Volumetric 3D printing techniques have been developed recently to solve the slow speed problem in conventional layer-by-layer 3D printing. Computational axial lithography (CAL) can build parts in a single exposure, providing unprecedented fabrication speed and potential applications in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine (18). However, the resolution of CAL has been limited to microscale so far. With xolography (19) photoswitchable polymers enable local polymerization inside a monomer volume upon linear excitation by intersecting laser beams and laser sheets of various wavelengths, which produce 10 times more resolution than CAL and four orders more volume than TPP. In both CAL and xolography, the resolution is limited by the wavelength of the laser beam and the laser sheet, as well as by the laser-polymer interaction. Furthermore, laser scattering and optical superposition influence manufacturing accuracy. Nanoscale high-speed 3D printing remains challenging. Another challenge of current 3D nanoprinting is the limited availability of functional nanomaterials for printing via TPP (20–27). The materials available for printing are limited to photosensitive polymers and a few photocopiable precious metals (28, 29). There have been many attempts to develop nonphotosensitive materials that can be used for direct writing (30–32), but most of them have only been achieved by physically mixing functional particles with photosensitive polymers (14, 33–35).

Laser direct writing methods and the expansion of printable materials are essential to the practical application of 3D nanoprinting. In contrast to conventional photoreduction or TPP, optical force brush (OFB) uses the photodynamic forces based on femtosecond laser–induced multiphoton absorption, resulting in the accumulation of free radicals in the precursor solution and an increase in their concentration locally. The optical force in OFB allows radicals to aggregate quickly in a smaller area, which leads to faster solidification rate, narrower lines at low polymerization thresholds, and higher sensitivity to laser energy. This allows free-space 3D printing in liquid without the limitations of low speed due to layer-by-layer processing and limited printable materials via TPP and photoreduction, as well as linewidth unconstrained by optical limit. During OFB, we are able to control free radical activation and solidification using instantly adjustable laser parameters, as well as arbitrary scanning paths enabled by the ultrahigh solidification rate and high sensitivity to laser intensity. As a result of free-space writing with continuously adjustable linewidth at the nanoscale, complex nanostructures are possible, including spider webs, bird nests, and other suspended irregular structures with continuously changing geometric dimensions. Continuously adjusting linewidth allows us to print 3D nanostructures with stronger mechanical properties than layer-by-layer printing, which allows us to print many 3D nanostructures. This study will uncover how free-space laser writing uses photomechanical forces to enable OFB.

RESULT

Free space painting via femtoseconds laser

In general, when the time scale reaches the femtosecond scale, the molecule absorbs the photon and is excited into an electronically excited state with a repulsive potential energy surface, and the excited molecule can dissociate directly along the repulsive potential energy surface, resulting in free radicals. A multiphoton absorption mechanism can be used to absorb ultrashort pulse photon energy in molecules and activate the electron transition between the ground state and the excited state. By irradiating these active radicals with a femtosecond laser, the optical forces can aggregate them rapidly and synthesize them into macromolecules, completing the solidification quickly without any postprocessing while minimizing the solvent molecules’ thermal motion. By controlling the laser intensity and scanning speed, the free radicals can consolidate and solidify into a line of widely tunable width. The linewidth could be much smaller than the beam size, thus breaking the optical limit. In addition, as the laser beam scans at high speed, the optical forces replenish free radicals in the solution constantly and rapidly, thus facilitating the solidification of unsupported space and allowing free-space printing on any axis plane at a tunable depth and width, as if painting in space. In this study, we developed a hydrogel-based ink as a photoswitch that can be activated upon femtosecond laser writing via two-photon absorption (TPA), as the radicals in the gel absorb photon energy from the femtosecond laser and free the radicals from the binding energy in the molecules. In addition, the long-chain molecules can be connected to different functional groups, thus providing opportunities for various application scenarios. This printable hydrogel-based ink is highly biocompatible, elastic, and flexible, which extends many applications to free-space printable nanostructures in biomedical.

As shown in Fig. 1, the laser beam moves freely in the solution similar to a pen in space, and there are three steps involved: activation, aggregation, and solidification of free radicals. In the first step, multiphoton absorption occurs in laser irradiation as the radicals absorb laser energy directly, driving molecules from a ground state into an excited state. When the multiphoton energy is higher than the energy of the C─C unsaturated chemical bond, the group in the precursor breaks at the α-position (Norrish I reaction) and the reactive radical is formed. Second, the femtosecond laser greatly affects the motion distribution of the free radicals in the liquid, resulting in a concentration of reactive radicals. Laser beam intensity also shows a Gaussian distribution, and the focal point at the waist of the beam is also the most intense. Upon irradiation with a femtosecond laser, the reactive groups in the liquid are affected by the optical forces, and the free radicals have moved across all directions toward the center point by the action of the laser (see movie S4). The reactive groups are mainly affected by the following forces (Fig. 1A): first, natural forces, such as gravity, buoyancy, etc., and second, radiation pressure, thermophilic force, optical force, Brownian force, etc. generated by the optical trap [see section 1 in the Supplementary Materials; (36–42)]. The density of absorbing free radicals in the experiment is 1.3 g/cm3 and the diameter is 30 nm, and at the laser power of 10 mW, it is calculated that the difference between gravity and buoyancy is ~10−27 N, the optical radiation pressure is less than ~10−9 N, the photothermolysis force is about ~10−17 N, and the photophoretic force is 0.024 N. It can be seen that the photophoretic force plays a decisive role in the motion of free radicals compared to other forces, and the influence of the laser on the motion and distribution of free radicals can be adjusted by adjusting the photophoretic force, so that various density of free radicals can be concentrated in the laser scanning path. In the femtosecond laser, the reactive radicals are cross-linked from the monomer to the polymer. The depth-to-width ratio of the line can be decided by the concentration of radicals in the region where the photochemical reaction occurs, when the concentration is higher than the solidification threshold. The wavelength of the lens and the numerical aperture of the lens are traditionally used to determine the smallest linewidth based on Rayleigh criterion. Because of optical forces in two-photon or multiphoton absorption, free radicals can aggregate in a smaller range, leading to a lower solidification threshold and narrower linewidths during solidification reactions (Fig.1H). More specifically, it is well known that the solidification process begins slowly, increases rapidly and automatically accelerates in the middle stages, and gradually decreases toward the end of the process to roughly equal the rate at which the chain grows. As a result of an increase in the initial radical concentration, the automatic acceleration in the middle stages is more likely. The process is directly correlated with the amount of change in the unit radical concentration. On the basis of the kinetic equation of solidification, the rate equation for actual solidification can be derived.

Polymerization rates for TPP and OFB were calculated separately using a multiphysics model, which is shown in section 1.2 in the Supplementary Materials, with the results shown in Fig. 1I. On the basis of the simulation results, both OFB and TPP accelerate solidification rate greatly due to TPA and photodynamic aggregation, which enables both methods to react in a very small area at room temperature. The polymerization rate of this reaction is around 10−2 mol liter−1 s−1 at room temperature. The concentration of radicals generated per unit area increases nonlinearly when the laser intensity increases near the critical threshold where multiphoton absorption occurs, which is typically very sensitive to laser energy. As a result, the laser-curing rate increases as well. As compared to TPP, the solidification rate of OFB increases more rapidly with increasing laser intensity, i.e., the more intense the light, the greater the advantage. In the case of OFB and TPP, the solidification rates were 0.211 and 0.011 mol liter−1 s−1 at a laser power of 50 mW, respectively. In contrast, when the laser power was increased to 500 mW, the solidification rates of OFB and TPP were 57.66 and 42.68 mol liter−1 s−1, respectively, a thousand times the normal curing rate. Consequently, the OFB method is highly sensitive to spatially arbitrary shapes of unsupported structures due to its fast solidification rate and low solidification threshold. As a result, we found that the photochemical switch can be quantified by adjusting the laser parameters, adjusting the laser field intensity distribution, and changing the photochemical reaction threshold of the precursor material so that we can print a series of lines with different aspect ratios. OFB with a largely tunable linewidth can avoid the connection interface generated by layered printing methods and enhance the mechanical properties of the structure. The designable aspect ratio also leads to the designable mechanical properties. As a result, some multidimensional structures can be printed, thus enabling adjustable laser printing in space in a nonaxial direction, thereby enhancing the mechanical properties of the structure.

In Fig. 1 (B to G), the laser free-writing technology is demonstrated by the ability to adjust the linewidth of the curing line continuously with extremely smooth line edges while continuously adjusting laser power, scanning speed, and defocusing. Variations in linewidth vary smoothly from tens to hundreds of nanometers, and chemical concentration can be controlled using laser power and scanning speed, even when the beam diameter is the same. Figure 1B shows Chinese characters written by OFB. With this process, the laser acts on the material as a brush would act on paper, with continuously adjustable strokes, so that the depth-to-width ratio of the written lines can be smoothly adjusted. In contrast to layer-by-layer and line-by-line printing, this method allows for more design flexibility. For the transformation of a printed structure from 1.5D to 3D, it is possible to change the mechanical properties of the hydrogel material from more flexible to more rigid by changing the synthesis path of the hydrogel material. Among the characteristics of OFB is its ability to print randomly disordered spatially free structures that are not always written axially and cannot be printed by traditional printing methods. The structure in Fig. 1C shows a randomly ordered bird’s nest structure with branches that support one another and thicken where stress is weak to increase the strength of the structure. As shown in Fig. 1D, a DNA double helix structure can be printed quickly and directly without any support structure, with a nucleic acid backbone and a base stacking structure of 500 nm. All of these microstructures can be printed very rapidly with variable details, and the entire process can be completed in seconds. With a tunable mechanical property, printing details with different mechanical properties can also be accomplished simultaneously, as shown in Fig. 1E, which uses an optical-chemical switch between the laser and tunable materials to produce a spider web structure with rigid struts and naturally dangling spider webs. This free-space writing easily breaks the technical limit for fast writing of arbitrary structures, such as the pavilion in Fig. 1F and the skeletonized sphere structure shown in Fig. 1G. The images (see fig. S2) show that this process can tune the linewidth to vary continuously on small scales, such as between 173 and 232 nm, and also on large scales, such as between 1.5 and 4 μm.

Further, this approach can greatly improve the efficiency of the writing structure, since, as we all know, layer-by-layer, line-by-line printing is a method in which the number of layers is directly correlated with the thickness resolution. Increasing the number of layers results in higher resolution but a longer laser traveling path and more time for the laser returning to its origin and slower speed, as shown in Fig. 1J. The comparison of laser scan paths for the two printing methods is shown in fig. S3. The time required for comparison is calculated by using OFB and layer-by-layer, line-by-line printing for the same resolution. It is found that for free structures such as Chinese characters, the speed is increased by 30 to 400 times by using OFB, as shown in the red bar of Fig. 1J. The reason is that OFB requires only a few strokes to write the graphical path of the same volume, with very little laser idle time, while the path of the line-by-line writing method is increased hundreds of times. For solid structures such as cylinders, the speed was increased by 50 to 500 times; for skeletonized structures such as helix, football skeletons, pavilions, etc., the speed was increased by more than 30 to 700 times; and for some complex structures, such as overhanging structures, nested structures, etc., OFB can complete accurate printing within seconds, while the traditional layer printing method cannot print accurately due to the existence of layer gaps or need to take a thousand times more time to achieve the same accuracy and resolution. Thus, OFB can greatly improve 3D nanostructure writing efficiency and accuracy.

Principle and simulation

With femtosecond laser pulse interaction with hydrogel-based photoswitch ink, nonlinear effects induced the activation, clustering, and aggregation of the free radicals. As the free radical concentrations increase over time and the concentration of active free radicals reaches a certain threshold (eq. S18 in section 2.3 of the Supplementary Materials), solidification occurs. As the femtosecond laser irradiates the material, the kinetic energy from the photons is exchanged with the active free radicals, which move by the optical force, resulting in displacement, acceleration, and rotation. As the diameter of active radicals is much smaller than the femtosecond laser wavelength, optical forces are enabled to align these active radicals toward the focal point. Femtosecond lasers have an optical effect on active radicals that only occurs in very short pulse durations and enough laser intensity, causing free radicals to move immediately after activation. In contrast to the normal ultraviolet curing process, optical effects during OFB enable printing of high resolution well below the spot size (see fig. S4). As free radicals are gathered at the center, femtosecond pulses continue to act on the sample, resulting in the concentration of free radicals increasing continuously on both sides and reaching the solidification threshold one after another, resulting in a continuously increasing linewidth. Compared to the surrounding area, the center has the highest laser intensity, while the concentration of free radicals is much higher. Because the movement of free radicals and thus the concentration of free radicals are very sensitive to the pulse intensity and increase nonlinearly near the intensity threshold, the linewidth after solidification is more sensitive to laser intensities during OFB, resulting in sharp and high-resolution 3D nanoprinting.

Through numerical simulation, the fundamental mechanisms of these processes are explored. A multiphysics simulation model is used to obtain the motion and composite process of the radicals based on the experimental parameters shown in Fig. 2A. The whole process occurs at a power of several milliwatts and is completed within a few nanoseconds. This phase transition’s high sensitivity to lasers combined with its high speed creates a substantial degree of freedom in space writing because the printing process finishes quickly enough that the structure needs no bottom support. Upon laser irradiation, the radical is generated with an initial velocity. The subsequent analysis of the velocity vector distribution reveals an interesting pattern: The final velocity vectors extend radially outward from the center, with zero velocity observed precisely at the center. Moreover, the magnitude of the velocity increases as the radical moves away from the center. This behavior can be attributed to the interplay of optical forces. As the radical approaches the center, the light force exerted on it diminishes along the X direction while concurrently intensifying in the Z direction. Consequently, this interaction gives rise to a progressive reduction in acceleration until the radical attains a state of equilibrium. The observed velocity vector distribution underscores the intricate interplay between the optical forces and the resulting motion of the radical.

When radicals are trapped together by electrostatic adsorption, their speed eventually decreases to zero. Radicals that are optically trapped aggregate in this manner. Consequently, the laser can also be used to manipulate the aggregation of radicals. The in-depth study of this phenomenon reveals that, when a certain laser parameter is applied, the radical density distribution approaches a Gaussian distribution (Fig. 2C), while free radical density can solidify above the threshold of aggregation (orange line in Fig. 2C, calculated in section 2.3 in the Supplementary Materials), producing a linewidth that is smaller than the laser beam size, breaking the optical limit. It is the point where the aggregation threshold intersects the free radical density distribution curve that determines the linewidth. Using 20 mW power, the linewidth of the hydrogel base will reach 150 nm and will increase to 3 μm with 500 mW power. As the laser parameters are continuously changed, the linewidth can vary smoothly. The distribution of radicals under continuous laser scanning in three dimensions has been modeled and compared with experiments, as shown in Fig. 2D. It was found that the correspondingly varying linewidths were obtained after controlling different laser parameters, which can either be continuously adjustable in a rod with continuous varying diameter or abruptly varying in continuous beads with abrupt varying diameter.

Upon further refinement of the experimental results, it was determined that the optical force applied to the radicals is directly related to the number of pulses, the intensity of the laser field, and the absorption coefficient of the two-photon cross-section of the material. Photochemical reactions occur within one pulse cycle, and no additional radicals are generated upon the disappearance of a pulse. The number of radicals generated increases with the number of pulses (as illustrated in Fig. 2E), which is positively correlated with the square of the laser power (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, the higher absorption coefficient of the TPA cross-section of the material results in an increase in the number of active radicals (Fig. 2G). The radical size has a substantial impact on the linewidth limit (Fig. 2H), and our simulation shows that the linewidth increases nonlinearly with the free radical sizes. With a radical of 1 nm, the linewidth can reach 20 nm, which is much smaller than the laser beam diameter (fig. S5A). Because of the presence of the optical force, OFB produces a more concentrated radical distribution than TPP (Fig. 2I). In addition, OFB achieves the same radical density as TPP but at lower power (i.e., easier to polymerize), while at the same power, OFB attains a narrower linewidth than TPP (i.e., higher resolution).

In addition, simulations were conducted to evaluate the effects of power, scanning speed, and defocus amount on linewidth and height. According to Fig. 2J, it is found that the power has a great impact near the aggregation threshold when the power increases in the range of 20 to 200 mW, and the linewidth and height both increase rapidly as the power increases. However, when the power exceeds 200 mW, the increase tends to level off, whereas if the power exceeds this level, the lines are ablated. The effect of scan speed is illustrated in Fig. 2K. With increasing scan speed, the linewidth and height decrease rapidly. In addition, solidification will not occur if the pulse count falls below a certain minimum level required for photochemical reactions to occur. Figure 2L illustrates the impact of positive and negative out-of-focus amounts on the width and height of the printed structure when the laser focal point is in proximity to the substrate. The printing process takes place at the focal position, where the force direction consistently points toward the center of the laser focal point. In cases where the focal point is suspended, the effects of positive and negative out-of-focus amounts are identical. However, when the focal point is close to the base, positive out-of-focus results in the focal point position being located below the base. Consequently, the effect of out-of-focus on line height exhibits a linear relationship. In contrast, negative out-of-focus has no impact on linewidth, whereas positive out-of-focus leads to a decrease in linewidth following an arc-like pattern. This phenomenon arises from the circular or elliptical cross-sectional shape of the printed line. This model provides us with a solid foundation for designing experiments in which the line heights and widths of the lines are continuously adjustable to write more complex structures. The free-space painting approach allows femtosecond lasers to print complex structures in nonaxial or unsupported space.

Nested muscle systems and electroresponsive properties

This technology has allowed us to print muscle belly and tendon tissues that are composed of multilayered nesting of fibers and fiber bundles, which are difficult to print using traditional 3D printing methods. We not only can print the muscle’s internal and external shape but can also activate the muscle’s movement upon electrical stimulation by using a functional hydrogel-based ink. This marks the initial instance of simultaneously achieving both structural and functional bionic nanoprinting. Figure 3 (A to C) demonstrates the structure of the rat hamstrings’ tendon and belly. Figure 3 (D to F and G to I) shows the structure of the muscle belly and tendon printed by OFB and layer-by-layer method, respectively. Multilayer structures are printed in 3D space while suspended without support, and the thickness of the muscle fibers turns continuously from thin to thick, as well as the transverse muscle structure with variable depth-to-width ratio. By using OFB, which can be adapted to a variety of self-designed functional materials, we prepared the gel electroresponsive material (GERM), which is discussed in the experimental section. With this ink, muscle structures can be printed with adjustable mechanical and electrical properties, and the material can impart a variety of functionalities to the structures.

By adjusting the ratio of Gelatin Methacrylate (GelMA) in the GERM synthesis step, the final mechanical properties of GERM-printed structures can be manipulated (Fig. 3J). At 10% GelMA concentration, the Young’s modulus of GERM-printed structure is 5.76 GPa and the hardness is 0.315 GPa with good flexibility, while at 70% GelMA concentration, the Young’s modulus of GERM-printed structure increases to 7.24 GPa, the hardness increases to 0.367 GPa, and the printed structure is rigid at this time. At 15% or more concentration, the material changes from soft to hard. By varying the concentration of free radicals in the precursor and the solidification threshold, it is possible to adapt the material’s mechanical properties to different structural designs. For printing muscle structures, the diastolic and contractile movements require a certain flexibility of the ink material, whereas printing unsupported suspensions in solution requires a certain rigidity of the ink material, and the experiment found that at a GelMA concentration of 30%, this requirement could be perfectly met. Using this feature, OFB can modify the mechanical properties of micro- and nanostructures based on their application requirements.

As muscle fibers have continuous, one-piece bundle structures without breakage points, their overall strength is several times greater. The free-space painting technique using OFB replicates the process of assembling muscle fibers by twisting a hydrogel-based material with low strength into a single strand, which evenly disperses residual stress when it is stressed, so that when the muscle movement process is mimicked by electric field stimulation, the multiple expansions and contractions do not rupture or break. As shown in Fig. 3L, the bionic muscle fiber bundle (left) gradually thickened due to free-space painting via OFB to prevent contact stress during structure manufacture. Compared with the muscle structure printed layer by layer (right), not only is the resolution higher, but also the structure stress distribution is more uniform under stress, the fragility point is reduced, and the maximum stress is reduced by 45%, resulting in excellent mechanical properties. It can be seen that M3 via OFB has a Young’s modulus of 7.11 GPa and a hardness of 0.33 GPa for the same 30% concentration of GERM ink, while LM3 via layer-by-layer method has a Young’s modulus of only 0.054 GPa and a hardness of 0.005 GPa (as shown in Fig. 3K). As shown in Fig. 3M, in the simulation where the left end of the muscle is fixed and displacement is applied to the right, the OFB method yields strains of up to 160%, while the muscle structure printed by layers has only 87% strain and withstand much less maximum stress. With the same materials and parameters, this difference comes from the way the OFB method prints in one stroke, allowing fewer junctions in the structure, less shear stress inside the structure, and a substantial increase in tensile properties. As shown by Fig. 3N, residual stresses are concentrated at one point in a layer-by-layer structure, but one-shot printing by OFB allows for a more uniform distribution of residual stresses, with even the weakest points receiving less than 40% of the stresses of layer-by-layer printing. As a result, the overhanging 3D structure in Fig. 1 printed by one-shot solidification via OFB is more stable and will not collapse.

Structure of muscles is composed of fibers stacked on top of each other and nested in an orderly manner. In a muscle structure, a bundle of muscle fibers is composed of numerous muscle fibers within a bundle of muscle fibers, and a bundle of muscle fibers is composed of multiple protofibers within a bundle of muscle fibers, resulting in a complex multilayered nested structure that contributes to the strength of the muscle structure. With the free-space OFB painting technique, a wide variety of bionic structures can be printed. Complex biological structures can be printed using this method, which is not possible with conventional printing procedures. As shown in Fig. 4 (A and B), the 3D model and printed parts, we designed and printed complex 3D structures such as vascular networks, hearts, muscle fiber bundles, etc. with GERM inks. In addition to printing bionic structures, OFB can also produce vascular models, which allow direct printing of arteries, veins, and tiny capillaries at the junction. It is also possible to design and construct micron-scale models of the heart, in which both the blood vessels and the heart can be well integrated together. The nanofiber bundles can be designed and fabricated for bionic muscle models, in which the single protofibers have continuously varying dimensions at the nanoscale, with clear and smooth edges, and are bound together at the tendons, morphologically restoring the multilayered nested structure of muscles. According to Fig. 4 (C and D), we have printed almost all forms of human muscle with GERM ink, including mesomuscular, long multimuscular, and pinnate muscles. Micron-level fiber bundles composed of nanoscale protofibers may be arbitrarily composed into various morphological muscle structures, as illustrated in Fig. 4C. As the printed single protofibers vary in size at the nanoscale, they have smooth and clear edges and bundle together at the tendons, morphologically restoring the muscle’s multilayered nested structure. Because of the special functionality of the printed material, it is also possible to realize dynamic functions of muscle, including contraction and extension of long muscles, closure and unfolding of rotator muscles, and diastole and contraction of vascular muscles.

The specific muscle motion diagram is shown in Fig. 4E. It is observed that when the bionic longus muscle is stimulated, it rapidly expands and reaches a deformation variable of 150% within 5 s as a result of the applied electric field. The bionic longus muscle gradually retracts and returns to its initial state when the stimulation model is removed. This is similar to the way muscles receive bioelectric signals in an organism, and movements and forces are identical as well. For the printed vascular models (Fig. 4F), within just a few seconds of applying electrical stimulation, the vascular undergoes uniform expansion in different directions, and after removing the electrical stimulation, it slowly returns to its original shape, which is made possible by the GERM functional gel we use. In the micron-scale bionic heart, both the blood vessels and the heart can be stimulated by electrical stimulation to make expanding and contracting motions (Fig. 4G), which are necessary to perform functional pumping of the blood. Essentially, these motions mimic the behavior of living tissues, enabling micro- and nanoscale 3D printing in a wide range of biomedical applications.

The high photosensitivity of hydrogel-based materials and the ability to attach functional groups, such as electrical stimulation response, to their long chains of gelatin made it possible to design and prepare PGERM materials (see Materials and Methods), convert into GERM materials, and print out micro/nanostructures simultaneously. Applying an electric field to the micro-/nanostructure, it was found that the structure started to dissolve at a low voltage of 6 V. The structure dissolution rate reached a maximum of 225% at a voltage of 11 V (as shown in Fig. 4H), showing an extremely excellent low-voltage and highly variable performance. As shown in Fig. 4I, when an electric field is applied to GERM, iron ions gain electrons and rapidly (about 3 s) leave cyclodextrin’s cage structure, causing the volume of the overall GERM to expand; after removing the electric field, the iron ions complex with cyclodextrin, and the volume gradually retracts (about 15 s) automatically. A Cyclic Voltammetry (CV) test was performed with the GERM material coated on the electrode sheet, and the redox peak of iron ions (attachment) was stable after 20 cycles (Fig. 4J), indicating a stable and durable electrical stimulation response. Furthermore, the GERM structure printed with OFB has excellent biocompatibility. In the t3t rat cell death experiment (fig. S11), there was almost no cell death after more than 48 hours of incubation, which means the GERM material was overall harmless to the cells. In the CK-8 cell proliferation experiment (Fig. 4K), the GERM-containing culture grew better than the blank group, which addresses challenges associated with 3D nanoprinting nontoxic biomaterials. Moreover, the printed micro- and nanostructures can be completely implanted into an organism, making it possible to realize both structural and functional biostructures at the micro- and nanoscales. As a result, free-space painting via OFB opens up possibilities for the application of multifunctional micro- and nanostructures in biology.

DISCUSSION

Optical force brush (OFB) is a technique that uses a femtosecond laser paintbrush to print functional structures. The advantages of OFB stem from its unique capabilities, and the underlying process mechanism of optical force enabled nanopainting, including (i) ultrahigh solidification rate: OFB enables rapid solidification of the hydrogel ink photoswitch due to the precise and controlled application of the femtosecond laser. This high solidification rate allows for efficient and quick printing of complex structures. (ii) Low solidification threshold: OFB exhibits a low threshold for solidification, meaning that even low-intensity laser beams can induce the desired solidification of the hydrogel ink photoswitch. This characteristic allows for greater control over the printing process and reduces the risk of unwanted solidification. (iii) High sensitivity to laser: OFB is highly sensitive to the laser, enabling precise manipulation of the printing process. This sensitivity ensures accurate control over the line depth, width, and height ratios, allowing for the creation of intricate structures with fine details. (iv) True 3D printing freedom: OFB offers true freedom in the printing process by allowing printing in any axial plane. Unlike traditional layer-by-layer approaches, OFB enables continuous printing in three dimensions, eliminating the need for predefined layers and enabling seamless transitions between different planes.

This work investigated the mechanism of optical forces for nanopainting in free space during OFB. The fundamental mechanism behind OFB involves the interaction of the femtosecond laser with free radicals in the hydrogel ink photoswitch. This mechanism has been explored through numerical simulations. The femtosecond laser generates optical forces through the principle of optical tweezers. When focused on the hydrogel ink photoswitch, the laser induces localized heating, resulting in the formation of free radicals. These free radicals initiate a polymerization process within the hydrogel, leading to solidification in the irradiated regions. The precise control of the laser beam allows for spatially controlled solidification, enabling the formation of complex structures with high resolution. By carefully designing the hydrogel inks, the contraction and diastole of printed muscle structures can be achieved at low voltage, facilitating biomimicry of structural and functional properties.

The development of OFB represents a substantial advancement in the field of 3D printing and nanofabrication. OFB enables the printing of functional structures by using hydrogel inks with tunable mechanical and electrical stimulation properties. The ability to create complex, functional architectures opens up possibilities for applications in various fields, such as tissue engineering, soft robotics, and microelectronics. OFB allows for printing in any axial plane, enabling the fabrication of structures with intricate geometries that were previously challenging or impossible to achieve using conventional layer-by-layer approaches. This freedom in design and fabrication expands the possibilities for creating complex, customized objects. OFB offers high-resolution printing with continuously tunable line depth and width ratios in a single stroke. The ability to instantly adjust the width from sub–50 nm to a few microns and vary the height without any steps allows for the fabrication of structures with fine details and diverse morphologies. The research demonstrates the feasibility of OFB by showcasing multiple morphologies of human muscle structures as proof-of-concept applications. This highlights the potential of OFB for creating functional bionic structures and paves the way for further exploration in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine.

As a perspective on the optical force brush (OFB) technique, we further discuss its potential applications and challenges in functional micro/nano 3D nanostructures. While OFB has demonstrated designability and practicality, further exploration is needed to address challenges related to print volume scalability and material design. By using different printing inks, OFB enables the creation of functional 3D nanostructures, with hydrogel-based materials offering biocompatible properties and tunable mechanical features. This makes OFB well suited for rigid or flexible microfunctional devices, particularly in applications such as intrabiological drug delivery, scaffolds, and sensor devices. The continuous advancements in smart materials and advanced processing technologies unlock opportunities for complex structural device designs. In addition, OFB’s capability for free-space coating holds promise for achieving breakthrough properties in new 3D micro- and nanodevices.

Fabricating functional micro/nano 3D nanostructures is a burgeoning field, and OFB has emerged as a promising technique. OFB offers a versatile platform by leveraging different printing inks, enabling the creation of functional 3D nanostructures. Hydrogel-based materials, known for their rich functional groups and tunable mechanical properties, are particularly suitable for OFB. These materials exhibit excellent biocompatibility, making OFB a compelling choice for various applications, including rigid or flexible microfunctional devices. Intrabiological drug delivery, scaffolds, and sensor devices represent areas where OFB can revolutionize the field.

While OFB has demonstrated its capabilities at small scales, there is a need for further research to address challenges related to print volume scalability. Exploring strategies to enhance printing speed, optimizing laser parameters, and developing parallelization techniques will be critical to enable large-scale fabrication of functional micro/nano 3D nanostructures. Tailoring ink formulations specifically for OFB can unlock unprecedented functionalities. Investigating hydrogel materials with enhanced mechanical properties and improved biocompatibility, and stimuli-responsive behavior will broaden the range of applications for OFB. In addition, exploring the incorporation of functional nanoparticles and biomolecules into the inks can lead to the fabrication of hybrid structures with tailored properties.

OFB holds tremendous potential for a wide range of applications. In the field of intrabiological drug delivery, OFB’s precise control over architecture and composition is critical for achieving effective and targeted drug release. The ability to create intricate scaffolds using OFB facilitates tissue engineering, allowing for the development of structures that closely mimic the natural extracellular matrix. Furthermore, OFB’s high-resolution printing capabilities make it well suited for sensor devices, where tailored micro- and nanostructures can enhance sensing performance. By addressing challenges in material design and process optimization, OFB enables the fabrication of complex structural devices with breakthrough properties. The ability of OFB to perform free-space coating opens up possibilities for the creation of advanced 3D micro- and nanodevices with enhanced functionalities and performance.

In conclusion, our study introduced the optical force brush (OFB) technique, a femtosecond laser–based method for printing functional structures with true 3D printing freedom. OFB allows printing in any axial plane, providing continuously tunable line depth-to-width ratios in a single stroke, and instant width adjustments from sub–50 nm to a few microns, without step stairs. Through numerical simulations, we investigated the fundamental mechanism of OFB, revealing its ultrahigh solidification rates, low solidification thresholds, and high sensitivity to laser energy. The use of designed hydrogels with adjustable mechanical and electrical properties enables the fabrication of functional 3D nanostructures using OFB. We demonstrated the potential of OFB by printing multiple morphologies of human muscle structures, achieving structural and functional biomimicry. Furthermore, OFB is compatible with other micro/nanofabrication technologies, allowing for scalable and facile fabrication. With the continuous development of smart materials and advanced processing technologies, OFB holds promise for enabling complex structural device designs and broad applications for new 3D micro/nanodevices with breakthrough properties.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Preparation of hydrogel precursor

A simple one-step nucleophilic addition reaction was used to prepare β-CD-AOI2 (see in S3), and a two-step method was used to synthesize esterified ferrocene acrylate (A-TEG-Fer; see in S3). The complexation process was completed by dissolving β-CD-AOI2 (0.5 g) in 50 ml of N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF; Sigma-Aldrich) and adding 0.126 g of A-TEG-Fer, followed by stirring at room temperature for 24 hours. When freeze-dried, a white powder-like host-guest supramolecular electro responsive material (ERM) was obtained. ERM (100 mg) and GelMA (2 mg; Sigma-Aldrich) were dissolved in water, mixed, and stirred for 24 hours. The suspension was obtained, filtered, and dried to obtain a yellow powder, which is PGERM (precursor of GERM; see fig. S6 for a diagram illustrating the synthesis process). A total of 20 mg of PGERM and 2 mg of C16H16LiO3P (LAP, Sigma-Aldrich) were dissolved in a mixture of deionized water and DMF, stirred thoroughly to dissolve completely and stored in brown glass bottles.

OFB fabrication processing

A coverslip (24 mm by 24 mm LAP 170 μm; Citotest Scientific Ltd.) was used as a glass substrate in this work. The glass substrates were rinsed in a solution consisting of 100 ml of ethanol and 5 ml of deionized water for 1 hour and then dried in vacuum and set aside. Ten microliters were taken and placed in a glass recess, sealed with tape, and the micro/nanostructures were fabricated using a laboratory-built OFB system (see fig. S7 for a schematic diagram of the synthesis process). The laser system outputs a linearly polarized light with horizontal orientation, an average power of 2 W, a pulse repetition frequency of 4.91 MHz, a pulse width of 474 fs, a central wavelength of 1030 nm, and a wavelength output of 515 nm, and after frequency doubling, the laser is a single pulse of 478 nJ and the laser beam is guided by a combination of 4f imaging system and an oil immersion objective lens (Olympus; 1.42 numerical aperture, ×60) tightly focused in the sample. A piezoelectric stage (Physik Instrumente) was used to control the focused laser spot motion. After fabrication, the laser-written structures were rinsed in ethanol and water for several minutes to remove other solutions, and the microstructures were obtained on a glass substrate.
