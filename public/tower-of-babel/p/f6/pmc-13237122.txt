Local Recurrence after Microsurgical Clipping of Unruptured Intracranial Aneurysms: Incidence, Timing, and Risk Factors over 20 Years
Authors: Shunsuke KAWAMOTO, Go IKEDA, Shunsuke FUKAYA, Kanae OKUNUKI, Hiroyoshi AKUTSU
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This retrospective cohort study evaluated long-term local recurrence rates following microsurgical clipping of anterior circulation unruptured intracranial aneurysms. Between April 2003 and August 2025, 657 patients underwent 700 procedures for 786 aneurysms. Local recurrence was assessed by computed tomography angiography at 5-year intervals. During a mean follow-up of 10.5 ± 3.5 years (6,927.8 patient-years), 9 local recurrences were identified, yielding annual recurrence rates of 0.156% per patient-year and 0.111% per aneurysm-year. Cumulative recurrence-free rates were 100% at 5 years, 99.1% at 10 years, and 98.6% at 15 years. Location-specific analysis revealed significantly higher recurrence rates for internal carotid-anterior choroidal artery aneurysms (0.630% annually) and internal carotid-posterior communicating artery aneurysms (0.262% annually) compared with other locations (p = 0.011). Review of recurrent cases identified 3 mechanistic patterns: closure-line regrowth after parallel clipping (Pattern A, n = 2), residual thin-walled basal footprint in broad-based aneurysms (Pattern B, n = 2), and sub-angiographic remnants preserved for perforator safety (Pattern C, n = 5). The predominance of Pattern C at anterior choroidal artery and posterior communicating artery locations explains the location-specific recurrence risk. All recurrences occurred in aneurysms with complete obliteration (Sindou Grade 0) on early postoperative imaging, whereas no recurrences were observed among 26 cases (3.3%) with small neck remnants (Sindou Grades I-II). Microsurgical clipping of unruptured intracranial aneurysms demonstrated excellent long-term durability with very low local recurrence rates, though the gradual increase beyond 10 years highlights the need for continued surveillance, particularly at locations requiring perforator-preserving techniques.

Introduction

Unruptured intracranial aneurysms (UIAs) are increasingly detected owing to the widespread use of noninvasive neuroimaging. Management strategies for UIAs have evolved substantially over the past decade, with growing emphasis on balancing rupture risk against treatment-related morbidity.1) Risk stratification models such as the Population, Hypertension, Age, Size, Earlier subarachnoid hemorrhage, and Site (PHASES) score have been widely adopted to estimate rupture risk based on patient- and aneurysm-specific factors, including population, hypertension, age, size, earlier subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), and aneurysm site.2) Current American Heart Association/American Stroke Association (AHA/ASA) guidelines recommend individualized treatment decisions based on aneurysm size, location, patient risk factors, and life expectancy, while also emphasizing the role of long-term surveillance imaging for selected patients.3) Microsurgical clipping has long been established as a durable treatment for intracranial aneurysms, with extensive evidence supporting its efficacy in preventing SAH in both ruptured and unruptured cases.4,5) However, long-term follow-up studies have demonstrated that a small subset of clipped aneurysms may develop local recurrence, potentially leading to re-rupture and SAH.6,7) Though the risk of local recurrence after clipping has been well documented for ruptured aneurysms,8,9) comprehensive data specifically addressing UIAs remain limited.

The surgical management of UIAs presents unique challenges compared to ruptured aneurysms, as safety considerations often take precedence over achieving perfect aneurysm obliteration.10,11) This “safety-first” approach may theoretically result in more conservative clipping techniques, potentially affecting long-term recurrence rates. Furthermore, the natural history of small UIAs shows relatively low annual rupture rates,12,13) making long-term follow-up essential for meaningful assessment of treatment durability.

Previous studies examining local recurrence after aneurysm clipping have predominantly focused on mixed populations of ruptured and unruptured aneurysms,14,15) with annual recurrence rates ranging from 0.26% to 0.52% for completely clipped aneurysms and up to 1.83% for those with postoperative remnants.16) However, specific data for UIAs treated with microsurgical clipping remain scarce, particularly regarding very long-term outcomes extending beyond 10 years.

Advances in multidetector computed tomography angiography (CTA), particularly 64-row and higher-detector systems, have enabled more precise and less invasive surveillance of clipped aneurysms, with reported sensitivities exceeding 97% for aneurysm detection.17,18) This has facilitated comprehensive long-term follow-up studies that can provide valuable insights into the true durability of microsurgical treatment for UIAs.

The present study aims to address this knowledge gap by presenting a comprehensive analysis of local recurrence rates following microsurgical clipping of anterior circulation UIAs, based on a large single-center series with systematic long-term follow-up extending over 22 years.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study analyzed a prospectively maintained database of clinical and imaging data collected between April 2003 and August 2025. Patient enrollment and data acquisition, including aneurysm characteristics, operative details, and postoperative imaging findings, were performed in real time as part of routine clinical practice. A microsurgical aneurysm database was established in 2003 at Dokkyo Medical University, and long-term CTA surveillance at standardized intervals was incorporated into the institutional follow-up protocol from the outset. Clinical variables, intraoperative findings, and imaging data were recorded contemporaneously, forming the basis of the present retrospective analysis.

The present investigation consists of a retrospective analysis of this accumulated cohort, using predefined imaging criteria for recurrence detection. The surgical and imaging protocols remained identical throughout the study period. The cohort originated at Dokkyo Medical University (April 2003-March 2023) and continued seamlessly at Nasu Red Cross Hospital following the senior author's relocation in April 2023. Details of ethical approval are provided in the Ethics section.

Patient selection criteria

Surgical candidacy was determined according to the criteria described in our previous report.19) Surgery was considered only for anterior circulation UIAs in which preoperative imaging confirmed that a direct microsurgical approach could be performed safely. Specifically, surgery was undertaken only when all of the following conditions were satisfied: a favorable surgical corridor allowing adequate exposure without excessive brain retraction; the absence of adherent perforating vessels or eloquent brain involvement; and a high likelihood of achieving complete aneurysm occlusion without compromising the parent artery or perforating arteries was anticipated.

For study inclusion, patients were required to have an anterior circulation UIA confirmed by CTA or digital subtraction angiography and to be asymptomatic at the time of surgery, defined as the absence of sentinel headache, focal neurological deficits, cranial nerve dysfunction, or radiologically evident parenchymal changes attributable to the aneurysm. Exclusion criteria included posterior circulation aneurysms, symptomatic aneurysms, lesions associated with pre-existing parenchymal abnormalities on imaging, giant aneurysms measuring ≥25 mm, lesions with residual domes requiring early additional endovascular treatment, and cases lacking adequate clinical records or long-term follow-up.

During the study period, 1,040 microsurgical clipping procedures were performed in 979 patients with anterior circulation UIAs. Five patients were excluded: 2 with previously ruptured aneurysms treated by endovascular means who also had concomitant unruptured aneurysms clipped after recovery from SAH, but whose originally ruptured lesions re-ruptured during follow-up (posterior communicating artery [PCoA] and basilar artery top, 1 each), leading to death; and 3 with early postoperative neck remnants (anterior communicating artery [ACoA], 1; PCoA, 1; internal carotid [IC] artery [ICA]-C2 1) requiring additional endovascular treatment during the early postoperative period. After excluding these cases, 1,035 procedures in 974 patients remained eligible for surveillance. A further 33 patients with remote follow-up and 11 with early postoperative dropout were excluded, resulting in a final source population of 930 patients undergoing 990 procedures for 1,095 aneurysms (655 females, 275 males; mean age, 61.8 ± 10.1 years; range, 27-86 years).

For the local recurrence analysis, the cohort was further restricted to 657 patients who had reached at least 5 years of postoperative follow-up, corresponding to 700 procedures for 786 aneurysms, with delayed entry into the risk set at the 5-year timepoint. Patients with less than 5 years of follow-up at the time of data cutoff (August 2025) were classified as not yet eligible rather than excluded, ensuring that shorter follow-up duration alone did not introduce attrition bias.

Treatment selection during the study period

Treatment modality selection followed institutional practice guidelines. For anterior-circulation UIAs, 111 endovascular procedures were performed (coiling 84, flow-diverter placement 16, Woven EndoBridge [WEB] embolization 11). The majority of endovascularly treated aneurysms were paraclinoid ICA lesions with limited surgical accessibility. Six PCoA aneurysms and 8 ACoA aneurysms were treated endovascularly, together with 1 IC bifurcation lesion. In addition, 3 middle cerebral artery (MCA) aneurysms and 1 distal anterior cerebral artery (ACA) aneurysm underwent primary endovascular treatment, although these constituted a small minority of the cohort.

As a result of these institutional practices, the clipping and coiling cohorts represented largely non-overlapping anatomical subpopulations: aneurysms selected for microsurgical clipping typically had favorable surgical anatomy as defined above, whereas endovascular therapy was chosen for lesions with morphological constraints that limited safe microsurgical exposure. Because the present study focuses on the long-term durability of microsurgical clipping, detailed endovascular outcomes were not analyzed. Comparative information is available from published multicenter endovascular studies and meta-analyses.

Surgical team

The vast majority of procedures (>95%) were performed by the senior author (S.K.), a cerebrovascular neurosurgeon with extensive experience (>500 clipping cases by 2003). In a minority of cases, other staff neurosurgeons with >100 prior clipping procedures served as primary operators under S.K.'s direct supervision, with S.K. assuming full responsibility for surgical outcomes.

Surgical technique and intraoperative safety strategies

All procedures followed a standardized microsurgical protocol. Institutional practice favored a clipping-first policy for anterior circulation aneurysms, particularly MCA aneurysms approached through wide, shallow operative corridors achieving high occlusion rates.20-22) For non-MCA aneurysms, clipping was offered selectively to cases with risk profiles comparable to MCA aneurysms. Even when endovascular treatment was technically feasible, microsurgical clipping was prioritized when superior long-term durability was expected.23)

Key intraoperative elements included temporary proximal control, meticulous sharp dissection of the aneurysm neck, intraoperative indocyanine green videoangiography,24,25) and continuous motor-evoked potential (MEP) monitoring.26) MEP change was defined as ≥50% amplitude reduction from baseline persisting >1 minute despite correction of systemic factors.

All operations employed standardized brain-protective strategies, including generous cerebrospinal fluid drainage for brain relaxation, wide but minimally invasive dissection of the sylvian or interhemispheric fissure, avoidance of excessive fixed retraction, and judicious, time-limited temporary arterial occlusion. When intraoperative findings revealed unanticipated anatomical complexity or perforator involvement posing a risk to neurological function despite preoperative assessment suggesting otherwise, a safety-first strategy, defined as intentional preservation of small neck remnants (Sindou grades I-II15)) to prevent perforator injury or parent-vessel stenosis, was adopted. Small cortical vein sacrifice was minimized whenever possible.

The trans-sylvian approach was used in most cases, whereas the interhemispheric approach was employed for all distal ACA aneurysms and for ACoA aneurysms when it was anticipated to provide more reliable preservation of perforating arteries and safer dissection between the aneurysm and adjacent normal vessels than the trans-sylvian route.

Follow-up protocol and imaging assessment

Postoperative and long-term imaging follow-up were conducted under the direct supervision of the senior author throughout the study period. CTA surveillance was scheduled at 5 years (±6 months) after clipping and approximately every 5 years thereafter. Patients not yet reaching the 5-year window at data cutoff (August 2025) were classified as not yet eligible rather than excluded.

CTA acquisition

Both early postoperative and follow-up imaging assessments used standard multidetector CTA protocols on either a 64-slice scanner (Aquilion 64, Canon Medical Systems, Otawara, Japan; used 2003-2012) or a 320-slice scanner (Aquilion ONE, Canon Medical Systems; used 2013 onwards), with 0.5-mm slice thickness. Image acquisition parameters included 120-kV tube voltage, automatic tube-current modulation (approximately 200-400 mA), and a 220-mm field of view. Non-ionic iodinated contrast (Iopamiron 370, Bayer Schering Pharma, Berlin, Germany) was administered intravenously at 1.5 mL/kg at 4 mL/s, followed by a 30-mL saline flush. Images were obtained during the arterial phase using automated bolus tracking (threshold 150 Hounsfield units in the ICA).

Reconstruction and review

Images were reconstructed using multiplanar reformats (MPRs) and three-dimensional (3D) volume rendering (VR) to optimize visualization of the clip-neck interface. A dedicated workstation was used for 3D processing (Ziostation2, Ziosoft, Tokyo, Japan, from 2013 onwards); during the earlier period, reconstructions were performed on the vendor's scanner console using standard MPR and VR tools. Both source images and 3D reconstructions were independently reviewed by 2 board-certified neuroradiologists blinded to clinical outcomes and surgical details. Discordant interpretations were resolved by consensus; when consensus could not be reached, a third senior neuroradiologist adjudicated the final call. Formal inter-rater statistics (e.g., Cohen's kappa) were not calculated, and agreement is therefore reported qualitatively rather than quantitatively.

Definitions and classification

Postoperative neck remnants were classified according to the Sindou classification system:15) Grade I (remnant <50% of original neck), Grade II (remnant ≥50% of original neck), Grade III (neck plus lobular portion of dome), Grade IV (<75% of dome remnant), and Grade V (≥75% of dome remnant). Classification was performed independently by 2 board-certified neuroradiologists on multiplanar reformatted CTA images, with measurements obtained using electronic calipers on the workstation. The maximum diameter of any residual contrast-opacified space adjacent to the clip was measured in millimeters and compared with the original aneurysm neck width on preoperative imaging. Discordant classifications were resolved by consensus, with adjudication by a third neuroradiologist when necessary.

Local recurrence was defined according to previously published criteria from long-term CTA follow-up studies.6-8,14,15) Recurrence was diagnosed when a new or enlarging contrast-opacified outpouching contiguous with the clip site was observed on follow-up CTA, meeting at least 1 of the following criteria: 1) a new contrast-opacified space measuring ≥2 mm in maximum diameter on MPR images that was not present on early postoperative or prior follow-up CTA; 2) enlargement of a previously stable neck remnant by ≥2 mm in maximum diameter compared with prior imaging; or 3) appearance of a new lobular outpouching with a distinct neck at the clip-aneurysm interface, even if <2 mm, when morphologically distinct from a simple residual neck.

All measurements were performed on 0.5-mm source images and verified on orthogonal planes and 3D reconstructions. To ensure reproducibility, diameters were measured perpendicular to the parent vessel's long axis at the site of maximal opacification, and the presence of recurrence required concordant findings on both axial source images and at least 1 additional plane. Stable neck remnants without morphological change across serial CTAs were not classified as recurrences, whereas any progressive enlargement meeting the above criteria or development of a new lobular component was classified as a recurrence.

Clip orientation (parallel or perpendicular relative to the parent-vessel axis) was determined from operative records and intraoperative photographs when available. In 5 of the 9 recurrence cases, intraoperative photographs were available for review. For the remaining 4 cases, imaging data were no longer available because intraoperative photographs and CTA datasets are retained for only 5 years under institutional policy. In these cases, clip-orientation information was obtained exclusively from operative notes.

Statistical analysis

The risk set for recurrence analysis began 5 years after surgery (delayed entry). Observation time for each aneurysm accrued from the 5-year CTA time point to the earliest of recurrence detection, last evaluable CTA, or censoring.

Given the interval-censored nature of surveillance data with scheduled CTA at approximately 5-year intervals, discrete-time analysis at each surveillance time point was used as the primary analytical method for assessing cumulative recurrence rates. Cumulative recurrence-free rates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated at each surveillance time point (5, 10, 15, and 20 years) using the initial at-risk population as the denominator, with Wilson score intervals for proportions.

Kaplan-Meier analysis was also performed for sensitivity comparison, but was not used as the primary analytical method. While Kaplan-Meier analysis is widely used in the literature for aneurysm recurrence studies, the discrete-time approach more accurately reflects the actual surveillance schedule and provides more transparent estimates of recurrence risk at specific time points when surveillance imaging was actually performed.

Annualized recurrence rates per 100 patient-years and aneurysm-years were calculated with exact Poisson CIs using Byar's approximation. Location-specific annual recurrence rates were also computed based on patient-years of observation, with 95% CIs estimated using exact Poisson methods. For subgroups with 0 events, the one-sided 97.5% upper exact Poisson confidence limit for the annual rate was calculated to quantify the uncertainty associated with zero-event observations.

Risk factors for local recurrence were evaluated using univariate analyses only, as the small number of events would have yielded unstable estimates and a high risk of overfitting in multivariable models.

Delayed entry at 5 years was chosen because (1) no recurrences were observed before the 5-year surveillance CTA in our cohort, (2) early postoperative imaging has limited sensitivity for detecting biologically meaningful regrowth as opposed to immediate technical remnants, and (3) the pathophysiological processes underlying late recurrence, such as hemodynamic stress and vessel-wall remodeling, are unlikely to manifest within the first several years. Defining the risk set from the time of the first scheduled long-term CTA, therefore, more accurately represents the clinically relevant recurrence window and prevents inflation of person-time during a period with negligible observed risk.

As a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the robustness of discrete-time estimates, conventional Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed using midpoint imputation for recurrence timing. Given that the exact date of recurrence onset cannot be determined from interval-censored surveillance data, the midpoint between the last recurrence-free CTA and the CTA detecting recurrence was assigned as the event time for Kaplan-Meier analysis.

All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 30.0.0.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of both participating institutions (Dokkyo Medical University approval number: [Approval No. R-81-2J]; Nasu Red Cross Hospital approval number: [Approval No. 2025-04]). Written informed consent for surgical treatment and routine postoperative imaging surveillance was obtained from all patients.

For the retrospective use of clinical data in this research, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived by both ethics committees, and an opt-out procedure was implemented in accordance with the Japanese Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects. Patients were informed of the study through public notices at both institutions, and those who declined participation were excluded from the analysis.

Results

Patient demographics and baseline characteristics

Procedural safety and perioperative morbidity associated with the same safety-first microsurgical strategy were analyzed in a separate study focusing on early postoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and clinical outcomes.27) In summary, symptomatic ischemic events occurred in only 1.5% of procedures, and all patients achieved excellent functional outcomes (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] 0 -1) at final follow-up, with an overall MRI-detected ischemic lesion rate of 7.0%. These results confirm that the safety-first clipping approach achieves excellent radiological and clinical safety. The present study, therefore, focuses on the long-term durability of aneurysm obliteration and the risk of late local recurrence.

The study population comprised 657 patients (464 females, 70.6%) with a mean age of 61.8 ± 10.1 years (range, 27-86 years) at the time of surgery. Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. A family history of SAH was present in 220 patients (33.5%), and 32 patients (4.9%) had a personal history of SAH. Hypertension was documented in 393 patients (59.8%), defined as systolic blood pressure ≥130 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥85 mmHg on at least 2 separate occasions, or current use of antihypertensive medication regardless of blood pressure values at presentation. A smoking history within 1 year before surgery was present in 123 patients (18.7%). Multiple aneurysms were observed in 208 patients (31.7%): 160 patients had 2 aneurysms, 41 had 3, six had 4, and 1 patient had 5.

Follow-up duration and surveillance compliance

The mean follow-up duration was 10.5 ± 3.5 years on a patient basis (median, 10.0 years; interquartile range [IQR], 5.1 years; maximum, 21.8 years) and 11.7 ± 5.3 years on an aneurysm basis (median, 10.4 years; IQR, 6.0 years; maximum, 42.7 years). Total clinical observation time accumulated to 6,927.8 patient-years and 8,175.3 aneurysm-years. CTA-based radiological follow-up accounted for 5,171.1 patient-years and 7,317.9 aneurysm-years, corresponding to a mean radiological observation duration of 9.3 years per aneurysm.

Among 657 patients (700 procedures for primary target aneurysms) who reached at least 5 years of postoperative follow-up, 598 patients (91.0%) underwent CTA surveillance at the 5-year timepoint, corresponding to 639 primary target aneurysms (91.3%). Among 298 patients (311 procedures) who reached at least 10 years of follow-up, 271 patients (90.9%) completed CTA surveillance, corresponding to 282 primary target aneurysms (90.7%). Reasons for non-compliance included patient refusal (3 at 5 years, 2 additional at 10 years), renal dysfunction (37 at 5 years, 15 additional at 10 years), and contrast allergy (19 at 5 years, 10 additional at 10 years).

Local recurrence rates

During the observation period, 9 local recurrences were identified, all occurring more than 5 years after surgery. The annual recurrence rate was 0.156% per patient-year (95% CI, 0.067-0.308) and 0.111% per aneurysm-year (95% CI, 0.048-0.218).

Recurrence detection at scheduled surveillance time points is presented in Table 3. At the 5-year surveillance time point, no recurrences had been detected among 639 aneurysms undergoing CTA (100% recurrence-free rate; 95% CI, 99.4-100.0). By the 10-year time point, 6 recurrences were newly detected among 282 aneurysms undergoing surveillance, resulting in a cumulative recurrence-free rate of 99.1% (95% CI, 98.0-99.6). At the 15-year time point, 3 additional recurrences were identified among 71 aneurysms, with the cumulative recurrence-free rate declining to 98.6% (95% CI, 97.3-99.3). No new recurrences were detected among the 11 aneurysms undergoing 20-year surveillance, indicating a cumulative recurrence risk of approximately 1.4% over 15-20 years of follow-up.

Location-specific recurrence analysis

Local recurrences were distributed as follows: ICA territory (n = 4, comprising 2 at the anterior choroidal artery [AChA] and 2 at the PCoA), MCA territory (n = 4), and ACA territory (n = 1, at the ACoA). Location-specific annual recurrence rates based on patient-years of observation are summarized in Table 2. Among all sites, the AChA demonstrated the highest numerical recurrence rate (0.630% per patient-year), approximately 2.4-7.4 times higher than rates at other locations (range, 0.085%-0.262% per patient-year). The PCoA also showed a modestly elevated rate (0.262% per patient-year) and, together with the AChA, accounted for nearly half of all recurrences (4 of 9 cases) within the ICA territory. Chi-square testing comparing recurrence rates across all aneurysm locations yielded χ2 = 6.41, df = 8, p = 0.011.

SAH during follow-up

Ten cases of SAH occurred during the observation period. Only 1 case originated from a previously clipped aneurysm: a 66-year-old woman with a right PCoA aneurysm who had documented recurrence at 9.5 years post-clipping (Case 3 in Table 4). The recurrence was treated with coil embolization; however, the patient subsequently experienced re-recurrence and rupture 4 years after coiling (13.6 years after initial clipping). The remaining 9 cases comprised 4 de novo aneurysms and 5 untreated aneurysms. This single rupture event among clipped aneurysms represents a rupture rate of 0.014% per patient-year (1/6,927.8 patient-years) or 0.012% per aneurysm-year (1/8,175.3 aneurysm-years), underscoring the overall durability of microsurgical treatment. Notably, the rupture occurred after secondary endovascular treatment for recurrence, rather than from the primary clipping site.

Discussion

Principal findings

This comprehensive long-term study demonstrates that microsurgical clipping of anterior circulation UIAs provides excellent durability, with very low rates of local recurrence even when safety-prioritized surgical approaches are employed. The annual recurrence rate of 0.111%-0.156% observed in our cohort lies at the lower end of previously reported rates for mixed populations of ruptured and unruptured aneurysms,28,29) indicating that the conservative surgical approach often necessary for UIAs does not compromise long-term durability. Indeed, the 10-year cumulative risk of approximately 1%-2% observed in this study aligns with prior evidence supporting the lasting efficacy of microsurgical clipping.1)

Temporal pattern of recurrence

A particularly noteworthy finding of this study is the complete absence of recurrences within the first 5 years after surgery. This delayed onset suggests that late recurrences may reflect biological processes, such as aneurysm wall remodeling or hemodynamic stress, rather than immediate mechanical failure of clipping.6,7) Similar observations in prior studies support the view that local recurrence is not merely a technical complication but may result from progressive vascular changes occurring over time.

In our cohort, cumulative recurrence-free rates remained at 100% through the 5-year surveillance time point, then declined to 99.1% at 10 years and 98.6% at 15 years, indicating an approximate 1.4% cumulative recurrence risk over 15 years of follow-up. These findings underscore the importance of extended imaging surveillance in appropriately selected patients, particularly beyond the 10-year time point, when the majority of recurrences in our series were first detected.

Location-specific risk

Chi-square analysis demonstrated an overall variation in recurrence rates across aneurysm locations (p = 0.011), although this finding should be interpreted with caution, given the small number of recurrence events. Recurrences were more frequently observed within the ICA territory, particularly at the AChA and PCoA sites.

Although the total number of recurrence events was limited, the recurrence rate for AChA aneurysms (0.630% per patient-year) was several-fold higher than the rates observed at other locations (0.085%-0.262%). AChA and PCoA aneurysms together accounted for nearly half of all recurrences (4 of 9 cases), despite representing only 21.5% of treated aneurysms (169 of 786).

These observations raise the possibility that aneurysms arising from the ICA territory, particularly the AChA segment, may have anatomical or hemodynamic characteristics that make complete and durable occlusion more challenging. The need for preservation of the AChA and the thin-walled or branching aneurysm morphology frequently encountered at this site may contribute to this tendency, although these hypotheses require further investigation.

Given the limited number of recurrence events and the wide CIs associated with location-specific estimates, these findings should be considered exploratory and require validation in larger multicenter cohorts with standardized long-term imaging follow-up.

Mechanisms of local recurrence

Review of intraoperative recordings and postoperative imaging in the 9 recurrent cases enabled identification of 3 distinct mechanistic patterns underlying late recurrence (Figure 2). This classification provides insight into why aneurysms judged completely obliterated on early postoperative imaging may subsequently develop regrowth.

Pattern A (closure-line recurrence after parallel-oriented clipping) was observed in 2 cases, both involving MCA bifurcation aneurysms. In bifurcation-type aneurysms, clip orientation significantly influences the geometry of residual neck tissue. As described by Ishikawa et al.,30) clipping parallel to the efferent arteries necessarily results in a wider residual neck base along the closure line, whereas perpendicular clipping may leave a smaller “dog-ear” remnant. In our Pattern A cases, a thin-walled arterial segment located immediately adjacent to the clip blades was not part of the original aneurysm dome but appeared to represent a structurally abnormal transition zone at the aneurysm-parent artery interface. This portion could not be fully incorporated within the clip blades because of morphological constraints. Over time, this thin-walled segment was exposed to sustained hemodynamic stress and subsequently developed regrowth. These observations underscore that the aneurysm-parent artery junction may harbor subclinical wall abnormalities that are prone to delayed enlargement even when the primary aneurysm dome is completely obliterated.31,32)

Pattern B (recurrence from broad-based neck with residual thin-walled basal footprint) was observed in 2 cases. These aneurysms exhibited a gradually tapering, broad-based neck morphology that precluded complete incorporation of the basal thin-walled segment even with perpendicular-oriented clipping. In bifurcation aneurysms with such morphology, the curved 3D geometry of the aneurysm orifice cannot be fully addressed by a linear clip closure line.33) Furthermore, Burkhardt et al.34) noted that unintentional remnants, in contrast to intentional remnants left to preserve perforators, may consist of diseased aneurysm wall rather than normal vessel tissue. Such remnants are therefore more susceptible to progressive degeneration and subsequent regrowth.34) The regrowth observed in our Pattern B cases likely reflects this phenomenon, wherein residual thin-walled tissue at the aneurysm base continued to experience pathological remodeling under hemodynamic forces.

Pattern C (recurrence from sub-angiographic remnant preserved for perforator safety) was the most common pattern, observed in 5 cases. All occurred at locations where critical perforating arteries arise in close proximity to the aneurysm neck: AChA (n = 2), PCoA (n = 2), and ACoA (n = 1). As emphasized by Joo et al.35) in their comprehensive review, preservation of perforating arteries is paramount in aneurysm surgery because occlusion of even small-caliber perforators can result in devastating neurological deficits. In Pattern C cases, the clip was intentionally positioned to maintain patency of these perforators, necessarily leaving a microscopic neck remnant at the perforator origin. Although these remnants appeared completely obliterated on early postoperative CTA, a finding that likely reflects their being below the spatial resolution threshold of routine imaging, they contained residual neck tissue that gradually enlarged under sustained wall shear stress and ongoing hemodynamic remodeling.36,37)

The predominance of Pattern C at AChA and PCoA locations provides a mechanistic explanation for the higher location-specific recurrence rates observed at these sites. The anatomical necessity of preserving the AChA and PCoA, combined with the complex three-dimensional relationship between these perforators and the aneurysm neck, inherently limits the degree of neck obliteration achievable without risking ischemic injury. This represents a fundamental trade-off in safety-first microsurgical philosophy: accepting a higher probability of late recurrence in exchange for minimizing the risk of immediate, irreversible neurological deficit.

These 3 patterns collectively suggest that late recurrence after microsurgical clipping reflects the interaction between residual subclinical wall abnormalities, whether intentional or unintentional, sustained hemodynamic stress, and progressive vascular remodeling. This mechanism appears fundamentally distinct from early recurrence caused by clip slippage or technical failure.38) The delayed onset of all recurrences in this cohort (>5 years) supports this interpretation, aligning with emerging evidence that aneurysm wall biology, inflammation, and hemodynamic forces continue to influence vascular remodeling long after surgical intervention.39)

Neck-remnant and safety-prioritized clipping

One of the most important findings of this study is that a carefully balanced surgical strategy prioritizing neurological safety while aiming for durable occlusion can achieve excellent long-term durability. Intentional small neck remnants (Sindou Grades I-II) were deliberately left in 3.3% of cases to avoid perforator injury or parent-artery stenosis, yet no recurrences were observed in this subgroup during extended follow-up. This demonstrates that, when performed under meticulous technique and selective indication, a safety-first approach can prevent SAH while maintaining an extremely low rate of local regrowth, thus fully achieving the fundamental goal of UIA surgery: to protect the patient without causing harm.40,41) However, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence that small neck remnants protect against recurrence; rather, it reflects the limited size of the remnant subgroup and the close surveillance applied to these cases.

At the same time, all 9 recurrences in this cohort occurred in aneurysms that had been judged completely obliterated (Sindou Grade 0) on early postoperative CTA. This paradoxical observation underscores that even “perfect” clipping does not guarantee a permanent cure. The morphology of recurrent aneurysms varied widely; some were located near the original neck, others along the clip-vessel interface, but no reproducible imaging pattern could be identified. Such heterogeneity implies that late recurrence reflects multifactorial biological and hemodynamic processes, including vessel-wall remodeling and microscopic remnants below the detection threshold of routine imaging, rather than purely technical failure.

Taken together, these results reaffirm that a conservative microsurgical philosophy, emphasizing preservation of critical perforators and parent vessels, ensures durable aneurysm control with minimal morbidity, while also highlighting that long-term imaging surveillance remains essential, even after apparently complete obliteration. The combination of surgical prudence and structured follow-up represents the most reliable strategy for achieving both safety and durability in contemporary microsurgical management of UIA.

Comparison with literature

Previous studies have reported annual recurrence rates between 0.26% and 0.52% among completely clipped aneurysms, although many of these series included mixed cohorts with variable imaging schedules. Brown et al.8) reported a 1.5% recurrence rate over a mean follow-up of 9.5 years in a mixed cohort of ruptured and unruptured aneurysms, identifying incomplete clipping and younger age as significant predictors of recurrence. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Spiessberger et al.42) estimated a cumulative 20-year risk of 9.6%-22% for either recurrence or de novo aneurysm formation, underscoring the potential for very late events and the rationale for long-term surveillance. Our annual recurrence rate of 0.111%-0.156% lies at the lower end of these ranges, likely reflecting both the favorable case selection inherent in an unruptured anterior circulation cohort and the technical consistency associated with a single-surgeon series. Advances in intraoperative monitoring, high-resolution microscopy, and intraoperative verification of complete obliteration may also contribute to improved long-term durability. The absence of early recurrences (within 5 years) in our cohort contrasts with earlier reports and may reflect refinements in surgical technique and case selection.

In contrast, long-term endovascular series have reported substantially higher recurrence and retreatment rates. A recent meta-analysis restricted to unruptured aneurysms by Krag et al.43) demonstrated pooled long-term recanalization and retreatment rates of 20.8% and 10.3%, respectively, with delayed rupture occurring in 0.6% of cases. Rizvi et al.44) similarly reported a rupture rate of 0.48 per 100 patient-years and a retreatment rate of approximately 5% after endovascular treatment of unruptured aneurysms. Location-specific comparisons also favor microsurgical clipping in several anatomical subgroups: Sattari et al.45) found higher complete occlusion and lower retreatment rates after clipping of ACoA aneurysms, whereas Sturiale et al.22) reported similar findings for MCA aneurysms in a multicenter Italian cohort. More recently, Goertz et al.46) compared WEB embolization with microsurgical clipping for unruptured anterior circulation aneurysms, showing comparable safety profiles but higher retreatment rates in the WEB group. Against this background, our estimated 10-year recurrence risk of approximately 1%-2% highlights the durable efficacy of microsurgical clipping for unruptured anterior circulation aneurysms.

In Japan, the Unruptured Cerebral Aneurysm Study of Japan (UCAS Japan) provided key data on the natural history of unruptured aneurysms.12) Our findings extend this evidence by addressing long-term post-clipping durability, an aspect not evaluated in UCAS Japan, and are broadly consistent with current Japanese guidelines,47) which recommend individualized long-term follow-up based on aneurysm morphology, completeness of obliteration, and patient-specific risk factors.

Clinical implications

The complete absence of recurrences within the first 5 years has direct implications for imaging surveillance strategies. In this cohort of carefully selected low-risk patients with complete aneurysm obliteration, routine contrast-enhanced CTA during the early postoperative years may have limited yield for detecting late recurrence. However, this should not be interpreted as evidence to broadly defer early imaging, as these findings reflect a single surgical cohort with few recurrence events. Earlier CTA may remain appropriate in patients with higher-risk features such as incomplete obliteration, complex aneurysm morphology, or specific anatomical considerations.

Although numerically higher recurrence rates were observed in aneurysms arising from the ICA territory, particularly at the AChA and PCoA segments, these observations are exploratory and should be interpreted with caution, given the small number of events and wide CIs.

It should be emphasized that our findings apply only to small, intentionally preserved neck remnants (Sindou Grades I-II). Larger residual necks or dome remnants (Sindou Grades III-V) were not represented in this cohort and may warrant earlier or more frequent surveillance based on existing literature.

Finally, the finding that only 1 of 10 SAH events during follow-up originated from a previously clipped aneurysm highlights the importance of monitoring for de novo aneurysm formation and growth of untreated lesions, which may pose greater long-term risks than local recurrence. These results align with current AHA/ASA guidelines,3) which recommend individualized follow-up strategies based on aneurysm characteristics and patient-specific risk factors.

These findings provide a benchmark for the long-term durability achievable under optimal microsurgical conditions. However, validation in multicenter cohorts with diverse surgical teams is required before these results can be generalized to broader clinical practice.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this study represents a single-center, predominantly single-surgeon series (>95% of procedures performed by S.K.), which effectively constitutes a “personal series” and carries inherent limitations in generalizability. Although this design ensured exceptional technical consistency and minimized inter-operator variability, the very low recurrence rates observed may reflect a highly specialized surgical environment characterized by extensive prior microsurgical experience (>500 clipping cases before study initiation) and rigorous case selection. These outcomes may not be reproducible in settings with different surgical expertise, case volumes, or practice patterns and, therefore, should be interpreted as reflecting results achievable under optimal conditions rather than expected outcomes across all neurosurgical centers.

In addition, the distribution of aneurysm locations within the clipping cohort reflects intentional case selection rather than the full spectrum of UIAs treated during the study period. Endovascular treatment at our institution was preferentially applied to anatomically distinct lesions, primarily paraclinoid ICA aneurysms with limited microsurgical accessibility, which resulted in minimal overlap between treatment populations. Consequently, the recurrence rates reported here represent outcomes for a surgically selected subset of anterior circulation UIAs deemed favorable for microsurgical clipping and should not be generalized to all UIAs irrespective of treatment modality.

Second, the study lacks comparison with contemporary endovascular treatment outcomes, precluding conclusions regarding the relative long-term durability of surgical versus endovascular approaches.

Third, cognitive function was not systematically assessed, limiting our ability to evaluate subtle functional or quality-of-life outcomes.

The relatively small number of recurrence events (n = 9) imposed substantial constraints on the statistical power of all subgroup analyses, particularly those comparing recurrence risk across aneurysm locations. Even a few undetected recurrences among patients lost to follow-up could have meaningfully altered the reported incidence rates, raising the possibility that the true recurrence risk may be underestimated. Though chi-square analysis revealed statistically significant location-dependent differences (P = 0.011), the precision of site-specific rate estimates remains limited by wide CIs. Consequently, larger multicenter cohorts with sufficient event numbers will be required to validate the magnitude of location-specific recurrence risks and to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Another methodological consideration relates to the interval-censored nature of recurrence detection. Given that surveillance was performed at approximately 5-year intervals, the exact timing of recurrence onset between surveillance time points cannot be determined. Although many prior studies have employed Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with midpoint imputation for event timing, we chose discrete-time analysis as our primary method because it more transparently reflects the actual surveillance schedule and does not assume continuous observation. Sensitivity analyses using Kaplan-Meier methods yielded results consistent with our discrete-time estimates, supporting the robustness of our conclusions regarding long-term recurrence risk.

Nevertheless, the possibility of surveillance bias cannot be excluded because patients with symptoms or vascular risk factors may have undergone earlier or more frequent imaging, potentially increasing recurrence detection compared with asymptomatic individuals. Similarly, the strict inclusion criteria requiring long-term CTA follow-up and high procedural safety standards may have introduced selection bias, as patients with more complex aneurysms or higher perioperative risk were likely underrepresented.

Although overall follow-up adherence was high, approximately 10% of eligible patients did not undergo CTA at the scheduled intervals. This degree of attrition could have introduced bias if recurrence events occurred preferentially among patients lost to follow-up. Sensitivity analyses assuming a worst-case scenario, in which all missing patients experienced recurrence, resulted in a higher estimated recurrence rate but did not fundamentally alter the temporal pattern observed in this study, suggesting that our primary conclusions remain robust.

Finally, because remnant-bearing aneurysms were few, closely monitored, and often underwent early retreatment, late recurrences in that subgroup may have been censored or excluded by the ≥5-year delayed-entry design. Accordingly, the zero-event estimate in the remnant group should be interpreted together with its one-sided 95% upper confidence bound and should not be taken as evidence that small neck remnants confer protection against late recurrence. Furthermore, microscopic remnants undetectable on early CTA may have contributed to some late recurrences classified as occurring in completely obliterated aneurysms. Therefore, our findings should not be generalized to larger residual necks or dome remnants (Sindou Grades III-V), which were not represented in this cohort and may carry a substantially higher risk of regrowth.

Future directions

Several questions remain for future investigation. A longer follow-up will be needed to determine whether recurrence risk continues to rise linearly or eventually plateaus. Predictive models incorporating patient- and aneurysm-specific factors may help identify patients requiring more intensive surveillance, whereas comparative studies with contemporary endovascular techniques are needed to guide treatment selection for UIAs.

Advanced imaging approaches, including high-resolution vessel wall imaging, may refine recurrence risk prediction and surveillance strategies. The cost-effectiveness of different imaging schedules also warrants formal evaluation, given the very low recurrence rates demonstrated in this study.

Future imaging strategies should explore the feasibility of advanced non-contrast magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) techniques that minimize metallic clip artifacts while maintaining high diagnostic sensitivity for clinically significant local recurrence. Recent studies have highlighted the potential of Pointwise Encoding Time Reduction with Radial Acquisition (PETRA)-MRA, which significantly improves visualization of vessels adjacent to clips compared with conventional Time-of-Flight (TOF)-MRA.48) Additionally, metal artifact reduction methods such as Slice Encoding for Metal Artifact Correction (SEMAC) and View Angle Tilting (VAT)-based Wide-band Artifact Reduction Pulse Turbo Spin Echo (WARP TSE) sequences have demonstrated marked artifact suppression, reducing artifact size by up to ～90% while preserving image quality.49) These advances suggest a future where MRA could replace CTA for long-term surveillance, eliminating radiation and contrast risks while improving accessibility and cost-effectiveness.

Conclusions

Microsurgical clipping of anterior circulation UIAs demonstrates excellent long-term durability, with annual local recurrence rates of 0.111%-0.156%. It should be emphasized, however, that aneurysms judged to have no neck remnant on early postoperative imaging, which corresponds to complete clipping under standard radiographic criteria, may still develop late recurrence. This indicates that radiographic completeness does not necessarily guarantee a permanent cure. The complete absence of recurrences within 5 years after surgery, followed by the gradual emergence of events beyond 10 years, supports a biological rather than technical origin for late regrowth. Location-specific analysis revealed disproportionately higher recurrence rates at the AChA and PCoA segments, likely reflecting the anatomical necessity of preserving critical perforators at these sites.

These findings support microsurgical clipping as a durable and effective treatment option for appropriately selected UIAs, with long-term outcomes that justify the safety-first approach frequently required in anterior circulation aneurysm surgery. Continued surveillance beyond 10 years remains important, particularly for aneurysms at the AChA and PCoA locations, where perforator-preserving clipping may leave sub-angiographic remnants susceptible to delayed enlargement.
