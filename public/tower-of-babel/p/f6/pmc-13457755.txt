Quantitative assessment of prostate cancer using an individualized apparent diffusion coefficient intensity scale: a proposal
Authors: Michele Scialpi, Giuseppe Nazareno Antogiovanni, Francesco Bianconi, Barbara Palumbo, Guido Bellezza, Martina Mandarano, Saber Ammar, Giovanni Battista Scalera, Refky Nicola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this retrospective study, the diagnostic performance of an individualized apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) signal intensity (SI) scale for differentiating prostate cancer (PCa) from benign simplified Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System (S-PI-RADS) 4 lesions detected on biparametric magnetic resonance imaging (bpMRI) was evaluated. A total of 102 patients with biopsy-confirmed PCa and 79 patients with benign lesions (inflammation, HGPIN, ASAP) presenting as S-PI-RADS 4 were included. An individualized ADC SI scale was generated for each patient using maximum and minimum ADC values from the bladder and femoral head, respectively. The midpoint (ADC_mean) and the percentage difference (PD) between ADC_mean and lesion intensity (ADC_lesion) were calculated. Differences and diagnostic performance were assessed using Mann-Whitney U tests and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. PCa lesions demonstrated significantly lower ADC values and higher PDs compared with benign lesions (P<0.001). ROC analysis of PD yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.798. Using a PD cut-off of 14.67%, sensitivity and specificity for detecting PCa were 73.5% and 94.9%, respectively. Intra-reader repeatability was high [intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) 0.92–0.94], and PD values remained robust across variations in ADC fitting. The individualized ADC SI scale provides a patient-specific quantitative measure that effectively distinguishes malignant from benign S-PI-RADS 4 lesions. This approach complements visual bpMRI assessment, demonstrates high specificity, and has the potential to reduce unnecessary biopsies. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate its clinical utility.

Introduction

Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) plays a central role in prostate cancer (PCa) detection, as it highlights areas of restricted water diffusion associated with increased tumor cellularity and reduced extracellular space (1,2). Nevertheless, diffusion restriction is not specific to malignancy: benign conditions such as chronic prostatitis, stromal hyperplasia, and transition zone (TZ) nodules may also demonstrate low apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values (3). This overlap represents a well-recognized limitation of prostate magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and contributes to diagnostic uncertainty and potentially unnecessary biopsies.

The Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) has standardized prostate MRI interpretation and improved detection of clinically significant PCa; however, its diagnostic performance—particularly for PI-RADS category 4 lesions—remains reader-dependent and variable (3). The simplified PI-RADS (S-PI-RADS) based on bpMRI may represent a valid alternative to PI-RADS v2.1 (4). This system stratifies lesions into four categories, each linked to a defined management pathway. Although category 4 lesions are considered highly clinically significant and generally warrant biopsy, histopathology shows that approximately 22.9% of these lesions are benign, highlighting the potential for unnecessary biopsies (4).

Increasing evidence suggests that quantitative MRI biomarkers, including ADC-based metrics, may enhance diagnostic reliability, reduce interobserver variability, and support more objective lesion characterization (5).

Several normalization and scaling strategies have been proposed to overcome the known variability of absolute ADC values across scanners, protocols, and patients (6-9). Based on this concept, we propose a novel quantitative approach based on an individualized ADC signal intensity (SI) scale. This method complements standard visual assessment by contextualizing lesion ADC values within a patient-specific reference range, potentially improving differentiation between malignant and benign S-PI-RADS 4 lesions.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective study was conducted at the Division of Radiology I, Santa Maria della Misericordia Hospital, University of Perugia, Perugia, Italy. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Perugia University Hospital. Informed consent was waived in this retrospective study. Our retrospective study included 186 patients who underwent biparametric MRI (bpMRI; multiplanar T2-TSE, axial T1-GRE THRIVE, and DWI EPI with b-values of 0, 500, 1,000, and 1,500 s/mm2, and ADC map reconstruction) at 3.0 Tesla (Philips Achieva, Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) between April 2022 and May 2025 and had histopathological confirmation of prostate tissue.

In patients with multifocal lesions, the lesion with the highest Gleason score was selected for analysis. All evaluated lesions were visually classified as S-PI-RADS category 4 according to an S-PI-RADS system applied to bpMRI (4). Inclusion criteria were: (I) high-quality MRI acquisition; (II) histological confirmation obtained within 3 weeks after MRI; and (III) absence of prior pelvic treatment. Five patients were excluded due to insufficient image quality or previous prostate interventions. Malignant lesions were defined as biopsy-proven PCa with Gleason grading, while benign lesions included inflammation, high-grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia (HGPIN), and atypical small acinar proliferation (ASAP).

The final study population consisted of 181 patients, comprising 79 patients (mean age 68.7±6.8 years) with lesions classified as S-PI-RADS 4 on MRI but histologically confirmed as benign (inflammation, high-grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia, or atypical small acinar proliferation; Group 1), and 102 patients (mean age 73.0±7.9 years) with biopsy-proven PCa (peripheral zone n=53, transition zone n=49; Group 2).

Image analysis

Quantitative measurements were performed on markedly hypointense lesions identified on ADC maps (S-PI-RADS category 4). DICOM images were transferred to a PACS workstation (Agfa HealthCare Enterprise Imaging, AGFA EI, Morstel, Belgium). Two readers (M.S. and G.N.A., with 15 and 1 year of experience in 3.0T prostate MRI, respectively) reviewed all cases in consensus, blinded to clinical and histopathological data.

For each patient, an individualized ADC SI scale was constructed. The maximum ADC SI (ADC_max) was obtained using a circular region of interest (ROI) placed in the urinary bladder, while the minimum ADC value (ADC_min) was set to zero, corresponding to signal void in the femoral head. Repeatability and sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the stability of ADC reference measurements, an intra-reader repeatability analysis was conducted on a random subset of patients (n=30). The same reader repeated ROI placement for ADC_max (bladder) and ADC_min (femoral head) in two separate sessions. Repeatability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and repeatability coefficient (RC).

In addition, a sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate how variability in ADC reference values propagates into PD measurements. Simulated variations (±5%) were applied to ADC_max and ADC_min, and the resulting changes in PD values and receiver operating characteristic (ROC)-derived cut-offs were analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Differences in ADC values and PD between benign and malignant lesions were assessed using the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test. Diagnostic performance of PD was evaluated using ROC curve analysis. The optimal cut-off value was determined using the Youden index. Area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity were reported. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Mean ADC values, PDs, and ROC curve results for benign and malignant lesions are summarized in Figure 2. Malignant lesions demonstrated significantly lower ADC values compared with non-malignant lesions (1,327.05 vs. 1,735.49 SI; P<0.001). Conventional ADC values were also significantly different between malignant and benign lesions (P<0.001), confirming their known diagnostic relevance. Conversely, PD values were significantly higher in PCa compared with benign lesions (24.87%±15.24% vs. 11.40%±6.69%; P<0.001). ROC analysis of PD yielded an AUC of 0.798, indicating good discriminative performance. The optimal PD cut-off value of 14.67% provided a sensitivity of 73.5% and a specificity of 94.9% for distinguishing malignant from benign S-PI-RADS 4 lesions.

The intra-reader repeatability analysis demonstrated high stability of ADC reference measurements. ICC values were 0.94 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.89–0.97] for bladder ADC and 0.92 (95% CI: 0.86–0.96) for femoral head ADC. In the sensitivity analysis, simulated variations in ADC reference values resulted in limited changes in PD (mean ΔPD =2.1%). The ROC-derived cut-off remained stable within a narrow range (13.8–15.3%), indicating that the PD parameter is relatively robust to realistic variability in scale definition.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that an individualized ADC SI scale can effectively differentiate malignant from benign S-PI-RADS 4 prostate lesions, achieving good overall accuracy and particularly high specificity. These findings are consistent with prior literature showing that PCa exhibits lower ADC values due to increased cellular density and restricted diffusion, while benign inflammatory or hyperplastic conditions tend to retain higher ADC values despite visual diffusion restriction (10).

Several normalization and scaling approaches for ADC quantification have previously been proposed, including lesion-to-normal tissue ratios (e.g., normalized ADC ratios based on reference tissues) and intensity standardization methods such as Z-score normalization to reduce scanner and protocol variability in prostate multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) (6,7). These strategies aim to address the well-known variability of absolute ADC values related to scanner hardware, acquisition parameters, and post-processing software. In this context, our individualized ADC SI scale represents a complementary and conceptually simple approach, using patient-specific internal references to contextualize lesion SI rather than relying on absolute ADC thresholds. Several normalization approaches based on ADC ratios between lesions and normal-appearing prostate tissue have demonstrated improved diagnostic performance and reproducibility (11,12). However, these methods may be influenced by the presence of occult patologies or heterogeneity within reference tissue.

While ADC ratio approaches are patient-specific, our proposed individualized ADC SI scale differs conceptually by using reference-free tissue selection (free water in the bladder and signal void in bone), constructing a continuous patient-specific intensity scale, and employing a midpoint-based metric (PD) less sensitive to local tissue variability, and should therefore considered complementary rather than alternative to previously established ADC ratio methods (13-15).

Importantly, ADC measurements may be influenced by T2 relaxation properties, as suggested by recent studies highlighting T2-weighted signal effects and multi-compartment tissue behavior (16-18). While our patient-specific normalization may partially mitigate these effects, differences in intrinsic T2 across tissues suggest residual biases may remain. The PD metric should thus be considered a composite biomarker reflecting both diffusion restriction and underlying tissue relaxation properties.

From a clinical perspective, the proposed method has potential implications for current diagnostic pathways. S-PI-RADS 4 lesions frequently prompt biopsy, yet a substantial proportion prove to be benign or clinically insignificant. The selected PD threshold, derived using the Youden index, reflects a balance between sensitivity and specificity. However, at this cut-off, approximately 26.5% of cancers would not be detected if the PD parameter were used in isolation. For this reason, the proposed metric should not be considered a standalone diagnostic tool, but rather a complementary parameter to PI-RADS assessment. The relatively high false-negative rate must be interpreted within the context of patient management. Patients are not excluded from follow-up, but remain within a structured clinical pathway that includes PSA monitoring, clinical evaluation, and repeat imaging when indicated. Importantly, PD thresholds may be adjusted according to clinical priorities. In an additional analysis, lowering the PD cut-off to 10% increased sensitivity to 88.2%, at the expense of reduced specificity (72.1%). This flexibility may support different diagnostic strategies, such as rule-in versus rule-out approaches. Accordingly, the proposed method should not be used to exclude PCa, but rather to refine risk stratification within PI-RADS 4 lesions.

Additional analyses performed in this study demonstrated high intra-reader repeatability for ADC reference measurements, with excellent ICC values for both bladder and femoral head ROIs. Furthermore, sensitivity analysis showed limited propagation of variability into PD values, supporting the methodological robustness of the proposed approach.

A qualitative assessment suggested that false-negative cases were more frequently associated with lower Gleason scores, potentially reflecting less pronounced diffusion restriction in less aggressive tumors. However, a detailed subgroup analysis was limited by sample size and warrants further investigation.

Our study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design and single-center cohort limit the generalizability of the results and introduce potential selection bias. Second, quantitative ADC measurements are known to vary depending on scanner manufacturer, field strength, acquisition protocols, and post-processing techniques. While the proposed method may mitigate some of this variability through internal normalization, its reproducibility across different clinical settings remains to be formally established and requires validation in multi-center and multi-vendor studies.

Our proposed method is expected to be relatively robust to variations in ADC fitting. In this study, ADC maps were derived from four b-values, which we consider to provide a more robust and representative basis for the intended analysis. Accordingly, we do not anticipate substantial differences in the results compared with approaches using three or two b-values. Although conventional ADC values remain a well-established quantitative biomarker, the proposed PD parameter demonstrated particularly high specificity, suggesting a potential role in reducing false-positive findings within PI-RADS 4 lesions. However, this study was not designed as a direct comparative analysis between ADC metrics, and further prospective studies are required.

From a methodological standpoint, the proposed individualized ADC SI scale complements existing quantitative approaches, providing a standardized relative measure of lesion SI while minimizing reliance on subjective tissue selection. Its clinical integration with PI-RADS visual assessment offers a more robust framework for lesion characterization and personalized decision-making.

In conclusion, our results suggest that an individualized ADC SI scale provides meaningful quantitative information that complements visual bpMRI assessment. With further validation, this approach may contribute to more accurate, reproducible, and clinically actionable PCa imaging, ultimately reducing unnecessary biopsies while maintaining diagnostic safety.
