Regulatory mechanism of Angelica sinensis via the AMPK pathway in laying hens: against fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome
Authors: Fanlin Wu, Peng Ji, Xiaohu Wu, Feng Yang, Yayuan Yang, Yanan Lv, Changzhen Wang, Sanye Jier, Pengcheng Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome (FLHS) is considered as a “silent killer” of egg production. 120 laying hens were randomly divided into the control group (C), the model group (M) and the different intervention groups (ASH and ASL) of Angelica sinensis (AS). FLHS model in laying hens was built by feeding high-fat, high-protein diet and giving β-estradiol. The intervention effect and mechanism of AS was evaluated through AMPK pathway by Real-time quantitative PCR and Western blotting. Twenty-one chemical components in AS were identified and the contents were calculated. TG, TC, ALT in liver tissue and serum, and the liver index decreased after AS intervention. Additionally, IgG in ASH significantly increased (P < 0.05) compared with Group M. HE, Oil red and electron microscopy results showed that the FLHS model was successfully established, and AS could intervene FLHS.SOD and GSH in liver tissue of laying hens with FLHS increased after AS intervention. MDA, IL-6 and TNF-α in liver tissue of laying hens with FLHS decreased after AS intervention. The results of liver non-target lipidomics showed the occurrence of FLHS in laying hens was closely related to primary bile acid biosynthesis, bile secretion, and steroid hormone biosynthesis, and the related metabolism was disturbed after AS intervention. Beclin1, ULK1, CPT1, p-AMPK/AMPK and p-ACC protein expression in Group M were extremely significantly lower than Group C (P < 0.01), with an extremely significant increase in SREBP 1c protein expression (P < 0.01). After AS intervention, the protein expression of Beclin1, ULK1, CPT1, p-AMPK/AMPK and p-ACC extremely significantly increased (P < 0.01), and SREBP 1c extremely significantly decreased (P < 0.01). These results suggested that AS might regulate lipid deposition of FLHS in laying hens by modulating AMPK pathway. These findings provide a basis for elucidating the intervention mechanisms of AS on laying hens with FLHS.

Introduction

Fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome (FLHS) is a metabolic disease that primarily affects caged laying hens. Clinically, it is characterized by egg production reducing, hepatic steatosis, inflammatory response, liver rupture, hemorrhaging and sudden death. Based on the previous research, FLHS has a very high incidence in commercial, cage-reared chicken operations, accounting for 40%−70% of hen mortality (Tan et al., 2021; Gretarsson et al., 2023), bringing a huge economical impact to the commercial egg chicken industry (Shini et al., 2019; Yaqoob et al., 2024). It is considered as the “silent killer” of the laying hens industry.

Genetic (Feng et al., 2021), nutritional (Rozenboim et al., 2016), management (Mench et al., 2011), environmental (Lee et al., 1975) and endocrine factors (Xing et al., 2020) are all associated with the occurrence of FLHS in laying hens. As the research progress in this area, the “multiple hit theory”, which considered as the combined effects of fat deposition, insulin resistance, hormones, nutritional factors, inflammatory pathways and gut microbiota, had emerged as a more satisfactory explanation for the pathogenesis of FLHS (Wolford et al., 1974; Tilg et al., 2010). Current research suggested that the AMPK signaling pathway, also known as the “metabolic master switch”, played a pivotal role in the development of FLHS in laying hens. It primarily exerted the effects by influencing lipid metabolism (Zhang et al., 2021), autophagy (Tu et al., 2024), mitochondrial homeostasis (San et al., 2023), oxidative stress and inflammatory responses (Yao et al., 2022) (Fig. 1). The activity of AMPK is regulated by the concentration of AMP/ATP. Excessive AMP can activate AMPK through phosphorylation by the upstream kinase CAMKK2, primarily via LKB1 in poultry. Excessive AMP also binds directly to the Cystathionine β-synthase domain of the AMPK-γ subunit, activating AMPK activity. AMPK activation can catalyse the phosphorylation of downstream substrates, including mTOR, SIRT1, Nrf2 and NF-κB (Ma et al., 2023), thereby regulating multiple signalling pathways. Numerous studies have found that the pathogenesis of FLHS was similar to the metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease. Excessive lipid deposition in the liver was a common pathological feature of both conditions (San et al., 2023), and laying hens with FLHS can serve as an ideal model for studying metabolic associated fatty liver disease (Tsai et al., 2017; Hamid et al., 2019).Fig. 1The relationship between AMPK signaling pathway and FLHS in laying hens.Fig 1 dummy alt text

A large amount of data shows that plant/herbal extracts have potential therapeutic effects on some diseases in poultry that seriously affect economic benefits, and can improve some performance parameters (Gul et al., 2024). Acanthopanax senticosus is a well-known, highly effective traditional Chinese herbal medicine. Research has found that polysaccharides extracted from Acanthopanax senticosus as additives in broiler feed improved growth performance, immune status, antioxidant capacity and stimulated the growth of beneficial gut bacteria in broiler chickens (Long et al., 2021). Veratric acid, a phenolic acid compound extracted from the Chinese herbal plant “Lonicera japonica”, is renowned for its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. The study found that veratric acid could reduced abdominal fat accumulation in broilers, improved blood biochemical indicators, and significantly lowered the mRNA expression levels of SREBP 1c, FAS, and ACC involved in fatty acid synthesis in the broiler liver (Pan et al., 2024).

According to the traditional Chinese veterinary medicine theory, blood stasis in the liver is one of the primary pathomechanisms of FLHS in laying hens. Angelica sinensis (AS), an important local medicinal herb in Gansu Province, is associated with the liver, heart and spleen meridians. AS can nourish and activate blood circulation, protect the liver, reduce blood lipids and protect the vascular endothelium (Wang et al., 2015; Chen et al., 2022). Throughout history, traditional remedies have demonstrated the effectiveness of AS in draining, clearing, regulating, softening, warming, nourishing, and consolidating the liver. This makes AS a promising candidate for preventing and treating FLHS in laying hens. The role of AS in alleviating FLHS in laying hens, and whether it regulates hepatic lipid deposition by intervening in the AMPK pathway, remain to be elucidated. FLHS model in laying hens was built by feeding a high-fat, high-protein diet and giving β-estradiol in this study. In order to investigate the intervention effect of AS in laying hens with FLHS, the changes in liver histology, liver function indicators, immunological indicators, oxidative stress indicators and inflammatory factors of FLHS hens after AS intervention were analyzed. The occurrence mechanism of FLHS in laying hens and intervention mechanism of AS through AMPK pathway were studied by Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) and Western blotting.

Materials and methods

Drug preparation

AS was purchased from Lanzhou Huanghe Traditional Chinese Medicine Market Management Co., Ltd and identified as Danggui from Minxian County. 100 g of AS was grinded into a fine powder. 1,000 mL of distilled water was added and leaved to soak for 30 minutes. The condensation reflux method was used to extract the entire solution for 2 hours. The water extract was filtered and collected. This process was repeated twice more. The obtained water extracts were mixed, vacuum concentrated and freeze-dried. The dosage was proposed based on the clinical dosage of AS for chickens as specified in the 2020 edition of the Pharmacopoeia of the People's Republic of China on Veterinary Medicine. The yield of AS water extract was calculated to be 50%.

LC-MS/MS analysis of AS

The UHPLC-Q Exactive system (Thermo Fisher) was applied for this LC-MS analysis. Chromatographic conditions: The chromatographic column was ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.7 µm; Waters, Milford, USA); Mobile phase A was 2% acetonitrile water (containing 0.1% formic acid), mobile phase B was acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid), injection volume was 3 μL, and column temperature was 40 °C. Mass spectrum conditions: sample was ionized by electric spray, spray voltage: positive mode: 3500 V, negative mode: 3000 V; scanning range: 70-1050 m/z; resolution: 17500 MS2. The semi quantitative internal standard (L-2-chlorophenylalanine) method was used to calculate the content of each component.

Animals and treatments

All the animal experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs and the Lanzhou Institute of Husbandry and Pharmaceutical Sciences of Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Lanzhou, China) with the Animal experiment welfare ethics number of 2024-63. One hundred and twenty healthy 400 days old Hailan brown laying hens (Deqingyuan, Gansu) weighing 2,000 ± 50 g were raised at the Dawa Mountain Experimental Base of the Lanzhou Institute of Husbandry and Pharmaceutical Sciences of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. The laying hens were given free access to food and water. The environmental temperature was maintained at 25 ± 3°C with a humidity level of 65% ± 10%. A lighting regimen of 16 hours of light and 8 hours of darkness was employed. Following a seven-day adaptation period, the laying hens were randomly divided into the control group (C), the model group (M), the group receiving a low dose of AS (ASL), and the group receiving a high dose of AS (ASH). Each group comprised thirty laying hens (three replicates). The control group was fed a corn-soybean meal-based diet, and the composition of which was shown in Table 1. The other groups were received a high-energy, high-protein diet consisting of the basal diet plus soybean oil. They were also given three times of injections of 7.5 mg/kg body weight of β-estradiol dissolved in corn oil, administered every four days. From the first day of the experiment, the laying hens in the ASL and ASH groups were respectively fed a low dose (0.5 g/kg BW) and a high dose (1 g/kg BW) of AS for 28 consecutive days. Sample collection

At the end of the experiment, blood samples were collected from the saphenous vein in the chicken wing and placed in a vacuum coagulation tube. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 4°C and stored at −20°C. The laying hens were weighed, and then 50 mg/kg of sodium pentobarbital was administered via intraperitoneal injection to anesthetize them. A postmortem examination was then performed and the liver was isolated to calculate the liver index (liver weight/body weight × 100%). The left lobe of the liver from each group was placed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution and electron microscope fixative for histological observation, and the remaining liver tissue was stored in liquid nitrogen for biochemical analysis to determine the relative expression levels of liver-related mRNA and protein.

Serum index tests

TG, TC and ALT levels were measured using the IDEXX Catalyst One fully automatic animal specific biochemical analyzer. IgA, IgM and IgG levels were measured using the Shanghai mlbio ELISA kit.

Liver index tests

The frozen liver tissue was ground into a homogenate with pre-cooled PBS, which was then centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 10 minutes, then the supernatant was collected. TG, TC and ALT levels were measured using the Nanjing Jiancheng test kit. MDA, SOD, GSH, TNF-α and IL-6 levels were measured using the Shanghai mlbio ELISA kit.

H&E staining

Liver tissue was collected from the same location in the left lobe. The tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, then dehydrated, embedded, and sectioned into 5 μm slices. Then, the slices were dewaxed, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and examined under a microscope.

Oil Red O staining

The liver tissue was fixed by OCT embedding and continuously sliced using a constant temperature frozen slicer (Leica, Germany). The slices were fixed with formaldehyde, stained with Oil Red O, and counterstained with hematoxylin. The slices were differentiated with hydrochloric acid ethanol and treated with anti-blue before being sealed with glycerol. Then, they were observed and recorded under an optical microscope, and performed quantitative analysis of lipid droplets.

Electron microscopy observation

A piece of liver tissue measuring approximately 1 mm³ was cut and fixed in electron microscope fixative at 4°C for seven days. It was fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide at pH 7.4 and 4°C for 24 hours. It was then dehydrated using a gradient of alcohol and acetone. Next, an equal volume of 100% acetone with an embedding agent was mixed and leaved to soak for one hour. The tissue block was embedded in the epoxy resin and sliced into sections of 50-60 µm thick using an ultramicrotome. The slices were mounted on small copper grids and stained with a mixture of uranium acetate and lead citrate. Finally, the stained sections were observed under an electron microscope. When conducting histological evaluation, the principle of sample numbering encryption was adopted, and the paraffin blocks and sections of chicken liver were uniformly and randomly numbered by experimental personnel who did not participate in tissue scoring. Then, the sections were uniformly prepared, and the histological evaluator did not know the grouping of chicken liver samples.

Non-target lipidomics detection

The non-target lipidomics testing of liver in laying hens was conducted by Beijing Baimaike Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The raw data collected using MassLynx V4.2 was subjected to peak extraction, peak alignment, and other data processing operations using Progenesis QI software. Identification was carried out based on the online database of Progenesis QI software and the self built database of Baimaike, while theoretical fragment identification was performed. The mass number deviation was within 100 ppm.

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

The Trizol method was employed to extract total RNA from chicken liver tissue. The quality and concentration of the total RNA were then determined using a micro-nucleic acid analyser (NanoDrop 2000). The RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA using an Evo M-MLV reverse transcription premix kit (AG). Primers were designed and synthesised by Beijing Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Table 2). Real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR (Thermo Fisher) was used to detect the mRNA levels of AMPK, mTOR, ULK1, Beclin1, SREBF1, ACC and CPT1A with β-actin as the internal control. Western blotting

Liver tissue samples were obtained from each group of laying hens and a high-efficiency RIPA tissue/cell lysis buffer (Solarbio, Beijing) was used to extract the total protein. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 4°C for 10 minutes at 12,000 g, after which the protein concentration was measured. The sample was denatured by boiling. Then SDS-PAGE electrophoresis was performed and the gel was immediately transferred to a PVDF membrane. The PVDF membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk powder and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against AMPK, SREBP 1c, p-ACC (Proteintech, Wuhan), p-AMPK, Beclin1, mTOR, ULK1 and CPT1 (Servicebio, Wuhan). The secondary antibody (Servicebio, Wuhan) was incubated at room temperature for one hour. Then, the PVDF membrane was washed three times with TBST containing 0.1% Tween-20 for ten minutes each time. The chemiluminescence reagent ECL (Yoche, Shanghai) was added to observe the protein bands. Finally, Image J software was used to calculate the protein expression level of each group.

Data processing

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to evaluate the normality of data distribution, and Levene’s test was used to assess the homogeneity of variance. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons among multiple groups, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test as post hoc analysis. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). P < 0.05 indicates significant differences between groups, while P < 0.01 indicates extremely significant differences between groups.

Discussion

In the large-scale chicken farming model, in order to pursue high yield and efficiency, farms generally adopt a high-fat and high protein diet feeding mode, which easily disrupts the lipid metabolism homeostasis of laying hens and induces a high incidence of FLHS in laying hens. This not only significantly reduces the production performance of laying hens, increases the risk of mass mortality and breeding economic losses, but also may indirectly bury egg safety hazards due to metabolic abnormalities and abuse of exogenous substances. Screening candidate regulatory substances that are safe, efficient, dosage controllable, economically suitable, and clarifying their mechanisms of action and application adaptability, has become an urgent scientific and production issue that needs to be addressed in the fields of healthy egg chicken farming and FLHS prevention and control.

The FLHS model in laying hens was successfully constructed using a high-fat and high-protein diet combined with β - estradiol injection in this study. Based on this, the intervention effect of AS was systematically evaluated by analyzing serum and liver biochemical indicators, observing liver histology, and studying liver non-target lipid metabolomics, and the regulatory mechanism of AS by activating the AMPK signaling pathway, regulating the expression of key factors in lipid metabolism, inhibiting abnormal accumulation of liver lipids, and reducing oxidative damage and inflammatory response of liver cells was further elucidated. In addition, liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry technology was used to analyze the active ingredients of AS, providing data support for elucidating the functional substance basis of AS against FLHS.

Traditional Chinese veterinary medicine has unique characteristics and great potential for preventing and treating chronic diseases, as well as controlling epidemics. Furthermore, as the increase of concerns about food safety, the use of traditional Chinese veterinary medicine for the prevention and treatment of diseases in livestock and poultry has become a popular research topic. According to traditional Chinese veterinary medicine, liver blood stasis is one of the core pathomechanisms of FLHS in laying hens. AS has blood-nourishing, blood-activating, liver-protecting and lipid-lowering properties, which making it as a promising therapeutic agent for the prevention and treatment on FLHS in laying hens. This study found that AS intervention was related to twenty-one active ingredients, including ferulic acid and ligustilide in AS. Some literature data showed that AS could indeed regulate lipid metabolism (Qu et al., 2019; Lu et al., 2025). Chuanxiongzine alleviated alcohol-induced hepatic steatosis through a Nrf2/HIF-1α pathway-dependent mechanism (Lu et al., 2017). Ferulic acid could promote fatty acid oxidation and energy expenditure, enhance barrier function in both proximal and distal intestinal segments, thereby preventing and reducing hepatic lipid accumulation and inflammatory responses in mice with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (Luo et al., 2022; Fu et al., 2023). Ligustilide could treat alcoholic hepatic steatosis by targeting RXFP1 (Yang et al., 2024). The lipid-lowering effect of AS polysaccharides may stem from dual inhibition of lipid synthesis and CD36-mediated lipid uptake (He et al., 2022).

Unlike mammals, poultry rely on the livers as the primary organ for lipid metabolism. Some studies conducting in vitro and on intact chicks suggest that the liver was the primary site of lipid biosynthesis in chicken (Leveille et al., 1975). Furthermore, transporter 4, an important insulin-responsive protein found in mammals, is absent in chickens. This absence may be associated with the hyperglycaemia and insulin resistance observed in chickens (Carver et al., 2001; Byers et al., 2017). Additionally, the glucose concentration in poultry was two to three times higher than that in mammals of equivalent body weight (Simon et al., 2011). This natural “insulin tolerance” makes poultry more susceptible to hyperlipidaemia and hyperglycaemia, significantly increasing the risk of liver damage.

Fat metabolism in poultry primarily occurs in the liver (Liu et al., 2023). The core pathogenesis of FLHS involves the massive accumulation of lipid droplets (LDs) in the liver, leading to hepatic steatosis and liver vascular injury (Shini et al., 2020). LDs are important liver organelles that store triglycerides and cholesterol. Lipophagy is the process by which organisms selectively degrade LDs via the lysosomal autophagy pathway. LDs are engulfed, either partially or completely, by autophagosome membranes, which then fuse with lysosomes. The action of hydrolytic enzymes produces endogenous free fatty acids, which promote β-oxidation and are crucial for regulating lipid metabolism (Claude-Taupin et al., 2024). The occurrence and progression of lipophagy are regulated by multiple factors, including genes, transcription factors and enzymes (Liu et al., 2020). This study found that AS intervention increased the number of autophagic lysosomes within the hepatocytes of FLHS in laying hens, containing visible lipid droplets. These results suggested that AS might regulate lipid metabolism of FLHS in laying hens by enhancing lipophagy. This was also consistent with the results of non-target lipidomics research in this study.

AMPK plays a significant role in the cellular energy metabolism and the development and progression of FLHS. AMPK was closely related to lipophagy and lipid metabolism pathways (Lv et al., 2025). AMPK activation inhibits anabolic processes and promotes catabolism, consequently reducing the extent of lipid deposition (Ma et al., 2019). mTOR is a key regulator of cellular growth and autophagy, as well as one of the most important downstream target proteins of AMPK. When being activated, AMPK phosphorylates the TSC1/2 protein complex, thereby inhibiting mTOR activity and enhancing lipolysis, which reduces hepatic lipid accumulation (Liao et al., 2024; Tu et al., 2024). The interaction between AMPK and mTOR plays a key role in regulating cellular autophagy (Hwang et al., 2021). Some research indicated that lipophagy level was lower in the FLHS state (Tu et al., 2024; Miao et al., 2025). Lipophagy level in the body was closely related to metabolic dysfunction. Disrupted lipophagy levels further worsen hepatic lipid deposition (Jian et al., 2024). It was found that there have been few studies on the intervention of AS in FLHS laying hens through literature review. However, some data shows that the active ingredients of AS could indeed intervened in autophagy by regulating the AMPK pathway (Wu et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023). There was also reported that the active ingredient ferulic acid in AS could activated the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway (Chen et al., 2023). Ferulic acid could also improved liver inflammation and fibrotic liver injury by inhibiting PTP1B activity and promoting AMPK phosphorylation (Wu et al., 2021), and could improve lipid metabolism in alcoholic liver disease through AMPK/ACC and PI3K/AKT pathways (Lu et al., 2025). This study found that AS intervention significantly regulated AMPK pathway-related genes and proteins in FLHS laying hens. This suggested that AS could regulate lipid metabolism in FLHS laying hens by increasing lipophagy via AMPK pathway.

In terms of practical production and financial advantages, AS can be utilized as a regulatory additive for the purpose of promoting healthy chicken farming practices. Appropriate intervention doses could effectively alleviated liver lipid accumulation lesions, improved production performance, and reduced economic losses caused by FLHS in laying hens. From the perspective of food safety and public health, screening the effective dosage of AS could provided important data support for clinical standardized administration, and ensured the safety of animal derived food.

In summary, this study not only explored the intervention effect of AS on FLHS in laying hens, but also analyzed its regulatory targets and modes of action that rely on the AMPK pathway to intervene in FLHS. This could provided experimental basis and theoretical reference for the scientific dosage selection, large-scale feed application, and improvement of breeding efficiency of AS in laying hens, and also provided new ideas for the development of green prevention and control strategies and new functional additives for FLHS in laying hens. It is worth mentioning that this experiment did not set up an AMPK inhibition group, which has limitations in reverse validation of the AMPK pathway function. In the future, we will carry out this work to further verify the effect of AS on the AMPK pathway on FLHS in laying hens. In addition, we will also involve long-term safety evaluation of drugs and intervention effects on other breeds of laying hens in the later stage, in order to expand the credibility of the trial and provide more basis for the clinical application of AS.
