Doxycycline for epistaxis in hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia: genotype-stratified outcomes
Authors: Jinelis Santiago-Beniquez, Avraham Adelman, Eunice Im, Anil Patel, Nikita Chapurin, Christina Eagan, Ali Ataya, Marc Zumberg, Jennifer K Mulligan, Carl Atkinson, Brian C Lobo, Jeb M Justice
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Doxycycline reduces ESS and does not appear to be associated with genotype.•Doxycycline may be an effective conservative option for the management of epistaxis in HHT.

Introduction

Hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT), also known as Osler-Weber-Rendu syndrome, is a rare autosomal dominant disorder with an estimated prevalence of 1 in 5000.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 HHT is characterized by loss-of-function mutations that affect the transforming growth factor β and vascular endothelial growth factor signaling pathways. The most common genotypes affected are endoglin (ENG) and activin receptor-like kinase 1 (ACVRL1/ALK1), which contribute to 90% of patients with HHT, whereas the SMAD4 gene is responsible for 2%.1,5,8 HHT is distinguished by arteriovenous malformations and mucosal telangiectasia that affect the nose, mouth, gastrointestinal tract, brain, liver, and lungs.1,4 The clinical manifestations include epistaxis, gastrointestinal hemorrhage, and iron deficiency anemia, with recurrent epistaxis being the most common, affecting ∼90% of the patients.5

Current treatment ranges from conservative medical therapy starting with topical nasal saline, oral tranexamic acid, and progressing to systemic antiangiogenic agents and extending to more invasive surgical interventions such as ablative therapies and septodermoplasty.1,3 Epistaxis management is still challenging, and although it has evolved, there is still a need for more accessible, safe, and effective therapies. Doxycycline is a proposed option because of its availability, low cost, safety, and antiangiogenic effect, having been shown to downregulate matrix metalloproteinases in both in vitro and translational models.9,10 Matrix metalloproteinase is involved in the vascular remodeling and angiogenesis processes and could potentially reduce telangiectasia vessels. Doxycycline has been investigated as a potential treatment option, particularly in patients with contraindications for alternative therapies like tranexamic acid and bevacizumab.

Previous studies have focused on the effectiveness of doxycycline; however, many questions remain unanswered, and the results remain inconclusive. A previous small retrospective study showed the effectiveness of doxycycline.11 Although 2 prospective clinical trials showed no statistical significance in reduction of epistaxis severity score (ESS), the NOSTRIL trial post hoc outcome analysis did show reduced incidence of gushing epistaxis with doxycycline, and the longitudinal analysis demonstrated reduction in frequency and duration of bleeding with doxycycline over time for a sample size of 22 patients.12,13 The discrepancy between these studies opens the need to identify possible predictors of response, whether clinical, genetic, or biologic. No previous studies have focused on the outcomes of doxycycline stratified by HHT genotypes. Differences in genetic mutations play a role in clinical phenotypes, with patients with ENG being more susceptible to earlier and more severe nosebleeds than patients with an ACVRL1 mutation.14,15 Furthermore, genotype may influence the treatment course, and this study aims to analyze the effect of doxycycline based on differential genotypes.

Methods

We performed a retrospective chart review of patients with HHT aged ≥18 years who were treated for epistaxis within the University of Florida health system (Gainesville, FL) between January 2022 and December 2024. According to the Curacao criteria, the diagnosis of HHT was defined as meeting ≥3 of the 4 criteria or positive genetic testing results; all patients included had a history of epistaxis as part of the Curacao criteria.16 Doxycycline was an inclusion requirement for this study, and treatment was initiated at the index visit, allowing collection of pretreatment and posttreatment assessments.

To isolate its treatment effects, we only included patients who were either not undergoing any systemic treatments (eg, tranexamic acid) or those who had been on stable doses of systemic treatments without any recent changes. The dosage of doxycycline was 100 mg by mouth twice daily. Patients were instructed to take doxycycline ≥2 hours before or after a meal, and patients were counseled about doxycycline absorption being blocked by calcium in the gastrointestinal tract.

We collected demographic and clinical data, including genetic mutations, baseline ESS, and diagnostic laboratory results (hemoglobin and hematocrit). We only considered laboratory results when they were collected within 30 days before and after the ESS that they were correlated with. ESS, a validated epistaxis scoring index (0-10), quantifies the severity of epistaxis using the frequency, duration, severity, and medical impacts (ie, anemia and need for blood transfusions) of symptoms, which are stratified into the following categories: mild (1-4), moderate (4-7), and severe (7-10).17

After analyzing the cohort as a whole, we subdivided the doxycycline cohort into responder and nonresponder groups based on both subjective and objective measurements (ESS). The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) in the change in ESS in HHT is defined as 0.71.18 In cases where subjective and objective changes conflicted with each other, patients were placed according to their objective changes; χ2 analysis was used to determine whether HHT genotype influenced response to doxycycline. Changes in median ESS were analyzed with Wilcoxon signed rank tests, and laboratory parameters were analyzed using paired t tests in GraphPad Prism 10.0 (La Jolla, CA). The significance level was set at .05.

The institutional review board of the University of Florida approved (IRB202400235) this study as an exempt study with minimal risks. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

The entire cohort (N = 41) was 61% female, 90.2% White, and had a mean age of 58.1 years (standard deviation [SD], 17.2). The predominant genotype was ACVRL1 (65.9%). Baseline median ESS from all patients was 4.4 (interquartile range [IQR], 3.3), which is “moderate” severity. The mean baseline hemoglobin (n = 17) and hematocrit (n = 16), based on available data, were 11.9 g/dL (SD, 2.3) and 40.0% (SD, 5.5), respectively. Discussion

Despite oral tranexamic acid and surgical options, epistaxis management remains a challenge in patients with HHT.19,20 In this study, we analyzed the effectiveness and safety of doxycycline as well as its association with genotype variability. The response to doxycycline continues to be inconclusive. A previous retrospective study showed that doxycycline was effective in some patients.11 However, evidence presented in 2 prospective randomized placebo-controlled studies demonstrated no significant efficacy over placebo.12,13 In our cohort overall, doxycycline appears to significantly reduce ESS, exceeding the MCID. However, we observed no significant differences in response from ACVRL1, ENG, or SMAD4 mutations. The lack of association with genotypes creates a broader question: why does doxycycline appear to help some patients over others?

When interpreting and comparing our results with those of previous investigations, an important factor to take into consideration is the length of treatment. In previous studies, the length of treatment was 2 months, and no significant effect was observed.12,13 However, our cohort had a mean treatment duration of 4.1 months. This finding raises the possibility that treatment duration may be a contributing factor for doxycycline response and that it may require sustained administration for a longer period to exert measurable improvement, potentially reflecting its mechanism of action of vascular remodeling and inhibition of metalloproteinases. This observation is aligned with the NOSTRIL trial post hoc longitudinal analyses, which suggested reductions in bleeding frequency (P = .01) and duration (P = .03) over time; this reinforces the possibility of a delayed effect.12 Additionally, most of the nonresponders were female (∼60%), which may suggest a sex-based difference in treatment response. Future studies should look into the time-dependent and sex-based effects of doxycycline.

In both randomized, placebo-controlled trials of doxycycline, there was evidence of safety with mild adverse effects, mostly gastrointestinal and dermatologic reactions. There was no significant difference between doxycycline and placebo in adverse effects.12,13 Doxycycline effectiveness can also be affected by drug administration. The absorption of doxycycline can be affected by the ingestion of calcium-rich foods, iron supplementation, and antacids, directly affecting bioavailability.21, 22, 23 Furthermore, it is important to counsel patients on the role of calcium-rich foods and how it is important to take doxycycline ≥2 hours after eating. This opens the need for further investigation, incorporating adherence monitoring and evaluating the impact of bioavailability on outcomes.

In comparison to the previous studies, our clinical experience suggested heterogeneity in individual response, supporting a selective use of doxycycline and motivating exploration of the factors previously mentioned and how they contribute to treatment effect. Doxycycline has several advantages over existing therapies.19,20 It is inexpensive, well tolerated, and orally administered, making it accessible. In comparison with systemic antiangiogenics, such as bevacizumab or pazopanib, doxycycline does not require IV delivery or specialized monitoring.24,25 Furthermore, in contrast to antifibrinolytic agents, doxycycline offers the potential for once or twice daily dosing with a favorable safety profile.4,7,20 Our findings support a likely genotype-independent option for patients with epistaxis, which, although not a replacement for established therapies, may benefit as an add-on or adjunctive therapy for those who are not candidates for or lack access to systemic antiangiogenic therapies.

The study limitations include a retrospective design and a small sample size, underrepresentation of some genotypes, reduced statistical power, and generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, genotype subgroup analyses should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis generating rather than definitive. The generalizability of these findings may be limited, as the study population was centered in a single institution and may not reflect patients from other populations, clinical settings, or geographical regions.

Another limitation is relying on self-reported data and using ESS, which is a subjective outcome susceptible to recall and reporting bias and does not always correlate with objective measures. Additional laboratory parameters such as ferritin, iron, and transferrin saturations were not consistently available, making a comprehensive assessment not possible. When interpreting these results, it is important to consider the distinction between objective hematologic parameters and patient-reported symptom burden. These outcomes may not always show concordant changes over time. Finally, ESS measurements were not longitudinal during follow-up, making it impossible to follow trends over time.

Our study, to the best of our knowledge, is the first to stratify doxycycline response by genotype (ENG, ACVRL1, and SMAD4), and although no differential effect was observed, this approach is fundamental for the progression of precision medicine in HHT. Doxycycline appears to provide clinically significant reductions in epistaxis severity, regardless of genotype. Although preliminary, our data endorse doxycycline as a repurposed adjunct therapy for the management of HHT.

Although our data are limited, the lack of certain genotypes under- or overrepresentation among responders vs nonresponders suggests that further investigations of genotype-specific doxycycline response may not be fruitful. However, future larger, prospective, multicenter trials with patient counseling on the role of calcium-rich foods and the importance of taking doxycycline ≥2 hours away from food are needed to clarify the role of doxycycline in recurrent epistaxis in patients with HHT, with longer treatment durations to determine whether these results are reproducible. Further investigation should also evaluate pharmacokinetics and pharmacogenetic factors and assess health care use outcomes such as transfusion requirements and procedural interventions.
