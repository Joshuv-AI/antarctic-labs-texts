Interactive Effects of Parental Age on Adverse Birth Outcomes in Japan: An Investigation of Nationwide Birth Data
Authors: Tasuku Okui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesWe investigated the interactive effects of parents’ ages, as well as the overall effects of paternal age, on adverse birth outcomes using nationwide data from Japan.MethodsLive birth data from the Vital Statistics Surveys in Japan from 2010 to 2023 were analyzed. Preterm birth, low birth weight, macrosomia, small-for-gestational-age (SGA) status, and large-for-gestational-age status were defined as adverse birth outcomes. Log-binomial regression models were used to examine associations between maternal and paternal ages and adverse birth outcomes. In addition to regression analyses using maternal and paternal age groups separately, we conducted regression analyses using combined parental age groups to assess interactive effects.ResultsData from 12 279 842 live births were analyzed. Overall, the risk ratio (RR) increased significantly with an increase in the paternal age categories for all adverse birth outcomes. In contrast, among mothers aged ≥30 years, the RRs for preterm birth, low birth weight, and SGA status were higher for infants of fathers aged ≤24 years than for those of fathers aged ≥35 years. In particular, the RRs for these outcomes, as well as macrosomia, were highest for the combination of the youngest paternal age group (≤24 years) and the oldest maternal age group (≥40 years).ConclusionsAnalysis of nationwide birth data indicated that the effects of paternal age on adverse birth outcomes varied according to maternal age, underscoring the importance of assessing pregnancy risk according to combined parental age.

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

Parental age can affect children’s health, and older parental age has been associated with pediatric health-related outcomes such as developmental delay, cardiometabolic risk, and hospital admissions [1–4]. In Japan, the mean age of parents at childbirth has increased for decades because of later marriage and delayed pregnancy [5], and the proportions of older mothers and fathers among parents of live born children have increased accordingly. Therefore, the risks of adverse outcomes associated with parental age in Japan warrant further assessment.

Parental age has been associated with pregnancy outcomes, and both younger and older parental ages, particularly <20 years and ≥40 years in many studies, have been associated with outcomes such as stillbirth, pregnancy complications, and preterm birth [6–11]. In Japan, several studies have reported associations between older maternal age (≥35 years) and adverse birth outcomes [7,12]. Older paternal age (≥35 years) has also been associated with low birth weight, preterm birth, and macrosomia in some studies [13–15], although studies on associations between paternal age and adverse birth outcomes remain limited. In particular, nationwide data from Japan have not been used to investigate associations between paternal age and adverse birth outcomes, except for macrosomia. In addition, the interactive effects of both parents’ ages, defined here as the joint effects of parental age-group combinations, on adverse birth outcomes have not been investigated in Japan. A few studies in other countries have identified such interactive effects; for example, the risks of adverse birth outcomes and infant mortality tended to be higher among pairs consisting of younger fathers (<30 years) and older mothers (≥40 years), as well as among parents with a large age gap [9,16,17]. These interactive effects should also be examined in Japan because evidence remains limited worldwide. To the best of our knowledge, only 2 studies, conducted in the United States and China, have investigated the interactive effects of parents’ ages on adverse birth outcomes such as preterm birth, low birth weight, and macrosomia [9,17]. However, parents’ socio-demographic characteristics and the rates of each adverse birth outcome differ substantially between Japan and those countries [18–21]. In addition, the sample size of the study conducted in China was approximately 16 000 [9]. Large-scale data are preferable for investigating these associations because births involving a large parental age difference are generally uncommon. It is also meaningful to examine these associations using nationwide data from a country other than the United States. If the effect of one parent’s age on adverse birth outcomes differs according to the spouse’s age, pregnancy risk could be assessed more precisely, and populations requiring support could be identified by considering combined parental age rather than each parent’s age separately.

In this study, we used nationwide data from Japan to investigate the interactive effects of parents’ ages, as well as the overall effects of paternal age, on adverse birth outcomes. In particular, we examined whether the effect of one parent’s age on adverse birth outcomes differed according to the spouse’s age.

METHODS

Data

Live birth data from the Vital Statistics Surveys in Japan from 2010 to 2023 were used in the analysis; these data cover all live births reported to local governments in Japan. The data were provided by the National Statistics Center in Japan and analyzed at an on-site facility for official statistics.

Preterm birth, low birth weight, macrosomia, small-for-gestational-age (SGA) status, and large-for-gestational-age (LGA) status were considered adverse birth outcomes. Preterm birth was defined as delivery at <37 weeks of gestation. Low birth weight and macrosomia were defined as birth weights of <2500 g and ≥4000 g, respectively. SGA and LGA status were defined as birth weights below the 10th percentile and above the 90th percentile, respectively, on the neonatal anthropometric chart in Japan according to gestational age, sex, and parity [22]. Because the anthropometric chart covers only infants delivered at 22–41 weeks of gestation, analyses of SGA and LGA status included only those infants.

The birth characteristics used in the analysis were parental ages, household occupation, parental nationalities, region, parity, and birth year. Maternal age was categorized as ≤19 years, 20–24 years, 25–29 years, 30–34 years, 35–39 years, and ≥40 years; paternal age was categorized as ≤24 years, 25–29 years, 30–34 years, 35–39 years, 40–44 years, and ≥45 years. Different age categories were used for maternal and paternal age because their age distributions differed. Specifically, the numbers of mothers aged ≥45 years and fathers aged <20 years were relatively small, making it difficult to analyze the interactive effects of these parental age groups. To analyze the effects of the combination of younger fathers and older mothers, mothers aged ≥40 years and fathers aged ≤24 years were each categorized into a single age group. Parental age groups were used as categorical variables in the analysis, and a combined parental age-group variable was also used. Parental nationality was categorized as Japanese or non-Japanese, and parity was categorized as primiparous or multiparous. Region was categorized as metropolitan, urban, or non-urban. Metropolitan areas comprised government ordinance-designated cities and the 23 wards of Tokyo, and urban areas comprised other cities. “Household occupation” referred to the employment status or occupation of the top earner in the household, including farmers, self-employed persons, full-time workers in a small company, full-time workers in a larger company, other workers, and unemployed persons. Small companies were defined as those with <100 employees. By contrast, the category of full-time workers in larger companies included those working in companies with ≥100 employees, executives, and public servants.

The study population was restricted to live singleton births occurring in Japan. Births to unmarried mothers were excluded. Observations with missing data were also excluded because the proportion of missing data was small (3.3%).

Statistical Analysis

The proportions of preterm birth, low birth weight, macrosomia, SGA status, and LGA status were calculated for each combination of parental age groups. Log-binomial regression models were used to examine associations between parental age groups and adverse birth outcomes. Each adverse birth outcome served as the outcome variable, and maternal and paternal age groups, household occupation, maternal and paternal nationalities, region, parity, and birth year were included as explanatory variables. The 30–34-year age group was used as the reference category for both maternal and paternal age because the number of births was highest in that category for both parents. In addition to analyses using maternal and paternal age groups separately, an analysis using a combined parental age-group variable was conducted to investigate interactive effects, defined as the joint effects of parental age groups. Sandwich variance estimators were used in the regression analyses because they are often used to address non-independence of observations [23]. Risk ratios (RRs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values were calculated, and p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. A heatmap was also used to show the RRs by parents’ age groups. The ggplot2, ggpubr, lmtest, and sandwich packages in R version 4.4 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) were used for all statistical analyses. The statistics shown in this study were generated by the authors using data provided by the administrative institution and are not statistics published by the government.

Ethics Statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Kyushu University Institutional Review Board for Clinical Research (No. 25059). Informed consent was not required because the official statistical data were provided under the Statistics Act in Japan.

RESULTS

Figure 1 shows the flowchart for selecting the study population. Data from 12 279 842 live births were included in the analysis.

Supplemental Material 1 lists the number of births according to birth characteristics. The number of births to parents aged 30–34 years was the largest in both the maternal and paternal age groups.

Supplemental Material 2 shows the number of births for each combination of parental age groups. The number of births decreased as the difference between parental ages increased.

Table 1 shows the numbers and proportions (%) of adverse birth outcomes for each combination of parental age groups. The rates of preterm birth, low birth weight, and SGA status were higher among mothers aged ≤19 years and ≥40 years, regardless of paternal age, and the rates of adverse birth outcomes tended to be highest among infants of mothers aged ≥40 years, regardless of paternal age. Among fathers, the rates of all adverse birth outcomes except SGA status were higher when fathers were aged ≥45 years, regardless of maternal age. In addition, the rates of all adverse birth outcomes except LGA status tended to be higher among infants of fathers aged ≤24 years, and this pattern was evident among infants born to mothers aged ≥30 years.

Table 2 shows the results of the log-binomial regression models using maternal and paternal age groups separately. The RRs increased with an increase in parental age categories and were highest among infants of mothers aged ≥40 years and those of fathers aged ≥45 years for all adverse birth outcomes. Specifically, the RRs for mothers aged ≥40 years were 1.50 (95% CI, 1.48 to 1.51) for preterm birth, 1.33 (95% CI, 1.32 to 1.34) for low birth weight, 1.10 (95% CI, 1.07 to 1.14) for macrosomia, 1.10 (95% CI, 1.09 to 1.11) for SGA status, and 1.16 (95% CI, 1.15 to 1.17) for LGA status. The RRs for fathers aged ≥45 years were 1.14 (95% CI, 1.12 to 1.15) for preterm birth, 1.09 (95% CI, 1.08 to 1.10) for low birth weight, 1.21 (95% CI, 1.18 to 1.25) for macrosomia, 1.04 (95% CI, 1.02 to 1.05) for SGA status, and 1.11 (95% CI, 1.10 to 1.12) for LGA status.

Table 3 shows the results of log-binomial regression models using combined maternal and paternal age groups. The point estimates are also shown as heatmaps in Figure 2. Older maternal age (35–39 and ≥40 years) was significantly associated with adverse birth outcomes in most paternal age groups. In addition, when mothers were aged ≥30 years, older paternal age (35–39, 40–44, and ≥45 years) was significantly associated with adverse birth outcomes. However, among mothers aged ≥30 years, the RRs for preterm birth, low birth weight, and SGA status were higher when fathers were younger (≤24 years) than when fathers were older (35–39, 40–44, or ≥45 years). In particular, the RRs for preterm birth, low birth weight, macrosomia, and SGA status were highest for the combination of fathers aged ≤24 years and mothers aged ≥40 years.

DISCUSSION

This study showed that, in analyses using maternal and paternal age groups separately, the RR was highest in the oldest paternal age category (≥45 years) for all adverse birth outcomes. In contrast, among infants of mothers aged ≥30 years, the RRs for preterm birth, low birth weight, and SGA status were higher when fathers were aged ≤24 years than when fathers were aged ≥35 years. These findings indicate that the effects of paternal age differed according to maternal age; possible reasons and implications are discussed below.

Associations between paternal age groups and adverse birth outcomes were confirmed: older paternal age (≥35 years) was associated with a higher overall risk of adverse birth outcomes. This finding is consistent with previous studies in Japan and other countries [8,14,15,24,25]. Advanced paternal age has been associated with disadvantages such as deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) damage in sperm and chromosomal abnormalities [26], and DNA damage is associated with pregnancy complications [27]. Older paternal age, defined as ≥45 years in Khandwala et al. [28], 35–44 and ≥45 years in Harlap et al. [29], and ≥30 years in Yin et al. [9], has been associated with a higher risk of pregnancy complications such as preeclampsia and gestational diabetes [9,28,29]. Gestational diabetes is a risk factor for macrosomia and LGA status [30,31], and epigenetic growth factors in the placenta and embryo may contribute to increased placental weight in pregnancies involving older fathers [28].

In contrast, the effects of paternal age groups on adverse birth outcomes differed according to maternal age; younger paternal age (≤24 years) was more strongly associated with a higher risk of preterm birth, low birth weight, and SGA status among infants of mothers aged ≥30 years. In addition, the risks of preterm birth, low birth weight, macrosomia, and SGA status were highest for the combination of the youngest paternal age group (≤24 years) and the oldest maternal age group (≥40 years). Studies in other countries have reported associations between younger paternal age, defined as <25 years in Alio et al. [8] and Goisis et al. [10] and <20 years in Chen et al. [11], and adverse birth outcomes [8,10,11]. Moreover, some studies in other countries have shown adverse effects of combinations of younger fathers and older mothers, defined as fathers aged <21 years and mothers aged ≥21 years in Doamekpor et al. [16], mothers aged 25–29 years and fathers who were 9–12 years younger [17], and fathers aged <30 years and mothers aged ≥40 years in Yin et al. [9], on perinatal outcomes and infant mortality [9,16,17]. Doamekpor et al. [16] noted that adolescent fathers tended to be economically disadvantaged, which might increase anxiety among mothers during pregnancy. Another study noted that lower socioeconomic conditions and lifestyle factors, such as smoking and alcohol consumption, may affect birth outcomes and that interactions between these socioeconomic and lifestyle factors in younger fathers and older maternal age might increase the risk of adverse birth outcomes [17]. Younger fathers tend to be socioeconomically disadvantaged in other countries [32,33], but socioeconomic status may also vary according to maternal age. A study in the United States indicated that people married to spouses of substantially different ages tended to have lower educational attainment and lower wages [34]. A study in Sweden also showed that a smaller age difference between spouses was associated with higher educational attainment and higher income [35]. Lower socioeconomic status, including lower educational attainment and lower income, predicts adverse birth outcomes in Japan [14,36]. Our analysis using each parental age group separately also showed that the oldest maternal age group (≥40 years) was more strongly associated with a higher risk of adverse birth outcomes than the oldest paternal age group (≥45 years). Therefore, previous studies and the present findings suggest that pairs consisting of fathers in the youngest age group and mothers in the oldest age group had the highest risk for some adverse birth outcomes because of social factors, such as lower socioeconomic status, in addition to biological factors, such as older maternal age.

The risks of adverse birth outcomes were highest in the oldest maternal age group (≥40 years), regardless of paternal age. Advanced maternal age is known to be associated with adverse birth outcomes in Japan and other countries [7,37]. Aging in women is accompanied by changes in the women reproductive system, including the uterus and ovaries, which may in turn affect the placenta and contribute to endocrine disturbances, pregnancy complications, and chromosomal abnormalities [38,39]. Because advanced maternal age is associated with adverse birth outcomes through biological pathways, it may contribute to such outcomes regardless of paternal age.

Using nationwide data, this study showed that older paternal age (≥35 years) was associated with adverse birth outcomes in Japan and that the effects of paternal age on birth outcomes differed according to maternal age. Therefore, medical practitioners may need to consider paternal age, rather than focusing only on maternal age, when assessing pregnancy risk in preconception and prenatal care. In particular, specialized health care support may be needed for couples consisting of younger fathers (<30 years) and older mothers (≥40 years). Future studies should examine couple-level characteristics, such as lifestyle behaviors, household income, and fathers’ support of mothers during pregnancy, in relation to parental age. Identifying social needs associated with combined parental age, as well as health care needs, may help reduce the rates of adverse birth outcomes among high-risk groups. Future studies should also examine the long-term effects of parental age on child health using longitudinal data, as well as associations between parental age and pregnancy complications.

A major limitation of this study is that the Vital Statistics Surveys did not include information on some parental characteristics, including pregnancy complications, educational attainment, and income. Information on educational attainment and income would have allowed us to evaluate socioeconomic differences between couples according to combined parental age. In addition, information on pregnancy complications would have helped clarify the mechanisms underlying associations between combined parental age and adverse birth outcomes. By contrast, a strength of this study is that nationwide birth data allowed us to identify overall patterns of association between parental age and adverse birth outcomes in Japan. These nationwide data also enabled us to evaluate the risk of adverse birth outcomes for each combination of parental age groups.

In conclusion, this analysis of nationwide birth data indicated that the effect of paternal age on adverse birth outcomes varied according to maternal age. These findings suggest that combined parental age is meaningful when assessing pregnancy risk in large-scale epidemiological studies and clinical practice. In addition, preventive measures and risk management for adverse birth outcomes, such as increased use of preconception and prenatal care and lifestyle modification, may be particularly important for pairs consisting of older mothers and younger fathers.
