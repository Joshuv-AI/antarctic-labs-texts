Investigating cytotoxic and inflammatory human IL-7 receptor low effector memory CD8+ T cells in lupus
Authors: Sang Jin Lee, Min Sun Shin, Yeon-Joo Lee, Lais Osmani, Won Jae Seong, Helen Cai, Jennefer Par-Young, Jong Gyun Ahn, Hong-Jai Park, Minhyung Kim, Serhan Unlu, Hyoungsu Kim, Man Hoon Han, Xuemei Dong, Sungyong You, Eun Bong Lee, Insoo Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThis study aims to interrogate the implications of CD8+ T cells in lupus by examining CD8+ T cell heterogeneity and assessing the significance of this heterogeneity in promoting inflammation and tissue damage.MethodsOur own and publicly available RNA-seq and microarray data from the peripheral blood and kidney tissues of patients with lupus were analysed. Imaging Mass Cytometry (IMC) analysis of immune cells was conducted in lupus and normal kidney tissues. The effects of CD8+ T cell subsets on neutrophils were evaluated ex vivo.FindingsOur scRNA-seq analysis showed an accumulation of effector memory (EM) CD8+ T cell subsets that expressed low levels of the IL7 receptor gene (IL7Rlow) but high levels of effector molecule genes, including GZMB, GZMK, PRF1, and GNLY, in the peripheral blood and kidneys of patients with lupus. CD8+ T cell infiltrations and cytotoxic molecule expression in lupus kidney tissues were associated with treatment outcomes, as determined by IMC. The gene signatures of IL7Rlow CD8+ T cell subsets correlated with type I IFN gene signature in the peripheral blood of paediatric and adult patients with lupus. IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells induced neutrophil extracellular trap (NET), a key inflammatory pathway in lupus pathogenesis, dependently of TNF-α and IFN-γ.InterpretationOur study provides insights into lupus pathogenesis by demonstrating the clinical and biological implications of cytotoxic and inflammatory IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cell accumulation in lupus. Research in context

Evidence before this study

The possible implications of CD8+ T cells, such as granzymes K and B expressing cells, in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE or lupus) have been reported, but their precise pathogenic roles, particularly in nephritis, are not fully understood. Previous evidence suggests that memory CD8+ T cells, including those expressing cytotoxic molecules, may accumulate in the peripheral blood of patients with lupus, correlating with disease activity.

Added value of this study

Single-cell RNA sequencing and Imaging Mass Cytometry have identified an accumulation of IL7Rlow effector memory (EM) CD8+ T cells in lupus nephritis that exhibit heightened cytotoxic and inflammatory molecular signatures in association with increased type I interferon signatures and poor treatment outcomes. Functionally, IL7RlowEM CD8+ T cells drive neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation via IFN-γ and TNF-α, supporting a feed-forward mechanism of inflammation.

Implications of all the available evidence

Profiling IL7RlowEM CD8+ T cells in the kidneys could serve as a biomarker for predicting treatment responsiveness and disease progression in lupus nephritis. Therapeutic strategies targeting this pathogenic CD8+ T cell subset or their proinflammatory cytokine signals may improve renal outcomes.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE or lupus) is an autoimmune inflammatory disease characterised by dysregulated immune responses against self-antigens with the production of autoantibodies, especially anti-nuclear antibodies.1 Approximately 40% of patients with lupus develop nephritis which requires intensive immunosuppressive therapy and dictates prognosis.2 Despite a reduction in mortality among patients with lupus nephritis likely related to the use of immunosuppressants, the incidence of its progression to end-stage renal disease remains unchanged.3 Studies reported the roles of innate and adaptive immune cells in initiating and/or promoting autoimmune response, inflammation, and tissue injury in lupus.4 However, the contributions of individual immune cells to the clinical phenotypes, disease activity, and treatment response appear to be diverse.

Memory T cells are heterogeneous populations in phenotype and function. Cytokine receptors can define memory T cell subsets with distinct features.5, 6, 7, 8 We previously reported two subsets of human effector memory (EM, CCR7−CD45RA+/−) CD8+ T cells with the expression of high and low levels of IL-7 receptor alpha chain (IL7R or CD127), a cytokine receptor involved in T cell homoeostasis, which were referred to as IL7Rhigh and low EM CD8+ T cells, respectively.5,6,9,10 IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells are highly cytotoxic and inflammatory cells with increased levels of IFN-γ, TNF-α, perforin, and granzyme B as compared to other CD8+ T cell subsets including IL7Rhigh EM CD8+ T cells.5,6 Of note, we found an accumulation of peripheral IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells in patients with lupus, correlating with disease activity.11

The role of CD8+ T cells in the pathogenesis of lupus is largely unknown, although these cells express cytotoxic molecules and produce inflammatory cytokines. Nonetheless, evidence that CD8+ T cell regulation might be aberrant in SLE has emerged. Most human studies showed the accumulation of memory CD8+ T cells, including those expressing cytotoxic molecules, in the peripheral blood of patients with lupus that correlated with disease activity or outcomes.12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18 In addition to causing inflammation and directly damaging tissue, the accumulation of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells could lead to increased generation of soluble nucleosomes, target antigenic structures in lupus, from target cells by inducing tissue damage.13 The presence of CD8+ T cells expressing GZMK and GZMB were reported in lupus nephritis tissue.19,20 In class III and IV lupus nephritis, the predominance of CD8+ T cells among periglomerular infiltrating cells was associated with increased renal activity index,21 supporting the potential role of CD8+ T cells in the pathogenesis of lupus.

Here we investigated the implications of memory CD8+ T cell subsets in lupus using single cell (sc) RNA-seq and microarray data analyses, Imaging Mass Cytometry (IMC), and ex vivo functional experiments. The results of our study provide insights into the pathogenesis of lupus by demonstrating the accumulation of cytotoxic and inflammatory IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells in the peripheral blood and kidneys of patients with lupus, the relationship of such cell infiltrations and cytotoxic molecules in the kidneys with clinical outcomes of lupus nephritis, and the functional effects of IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells on augmenting neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, an inflammatory pathway involved in lupus.22, 23, 24, 25

scRNA-seq analysis, publicly available datasets, and data processing

For scRNA-seq analysis, PBMCs were diluted into the Chromium Next GEM Chip G to yield a recovery of approximately 3000 single cells. scRNA-seq libraries were prepared using a Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ reagent kit v3.1 (10× Genomics, Pleasanton, CA). The libraries were sequenced in multiplex on the Novaseq 6000 sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, CA) to produce on average a minimum of 60,000 reads per single cell. Sequencing reads were processed with Cell Ranger version 3.0.1 using human reference transcriptomes. Publicly available scRNA-seq and microarray datasets used in this study included the Accelerated Medicine Program (AMP) dataset (ImmPort, access code SDY997),28 a dataset published by Pérez et al. (GSE174188, referred to as Perez dataset),18 and lupus peripheral blood microarray datasets (GSE65391, GSE138458).29,30 In the microarray datasets, the signatures of type I IFN and IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cell genes were calculated (see details in Supplementary Materials and Methods).

Quality control, filtering, data clustering and visualisation, and the differential expression analysis through the gene expression matrix of the kidney tissues and PBMCs were performed using the R package Seurat (v4.1.2). To correct technical differences between datasets (i.e., batch effects), we used the FindIntegrationAnchors and IntegrateData functions with 30 principal components in Seurat software.31,32 This anchor-based integration framework enables correction of batch effects by identifying shared biological states across datasets. To filter out low quality and doublet cells, we removed i) cells with >25% and 5% mitochondrial genes in kidney tissues and PBMCs, respectively, ii) cells expressing <300 genes or >4000 genes, and iii) cells with <500 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) or > 40, 000 UMIs. Pseudotime trajectory analysis was conducted using R package Monacle 3.33 Details for scRNA-seq data analysis were provided in Supplementary Materials and Methods.

IMC analysis of kidney tissues

Pre-existing formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded biopsied tissue samples of lupus nephritis and normal kidneys were analysed (see Supplementary Materials and Methods and Supplementary Table S1 for clinical characteristics).6 Kidney tissues were stained with a panel of metal-isotype-tagged Abs (Supplementary Table S2) followed by cisplatin staining. Tissue sections were processed, and IMC data was acquired on a Cytometry by Time-Of-Flight Helios coupled to a Hyperion Imaging System (Standard BioTools). Cell detection, segmentation, and measurements were performed using QuPath software (version 0.4.3). Single cell analysis of IMC data was performed using Scanpy (version 1.9.3).34 Batch correction was performed using BBKNN (version 1.6.0).35 Construction of spatial neighbours graph and subsequent neighbourhood enrichment analysis were performed separately for the treatment responder and non-responder groups using Squidpy (v 1.5.0).

Cell purification, stimulation, NET assay, flow cytometry, qPCR, and in vitro migration assay

IL7Rlow and high EM CD8+ T cells were purified from PBMCs using a FACSAria (BD Biosciences) and incubated for 18 h with or without anti-CD3 Abs (OKT3, BD Biosciences). Cell culture supernatants (SUPs) were used for NET assay and monocyte activation. For neutrophil activation and NET assay, neutrophils were purified freshly from peripheral blood using a commercially available neutrophil isolation kit (Stemcell Technologies, Cambridge, MA). Details for the NET assay, flow cytometry, qPCR, and in vitro migration assay of CD8+ T cell subsets in response to IL-8 are provided in Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Statistical analysis

The paired t-test and the repeated measure one-way ANOVA test with the Sidak's multiple comparison test were done using GraphPad Prism 10. The Spearman's rank correlation coefficient analysis was done using R. The normality of data was assessed using a Q–Q plot. P-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

The funders had no role in study design, data collection, data analyses, interpretation, or writing of report.

These data were integrated with a publicly available scRNA-seq dataset from 29 patients with lupus nephritis and 8 normal kidney tissue samples generated by the AMP. Detailed clinical information for the publicly available dataset was previously provided in a published article that originally generated this dataset.28 The final dataset, referred to as Lee-AMP dataset, comprised 16,256 CD45+ nucleated haematopoietic cells from normal kidneys (168 cells), lupus kidneys (2934 cells), PBMCs at flare (7909 cells), and PBMCs at follow up (5245 cells). Clustering of T cells (9385 cells) identified 12 clusters (subsets) including five CD4+ and seven CD8+ T cell clusters (Fig. 1b and c). CCR7 gene is highly expressed by naïve and central memory (CM) T cells, while EM T cells have low CCR7 levels. Previously, we found IL7Rhigh and low cell subsets in EM CD8+ T cells with distinct expression of cytotoxic molecules.5,10,36 Similarly, EM CD8+ T cell clusters that expressed high and low levels of the IL7R gene were identified (Fig. 1b). IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells had an increase in the expression of cytotoxicity-related genes (GNLY, GZMB, PRF1, GZMH, GZMM and GZMA) compared to IL7Rhigh EM CD8+ T cells (Fig. 1d). The gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) also revealed enrichment in pathways related to cytolysis, endopeptidase activity, lytic vacuole, and lysosome which are related to cytotoxic molecule storage, secretion, and activation (Fig. 1e). Based on the expression of GZMK and GZMB, IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells were further divided into three clusters (subsets) which expressed GZMH, PRF1, and/or GNLY (Fig. 1b and f). IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cell clusters substantially increased in lupus kidney tissues compared to normal kidney tissues (Fig. 1g). At the flare, IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells with high GZMB expression modestly increased in PBMCs of patients with lupus compared to the 6-week follow-up point, while both CCR7+CD27high CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, which included naïve and CM cells, decreased (Fig. 1g).

A trajectory analysis on the scRNA-seq data showed that the most differentiated CD8+ T cells were IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells with high levels of GZMB, PRF1, and GNLY (Fig. 2a–c). The pseudotime trajectory identified three expressional patterns among the top 200 representative genes in CD8+ T cells as they transitioned from CCR7+CD27high cluster to CCR7−IL7RlowGZMBhigh cluster (Fig. 2d). An independent scRNA-seq dataset showed the accumulation of IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cell subsets with cytotoxicity-related genes in the peripheral blood of patients with lupus

To further our findings, we investigated CD8+ T cells in an independent scRNA-seq dataset of PBMCs from patients with lupus and controls (GSE174188, referred to as Perez dataset).18 This dataset comprises samples from 162 patients with lupus, including 19 patients in active disease flare (“Flare” group) with 10 matched samples obtained after treatment (“Treated” group), 143 patients with managed disease (“Managed” group), and 43 healthy controls (“Healthy” group). Based on CD3, CD8A, CD8B, and TCRB expression, we identified CD8+ T cells (n = 187,317), which included five CCR7+ and six CCR7- clusters (Fig. 3a). Four of the six CCR7- EM CD8+ T cell clusters expressed low levels of the IL7R gene while two other subsets expressed high levels of this gene. The cytotoxicity-associated genes GZMB, GZMH, PRF1, GNLY, and FGFBP2 were highly upregulated in CCR7−IL7Rlow cells compared to CCR7−IL7Rhigh cells (Supplementary Figure S2). The results of GSEA showed enriched pathways such as cytolytic granule and endopeptidase complex in IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3b). Two of the four IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cell clusters expressed medium to high levels of GZMB, annotated as IL7RlowGZMBmed and high, respectively, also had high levels of GZMH, GNLY, and FGFBP2. The other two IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cell clusters, annotated as GZMK + CD27high and low, respectively, expressed high levels of GZMK but relatively low levels of GZMB, GNLY, and FGFBP2 (Fig. 3c). The four IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cell clusters increased in all three groups of patients with lupus (Managed, Flare, and Treated groups) compared to healthy controls (Fig. 3d). IL7RlowGZMK + CD27high subset, which contains cells expressing IFI27 and CD38, was increased in the Flare group compared to the Managed and Treated groups (Fig. 3d). The GSEA analysis of the IL7RlowGZMK + CD27high subset in this patient group showed the enrichment of pathways related to cell division and cell cycle regulation, suggesting that these cells have undergone cell activation (Supplementary Figure S3). CD8+ T cell infiltrations and cytotoxic molecule levels in the kidneys were associated with clinical outcomes in lupus nephritis

We examined CD8+ T cells and cytotoxic molecules in lupus nephritis tissue at the protein level using IMC as well as the relationship of such findings with treatment outcomes. Based on treatment outcomes after kidney biopsies, lupus nephritis tissue samples were divided into treatment responder and non-responder groups (Supplementary Table S1). Cell segmentations from all 19-kidney tissue samples, including two healthy controls, yielded approximately 82,000 cells across 14 clusters (data not shown). To focus on immune cells, we analysed haematopoietic (CD45+) cells. We identified eight different types of immune cells including CD14+CD16+ myeloid (CD68+, CD11c+), B (CD20+), NK (CD56+), CD4+ T (CD3+, CD4+), and CD8+ T (CD3+, CD8+) cells, and three mixed cell populations including mixed myeloid cells (Fig. 4a and b). In one representative treatment non-responder renal tissue, germinal centre-like structures formed with T cells surrounding B cells, and scattered CD8+ T cells were observed around tubular cells (Fig. 4c, tubule staining with CD164 and/or CK7). CD8+ T cells that were IL7Rlow were located in the interstitial, periglomerular, and intraglomerular areas, consistent with previous reports21,37 (Fig. 4c). The number of CD45+ cells per area of renal tissue was significantly higher in the treatment non-responder group compared to the treatment responder group (Fig. 4d), while few CD45+ cells were observed in normal kidney tissue. When comparing the relative proportions of individual immune cell types, the treatment non-responder group showed an increased proportion of CD8+ T cells, while the proportion of NK cells decreased (Fig. 4d, right panel). Additionally, cells expressing CD8 and cytotoxic molecules, including granulysin, granzyme B and perforin, were more abundant in the treatment non-responder group than in the treatment responder group (Fig. 4e). We also detected the presence of these cytotoxic molecules in the tubules of lupus nephritis (Fig. 4f), which likely reflects the binding of cytotoxic molecules to tubule cells. Our scRNA-seq analysis revealed an accumulation of IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells in the peripheral blood and kidneys of patients with lupus. Subsets of these cells, including GZMK+, GZMBhigh, and GZMBmed, largely expressed ISGs (Fig. 5a and b), suggesting a link between such cell accumulation and type I IFN. We thus analysed the correlation between the top 200 genes highly expressed by the IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cell subsets, referred to as the gene signatures of the individual cell subsets, and the type I IFN gene signature in the microarray datasets of peripheral blood of paediatric and adult patients with lupus. The type I IFN gene signature strongly correlated with the gene signatures of IL7RlowGZMBhigh and IL7RlowGZMKhigh EM CD8+ T cell subsets (Fig. 5c–f). IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells had increased capacity to induce neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) with IL-8 production

We found an accumulation of cytotoxic IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells in patients with lupus. Such cells could induce inflammation and damage. Our previous study reported that IL-7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells express high levels of the chemokine IL-8 receptor CXCR1,38 suggesting the interface of these cells with neutrophils which produce high levels of IL-8.39 Neutrophil activation has various functional effects, including migration into tissue, release of granule contents, and production of chemokines.40 In addition, neutrophils can form NETs which are composed of nuclear and granule contents that can entrap and kill microorganisms.40 Of note, NET formation or NETosis is known to promote autoimmunity, inflammation, and tissue damage in diseases including lupus.40 IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells can produce high levels of IFN-γ and TNF-α (Supplementary Figure S4),38 which could induce NETosis41,42 We thus evaluated whether IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells could promote NET formation. SUPs of FACS-purified IL7Rlow and high EM CD8+ T cells stimulated with or without anti-CD3 Abs were added to neutrophils followed by measuring myeloperoxidase (MPO) and MPO-DNA complex as markers for NET formation. Neutrophils incubated with SUPs of anti-CD3 Ab-stimulated IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells had increased levels of MPO and MPO-DNA complex compared to neutrophils incubated with SUPs of unstimulated IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells and stimulated IL7Rhigh EM CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6a and b). We also analysed the chemokine IL-8 which is released from activated neutrophils and forms neutrophil-driven immunopathology by further recruiting neutrophils. Indeed, neutrophils incubated with SUP of anti-CD3 Ab-stimulated IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells produced higher levels of IL-8 compared to neutrophils incubated with SUP of unstimulated IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells and stimulated IL7Rhigh EM CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6c). The possible interface of IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells and neutrophils via IL-8 was further supported by the finding that IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells had increased gene and protein expression of CXCR1 and CXCR2, the IL-8 receptors, compared to IL7Rhigh EM CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6e and f). Indeed, IL7R expression had an inverse quantitative relationship with CXCR1 and CXCR2 expression in EM CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6g) as determined by the conditional-Density Resampled Estimate of Mutual Information (DREMI) and conditional-Density Rescaled Visualisation (DREVI) algorithms.38,43 DREMI computes mutual information that describes how the state of Y varies with different states of X. DREMI scores indicate the strength of the statistical dependency between two molecules. DREVI visualises the function underlying this molecular interaction. Analysing chemotaxis to IL-8, IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells had increased migration to IL-8 compared to IL7Rhigh EM CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6h). Overall, these findings support a feed-forward mechanism for inflammatory responses mediated by the collaboration between IL7Rαlow EM CD8+ T cells and neutrophils via IL-8 and its receptors.

Discussion

We investigated the implications of CD8+ T cells in lupus by examining the characteristics of CD8+ T cells in the kidneys and PBMCs of patients with lupus and interrogating functional significance of CD8+ T cells in promoting innate immunity in the context of lupus. Our scRNA-seq analysis showed an accumulation of IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cell subsets which expressed cytotoxicity-related genes in the peripheral blood and kidneys of patients with lupus. Patients with lupus nephritis who were treatment non-responders had increased numbers and expression of CD8+ T cells and cytotoxic molecules, respectively, at the protein level in the kidney tissue as compared to treatment responders, supporting the involvement of such cells and molecules in impairing renal function. Indeed, IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells induced NET formation which is involved in the pathogenesis of lupus.23,44 These findings advance our understanding of the implications of CD8+ T cells, especially IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells expressing cytotoxic molecules, in sustaining inflammation and tissue damage in lupus.

We found an accumulation of IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cell subsets in the lupus kidney tissue and PBMCs obtained at flare as determined by scRNA-seq analysis. These subsets included one with high levels of GZMK and moderate levels of GZMH and PRF1 as well as two subsets expressing moderate to high levels of GZMB, GZMH, PRF1, and GNLY, but not GZMK. Similar findings were noticed in a large, publicly available scRNA-seq dataset from the PBMCs of patients with lupus and healthy controls. These findings are in line with the results of previous studies, including our own, which reported an accumulation of EM CD8+ T cells in peripheral blood of patients with lupus as determined by flow cytometry.11, 12, 13,15, 16, 17, 18 The accumulation of cytotoxic IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells is likely secondary to immune stimulations because our trajectory analysis showed that these cells were late differentiated cells compared to CD8+ T cells expressing high levels of IL7R and low levels of cytotoxicity-related genes. Indeed, the results of our study provide new insights into the expression of GZMK and its relationship with genes encoding other effector molecules in IL7Rlow and high EM CD8+ T cells. GZMK expressing EM CD8+ T cells were present in both IL7Rlow and high EM CD8+ T cells in peripheral blood and kidney tissues of healthy subjects and patients with lupus in our study. While a fraction of such cells expressed low to modest levels of genes encoding cytotoxic molecules including GZMB, GZMH, and GNLY, these genes were highly expressed in IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells without expressing GZMK. A combination of IL7R, GZMK, and cytotoxic molecules could be markers defining heterogeneity in EM CD8+ T cell subsets, which may alter in pathologic conditions like in lupus. We recognise that the number of immune cells (CD45RA+) in the normal kidney tissues from the AMP data used in our analysis was relatively small. Although this could affect the comprehensive assessment of EM CD8+ T cells subsets, half of the immune cells in the normal kidney tissues were T cells (Supplementary Figure S1b).

IL7R is known to become down regulated in T cells upon antigenic and cytokine stimulations.5,45 For instance, the accumulation of IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells were found in humans with ageing, especially at greater levels in the context of cytomegalovirus infection, as well as individuals with primary immunodeficiency and recurrent infections.5,46,47 Type I IFN, which increases in lupus, can play a role in upregulating cytotoxic molecules and increasing memory CD8+ T cells.48, 49, 50 In our study, the accumulated IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells with high levels of cytotoxicity-related genes had increased type I IFN gene signature in patients with lupus, supporting the possibility of type I IFN in promoting cytotoxic molecules in CD8+ T cells in lupus. This point is further supported by the correlation of cytotoxic IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cell gene signatures with the type I IFN gene signature in the peripheral blood of paediatric and adult patients with lupus. These findings provide a mechanistic justification for targeting type I IFN in lupus.

Of note, in Fig. 5B, type I IFN scores of three IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cell subsets, including IL7RlowGZMK + CD27high, GZMBmed, and GZMBhigh cells, were higher in the Managed lupus group than in the Healthy control group. However, the same cell subsets, especially the IL7RlowGZMK + CD27high subset, in the Treated lupus group appeared to have increased type I IFN scores compared to those in the Flare lupus group. This finding could be explained by the fact that only 10 out of 19 patients in the Flare group were included in the Treated group following treatment for flares. This discrepancy (19 vs. 10 subjects included at the times of flares and treatments (follow up), respectively) could affect the results of type I IFN scores because the 9 subjects in the Flare group who were not included in the Treated group for follow-up scRNA-seq analysis could have lower levels of type I scores at the time of flares compared to 10 subjects included in both Treated and Flare groups, resulting in decreased type I IFN scores in the Flare group.

Studies reported the relationship of CD8+ T cell infiltrations in the kidneys, especially tubulointerstitial CD8+ T cells, with the prognosis of lupus nephritis21,37,51 although the exact mechanism(s) underlying such a relationship is unclear. In our study, the accumulated IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cell subsets in the peripheral blood and kidney tissues of patients with lupus expressed high levels of cytotoxic molecule encoding genes although the heterogeneity exists in these cells. For instance, the expression of GZMB and GZMK appears to be mutually exclusive; IL7RlowGZMBhigh cells substantially expressed GNLY while IL7RlowGZMKhigh cells have low levels of these genes. Other cytotoxicity-related genes, including GZMH and PRF1, were expressed in both IL7RlowGZMBhigh and GZMKhigh EM CD8+ T cell subsets although the expression levels of these genes gradually increased with GZMB expression. Our IMC analysis suggests the possible pathologic implication of cytotoxic IL7Rlow CD8+ T cell accumulation since the frequency of CD8+ T cells and expression levels of cytotoxic molecules in the kidneys were associated with treatment outcomes of lupus nephritis. This notion is furthered by the staining of cytotoxic molecules, including granzyme B, perforin, and granulysin, in the kidney tubules of lupus nephritis. Granzyme B and perforin can synergistically induce apoptosis via caspase proteolytic activity.52 Granulysin can directly bind to the cell surface, disrupting membrane and causing ion fluxes and mitochondrial damage.53 These findings suggest that cytotoxic CD8+ T cells contribute to tissue damage and cell death in lupus nephritis by releasing cytotoxic molecules, which may provide a mechanistic explanation for the relationship of CD8+ T cell infiltrations with renal prognosis in lupus nephritis. We clearly recognise the clinical heterogeneity in lupus (e.g., disease activity), which could be related in part to CD8+ T cell immunity, possibly affecting treatment outcomes. Some CD8+ T cells have low expression levels of cytotoxic molecules in the IMC analysis of lupus nephritis, which likely reflects the heterogeneity of CD8+ T cells as noticed in our scRNA-seq analysis. Cytotoxic molecules identified in the kidney tubules of lupus nephritis could originate from non-CD8+ T cells such as NK cells. Of note, when comparing the relative proportions of individual immune cell types in our IMC analysis, the treatment non-responder group showed a decreased proportion of NK cells compared to the treatment responder group.

The pathologic impacts of accumulated cytotoxic IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells in lupus could be more than directly damaging tissue and killing cells. This point is supported by the results of our study showing the effect of IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells in increasing NET formation and IL-8 production in neutrophils via secreting IFN-γ and TNF-α. IFN-γ or TNF-α alone induced MPO-DNA complex formation, a marker for NET, and IL-8 production from neutrophils although a combination of both cytokines had a greater effect on these measurements (Supplementary Figure S5). In IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells, CXCR1high cells had higher levels of T-bet, the master regulator for IFN-γ and TNF-α expression,54 and CXCR2 expression compared to CXCR1low cells (Supplementary Figure S6). Thus, CXCR1highIL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells that can migrate to neutrophils via CXCR1/CXCR2 and IL-8 interface and express T-bet to produce IFN-γ and TNF-α would be the cellular source for the induction of the NET formation and IL-8 production from neutrophils. Dysregulated NET formation can promote inflammation and tissue injury in pathologic conditions such as lupus, including nephritis.22, 23, 24, 25 Increased IL-8, CXCR1, neutrophil infiltrations, and NET accumulation have been reported in lupus nephritis kidney tissue.25,55,56 Also, increased levels of IL-8 were reported in the urine of patients with active lupus nephritis than in those with inactive LN and in normal individuals.57 IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells had similar effects on neutrophils from patients with lupus as measured by MPO-DNA complex formation and IL-8 production (Supplementary Figure S7). The effects of IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells on neutrophils could be more than inducing NET formation and IL-8 production since neutrophils produced modest levels of CCL2 (MCP1), CCL3 (MIP1a), IL-6, M-CSF, and B cell activating factor (BAFF) in the presence of the SUPs of anti-CD3 Ab-stimulated IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Figure S8). CCL3 binds CCR1, CCR4, and CCR5 which are expressed on monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, B and/or T cells.58 The concentrations of CCL3 in the urine of patients with lupus nephritis increased.59

A recent study reported the enrichment of CD8+ T cells expressing GZMK in the kidney tissues of patients with lupus as compared to the peripheral blood of patients with lupus,20 which is in line with the results of our study. In this study, GZMK+ CD8+ T cells were found to promote the differentiation of atypical or age/autoimmune-associated B cells, which can produce autoantibodies, via IFN-γ production. A combination of this finding with ours further supports the pathogenic implications of CD8+ T cells, especially EM CD8+ T cells expressing effector molecules, in promoting autoimmune responses, inflammation, and tissue damage in lupus. Overall, our findings also demonstrate the collaborative interface of IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells with neutrophils based on IL-8 and its receptors CXCR1/2 in the human system. Having high levels of CXCR1/2, IL7Rαlow EM CD8+ T cells, neutrophils can move to the inflamed area with high levels of IL-8, resulting in cellular interactions involving IFN-γ and TNF-α secreted from IL7Rαlow EM CD8+ T cells and subsequent activation of neutrophils (Supplementary Figure S9, model). Such interface would provide a feed-forward mechanism of inflammation by releasing autoantigens and inflammatory mediators from neutrophils. These findings also raise the possible benefits of targeting such cellular interface in treating lupus.

We observed increased FOXP3+ Treg subset in lupus nephritis compared to normal kidney tissues in Fig. 1G as determined by scRNA-seq. It is known that FOXP3 can be induced in CD4+ T cells in response to T cell receptor (TCR) triggering, IL-2, and TGF-β, which is considered as a counterregulatory response to heightened inflammation.60 We thus speculate that increased FOXP3+ Treg cells in lupus nephritis tissues may represent a compensatory response to local inflammation in the kidneys of patients with SLE. This notion is supported by the results of a study showing increased FOXP3 expression in CD4+IL-17 A+ T cells in the kidney tissues of lupus nephritis compared to those of IgA nephropathy.61

We recognise limitations of our study. These include relatively small sample sizes in the scRNA-seq datasets of the paired peripheral blood samples and the fewer cell numbers of normal kidney tissues compared to those of lupus nephritis tissue in the scRNA-seq data. We also acknowledge the clinical heterogeneity of lupus, which supports the involvement of multiple pathogenic mechanisms. Thus, CD8+ T cell-mediated pathogenic contributions may vary across patients with SLE.

Taken together, our study provides insights into the pathogenesis of lupus by demonstrating the accumulation of cytotoxic IL7Rlow EM CD8+ T cells in the peripheral blood and kidneys of patients with lupus and the relationship of such cell infiltrations and cytotoxic molecules in the kidneys with clinical outcomes of lupus nephritis as well as by showing augmented NET formation and IL-8 production, the innate immune responses involved in lupus, by IL7Rαlow EM CD8+ T cells. These findings support the pathogenic implications of CD8+ T cells in lupus through directly damaging cells and tissues and collaboratively interfacing with neutrophils, raising the consideration of therapeutic targeting CD8+ T cell-mediated inflammation and damage.
