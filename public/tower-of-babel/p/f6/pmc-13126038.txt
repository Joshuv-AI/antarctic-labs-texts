Energy availability outperforms topographic heterogeneity in predicting global patterns of mountain endemic plant richness
Authors: Yuanyuan Gu, Jiaxin Ji, Zhimin Li, Zihan Jiang, Wenguang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMountain uplift alters both topographic complexity and energy patterns. However, the relative importance (RI) of these factors in shaping mountain diversity has not been systematically analyzed. This study aims to evaluate the RI of energy and topographic heterogeneity in the diversity pattern of endemic mountain plant genera in the world. The results show that energy (Tree: 69.45% ± 32.11%; Shrub: 64.44% ± 32.90%) is more explanatory than topographic heterogeneity (Tree: 58.54% ± 36.25%; Shrub: 64.09% ± 28.96%) in predicting the richness pattern of endemic woody taxa. For endemic herb plants, topographic heterogeneity is more explanatory (76.70% ± 14.34%). Across most mountain systems, the RI of energy (1.59 ± 0.85) is greater than that of topographic heterogeneity (1.26 ± 0.75). However, the importance of topographic heterogeneity is more prominent in mountainous areas with stable energy supplies (Cape of Good Hope). These findings provide a new perspective on the study of mountain diversity and emphasize the role of energy in diversity that cannot be ignored.

Introduction

Mountains cover approximately 24% of the Earth’s land surface and have extremely high biodiversity, making them an important system for studying global biodiversity patterns.1,2,3 Mountain ecosystems have long been central to ecological research. Relevant studies examining the roles of topographic complexity, climate variability, and human activities in shaping biodiversity patterns.4 However, how these factors interact and jointly influence mountain biodiversity remains poorly understood. Therefore, identifying the determinants of global mountain diversity remains a critical focus of contemporary ecological research.

Mountain uplifts alter both topographic complexity and the distribution of energy.3,5,6 In this context, topographic heterogeneity and energy are regarded as the two primary drivers of the spatial pattern of mountain biodiversity.3,7,8 Topographic heterogeneity promotes the coexistence of more species by increasing environmental gradients and habitat types, thereby increasing regional species diversity.9,10 In terms of energy, according to the species-energy hypothesis, habitats with greater energy availability can support higher numbers of individuals, thereby increasing species richness in a region.11,12

However, debate persists over whether topographic heterogeneity or energy is the dominant driver of global mountain biodiversity. The effects of topographic complexity on biodiversity have been extensively studied,10,13 and energy remains underexplored. This limits a comprehensive understanding of the drivers of mountain biodiversity. To fill this research gap, our core assumption is that energy is more important in mountain diversity. We will test this assumption with two objectives: (1) to quantify and compare the relative importance (RI) of both factors for biodiversity; and (2) to assess how their importance varies across mountain systems and plant taxa.

We focused on mountain endemic plant genera because they are usually distributed in a narrow range, and their survival and evolution are closely related to environmental factors such as local climate and topography.14 In contrast, the widely distributed species occur across multiple environments, resulting in a more ambiguous relationship between species richness and environmental factors. For example, the Andes host approximately 20,000 endemic plant species—more than any other mountain system15—making them an ideal natural laboratory for studying the processes of biodiversity formation.

Discussion

Our results provide novel evidence that energy may better explain the global patterns of diversity in mountain endemic plant genera than topographic heterogeneity. By comparatively analyzing endemic plant taxa across ten major mountain systems, we largely removed the confounding effects of differences in topography, area, and historical background among ranges, thereby obtaining more general conclusions.

Our results showed that in most mountain systems, energy had a higher RI for the diversity of endemic mountain plant taxa. This indicates that energy plays a dominant role in shaping large-scale diversity patterns. This is consistent with the species-energy hypothesis11 and the metabolic theory19: Higher energy flux increases productivity and population sizes, accelerates metabolic and evolutionary rates, and thereby promotes the coexistence of more species and higher lineage diversity. Monsoon trajectories and moisture pathways may also influence mountain diversity by shaping precipitation patterns, because the windward slope of the mountain usually has more precipitation. According to the water-energy dynamic hypothesis, water has an important influence on the energy utilization of organisms.20 O’Brien et al. believed that owing to the role of water, with the increase in energy, the diversity increased first and then decreased.20,21 Taking the Himalaya-Tibet Plateau-Hengduan Mountains as an example, the uplift of the mountain facilitated the formation and intensification of the monsoon.22 This results in higher biodiversity on the southern slope of the Himalayas.23 Similarly, the Andes block moisture originating from the Atlantic Ocean, resulting in higher biodiversity in the northern Andes.24 Meanwhile, the Andes are a young and tectonically active orogen,25 characterized by extensive ecotonal transitions and strong spatial environmental filtering.26,27 Together with energy, these processes are likely to contribute to and maintain the exceptionally high levels of plant endemism in this region. Because we considered only temperature, precipitation, AET, and solar radiation, the influence of other factors on energy cannot be ruled out. Nutrients in soil also play a key role in energy acquisition,28,29 and their water retention capacity will affect the absorption of water by plants, which may be a key factor influencing diversity in certain arid regions. In addition, topographic heterogeneity was more important in certain mountain systems, indicating that its role should not be overlooked. For instance, Zhang et al.30 found that in the Hengduan Mountains region, topographic heterogeneity still played a key role in shaping endemism and phylogenetic structure. Given the single spatial grain used in this study, it remains difficult to determine whether energy would also be more important than topographic heterogeneity at other spatial scales.

This study revealed that energy was more explanatory in predicting the diversity of endemic tree taxa, while the topographic heterogeneity played a more crucial role in endemic herb taxa. In analyzing endemic tree taxa, we discovered that energy exhibited higher importance. The findings corroborate those of previous studies.31,32,33,34,35 However, the diversity patterns of herb taxa showed higher sensitivity to topographic heterogeneity, similar to the results of previous studies.36,37 This phenomenon may be attributed to the distinct characteristics of plant taxa during species differentiation. Woody plants often have limited diffusivity, leading to relatively stable population genetic structure and weak gene flow.38 This limited dispersal capacity makes it difficult for woody plants to quickly track climate change or migrate to new habitats.39,40 Thus, woody taxa may depend more on local energy conditions to maintain metabolism and population size. In contrast, herbaceous plants have short lifespans and strong diffusivity. In heterogeneous environments, complex terrain can impose diverse local selection pressures on widespread populations while also providing geographical isolation.9 Therefore, in the mountain system, topographic heterogeneity may play a more direct role in the population differentiation and diversity maintenance of herb taxa.

In some mountain systems, diversity patterns are driven primarily by topographic heterogeneity. For example, in the Cape of Good Hope region, the importance of topographic heterogeneity was higher. This finding is consistent with existing research.41 Consequently, species differentiation and coexistence in this region are driven more by topographic heterogeneity than by responses to energy differences, thereby shaping the unique biodiversity pattern in the region. In this context, energy may no longer be a limiting factor, and the importance of topographic heterogeneity is more prominent. In addition, the biodiversity of the Cape of Good Hope area is also affected by other factors. For example, fires play a key role in the ecological evolution of the region, and long-term and frequent fires have shaped the regeneration and adaptation characteristics of plant communities.42,43 At the same time, soil types and bedrock characteristics exert strong ecological filtering effects on plant distribution and species differentiation.44,45 In addition, climatic fluctuations and geological uplift since the Neogene have promoted the formation and isolation of different ecosystems, providing favorable conditions for species differentiation.46 Combined with a long evolutionary history and regional climatic stability,47 these factors have jointly shaped the unique diversity pattern of the Cape Floristic Region, one of the world’s biodiversity hotspots.

Our finding that “energy dominates the diversity of mountain endemic plant taxa” provides a key framework for predicting the potential impact of future climate change. Given that climate change represents an adjustment of the global energy landscape, its impacts are expected to reshape mountain ecosystems.48 In energy-driven mountains, such as the Himalayas-Tibetan Plateau-Hengduan Mts., climate change can disrupt the interaction between plants and pollinators, which has a negative impact on both.49,50 In addition, climate warming has been shown to increase pressure from pests and pathogens.51 Changes in precipitation patterns may lead to extreme precipitation and long drought periods.52,53 Based on our research results, the current energy-sensitive mountain areas may be the most vulnerable to future climate change.

Limitations of the study

This study was mainly based on global-scale data, which introduced two inherent limitations. First, at the coverage level of spatial and environmental factors, limited by the resolution and accessibility of global data, it is difficult to explicitly incorporate many local drivers that have been shown to be equally important to biodiversity patterns, such as soil physical and chemical properties, wind direction and wind intensity, small-scale disturbances, and land use patterns.54,55,56 Therefore, the results of this study mainly reflect the associations of energy and topographic heterogeneity on a larger spatial scale, and the characterization of local-scale processes may not be complete. Second, in the mechanistic analysis, our model revealed statistical correlations but could not directly observe the specific ecological and evolutionary processes of species formation driven by energy and topographic heterogeneity. This limitation may have led to an underestimation of the role of topographic heterogeneity. Future research should overcome these limitations by integrating multi-scale data.

Our results revealed the importance of energy in the diversity patterns of mountain endemic plant taxa, particularly for woody taxa, whereas topographic heterogeneity remained influential for herb taxa. Energy is more important in most mountains. In conclusion, these results suggest that the diversity of mountain flora cannot be attributed to topography alone. Energy and topography must be considered together when interpreting and predicting mountain diversity patterns. This provides a novel perspective for understanding the diversity of mountain plants.

Method details

Data collection

Combining relevant literature and monographs (supplemental information, Table S2), we obtained high-precision geospatial data by retrieving species distribution records for these endemic genera from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF), Map of Life (MOL), Integrated Digitalized Biocollections (iDigBio), and Chinese Virtual Herbarium (CVH). First, we filtered the original distribution data to address biases in species occurrence. By removing (1) records with coordinate uncertainty greater than 25 km, (2) fossil records and records of unknown origin, (3) records collected before 1945, and (4) records with individual counts less than 1 and more than 99.63 Second, we used the R package “CoordinateCleaner”60 (version3.0.1) in R version 4.4.2 to clean the data. Finally, 227,310 records were retained. The study was grounded in the mountain endemic plants cataloged in the POWO database, with several additional entries. Following Dagnino et al. (2020),64 we treated a genus as mountain endemic genera when the proportion of its species occurring in mountains was ≥75%, resulting in 722 endemic genera. All species names were validated and standardized using WFO, POWO, and IPNI databases.

We classified the species into trees, shrubs, and herbs based on taxonomic information available from the following plant databases: POWO, WFO, and Flora of China. Finally, the distribution data were transferred to 0.25° × 0.25°grid cells to calculate endemic species richness. Compared with the commonly used 0.5°or 1°spatial scale,18,65 this resolution can better characterize the main environmental gradients in the mountainous environments, and was consistent with the research scale of Gottfried et al. (2012).66

Selection of environmental variables and data processing

Four factors were selected to characterize energy availability from various perspectives (Table 1): mean annual temperature (MAT), mean annual precipitation (MAP), actual evapotranspiration (AET), and mean annual solar radiation (MASR). Four topographic heterogeneity factors were selected (Table 1): mean elevation (ME), topographic relief (TR), standard deviation of elevation (STDE), and the ratio of surface area (TRSA).18,67 All climate data were sourced from WorldClim (https://www.worldclim.org/) at a spatial resolution of 30 arc seconds. Elevation data were obtained from the GDEM V3 30-meter Digital Elevation Model (https://www.gscloud.cn/). Global mountain polygon data were sourced from the GMBA mountain inventory_V1.2.68

All environmental variables were uniformly resampled to a grid resolution of 0.25 ° × 0.25 ° using bilinear interpolation with an area ratio of ≥50%. MAT, MAP, MASR, ME, TR, and STDE were directly extracted using ArcMap 10.8. The AET17 calculation formula is as follows. Statistical analysis

Based on the “mgcv” package (version 1.9–1) in R version 4.4.2, we employed generalized additive models (GAMs) to analyze the relationships between environmental variables and endemic species richness. The GAM model is a semi-parametric extension of the generalized linear model (GLM), which can directly fit the nonlinear relationship between the dependent variable and multiple independent variables. It is suitable for function analysis of various types of distribution.61,70

First, we performed single-factor prediction fitting of endemic species richness for each variable. We employed the Poisson error distribution or quasi-Poisson error distribution (variance >mean) based on the data feature characteristics of endemic species richness. We assessed the goodness of fit for each model and the strength of the relationship between predictors and endemic species richness by calculating the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). The QAIC value represents a modification of the AIC, tailored for overdispersed response variables through the application of quasi-likelihood methods.71 Additionally, for all significant single-factor prediction models, we extracted the deviance explained (D-E) from the GAM output to quantify the extent to which each model explains the variation in endemic species richness.

We used spatial GAMs to account for residual spatial autocorrelation and thereby reduce bias in parameter estimates and significance tests. Residual spatial autocorrelation was assessed using Moran’s I tests72 results [using the R package “spdep” (version 1.4–1)]. When significant spatial autocorrelation was detected, we refitted all single-factor and global models with a spatial smoothing term of geographic coordinates [s(Longitude, Latitude)].

We employed a multi-model information theory approach to assess the importance of environmental variables in predicting endemic species richness patterns among various mountain endemic plant taxa.71 The explanatory variables in the principal model were selected based on the significant single-factor prediction models identified in the previous step. Prior to model selection, we needed to solve the collinearity problem between the variables.73 When a strong correlation was found between two variables (Pearson’s |r| > 0.7),74 we excluded variables with higher AIC/QAIC.

Model selection was performed using the dredge function from the R package “MuMIN”(version1.48.4) in R version 4.4.2.62 In this process, we performed various combinations of all selected variables. We assessed the relative importance of each explanatory variable by calculating the sum of the Akaike weights across all candidate models.74 We used the method of permutation importance and summed the relative importance of the variables to quantify the overall importance of energy and topographic heterogeneity.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Using Microsoft Excel 2016, we calculated the explanatory rates of different mountain endemic plant taxa, as well as the relative importance of energy and topographic heterogeneity in different mountain systems (mean ± standard deviation). In R software (version 4.4.2), generalized additive models (GAMs) were used to analyze the relationships between environmental variables and species richness, and to evaluate the explanatory power and relative importance of each environmental factor. At the same time, the spdep package (version 1.4–1) was used to detect and address potential spatial autocorrelation issues. Finally, a multi-model inference approach using the MuMIn package (version 1.48.4) was employed to compare the relative importance of different combinations of variables. The correlation analysis among environmental variables was also conducted in this version of R software.
