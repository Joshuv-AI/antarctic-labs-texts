Dissecting seed composition QTL from wild soybean: fine-mapping, candidate gene identification, and evaluation of introgression effects on agronomic performance
Authors: Elizabeth De Meyer, Anser Mahmood, Mariola Usovsky, Jason Gillman, Bing Yang, Jared Decker, Qijian Song, Clinton Meinhardt, Michael Dennigmann, Asheesh K. Singh, George Graef, Eliana Monteverde, Andrew Scaboo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Key messageSeed composition QTL from wild soybean were confirmed and validated in two genetic backgrounds across multiple environments, candidate genes were identified, and agronomic performance of backcross introgression lines was evaluated.AbstractThrough selection for soybean yield, breeders have inadvertently reduced seed protein content and increased oil due to phenotypic and genetic correlations between these three traits. Therefore, identifying alleles that increase protein without adversely affecting oil and yield is of interest for breeders and the entire soybean value chain. Previously, a G. max × G. soja population was used to map a protein-associated region to ~ 4.6 Mbp on chromosome (Chr) 14. The G. soja allele significantly increased protein 6.5-7.2 g kg−1, without significantly decreasing oil. Additionally, two oil quantitative trait loci (QTL) were reported on Chrs 8 and 14. In this study, we aimed to confirm the Chr 14 protein QTL, evaluate QTL effects on seed composition and agronomic performance, and further fine-map to identify candidate genes. We validated and fine-mapped the Chr 14 protein QTL to a 0.6 Mbp region in a different genetic background, where the G. soja allele significantly increased protein by 9.3 g kg−1. Further, we confirmed the Chr 14 oil QTL linked to the protein QTL and the Chr 8 oil QTL. Chr 14 protein QTL effects on agronomic traits were evaluated in a backcross population across eight environments. The QTL significantly increased protein content, without significantly impacting oil, maturity, or plant height. While the QTL impacted yield and lodging, its effect and significance varied within environments. The candidate genes identified for these three validated seed composition QTL, along with additional molecular markers developed, offer valuable resources for improving seed composition in soybean breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00122-025-05048-x.

Introduction

Soybean [Glycine max (L.) Merr.] is a valuable crop worldwide, due to its relatively high protein and oil content and diverse uses in industrial applications, as livestock feed, and human food (Wilson 2004; Masuda and Goldsmith 2009). In 2022, soybean production worldwide totaled 353.7 million metric tons, more than tripling from the 114.0 million metric tons produced thirty years earlier in 1992 (FAO 2025). Soybean seed protein and oil content vary but are typically ~ 400 g kg−1 and ~ 200 g kg−1, respectively, forming the basis of the two main economically important components of soybean, protein meal and refined oil (Wilson 2004; Warrington et al. 2015). In the USA alone, the value of soybean production in the 2022/2023 marketing year was $60.7 billion, and for the same year, soybean made up 69% of the world’s protein meal consumption and 59% of the world’s oilseed production (ASA 2024).

To meet increasing demands over time, soybean breeders have continually developed and released cultivars with improved grain yield (Rincker et al. 2014; Rogers et al. 2015; Boehm et al. 2019). However, by prioritizing selection for soybean yield, breeders have inadvertently reduced seed protein content and increased oil content due to the negative phenotypic correlation and pleiotropic effect of major seed protein genes with oil and yield (Chung et al. 2003; Wilson 2004; Warrington et al. 2015; Guo et al. 2022). In an evaluation of genetic improvement for US soybean maturity groups (MG) II, III, and IV, the linear rate of genetic yield gain was 20 to 23 kg ha−1 year−1 from breeding efforts between 1923 and 2008 (Rincker et al. 2014). Seed protein decreased at a rate of 0.16 to 0.22 g kg−1 year−1 depending on MG, while seed oil content increased at a rate of 0.05 to 0.14 g kg−1 year−1 (Rincker et al. 2014). Additional genetic gain studies including MG IV, V, VI, and VII soybean cultivars released from 1928 to 2008 showed similar genetic gain estimates for yield, protein, and oil (Rogers et al. 2015; Boehm et al. 2019). These trends in genetic gain over time could result from selection of individual genes with pleiotropic effects on multiple traits or tightly linked genes impacting correlated traits (Chung et al. 2003; Leamy et al. 2017; Goettel et al. 2022).

Selective breeding for increased yield, in addition to domestication and genetic bottlenecks, has traditionally resulted in unintentional shifts in allelic frequencies, increased linkage disequilibrium (LD), and the loss of rare alleles (Halliburton 2004). A study examining nucleotide diversity in wild soybean (G. soja), landraces, North American ancestors, and elite cultivars showed that half of the genetic diversity in G. soja was lost in elite cultivars due to domestication and selection (Hyten et al. 2006). Reductions in nucleotide diversity have been reported in additional studies due to domestication-related genetic bottlenecks (Li et al. 2014; Zhou et al. 2015; Valliyodan et al. 2016). It has also been shown that only 19% of the rare alleles in G. soja remain in elite cultivars (Hyten et al. 2006). Diversity of G. soja has been utilized to identify quantitative trait loci (QTL) underlying traits of interest, including soybean cyst nematode resistance (Wang et al. 2001; Winter et al. 2007; Zhang et al. 2017b), seed yield (Concibido et al. 2003; Wang et al. 2004; Beche et al. 2020), and seed composition (Leamy et al. 2017; Beche et al. 2020; Fliege et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2025). Therefore, diverse germplasm and wild soybean accessions can offer valuable genetic resources for improving seed composition in modern cultivars (Wilson 2004; Hyten et al. 2006; La et al. 2019). An evaluation of a core collection of the USDA-ARS Germplasm Resources Information Network (GRIN) Soybean Germplasm Collection observed G. soja accession seed protein content ranging from 392.6 to 481.7 g kg−1 and seed oil ranging from 157.6 to 175.8 g kg−1, while for eight G. max accessions, protein content ranged from 360.2 to 402.5 g kg−1 and oil content ranged from 214.0 to 258.9 g kg−1 (La et al. 2019). Identification, characterization, and introgression of the alleles underlying this phenotypic variation in protein and oil content in G. soja germplasm can be beneficial for the development of high-yielding soybean cultivars with improved seed composition profiles.

Quantitative trait loci for seed protein and oil content have been mapped to all 20 soybean chromosomes, with a SoyBase query of “seed protein” and “seed oil” returning 204 QTL and 252 QTL, respectively, as of May 2025 (Grant et al. 2009). Of these numerous QTL, two major protein QTL on chromosomes (Chrs) 15 and 20, initially mapped by Diers et al. (1992), have been fully characterized and the genes underlying the QTL cloned (Miao et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2020; Fliege et al. 2022; Goettel et al. 2022). Both genes are associated with significantly increased seed protein content from G. soja alleles; however, they are also associated with decreased oil content (Miao et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2020; Fliege et al. 2022; Goettel et al. 2022). These two major QTL have also been identified in multiple biparental mapping and association studies, although the effect size of the QTL varied depending on population and environment (Lee et al. 1996; Brummer et al. 1997; Sebolt et al. 2000; Chung et al. 2003; Fasoula et al. 2004; Wang et al. 2014; Warrington et al. 2015; Bandillo et al. 2015; Kim et al. 2016; Qi et al. 2016; Phansak et al. 2016; Chen et al. 2025). Impacts of these QTL on yield have also been examined using backcross introgression lines (Brzostowski et al. 2017; Brzostowski and Diers 2017). The Chr 15 QTL high-protein allele showed a nonsignificant decrease in yield across environments (Brzostowski and Diers 2017), while Chr 20 high-protein alleles showed more variable impacts on yield depending on genetic background and environment (Brzostowski et al. 2017). Additional seed protein QTL from wild soybean have been reported on Chrs 6, 8, 13, 14, 18, 19, and 20 in genome wide association studies (GWAS) and QTL mapping studies along with seed oil QTL mapped to Chrs 2, 3, 7, 8, 9, 14, 15, 17, 19, and 20 (Leamy et al. 2017; Patil et al. 2018). A recent nested association mapping (NAM) study using interspecific populations reported protein and oil QTL on all 20 chromosomes (Chen et al. 2025). These studies demonstrate the utility of wild soybean for improving seed composition; however, further research is needed to identify and characterize loci responsible for increasing protein without adversely affecting oil and yield.

Previously, two novel QTL, one for seed protein content on Chr 14 and another for seed oil content on Chr 8, were detected within a recombinant inbred line (RIL) population derived from a cross between a MG V G. max cultivar Osage (Chen et al. 2007) and a G. soja accession PI 593983 (La 2018). This population was also used to map QTL for yield, maturity, height, lodging score, and branch length (La 2018). The Chr 14 protein QTL was subsequently validated and fine-mapped using a residual heterozygote derived near isogenic line (RHD-NIL) population (Yang et al. 2022). The RHD-NIL population was used to fine-map the region from ~ 16.5 to ~ 4.6 megabase pairs (Mbp) containing 223 genes which were narrowed to eight candidate genes based on gene ontologies (Yang et al. 2022). The G. soja allele in this region significantly increased protein by 6.5–7.2 g kg−1, without significantly decreasing oil (Yang et al. 2022). However, strong negative correlations between seed protein and oil content were observed in both studies (r = –0.77 and r = -0.85; p < 0.0001). In our current study, we aimed to: (1) confirm the Chr 14 protein QTL, Chr 14 oil QTL, and Chr 8 oil QTL in a different genetic background using an F3-derived confirmation mapping population; (2) develop BC3F2-derived QTL introgression lines to validate the Chr 14 protein QTL effects on seed composition and evaluate agronomic performance; (3) fine-map the Chr 14 protein QTL by developing additional molecular markers within the Chr 14 protein QTL region to increase resolution and facilitate marker-assisted selection; and (4) identify candidate genes for seed protein and oil content within the fine-mapped regions.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

An F3-derived confirmation mapping population was developed by crossing LD15-6762 × TL14-110–822 in the summer of 2020 at the Farm for Soy Innovation, Columbia, MO. The TL14-110–822 parent was an F9:11 RHD-NIL previously described in Yang et al. (2022) which carries the G. soja PI 593983 allele for the Chr 14 seed protein QTL region but not the Chr 20 seed protein QTL. The LD15-6762 parent is an elite MG III cultivar developed by the University of Illinois soybean breeding program, which was selected for its relatively lower protein content than the TL14-110–822 parent and appropriate MG for northern Missouri. F1 plants from this initial cross were grown in a winter nursery in Ponce, Puerto Rico 2020–2021, and resulting F2 seeds were grown at the Farm for Soy Innovation, Columbia, MO, in summer 2021. F2 plants grown in Columbia, MO, were single plant threshed to collect F2:3 seeds. Ten F2:3 seeds from each F2 plant were grown in a winter nursery in Kekaha, Kauai, Hawaii 2021–2022, a single plant was tagged, and one trifoliate leaf was tissue-sampled and sent back to Columbia, MO, for DNA extraction. The tagged F2:3 plants were single plant harvested, and the F3:4 seeds were sent back to Columbia, MO. This resulted in a total of 213 F3:4 lines which were used for field experiments and subsequent confirmation linkage mapping.

A BC3F2-derived backcross population was developed by initially crossing SA14-9653 × TL14-110–822 in the summer of 2020 at the Farm for Soy Innovation, Columbia, MO. The TL14-110–822 parent was an F9:11 RHD-NIL previously described in Yang et al. (2022) which carries the G. soja allele for the protein QTL region. The SA14-9653 parent is an elite MG III cultivar developed by the University of Missouri Northern Missouri soybean breeding program, which was selected for its relatively high protein content for commodity soybean and appropriate MG for northern Missouri. F1 plants from this initial cross were grown in a winter nursery in Ponce, Puerto Rico 2020–2021, and the following cross was made: SA14-9653 × F1 (SA14-9653 × TL14-110–822). At each stage of backcrossing, the ED-5 kompetitive allele-specific PCR (KASP) assay (SNP position: Chr14:8,059,955; Wm82.a2.v1), previously developed within the QTL region by Yang et al. (2022), was used to select offspring heterozygous for the ED-5 assay for further crossing. BC1F1 seeds resulting from the first backcross were grown during the second season in Ponce, PR in spring 2021 and the following cross was made using selected ED-5 heterozygous plants: SA14-9653 × F1 [SA14-9653 × F1 (SA14-9653 × TL14-110–822)]. The resulting BC2F1 seeds were grown in summer 2021 at the Farm for Soy Innovation, Columbia, MO, and the following cross was made: SA14-9653 × F1 [SA14-9653 × F1 [SA14-9653 × F1 (SA14-9653 × TL14-110–822)]]. During the winter of 2021–2022, the BC3F1 and BC3F2 generations were grown at a winter nursery in Kekaha, Kauai, HI. ED-5 heterozygous plants were selected within all BC3F1 plants and allowed to self-pollinate to create BC3F2 seeds. The resulting BC3F2 plant populations were grown, and ED-5 homozygous plants were selected and harvested separately. The BC3F2:3 seeds were sent back to the Farm for Soy Innovation, Columbia, MO, grown in plant rows, and plant rows were bulk harvested for seed increase in summer 2022. This resulted in a total of 77 BC3F2:4 lines which were used for subsequent field trials, with 41 lines homozygous for the G. soja allele and 36 lines homozygous for the G. max allele based on the ED-5 assay.

Field experiments

Confirmation mapping population field experiments

The F3-derived confirmation mapping population of 213 lines, along with LD15-6762 and TL14-110–822 parent lines, were grown in four environments with two replications in randomized complete block design (RCBD) across three years. The four environments included 2022, 2023, and 2024 at the Farm for Soy Innovation in Columbia, MO (22CLM, 23CLM, and 24CLM), and 2024 at Ross Jones Research Farm in Novelty, MO (24NOV). Field trials included one-row plots planted using a four-row ALMACO cone planter with Kinze row units (ALMACO, Nevada, IA, USA) with 0.76 m spacing between rows. The 23CLM environment had plot dimensions of 0.76 m by 2.74 m, with seeds sown at the rate of 22 seeds m–1, and alleys of 0.91 m between ranges. The 24CLM and 24NOV environments had plot dimensions of 0.76 m by 3.66 m, with seeds sown at the rate of 16 seeds m–1, and alleys of 1.22 m between ranges. Phenotypes were collected on each plot for flower and pubescence color, days from planting to physiological maturity (95%), lodging score (1–5 scale), and plant height (cm). Maturity days after planting (DAP) was measured as the number of days from planting until 95% of the pods reached R8 reproductive stage (Fehr et al. 1971). Lodging was rated at maturity on a scale 1 to 5, where 1 indicates all plants fully upright, and 5 indicates all plants fully lodged. Plots were manually harvested and threshed at maturity. Due to heavy rain and reduced emergence, the 22CLM environment was removed from further analysis.

Backcross population field experiments

The BC3F2-derived backcross population of 77 lines, along with the recurrent parent SA14-9653 and four check cultivars, were grown in eight environments with three replications in randomized complete block design (RCBD) across two years. The eight environments included two 2023 environments at the Farm for Soy Innovation in Columbia, MO (23CLM), and Graves-Chapple Research Center in Rock Port, MO (23ROK), along with six 2024 environments at Ross Jones Research Farm in Novelty, MO (24NOV), Graves-Chapple Research Center in Rock Port, MO (24ROK), St. Joseph, MO (24STJMO), Urbana, IL (24ILL), Julius Farm near Ames, IA (24IOW), and Lincoln, NE (24NEB). All eight environments utilized four-row plots with 0.76 m spacing between rows. Plot dimensions were 3.05 m by 3.66 m, with seeds sown at the rate of 33 seeds m–1, and alleys of 1.22 m between ranges. Phenotypes were collected on the middle two rows of each plot for flower and pubescence color, days from planting to physiological maturity (95%), lodging score (1–5 scale), and plant height (cm). Maturity (DAP) was measured as the number of days from planting until 95% of the pods reached R8 reproductive stage (Fehr et al. 1971). The middle two rows of each plot were combine harvested, and grain yield and seed moisture per plot were measured during harvest. Grain yield was adjusted to 13% moisture basis and converted to kg ha−1.

Phenotyping for seed protein and oil content

Confirmation mapping population

Samples of ~ 50 mL clean seed were prepared for all plots of the 213 F3-derived lines and parent lines and whole seed samples were sent to the University of Missouri Agricultural Experiment Station Chemical Laboratories (ESCL) in Columbia, MO, for seed composition analyses. Seed samples were ground and moisture content was measured by vacuum oven (AOAC International 2006a). Crude protein was analyzed by combustion (AOAC International 2006b) using a TruSpec model FP-428 nitrogen analyzer (LECO, St. Joseph, MI, USA) and multiplying the total nitrogen concentration by 6.25 to estimate crude protein content. Crude fat was analyzed using ether extraction and the weight of extracted fat was measured and reported as percent of total sample weight (AOAC International 2000). Moisture was reported as a percentage of the seed as-is, and crude protein and crude fat values were reported as percent on a dry matter basis and were converted to g kg−1 before further analyses. Crude protein is referred to as protein content and crude fat as oil content throughout the following sections.

Backcross population

Samples of ~ 50 mL clean seed were prepared from all plots of the 77 BC3F2-derived backcross lines and recurrent parent SA14-9653 grown in all eight environments. Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIR) was used to predict seed composition of whole seed samples on a Perten DA 7250 machine (PerkinElmer, Inc., Stockholm, Sweden) at the Farm for Soy Innovation in Columbia, MO. NIR was used to phenotype seed composition due to consistent yellow seed coat color within the backcross lines compared to segregating seed coat color within the confirmation mapping population. Calibrations are updated annually by the Soybean NIR Consortium. This study used the NIR calibration for 2022 whole soybean. Moisture was reported as a percentage of the seed as-is, and the protein and oil content values were reported as percent on a dry matter basis and were converted to g kg−1 before further analyses.

Genotypic data

Confirmation mapping population 3K SNP genotyping

Single leaves of F3 plants growing in winter nursery in Kekaha, Kauai, HI, were lyophilized and sent to the Farm for Soy Innovation in Columbia, MO. Seed of parent lines (LD15-6762 and TL14-110–822) harvested from the original crossing source were also grown and leaf tips from the first trifoliates of six different plants were tissue-sampled for each parent. Total genomic DNA was extracted using a modified cetyl trimethyl ammonium bromide (CTAB) method (Doyle and Doyle 1987). DNA samples were sent to the Soybean Genomics and Improvement Laboratory, USDA-ARS, Beltsville, MD, for genotyping with the Illumina Infinium BARCSoySNP3K BeadChip, containing a subset of the BARCSoySNP6K BeadChip (Song et al. 2024). SNP alleles were called using GenomeStudio Genotyping Module 2.0 (Illumina, Inc. San Diego, CA, USA). The SNP positions were converted from Wm82.a1.v1 assembly to Wm82.a2.v1 (Schmutz et al. 2010). SNP positions were filtered in TASSEL (Bradbury et al. 2007) by removing those with greater than 10% missing and heterozygosity greater than 30% or less than 20% (based on 25% expected in the F3), which resulted in 1,028 polymorphic SNPs. Parent offspring analysis was conducted using the using the R package “ParentOffspring” (Abdel-Haleem et al. 2013), and no lines were under 95% similarity. TASSEL (Bradbury et al. 2007) was used to conduct ABH parental calls. The “ABHgenotypeR” package in R (Reuscher and Furuta 2016) functions “imputeByFlanks,” “correctUndercalledHets,” and “correctStretches” were used with maxHapLength = 2 to QC and impute missing data based on parental haplotypes.

Backcross population 6K SNP genotyping

Approximately 15 BC3F2:4 seeds of each backcross line were grown in the greenhouse, leaf tips from the first trifoliates of six different plants were tissue-sampled for each line, and total genomic DNA was extracted using a modified cetyl trimethyl ammonium bromide (CTAB) method (Doyle and Doyle 1987). DNA samples were sent to the Soybean Genomics and Improvement Laboratory, USDA-ARS, Beltsville, MD, for genotyping with the Illumina Infinium BARCSoySNP6K BeadChip (Song et al. 2020). SNP alleles were called using GenomeStudio Genotyping Module 2.0 (Illumina, Inc. San Diego, CA, USA). The SNP positions were converted from Wm82.a1.v1 assembly to Wm82.a2.v1 (Schmutz et al. 2010). SNP positions were filtered in TASSEL (Bradbury et al. 2007) by removing those with greater than 50% missing, minor allele frequency less than 5%, and heterozygosity greater than 50%, which resulted in 933 polymorphic SNPs. Missing data was imputed using LD-KNNi in TASSEL (Bradbury et al. 2007), considering 100 high-LD sites, 60 nearest neighbors, and a maximum LD distance of 10 Mbps.

Whole-genome re-sequencing

Nineteen backcross lines were selected for whole-genome re-sequencing (WGRS) based on high and low protein content within ED-5 assay homozygous allelic classes. These included the five highest and five lowest protein lines with the ED-5 G. soja allele and the five highest and four lowest protein lines with the ED-5 G. max allele. The SA14-9653 recurrent parent was also included. Approximately 10 BC3F2:4 seeds of each backcross line and SA14-9653 seed from the original crossing block were grown in the greenhouse, and the first trifoliate leaf set was collected from one plant per line. Total genomic DNA was extracted using a Promega Maxwell® RSC Instrument with the Maxwell® RSC PureFood GMO and Authentication Kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). DNA samples were sent to Azenta Life Sciences (Indianapolis, IN, USA) for WGRS at 15 × coverage using Illumina paired-end 150 bp reads. WGRS data for the TL14-110–822 donor parent was previously generated by Yang et al. (2022), and FASTQ files were retrieved from NCBI SRA (Run ID: SRR19807611). Variants were called using the SnakyVC pipeline (Chan et al. 2023) with Wm82.a6.v1 assembly (Espina et al. 2024) downloaded from Phytozome (Goodstein et al. 2012) used as the reference genome for read mapping. GCViT (Wilkey et al. 2020) was used to visualize introgressions across the genome in eight backcross lines with the Chr 14 protein QTL G. soja allele.

Molecular marker assay development and genotyping

A total of 18 KASP assays were developed from 5 to 13 Mbps (Wm82.a2.v1) on Chr 14 to increase resolution for fine-mapping (Table S1). Forty-one assays were initially developed every 200 kb across the region and were selected based on SNP position and confirmed polymorphism between the parental lines of the confirmation and backcross populations. Assays were manufactured by KASP-by-design from LGC Biosearch Technologies (LGC Genomics, LLC, Alexandria, MN, USA). Assays were initially tested for allele discrimination between parental lines, and 18 KASP assays with clear separation of allelic groups were used for further genotyping. KASP assays were named according to their Wm82.a2.v1 physical positions on Chr 14 in Mbp.

The 18 developed KASP assays and the ED-5 KASP assay were used to genotype both the confirmation mapping and backcross populations and their parental lines. Approximately 20 F3:4 and BC3F2:4 seeds of each confirmation mapping and backcross line, respectively, were grown in the greenhouse, and leaf punches from the first trifoliates of 12 different plants were bulked for each line. DNA was extracted using a nonhazardous method described by (King et al. 2014) and DNA concentration was adjusted to 80 ng uL−1 using a Mantis Liquid Dispenser (Formulatrix, Dubai, United Arab Emirates). KASP reaction mixtures and conditions were prepared according to the standard protocol described by LGC Biosearch Technologies (LGC Genomics, LLC, Alexandria, MN, USA). Fluorescent end-point genotyping assays were conducted using a Roche LightCycler 480-II instrument (Roche Applied Sciences, Indianapolis, IN, USA). Addition of the 19 KASP markers to the SNP array-based genotyping data resulted in a total of 1,047 polymorphic markers for the confirmation mapping population and 952 for the backcross population.

Linkage map construction and QTL analysis

For the confirmation mapping population of 213 F3-derived lines with 1,047 polymorphic SNP markers, a linkage map was generated using the R “qtl” package (Broman et al. 2003). Genetic distances were estimated using the “est.map” function, with the genotyping error rate set to 0.0001. Recombination fractions between all pairs of markers were estimated using the “est.rf” function. QTL analysis for all traits was performed using the R “qtl” package (Broman et al. 2003). The “calc.genoprob” function was used to compute genotype probabilities at observed marker positions using the Kosambi mapping function, with a genotyping error rate of 0.0001. Composite interval mapping (“cim” function) was performed using the Haley–Knott method with default parameters to detect QTL. Empirical logarithm of odds (LOD) thresholds were determined at the 5% probability level using 1,000 permutations for each trait. The percentage of phenotypic variance explained by significant QTL was estimated using the “effectplot” function. Confidence intervals for significant QTL were determined using the “bayesint” function with a 95% confidence interval.

Fine-mapping and candidate gene identification

To identify candidate genes within three confirmed QTL regions (Wm82.a2.v1 reference genome positions Chr14:7,980,639–8595308 for protein content, and Chr08:8,725,772–9,428,417 and Chr14:4,602,230–6561966 for oil content), we utilized WGRS data collected on the TL14-110–822 parent, SA14-9653 parent, and a subset of backcross lines. Variant call data including SNPs and small insertions and deletions (indels) resulting from raw read alignments against the Wm82.a6.v1 assembly (Espina et al. 2024) was evaluated using the Soybean Genome Explorer Tool (https://github.com/AnserM/SGE/). This tool allows comparison of variant call data from cultivars and accessions to generate reports of polymorphic variants categorized by their SnpEff variant annotations (Cingolani et al. 2012). The variants are categorized into low-, moderate-, or high-impact categories based on their predicted impact on coding sequences of annotated genes. High-impact variants are categorized as having a predicted high impact in the resulting protein, likely resulting in protein truncation, loss of function, or causing nonsense mediated decay (Cingolani et al. 2012). The QTL region positions identified using Wm82.a2.v1 were updated to Wm82.a6.v1 by BLAST of 300 bp surrounding sequence using Phytozome (Goodstein et al. 2012). For our analysis, the genes containing high-impact variants originating from the TL14-110–822 parent within the QTL regions were considered candidates. In addition to comparisons of WGRS data, gene ontology descriptions including Arabidopsis ortholog ontologies, EuKaryotic Orthologous Groups (KOG) and InterPro descriptions for the candidate genes within QTL regions were retrieved from Phytozome (Goodstein et al. 2012). The ontologies of genes within the QTL region were evaluated for biological relevance to soybean seed protein and oil content to identify promising candidates. Additionally, genes within each of the three QTL regions were evaluated for gene ontology enrichment at GlycineMine (https://mines.legumeinfo.org/glycinemine/begin.do). For this analysis, the p values were adjusted using Bonferonni–Holm correction and the threshold was set to 0.05.

Evaluation of Chr 14 protein QTL impacts on agronomic traits

For the backcross population, the ED-5 assay was used to evaluate effects of the Chr 14 protein QTL region on protein and oil content and agronomic traits. Three lines were identified as outliers for maturity which contained an alternate E2 maturity gene allele and removed from further analyses. One additional line was removed based on consistent heterozygous allele calls for markers in the QTL region. This resulted in 41 lines homozygous for G. soja and 32 lines homozygous for G. max at the ED-5 marker which were evaluated for QTL effects on seed quality and agronomic performance. The backcross lines and recurrent parent were plotted by ED-5 marker allele for protein content, oil content, yield, maturity, plant height, and lodging using “ggplot2” in R (Wickham 2016). Significant differences for traits by ED-5 marker allele were determined by ANOVA using the “aov” function in R (R Core Team 2024). Tukey HSD tests were also performed using the “HSD.test” function in the “agricolae” package in R (de Mendiburu 2023) on the backcross population evaluated across eight environments with three replications per environment to determine significant differences (α = 0.05) in backcross lines compared with the recurrent parent SA14-9653. Tukey HSD tests were performed for all traits on two sets of the backcross lines separated by their homozygous ED-5 marker allele.

Agronomic traits

QTL for maturity across all environments were detected on Chrs 6 and 10, each explaining 68.5 and 8.9% of the total phenotypic variance, respectively (Fig. S6; Table 3). The TL14-110–822 parent allele for the peak SNP markers at each maturity QTL showed a 15.2 day increase for Chr 6 and a 5.3 day increase for Chr 10 (Fig. S6; Table 3). Fine-mapping and candidate gene identification for seed composition QTL

Through our confirmation mapping, we were able to confirm three previously identified QTL for protein and oil content from Yang et al. (2022) and further fine-map two of the regions (Fig. 4). WGRS data within these regions from backcross and parent lines were used to identify candidate genes containing high-impact variants originating from the TL14-110–822 parent. The Wm82.a6.v1 reference genome was used (Espina et al. 2024) and the positions queried included the narrowest overlapping region for each confirmed QTL updated to Wm82.a6.v1 positions by BLAST: Chr 14 protein QTL at Chr14:8,371,815–8,909,883; Chr 8 oil QTL at Chr08:8,770,303–9,465,207, and Chr 14 oil QTL at Chr14:5,444,706–6,434,386. This analysis resulted in a total of two candidate genes for the Chr 14 protein QTL, five for the Chr 8 oil QTL, and six for the Chr 14 oil QTL (Table 4). Evaluation of gene ontologies for genes within QTL regions did not reveal additional promising candidates with biological relevance to seed composition in soybean. Agronomic and seed composition effects of Chr 14 protein QTL introgression

To evaluate Chr 14 protein QTL introgression effects on seed composition and agronomic performance, a backcross population was grown in eight environments. The Chr 14 QTL high-protein allele from G. soja was selected at each round of backcrossing using the ED-5 KASP marker developed by Yang et al. (2022), which was also the peak marker of Chr 14 protein QTL mapped in this study. In total, 41 lines homozygous for G. soja and 32 lines homozygous for G. max at the ED-5 marker were evaluated in 8 unique environments. Across all environments, the G. soja ED-5 marker allele was associated with a significant increase in protein content of 5.04 g kg−1 over the G. max allele and explained 17.3% of the total phenotypic variance (Fig. 5a). Tukey HSD tests were also performed on the backcross population evaluated across eight environments to determine significant differences (α = 0.05) in backcross lines compared with the recurrent parent SA14-9653. The HSD tests were performed on two sets of the backcross lines separated by their homozygous ED-5 marker allele. For protein content of the 41 backcross lines with the G. soja ED-5 allele, 28 lines had significantly higher protein and none had significantly lower protein than the recurrent parent, with the highest protein backcross line having 22.4 g kg−1 more protein than the recurrent parent (Tables S13, S14). For oil content, one backcross line had significantly lower oil by 5.9 g kg−1 than the recurrent parent. For yield, one backcross line had a significantly lower yield by 543.2 kg ha−1 than the recurrent parent. For maturity, backcross lines with significantly earlier and later maturity than the recurrent parent were observed. For plant height, some backcross lines were significantly taller than the recurrent parent. For lodging, G. soja ED-5 allele backcross lines were observed with significantly higher lodging scores than the recurrent parent (Tables S13, S14).

For protein content in 36 backcross lines with the G. max ED-5 allele, 14 lines had significantly higher protein and none had significantly lower protein than the recurrent parent, with the highest protein backcross line having 13.6 g kg−1 more protein than the recurrent parent (Tables S15, S16). For oil content, one backcross line had significantly lower oil by 9.6 g kg−1 than the recurrent parent. For yield, no backcross lines had significantly different yield than the recurrent parent. For maturity, backcross lines were observed with significantly earlier and later maturity than the recurrent parent. For plant height, backcross lines were observed which were significantly taller than the recurrent parent. For lodging, G. max ED-5 allele backcross lines were observed with significantly greater lodging than the recurrent parent (Tables S15, 16).

Due to the variability observed for all traits within each homozygous ED-5 allele grouping, whole-genome re-sequencing (WGRS) was performed on backcross lines to examine potential introgressions from the donor parent within the genome outside of the Chr 14 protein QTL region. A subset of 18 backcross lines with high and low protein content within ED-5 assay homozygous allelic classes and the recurrent parent SA14-9653 were WGRS and reads were aligned to the Wm82.a6.v1 assembly (Espina et al. 2024). To visualize introgressions across the genome within the eight backcross lines containing the Chr 14 protein QTL G. soja allele, GCViT (Wilkey et al. 2020) was used (Fig. S11). Only eight backcross lines were visualized since the rest did not contain the high-protein Chr 14 G. soja allele. Introgressions originating from the donor parent were observed on multiple chromosomes in these eight backcross lines (Fig. S11). SNP array-based genotyping was also conducted on the entire backcross population of 77 lines, and of 6K initial SNP markers, 933 polymorphic SNPs remained after filtration on all chromosomes except 17, indicating donor parent introgressions throughout the genome.

Discussion

Through this study, we confirmed and validated a protein QTL on Chr 14 derived from the G. soja accession PI 593983 in different genetic backgrounds than previously reported using a biparental mapping population, evaluated effects of the QTL on seed composition and agronomic traits with a backcross population, and identified candidate genes within the fine-mapped region using whole-genome re-sequencing data. Additionally, we confirmed two oil QTL: one on Chr 8 and another on Chr 14, which overlap with previously identified regions originating from PI 593983 (Yang et al. 2022). The confirmation of these QTL across genetic backgrounds and multiple environments provide concrete evidence for the utility of these QTL when breeding for improved seed composition. By utilizing whole-genome re-sequencing data, we also narrowed candidate gene lists for all three regions and developed additional molecular markers for introgression of the Chr 14 protein QTL within breeding programs.

Within our confirmation mapping and backcross populations, we evaluated seed composition and agronomic traits in multiple environments. The TL14-110–822 parent used for both populations in this study was an F9-derived RHD-NIL from a cross between wild and cultivated soybean (Yang et al. 2022). Theoretically, this line contains approximately 50% G. soja alleles throughout its genome, along with the G. soja allele for the Chr 14 protein QTL region (Yang et al. 2022); however, this line does not carry the Chr 20 seed protein QTL that was originally mapped in PI 593983 (Yang et al. 2022). Removing the effect of the Chr 20 seed protein QTL from TL14-110–822 line enabled mapping of additional protein QTL. Genetic diversity inherited from G. soja alleles likely contributed to the phenotypic variation for all traits observed in both populations. The broad-sense heritabilities observed for both populations across traits reflect high contributions of genetic variance to the total phenotypic variance. For the confirmation mapping population, heritabilities were: 0.86 for protein; 0.88 for oil; 0.96 for maturity; 0.96 for height; and 0.91 for lodging. Within the backcross population, similar heritabilities were observed for these five traits and yield heritability was 0.62. These heritabilities are similar to those previously reported for protein and oil content (Diers et al. 1992; Chen et al. 2025) and agronomic traits and yield (Diers et al. 2018; Beche et al. 2020). The significant negative correlation between protein and oil content observed in the confirmation mapping population is consistent with previous reports of G. max and G. soja grown in North America (Chung et al. 2003; Leamy et al. 2016; La et al. 2019). Interestingly, this negative correlation was not observed within the backcross population, potentially indicating reduced contributions of pleiotropy or linkage between protein and oil within this population. Several backcross lines with broken linkage between the Chr 14 protein QTL and Chr 14 oil QTL were observed; however, the effects of these QTL could not be quantified separately due to confounding effects of varying genetic backgrounds among the backcross lines. Further backcrossing would be required to develop isogenic lines containing either QTL to fully quantify their effects on seed composition and yield.

By mapping seed protein and oil content QTL in the confirmation mapping population, we confirmed a protein QTL on Chr 14 and oil QTL on Chrs 8 and 14, which all overlap with the previously identified regions originating from PI 593983 in Yang et al. (2022). Our main region of interest for this study was the Chr 14 protein QTL, which we were able to confirm using two different populations and fine-map from ~ 4.6 Mbp to 0.6 Mbp. The effect size of this QTL across environments was 9.29 g kg−1, which is greater than previously observed effect of 6.5 to 7.2 g kg−1 (Yang et al. 2022). While this QTL was not significant in the 24CLM environment at the permutation LOD threshold of 4.55, it was significant at a LOD threshold of 3. This environment was planted ~ 2 weeks later than the other two environments and the latest maturing lines were impacted by a freeze. The 24CLM environment also had the lowest environment mean and parent means for protein content. This corresponds with findings by Morris et al. (2021), where protein content declined with later planting date in MG II-V lines grown in North Carolina. Our Chr 14 protein QTL region was also not significant when mapping oil content QTL, which supports the observation from Yang et al. (2022) that this region from G. soja significantly increases protein content without significantly decreasing oil content. Although the Chr 14 protein QTL region did not appear when mapping oil content QTL, we did observe an oil QTL upstream. This ~ 2.0 Mbp region (Chr14:4,602,230–6,561,966) overlaps with a 1.0 Mbp region (Chr14:5,509,372–6,487,608) associated with oil reported by Yang et al. (2022). The G. soja allele for the peak SNP in this region decreased oil by 7.69 g kg−1, while the peak protein QTL SNP downstream (Chr14:8,059,955) showed a decrease of 5.82 g kg−1, potentially due to linkage in some lines with this oil QTL. These results support the presence of two separate QTL independently controlling protein and oil content on Chr 14.

Protein and oil QTL on Chr 14 have been reported in previous studies (Zhang et al. 2004; Kabelka et al. 2004; Reinprecht et al. 2006; Akond et al. 2014; Sonah et al. 2015; Warrington et al. 2015; Leamy et al. 2017; Huang et al. 2020; Diers et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2025; Fang et al. 2025). While several of these reported QTL for protein content were identified outside of our region (Zhang et al. 2004; Reinprecht et al. 2006; Akond et al. 2014; Sonah et al. 2015; Warrington et al. 2015; Leamy et al. 2017; Huang et al. 2020; Fang et al. 2025), three were reported in close proximity or were overlapping our fine-mapped protein region (Kabelka et al. 2004; Diers et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2025). Kabelka et al. (2004) utilized a biparental G. max population and identified a protein QTL on linkage group B2 (Chr 14) with an effect size of 2.7 g kg−1 protein. A study evaluating seed composition QTL in the SoyNAM population identified a protein QTL at ~ 5 Mbp on Chr 14 (Diers et al. 2023). Recently, an additional NAM study utilizing interspecific crosses identified a protein QTL at ~ 9 Mbp from wild soybean which explained 10% of the total phenotypic variance (Chen et al. 2025). An oil QTL on Chr 14 was also identified downstream of our oil region (Huang et al. 2020), while three QTL were reported near our fine-mapped oil region (Chen et al. 2025). Chen et al. (2025) identified three oil QTL on Chr 14 at ~ 5, ~ 8, and ~ 9 Mbp in interspecific NAM populations. The presence of nonoverlapping protein and oil QTL on Chr 14 within these studies provide further evidence for the presence of independent QTL controlling seed protein and oil content.

We were also able to confirm and fine-map a Chr 8 oil QTL previously identified by La (2018). This region was previously mapped to ~ 2.3 Mbp, which we narrowed to 0.7 Mbp. The TL14-110–822 parent allele for the peak Chr 8 QTL SNP (Chr08:8,725,772) decreased oil content by 8.66 g kg−1, which is similar to the effect of 9.6 g kg−1 observed originally for the G. soja parent allele in La (2018). This QTL was initially observed in RILs (La 2018) but unable to be validated using RHD-NILs (Yang et al. 2022). Due to the inability to validate the QTL, the initial QTL was considered a false hit and attributed to segregation of the seed coat color inhibitor locus (I locus) on Chr 8 (Senda et al. 2012) impacting the accuracy of seed composition prediction by NIR (Baianu et al. 2012). In this study, due to seed coat color segregation within the confirmation mapping population, wet laboratory analyses were conducted to evaluate seed protein and oil content, removing any potential impact of the I locus on linkage mapping results. Therefore, we consider the Chr 8 oil QTL to be a valid QTL which we were able to confirm and fine-map in a unique genetic background. Previously, protein and oil QTL on Chr 8 have been reported in several studies (Reinprecht et al. 2006; Zhang et al. 2017a, b; Huang et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2025; Fang et al. 2025). Oil QTL on Chr 8 have been identified outside of our oil region (Huang et al. 2020; Fang et al. 2025), while others have been reported in close proximity to our fine-mapped oil region (Chen et al. 2025; Fang et al. 2025). A NAM study using G. max × G. soja populations identified an oil-associated region of Chr08:8,657,875–8,725,772 (Chen et al. 2025), directly bordering our QTL confidence interval. A QTL mapping study identified an additional oil QTL at ~ 6 Mbp on Chr 8 (Fang et al. 2025). Other studies have also reported protein QTL at ~ 9 Mbp on Chr 8, although these regions were not associated with oil in these studies (Reinprecht et al. 2006; Zhang et al. 2017a, b).

In addition to the protein and oil QTL observed on Chrs 8 and 14, we observed protein QTL on Chrs 6, 9, and 16, and oil QTL on Chrs 6 and 19. We also observed QTL for agronomic traits on several chromosomes: maturity on Chrs 6 and 10; plant height on Chrs 6 and 19; and lodging on Chrs 1, 6, 10, 17, and 19. The regions of QTL identified for all traits on Chr 6 varied slightly, but all included the Chr06:20,686,842 marker which was the peak SNP for maturity. Based on molecular marker data for the parent lines, the E1 maturity gene was known to be segregating for late (E1) and early (e1-as) maturity alleles within this population. The E1 gene has been cloned (Xia et al. 2012), corresponding to Glyma.06G207800 in Wm82.a2.v1 reference genome (Chr06:20,207,077–20,20,7940), ~ 0.4 Mbp from our maturity peak SNP. E loci, including E1, are reported to have strong pleiotropic effects on many other traits, including seed composition, plant height, and lodging (Cober and Morrison 2010). The region we identified on Chr 10 for maturity and lodging was located only 0.16 Mbp away from another E locus, E2. The gene underlying this locus has been cloned and corresponds to Glyma.10G221500 (Chr10:45,294,735–45,316,121) (Watanabe et al. 2011). Based on marker data for the parent lines, we initially surmised that both lines contained the functional E2 allele. However, upon further examination of WGRS data, we determined that the G. soja allele inherited from PI 593983 is an ancestral allele observed in G. soja with later maturity (Wang et al. 2023).

Our confirmation mapping population was also segregating for the determinacy gene Dt1, which has been cloned and corresponds to Glyma.19g194300 (Chr19:45,183,357–45,185,175) (Tian et al. 2010). This gene is located only 20 kbp from our height peak SNP and 0.18 Mbp from our peak lodging SNP for 19b. Additionally, we observed an oil QTL peak SNP only 0.8 Mbp away from Dt1. A recent report demonstrated that Dt1 interacts with the sucrose transporter ChrSWEET10a, and although no significant differences were observed, seed protein and oil content varied between the Dt1 and dt1 alleles (Li et al. 2024). Additional lodging QTL were identified on Chrs 1, 17, and 19. A NAM study using three biparental interspecific soybean populations found lodging-associated SNPs at Chr01:45,064,727 and Chr19:1,489,018 (Beche et al. 2020), ~ 3 Mbp from our Chr 1 peak SNP and 0.12 Mbp from a Chr 19a peak SNP. For the two remaining protein QTL we observed, another G. max × G. soja NAM study found protein-associated SNPs within ~ 3 Mbp of our QTL peak SNPs on Chrs 9 and 16 (Chen et al. 2025).

We evaluated the effects of the Chr 14 protein QTL on seed composition and agronomic traits in an additional genetic background and across 8 unique environments. To examine effects of the QTL, we used the ED-5 KASP marker (Chr14:8,059,955) developed by Yang et al. (2022) for mean separation of backcross lines by their homozygous allelic classes. We observed consistent significant increases in protein content associated with the G. soja allele within and across all eight environments; however, the effect size varied by environment. The effect on oil content also varied by environment, with a significant decrease only observed in two of the eight environments and no significant difference overall. Similar patterns were observed for yield by environment, with significance in three of the eight environments and overall. These differences for seed composition and yield by environment reflect those observed for the major seed protein QTL on Chrs 15 and 20, where the effect sizes and significance varied by environment (Brzostowski et al. 2017; Brzostowski and Diers 2017). Similarly, the protein content effect size for our Chr 14 protein QTL varied between the backcross population and confirmation mapping population. Differences in effect size of the Chrs 15 and 20 have also been observed depending on the genetic background (Brzostowski et al. 2017; Brzostowski and Diers 2017). We also observed significant differences in backcross line trait values compared to the SA14-9653 recurrent parent for several traits, reflecting potential variation introduced from the TL14-110–822 donor parent. Several backcross lines identified with higher protein and equivalent oil and yield as the recurrent parent could be especially useful as a resource for breeding improved seed composition cultivars with no yield penalty. Through examining WGRS and 6K SNP genotype data within the backcross lines, we observed polymorphisms reflecting donor parent introgressions throughout the genome. Additionally, the effects of the Chr 14 protein QTL in the backcross population could be confounded by linkage with the Chr 14 oil QTL in some lines. Further research is needed to determine potential impacts of these introgressions beyond the Chr 14 protein QTL on protein and other traits.

By further examining the WGRS data collected, we were able to narrow candidate gene lists for each of the three confirmed QTL regions. We used the updated Williams 82 reference genome version Wm82.a6.v1 for variant calling and candidate gene identification (Espina et al. 2024). Different reference genome versions were used for mapping and candidate gene identification due to their availability at the time of analysis. The SoySNP3K data used for mapping was originally provided with Wm82.a1.v1 reference genome positions and was subsequently converted to Wm82.a2.v1 using published coordinate conversion files (Song et al. 2020). A more complete and up-to-date genome assembly, Wm82.a6.v1, became available after the confirmation mapping was completed (Espina et al. 2024). Therefore, candidate gene analyses were performed using raw WGRS reads aligned to the Wm82.a6.v1 reference genome to enable more accurate and comprehensive identification of candidate genes. Annotated genes within each Wm82.a6.v1 region were examined, and we retained candidate genes containing high-impact variants originating from the TL14-110–822 parent. Through this analysis, we were able to narrow the candidate genes from 27 to two for the Chr 14 protein QTL, from 83 to five for the Chr 8 oil QTL, and from 97 to six for the Chr 14 oil QTL. Although these candidate genes contain predicted high-impact variants, it is possible that other candidate genes exist within the regions with missense variants, promotor variants, or larger structural variants that could also impact gene function or expression level. However, the effects of these variants are more difficult to characterize with currently available tools. The candidate genes within the three QTL regions contained various KOG and InterPro annotations. Within the Chr 8 oil QTL containing five candidate genes, Glyma.08G114200 had no KOG or InterPro annotations; however, the Arabidopsis ortholog is the SUB1 gene. In Arabidopsis, SUB1 functions within the cryptochrome and phytochrome signaling pathways (Guo et al. 2001), while SUB1 genes in rice are involved in submergence tolerance (Septiningsih et al. 2009). Three other genes had KOG and InterPro annotations of “mitochondrial oxoglutarate/malate carrier proteins,” “glutathione S-transferase,” and “malectin.” Within the Chr 14 oil QTL region, a total of six candidate genes were identified which also had various annotations, including “actin-related protein—Arp5p,” “predicted Ca2+-dependent phospholipid-binding protein,” and “cytochrome P450 CYP4/CYP19/CYP26 subfamilies.”

There were two candidate genes with high-impact variants within the Chr 14 protein QTL region. Glyma.14G089900 had no KOG or InterPro annotation information, although the Arabidopsis annotation is “SWIM zinc finger family protein.” This gene was reported as a candidate gene for oil content in a GWAS using wild soybean by Kim et al. (2023). The other candidate gene Glyma.14G089600 was annotated as “Myc-type, basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) domain.” The Arabidopsis ortholog is bHLH74, which is a transcription factor shown to be involved in root growth (Bao et al. 2014). In soybean, bHLH transcription factors are involved in a variety of functions, such as pigment biosynthesis, photosynthesis, light signaling, and seed pod development (Hudson and Hudson 2015). Hudson and Hudson (2015) reported expression of Glyma.14G089600 in all examined tissues, including leaf, flower, root, nodule, seed, and pod. Glyma.14G089600 was also reported by Chen et al. (2023) to regulate accumulation of isoflavones in soybean seed. Interestingly, the functional effect prediction of the Glyma.14G089600 variant originating from G. soja in our study is a frameshift mutation. Additional research will be necessary to determine which gene within each of our three QTL regions is causative for the observed increases in protein content and decreases in oil content originating from G. soja.

Conclusion

Overall, this study provides confirmation of a protein QTL on Chr 14 from G. soja in a different genetic background than previously reported, comprehensive evaluation of the QTL effects on seed composition and agronomic traits, along with a fine-mapped region and narrowed candidate gene list. Additionally, we confirmed two oil QTL on Chrs 8 and 14 and provide candidate genes within these regions. The oil QTL on Chr 14 was located upstream of the protein QTL, where the G. soja allele was associated with a decrease in oil content. Our results support the presence of two separate QTL independently associated with protein and oil content on Chr 14. Overall, in our backcross population the ED-5 KASP marker (Chr:8,059,955) was associated with a significant increase in protein content, without significantly decreasing oil. This could be attributed to linkage between protein and oil QTL being broken in some backcross lines. However, due to the donor parent introgressions throughout backcross line genomes, additional research will be needed to dissect this region while controlling for genetic background. To improve seed composition within breeding programs, several high-yielding backcross lines with improved seed composition have been identified. Additionally, the molecular markers developed flanking the narrowed Chr 14 protein QTL region can be utilized to efficiently introgress the G. soja allele and improve protein content without decreasing oil content. This work highlights the potential of utilizing beneficial alleles from wild soybean for improvement of cultivated soybean seed composition.
