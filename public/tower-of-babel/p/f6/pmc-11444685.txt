Role of prolonged packing in postoperative anorectal abscess management: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Dain (Raina) Kim, Kevin Verhoeff, Uzair Jogiat, Alex Miles, Janice Y Kung, Valentin Mocanu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Prolonged packing of anorectal abscess cavities with internal dressings after incision and drainage is frequently used, but the efficacy of this practice remains controversial. Some studies highlight its use in hemostasis and preventing fistula and abscess recurrence, whereas others describe its economic burden and increase in pain. In this systematic review, we examine current evidence on the impact of packing after incision and drainage for anorectal abscesses.Methods:The medical librarian conducted a comprehensive literature search on January 5, 2023. We conducted the meta-analysis using RevMan 5.4.1 software with a Mantel–Haenszel random-effects model.Results:We identified 3 randomized controlled trials, comprising 490 patients. Of those, 241 patients (49%) received postoperative packing; most patients were male (n = 158, 65.6%), with a median age of 40.5 years and a follow-up of 6 months. Meta-analysis showed that prolonged wound packing was associated with delayed wound healing and increased pain, but no difference in abscess recurrence or fistula formation.Conclusion:In this systematic review of current evidence highlighting the impact of packing after incision and drainage for anorectal abscesses, we found that the practice is not associated with significant differences in abscess recurrence and fistula formation, but is associated with increased postoperative pain and delayed wound healing.

Suppurative cryptoglandular anorectal disease refers to a spectrum of anorectal infections involving blockage of the base of the anal crypts of Morgagni responsible for drainage of secretory anal glands.1–3 Together, these diseases represent one of the most common surgical presentations to emergency departments, with an approximate annual incidence of 0.02% and about 68 000 to 96 000 cases per year in the United States.4–6 Although the current standard of care for management of this condition involves incision and drainage and is well accepted, debate exists regarding the role of postoperative packing.1,7 Packing of anorectal abscess cavities with internal dressings for longer than 24–48 hours is thought to benefit wound healing and recovery, yet prolonged postoperative packing has been used variably and its role remains controversial.8–10

Proponents of prolonged packing suggest it is valuable not only for initial use in ensuring early postoperative hemostasis, but for its prolonged role in preventing abscess recurrence, fistula formation, and delayed wound healing.11,12 Others who argue against its use highlight the potentially unnecessary burden of excess health care resources for a practice with minimal evidence demonstrating clinical benefit.13–15 These studies have added to the controversy regarding the role of postoperative packing, further questioning routine adoption of this practice by suggesting that prolonged packing may instead be associated with increased morbidity and patient discomfort. Clarifying the utility of packing after incision and drainage of anorectal abscesses may thus serve to standardize care, optimize patient outcomes, and minimize the burden to ever-limited health care systems.

A Cochrane review by Smith and colleagues evaluated the role of postoperative packing for anorectal abscess but was limited by a small sample size of 64 patients, inconclusively reporting either no clear evidence of difference or low-quality evidence across all domains, including time to healing, pain, fistula formation, and abscess recurrence.11 The objective of our updated systematic review was to characterize and evaluate the impact of internal packing duration of abscess cavities after incision and drainage of anorectal abscesses while incorporating new data from recently published randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Our outcomes include evaluation of postoperative pain, recurrence of abscesses, fistula formation, and healing time. A better understanding of these outcomes may provide actionable, high-quality evidence to inform current surgical practice.

Methods

Study design and research question formulation

The reporting of this systematic review was guided by the standards of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Statement.16 We developed the full concept and design of the study under the framework of PICOS (population, intervention, comparison, outcome, and study design).

This systematic review included only RCTs, resulting in 3 studies for evaluation. The target population for this study was adult patients with anorectal abscess who underwent incision and drainage surgery. The intervention was prolonged packing of abscess cavity beyond 24 hours after incision and drainage, and the comparison group had temporary packing for up to 24 hours after incision and drainage. The primary outcomes of interest were fistula and recurrent abscess as well as postoperative pain. Our secondary outcome was wound healing time. Our inclusion criteria were comparative studies in English, consisting of at least 10 patients in total from both groups with anorectal abscess being followed up postoperatively after surgical incision and drainage.

Search and extraction strategy

The medical librarian (J.Y.K.) developed and executed comprehensive searches in Ovid MEDLINE, Ovid Embase, Cumulated Index in Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Cochrane Library (via Wiley), and Scopus on January 5, 2023 (Appendix 1, Table 1, available at www.canjsurg.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cjs.008423/tab-related-content). To capture all relevant literature pertaining to postoperative anorectal abscess cavity packing and its associated adverse outcomes, we carefully selected relevant keywords and controlled vocabulary. Searches were limited to English language.

A total of 728 results were retrieved and, after we removed duplicates, 470 unique results remained for the initial title and abstract screening. In addition to subscription databases, the research team searched trial registries (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov) and Google Scholar. We evaluated the first 200 results from Google Scholar for inclusion, to supplement the results found from bibliographic databases. We also reviewed bibliographies from included studies.

Two reviewers (V.M., D.K.) first independently screened titles for inclusion, then abstract and full-text review. After screening, the same 2 reviewers assessed primary and secondary outcomes for suitability for meta-analysis, with the extraction of remaining data. A third independent reviewer (K.V.) adjudicated disputes between studies.

Study inclusion and exclusion criteria

We selected studies after systematic review according to the following inclusion criteria: patients who had undergone incision and drainage surgery for anorectal abscess and received packing with wound dressing immediately after the operation, and studies with a comparator group of at least 5 patients. We defined exclusion criteria as non-English studies, case reports, noncomparative studies, and animal studies.

Variable definitions, outcomes, and data analysis

We characterized the extracted data from the included studies upon patient demographics such as age, sex, weight, comorbidities, and the patient’s abscess types. The study details included the year, design, and location of the study, and information on follow-up. Once we had extracted the data, we divided it into 2 intervention groups for analysis: a prolonged packing group (patients with packing placed for more than 24 hours until wound resolution) and a temporary packing (nonprolonged packing group; i.e., patients with packing placed for less than 24 hours).

The primary outcomes of our study were postoperative pain, defined using the 10-cm visual analogue scale (VAS), abscess recurrence assessed at follow-up visits, and fistula formation in patients with anorectal abscess who received internal packing after surgical incision and drainage. The secondary outcome of our study was healing time, which was measured during the follow-up period of 6 months.

Risk of bias assessment

Because all 3 studies that we evaluated were RCTs, 2 reviewers assessed risk of bias using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB 2.0) tool for RCTs, as well as the bias assessment provided by the RevMan software.17 The RoB 2.0 tool provides an assessment framework that considers 5 domains in which bias could be introduced. The risk of bias was then judged for each domain as low, high, or some concerns.

Statistical analysis

We reported categorical data in percentages or absolute values. For continuous data, we estimated means from median values with the formula created by Wan and colleagues.18 We used weighted means or percentages for reporting description of patient demographics. We used RevMan 5.4 software to conduct a meta-analysis of the primary and secondary outcomes of interest when at least 3 studies reported comparator outcomes. Assuming that there is variance of true effect estimates among studies, our analysis consisted of application of the Mantel–Haenszel random-effects model. We evaluated the included studies for heterogeneity, with significance level set at p < 0.05. The heterogeneity was graded using the I2 statistic as low (< 50%), moderate (50%–75%), or high (> 75%).19

Results

Study selection

We retrieved a total of 728 results; after we removed duplicates, 470 unique results remained for the initial title and abstract screening. After the title screening, we included 7 studies for abstract review. We included all 7 studies for full-text review, and of those, 3 studies met inclusion criteria (Figure 1). No additional studies were included after bibliography review or grey literature search.

Summary of search results, record identification, and study selection.

Characteristics of study population

Within the 3 studies,12,13,20 a total of 490 patients underwent anorectal abscess incision and drainage (Table 1). Of those, 241 (49.2%) patients were in the packing group, whereas 249 (50.8%) patients were in the nonpacking group. As shown in Table 1, patients in the nonpacking group for all 3 studies had temporary wound packing with the dressing removed within the first 24 hours postoperatively, whereas in the packing group, patients had prolonged packing under the care of a nurse until the wound was fully healed (median 26 days). Participants in the studies by Newton and colleagues12 and Perera and colleagues13 were followed up for 6 months to investigate the outcome of postoperative packing compared with nonpacking. Most patients in all 3 studies were male (n = 331, 67.6%), with the median age ranging from 32.5 to 48 years (Table 2).

Demographics of included patients

Body mass index was not reported in the included studies, but Newton and colleagues12 reported various other patient characteristics, including the proportion of patients who had diabetes, were current smokers, or were taking immunosuppressants or anticoagulants. Perrera and colleagues13 and Tonkin and colleagues20 excluded patients with diagnosed Crohn disease at the time of surgery, and Perrera and colleagues13 also excluded patients with underlying malignancy or immunosuppression. No data were reported on other factors associated with healing, including malnutrition, albumin, or antibiotics use.

Abscess characteristics were also recorded with the patient demographics, as shown in Table 2, which indicated the type and size of abscess as well as the median duration of symptoms. According to Tonkin and colleagues,20 deep abscess was referred to as “ischiorectal or intersphincteric abscess,” whereas superficial abscess was referred to as “submucosal abscess.” Perera and colleagues13 reported that 64% of the participants had deep abscess, whereas Tonkin and colleagues20 reported 65% with superficial abscess and 33% with deep abscess. Both studies reported the same median abscess size of 30 mm for both the packing and nonpacking groups.

Outcomes of postoperative prolonged packing versus no prolonged packing

Postoperative pain

Postoperative pain was reduced in the nonpacking group compared with the packing group, although statistical significance was not reached in the study by Tonkin and colleagues20 (Table 3). Perera and colleagues13 and Tonkin and colleagues20 used the 10-cm VAS, with 0 being no pain and 10 being the worst pain imaginable, whereas Newton and colleagues12 used a 100-mm point VAS, with 0 being no pain and 100 being the worst pain. The median 2-week postoperative VAS score for Perera and colleagues13 was 2/10 for the packing group and 0 for the nonpacking group (p = 0.030), whereas for Newton and colleagues,12 the VAS score was 38.2/100 for the packing group and 28.2/100 for the the nonpacking group (p < 0.001). Although pain scores for Tonkin and colleagues20 appeared reduced in the nonpacking group, this did not attain statistical significance. Within the sensitivity analyses for pain scores that were reported by Newton and colleagues,12 even after taking outliers, deviations, and confounders into account, pain reduction for the nonpacking group remained highly significant, with the difference in mean maximum scores being consistently around 10 and higher.

Abscess recurrence and fistula formation

We found no significant difference in abscess recurrence rates, which appeared to be lower in the packing group than the nonpacking group in Newton and colleagues12 (3% for packing, 6% for nonpacking) and Tonkin and colleagues20 (5% for packing, 13% for nonpacking), as shown in Table 3. Similarly, for postoperative fistula formation rates, Newton and colleagues12 (15% for packing, 11% for nonpacking; p = 0.204), Tonkin and colleagues20 (20% for packing, 8.7% for nonpacking; p = 0.38), and Perera and colleagues13 (12.5% for packing, 0% for nonpacking) all reported a trend toward decreased fistula rates for the nonpacking group compared with the packing group, but these differences were not significant.

Delayed wound healing

Perera and colleagues13 demonstrated shorter healing time in the nonpacking group (19.5 d) than in the packing group (26.8 d) but did not define wound healing delay between groups, limiting inclusion for meta-analysis. Tonkin and colleagues20 did not explicitly define delayed wound healing but reported equal numbers of patients with delayed wound healing in both groups (n = 2, p = 1). Newton and colleagues12 assessed for presence of healing, defined as complete wound epithelization at the patient’s follow-up visits at intervals of 4, 8, and 26 weeks, and found significantly higher healing rates in patients without packing (78.9%) than in those with packing (68.7%; p = 0.046).

Meta-analysis of postoperative outcome for prolonged packing versus nonpacking groups

Meta-analyses of postoperative recurrent abscess (odds ratio [OR] 0.52, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.23–1.17; p = 0.11), as shown in Figure 2, demonstrated that there was no significant difference in recurrent abscesses between the packing and nonpacking groups. We did not see any significant differences with respect to fistula between the 2 groups, as shown in Figure 3 (OR 1.54, 95% CI 0.90–2.63; p = 0.11). Meta-analysis also demonstrated that the packing was associated with delayed wound healing (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.46–0.98; p = 0.04; Figure 4). There was a low degree of heterogeneity for all 3 meta-analyses (I2 = 0%).

Study risk of bias

We assessed the 3 RCTs for risk of bias using the revised Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool (Figure 5).17 Using this tool, we scored Newton and colleagues12 as a high-quality study at low risk for bias considering all 5 domains of the tool, whereas Tonkin and colleagues20 and Perera and colleagues13 were scored as moderate quality, with overall low risk of bias.

Risk of bias evaluation using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB) 2.0 tool and RevMan software17 for included studies.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs evaluating the role of extended wound packing after incision and drainage of anorectal abscess. We included 3 studies comprising 490 patients for evaluation. Meta-analysis showed that prolonged wound packing was not associated with significant differences in fistula reduction or abscess recurrence, but may be associated with delayed wound healing and increased pain. These findings bring into question the use and efficacy of prolonged packing for more than 24 hours after anorectal abscess drainage.

Postoperative wound healing occurs through 3 complex stages, known as hemostasis, inflammation, and repair.21,22 Hemostasis is an immediate response involving restriction of blood vessels at the site of injury, limiting blood loss. Inflammatory process stimulates wound repair and is responsible for providing nutrients and removing any bacteria or debris at the site.23,24 Finally, the repair process involves epithelization, capillary proliferation, and fibroplasia.25 Despite the complex physiology underlying wound healing,26 one of the few immediate factors clinicians can control are wound dressings and packing. Although there are many different types of dressing, certain types — including the conventional gauze permeable dressing used for packing — can lead to prolonged inflammation, causing dehydration of the wound21 and potentially delaying wound healing by impending effective tissue granulation.21 Our study was not designed to evaluate the pathophysiology of wound healing relating to perineal packing, but this mechanism may be responsible for the findings presented herein.

Several studies that have been published on the topic of the efficacy of extended postoperative packing for general skin abscesses similarly failed to demonstrate its benefits when compared with nonpacking, in keeping with the results of our study.27,28 An RCT by Kessler and colleagues29 examined the effects of wound packing versus nonpacking on treatment failure rates and recurrences of abscess for patients aged 1–25 years with superficial skin abscesses undergoing incision and drainage. The authors similarly concluded that packing did not significantly affect rates of abscess recurrence or treatment failure compared with nonpacking. List and colleagues30 performed a review of literature that similarly concluded that packing for skin abscess treatment did not significantly affect rates of treatment failure, abscess recurrence, or need for secondary intervention in wounds, but was associated with increased pain. Both of these studies on skin abscess treatment with packing supported the elimination of routine abscess packing. This parallels our study, which provides further evidence for adoption of nonprolonged (> 24 h) packing strategies after incision and drainage of anorectal abscesses.

Although there is a paucity of current reviews on our topic, a Cochrane review by Smith and colleagues11 that studied the effects of internal dressing in anorectal abscess cavity attempted to evaluate the role of postoperative packing but was limited by a lack of trials and by sample size. This review included only 2 studies, with a total of 64 participants, and inconclusively reported either no clear evidence of difference or low-quality evidence for the outcomes including time to healing, pain, fistula formation, and abscess recurrence. In contrast, our systematic review included 3 studies with 490 participants, and our results demonstrated significance in our primary outcomes, providing potentially practice-changing conclusions for one of the most common surgical conditions worldwide.

Many studies presented high rates of clinical cure regardless of postoperative treatment after incision and drainage of skin abscess.30 Surgical gauze for wound dressing and packing has been favoured by most surgeons, but it becomes hard and adherent, bringing pain and difficulty with dressing changes.31 Our work shows that postoperative packing of anorectal abscess cavities leads to greater pain compared with nonpacking, with no difference in other clinical outcomes such as fistula and recurrent abscesses. However, statistical significance for overall reduction of pain for the nonpacking group was not reached in the study by Tonkin and colleagues.20

The incidence of anorectal abscess in the US has been reported to range from 68 000 to 96 000 per year,32 and in the United Kingdom the estimated minimum annual cost to the National Health Service for community care involving postoperative packing of anorectal abscess has been estimated to range from £1.4 to £2.5 million.13 In addition, dressings for wound treatment are generally very costly.15,33,34 Considering the prevalence of these procedures in North America and the cost of packing per year, it is worth extrapolating the cost savings if postoperative wound packing were to be discontinued as standard management for anorectal abscess.

Limitations

This study has a number of limitations. Of the 3 included studies, 2 were pilot studies with small sample sizes, which may have reduced the generalizability of the study results. All 3 studies were conducted in different countries, outside of North America, which also limits generalizability. Although the studies briefly mentioned that the practice of postoperative packing creates economic burden, they provided no economic data. Similarly, none of the 3 studies presented nuanced data on many other factors, such as surgical techniques, dressing type, or various comorbidities of the patients, which could potentially act as confounding factors to wound healing. Another factor that could confound our study results is that patients’ antibiotic use was not clearly documented in all studies. In addition, not all studies comprehensively reported factors that contribute to healing, such as Crohn disease, nutritional status, or immunosuppression, as stated as a part of the exclusion criteria in the study by Perera and colleagues,13 which could also be a limiting factor for the results. Although the lack of nuanced abscess characteristics may confound our results, included patients were all randomized before incision and drainage, which should overcome this shortcoming, especially given that none of the 3 studies presented significant differences between abscess types across intervention groups. There was also heterogeneity of data for reporting of pain and delayed wound healing, which increased the risk of bias of included studies. In addition, given the nature of the intervention, it is not possible to truly blind patients to either intervention. With the paucity of trials, the low to moderate source of bias within included studies, and the fact that 1 study included more than 80% of all patients, we did not conduct a formal certainty of evidence assessment, which limits the strength of our recommendation. Last, it is important to acknowledge that the conclusions of our work were strongly weighted by 1 high-quality and high-volume RCT with a 6-month follow-up.12 This should be kept in perspective when using our findings to develop generalized practice guidelines, emphasizing the need to further re-evaluate these results upon completion of future studies on this topic.

Conclusion

Prolonged packing after incision and drainage of anorectal abscess was not associated with improved rates of abscess recurrence or fistula formation, but did increase postoperative pain when compared with nonpacking. This systematic review argues against routine packing after incision and drainage of anorectal abscesses, but further work is needed to contextualize this practice with respect to patient-reported outcomes and economic implications.
