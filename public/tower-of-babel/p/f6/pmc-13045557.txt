Arrhythmogenic Cardiomyopathy Versus Viral Myocarditis in a Professional Athlete

History of Presentation

A 26-year-old professional football cornerback was referred for evaluation after routine preseason electrocardiogram (ECG) screening that demonstrated symmetric T-wave inversions in leads V4-V6 and II, III, and aVF (Figure 1E). He denied palpitations, dizziness, chest discomfort, exertional dyspnea, prior syncope, or any decline in athletic performance. His year-round training routine included high-intensity conditioning and resistance sessions.

On examination, he appeared well-conditioned, with a resting heart rate of 48 beats/min and a blood pressure of 118/60 mm Hg. Cardiopulmonary, vascular, and systemic examinations were unremarkable.

Past Medical History

The athlete reported a severe viral illness consistent with COVID-19 in early 2020, associated with fever, fatigue, and myalgias but without any documented cardiac involvement. He recovered fully and returned to training within 4 weeks without persistent cardiopulmonary limitations. He denied any history of cardiovascular disease, stimulant use, anabolic steroids, alcohol misuse, or tobacco consumption. Family history was negative for sudden cardiac death, coronary artery disease, cardiomyopathy, or inherited arrhythmia syndromes. He did report a prior rib fracture and pneumothorax from sports trauma, which had healed fully.

Investigations

Electrocardiography

His draft-year National Football League Combine ECG results in early 2020 were normal (Figure 1A). During the subsequent 4 seasons with his previous team (2020-2024), serial ECGs consistently demonstrated lateral T-wave inversions (Figures 1B to 1D); however, these abnormalities were not recognized or acted upon. On joining our organization, he underwent a repeat ECG, and the persistent abnormalities were promptly identified, prompting comprehensive evaluation.

Laboratory Studies

Cardiac biomarkers, inflammatory markers, and natriuretic peptides were all within normal limits.

Exercise Testing

He achieved 16.2 metabolic equivalents on a customized maximal exercise treadmill stress test without ischemia or ventricular arrhythmias. Premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) present during warm-up were suppressed at peak exertion.

Holter Monitoring

Forty-eight hours of ambulatory rhythm monitoring—including periods of high-intensity training—demonstrated a PVC burden of <1% with no episodes of ventricular tachycardia.

Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography showed normal biventricular systolic function with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 55%. Mild chamber dilatation was present without wall motion abnormalities or valvular pathology.

Cardiac magnetic resonance

Cardiac magnetic resonance revealed an enlarged left ventricular cavity (left ventricular end-diastolic volume indexed, 152 mL/m2, z-score +4) with mildly reduced systolic function (LVEF 46%). The right ventricle was normal in size and systolic function (Figures 2A to 2C). Two discrete regions of nonischemic replacement fibrosis were identified.

Cardiac Magnetic Resonance Demonstrating Nonischemic Replacement Fibrosis

(A) Four-chamber late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) view showing left ventricular enlargement with mildly reduced systolic function. (B) Two-chamber LGE view confirming global dilation. (C) Short-axis LGE demonstrating subendocardial-to-transmural inferolateral fibrosis and mid-myocardial-to-transmural septal and apical fibrosis, consistent with a nonischemic pattern.

The first consisted of an ill-defined subendocardial-to-transmural late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) focus in the midlateral wall, corresponding to inferolateral wall thinning to approximately 5 mm with reduced contraction.

The second was a sharply marginated mid-myocardial-to-transmural LGE region involving the mid-to-apical septum and extending into the apical segments, with diffusely elevated extracellular volume (>40%) despite normal apical T1 values.

No myocardial edema or elevated T2 values were present, arguing against active myocarditis. Localized hypertrabeculation from the mid-to-apical lateral wall was noted but did not meet diagnostic criteria for noncompaction.

Genetic Testing

A 48-gene cardiomyopathy panel, including all major desmosomal genes, was negative for pathogenic variants.

Given the limitations of diagnostic certainty, the multidisciplinary team emphasized individualized interpretation within the athlete's physiologic and clinical context.

Management

Given the diagnostic uncertainty but reassuring clinical and arrhythmic profile, the case was evaluated through a series of multidisciplinary shared decision-making (SDM) discussions involving sports cardiology, sports medicine, athletic training staff, coaching leadership, team management, the athlete, and his family. These deliberations centered on balancing the potential risks of unnecessary restriction—which carries significant psychological and career impact—against his reassuring clinical stability and absence of concerning arrhythmic features.

A conditional clearance plan was ultimately established, emphasizing symptom vigilance, reinforcement of an emergency action plan with immediate and consistent automated external defibrillator availability during all training and competition, and structured longitudinal surveillance, including an annual cardiac magnetic resonance, annual echocardiography, and biannual ECG and ambulatory rhythm monitoring, with immediate reassessment for any new symptoms.

Given his mildly reduced LVEF, he was initiated on maximally tolerated guideline-directed medical therapy with metoprolol succinate extended-release 25 mg daily and valsartan 40 mg daily, both well tolerated, to support favorable remodeling.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At 1-year follow-up, the athlete remained asymptomatic and fully active, with unchanged ECG findings and only rare PVCs on ambulatory monitoring. Biomarkers remained normal, and serial assessments demonstrated no structural or functional progression. Ongoing surveillance was recommended as part of his conditional clearance framework.

Discussion

This case exemplifies the clinical and ethical complexity of distinguishing ACM from post–myocarditis fibrosis in competitive athletes. The overlap in ECG repolarization abnormalities and nonischemic LGE patterns creates substantial diagnostic ambiguity, particularly when fibrosis involves the inferolateral wall or interventricular septum—regions commonly implicated in both postinflammatory injury and left-dominant ACM. The presence of 2 distinct areas of nonischemic replacement fibrosis, including subendocardial-to-transmural enhancement with inferolateral wall thinning and mid-myocardial-to-transmural enhancement in the septal-to-apical region, further complicates interpretation. ACM is classically associated with pathogenic desmosomal variants leading to progressive fibro-fatty replacement and malignant ventricular arrhythmias, yet nearly half of modern ACM presentations are gene-negative.1 These gene-elusive phenotypes may reflect acquired processes, including prior myocarditis. Conversely, viral myocarditis—including COVID-19–related injury—can result in persistent midwall and septal fibrosis despite clinical recovery, producing a pattern that may closely resemble ACM on cardiac magnetic resonance.2,3 In athletes, distinguishing healed myocarditis from early, nongenetic ACM becomes especially challenging when fibrosis is stable and arrhythmic burden is low.

Cardiac magnetic resonance has transformed the evaluation of cardiomyopathy, but fibrosis must be interpreted within clinical context. The differentiation between pathologic scar and more benign variants seen in highly trained athletes is not purely imaging-dependent; it requires integration of ventricular function, tissue characterization, arrhythmic phenotype, and longitudinal stability. Updated guidelines emphasize that isolated fibrosis in the absence of ventricular arrhythmias, progressive remodeling, or genetic abnormalities may represent a chronic but clinically stable postinflammatory process rather than evolving cardiomyopathy.4, 5, 6

These distinctions carry meaningful prognostic implications. Postinflammatory scarring may remain clinically quiescent, whereas classical ACM carries an intrinsically elevated risk of malignant ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death. In high-performance athletes, over-restriction may result in profound psychological and career disruption, whereas overly permissive clearance risks avoidable harm. SDM provides an ethically grounded framework to navigate this uncertainty. Through SDM, clinicians can balance medical evidence with athlete values, performance demands, and individual risk tolerance. In this case, SDM enabled continued athletic participation under structured surveillance while prioritizing safety, psychological well-being, and career continuity. Similar approaches are applicable to other “gray-zone” phenotypes encountered in sports cardiology, including borderline ventricular remodeling and isolated scar in otherwise normal hearts.

Conclusions

This case underscores the diagnostic limitations of current imaging and genetic testing in distinguishing healed myocarditis from ACM in competitive athletes. Nonischemic replacement fibrosis, particularly when involving both lateral and septal-to-apical regions, requires careful integration of tissue characterization, arrhythmic burden, ventricular function, and longitudinal imaging stability. A structured strategy incorporating multimodal testing, contextual interpretation, and SDM offers a practical and ethically sound pathway for individualized, safe athletic participation. Continued surveillance remains essential to identify evolving pathology while supporting athlete autonomy and career longevity.
