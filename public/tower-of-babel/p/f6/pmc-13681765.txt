Trimester-specific associations of maternal vitamin D metabolic markers with risks of small vulnerable newborns: a prospective birth cohort study
Authors: Shanshan Wang, Yonghua Xiao, Hongjuan Liu, Mingqi Ran, Xinyu Lu, Yundi Mu, Ningning Cui, Jialin Sun, Xudong Wu, Jian Shen, Xuefeng Yang, Xiaomin Wu, Liping Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVitamin D metabolites beyond total 25(OH)D may influence fetal development, yet their trimester-specific associations with small vulnerable newborns (SVN) remain underexplored. This study examined longitudinal associations of multiple vitamin D metabolic markers with SVN-related outcomes and identified key determinants during pregnancy.MethodsIn this prospective cohort of 2,505 mother-infant pairs from China, serum levels of five vitamin D metabolites 25(OH)D2, 25(OH)D3, total 25(OH)D, 3-epi-25(OH)D3, and 24,25(OH)2D3] were quantified using HPLC-MS/MS. Two vitamin D metabolite ratios (VMR), VMR1 [24,25(OH)2D3/25(OH)D3] and VMR2 [3-epi-25(OH)D3/25(OH)D3], were derived. Outcomes included low birth weight (LBW), preterm birth (PTB), small for gestational age (SGA), and composite SVN. Associations were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression and restricted cubic spline models. Determinants, including dietary intake, supplementation, season of sample collection, and sociodemographic factors, were evaluated via multivariable linear regression.ResultsVitamin D deficiency decreased from 42.0% in the first trimester to 13.8% in the third trimester. Season and vitamin D intake were major determinants, with supplementation showing stronger correlations than diet. In early pregnancy, higher levels of 25(OH)D3, total 25(OH)D, 24,25(OH)2D3, and VMR1 were linked to lower risks of SVN-related outcomes, particularly PTB and SVN; however, several of these inverse associations were attenuated after FDR-based correction for multiple testing. In contrast, elevated 3-epi-25(OH)D3 and VMR2 remained robustly associated with increased risks of SGA and SVN after correction. In mid-pregnancy, 25(OH)D3 and total 25(OH)D were inversely associated with SGA, whereas 25(OH)D2, 3-epi-25(OH)D3, and VMR2 were positively associated with PTB, SGA or SVN. Nonlinear patterns included an S-shaped association between 3-epi-25(OH)D3 and SGA and inverted U-shaped associations between 25(OH)D2 and multiple outcomes (P nonlinearity < 0.05). In late pregnancy, associations were largely attenuated, with VMR2 showing only marginal inverse trends for SGA (P for trend = 0.058) and SVN (P for trend = 0.064).ConclusionsTrimester-specific vitamin D metabolic profiles showed differential associations with neonatal risks, with stronger corrected evidence for adverse associations related to epimerization-derived markers than for early inverse associations of conventional 25(OH)D metabolites. These findings support comprehensive vitamin D metabolic monitoring and further investigation of tailored supplementation strategies to improve maternal-fetal health.Trial registrationChiCTR1800016908.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-026-05087-1.Keywords: Vitamin D, Small newborns, Low birth weight, Preterm birth, Small for gestational age, Determinants

Background

Globally, one in four newborns is born too small or too early, with low birth weight (LBW), preterm birth (PTB), and small for gestational age (SGA) affecting 14.6%, 9.9%, and 17.4% of births, respectively [1–4]. These adverse outcomes elevate the risks of neonatal mortality, neurodevelopmental impairments, and long-term chronic diseases [1, 4]. As a substantial proportion of at-risk infants may be missed when relying on a single indicator, the 2023 Lancet Small Vulnerable Newborn Series proposed the composite concept of the small vulnerable newborn (SVN), integrating LBW, PTB, and SGA to better capture neonatal vulnerability [5]. Recent studies have further supported the value of the SVN framework for population surveillance and evaluation of prenatal nutritional interventions [6, 7].

Vitamin D is essential for fetal growth and development, with different metabolites reflecting distinct aspects of transport, receptor activation, epimerization, and catabolism [8]. During different stages of pregnancy, vitamin D metabolism undergoes substantial adaptive changes in circulating metabolites, vitamin D-binding protein, and placental vitamin D handling, which are critical for supporting fetal skeletal mineralization and maternal-fetal immune tolerance, suggesting that the implications of maternal vitamin D status may vary across gestation [8–10]. However, most prior research has centered on 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D], yielding inconsistent associations with LBW, PTB, and SGA [11, 12].

Emerging evidence suggests alternative markers may provide additional insight into vitamin D metabolism. For instance, 24,25(OH)2D3, the major downstream metabolite of 25(OH)D3, reflects CYP24A1-mediated catabolism, while its ratio to 25(OH)D3 (vitamin D metabolite ratio 1, VMR1) has been associated with response to VD3 supplementation [13] and bone health [14]. Likewise, 3-epi-25(OH)D3 (an epimer of 25(OH)D3) and its ratio to 25(OH)D3 (VMR2) reflect the epimerization pathway; as immunoassays cannot distinguish 25(OH)D3 from 3-epi-25(OH)D3, ignoring this epimer may lead to underestimation of vitamin D deficiency risk [15]. Supporting this relevance, a Japanese study (n = 297) found that among pregnant women with vitamin D insufficiency, VMR2, rather than 25(OH)D3 and 24,25(OH)2D3, was independently associated with neonatal anthropometric parameters [16]. Additionally, studies by Ooms et al. comparing term versus preterm neonates reported higher 3‑epi‑25(OH)D3 levels or VMR2 in preterm infants [17]. Nevertheless, existing evidence is largely based on small-sample, cross-sectional, or single-time-point studies that have generally assessed only total 25(OH)D; comprehensive longitudinal assessments of these metabolites during pregnancy remain scarce, and their implications for SVN-related outcomes are largely unexplored.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate trimester-specific associations and dose-response relationships between multiple vitamin D metabolic markers and the risks of SVN-related outcomes in a prospective cohort of pregnant Chinese women, while also exploring key determinants influencing these markers throughout gestation.

Methods

Study participants

The Tongji Birth Cohort (TJBC) is an ongoing, prospective multicenter study initiated in 2018 in Wuhan, China, designed to investigate how maternal nutrition, lifestyle, and environmental exposures during pregnancy influence maternal and child health [18]. For this analysis, 3501 pregnant women enrolled between March 2018 and June 2023 were considered. After excluding those with stillbirth or abortion (n = 5), missing blood samples (n = 830), or missing birth outcome data (n = 161), 2505 mother-infant pairs were included. Based on routine antenatal care schedules in China, vitamin D metabolites were measured in serum samples from 1118 women in early pregnancy (≤ 16+ 6 weeks, corresponding to initial registration), 1535 in mid-pregnancy (17–28+ 6 weeks, covering the OGTT screening period), and 1097 in late pregnancy (≥ 29 weeks) (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). It is noted that, with 215 women having serum samples available at all three pregnancy stages. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College (2021IEC [S044]). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Determination of serum vitamin D metabolites

Blood samples were collected at each pregnancy stage, processed to obtain serum, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Serum vitamin D metabolites were quantified at the Wuhan Center for Disease Control and Prevention using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) with an ACQUITY UPLC I-Class PLUS system coupled to a Xevo TQ Absolute triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). Five metabolites were measured: 25(OH)D2, 25(OH)D3, 3-epi-25(OH)D3, 24,25(OH)2D2, 24,25(OH)2D3, based on previously published protocols with modifications [19, 20].

Detailed methods for sample preparation, instrument settings, and validation are provided in Additional file 1: Supplementary Methods, Tables S1-S3, and Fig. S2. Total 25(OH)D was calculated as the sum of 25(OH)D2 and 25(OH)D3, and classified as deficient (< 20 ng/mL), insufficient (20–29.9 ng/mL), or sufficient (≥ 30 ng/mL) based on the Endocrine Society guidelines [21]. VMR1 was calculated as [24,25(OH)2D3 × 100]/25(OH)D3, and VMR2 as [3-epi-25(OH)D3 × 100]/25(OH)D3. Values below the limit of detection (LOD) were imputed as LOD divided by √2.

Assessment of different sources of vitamin D intake

Dietary intake was evaluated using a previously validated semi-quantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) [22]. Due to the absence of vitamin D data in the China Food Composition Database (2nd edition) [23], dietary vitamin D intake was estimated using the widely-utilized USDA FoodData Central database (Version Current: April 2024) [24]. Based on dietary patterns among pregnant women in Wuhan and previous literature [25, 26], approximately 50 vitamin D-rich foods were categorized into seven groups: poultry and livestock meat, processed meat, aquatic products, eggs, dairy products, fungus, and others. Among these, aquatic products included freshwater fish, marine fish, shrimp and crab, and mollusks, covering nearly 30 commonly consumed food items. All nutrient intakes were energy-adjusted using the nutrient residual method. Information on supplement use (e.g., type, brand, dosage, duration, and frequency) was collected at baseline and updated at each follow-up visit. For trimester-specific analyses, supplement exposure was assigned based on the report obtained at the visit closest to the corresponding blood sampling time, thereby better reflecting changes in supplement use across pregnancy. Total vitamin D intake was calculated as the sum of dietary and supplemental sources.

Outcomes

Birth outcome (gestational age, infant sex, birth weight, and length) were extracted from medical records. LBW was defined as birth weight < 2500 g, PTB as delivery before 37 weeks, and SGA as birth weight < 10th percentile based on Chinese standards [27]. Infants with any of these conditions were classified as SVN [1].

Covariates

Data on maternal and paternal age, education, income, and occupation were collected via self-reported questionnaires at enrollment. Pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) was calculated from self-reported weight and height. Gestational weight gain (GWG) was defined as the difference between the latest prenatal and pre-pregnancy weight. Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) was diagnosed based on clinical screening records at 24–28 weeks. Passive smoking referred to frequent secondhand smoke exposure in the past month. Alcohol consumption meant drinking at least once weekly per trimester. Regular exercise was defined as ≥ 3 times/week in the past month. Sun protection indicated use of protective measures outdoors. Regular supplement use (folic acid, iron, and vitamin D) was defined as achieving the Chinese recommended nutrient intake (RNI) [28]. Dietary quality was evaluated using the Chinese Healthy Eating Index (CHEI) [29]. Missing covariate data were handled by multiple imputations.

Statistical analysis

Data were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), median (interquartile range [IQR]), or n (%), as appropriate. Baseline characteristics of included and excluded participants were also compared using P values and standardized mean differences (SMDs) to assess potential selection bias. Vitamin D metabolic markers were analyzed as ln-transformed continuous and quartile-based categorical variables. Logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for associations with SVN-related outcomes, with the lowest quartile (Q1) as the reference. Two models were fitted: Model 1, unadjusted; and Model 2, adjusted for maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, GWG, ethnicity, education level, monthly income, occupation, gravidity, primiparity, passive smoking, drinking, season of sample collection, regular exercise, time spent outdoors, sun protection, use of iron or folic acid supplements, neonatal sex, and labor way. Covariates in Model 2 were selected a priori based on existing literature and biological plausibility, prioritizing factors related to both vitamin D status and birth outcomes. Since direct ultraviolet exposure and skin pigmentation were not measured, season of sample collection, time spent outdoors, and sun protection behaviors were included as proxy indicators of sunlight-related vitamin D exposure [30, 31]. Restricted cubic spline analysis with three knots placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles was performed to assess potential nonlinear associations, with the 25th percentile used as the reference value, following a commonly used percentile-based spline approach for trend analysis of continuous variables [32]. Likelihood ratio tests were applied to evaluate nonlinearity.

Stratified analyses examined potential effect modification by maternal age, regular exercise, neonatal sex, and season of sample collection, with interaction tested via likelihood ratio. Sensitivity analyses excluded women with overweight/obesity, GDM, or smoking/alcohol exposure, adjusted for paternal factors and CHEI, and used alternative trimester cutoffs (< 14, 14–27+ 6, ≥ 28 weeks). False discovery rate (FDR) adjustment using the Benjamini–Hochberg method was additionally applied to the analyses of ln-transformed vitamin D metabolic markers and SVN-related outcomes within each pregnancy stage. Analyses were performed in R (version 4.2.2), with two-tailed P < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

Basic characteristics of the study population

Maternal, paternal, and neonatal characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean maternal age was 28.8 ± 3.5 years, and the average pre-pregnancy BMI was 21.1 ± 2.9 kg/m2. The incidences of LBW, PTB, SGA, and SVN were 3.3%, 4.2%, 6.2%, and 10.1%, respectively. Sociodemographic characteristics were broadly comparable across trimesters, while the proportions of women consistently using folic acid, iron, or vitamin D supplements declined over the course of pregnancy (Table 2). Included and excluded participants showed broadly similar baseline characteristics (Additional file 1: Table S4). Although maternal age and pre-pregnancy BMI differed significantly between groups (P < 0.05), the corresponding SMDs were small (both < 0.2). Most variables showed negligible to small imbalance, while occupation showed a moderate imbalance (SMD = 0.218).

Distribution, correlations, and determinants of vitamin D metabolic markers

The distributions of vitamin D metabolic markers across pregnancy are shown in Table 3. Concentrations of 25(OH)D3, 3-epi-25(OH)D3, total 25(OH)D, and 24,25(OH)2D3 generally increased across pregnancy. Similar patterns were observed among the 215 women with measurements available at all three trimesters (Additional file 1: Table S5). Seasonal variation was also evident, with higher levels of several vitamin D metabolic markers in summer and autumn (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). Consistent with this pattern, the prevalence of vitamin D deficiency decreased from 42.0% in the first trimester to 13.8% in the third and was highest in spring and winter (Additional file 1: Fig. S4).

Within each trimester, serum concentrations of 25(OH)D3, total 25(OH)D, 3-epi-25(OH)D3, 24,25(OH)2D3, and VMR1 were significantly and positively correlated (r range: 0.35–0.99). Notably, 25(OH)D3 showed strong correlations with 3-epi-25(OH)D3 and 24,25(OH)2D3 (r > 0.85), suggesting closely synchronized metabolism among these metabolites. By contrast, 25(OH)D2 and VMR2 exhibited inverse or weak correlations with 25(OH)D3 (Additional file 1: Fig. S5). Vitamin D3 supplementation was positively associated with serum levels of 25(OH)D3, total 25(OH)D, 3-epi-25(OH)D3, 24,25(OH)2D3, and VMR1 (r range: 0.206–0.562), but inversely correlated with 25(OH)D2 and VMR2. A complementary pattern was observed for vitamin D2 supplementation, which was positively associated with 25(OH)D2 but inversely correlated with the other metabolites. Compared with supplementation, dietary vitamin D intake showed generally weaker correlations with circulating vitamin D metabolic marker (Additional file 1: Fig. S6).

Determinants of serum vitamin D metabolic markers across pregnancy stages are summarized in Additional file 1: Tables S6–S8. Consistent with the above analyses, season of blood collection and vitamin D intake (from supplements or diet) were the primary influencing factors for most metabolites (especially 25(OH)D3, total 25(OH)D, 3-epi-25(OH)D3, and 24,25(OH)2D3) throughout gestation. Additional maternal characteristics, including pre-pregnancy BMI, monthly income, gravidity, primiparity, regular exercise, and sun protection behaviors, were also significantly associated with vitamin D metabolites, although these associations varied by trimester.

Main analysis

Trimester-specific associations between quartiles of vitamin D metabolic markers and the risk of small vulnerable newborns are presented in Table 4. In the first trimester, higher levels of 25(OH)D3 and total 25(OH)D (Q3 and Q4 vs. Q1) were significantly associated with reduced risks of PTB and SVN (P for trend < 0.05). Greater 24,25(OH)2D3 levels were also linked to lower risks of PTB and SVN (P for trend = 0.022 and 0.052, respectively), while women in the highest quartile of VMR1 had a lower risk of PTB (OR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.14, 0.85, P for trend = 0.028). Conversely, increased 3-epi-25(OH)D3 levels were associated with an increased risk of SGA (OR for Q4 = 2.99, 95% CI: 1.30, 6.91), and elevated VMR2 levels were associated with increased risks of LBW, SGA, and SVN. In the second trimester, women in the highest quartile of 25(OH)D3 and total 25(OH)D had a lower risk of SGA, whereas increased 25(OH)D2 levels were positively associated with higher risks of PTB and SVN. Elevated 3-epi-25(OH)D3 and VMR2 levels were consistently associated with higher risks of SGA and SVN. In the third trimester, few associations remained evident; only VMR2 in the highest quartile showed marginal inverse associations with SGA (P for trend = 0.058) and SVN (P for trend = 0.064).

Dose–response relationship

Trimester-specific dose–response relationships between vitamin D metabolic marker and the risk of small vulnerable newborns are illustrated in Figs. 1–3. No significant nonlinear associations were observed in the first trimester. In the second trimester, inverted U-shaped nonlinear associations were confirmed between 25(OH)D2 and the risk of LBW (P overall = 0.031, P nonlinearity = 0.010), PTB (P overall = 0.021, P nonlinearity = 0.015), SGA (P overall = 0.016, P nonlinearity = 0.013), and SVN (P overall < 0.001, P nonlinearity < 0.001). These patterns indicate non-monotonic associations across the exposure range, with relatively higher risk at intermediate levels than at lower or higher levels. Additionally, an S‑shaped relationship was observed between 3-epi-25(OH)D3 and SGA risk (P overall < 0.001, P nonlinearity = 0.003), suggesting a possible threshold-dependent pattern. In the third trimester, a U-shaped nonlinear relationship was found between 25(OH)D2 and SGA risk (P overall = 0.036, P nonlinearity = 0.014), indicating that the risk is relatively lower within the mid‑range of exposure but higher at both lower and higher levels. For 25(OH)D3 and total 25(OH)D in relation to PTB risk, tests for nonlinearity suggested possible deviation from linearity (P nonlinearity = 0.042 and 0.041, respectively), although the overall associations were not statistically significant.

Sensitivity analyses and subgroup analyses

Sensitivity analyses yielded results broadly similar to the main analyses (Additional file 1: Tables S12–S17). When alternative trimester cutoffs were applied, findings were generally consistent, although no significant associations were observed for 25(OH)D3 or total 25(OH)D in early pregnancy—possibly reflecting the reduced sample size in that subgroup. Likewise, restricting analyses to subgroups excluding overweight/obese women or those with GDM revealed no statistically significant associations between 25(OH)D3 or total 25(OH)D and the risks of PTB or SVN.

Stratified analyses revealed potential effect modifications (Additional file 1: Fig. S7–S9). In the first trimester, positive associations of VMR2 with PTB and SVN were observed only in women aged ≥ 30 years (P interaction = 0.013 and 0.015, respectively), and the positive association of 3-epi-25(OH)D3 with SGA was stronger among those who exercised regularly (P interaction = 0.024). In the second trimester, significant interactions were primarily found for LBW risk, stratified by maternal age, regular exercise, and neonatal sex. In the third trimester, significant interactions were identified for 25(OH)D3 and total 25(OH)D with SGA risk. Additionally, neonatal sex modified the associations of 24,25(OH)2D3 and VMR1 with LBW risk, and season of blood collection modified the association of 3-epi-25(OH)D3 with PTB risk (P interaction = 0.045).

Discussion

In this prospective multicenter birth cohort, we quantified a broad panel of maternal vitamin D metabolic markers across pregnancy using validated UPLC-MS/MS and examined their trimester-specific associations with SVN-related outcomes. Higher first-trimester concentrations of 25(OH)D3, total 25(OH)D, 24,25(OH)2D3, and VMR1 were associated with a reduced risk of SVN-related outcomes, whereas elevated 3-epi-25(OH)D3 and VMR2 levels were associated with increased risk of SGA or SVN. After FDR correction, several first-trimester inverse associations were attenuated, whereas the positive associations of 3-epi-25(OH)D3 and VMR2 with SGA/SVN remained robust. In the second trimester, higher levels of 25(OH)D3 and total 25(OH)D were also associated with reduced risk of SGA, while higher levels of 25(OH)D2, 3-epi-25(OH)D3, and VMR2 were linked to elevated risk of PTB, SGA or SVN. Fewer associations were evident in the third trimester, and the overall patterns remained broadly consistent across sensitivity analyses. Taken together, these findings suggest stage-specific associations between maternal vitamin D metabolic markers and adverse birth outcomes, with early-to-mid pregnancy potentially representing a more relevant period.

Vitamin D metabolic markers

25(OH)D is the primary biomarker of vitamin D status. In our cohort, the prevalence of vitamin D deficiency [25(OH)D < 20 ng/mL] declined from 42.0% to 13.8% across trimesters. While lower than global (46–87%) and Chinese national (74.9%) estimates [33], these rates remain higher than those reported in Canada [34]. Consistent with longitudinal observations by O’Callaghan et al. [35], median levels of 25(OH)D3, 3-epi-25(OH)D3, 24,25(OH)2D3, VMR1 increased progressively during pregnancy. This pattern may partly reflect estrogen-related upregulation of vitamin D-binding protein (VDBP) [9]. Compared with pregnancy cohorts from Japan [16], Nigeria [36], and Ireland [35], our participants showed distinct metabolic profiles, likely reflecting differences in population characteristics, gestational timing, and analytical methods.

Strong positive correlations among 25(OH)D3, 3-epi-25(OH)D3, and 24,25(OH)2D3 (r range: 0.35–0.99) were consistent with their close metabolic interrelationships, with 25(OH)D3 serving as a common precursor for both epimerization and catabolism. VMR1 showed moderate correlations with 25(OH)D3 (r range: 0.35–0.53), slightly lower than those reported in a Japanese study (r = 0.732) [16]. By contrast, 25(OH)D2 showed weak or inverse correlations with 25(OH)D3, consistent with NHANES findings [37], while its weak positive correlations with 3-epi-25(OH)D3 and VMR2 warrant further functional investigation.

Furthermore, vitamin D supplementation, particularly vitamin D3, showed stronger correlations with vitamin D metabolic markers than dietary intake, in line with previous studies reporting associations of supplementation with circulating 25(OH)D or 3-epi-25(OH)D3, but not with dietary vitamin D intake [38, 39]. The weaker correlations observed for dietary intake may partly reflect measurement limitations (e.g., non-local food database, recall bias in FFQ), bioavailability differences, and inter-individual variation in absorption and metabolism. Interestingly, VD2 supplementation was weakly and inversely associated with serum 25(OH)D3, aligning with the negative correlation between their serum levels. Overall, these findings suggest that vitamin D3 supplementation may be more strongly reflected in circulating vitamin D metabolic markers than dietary intake.

Determinants of vitamin D metabolic markers

This study identifies season of blood collection and vitamin D supplementation were major determinants of vitamin D metabolic markers. Samples drawn in summer or autumn, along with higher vitamin D intake, were associated with elevated 25(OH)D3, total 25(OH)D, 3-epi-25(OH)D3, and 24,25(OH)2D3 levels, and decreased 25(OH)D2 levels. These patterns are consistent with the established associations of season and supplementation with circulating 25(OH)D3 [30, 40, 41]. Notably, seasonal variation in 25(OH)D appeared less pronounced in the third trimester, which may partly reflect cumulative prenatal supplementation, differences in seasonal distribution of blood collection across trimesters, and physiological adaptations in late pregnancy (e.g., increased vitamin D-binding protein).

Evidence on the impact of other factors on vitamin D metabolites remains limited and inconsistent [42, 43]. In this study, regular exercise was associated with higher 25(OH)D3 and total 25(OH)D in the second trimester, as well as increased 24,25(OH)2D3 and VMR1 in the third trimester, which may reflect exercise-related differences in vitamin D status and calcium homeostasis [44, 45]. Whereas weekly outdoor activity showed no significant associations, likely due to limited overall exposure. Links between 25(OH)D and sociodemographic or lifestyle factors (e.g., maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, parity) vary across studies [30, 46], with some emerging only at specific gestational stages [30].

Primary outcomes

Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have yielded mixed evidence on maternal 25(OH)D and SVN-related outcomes [11, 12]. Our prior meta-analysis of 72 studies identified inverse associations with LBW, PTB, and SGA [11], whereas a multisite prospective cohort highlighted time-dependent effects, especially in early gestation [47]. These inconsistencies across studies may partly reflect differences in study design, timing of blood sampling, population characteristics, and analytical techniques. In the current study, higher 25(OH)D3 and total 25(OH)D levels in early-to-mid pregnancy were linked to lower SVN risks, reinforcing the importance of adequate vitamin D status during these periods.

For 3-epi-25(OH)D3 and VMR2, prior cross‑sectional studies on their associations with PTB have yielded inconsistent findings [17, 48], while some studies have linked higher levels of these markers to adverse neonatal anthropometric outcomes [16, 36, 49]. In the present study, elevated first‑ or second‑trimester 3-epi-25(OH)D3 and VMR2 were associated with increased risks of LBW, SGA, and SVN. However, direct comparability with previous reports is constrained, as most earlier studies are small-scale cross-sectional designs that relied on paired cord blood comparisons between preterm and term neonates, and prospective study linking these metabolic markers to birth outcomes remain limited [16, 17, 48]. Mechanistically, 3-epi-25(OH)D3 can be further metabolized to 3-epi-1α,25(OH)2D3, which retains some biological activity but exhibits lower VDR transactivation, weaker regulation of osteocalcin and bone-related pathways, and slower metabolic clearance compared with classical 1,25(OH)2D3 [50–52]. Elevated 3-epi-25(OH)D3 has also been observed in preeclampsia, a condition linked to placental dysfunction [53]. These observations indicate that 3-epi-25(OH)D3 is a biologically active metabolite with potential clinical relevance, although the specific physiological role of this epimer remains unclear. Of note, the S-shaped association between mid‑pregnancy 3-epi-25(OH)D3 and SGA suggests potential threshold-dependent effects, whereas the inverse associations observed in late pregnancy should be interpreted cautiously and warrant confirmation in future studies.

We also observed inverse associations of 24,25(OH)2D3 and VMR1 with PTB and SVN. Although 24,25(OH)2D3 is traditionally viewed as a catabolic metabolite, in vitro studies suggest it may influence extracellular matrix regulation by inhibiting collagen synthesis, despite its bone-resorptive effect being ~ 1000-fold weaker than 1,25(OH)2D3 [54]. Besides, higher VMR1 may reflect enhanced CYP24A1-mediated vitamin D turnover, potentially preventing excessive 1,25(OH)2D3 accumulation and reducing the risk of hypercalcemia-related placental calcification, a condition linked to impaired intervillous blood flow and fetal distress [55]. Importantly, most prior studies of 24,25(OH)2D3 or VMR1 have focused on non-pregnant populations and bone health, thereby leaving limited evidence for pregnancy.

The present study further revealed that elevated mid-pregnancy 25(OH)D2 levels were positively associated with PTB and SVN, suggesting a possible link to metabolic antagonism with 25(OH)D3. This finding is consistent with previous studies of inverse correlations between 25(OH)D2 and both 25(OH)D3 and 1,25(OH)2D3 [56]. To date, the relative efficacy and biological effects of vitamin D2 versus D3 supplementation remain debated. A potential explanation is that both vitamin D2 and 25(OH)D2 have lower affinity for vitamin D-binding protein than their D3 counterparts, which may lead to higher free 25(OH)D levels and differential skeletal outcomes [56, 57]. Our spline analyses further suggested non-monotonic patterns, with inverted U-shaped associations in the second trimester and a U-shaped relationship with SGA in the third trimester, suggesting threshold-dependent or biphasic effects rather than simple linear associations. As few perinatal studies have evaluated 25(OH)D2 separately from 25(OH)D3, these findings are novel but preliminary. Large-scale studies are warranted to define the optimal range of 25(OH)D2 during pregnancy, its interactions with other vitamin D metabolites, and its biological relevance to maternal and neonatal outcomes. Collectively, these trimester-specific associations highlight the complexity of vitamin D metabolism in pregnancy, although their biological significance remains unclear. Future studies incorporating functional models (e.g., trophoblast or placental organoids) are necessary to clarify the physiological relevance of these metabolites.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including a large sample size, prospective design, and use of a sensitive UPLC-MS/MS method for quantifying multiple vitamin D metabolites across pregnancy stages. To our knowledge, this is the first investigation to delineate trimester-specific associations and dose‑response relationships between a broad panel of vitamin D metabolic markers and SVN-related outcomes, with rigorous adjustment for potential confounders.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, we could not reliably detect key active forms such as 1,25(OH)2D3, and the non-local food database may have introduced measurement error due to cross-country differences in food composition, fortification, and cooking methods. Second, our single-center design may restrict the extrapolation of our findings to populations with disparate environmental, dietary, or genetic backgrounds. Third, although baseline characteristics were largely comparable between included and excluded participants, the moderate imbalance in maternal occupation suggests potential selection bias. However, the overall influence on our findings is likely modest given the general similarity of most other variables. Fourth, despite adjustment for a wide range of sociodemographic, behavioral, and pregnancy-related factors, residual confounding from unmeasured factors, including direct ultraviolet exposure, skin pigmentation, and environmental pollutants, cannot be entirely ruled out. Finally, differences in participant characteristics and seasonal distribution of blood collection across trimesters, together with the limited number of participants with complete longitudinal serum samples, may have influenced the comparability of trimester-specific associations and highlight the need for validation in larger multi‑center cohorts.

Conclusions

Maternal vitamin D metabolic markers exhibit distinct, trimester-specific associations with SVN-related outcomes. Early-to-mid pregnancy may be a period of particular relevance, during which elevated 3-epi-25(OH)D3 and VMR2 showed more robust associations with increased SGA/SVN risk, whereas early inverse associations involving 25(OH)D3, total 25(OH)D, and 24,25(OH)2D3 were attenuated after FDR correction and should be interpreted cautiously. These findings emphasize the importance of assessing a broader panel of vitamin D metabolic markers beyond total 25(OH)D to better understand fetal growth and guide personalized vitamin D supplementation. Further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms and long-term implications.
