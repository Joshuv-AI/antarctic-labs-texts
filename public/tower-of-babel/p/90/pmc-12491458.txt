Determinants of treatment success and cost implications in MDR/RR-TB patients: a prospective cohort study in China
Authors: Meijuan Tian, Hui Ding, Peng Lu, Qiao Liu, Xiaoyan Ding, Jingjing Pan, Limei Zhu, Haitao Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Multidrug-resistant or rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (MDR/RR-TB) remains a critical global health threat. While novel regimens offer promise, the impact of socioeconomic determinants and clinical factors on treatment success is inadequately characterized, hindering targeted interventions. A prospective cohort study was conducted across 13 TB-designated hospitals in Jiangsu Province, China from 2021 to 2022. Binary logistic regression identified predictors of treatment success, with model performance assessed via Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves assessing predictive performance. The overall treatment success rate for patients with MDR/RR-TB was 67.38%, with the short-term regimen achieving a success rate of 74.4%, new long-term oral regimens at 66.7%, new long-term injectable regimens at 71.0%, traditional long-term regimens at 60.3%, though differences were not statistically significant (P = 0.454). Patients educated at the junior and senior high school levels (OR = 3.95, 95% CI: 1.70, 9.19, P = 0.001) and at the college level or above (OR = 3.13, 95% CI: 1.03, 9.51, P = 0.044) exhibited significantly higher success rates compared to those with primary school education or lower. Moreover, it underscores the irrelevance of cost to treatment outcomes. Additionally, urban workers (OR = 4.53, 95% CI: 1.22, 16.86, P = 0.024), urban residents (OR = 4.61, 95% CI: 1.25, 17.04, P = 0.022), and individuals covered by other medical insurance, including public medical insurance (OR = 8.82, 95% CI: 1.50, 51.76, P = 0.016), demonstrated higher treatment success rates compared to those without medical insurance. Conversely, hypokalemia (OR = 0.12, 95% CI: 0.02, 0.61, P = 0.010) was identified as a risk factor for successful treatment. Treatment costs demonstrated no significant association with outcomes (OR = 1.06, 95%CI: 0.96, 1.17, P = 0.284). Prioritizing health literacy programs, insurance expansion, and hypokalemia monitoring is essential for improving treatment success.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17061-7.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant global public health challenge. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the number of TB cases worldwide reached 10.8 million in 2023, with an incidence rate of 134 cases per 100,000 people—representing a net reduction of only 8.7% from 2015 to 20231. In 2023, China accounted for 6.8% of global TB cases, ranking third worldwide, highlighting the country’s continued high TB burden.

In 2023, the global prevalence of MDR/RR-TB reached 400,000 cases, with 176,000 new cases diagnosed, underscoring its continued global health threat1. The complexity and prolonged nature of MDR/RR-TB regimens, coupled with high medication costs, impose a substantial financial burden on patients. A review of the literature estimated the mean direct cost incurred by MDR-TB patients at $1,936.25 (SD ± $1,897.03), with indirect costs averaging $1,200.35 (SD ± $489.76), resulting in a total mean expenditure of $3,151.19 (SD ± $2,379.15)2. Notably, approximately 82% of DR-TB patients experience catastrophic health expenditures during their treatment, far from the End TB Strategy’s goal of eliminating financial hardship associated with TB care3. Therefore, the financial burden on MDR/RR-TB patients warrants particular attention.

Chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of MDR-TB treatment, and continuous optimization of treatment regimens is critical to achieving global TB eradication goals. Short-term regimens enhance patient adherence, minimize adverse drug reactions, and reduce treatment costs4. The principle of “all-oral, short-course, low-toxicity” therapy has become the rudiments of novel MDR-TB treatment strategies5. The success rates of short-course regimens range recommended by WHO from 75.2 to 93%, with associated costs primarily falling between USD 4334 and USD 66185–10. In contrast, longer regimens achieve success rates of 52–66%, while costs vary widely from USD 1,839 to USD 834010–13. Furthermore, a phase III non-inferiority trial indicated comparable treatment success rates between a shorter regimen (78.8%) and a longer regimen (79.8%)8. These potential health and economic benefits underscore the rationale for increased investment in shorter regimens.

A key challenge in combating TB is enhancing treatment success among MDR/RR-TB patients. In 2023, The current average global treatment success rate for MDR/RR-TB (68%) remains suboptimal1. Ensuring timely access to high-quality treatment is imperative to reduce relapse rates and mortality among MDR/RR-TB patients. Understanding the factors influencing successful MDR/RR-TB treatment outcomes is essential for improving TB program management. Current research focuses on both TB-specific factors and social determinants of health (SDOH) impacting treatment success. Systematic reviews identify SDOH such as low income (OR = 1.77), low educational attainment (OR = 1.69), and alcohol abuse (OR = 2.13) as significant predictors of MDR-TB treatment failure14. TB-specific factors, including treatment regimen, time to sputum culture conversion, and patient history, are also strong predictors of treatment outcomes15. Economically, studies demonstrate that the proportion of household expenditure allocated to treatment costs (OR = 1.7) significantly influences TB treatment outcomes16.

However, a notable gap exists in research specifically examining the impact of treatment costs and economic burden on MDR/RR-TB treatment outcomes. Existing literature primarily focuses on the cost-effectiveness of different regimens or the factors influencing cost-effectiveness2,17,18. The direct effect of treatment expenditures on therapeutic success remains unclear. Therefore, to address this gap and elucidate the role of treatment costs in MDR/RR-TB outcomes, considering both the potential benefits of TB regimens and their associated costs motivated our study. This study aims to examine the impact of treatment regimen and associated costs on successful treatment outcomes among patients with MDR/RR-TB.

Methods

Study setting

The prospective cohort study was conducted from January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2022, across 13 municipal tuberculosis-designated medical hospitals in Jiangsu Province, China. This study enrolled patients with drug susceptibility testing-confirmed MDR/RR-TB, who were subsequently allocated to one of four World Health Organization-recommended treatment regimens based on comprehensive clinical assessment, prior anti-tuberculosis treatment history, and second-line drug resistance profiles2,19,20. Short-term treatment regimens: 4–6 Am-Mfx-Pto-Cfz-E-Z-HINH/5 Mfx-Cfz-E-Z; New long-term oral regimen: 6 Lfx (Mfx)-Lzd-Cfz-Cs-Z (E, Pto)/12–14 Lfx (Mfx)-Cfz-Cs-Z (E, Pto); and new long-term injectable regimens: 6 Mfx (Lfx)-Lzd-Cfz (Cs)-Am-P-to-Z (E)/12 Mfx (Lfx)-Cfz (Cs)-Pto-Z (E); and traditional long-term regimens: 6 Cm (Am)-Lfx (Mfx)-Pto (PAS, E)-Cs (PAS, E)-Z/18 Lfx (Mfx) - Pto (PAS, E) - Cs (PAS, E) - Z21.

Study population

All eligible individuals meeting the following criteria were enrolled in this cohort: (1) diagnosis of MDR-TB or RR-TB; (2) age ≥ 15 years; (3) availability of sputum specimens for Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) testing; (4) provision of written informed consent. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) current hepatic, renal, metabolic, autoimmune, endocrine, or malignant diseases; (2) hematological dysfunction, long-term immunosuppressant use, or HIV/AIDS; (3) psychiatric or epilepsy history; (4) pregnancy or lactation; (5) refusal to provide informed consent, as previously described21; the research flowchart is presented in Fig. 1.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was treatment success versus adverse outcomes, defined per WHO guidelines22. Treatment success represents the composite of cured patients and those achieving treatment completion. Specifically, cured denotes pulmonary tuberculosis patients with bacteriologically confirmed TB at baseline who completed treatment per national policy while demonstrating bacteriological response without evidence of failure. Treatment completion describes patients who fulfilled the national treatment protocol without meeting criteria for either cure or failure. The sputum culture of successfully treated patients turns negative at least before the enhancement period (6 months). Conversely, adverse outcomes encompass four categories: treatment failure, meaning regimens requiring permanent termination or fundamental strategy alteration; death, covering mortality occurring Original treatment initiation or during therapy; loss to follow-up, defined as either never starting treatment or experiencing treatment interruption lasting two consecutive months or longer; and not evaluated, referring to patients lacking any outcome assignment. Patients underwent monthly follow-ups during the intensive treatment phase and bimonthly follow-ups during the continuation phase, with qualitative assessments conducted using structured questionnaires.

Study definitions

Patients were classified based on drug susceptibility testing results as either RR-TB or MDR-TB; RR-TB was defined as resistance to rifampicin alone, whereas MDR-TB was characterized by resistance to at least two first-line anti-tuberculosis drugs, including isoniazid and rifampicin, simultaneously. Additionally, patients were categorized according to treatment history as either primary or retreatment cases. Primary treatment cases included individuals who had never received anti-tuberculosis drug therapy or had undergone an incomplete course of a standard chemotherapy regimen (i.e., less than one month of irregular chemotherapy). Retreatment cases were defined as those who had either received inappropriate or irregular anti-tuberculosis treatment for ≥ 1 month, experienced treatment failure, or relapsed following prior therapy1,23.

Adverse reactions

Adverse events (AEs) were stratified by severity using the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases Division of AIDS (NIAID DAIDS) grading system, while serious adverse events (SAEs) were assessed using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version, which defines severity as follows: Grade 1 (Mild), Grade 2 (Moderate), Grade 3 (Severe or medically significant but not immediately life-threatening), Grade 4 (Life-threatening; requiring urgent intervention), and Grade 5 (Death related to adverse event)23,24.

Cost evaluation

Direct cost, also known as total costs (TC) included direct medical expenses(DME) (registration, consultation, hospitalization, investigation, or medication) and non-medical (food, travel, and nutritional supplements). Indirect costs included overall loss of wages due to productivity loss, missed work, loss of time, loss of income, and caregiving work25. Medical expense reimbursement (MER) was the portion of DME covered by insurance or reimbursement schemes, and remaining DME was the out of pocket expenses (OOPs)21. Costs were primarily sourced from hospital billing records, supplemented by patient self-reports for out-of-pocket expenses. Self-reported data underwent cross-referencing with medical bills, consistency checks via follow-up calls.

The dependent and independent variables

The dependent variable was treatment outcome, categorized dichotomously as successful treatment or unsuccessful treatment. Independent variables encompassed demographic factors (age, sex, educational levels, BMI, registered residence); tuberculosis-related factors (treatment regimens, drug resistance results, adverse reaction, symptom, therapeutic category, delayed treatment); socioeconomic impacts (impact on life, subsistence, divorce or separation, unemployment, interruption of studies, social discrimination, other effects); along with economic burden measures (DME, MER, OOPs, direct non-medical expenses, TC).

Statistical analysis

Data were collected via structured questionnaires, collated in Microsoft Excel, and analyzed using SPSS 23.0. All patient identifiers were removed prior to analysis to ensure anonymization and protect patient privacy. Descriptive statistics were computed for all study variables. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %), while continuous variables—summarized using medians with interquartile range (IQR)—are reported as median (IQR). The Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical data comparison, and the Mann-Whitney U test was applied to continuous data, according to the overall non-normal distribution. Correlation analysis was conducted using Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlation coefficients, and correlation heatmaps were generated using the ggcorrplot package in R. Multicollinearity was diagnosed based on correlation coefficients, tolerance values (> 0.1), and variance inflation factors (VIF < 5). Binary logistic regression was performed using variables that demonstrated significant between-group differences and no evidence of multicollinearity. The full model included gender, age, treatment regimen, hypokalemia, muscle soreness, hyperuricemia, education level, medical insurance (MI), TC, and MER as covariates, with MER and TC treated as continuous variables; In Multivariable Model I, the model is adjusted for sex, age, treatment regimens, hypokalemia, muscle soreness, hyperuricemia, education, MI, MER. In Multivariable Model II, the model is adjusted for sex, age, treatment regimens, hypokalemia, muscle soreness, hyperuricemia, education, MI, TC. Briefly, Model I was the full model with MER eliminated; Model II was the full model with TC removed. Predictive performance was evaluated using ROC curves and the area under the curve (AUC), and a threshold of 0.5 was applied to classify successful treatment outcomes. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy are parameters used to evaluate the predictive performance of the model. The associations between the four treatment regimens and AEs were assessed using the chi-square test. For statistically significant associations identified by the chi-square test, pairwise comparisons between regimens were performed using the Bonferroni method to adjust for multiple testing, setting the significance level at α = 0.05/6 = 0.008. Treatment regimens and AEs demonstrating statistically significant associations in the chi-square test and subsequent Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons were included as independent and dependent variables, respectively, in subsequent binary logistic regression analyses to further investigate the influence of the treatment regimens on the occurrence of specific AEs. A two-sided significance level of α = 0.05 was used, with P ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethical approval

The study involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration and received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Jiangsu Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention (Ethics Approval No. JSJK2023-B022-01). Financial support was provided by the Jiangsu Provincial Health Commission Scientific Research Project (Grant/Award Number: MQ2024008). Experimental protocols were performed in compliance with relevant institutional guidelines and regulatory requirements, with informed consent obtained from all participants.

Results

Demographic characteristics in MDR/RR-TB patients

A total of 253 MDR/RR-TB patients were enrolled, with 20 excluded for incomplete data or inadequate sputum culture results at 18–24 months, yielding 233 (92.1%) for final analysis (Fig. 1). The cohort comprised 161 males and 72 females, with the majority (51.1%) having attained a high school education level. No SAEs were observed in patients. Treatment regimens included: short-term regimen for 35 patients, new long-term all-oral regimen for 66 patients, new long-term injectable-containing regimen for 69 patients, and conventional long-term regimen for 63 patients. Local residents and normal weight constituted the majority. Cases included 150 new and 83 retreatment patients. Among those undergoing regular treatment, 157 patients (67.38%) achieved treatment success, with 129 being cured and 28 completing treatment. Unfortunately, 56 patients experienced treatment failure, 16 were lost to follow-up, and 4 died. The treatment success rates were 65.2% for males and 72.2% for females. The success rates for various treatment regimens were as follows: short-term regimen (74.3%), new long-term oral regimen (66.7%), new long-term injectable regimen (71.0%), and traditional long-term regimen (60.3%); however, no statistically significant differences were observed between the groups (Table 1).

Univariate analysis of treatment outcomes in MDR/RR-TB patients

Factors such as younger age, education level beyond middle school (73.9%), presence of muscle soreness (83.9%), lower incidence of hypokalemia (30.0%), and presence of hyperuricemia (84.6%) were found to be associated with a higher likelihood of treatment success. Among the 157 patients who successfully completed treatment, 57 (74.0%) were beneficiaries of urban workers’ medical insurance, and 58 (69.0%) had urban residents’ medical insurance. Patients who achieved treatment success exhibited higher MER costs (RMB 131,000 vs. RMB 129,100, χ2=5171, P = 0.099) and TC (RMB 175,400 vs. RMB 162,200, χ2=5148, P = 0.090) compared to those who experienced treatment failure (Table 1). Other variables with no statistically significant differences are shown in Appendix Table S2.

Factors influencing treatment success among MDR/RR-TB patients

In Model I, the regression coefficients from the Omnibus test showed no significant differences except for the constant term (χ2 = 45.54, P < 0.001). A high correlation was observed between MER and TC (R = 0.87, P < 0.001) (Appendix Fig. S1). Table 2 demonstrates that education at the junior and senior high school levels (OR = 3.95, 95% CI: 1.70, 9.19; P = 0.001) and at the college and above levels (OR = 3.13, 95% CI: 1.03, 9.51; P = 0.044) were significant factors contributing to the successful treatment rate of MDR/RR-TB patients, when compared to those with primary school education or below. Further analysis revealed that medical insurance, specifically for urban workers (OR = 4.53, 95% CI: 1.22, 16.86; P = 0.024), urban residents (OR = 4.61, 95% CI: 1.25, 17.04; P = 0.022), and other types of medical insurance (OR = 8.82, 95% CI: 1.50, 51.76; P = 0.016), was associated with a higher treatment success rate compared to the absence of medical insurance. Additionally, the presence of hypokalemia (OR = 0.12, 95% CI: 0.02, 0.61; P = 0.010) was found to be a risk factor for reduced treatment success rates. The findings of the full model were consistent with those of Model II, with the utilization of medical insurance, education level, and presence of hypokalemia all identified as key factors influencing the successful treatment of MDR/RR-TB patients (Table 2).

The discriminative ability of Model I (AUC = 0.763, 95% CI: 0.698, 0.828) was higher than that of Model II (AUC = 0.761, 95% CI: 0.696, 0.826) and the full model (AUC = 0.760, 95% CI: 0.695, 0.826) (Fig. 2). Association between treatment regimens and adverse events

Chi-square analysis revealed statistically significant differences in the incidence of specific adverse events among the four treatment regimens: drug-induced liver injury (DILI; χ2 = 9.40, P = 0.021) and depression (χ2 = 7.16, P = 0.015). Subsequent pairwise comparisons indicated a statistically significant difference in the incidence of DILI specifically between the short-term treatment regimen and the new long-term oral regimen (Supplementary Table S3). Binary logistic regression analysis demonstrated that, compared to the new long-term oral regimens, the short-term treatment regimen was a significant risk factor for DILI in both the unadjusted model (OR = 7.27, 95% CI: 1.82, 29.02, P = 0.005) and the model adjusted for age and sex (OR = 8.67, 95% CI: 2.04, 36.81, P = 0.003) (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

This study presents a comprehensive analysis of MDR/RR-TB treatment outcomes, revealing an overall success rate of 67.38%. Crucially, our analysis identified education level, health insurance status, and hypokalemia as statistically significant determinants of treatment success, whereas treatment regimens and associated costs demonstrated no significant correlation—a finding requiring nuanced interpretation.

Educational attainment demonstrated significant protective effects, with higher education levels (OR = 0.039) correlating with superior outcomes26. This advantage likely operates through enhanced health literacy, which elevates treatment adherence (RR = 1.83) and completion rates (RR = 2.15)27. Higher levels of education often correlate with improved health literacy, which in turn enhances treatment adherence28. China’s National Tuberculosis Control Program (2024–2030) strategically targets high-education settings through its “Five Entries” initiative (hospitals, communities, enterprises, schools, households), implementing comprehensive educational measures including health curricula, awareness lectures, and specialized student manuals. Complementary clinical support systems - telephone consultations, home visits, electronic pillboxes, and peer-support networks - further strengthen adherence29,30.

The substantial influence of health insurance stratification on therapeutic outcomes constitutes a pivotal observation. Patients covered by Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance (UEBMI) achieved 74.0% treatment success, significantly outperforming those under Urban Resident Basic Medical Insurance (URRBMI; 69.0%) and New Cooperative Medical Scheme (NCMS; 52.6%). This gradient directly corresponds to reimbursement differentials: 98% for UEBMI, 90% for URRBMI, and 75% for NCMS recipients in primary care facilities31. These findings align with established literature documenting reduced treatment failure among insured populations and reflect China’s progressive policy initiatives to strengthen financial protection32. Notable interventions include free first-line antitubercular drugs, complimentary initial and 6-month follow-up chest radiography, means-tested medication subsidies, and medical assistance through Civil Affairs Bureaus for impoverished patients - collectively establishing multi-source financing mechanisms to alleviate MDR/RR-TB burdens31. Nevertheless, persistent systemic challenges require attention, including restrictive eligibility thresholds for poverty assistance programs and institutional financial disincentives that compromise reimbursement adequacy. Comprehensive integration of social resources remains imperative for optimizing social protection frameworks.

Financial burdens, such as catastrophic costs calculated by TC relative to annual household income and effective compensation ratio (ECR) calculated by TC in conjunction with OOPs, underscore the detrimental impact of economic strain on treatment success33,34. Paradoxically, our multivariate analysis revealed no direct statistical association between treatment costs and therapeutic success16,35,36. This apparent contradiction may be contextualized by the region’s exceptional economic profile: with 2024 GDP growth (5.8%) leading national development and urbanization rates between 64.4% and 77.6%, robust economic conditions likely mitigated perceived financial hardship. Simultaneously, heightened urbanization correlates with both increased hospitalization expenditures (0.099% and 0.9% rise per 1% increase in per capita GDP and urbanization rate, respectively) and superior healthcare accessibility37. The observed patient preference for tertiary hospitals over primary clinics, despite greater travel burdens, enhanced treatment quality - potentially counterbalancing economic disadvantages38. Additional explanatory factors include our study’s limited sample size and the counterintuitive phenomenon whereby financial strain may enforce strict adherence as a mechanism.

Despite the unsatisfactory association of costs with treatment outcomes, its extent warrants further exploration. A meta-analysis up to 2022 revealed that the total cost of treating MDR-TB patients ranged from $650 to $8,266 across 11 countries, with mean direct and indirect costs of $1,936.25 (SD ± $1,897.03) and $1,200.35 (SD ± $489.76), respectively2. After currency conversion (1 USD = 7.29 RMB), the median OOPs of $3,045 exceeded the global average, while the median indirect cost of $658.43 was lower. These variations may result from differences in treatment protocols and the requirement for patients to undergo more than eight follow-up examinations, leading to higher costs. Importantly, MER substantially exceeded both out-of-pocket and indirect costs, suggesting this mechanism effectively reduced financial barriers to care continuity.

Concurrently, hypokalemia incidence in our cohort (4.3%) was markedly lower than literature reports (31.3%), though its presence significantly compromised treatment success (OR = 0.12)39–42. This association likely stems from cumulative drug toxicity, pharmacokinetic interactions, and adherence deficits, necessitating vigilant electrolyte monitoring during therapy43–45. Shorter treatment duration does not inherently confer improved safety46,47. While systematic reviews indicated comparable overall adverse event rates between short-term and long-term regimens (32.3% vs. 37.6%), suggesting similar safety profiles, specific studies demonstrated a higher incidence of hepatotoxicity with short-term regimens (8.9% vs. 5.7%)48,49. This elevated DILI risk is primarily attributable to the combined administration, extended duration, and intensified dosing of hepatotoxic agents—notably high-dose isoniaiazid (INH), protionamide (Pto), and pyrazinamide (Z)50–52. Song et al. observed markedly higher DILI rates with the WHO shorter regimen (18.3%) than with levofloxacin-based (3.5%) or bedaquiline-containing short-course regimens (4.6%)53. These findings highlight the necessity to optimize therapeutic protocols by balancing safety with treatment duration while implementing rigorous adverse effect monitoring to mitigate hepatotoxic risk.

Interestingly, regarding therapeutic protocols, we observed no statistically significant difference in efficacy between short-term regimen and long-term regimens, corroborating Bulabula et al.54yet contrasting with reports of short-term regimen superiority (90.3% vs. 78.3% success rates)55. Our findings suggest that short-term regimens warrant further investigation as a hypothesis-generating observation. Future large-scale comparative studies with adequate statistical power are essential to conclusively evaluate relative regimen efficacy.

This research provides unprecedented integration of clinical, socioeconomic, and financial variables in MDR/RR-TB outcome analysis. The comprehensive methodology represents a significant advancement over prior unidimensional approaches. However, several limitations merit acknowledgment: Absence of granular adherence metrics and socioeconomic data precluded mediation analysis of cost-outcome pathways. The economic burden of costly treatments coupled with employment insecurity fears demonstrably undermines tuberculosis treatment adherence, frequently precipitating therapy interruption; however, this study did not account for adherence’s mediating role in the cost-outcome pathway. Future investigations should therefore incorporate real-time adherence monitoring through structured interventions—including reminder letters, telephone follow-ups, home visits and electronic pillboxes—supplemented by psychological support mechanisms such as counseling and peer-support networks to systematical enhance medication compliance27,56. Although survey missingness (7.9%) was unlikely to compromise analytical validity, the modest sample size constrains generalizability and necessitates validation in expanded cohorts. While our predictive models identified robust determinants of treatment success, their moderate specificity indicates the need for incorporating novel predictors (genomic markers, social determinants) and advanced analytical techniques (ensemble classifiers) in subsequent research.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that higher education levels, medical insurance, and effective management of hypokalemia are crucial factors in mitigating cacoethic treatment outcomes in MDR/RR-TB patients. Treatment regimens and costs were found to be unrelated to outcomes. Social support systems especially medical insurance coverage and financial support weigh with optimized treatment outcomes while alleviating the burden of MDR/RR-TB. Future large-scale studies employing machine learning methodologies are warranted to conduct integrated analyses of social security frameworks and treatment adherence patterns, rigorously evaluating their collective impact on cost-effectiveness and therapeutic outcomes—particularly following the implementation of novel ultra-short regimens with low toxicity.
