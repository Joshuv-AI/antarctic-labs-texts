Holistic symptom burden and prognostic value of palliative care needs in heart failure: insights from the integrated palliative care outcome scale
Authors: Do Thi Hien, Nguyen Thi Kieu Ly, Nguyen Quy Quyen, Le Thanh Ha, Nguyen Thi Hong Nga, To Lan Phuong, Nguyen Thanh Hai, Doan Van Nghia, Do Thi Trang, Pham Truong Son, Nguyen Van Hinh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHeart failure (HF) patients experience significant palliative care needs (PCN), which can be assessed using the Integrated Palliative Care Outcome Scale (IPOS). This study evaluates PCN in hospitalized HF patients and their association with patient characteristics and mortality.Population and methodsA prospective observational study was conducted on 160 hospitalized HF patients in Vietnam. PCN were assessed using IPOS (17 questions, scored 0–4, none to severe). PCN was defined as ≥ 2 items scoring 4 or ≥ 3 items scoring ≥ 3. All-cause mortality was tracked post-discharge. ResultsPCN were identified in 59.4% of patients, with shortness of breath (57.5%), weakness (51.9%), and pain (45.6%) being the most common symptoms at moderate severity. PCN correlated with longer HF duration, lower eGFR, and specific medication use (intravenous drugs, RAAS inhibitors, MRAs). PCN independently predicted higher post-discharge mortality (adjusted HR: 2.63, 95% CI 1.06–6.53, p=0.037).ConclusionPCN are prevalent in hospitalized HF patients and independently associated with increased mortality, underscoring the need for routine PCN assessment and early palliative care integration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-025-01897-x.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a common disease and a leading cause of death and hospitalization worldwide. It affects approximately 64.3 million people worldwide by 2017, with high mortality rates of 15–30% at one year and 50–75% at five years, despite advances in treatment [1, 2]. Patients with HF experience a multidimensional symptom burden—physical, emotional, and spiritual—comparable to or exceeding that of cancer patients [3]. Sufficient palliative care for heart failure patients was demonstrated to improve patient outcomes, including physical and emotional symptoms, increase quality of life and satisfaction, decrease the cost and utility of medical service, and reduce rehospitalization [4–7]. However, patients with heart failure had inadequate and less palliative care access than those with cancer, and most care was only provided at the end of life, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [8–11]. This gap is partly due to challenges in identifying palliative care needs (PCN) in HF, which are not fully captured by traditional functional classifications like New York Heart Association (NYHA) class [12].

The Integrated Palliative Care Outcome Scale (IPOS) is a validated patient-reported outcome measure that assesses physical and psychosocial symptoms using a 17-item, 5-point Likert scale [13]. Widely used in advanced illnesses like cancer, IPOS has shown feasibility in HF to identify high symptom burdens [14, 15]. Prior studies suggest that worsening IPOS scores during HF hospitalization predict higher post-discharge mortality, but the relationship between PCN at admission and patient characteristics or outcomes in LMICs remains underexplored [16].

We hypothesized that hospitalized HF patients with PCN, assessed by IPOS at admission, would have higher post-discharge mortality compared to those without PCN. This study investigates the prevalence of PCN, their association with patient characteristics, and their prognostic value in Vietnamese hospitalized HF patients, aiming to inform early palliative care integration in LMIC settings.

Population and methods

Study design and population recruitment

A single-center, prospective observational study was conducted on 160 consecutive hospitalized patients with heart failure at 108 Military Central Hospital, Hanoi, Vietnam from January 2023 to June 2023. All patients aged ≥ 18 years old and were diagnosed with heart failure as the primary diagnosis according to the European Society of Cardiology guidelines for heart failure [17] at the Department of Cardiology during the study period. We excluded patients who had severe acute diseases such as sepsis, coma, acute stroke, depressed level of consciousness, early discharge (< 3 days from admission), in-hospital death, or could not fulfill all questions of IPOS (hard of hearing, unwillingness to respond, unable communication) and loss of follow-up. All-cause mortality was tracked via medical records and telephone interviews, cross-verified with hospital registries where possible. Patients with multiple hospitalizations were evaluated for their palliative care needs during only the first admission during the study period. The follow-up was finished on 15 May 2024.

Sample size estimation

The sample size was calculated to estimate palliative care needs (PCN) prevalence and its association with mortality in hospitalized heart failure patients using IPOS. Assuming a 55% PCN prevalence [1, 14], ± 8% precision, and 95% confidence (Z = 1.96), we needed 149 patients, adjusted to 165 with 10% loss. For mortality, targeting a hazard ratio ≥ 2.5 [16], 20% event rate [1], 80% power, and α = 0.05, a Cox model required 140 patients with 28 events [11]. We recruited 160 patients, sufficient for both objectives and feasible for our single-center study.

Data collection

The Integrated Palliative Care Outcome Scale (IPOS) assessed all patients’ symptoms from the first to the second day of index admission to capture acute symptom burden at presentation, aligning with prior studies [14, 15], though differing from serial assessments in others [16]. The IPOS was translated into Vietnamese using a forward-backward translation process by two independent bilingual translators, followed by pilot testing for comprehensibility among 10 HF patients. The coordinator could explain the questionnaire to patients if required. The patients answered all the questions by themselves for one hour. The IPOS consists of seventeen questions using a 5-point Likert scale, scored from 0 to 4, corresponding to no symptoms to severe symptoms. The item was considered as clinically significant symptoms if the score ≥ 2 (at least moderate level). Providers were not informed of IPOS scores to avoid influencing management, as this was an observational study. PCN was defined as having ≥ 2 IPOS items scored 4 (overwhelming) or ≥ 3 items scored ≥ 3 (severe), based on Roch et al. [14] to emphasize clinically significant burdens.

Variables

We collected the patient’s background and heart failure characteristics. The laboratory tests on admission including biochemistry and echocardiography were also obtained. We also collected data about management during the index hospitalization and variables before discharge to predict after-discharge mortality by Meta-Analysis Global Group in Chronic Heart Failure (MAGGIC score) [18]. After index admission, the information on all-cause death during follow-up was used for prognosis analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using R software, version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All statistical tests were two-tailed and a p-value < 0.05 was considered significant. Firstly, we collected and described patients’ demography and distribution of IPOS scores. For missing data, we omitted these cases and analyzed the remaining data. We exclude participants with missing data in the IPOS score and follow-up. Means and standard deviations were used to describe normally distributed continuous variables, and medians and 25th – 75th percentiles (IQR: Interquartile range) for non-continuous variables. Secondly, statistical tests were applied to compare the differences in all characteristics between groups with and without palliative needs. For continuous variables, unpaired Student’s t-test (normally distributed), or Mann-Whitney U-test (no assumption for distribution) was used to compare differences among groups. The Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was performed for dichotomous variables. Finally, we used the Kaplan-Meier method to estimate the all-cause mortality incidence and the log-rank test to compare the two groups. The Cox proportional hazards model was applied to calculate the hazard ratio with adjustment by age and MAGGIC risk score.

Results

The mean age of 160 study patients was 69.6, the male gender was 68.8%, mostly in New York Heart Association class II (49.4%) and the mean EF was 44.2%. Notably, PCN prevalence was high (59.4%) despite a majority in NYHA class II (49.4%), highlighting discordance between functional class and holistic symptom burden. Compared between groups with and without palliative care needs, there were significant differences in the duration of heart failure, usage of oral medication on admission (Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone system inhibitors and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists), intravenous drugs, and estimated glomerular fraction rate. High-sensitivity troponin T levels were elevated, potentially indicating concurrent myocardial injury beyond HF alone.

Regarding residency, the patients lived in both rural and urban areas equivalently. Almost all patients were educated at schools distributed equally from primary to high school. The majority of patients (96.9%) cohabitated with relatives, and farming was the predominant occupation (46.3%). Furthermore, there were no significant differences in socioeconomic factors between groups with and without palliative care needs.

The most common clinically significant physical symptoms were shortness of breath (57.5%), weakness (51.9%), and pain (45.6%), mostly at moderate level. Other symptoms were not commonly met, including digestive discomforts (nausea, vomiting, poor appetite, constipation, and dry mouth) and drowsiness.

Regarding emotional symptoms, patient and family anxiety mostly occurred at a moderate frequency (sometimes) with 43.1% and 51.3%, respectively. Whereas, depression was less common with only 38.1% and 31.3% of patients occasionally and sometimes had this symptom.

Regarding communication issues, moderate frequency (sometimes, a score of 2) was mostly met in feeling at peace (40%), sharing feelings (55%), and enough information (45%).

In practical issues, only about 10% of patients were highly addressed. Almost half of the patients just partly resolved their practical obstacles.

After a median of 405 days follow-up after discharge, 28 patients died with an average incident rate of 21.88 cases per 100 person-year (95% CI, 15.11–31.69). Groups of patients with palliative care needs had a higher risk of all-cause mortality compared to those without these needs (p = 0.024, log-rank test). By the Cox proportional hazards model and adjusted by the age and MAGGIC score, palliative care needs were an independent predictor of all-cause mortality after discharge with the adjusted hazard ratio of 2.63 (95% confidence interval 1.06–6.53, p = 0.037) (Fig. 1, 2, and 3).Fig. 1Study flow diagramFig. 2Distribution of IPOS score. Discussion

This study was designed to assess palliative care needs (PCN) in hospitalized heart failure (HF) patients in Vietnam using the Integrated Palliative Care Outcome Scale (IPOS), and to explore its association with patient characteristics and mortality. Key findings revealed that 59.4% of the 160 patients had PCN, with prevalent symptoms including shortness of breath (57.5%), weakness (51.9%), and pain (45.6%) at moderate levels. Emotional and communication issues, such as anxiety and sharing feelings, were also moderately frequent. PCN correlated with HF duration, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and specific medication use, and was independently associated with higher post-discharge mortality (adjusted HR: 2.63, 95% CI 1.06–6.53, p = 0.037).

Our findings were consistent with previous studies that the burden of holistic symptoms and total IPOS score was not correlated with heart failure severity (including NYHA class, HF stage, LVEF, NT-Pro BNP levels) and comorbidities (except depression) [16]. In our study population, the proportion of patients with depression was only 1.9%, therefore, it might not be sufficient to reveal the differences between the two groups. This correlation remarkably emphasized the importance of regular palliative care needs assessment and referral not only in advanced heart failure and end-of-life care but also in earlier severity and stages [19, 20]. Our study found significant correlations between PCN and specific patient factors. Longer heart failure duration was linked to higher PCN, likely due to progressive disease worsening symptoms over time [21]. Patients with PCN were less likely to use RAAS inhibitors and MRAs on admission, suggesting suboptimal management or due to low renal function, which may increase symptom burden [17]. Greater intravenous drug use in the PCN group (40% vs. 20%, p = 0.008) likely reflects acute symptom acuity, though overall intravenous diuretic rates were lower than typical for acute HF, suggesting milder decompensation in this cohort. Inotropes were not separately analyzed due to low usage. Greater use of intravenous drugs such as diuretics, and inotropes in the PCN group likely indicates acute decompensation requiring aggressive intervention. Such treatments, while addressing immediate symptoms like dyspnea, may not resolve underlying chronic needs, leaving patients vulnerable to persistent distress and poor outcomes [22]. Lower eGFR in patients with PCN may reflect cardiorenal syndrome, exacerbating symptoms like dyspnea and limiting treatment options, thus increasing mortality risk [23].

Even though the majority of patients have moderate severity of heart failure, the percentage of patients having PCN was 59.4%. The percentage of patients with heart failure who had PCN depends on the tool and method of evaluation. It is controversial about the criteria of palliative care needs in patients with heart failure and who should be referred to palliative care assessments [7, 24–26]. On a consensus by international experts about referral criteria for specialist palliative care in advanced heart failure, 56% of experts (mostly cardiologists) agreed with disease-based criteria with markers of severity disease (such as progression, hospitalization, escalation therapies, etc.), whereas, 40% of those (mostly are palliative care specialists) chose need-based criteria (such as symptoms burden, patient and family concerns) [24]. In our study, using IPOS, a need-based assessment, approximately 60% of hospitalized patients still had PCN, and the PCN was not correlated with heart failure severity. Furthermore, we focused on item-based PCN criteria rather than total IPOS scores (may dilute specific burdens) to prioritize severe symptoms, consistent with Carmen Roch et al. [14], who found that 56% of hospitalized heart failure patients had PCN. Further studies should be conducted to define the standard of palliative care evaluation.

In our study population, the patients have multi-dimensional symptom burdens at moderate levels. Firstly, in physical symptoms, shortness of breath was the most frequently reported symptom (57.5%). Our results were similar to previous studies in that the two common unmet symptoms in hospitalized heart failure patients were shortness of breath and weakness [16, 27]. However, the proportion of patients with significant clinical dyspnea was lower than that in the study by Carmen Roch et al. (64%) [14] and Campbell et al. (75%) [27] on European hospitalized patients with heart failure. This is consistent with the majority of our patients having NYHA class II (49.4%). The severity of the heart failure sample population might contribute to these differences. In our hospital, patients with advanced heart failure may be admitted to the cardiac intensive care unit, which was not included in our study, potentially contributing to the lower severity observed in our sample. Secondly, patients also underwent a moderate frequency of emotional symptoms, communication, and practical issues. Many studies showed that patients with heart failure have a high burden of spirit symptoms [14, 28]. About one-third of patients with heart failure could manifest with depression, and it correlated with more severe heart failure, worse quality of life, increased hospitalization, cost of care, and mortality [28–31]. Furthermore, not only patients but also their caregivers might suffer from anxiety [32–34]. Therefore, the Heart Failure Association of the European Society of Cardiology recommends discussing carefully and providing enough information for both patients and their families [35].

Some previous studies have found a correlation between palliative needs and adverse mortality outcomes. In the study by Lyndsay DeGroot et al. in heart failure patients aged older than 50 years, the group with higher unmet palliative needs (defined by total IPOS score ≥ 20) had higher odds of hospitalization or mortality after 6 months (odds ratio, 2.5; p < 0.01) compared to the group without palliative needs (total IPOS score < 20) [36]. Another study by Hamatani et al. on the Japanese hospitalized heart failure population showed that a worsened IPOS score (total score at discharge being higher than at admission during index hospitalization) was an independent predictor of poorer mortality after 1 year (HR: 2.28, 95% CI (1.02–5.09), p = 0.044) [16]. In our study, by using adapted criteria to determine palliative care needs focusing on the severity of symptoms instead of the total IPOS score (having palliative care needs if having two overwhelming levels (score of 4) or at least three severe levels (score ≥ 3)), we found additional finding that the palliative care needs at admission also a predictor of worsen prognosis. The mortality association may reflect unmeasured confounding by disease severity not fully captured by MAGGIC, rather than direct causality. Further studies with a larger population and longer follow-up should be conducted to determine the prediction value of the IPOS score, the level of symptoms on prognosis, and the effectiveness of palliative care on patients’ outcomes.

The independent association between PCN and increased mortality suggests a complex interplay of physiological, psychological, and systemic factors. Patients with high PCN, as identified by severe or overwhelming IPOS scores, likely experience a greater burden of uncontrolled symptoms such as dyspnea, pain, and weakness. These symptoms may reflect underlying disease progression or decompensation that standard HF treatments fail to address adequately. For instance, persistent dyspnea and weakness could indicate worsening cardiac function or fluid overload, contributing to a higher risk of fatal events like arrhythmias or sudden cardiac death [17]. Psychologically, the emotional burden of anxiety and poor communication (e.g., lack of peace or sharing feelings) may exacerbate stress-related pathways, and inflammation, both of which are linked to adverse HF outcomes [37]. Furthermore, unmet PCN may signal inadequate holistic care, delaying interventions that could mitigate symptom escalation and improve prognosis. This aligns with evidence that palliative care interventions when implemented, reduce mortality risk by addressing these multidimensional needs [4, 7].

Our study reinforces the importance of systematic PCN screening in HF patients. Current HF management guidelines recommend palliative care integration, yet its implementation remains suboptimal due to challenges in identifying patients who would benefit most [19]. The IPOS scale offers a feasible, patient-centered tool for identifying those with high symptom burden, potentially guiding timely palliative care referrals [38]. Additionally, our findings suggest that symptom severity, rather than HF classification alone, should inform palliative care decision-making [7]. Given the independent association between PCN and mortality, addressing these needs could improve survival, enhance quality of life, and reduce hospital readmissions [4, 7].

A key strength of this study is the prospective design and use of a validated patient-reported outcome measure (IPOS) to assess PCN comprehensively. Additionally, our analysis adjusted for key prognostic factors, strengthening the association between PCN and mortality. However, this study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, limiting generalizability. Second, while we adjusted for age and MAGGIC score, other potential confounders, such as socioeconomic factors and comorbid depression, were not fully accounted for. Third, providers’ unawareness of IPOS scores, precluded assessing intervention impacts, warranting further research on the benefits of early integration. Furthermore, cultural biases may have influenced self-reported symptoms, potentially underestimating depression/spiritual needs. Exclusion of patients with early in-hospital death or severe acute conditions limits generalizability to the sickest HF patients.

Conclusions

In this study of hospitalized HF patients in Vietnam, PCN was prevalent and independently associated with increased mortality. The findings highlight the necessity of proactive symptom assessment and early palliative care referral, regardless of HF severity. Future research should explore standardized PCN assessment protocols and evaluate the impact of palliative interventions on clinical outcomes. Integrating palliative care into HF management could improve existing care gaps, patient well-being, and survival.(Table 1 and 2)
