Palmar–plantar erythrodysesthesia syndrome resulting from toceranib phosphate in a dog with apocrine gland anal sac adenocarcinoma: a case report
Authors: Eunjoo Kim, Sung-Soo Kim, Min-Ok Ryu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn 11-year-old neutered male Miniature Poodle with a stage 3 apocrine gland adenocarcinoma was started on chemotherapy with toceranib phosphate after surgery. Beginning on day 10 of toceranib, the dog’s foot pads became erythematous and hyperkeratinized. The dog complained of pain, inability to walk, depression, and loss of appetite. The symptoms resolved when toceranib was discontinued and reappeared when toceranib was resumed. Grade 3 palmar–plantar erythrodysesthesia was identified as an adverse event of toceranib based on the VCOG-CTCAE and Naranjo scale. Although very rare in veterinary medicine, clinicians should consider that palmar–plantar erythrodysesthesia can occur after toceranib administration.Keywords: Case reports, hand-foot syndrome, tyrosine protein kinase inhibitor, canine diseases

INTRODUCTION

Toceranib phosphate is an anticancer drug approved by the FDA for canine mast cell tumors. Although approved for grades 1 and 2 canine mast cell tumors, it has been used widely for various cancers [1]. Recent studies showed that chemotherapy with toceranib phosphate improved the progression-free interval and median survival time for canine apocrine gland anal sac adenocarcinoma (AGASACA) [2,3].

Palmar–plantar erythrodysesthesia, also known as hand–foot syndrome, is a relatively common side effect of chemotherapy in humans. It is a disorder characterized by redness, marked discomfort, swelling, and tingling of the palms of the hands or the soles of the feet [4]. According to the CTCAE, it is classified into three grades based on the severity of skin changes, the presence or absence of pain, and the degree of limitation of activities of daily living [4]. It has been reported to occur with various anticancer agents, including sorafenib, sunitinib, cediranib, docetaxel, doxorubicin, cytarabine, and capecitabin [5,6]. On the other hand, its manifestation in veterinary practice is relatively rare. This case report presents an exceptional case of palmer–plantar erythrodysesthesia in a dog after treatment with toceranib phosphate for a stage 3 apocrine gland adenocarcinoma, shedding light on this infrequently reported adverse effect in veterinary medicine.

CASE PRESENTATION

An 11-year-old neutered male Miniature Poodle with a history of surgery for removing a perianal mass three months ago was presented for evaluation of metastasis and a general annual check-up. The histopathology of the resected mass revealed AGASACA. A physical examination showed no remarkable findings except for several subdermal soft masses. The blood analysis results, including complete blood count (CBC), serum chemistry, venous blood gas, and urinalysis, were normal. Diagnostic imaging, including thoracic and abdominal radiography, abdominal ultrasound, and echocardiogram, showed no remarkable findings.

Computed tomography (CT) revealed a soft tissue mass with ill-defined margins around the left external anal sphincter muscle (21.1 × 11.6 × 18.2 mm) and mild to moderate enlargement of the left sacral and left hypogastric lymph nodes. An epithelial malignancy was suspected on cytology via fine needle aspiration, and surgery resecting the mass and the left sacral and hypogastric lymph nodes was performed. On histopathology, the soft tissue mass around the anal sphincter muscle was identified as apocrine adenocarcinoma, and the sacral lymph node was interpreted as metastatic adenocarcinoma. No neoplasia was observed in the hypogastric lymph node. The dog was diagnosed as stage 3 AGASACA based on the Polton and Brearley clinical staging system [7].

For treatment purposes, toceranib phosphate (Palladia, Pfizer Inc., Italy) was administered at 25 mg (3.2 mg/kg) every other day with the owner’s informed consent. One week later (day 7), a physical examination revealed erythema and scaling around the mouth, and the owner complained of pruritus. On day 10, the foot pads were changed to erythema (Fig. 1). Toceranib was discontinued on day 11 because of suspected drug toxicity. After discontinuing toceranib, the erythema was mildly reduced, but the foot pads appeared depigmented.

On day 21, toceranib was reinitiated in consultation with the owners. After seven days, the owner complained of generalized erythema and scaling. After 12 days, the foot pads showed erythema, and the dog was depressed and anorectic. The erythema is a symptom of allergic dermatitis, but despite PDS administration, the erythematous lesion on the pads widened. The pads became edematous, and hyperkeratosis occurred from the pads to the metacarpal/metatarsal lesion. Finally, the dog stopped walking because of the pain, and toceranib was stopped (day 42).

After discontinuing toceranib, the dog had a good appetite and was no longer in pain or lame. The foot pads became black and normalized over time, starting 14 days after discontinuing toceranib. The Naranjo adverse drug reaction probability scale [8] was used to determine if a specific medication caused the side effects. The total score was 9, indicating a definite adverse drug reaction. Although the histopathology of the dermal lesions could not be performed because the owners did not give their consent, based on the history, clinical examination, Naranjo scale, and VCOG-CTCAE [9], a diagnosis of grade 3 plantar-palmar erythrodysesthesia was made as an adverse reaction secondary to toceranib phosphate.

Melphalan was prescribed after discontinuing toceranib, and the dog underwent several examinations, including CBC, serum chemistry panels, ionized calcium level, thoracic/abdominal X-ray, abdominal and perineal ultrasound monthly, and thoracic/abdominal CT every three months to evaluate metastasis, chemotherapy-induced side effects, and paraneoplastic syndrome. Five months after the start of melphalan, CT revealed a metastasis in the lung and the left sublumbar lymph node. Based on current reports that toceranib was effective in stage 3–4 AGASACA [2], a lower dose and lower frequency toceranib protocol (2.5 mg/kg PO every Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday) was initiated, and the dermatologic change was closely evaluated. After taking toceranib twice, the dog began to show the same skin problem as before (Fig. 2), and the doxorubicin/carboplatin protocol was initiated as an alternative to toceranib. Gene analysis was performed to differentiate mutations of the MDR1 gene, but no mutations were detected. All the diagnostic procedures and therapeutic interventions were performed with the informed consent of the owners.

DISCUSSION

In human medicine, skin toxicity has been reported in 70–90% of patients receiving an epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor (EGFR-TKI) [6,10]. The observed cutaneous effects include xerosis, pruritus, papulopustular eruption, paronychia, and hand-foot syndrome (palmar–plantar erythrodysesthesia) [5,6,10,11,12,13,14]. Several studies investigated the underlying mechanisms of these EGFR-TKI-induced skin toxicities, but they are not completely understood. Several studies suggest that skin toxicity may be associated with EGFR inhibition-induced keratinocyte apoptosis, inflammation or capillary endothelial damage, and the drug concentration of papillary dermis [15]. The skin toxicity may be associated with EGFR inhibition, vascular EGF-PDGFR inhibition, and inhibition of EGFR downstream signals, such as STAT3 [14].

In dogs, the skin toxicity of tyrosine kinase inhibitors was not frequently determined and was not as severe as human toxicity. The common side effects of toceranib include gastrointestinal signs, such as vomiting and diarrhea, neutropenia, anorexia, anemia, elevated liver enzymes, and lameness [1]. There are few reports of skin toxicity caused by toceranib in dogs. One case report of skin depigmentation and one case of foot pad thinning and depigmentation have been published [1,16]. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this case report is the first to describe the presentation of palmar–plantar erythrodysesthesia as an adverse effect of toceranib in dogs.

In this case, the skin toxicity of toceranib appeared similar to the skin toxicity of tyrosine kinase inhibitors reported in humans, and the mechanisms are believed to be similar. Despite the recent widespread use of toceranib in canine oncology, clinicians often do not recognize cutaneous toxicity as an adverse effect. Nevertheless, cutaneous adverse reactions with tyrosine kinase inhibitors, including toceranib, may occur in dogs. Considering this case and the other two reported cases [1,16], the skin toxicity of toceranib phosphate may occur within three days to three weeks after initiating the drug and resolve within one or a few weeks after discontinuation of the drug.

In the previously reported clinical trial, a dog with mild hand-foot syndrome tolerated a lower dose of toceranib after the side effects resolved [1]. In this case, however, the same side effect occurred only after two administrations at a 25% reduced dose, suggesting that it was not dose-dependent. In human medicine, there are guidelines for managing skin-related toxicities of EGFR TKIs [13,17]. Mild symptoms can be managed with topical ointments while continuing the anticancer treatment, while severe symptoms may warrant dose reduction or discontinuation. Similar approaches could be considered in dogs.

The 'rash-dosing,' the intentional use of a dose that induces mild symptoms of skin toxicity, is believed to enhance the anticancer efficacy based on some studies in humans [11,12]. Although veterinary evidence is lacking in this regard, if the symptoms are mild and can be managed with topical ointments without affecting the quality of life significantly, the continuation of toceranib may be beneficial despite the potential skin toxicity.

One limitation of this case report is the absence of a histopathological examination of the dermal lesions because the owners did not give their consent, and they disappeared after drug discontinuation. Histopathology could have provided valuable insights into the specific changes and characteristics of the affected skin tissues, further supporting the diagnosis of palmar–plantar erythrodysesthesia. In this case, however, the characteristics of the dermatologic lesions and the temporal relationship between drug administration and clinical signs are considered sufficient to diagnose palmar–plantar erythrodysesthesia.

In conclusion, this case report highlights the occurrence of palmar–plantar erythrodysesthesia as a rare adverse event in a dog receiving toceranib phosphate to treat stage 3 apocrine gland anal sac adenocarcinoma. The temporal relationship between the administration of toceranib and the development of symptoms supports the diagnosis of drug-induced palmar–plantar erythrodysesthesia. Clinicians should consider this potential adverse effect when using tyrosine kinase inhibitors in veterinary oncology. Further research will be needed to understand the underlying mechanisms and management strategies for such drug toxicities in dogs.
