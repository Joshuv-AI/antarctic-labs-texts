Workplace bullying and professional identity among male nurses: the mediating role of work engagement and the moderating effect of fear of negative evaluation
Authors: Yuhao Tong, Xiaohui Wang, Chunmei Lei, Xiong Jian, Xiaomei Xu, Zhao Zheng, Zixiang Ye, Yanping Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWorkplace bullying poses a critical occupational hazard in nursing. The gender and relative scarcity of male nurses heighten their vulnerability to bullying, triggering burnout and eroding their professional identity. These consequences compromise nurse retention, ultimately threatening patient safety and care quality. This study investigated the impact of workplace bullying on male nurses’ professional identity through work engagement mediation and the fear of negative evaluation moderation, with the aim of informing supportive workplace interventions.MethodFrom January to March 2025, a nonrandom convenience sampling methodology was utilized to recruit 369 male nursing professionals across nine tertiary-level general hospitals located within comparable geographic regions of Sichuan Province. The survey included questions on general demographic data as well as research-related scales: the Negative Acts Questionnaire-Revised (NAQ-R), the Nurses’ Professional Identity Scale (PIS), the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES), and the Brief Fear of Negative Evaluation Scale (BFNES). SPSS version 26.0 was used to organize and analyze the data.ResultsThis study initially recruited a cohort of 400 male nurses across surveyed institutions, with 369 complete datasets meeting the inclusion criteria retained for final analysis, yielding a 92.25% valid response rate. Hierarchical regression analysis indicated that workplace bullying and work engagement were significant factors influencing professional identity (ΔR2 = 0.320). Work engagement demonstrated partial mediation, accounting for 46.41% of the total effect of workplace bullying (indirect effect = -0.413). Moderation analyses revealed that fear of negative evaluation significantly moderated two pathways: (a) the direct effect of workplace bullying on professional identity (β = 0.020, p = 0.027) and (b) the first-stage mediation pathway through work engagement (β = 0.032, p < 0.001). Johnson‒Neyman analyses identified critical thresholds for fear of negative evaluation: workplace bullying negatively predicted work engagement when the FNE was less than 44.30 and professional identity when the FNE was less than 45.09.ConclusionThese findings suggest that workplace bullying and work engagement are independently associated with male nurses’ professional identity. Work engagement may serve as a partial mediator, whereas fear of negative evaluation appears to moderate both direct and indirect associations. Given the cross-sectional design, causal interpretations should be made cautiously. Nonetheless, these results provide valuable insights for developing targeted workplace interventions to support male nurses in bullying-prone settings.Keywords: Male nurses, Workplace bullying, Professional identity, Work engagement

Introduction

Nurses constitute half of the international healthcare workforce and are pivotal in facilitating clinical recuperation and optimizing geriatric health outcomes globally [1, 2]. Despite steady growth in the nursing population, persistent workforce shortages remain worldwide [3]. The International Council of Nurses (ICN) projects that the global nurse deficit will reach 13 million by 2030 [4]. By the end of 2022, China had 5.22 million registered nurses (3.71 per 1,000 people), but still faced a shortfall of nearly 4 million [5]. In comparison, the United States had 5.23 million nurses (15.6 per 1,000 people), yet is projected to experience a shortage of 1 million by 2030 [6, 7]. Similar challenges have been reported in the United Kingdom [8], Japan [9], and the Czech Republic [10]. Although recruitment strategies and international nurse mobility have partially alleviated these shortages, they have not addressed the entrenched gender imbalance in the profession [11].

Globally, women make up approximately 90% of the nursing workforce [12, 13]. Although the proportions vary across countries, the representation of male nurses remains consistently low worldwide: 9.6% in the United States [14], 11.7% in Australia [15], 11% in the United Kingdom [16], and only 3.5% in China [17]. This underrepresentation contributes to workforce inflexibility, increases ergonomic strain on female nurses, and elevates the risk of musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) [18]. It also exacerbates challenges in meeting patients’ privacy and psychosocial needs, thereby reinforcing gender stereotypes [19]. The underrepresentation of male nurses in critical care units such as ICUs, operating rooms, and emergency departments hinders team collaboration and compromises the quality of healthcare delivery, particularly in high-stakes clinical environments [20, 21]. Studies have shown that integrating male nurses into healthcare teams enhances emergency response effectiveness during critical incidents and reduces patient transfer times in clinical settings [22]. Furthermore, male nurses demonstrate notable advantages in areas such as high-intensity clinical work, emergency decision-making, and the operation of advanced medical equipment [23]. Increasing male representation is therefore essential for addressing workforce shortages and promoting gender-diverse models of care.

The ICN has identified improving gender balance, including greater representation of men, as a core strategy for strengthening the sustainability of the nursing workforce [24, 25]. However, the professional environment for male nurses remains unfavorable. Workplace bullying (WPB) is a major occupational health concern globally and is particularly prevalent in healthcare [26]. WPB is a pattern of repeated and unreasonable behavior directed at an individual or group that causes, or is likely to cause, psychological, physical, or economic harm. It involves a misuse of power and may include verbal abuse, social exclusion, intimidation, or other actions that undermine a person’s dignity and create a hostile work environment [27, 28]. The global rate of WPB among workers is estimated to range from 11% to 18% [29]. In healthcare, reported prevalence substantially higher, ranging from 55.2–91% in Asia, 28.7–63% in North America, and 4.1–31.3% in Europe [30]. A global review found that 39.7% of nurses have experienced WPB [31]. Johnson [32] reported prevalence rates of approximately 15.8% in South Korea, 22% in Israel, 21–31% in the United States, and 29–33% in Canada. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported that the incidence of WPB among nurses ranges from 17% to 94% [33]. In China, nearly half (44%) of nurses reported exposure to WPB within the past year [34].

These findings indicate that nursing is among the occupations with the highest prevalence of WPB. Some scholars argue that WPB is deeply entrenched in nursing, tracing back to Florence Nightingale, who reportedly employed bullying behaviors to train and discipline nurses [35]. Since the establishment of modern nursing, Nightingale’s belief that the profession should be female-led has contributed to the historical marginalization of male nurses [36]. This historical foundation reinforced gender stereotypes within nursing, making it more difficult for men to enter the profession. As Evans [21] noted, the gendered division of labor in nursing reflects patriarchal structures that historically assigned emotional and caregiving roles to women. Even when women are numerically dominant, they may unintentionally sustain gender hierarchies that marginalize men. These dynamics continue to shape male nurses’ experiences, exposing them to stigma and exclusion.

Research indicates that male nurses are often perceived as deviating from traditional masculine norms, which contributes to perceptions of incompetence, discrimination, and unfair treatment [37]. Their numerical underrepresentation further increases their visibility and the likelihood of being targeted. Leymann [38] emphasized that individuals occupying nontraditional positions within organizations—such as men in a female-dominated profession—are at greater risk of bullying. This heightened vulnerability arises from their increased visibility and perceived deviation from workplace norms. Salin (2015) [39] similarly reported that working in occupations traditionally dominated by the opposite gender constitutes a risk factor for WPB. Numerous studies have reported higher WPB prevalence among male nurses compared with their female counterparts [40–42]. In addition, WPB has been associated with demographic and occupational characteristics including gender, professional title, sleep quality, and family structure [43].

WPB, a distinct form of workplace violence, has been associated with a broad range of psychosocial and physiological consequences. These include fear, anxiety, burnout, depression, and diminished self-efficacy, as well as somatic symptoms such as sleep disturbances, dysregulated cortisol secretion, and gastrointestinal dysfunction [44–46]. Experimental studies further suggest that WPB, conceptualized as a form of social defeat, disrupts dopaminergic neurotransmission and induces structural and functional impairments in the hippocampus. These neurobiological alterations compromise emotional regulation and cognitive functioning, thereby diminishing professional performance [47, 48]. Male nurses exposed to WPB may consequently experience reduced work engagement, weakened PI, and increased turnover intentions. These outcomes not only intensify workforce shortages but also undermine the sustainability of the profession and impede efforts to build gender-inclusive healthcare environments [49, 50].

Therefore, elucidating the pathways through which WPB operates, enhancing male nurses’ PI, retaining and attracting qualified male nursing professionals, fostering a healthy work environment, and maintaining workforce stability are critical issues that warrant attention in current research.

Impact of workplace bullying on professional identity

Professional identity (PI) is developed through professional socialization, in which individuals reflectively internalize social positioning, interpersonal relationships, professional competencies, and values [51]. This process fosters a sense of belonging to the professional role and promotes its positive perception and evaluation [52]. This multidimensional construct plays a pivotal role in stabilizing the nursing workforce and enhancing the quality of healthcare services. Historically, nursing professionals have faced relatively low social status, often accompanied by diminished levels of PI [53]. Furthermore, male nurses typically exhibit lower levels of PI than their female counterparts do, a disparity shaped by traditional gender stereotypes and limited career advancement opportunities. A diminished PI has been associated with a greater likelihood of turnover intentions and career transitions among male nurses [54].

Given the importance of PI for nurses’ professional stability and retention, understanding the potential stressors that influence it is essential. Previous studies have shown that demographic factors, educational background, professional title, sleep quality, income, and the work environment significantly influence PI [55–59]. WPB has been identified as a major threat, reducing midwives’ PI and job satisfaction, while increasing turnover intentions and workforce shortages [60]. Similarly, Cao [61] reported that nursing students exposed to WPB during clinical internships exhibited lower PI, with greater bullying frequency and intensity linked to more severe declines. Due to their vulnerable position within hierarchical structures, nurses often struggle to defend themselves, which undermines their PI, attitudes, and work behaviors [62]. Wright [63] further noted that male nurses are more vulnerable to WPB and experience greater PI disruption than their female counterparts. From the perspective of Conservation of Resources (COR) theory, individuals strive to maintain resources such as self-esteem, social support, and professional identity [64]. WPB depletes these resources by diminishing male nurses’ professional worth and damaging interpersonal relationships, ultimately eroding their PI [65].

Therefore, this study proposes Hypothesis 1 (H1): WPB negatively predicts male nurses’ PI.

Impact of workplace bullying on work engagement

Work engagement (WE) describes a proactive cognitive-affective state characterized by vigor, dedication, and absorption [66]. Goodboy et al. [67] found that WPB negatively impacts WE by undermining basic psychological needs, such as autonomy, competence, and relatedness. This disruption reduces intrinsic motivation and depletes psychological resources, ultimately leading to a shift from active engagement to passive coping and declines in vigor, dedication, and absorption. Yu et al. [68] found a negative correlation between nurses’ WE levels and their exposure to WPB. Furthermore, Chan [69] identified exposure to WPB as an independent risk factor for decreased WE among nurses. According to the COR theory, WPB accelerates resource depletion by creating stress, undermining confidence, and limiting recovery opportunities [70]. This resource depletion contributes to emotional exhaustion, reduced job satisfaction, and weakened organizational commitment, ultimately undermining WE [71].

Therefore, this study proposes Hypothesis 2 (H2): WPB negatively predicts WE.

Mediating role of work engagement

WE involves multiple dimensions, including emotional, cognitive, and behavioral investment [72]. Zhang [73] reported that individuals who exhibit high levels of engagement, enthusiasm, and dedication across these dimensions tend to enhance their professional cognition and values, thereby strengthening their PI. A substantial body of research has demonstrated that WE significantly influences PI and that the two constructs are positively correlated [74–76].

According to the COR theory, WPB, as a chronic stressor, depletes nurses’ critical resources—including emotional regulation capacity, cognitive functioning, and social support networks [77]. Threatened by such resource loss, individuals tend to reduce their WE, which is reflected in diminished vigor, weakened dedication, and impaired absorption [78]. This decline in engagement disrupts the resource gain spiral essential for maintaining PI. Typically, high engagement generates accomplishment-related resources that promote the internalization of professional values and competencies [79]. When this generative cycle is interrupted, nurses are unable to replenish identity-sustaining resources, leading to a progressive erosion of PI—an effect particularly pronounced among male nurses [80].

Therefore, this study proposes the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 3 (H3):

WE positively predicts the PI of male nurses.

Hypothesis 4 (H4):

WE acts as a mediator in the relationship between WPB and PI among male nurses.

Moderated role of fear of negative evaluation

Fear of negative evaluation (FNE) is a central component of social anxiety and refers to the fear and anxiety experienced by individuals in response to anticipated or actual negative judgments in social contexts [81, 82]. Fear is considered an evolved psychological mechanism that has emerged as an adaptive response to recurrent survival threats. This enables individuals to detect and respond to danger and challenges, thereby enhancing their environmental adaptability [83]. This study draws on social rank theory, an evolutionary psychology framework positing that individuals are evolutionarily attuned to their perceived status within social hierarchies [84, 85]. Specifically, individuals with social anxiety tend to perceive themselves as occupying lower positions within these hierarchies [86]. Consequently, they prioritize maintaining stability in their hierarchical standing to avoid demotion resulting from criticism or rejection [87]. FNE thus functions as a protective mechanism against status degradation, mitigating the threat of social exclusion and negative judgment. In ancestral environments, social rank within groups directly influenced survival outcomes, and sensitivity to negative appraisal may have served as an adaptive vigilance mechanism to avoid detrimental evaluations and potential ostracism [88].

Furthermore, COR theory posits that individuals actively acquire, maintain, and protect valuable resources, including psychological and emotional assets, which are essential for coping with stressors [89]. In the context of WPB, when FNE is at low to moderate levels, individuals can effectively mobilize available resources to manage stress and sustain psychological well-being, thereby mitigating the negative impact of WPB on WE and PI [90]. However, when FNE is excessively high, heightened vigilance and anxiety are more likely to lead to resource depletion, which undermines coping capacity and exacerbates the detrimental effects of WPB [91].

Empirical research has further demonstrated that individuals with different levels of FNE exhibit varying sensitivity to negative social information and coping ability during interpersonal interactions [92–95]. Building on the above theoretical foundations and empirical evidence, this study posits that individuals with different levels of FNE may differ in their capacity to cope with WPB.

Therefore, Hypothesis 5 (H5) proposes that FNE moderates the relationship between WPB and psychological outcomes among male nurses.

Although an association between WPB and PI has been established, empirical research specifically targeting male nurses remains scarce. To date, no studies have investigated the interrelationships among WPB, PI, and WE in this population. WE, a positive emotional–motivational state, may mediate the relationship between WPB and PI. Moreover, FNE, a stable dispositional trait, may moderate this association. Therefore, this study aims to examine the impact of WPB on the PI of male nurses and to explore the potential mediating role of WE and the moderating effect of FNE. The findings are expected to provide empirical evidence and theoretical insight to inform targeted strategies for supporting and retaining competent male nursing professionals. Methods

Research design and sample

Data collection for this cross-sectional investigation spanned a three-month period from January to March 2025. A nonprobability convenience sampling approach was employed to enroll male nursing professionals across nine urban centers within Sichuan Province, China, including Chengdu, Nanchong, Suining, Deyang, Ya’an, Leshan, Mianyang, Chongzhou, and Qionglai. Data collection was performed via Questionnaire Star, a widely recognized and validated online survey platform commonly used in Chinese behavioral research.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) possession of a valid nursing qualification certificate and registered license; (2) age ≥18 years; (3) at least 1 year of clinical work experience; (4) no cognitive impairment; and (5) voluntary participation with informed consent. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) nurses who were on leave or not currently practicing during the survey period and (2) interns or nurses who were undergoing advanced training.

Following data collection, two researchers independently exported the completed survey data from the Questionnaire Star platform to ensure data integrity. A total of 400 participants initially completed the survey. After excluding surveys that were completed in less than 10 minutes (600 s) and those exhibiting systematic response patterns, such as inattentiveness or uniform answers, 369 valid questionnaires were retained. The study attained a 92.25% response validity rate.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Ethical Review Measures for Biomedical Research Involving Humans (2016) issued by the National Health and Family Planning Commission of the People’s Republic of China. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Chengdu University (Approval No. PJ2025–014–02, date: 2025–01–23).

Measures

Core demographic characteristics

The participants provided self-reported demographic and occupational information, including age, body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), job position, professional title, monthly income (Chinese yuan, CNY), marital status, and night shift status.

Workplace bullying

WPB was assessed via the 22-item Negative Acts Questionnaire-Revised (NAQ-R) developed by Einarsen et al. (2009) [96]. The scale evaluates three dimensions of workplace mistreatment: personal bullying, work-related bullying, and organizational injustice. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never, 5 = daily), yielding total scores from 22 to 110, with higher scores indicating more frequent exposure to WPB. The original instrument demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.915). In the present study, the NAQ-R also showed high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.874) and acceptable construct validity (χ2/df = 1.899, CFI = 0.887, RMSEA = 0.049).

Professional identity

PI was measured via the 30-item Professional Identity Scale for Nurses (PIS) developed by Liu et al. (2011) [97]. The scale comprises five domains: professional role evaluation, professional social support, professional social skills, coping with professional frustration, and professional self-reflection. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 30 to 150; higher scores reflect stronger PI. The original instrument demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.938). In this study, the PIS showed similarly high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.876) and acceptable construct validity (χ2/df = 1.973, CFI = 0.886, RMSEA = 0.051).

Work engagement

WE was evaluated via the 17-item Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES) developed by Schaufeli et al. (2006) [98, 99]. The scale consists of three subscales: vigor, dedication, and absorption. Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = never, 7 = always), producing total scores from 17 to 119; higher scores indicate greater engagement. The original UWES demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.938). In the present study, the UWES showed high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.888) and acceptable construct validity (χ2/df = 2.996, CFI = 0.893, RMSEA = 0.074).

Fear of negative evaluation

FNE was assessed via the 12-item Brief Fear of Negative Evaluation Scale (BFNES) developed by Leary et al. (1983) [100]. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), producing total scores from 12 to 60; higher scores indicate greater FNE and are categorized as low (12–28), moderate (29–44), or high (45–60). The validated Mandarin version, adapted by Cheng et al. (2015) [101], demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.898). In the present study, the BFNE showed high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.864) and adequate construct validity (χ2/df = 2.639, CFI = 0.928, RMSEA = 0.067).

Statistical analysis

EpiData version 3.1 was used for data entry and management. Statistical analyses were conducted via SPSS version 26.0, with mediation and moderation analyses performed via the PROCESS macro (version 3.5). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the participants’ demographic characteristics. The normality of continuous variables was assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test. Independent samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA were used to examine univariate associations between demographic variables and the PI, while Pearson correlation analysis was employed to assess the relationships among the main study variables. Hierarchical regression analysis was performed to identify predictors of PI among male nurses. The mediating effect of WE on the relationship between WPB and PI was examined via Model 4 of the PROCESS macro with 5,000 bootstrap samples. The moderating effect of FNE on the WPB–PI relationship was tested via Model 8 of the PROCESS macro, which also included 5,000 bootstrap samples. Johnson–Neyman plots were generated to identify regions of statistical significance in the moderation effect. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05; results with p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Data distribution of the variables

The normality of continuous variables was assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test. The results revealed that WPB, PI, WE, and FNE were approximately normally distributed, with Shapiro–Wilk statistics of 0.993 (p = 0.089), 0.994 (p = 0.148), 0.994 (p = 0.132), and 0.994 (p = 0.115), respectively. As all p-values exceeded 0.05, the assumption of normality was satisfied, supporting the use of parametric tests in subsequent analyses.

Basic characteristics of the research subjects and univariate analysis

The study included 369 male nurses, with a mean age of 32.17 years (SD = 5.03). The average PI score was 102.73 (SD = 20.64). A comparison of PI scores across different demographic groups revealed that male nurses with a master’s degree or higher had significantly higher PI scores than did those with a bachelor’s degree or lower (p < 0.05). Male nurses holding positions such as head nurses or nursing team leaders had significantly higher PI scores than did those not in such positions (p < 0.05). Additionally, male nurses with a monthly income exceeding 8,000 CNY had significantly higher PI scores than those earning below this threshold (p < 0.05). Male nurses who slept 8 hours or more had significantly higher PI scores than did those with less than 8 hours of sleep (p < 0.05). Furthermore, male nurses who had not worked night shifts had significantly higher PI scores than did those who had worked night shifts (p < 0.05), as shown in Table 1.

Analysis of the mediating effect of work engagement on the relationship between workplace bullying and professional identity among male nurses

We assessed the mediating effect of WE on the relationship between WPB and PI among male nurses. A simple mediation analysis was conducted via Model 4 of the PROCESS macro, with WPB as the independent variable, PI as the dependent variable, and WE as the mediator. Bootstrapping with 5,000 iterations was used to estimate the indirect effect and its confidence intervals.

As shown in Table 5, WE significantly mediated the association between WPB and PI. The direct effect of WPB on PI was − 0.477 (95% CI: −0.693 to − 0.261), explaining 53.60% of the total effect. The indirect effect through WE was − 0.413 (95% CI: −0.573 to − 0.274), accounting for 46.40% of the total effect. Since both confidence intervals excluded zero, the mediation effect was confirmed to be statistically significant. Discussion

This study investigated the factors influencing the professional identity of male nurses and explored the relationship between workplace bullying and professional identity. Additionally, it examines the mediating role of work engagement in this relationship and further analyzes the moderating effect of the interaction between FNE and workplace bullying on both the mediating and direct paths.

Analysis of the current state of professional identity among male nurses

The PI score among the male nurses in this study was 102.73 (SD = 20.64), indicating a moderate level. This finding is consistent with that of Zhang et al. [102] but notably lower than the score of 123.03 (SD = 15.33) reported by Mu et al. [103] for male nurses with junior titles. One possible explanation lies in the age differences between the samples. The participants in Mu et al.‘s study were younger (M = 21.10, SD = 0.68), whereas the average age in our sample was 32.17 years (SD = 5.03). Younger nurses who enter clinical practice may gain hands-on experience rapidly, which can deepen their understanding of nursing and strengthen their PI [104]. In contrast, the participants in our study were older and mainly worked in departments such as emergency care, operating rooms, and intensive care units. More than half had over 10 years of clinical experience. Prolonged shift work, circadian rhythm disruption, and sustained physical and emotional demands may contribute to fatigue and diminish individuals’ sense of PI. Moreover, Mu et al.‘s research was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, a time when healthcare workers received substantial public recognition, which may have positively influenced nurses’ PI [105].

Wang et al. [106] reported an average PI score of 122.60 (SD = 18.80) among Chinese nurses, which was also higher than that reported in our study. One likely reason is the very low proportion of male nurses in their sample, accounting for only 1%. Nursing has traditionally been defined by traits such as empathy, obedience, and caregiving, which reflect a feminine occupational identity [107]. Within this framework, male nurses are often perceived as deviating from gendered expectations and may be marginalized within the profession [108]. Gender socialization further discourages men from identifying with caregiving roles, leading to role conflict and reduced professional belonging [109]. This misalignment between societal expectations and occupational identity may contribute to a lower level of PI among male nurses [110].

In China, these dynamics are further exacerbated by the significant gender imbalance within the nursing workforce, where male nurses constitute only a small minority [111]. They often lack same-gender peers, role models, and access to gender-sensitive professional development opportunities [112]. Moreover, male nurses are frequently assigned to high-intensity departments, such as the emergency department and intensive care units, where chronic shift work and heavy workloads can contribute to psychological fatigue and a diminished sense of PI [113]. Collectively, these factors—rooted in gender socialization, institutional structures, and occupational segregation—may help explain the lower PI scores observed among the male nurses in this study.

Evidence from Mauritius offers a useful contrast. There, men make up nearly half of the nursing workforce, and the profession is viewed as gender-inclusive and socially legitimate rather than female-dominated or low-status [114]. In contrast, male nurses in China constitute a small minority, often lacking male peers, role models, and supportive networks [111]. As Hollup [114] noted, favorable conditions in Mauritius—such as job security, stable income, and career mobility—enhance the appeal and legitimacy of nursing. This contrast highlights that the diminished PI observed among male nurses in China is not intrinsic to the profession itself but rather a consequence of local gendered labor norms, institutional structures, workplace conditions, and cultural expectations. This cross-cultural comparison indirectly supports the view that male nurses in China tend to experience lower levels of PI.

Analysis of the current state of workplace bullying among male nurses

Nurses are particularly vulnerable to WPB and other forms of aggression [115]. In the present study, male nurses reported a mean WPB score of 38.06 (SD = 8.28), which is comparable to the findings of Jiao et al. [116] (M = 38.72, SD = 12.30), but markedly higher than the score reported by Peng et al. [117] (M = 28.87, SD = 8.92). Although the number of male nurses in China has gradually increased, they remain a minority in a profession traditionally dominated by women. Gender role stereotypes often position male nurses as deviating from conventional masculine norms, thereby increasing their vulnerability to social prejudice and discrimination. These biases may foster feelings of ridicule, isolation, and loneliness [118, 119]. Salin (2021) [120] offers a gendered framework for understanding WPB, proposing that bullying can function as a group-based mechanism for reinforcing traditional gender norms. From this perspective, male nurses—by challenging the expected feminine attributes associated with nursing—may elicit subtle exclusion or even overt hostility from colleagues. Furthermore, they are often assigned physically demanding tasks in high-acuity departments, heightening their risk of exposure to workplace aggression. Their relatively low professional status, unsatisfactory income, and limited institutional support may further intensify feelings of marginalization and powerlessness [121].

Despite the valuable contributions that male nurses make to the profession, they frequently encounter significant psychosocial barriers rooted in gender-role conflict and workplace bias. As River and Flood [122] noted, men are typically socialized to suppress emotions such as sadness or vulnerability and to adopt stoic or aggressive emotional scripts aligned with hegemonic masculinity. This discord between masculine norms and emotional labor—such as nurturing, empathy, and emotional expressiveness—embedded in nursing practice can lead to internal conflict and hinder the development of a stable professional identity. Consequently, male nurses may be perceived as violating dominant gender expectations—an impression that not only exposes them to stigma and exclusion but also shapes how they perceive themselves within the profession, often leading to self-doubt and emotional strain.

These observations do not justify WPB. Rather, they provide important insights into the gendered social mechanisms underlying its occurrence.

Direct effects of workplace bullying on the professional identity and work engagement of male nurses

Correlation analysis revealed a significant negative association between WPB and PI. Hierarchical regression further confirmed that WPB significantly predicts PI, thereby supporting H1. These findings align with those of Yoo et al. [123]. According to the COR theory, WPB depletes individuals’ personal and social resources, which may reduce their PI. WPB has been shown to negatively affect physical and mental health, organizational climate, and interpersonal relationships. In milder cases, it may lead to burnout, reduced PI, and increased turnover intentions, while in more severe cases, it has been associated with increased psychiatric medication use and suicidal behaviors [124, 125].

Similarly, this study revealed a significant negative correlation between WPB and WE, thereby supporting H2. These results are consistent with previous findings by Hamel [126] and Javed [127]. Holm et al. [128], in a longitudinal study, further demonstrated that WPB negatively predicts nurses’ WE over time. Grounded in both COR theory and social exchange theory, nurses who experience bullying are likely to expend greater emotional and cognitive resources to cope with workplace adversity, thereby increasing psychological costs. Concurrently, they often receive less recognition or support, which diminishes their sense of accomplishment and job satisfaction. As perceived costs increase and rewards decline, these nurses may reduce their level of engagement to maintain a perceived balance between effort and return [129].

Direct effect of work engagement on professional identity

This study revealed a significant positive correlation between WE and PI, with hierarchical regression confirming WE as a key predictor, thus supporting H3. These findings align with prior research by Xing et al. [76], highlighting the crucial role of WE in shaping PI. Many scholars have argued that PI influences WE, with WE often being conceptualized as an outcome variable because individuals with stronger PI tend to invest more in their work [130–132]. Zhang et al. [133] demonstrated that occupational identity is a positive predictor of WE among healthcare practitioners in Chinese primary healthcare institutions. An Australian study by Rayner et al. [134] revealed that the PI of Grade 2 occupational therapists employed in public health inpatient settings positively predicts WE. The aforementioned studies clearly demonstrate the positive impact of PI on WE.

Building on prior studies, this study speculates that the relationship between PI and WE may be bidirectional. Not only does PI positively influence WE, but conversely, higher levels of WE increase the likelihood of success, thereby reinforcing PI. A study by Samuel et al. [135] suggested that PI is continuously reconstructed, evolving in response to individual work experiences and contextual factors. Additionally, the level of PI is closely related to an individual’s work experience [136]. For male nurses, becoming a competent clinical practitioner requires years of study and practice. Male nurses, who often serve on the front lines of clinical care, experience heavy workloads and low compensation. Their sense of PI tends to be moderately low. However, career inertia, influenced by both internal and external work environments, often compels them to invest more effort in their roles. According to reinforcement theory, individuals are likely to repeat behaviors when they perceive positive outcomes from those actions [137]. For male nurses, WE not only improves clinical nursing quality but also promotes academic performance in teaching and research. This professional fulfillment fosters a sense of achievement and positive emotional experiences, which gradually increase PI levels. In turn, heightened PI reinforces WE, creating a reciprocal loop that enhances occupational retention intentions. Given the considerable body of research on the impact of PI on WE, this study explores and validates the predictive role of WE on PI.

Mediating role of work engagement in the relationship between workplace bullying and professional identity

This study confirmed H4, showing that WE partially mediates the relationship between WPB and PI. The mediating effect accounted for 46.41% of the total effect.

To date, no empirical studies have reported similar findings. The asymmetrical power dynamics between workplace bullies and their targets create an imbalance in resource exchange. Perpetrators, leveraging their dominant hierarchical positions, primarily extract resources, whereas bullied individuals, occupying subordinate roles, experience progressive resource depletion due to sustained compliance demands [138]. COR theory posits that resources are crucial for individuals to cope with stress. Resource asymmetry often leads male nurses to experience burnout and emotional exhaustion when exposed to occupational stressors, thereby reducing their cognitive and emotional investment in work tasks. This diminished engagement results in a loss of professional fulfillment and hedonic satisfaction, undermining occupational identity, which may lead them to fall into a “loss spiral”. Furthermore, such cycles may trigger WPB, leading to secondary resource depletion and exacerbating a series of more severe negative effects [139].

From a biological perspective, the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis represents the body’s primary stress–response system and is essential for adaptation to both acute and chronic stressors. Dysregulation of this system, often caused by prolonged stress, leads to HPA hyperactivation and elevated cortisol levels [140]. This cascade induces structural and functional changes in the hippocampus, disrupts feedback regulation, and ultimately impairs cognitive, emotional, and executive functions [141]. Such neurophysiological impairments may reduce occupational performance and negatively influence professional attitudes.

WPB can be conceptualized as a form of social defeat. Experimental studies in male mice have shown that exposure to social defeat leads to reduced sucrose preference (anhedonia), decreased weight gain, and elevated corticosterone levels [142]. Parvopassu et al. [143] further showed that aberrant social interactions, such as bullying, disrupt the dopaminergic system, which governs reward processing, motivation, and emotional regulation. These disruptions diminish interest in work-related rewards, thereby lowering WE and worsening psychological well-being. Prolonged stress may also affect gray and white matter integrity, including orbitofrontal cortex hyperactivity or atrophy, which contributes to emotional dysregulation and impaired decision-making [47]. Nolfe et al. [144] reported that chronic occupational stress is associated with reduced hippocampal volume, potentially impairing the cognitive processes essential to PI formation among male nurses.

Therefore, nursing managers should promptly address WPB and also prioritize the psychological well-being of male nurses, particularly those whose work performance may decline following interpersonal conflicts. Timely organizational support and access to appropriate mental health resources could help preserve PI, promote workplace integration, and ultimately reduce the incidence of WPB.

Moderating effect of fear of negative evaluation on workplace bullying and professional identity

In recent years, increasing scholarly attention has been devoted to the interplay between individual traits and contextual factors in shaping psychological and behavioral outcomes. FNE, a relatively stable dispositional trait, reflects individuals’ persistent concern about being negatively judged in social contexts [145]. In the present study, male nurses reported a mean FNE score of 35.54 (SD = 9.62), which was higher than the score reported by Doğan et al. [146] (M = 31.92, SD = 8.51). This discrepancy may be attributed to the unique challenges faced by male nurses, including persistent public stereotypes and misalignment between gender expectations and professional roles, both of which may heighten their sensitivity to social evaluation [147]. The findings further demonstrated that FNE moderated both the direct relationship between WPB and PI and the initial path in the mediation model involving WE, thereby supporting H5. In other words, when FNE is at low to moderate levels, it attenuates the negative associations of WPB with WE and PI; however, at high levels, FNE no longer exerts this buffering effect and may instead exacerbate these adverse outcomes.

From an evolutionary psychology standpoint, this pattern highlights the adaptive role of FNE in human social development [148]. As fundamentally social creatures, early humans who were more attuned to signs of social disapproval were better positioned to avoid group exclusion—a factor with potentially life-threatening consequences in ancestral environments [149]. Therefore, a moderate level of FNE may serve as an adaptive mechanism shaped by evolutionary processes, motivating individuals to monitor and adjust their behavior to maintain social acceptance and protect themselves from relational aggression or bullying [150]. Empirical evidence indicates that, in contemporary workplace settings, individuals with moderate levels of FNE may be more sensitive to group norms and more proactive in employing conflict-avoidance strategies [151, 152]. This social sensitivity may reduce the impact of WPB on PI and WE by promoting adaptive responses [153].

However, when FNE becomes excessive, its protective function may deteriorate. Extremely high FNE may reflect hyperactivation of ancient threat-detection systems, leading to hypervigilance, avoidance, and emotional withdrawal [154]. Individuals with elevated FNE are more likely to perceive negative social cues as threats to their standing and may resort to avoidance, passive compliance, or superficial appeasement when faced with higher-status aggressors (i.e., bullies) [155, 156]. While such strategies may reduce immediate conflict, they undermine genuine social engagement and emotional connection with colleagues [157, 158]. Over time, emotional distancing and sustained psychological tension may gradually deplete personal resources, thereby weakening PI and diminishing WE [159, 160].

Therefore, in addressing WPB, nursing managers should also consider the varying levels of FNE among male nurses. Providing psychological support that targets evaluation-related anxiety may help individuals develop healthier coping mechanisms, foster a more inclusive work environment, and ultimately reduce WPB. These interventions can also improve nurse retention and contribute to greater workforce stability.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although validated instruments were used, the reliance on self-report questionnaires introduces the risk of common method bias and social desirability effects, especially given the sensitive nature of workplace bullying and professional identity among male nurses. Second, the study adopted a cross-sectional design, which limits the ability to infer causal relationships between workplace bullying, work engagement, fear of negative evaluation, and professional identity. Longitudinal or experimental designs are recommended for future studies to establish causal pathways and temporal ordering of these variables. Third, the sample was drawn exclusively from male nurses in selected hospitals in Southwest China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other cultural, institutional, or gender-diverse settings. Fourth, the study did not control for potentially influential contextual variables, such as organizational climate, managerial support, or unit-specific stressors, which could confound the observed relationships. Fifth, while the fear of negative evaluation was treated as a linear moderator, its effects on work engagement and professional identity may follow nonlinear patterns or interact with other psychological constructs, which warrants further investigation. Finally, although all scales in this study demonstrated high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α values exceeding 0.90, such high coefficients may reflect item redundancy rather than robust psychometric properties. When multiple items capture highly similar content, reliability estimates can become inflated without necessarily improving construct validity. Therefore, these coefficients should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, given the exploratory nature of this research and the theoretical coherence of the constructs and measurement goals, they are considered acceptable within the context of this study. Future studies are encouraged to examine the dimensional structure of each scale, explore the uniqueness of items, and provide a more nuanced assessment of scale reliability.

Conclusion

This study confirmed that workplace bullying and work engagement are independently associated with male nurses’ professional identity. The results of the mediation analysis indicated that workplace bullying may influence professional identity both directly and indirectly via work engagement, which itself is positively associated with professional identity. Additionally, fear of negative evaluation was found to moderate the relationships between workplace bullying and both work engagement and professional identity. Specifically, lower levels of fear of negative evaluation appeared to buffer the adverse effects of bullying. These findings highlight the importance of assessing and addressing male nurses’ levels of work engagement and fear of negative evaluation in organizational interventions. From an evolutionary psychology perspective, future efforts should focus on developing evidence-based, contextually appropriate strategies to reduce workplace bullying, foster supportive work environments, and promote the professional identity of male nurses. While the cross-sectional nature of the study limits causal interpretations, the results provide valuable insights for enhancing workforce diversity, improving nurse retention, and advancing the sustainable development of the nursing profession.
