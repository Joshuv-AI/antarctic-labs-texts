Interaction between parental preconception and prenatal smoking and alcohol use on autism-like behaviour in preschoolers: a cross-sectional study in Huizhou, China
Authors: Shuang Wu, Huiting Chen, Jinming Liu, Ruoqing Chen, Weiying Liu, Yulan Wu, Zizi Liu, Feixiang Zeng, Dongyan Wen, Jiarong Lin, Lvping Li, Yu Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the associations between preconception and prenatal smoking and alcohol consumption and the risk of autism-like behaviour in preschool children, with the aim of providing evidence for early prevention strategies.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 19,089 children aged 3–6 years from 111 kindergartens in Huicheng District, Huizhou City, Guangdong Province, China, between June 2022 and June 2023. Logistic regression was used to analyse the associations between parental smoking, drinking and autism-like behaviour in children. Multiplicative and additive interaction models were applied to evaluate the interaction between smoking and drinking on autism-like behaviour in children.ResultsCompared with the unexposed group, children with maternal prenatal passive smoking exposure (OR = 1.327, 95% CI: 1.042 to 1.690) and those with a paternal history of smoking (OR = 1.643, 95% CI: 1.036 to 2.605) exhibited significantly higher odds of autism-like behaviour. Among mothers with a prepregnancy smoking history, a significant negative additive interaction was observed between smoking cessation during pregnancy and concurrent alcohol consumption (RERI = −2.170, 95% CI: −4.035 to −0.304).ConclusionsThis study identifies prenatal exposure to parental smoking—both maternal passive smoking and paternal smoking history—as a significant risk factor for autism-like behaviour in preschoolers. The finding of a negative additive interaction between maternal smoking cessation and concurrent alcohol consumption underscores the complexity of modifying health behaviours. These findings emphasise the key role of reducing parental tobacco and alcohol exposure in the primary prevention of autism spectrum disorder.Keywords: Children, Child Health, Child Psychiatry, Cross-Sectional Studies, Epidemiology

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a common neurodevelopmental disorder characterised by deficits in social communication and restricted, repetitive behaviours.1 The global health burden of ASD is substantial and growing, with an estimated worldwide prevalence of approximately 1%.2 Prevalence varies across regions, ranging from 0.7% among school-aged children in China3 to 27.6 per 1000 in the USA.4 Although genetic predispositions play a central role, environmental factors, including preconception and prenatal exposures, are estimated to account for 40%–50% of the liability to ASD,5 highlighting the preconception and prenatal periods as critical windows for prevention. During these periods, exposure to harmful substances, notably alcohol and tobacco, may adversely affect foetal brain development and increase the risk of ASD.6

Although both alcohol exposure and tobacco exposure have been associated with ASD risk, the latter has been more extensively studied. Evidence from a population-based cohort study suggested that maternal heavy smoking is associated with an increased risk of ASD in children, whereas the association for light smoking on ASD is weaker.7 A previous study has demonstrated that maternal smoking during pregnancy contributes to significant reductions in grey matter volumes in multiple brain regions.8 Additionally, paternal smoking has also been identified as a risk factor for ASD. According to the research conducted by Kim et al,9 paternal prenatal smoking accounted for 14.4% and 11.1% of the attributable risk for ASD in two independent samples, respectively. The underlying cause may be the effects of cigarette chemicals on sperm.10 Moreover, paternal prenatal smoking can lead to maternal passive smoking, which can induce foetal brain hypoxia or damage in brain nerves,11 thereby affecting foetal brain development and increasing the risk of ASD. However, not all studies have found consistent results, as a study using serum cotinine measurements reported no significant association between maternal prenatal tobacco smoke exposure and ASD risk in the offspring.12 Furthermore, most studies have not investigated prepregnancy and prenatal tobacco exposure separately. In particular, evidence regarding the association between paternal smoking during the prepregnancy period and ASD risk is scarce.

Similarly, prenatal alcohol exposure represents another prevalent and modifiable environmental factor, yet its specific role in ASD aetiology remains ambiguous. As an established teratogen, prenatal alcohol exposure may disrupt foetal development, which is especially harmful to the development of the nervous system.13 Maternal prenatal alcohol exposure adversely affects offspring’s behavioural and emotional outcomes.14 Moreover, paternal alcohol exposure before pregnancy may also increase the risk of behavioural problems in children through epigenetic modification in sperm. Alcohol-dependent fathers may pass on a genetic susceptibility to offspring, thereby exacerbating their behavioural problems.15 Despite these neurodevelopmental risks, the specific role of alcohol in the aetiology of ASD remains unclear. Recent evidence from a large multisite case-control study indicates that first-trimester prenatal alcohol exposure is associated with more severe social communication and interaction deficits among preschool-aged children with an ASD diagnosis.16 However, other studies found no significant association between maternal alcohol consumption before or during pregnancy and ASD risk in offspring.10 17 Overall, the epidemiological evidence linking prenatal alcohol exposure to ASD remains limited and inconsistent. Moreover, to our knowledge, no studies have specifically focused on the association between paternal drinking before pregnancy and the risk of ASD.

Crucially, tobacco and alcohol exposure often co-occur and should be jointly considered,18 yet their potential interaction regarding the risk of ASD remains entirely unexplored. While one case-control study assessed their synergistic effect on congenital heart disease, no significant interaction was found .19 Notably, this study focused on a physical disease outcome, rather than considering specific neurodevelopmental outcomes such as ASD.

Investigating the potential interactive effects of parental tobacco and alcohol exposure on autism-like behaviour among preschool-aged children is particularly crucial, as the preschool period represents a critical period for both brain maturation and the optimal window for early identification and intervention of ASD. Therefore, this study aims to examine the independent and interactive effects of parental preconception and prenatal smoking and alcohol use on autism-like behaviour in preschoolers. These findings may provide evidence for multifactorial prevention strategies targeting modifiable parental risk factors during critical windows of neurodevelopment.

Methods

Setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted in Huicheng District, the core urban district of Huizhou City, Guangdong Province, China. Guangdong is the most economically developed province in China, and Huizhou is a city within the Pearl River Delta (PRD) that has undergone rapid urbanisation.

Participants

A total of 20,356 children aged 3–6 years were recruited from 111 kindergartens in Huicheng District between June 2022 and June 2023. Participants were recruited using a cluster sampling method. Several administrative subdistricts were randomly selected. All kindergartens within the selected subdistricts were included, and all eligible children in those kindergartens were invited to participate. The inclusion criteria included (1) children aged 3–6 years and (2) informed consent obtained from parents or guardians. The exclusion criteria included (1) questionnaires with missing data in any key variable, such as demographic information or scores on the core assessment scale; (2) suffering from diagnosed developmental or psychiatric disorders other than ASD, such as attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, intellectual disability or anxiety disorders; (3) suffering from severe diseases of vital organs such as heart, liver and brain; and (4) parents or guardians refusing to complete the questionnaire. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 19,089 children were included in the final analysis. Measures

Basic information about the children and their parents was collected using self-compiled questionnaires. Specifically, the child’s information included sex, age, ethnicity, height, weight and birth outcomes, such as gestational age and birth weight. Parental information included their age at delivery, education level, marital relationship and health-related behaviours, such as smoking and alcohol consumption before and during pregnancy.

The Chinese version of the Clancy Autism Behaviour Scale (CABS) was used to measure autism-like behaviour.20 It consists of 14 items and the frequency of behaviour was rated on a three-point scale (0 = never, 1 = occasionally and 2 = often). In this study, a score of 14 or higher was used to identify children as exhibiting autism-like behaviour. The sensitivity of the Chinese CABS was 58%, the specificity was 84%, the positive predictive value (PPV) was 65%, and the negative predictive value (NPV) was 79%.20 The CABS had acceptable screening accuracy.

Procedure

This study recruited participants from 111 kindergartens in Huicheng District, Huizhou City. The data collection was carried out through an online survey, administered by trained researchers. To ensure consistency, all researchers involved in data collection received training on the study protocol, including how to administer the instruments and address any questions from participants. The participant’s responses were voluntary and anonymous.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in R V.4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the car (V.3.1.3), interactionR (V.0.1.7) and openxlsx (V.4.2.8) packages. Measurement data with normal distribution were presented as mean ± SD, and the comparison between groups was performed by an independent-samples t-test. The counting data were presented as percentages (%), and the comparison between groups was performed by the χ2 test. Smoking and drinking were considered as independent variables, and autism-like behaviour was considered as the dependent variable. To adjust for potential confounding, the following covariates were included in our analyses: child’s sex, child’s gestational age, single or multiple births, child’s birth weight and parental age at delivery. Logistic regression models were performed after adjusting for confounding factors. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Multiplicative and additive interactions were estimated between parental smoking and drinking on autism-like behaviour. Multiplicative interaction was tested using the Wald test. A p-value of the product term less than 0.05 indicated a multiplicative interaction between smoking and drinking. Additive interaction, which assesses whether the combined effect of two exposures on the absolute risk differs from the sum of their individual effects, was quantified using the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI). Given the cross-sectional design of our study, we calculated the RERI using ORs derived from logistic regression models as a proxy for relative risks, in accordance with established methodological guidance.21 22 An RERI of 0 indicates no additive interaction; an RERI greater than 0 suggests a positive additive interaction, and an RERI less than 0 suggests a negative additive interaction. When the 95% CI includes 0, the additive interaction is not statistically significant.

Multicollinearity analysis

To ensure the robustness of our findings, we conducted a multicollinearity diagnosis using variance inflation factors (VIF) among the independent variables. The VIF values for all variables ranged from 1.009 to 5.089: maternal active smoking before pregnancy (VIF = 2.553), maternal passive smoking before pregnancy (VIF = 5.089), maternal drinking before pregnancy (VIF = 1.108), maternal active smoking during pregnancy (VIF = 2.525), maternal passive smoking during pregnancy (VIF = 4.998), maternal drinking during pregnancy (VIF = 1.033), paternal smoking before pregnancy (VIF = 4.892), paternal drinking before pregnancy (VIF = 1.214), paternal smoking during pregnancy (VIF = 4.908), child sex (VIF = 1.009), gestational age (VIF = 1.166), single or multiple births (VIF = 1.043), birth weight (VIF = 1.144), paternal age at delivery (VIF = 2.222) and maternal age at delivery (VIF = 2.233). The highest VIF (5.089), for maternal passive smoking before pregnancy, indicated moderate collinearity but remained below the common threshold of 10. Given its theoretical importance as a key exposure variable, it was retained in the final model along with all others. In conclusion, multicollinearity was effectively controlled and does not exert a substantial influence on the analytical outcomes of the overall model.

Interactions between smoking and alcohol use on autism-like behaviour

As presented in table 4, after adjusting for the child’s sex, child’s gestational age, single or multiple births, child’s birth weight and parental age at delivery, a significant negative additive interaction was observed between maternal smoking cessation during pregnancy and drinking during pregnancy among mothers who smoked before pregnancy (RERI = −2.170, 95% CI: −4.035 to −0.304). There was no multiplicative interaction between these two exposures (p > 0.05). Furthermore, paternal smoking and drinking before and during the mother’s pregnancy were not significantly associated with autism-like behaviour in preschool children (all p > 0.05).

Discussion

Tobacco and alcohol use during pregnancy are well-documented risk factors for foetal developmental disorders and other adverse health outcomes.23 Therefore, this study investigated 19,089 preschool children from the Huizhou ASD Screening Programme to explore the effects of smoking and drinking on autism-like behaviour. This was the first study to focus on parental preconception and prenatal smoking and drinking and to examine their interaction. The results indicated that maternal passive smoking during pregnancy and paternal smoking history before pregnancy were associated with higher odds of autism-like behaviour in preschool children. In addition, a significant interaction was observed between maternal smoking cessation and drinking during pregnancy with respect to the risk of autism-like behaviour in preschool children.

Comparisons with previous studies

Our study showed that maternal passive smoking during pregnancy was associated with autism-like behaviour in preschool children, a finding consistent with an Egyptian case-control study24 and the Longhua Child Cohort Study.25 This association is biologically plausible, as nicotine and other toxicants in secondhand smoke may disrupt foetal neurodevelopment through hypoxia and altered neuronal nicotinic acetylcholine receptor signalling.26 However, there is no consensus on the relationship between maternal prenatal tobacco smoke exposure and ASD in offspring. A case-control study reported no association when considering both maternal active and passive smoking.12 Consistent with this, Caramaschi et al27 found weak evidence for a causal link between maternal smoking during pregnancy and ASD diagnosis or autism-related traits. They concluded that previously reported associations might be related to residual confounding by socioeconomic or familial factors. Our study found no evidence that maternal prenatal active smoking was linked to autism-like behaviour in offspring. This may be due to high rates of smoking cessation early in pregnancy, the low prevalence of smoking among women, which limits statistical power and potential under-reporting driven by social desirability bias.28

Most of the previous studies have focused on the effect of mothers’ unhealthy lifestyle habits on children with ASD, while relatively little is known about fathers’ unhealthy lifestyle habits. We found that a paternal history of smoking (even if quit during pregnancy) was associated with autism-like behaviour. The harmful chemicals contained in tobacco alter neurotransmitter activity in the offspring’s developing brain.28 Animal studies showed paternal nicotine exposure led to cognitive deficits and behavioural problems in their offspring, which were potentially related to epigenetic variations in key genes in the father’s sperm.29 30 Notably, nicotine-induced DNA methylation changes at dopamine receptor gene promoters may remain stable and persist even after paternal smoking cessation.29 However, the association between paternal smoking and ASD in offspring remains controversial. In a case-control study conducted in Brazil, Costa et al10 reported no significant association between paternal smoking before or during pregnancy and ASD. We did not observe a direct association between paternal smoking during pregnancy and autism-like behaviour in offspring. This suggests that the potential influence of paternal smoking may operate indirectly, possibly through maternal exposure to secondhand smoke.9

Prenatal alcohol exposure is linked to neurodevelopmental damage.31 Gestational alcohol exposure acts as an early life stressor, leading to significant disruptions in the embryonic nervous system through epigenetic mechanisms.32 However, we observed no evidence that maternal prenatal drinking or paternal preconception drinking is associated with autism-like behaviour in preschool children. This finding is consistent with a previous retrospective cohort study reporting no link between maternal prenatal alcohol consumption and ASD risk.33 The lack of association may reflect limited statistical power due to low self-reported drinking, which was likely influenced by underreporting or social desirability bias, as alcohol use during pregnancy is often concealed. Evidence on the role of paternal drinking in ASD aetiology remains limited. Any effect of paternal drinking would likely operate through preconception epigenetic alterations in sperm, but the threshold and persistence of such alterations remain unclear.34

To our knowledge, the negative additive interaction between maternal smoking cessation during pregnancy and concurrent alcohol consumption has not been previously reported in the literature. Specifically, the combined association of these exposures with autism-like behaviour is less than expected under additivity, meaning that the two exposures may dampen each other's effect rather than amplify it. This finding may reflect complex biological mechanisms. Transient ethanol exposure in immature rodents during postnatal day 7, a developmental stage comparable to the third trimester of human pregnancy, induces widespread activation of caspase-3 (a marker of neurodegeneration), which in turn mediates the reduction of two key methyltransferase proteins, DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) and DNA methyltransferase 3A (DNMT3A), and thereby impairs DNA methylation.35 Similarly, animal models of developmental nicotine exposure, which model maternal smoking during pregnancy, show sustained downregulation of key epigenetic regulators in the frontal cortex and hippocampus, including DNMT3A, methyl-CpG-binding protein 2 (MeCP2) and histone deacetylase 2 (HDAC2).36 These findings indicate that both prenatal alcohol and tobacco exposure can disrupt the DNA methylation programme during brain development, suggesting that their effects may converge on shared epigenetic pathways.37 The shared epigenetic targets of prenatal alcohol and maternal tobacco exposure may contribute to the negative additive interaction observed between maternal smoking cessation during pregnancy and alcohol consumption, as the neurodevelopmental impact of alcohol could be modulated by the history of tobacco exposure.

Strengths, limitations and future directions

There are several strengths in this study. First, the large sample size supported a thorough investigation of the association between prenatal parental smoking and alcohol use on autism-like behaviour. Second, the comprehensive range of relevant baseline variables allowed for extensive adjustment of potential confounders. However, some limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the association between parental smoking, drinking and autism-like behaviour in offspring. Second, the use of self-report questionnaires introduces the potential for recall bias and social desirability bias; furthermore, the lack of detailed information on tobacco and alcohol consumption, including amount used, frequency of consumption and consumption levels, hindered further analysis of consumption patterns. Future studies should incorporate standardised quantity-frequency measures to enable more detailed examination of tobacco and alcohol use patterns. Third, the generalisability of our findings is limited, as our sample was drawn solely from Huizhou City, Guangdong Province. Previous studies have documented substantial regional variation in exposure to tobacco and alcohol use across China.38 Nevertheless, Huizhou is situated within the PRD, a socioeconomically pivotal region that has undergone massive urban growth and is widely regarded as a microcosm of China’s rapid urbanisation.39 Therefore, our findings may provide valuable insights for other regions in China undergoing similar urbanisation processes. Future multicentre studies across diverse regions are warranted to confirm and extend our findings.

Conclusions

In summary, this study has identified maternal passive smoking and a history of paternal smoking (even if quit during pregnancy) as risk factors for autism-like behaviour in preschool children. A negative additive interaction was observed between maternal smoking cessation during pregnancy and concurrent alcohol consumption. However, no evidence was found to suggest that prenatal parental drinking independently influences autism-like behaviour. By identifying parental smoking as a major risk factor and elucidating its interaction with alcohol, these findings collectively establish the reduction of parental tobacco and alcohol exposure as a key target for the primary prevention of autism-like behaviour. This contributes to the foundation for more evidence-based and comprehensive strategies to mitigate ASD risk.
