Personalized machine learning prediction of next‐day migraine persistence using digital headache diary data
Authors: Ya‐Hsiu Tsai, Chen‐Lin Chiou, Jun‐Jun Lee, Chun‐Ming Yang, Kao‐Chang Lin, Tain‐Junn Cheng, Ja‐Hwung Su, Cheng‐Hsin Chuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives/BackgroundMigraine is a disabling neurological disorder with substantial interindividual variability. Predicting whether an ongoing migraine attack will persist into the following day remains challenging. We evaluated the feasibility and predictive performance of personalized machine learning models for predicting next‐day migraine persistence using longitudinal digital headache diary data.MethodsThis prospective longitudinal cohort study was designed for prognostic prediction model development. Participants were recruited from two medical centers in Taiwan between February 2023 and October 2023, and each completed a 3‐month digital headache diary observation period. Patients with 5–14 monthly headache days were included. Same‐day diary records from index migraine days were used to predict next‐day migraine persistence. Personalized and generalized models were developed using k‐nearest neighbors (KNN), support vector machine, random forest, and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). Model performance was evaluated using discrimination, calibration, and sensitivity analyses.ResultsA total of 25 patients were included. Compared with the generalized KNN model, the personalized KNN model achieved significantly higher area under the curve (AUC) (0.83 ± 0.09 vs. 0.63 ± 0.04; ΔAUC = 0.20). Among personalized models, KNN also outperformed support vector machine, random forest, and eXtreme gradient boosting, with ΔAUC values of 0.23, 0.15, and 0.16, respectively; the corresponding Holm‐adjusted p‐values were <0.001, 0.003, and <0.001. The personalized KNN model showed the lowest Brier score (0.13 ± 0.04), with a calibration intercept of 0.08 and calibration slope of 0.80. Ablation analysis indicated that the KNN model performance was most sensitive to the removal of pain intensity, menstrual cycle status, perceived medication effectiveness, and pain location, with pain intensity showing the largest effect.ConclusionPersonalized machine learning models based on digital headache diary data may feasibly predict next‐day persistence of ongoing migraine. Larger external validation studies are needed to confirm model generalizability and clinical applicability.

INTRODUCTION

Migraine affects 12%–20% of the global population, with peak prevalence among adults aged 25–55 years, and is nearly three times more common in women than in men. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 It causes substantial pain and impairs work productivity and quality of life. Because migraine is highly heterogeneous, with marked interindividual differences in triggers, prodromal symptoms, attack frequency, and treatment responses, prediction models that account for patient‐specific temporal patterns may be needed; however, individualized migraine forecasting remains underexplored.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) now enable adaptive analysis of complex health data to improve prediction. 5 , 6 , 7 Previous studies have shown the value of machine learning in migraine research, particularly for headache classification, medication overuse risk prediction, trigger analysis, and migraine forecasting. 8 Several models have achieved high diagnostic accuracy using questionnaire‐based or public data sets. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 For example, k‐nearest neighbors (KNN), support vector machine (SVM), random forest, and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)‐based models have been applied to classify migraine and other headache disorders, with reported accuracies ranging from 82% to 99%. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Machine learning has also been used to predict medication‐overuse headache, with one SVM‐based model reporting a sensitivity of 0.69 and specificity of 0.87; 14 however, migraine forecasting remains relatively underexplored. Stubberud et al. used mobile diary and wearable sensor data from 18 patients over 4 weeks, and their random forest model showed modest predictive performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.62. 15 Most prior studies relied on cross‐sectional data, population‐level models, or single‐time assessments, which may not capture individual migraine patterns over time. Digital headache diaries provide repeated patient‐reported data and may better support individualized prediction. Although mobile diary and wearable‐based studies have shown that migraine forecasting is feasible, few studies have specifically examined next‐day persistence of an ongoing migraine attack.

To address this gap, we developed personalized machine learning models using longitudinal digital headache diary data from the iHeaDiary application. The prediction target was next‐day migraine persistence, defined as whether a migraine present on day t persisted into day t + 1. We hypothesized that personalized models based on individual‐level diary data would outperform generalized models trained on pooled population‐level data.

METHODS

Study design and participants

This prospective longitudinal cohort study was conducted to develop and evaluate prognostic machine learning models for predicting next‐day migraine persistence. The prediction target was whether a migraine present on day t persisted into day t + 1. Participants were recruited from two medical centers in Taiwan between February 2023 and October 2023 and were followed for 3 months using a digital headache diary. Eligible participants were aged 20–65 years, had a clinical diagnosis of migraine according to International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition criteria, and were able to use a mobile application. 16 , 17 Participants with 5–14 headache days per month were included in the final analysis to focus on episodic migraine, exclude chronic migraine, and ensure sufficient outcome events for model development. 18 Participants with psychiatric comorbidities were excluded. No a priori sample size calculation was performed; the sample size was determined by the number of eligible participants during the recruitment period. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards of Chi Mei Medical Center (approval number 11101–005) and Chang Gung Medical Foundation, Taiwan (approval number 202300657B0C101). All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Data source and digital headache diary

Data were collected using iHeaDiary, a progressive web application developed by the study team in collaboration with neurologists and based on International Headache Society headache diary recommendations. The application captured daily patient‐reported diary data, including time of onset, headache duration, pain intensity, pain location, associated symptoms, aura, medication use, perceived medication effectiveness, and menstrual cycle status. Participants completed the Migraine Disability Assessment at baseline. 19 , 20 At study completion, iHeaDiary generated individualized visual reports summarizing each participant's headache patterns. Prediction target

The prediction target was next‐day migraine persistence. A migraine day was defined as a calendar day with a diary‐recorded migraine episode. Only migraine days on day t were used as index days. The binary outcome was coded as 1 if the migraine episode on day t continued beyond midnight or if persistence was recorded on day t + 1; otherwise, it was coded as 0. Days without diary entries were not assumed to be nonmigraine days. Day pairs with missing index‐day records or unavailable next‐day outcome information were excluded from model development and evaluation. Thus, the model predicted persistence of an existing migraine episode rather than new migraine onset.

Predictors

Nine diary‐derived predictors were used for model development: time of onset, headache duration, pain intensity, pain location, associated symptoms, aura, medication use, perceived medication effectiveness, and menstrual cycle status. Variables were converted into categorical formats before modeling, as summarized in Table S1. Categorical predictors were encoded using one‐hot encoding.

Data processing and partitioning

Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range [IQR]), as appropriate, according to data distribution assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Monthly headache frequency was reported as median (IQR) because of its skewed distribution, whereas pain intensity was reported as mean ± SD. Data were checked for missing values and outliers before model development. The final analytic data set had no missing values in the nine model predictors or prediction outcome; therefore, no imputation was performed. Diary records were partitioned chronologically to preserve temporal ordering and reduce information leakage. For generalized models, records were first split chronologically within each participant using an 80:20 ratio, and the training records were then pooled for model training. For personalized models, records were split chronologically within each participant using the same ratio. The Synthetic Minority Over‐Sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied only to the training partition in both modeling approaches, and no synthetic samples were generated from test data. 21 , 22 , 23

Model development

Four supervised machine learning algorithms were evaluated: KNN, SVM, random forest, and XGBoost. For generalized models, diary records were chronologically split within each participant using an 80:20 ratio; training records were pooled to train one model per algorithm, whereas held‐out test records were evaluated separately at the patient level. For personalized models, records were split using the same chronological 80:20 ratio, and one participant‐specific model was trained and tested for each participant. SMOTE was applied only to training data in both strategies, with no oversampling of test data.

Hyperparameter selection used training data only. For KNN, candidate values of k = 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 were compared using training‐set cross‐validation AUC, and k = 3 was selected before held‐out testing. SVM used a polynomial kernel with C = 1 and degree = 3; random forest used 300 trees, maximum depth = 5, and minimum samples per leaf = 5; and XGBoost used 200 estimators, learning rate = 0.05, maximum depth = 3, subsample = 0.8, colsample_bytree = 0.8, and log‐loss as the evaluation metric. Model evaluation

Model performance was assessed using accuracy, recall, precision, F1 score, specificity, and AUC. Recall and AUC were prioritized because the clinical objective was to identify next‐day persistence of an ongoing migraine. Receiver operating characteristic curves and AUCs were generated from held‐out test‐set predicted probabilities. Metrics were reported to two decimal places and summarized as mean ± SD across patient‐level estimates. The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for mean metrics were estimated using 10,000 patient‐level bootstrap resamples and are provided in Table S3. AUC differences were reported as ΔAUC. For generalized versus personalized SMOTE models, ΔAUC was defined as personalized SMOTE AUC minus generalized SMOTE AUC. The 95% CIs for ΔAUC were estimated using 1000 nonparametric bootstrap resamples. Paired comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed rank test with Holm adjustment for multiple comparisons. A two‐sided p‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Calibration was evaluated using the Brier score, calibration intercept, and calibration slope. Calibration intercept and slope were estimated from pooled held‐out predictions using a logistic calibration model, with the observed binary outcome regressed on the logit‐transformed predicted probability. Because held‐out observations per participant were limited, calibration plots were generated by pooling held‐out predictions across individualized test sets. Calibration analyses were interpreted as exploratory.

Feature sensitivity analysis

Feature sensitivity was assessed after model evaluation. For the model with the most favorable overall performance, ablation analysis was performed by sequentially removing each predictor and recalculating AUC. The resulting AUC decrease was reported as ΔAUC, with larger decreases indicating greater model sensitivity. Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) analysis was performed for the random forest model as a complementary interpretability analysis, with predictors ranked by mean absolute SHAP values. These analyses were interpreted as model‐based sensitivity and interpretability assessments, not as evidence of causal effects.

Software

All analyses were conducted using Python version 3.12 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE,) within the Anaconda environment. Data management and descriptive analyses were performed using Pandas. Machine learning models were developed using scikit‐learn, imbalanced‐learn, and XGBoost. The main package versions were NumPy 1.26.3, SciPy 1.12.0, scikit‐learn 1.4.0, imbalanced‐learn 0.11.0, and XGBoost 2.0.3.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics and 3‐month headache diary patterns

Of 135 recruited patients, 25 met the predefined analytic criterion of 5–14 monthly headache days and were included in the final episodic migraine cohort. The final analytic data set contained no missing values in the nine model predictors or prediction outcome; therefore, no imputation was performed. During the 3‐month observation period, patients contributed 593 migraine days. After excluding 3 terminal days outside the next‐day outcome window, 590 eligible index migraine‐day pairs were analyzed; 273 (46.3%) were classified as next‐day migraine persistence and 317 (53.7%) as nonpersistence. Among the 25 patients, 20 were female (80%), and the most common age group was 41–50 years (13/25, 52%). Thirteen patients (52%) had a bachelor's degree, and eight (32%) had a high school education or below. Migraine Disability Assessment grades indicated moderate or severe disability in 16 patients (64%). Most patients were employed (22/25, 88%), and four (16%) were first‐visit patients (Table 1).

Feature sensitivity of the personalized KNN model

Ablation analysis of the personalized KNN model showed the largest mean AUC decrease after removal of pain intensity (0.19), followed by menstrual cycle status, perceived medication effectiveness, and pain location (0.14 each). Removal of other predictors produced smaller AUC decreases (Figure 6A). Complementary SHAP analysis of the personalized random forest model showed the highest mean absolute SHAP values for pain intensity, perceived medication effectiveness, pain location, and associated symptoms, whereas aura and headache duration had the lowest values (Figure 6B).

DISCUSSION

Principal findings

This proof‐of‐concept study demonstrated the feasibility of using personalized machine learning models based on longitudinal digital headache diary data to predict next‐day persistence of ongoing migraine in patients with 5–14 monthly headache days. Using a chronological train–test split, the personalized KNN model demonstrated the most favorable overall performance and outperformed the generalized KNN model in patient‐level AUC comparisons. These findings suggest that individualized diary‐based models may capture within‐person symptom patterns relevant to short‐term migraine persistence, consistent with prior diary‐based migraine forecasting and personalized headache care studies. 15 , 24 , 25

Personalized modeling for next‐day migraine persistence

The relatively favorable performance of KNN may reflect the small, individualized structure of the data set. As a nonparametric, distance‐based algorithm, KNN classifies observations according to local similarity and may therefore capture recurrent within‐person symptom patterns in patient‐specific diary data. In contrast, tree‐based and boosting algorithms may require larger and more heterogeneous data sets to estimate stable decision structures. This interpretation should be considered exploratory.

Although prior migraine forecasting studies have combined mobile diary data with wearable‐derived physiological signals, 15 our findings suggest that self‐reported digital diary data may provide a feasible, low‐burden basis for personalized prediction of next‐day migraine persistence. This diary‐only approach may be useful for monitoring attack continuity in routine care or digital health settings.

Recent studies have shown the growing role of artificial intelligence in personalized headache management. Katsuki et al. 24 reported that AI‐based diagnostic models may improve diagnostic accuracy and agreement among nonspecialists, whereas Romozzi et al. 25 showed that machine learning models using early clinical data may help predict response to anti‐calcitonin gene‐related peptide (anti‐CGRP) monoclonal antibodies. In this context, the present findings suggest that personalized diary‐based modeling may complement existing AI applications in individualized headache care by focusing on short‐term prediction of migraine persistence rather than diagnosis or treatment‐response prediction.

Calibration and feature sensitivity

Calibration was assessed because AUC evaluates discrimination but not probability accuracy. Among personalized models, KNN had the lowest Brier score, indicating the lowest overall probabilistic prediction error among the evaluated personalized models. However, its calibration slope remained below 1, suggesting imperfect calibration and the need for recalibration or further validation before clinical implementation.

Feature sensitivity analysis showed that removing pain intensity, menstrual cycle status, perceived medication effectiveness, and pain location produced the largest decreases in personalized KNN performance. These findings should be interpreted as model‐based sensitivity rather than causal, independent, or generalizable feature importance. Complementary random forest SHAP analysis showed partially overlapping results, with higher mean absolute SHAP values for pain intensity, perceived medication effectiveness, pain location, and associated symptoms.

The contribution of menstrual cycle status is consistent with prior studies linking menstruation to migraine frequency and clinical features. Menstrual migraine has been reported to last longer, involve more severe associated symptoms, including photophobia, phonophobia, and nausea, and have a lower incidence of aura. 26 , 27 , 28 These findings support the inclusion of multidimensional diary features in individualized models for predicting migraine persistence.

Clinical relevance of predicting next‐day migraine persistence

Prediction of next‐day migraine persistence may provide clinically relevant information by distinguishing persistence of an existing attack from new migraine onset. This distinction is important because persistence may reflect a more prolonged attack course and a higher short‐term individual migraine burden. Although the present model does not directly guide drug selection, estimating the likelihood of next‐day persistence may help contextualize anticipatory guidance and individualized acute‐treatment planning. Recent real‐world evidence has shown heterogeneity in acute migraine treatment patterns. Katsuki et al. 29 used nationwide claims data and time‐series clustering to examine long‐term lasmiditan prescribing patterns in Japan, highlighting variability in clinical practice and the need to better understand optimal acute treatment use. Beyond medication‐related decisions, next‐day persistence prediction may help characterize short‐term attack burden at the individual level, particularly in patients with recurrent or prolonged migraine patterns.

LIMITATIONS

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, model performance was evaluated using internal validation within the current cohort. Although chronological train–test splitting was used to reduce temporal information leakage, the modest sample size and limited number of held‐out observations per participant may have affected the stability and precision of performance estimates. External validation in larger and more diverse cohorts is therefore warranted. The restriction of the analytic cohort to patients with 5–14 headache days per month enhanced clinical homogeneity but may limit applicability to patients with lower‐frequency episodic migraine or chronic migraine. Second, the predictors were limited to diary‐derived variables. Important migraine triggers and modulators, including sleep quality, acute stress, body mass index, physical activity, diet, caffeine intake, and environmental factors, were not available. This may have limited the models' ability to capture the full complexity of individual migraine dynamics. Third, SMOTE was applied only to the training partition to avoid test‐set leakage. Nevertheless, because SMOTE treats observations as independent, it may attenuate or distort temporal dependencies in longitudinal diary data. Accordingly, SMOTE‐based results should be interpreted cautiously. Future studies should examine alternative strategies for class imbalance, such as cost‐sensitive learning, class‐weighted algorithms, and threshold optimization based on precision–recall performance. Fourth, some continuous or ordinal variables, including pain intensity and headache duration, were categorized before model training. This preprocessing approach may have reduced information granularity and constrained the models' ability to identify individualized predictive thresholds. Future studies should evaluate approaches that preserve continuous or ordinal data structures and incorporate multimodal data sources, including wearable‐derived physiological signals, contextual factors, and environmental exposures.

CONCLUSION

This proof‐of‐concept study supports the feasibility of using personalized machine learning models based on digital headache diary data to predict next‐day persistence of ongoing migraine. In this cohort, the personalized KNN model demonstrated the most favorable overall performance and achieved a higher AUC than its generalized counterpart, suggesting the potential value of individualized prediction. Ablation analysis showed that KNN model performance was most sensitive to the removal of pain intensity, followed by menstrual cycle status, perceived medication effectiveness, and pain location. These findings suggest that routinely collected diary variables may provide clinically relevant information for short‐term migraine persistence prediction. Larger external validation studies, further calibration and potential recalibration, and prospective evaluation of clinical utility are required before clinical implementation.
