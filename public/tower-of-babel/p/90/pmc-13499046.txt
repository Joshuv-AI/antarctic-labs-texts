Clinical research is not an optional extra
Authors: Tom Solomon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClinical research underpins almost everything we do in the NHS, from diagnostics and therapeutics to service design and workforce development, while also driving economic growth through the life sciences sector. Yet the UK clinical research workforce is in decline, at a time of rising patient need. Financial pressures on universities, increasing clinical demand, fragile funding routes and inflexible training pathways, are making academic careers harder to enter and sustain. Valuable research delivered by resident doctors, consultants, SAS and locally employed doctors is also unrecognised and unsupported. Research should not be seen as an optional extra. It is mandated in policy, embedded in professional standards, and essential to improving outcomes. A whole workforce approach is needed. At the Royal College of Physicians, we are supporting doctors making the case for research in job planning and training, strengthening recognition and funding opportunities for early clinical research, and addressing structural barriers to academic careers through national policy engagement.

When I was a medical student in Mozambique studying hypoglycaemia in children with malaria, I saw for the first time how clinical research can lead to meaningful discoveries with real-world impact.1 That experience shaped my career, and I have since been fortunate to study neurological and emerging infections while continuing to care for patients.

Many of us trained at a time when research was embedded within higher specialty training. Undertaking an MD or PhD was essential for career progression, and clinicians often remained engaged in research throughout their subsequent careers, whether as clinical academics or NHS doctors contributing alongside clinical work. Research was not viewed as optional; it was recognised as fundamental to making progress in the NHS, to improving patient outcomes and to supporting economic growth.

Evidence continues to support this view: patients experience better outcomes in research-active hospitals, even if they are not directly enrolled in studies;2 NHS staff engaged in research report higher job satisfaction;3 and the economic return is also clear – every £1 invested in medical research generates an estimated additional return of around 25p every year thereafter, forever.4

The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated research’s importance unequivocally. The UK’s ability to rapidly establish platform trials, recruit at scale, generate practice-changing evidence and collaborate across sectors was built on decades of investment in research infrastructure and workforce.5 This capability cannot be assumed to persist if the workforce underpinning it continues to decline. Almost every intervention in the NHS is grounded in research: drugs, diagnostics, devices, surgical techniques and models of care all depend on evidence. Without sustained research capacity, the foundations of healthcare are gradually weakened.

Statutory responsibility

The importance of research is embedded in legislation, policy and professional standards. The Health and Care Act 2022 introduced explicit legal duties for integrated care boards, which must each facilitate or promote research relevant to the health service and ensure that evidence from research is used in practice.6 These duties form part of NHS England’s annual performance assessment, making research a statutory responsibility rather than a discretionary activity. NHS England guidance further emphasises that research should be embedded within integrated care systems, with expectations that clinicians are supported to undertake research and apply its findings.7

Professional standards reinforce this position. The 2024 update of the General Medical Council’s Good Medical Practice highlights research as essential to improving understanding, prevention and treatment of disease.8 The joint NIHR and Royal College of Physicians 2022 position statement, Making research everybody’s business, called for ringfenced research time in job plans, stronger links between medical directors and R&D leadership, recognition of clinical research as direct clinical activity, and workforce planning that includes research roles.9

The Medical training review: diagnostic report (October 2025), commissioned by NHS England and authored by Sir Chris Whitty and Sir Steve Powis, reaffirmed that clinical academic medicine is essential for delivering healthcare now and in the future.10 Taken together, these frameworks make clear that research is not optional: it is mandated and expected.

Declining clinical academic workforce

Despite this strong policy foundation, the clinical academic workforce is under significant strain. Surveys suggest that many resident doctors lack confidence in pursuing research careers. A 2025 RCP survey found that although 45% of new consultants had undertaken research during training, only 36% had protected research time in their consultant job plans.11 Independent analyses have documented a decline in medically qualified university research staff, both in absolute terms and relative to the growing NHS workforce. The 2025 MRC report Clinical researchers in the United Kingdom: Reversing the decline to improve population health and promote economic growth identified multiple contributing factors.12

Universities face financial constraints, and medically qualified researchers are costly. Research assessment systems may disadvantage those with substantial clinical commitments. Mid-career roles, critical for leadership development, are particularly vulnerable. Financial considerations also influence personal decisions. Student debt, uncertain pay progression, and the slower financial trajectory of academic careers can deter participation. Caring responsibilities and geographic constraints further complicate pathways that require mobility and repeated competition.

Within the NHS, service pressures are intense. Research is rarely treated as core activity in job planning, and even when time is allocated, it is often eroded by rota gaps and service demands. While clinical priorities understandably dominate, the cumulative effect is a gradual erosion of research capacity.

Training pathways have also become more demanding. Dual accreditation in general internal medicine and specialty training adds complexity and work pressures: clinical academics are effectively ʻtriple accrediting’. Funding structures introduce further uncertainty, with career progression often dependent on successive competitive fellowships, and a particularly challenging transition from PhD to clinical lecturer roles.

Mentorship remains important, but has become less visible in routine clinical settings. I remember, as a junior doctor in Oxford, being unable to distinguish university- and NHS-employed clinicians in terms of their clinical and academic work. Nowadays, academics are pushed increasingly towards university responsibilities, and NHS researchers towards their clinical roles.

Research beyond formal academic tracks

The research workforce extends far beyond formal academic posts. Resident doctors lead audits and quality improvement initiatives that drive change. Consultants, SAS doctors and locally employed doctors contribute to registries, pragmatic trials and service redesign. This wide workforce supports recruitment into NIHR- and industry-sponsored studies.

The NIHR provides several schemes to support clinicians in their academic endeavours (Panel). As chair of one of the NIHR Research for Patient Benefit programmes, I was privileged to see first-hand some wonderful research led by NHS doctors that resulted in significant change.13

For research to remain central to the NHS, it must be accessible across the workforce. This requires pathways that support both formal academic careers and wider participation. The second phase of NHS England’s Medical training review: co-design and reform, led by Dame Jane Dacre, offers an opportunity to address barriers affecting resident doctors’ academic aspirations.14, 7 Let us hope the opportunity is not wasted.

What needs to change

The 2025 MRC report outlined several priorities.12

First, protected time must be meaningful. Research time in job plans and training programmes should be explicit, safeguarded, and recognised as contributing directly to patient care and organisational performance.

Second, career pathways must be more flexible. Clinicians should be able to move in and out of research at different stages, with viable re-entry routes. Accreditation requirements should be reviewed where they create unnecessary barriers.

Third, greater national alignment is needed. A coherent career framework across funders and training bodies would reduce fragmentation and uncertainty, while maintaining appropriate selectivity.

Fourth, leadership and mentorship must be strengthened. Senior researchers should actively support the next generation, and organisations should recognise mentorship in appraisal and reward systems. Research performance of NHS trusts, local commissioning boards, and their equivalents in the devolved administrations, should be visible at board level as an indicator of organisational quality and ambition.

Finally, participation must be widened. Gender disparities and other inequities persist. Targeted support, flexible training options and accessible entry points are essential if research is to be genuinely inclusive.

Making progress

Already progress is being made in many of these areas. The Academy of Medical Sciences has established a UK Medical Science Careers Taskforce, bringing together leaders from academia, the NHS, MedTech, and the pharmaceutical sector to address gaps across career pathways.15

At the Royal College of Physicians, we are providing practical support to residents and doctors in substantive posts, whether on an academic track or not. Our resource kit Making the case for clinical research helps clinicians advocate for research time in job planning and training discussions, drawing on statutory duties and professional standards.15 It gives colleagues confidence that research is not an optional extra, but is part of the NHS’s core responsibility.

We are also reviewing the college’s research prizes, awards and lectureships to increase visibility and accessibility. External recognition can be a real boost in academic career progression, especially in the early career stages. I still remember the thrill of winning a prize for my medical student elective in Mozambique in 1990, and the pride of receiving the RCP Linacre lectureship and Moxon medal in later years. Our reorganisation of college awards will be a visible signal of the importance of research to the RCP. New and refreshed funding schemes are being developed, particularly aimed at supporting early-career clinicians entering research.

In parallel, a short-life working group has examined structural barriers to academic careers for physicians, including challenges associated with dual accreditation and balancing clinical and academic commitments. These findings will inform the next phase of the Medical education training review.14

While these initiatives alone will not reverse national trends, professional bodies play a critical role in shaping culture, expectations and policy, ensuring that research remains central to the identity and future of the profession. This imperative is reinforced by a position statement endorsed by all 23 medical royal colleges and faculties through the Academy of Medical Royal Colleges.16 If the NHS is to remain a learning health system, with innovation, continuous improvement and high-quality care, research must be embedded in everyday practice. This requires protecting clinical academic careers, broadening access to research opportunities, and ensuring that time, mentorship and recognition are built into the system.17

Without deliberate action, research capacity will continue to erode. With coordinated effort, however, the NHS can sustain its role as a global leader in research-driven healthcare, improving outcomes for patients while contributing to national prosperity.
