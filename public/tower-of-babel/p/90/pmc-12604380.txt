Recent clinical advances in nonconjugated antibodies and antibody-drug conjugates for colorectal cancer treatment
Authors: Ghasem Noorkhajavi, Armin Banakholdi, Amirhosein Torabi, Amirreza Zoghi, Effat Iranijam, Elham Safarzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe second most prevalent cause of cancer-related fatalities worldwide and the third most often diagnosed malignancy is colorectal cancer (CRC). The treatment of colorectal cancer has been transformed by recent developments in antibody-based therapeutics, such as antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) and non-conjugated monoclonal antibodies (mAbs). This review aims to elucidate the most impactful therapeutic strategies in CRC immunotherapy, emphasizing both established and emerging approaches.Main bodyWith an emphasis on nonconjugated mAbs (such as bevacizumab and cetuximab) and ADCs (such as Ado-trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1), Fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan), this study examines more than 150 clinical trials and peer-reviewed publications from sources including Scopus, PubMed, and Web of Science. The selection criteria included studies published within the last decade, emphasizing mechanisms of action, clinical efficacy, and safety profiles. Non-conjugated mAbs target specific tumor antigens to inhibit signaling pathways and stimulate immune-mediated cytotoxicity, while ADCs combine mAbs with cytotoxic payloads for precise tumor cell elimination. Clinical trials have demonstrated significant survival benefits with these agents in biomarker-selected populations, particularly in HER2-positive and microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H/dMMR) CRC subgroups. Challenges include drug resistance, variable antigen expression, and adverse effects, underscoring the need for biomarker-driven approaches and combination therapies.ConclusionsImmunotherapy, particularly non-conjugated mAbs and ADCs, transforms CRC treatment by improving survival and quality of life. Ongoing research is essential to address resistance mechanisms, optimize combination regimens, and integrate personalized therapies into clinical practice.

Introduction

Colon cancer, or CRC, is a major global health problem. The illness is the second most prevalent cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide and the third most common kind of cancer to be diagnosed. About 1.9 million individuals are affected by CRC, which can have a hereditary basis, vary by gender and ethnicity, and be influenced by environmental factors [1, 2]. Therefore, understanding the risk factors for CRC is crucial for prevention. Key contributors in CRC development include: family history, genetic predispositions, and inflammatory bowel diseases like ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease. Other significant threat factors encompass colon polyps, diabetes, a history of cholecystectomy, and unhealthy dietary patterns. Lifestyle choices, including obesity, physical inactivity, excessive alcohol intake, and smoking, further increase the risk of getting CRC. Therefore, the chance of developing colorectal cancer can be considerably decreased by implementing healthy lifestyle choices, getting frequent tests, and identifying these risk factors early [3]. In cases where CRC is diagnosed, radiation therapy, surgery, and chemotherapy are the most common therapeutic approaches for CRC. Each treatment modality has distinct applications, benefits, and limitations. Given these constraints, researchers are investigating novel therapeutic procedures, including antibody-based therapies that protect healthy cells while focusing on malignant cells [4]. Immunotherapy is a form of cancer treatment that functions by improving, enhancing, or altering the immune system’s capacity to identify and eliminate malfunctioning cells. Important varieties include monoclonal antibodies, T-cell transfer treatment (such as CAR T-cell therapy), and immune checkpoint inhibitors [5].

Nonconjugated antibodies are therapeutic agents that target cancer cell antigens without relying on drugs or radioactive substances. They affect tumor cells through several fundamental mechanisms and are now among the most successful and main strategies in cancer treatment. Specifically, their primary action is to inhibit signaling pathways that otherwise encourage the growth and spread of tumors by preventing growth factors from doing their job. Moreover, these antibodies give the immune system a better chance of recognizing and eliminating cancerous cells because they bind to specific antigens on cancer cells. Collectively, these mechanisms make nonconjugated antibodies a highly effective strategy in the struggle against cancer. In targeted therapy for CRC, these antibodies directly inhibit tumor growth and metastasis by hindering vital pathways required for the survival and multiplication of malignant cells [6]. The success of nonconjugated antibodies has been complemented by the development of antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), which combine the targeting power of mAbs with the cytotoxicity of drugs [7, 8]. When ADCs internalize cancer cells, they deliver cytotoxic payloads by binding to overexpressed antigens on their surfaces [9]. Cytotoxic drugs, linkers, and mAbs are the main components of ADCs. The capacity of the MAb to bind particular antigens on the outermost layer of cells that are malignant allows the cytotoxic treatment to be precisely delivered to the tumor site. Upon binding to its target and internalization, an ADC releases a cytotoxic drug that destroys cancer cells. Linkers play an important part in this procedure; they connect the cytotoxic drug to the monoclonal antibody, cleave upon binding to the target, and remain stable in circulation. This mechanism ensures that the drug is activated near the tumor cells, as a result diminishing off-target toxicity and raising the therapeutic value in the process [10, 11]. Recent advancements in nonconjugated antibodies and ADCs have improved CRC treatment by enhancing targeted cancer cell destruction and minimizing side effects. At the same time, optimized ADCs administer chemotherapy straight to malignant cells, diminishing harm to healthy tissues. These innovations promise to significantly improve therapeutic outcomes and extend patient survival in CRC treatment [12]. In this review, we look at the existing situation, efficacy, and prospects of nonconjugated antibodies and ADCs in treating CRC in light of these advances. By considering the latest developments, clinical outcomes, and potential for further innovation, this review offers a comprehensive understanding of how these therapies are reshaping CRC treatment and their capacity to raise the quality of life and survival of patients.

Tumor microenvironment in colorectal cancer

Undoubtedly, the tumor microenvironment (TME) has an enormous influence on the development, resistance, and course of therapy of CRC. Numerous non-cancerous cells, including cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), immune cells, extracellular matrix (ECM) components, and endothelial cells, are found in this area. External factors like hypoxia and chemotherapy also affect the TME. These factors can influence cancer cells’ behavior and the efficacy of treatments [13]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAM) and regulatory T cells (Tregs) suppress antitumor immunity by secreting growth factors and modulating the immune response. In addition to promoting angiogenesis and tumor growth, TAMs and Tregs secrete cytokines that increase tumor metastasis and expansion [14]. Cancer cells alter their metabolism for survival in hypoxic regions in the TME, where endothelial cells drive abnormal blood vessel growth (angiogenesis) using numerous factors like VEGF, FGF, and PDGF [15]. The ECM provides structural support and facilitates cancer cell migration and invasion. Immune checkpoint molecules, for instance, PD-L1, which impairs T cell activity, engage with the environment surrounding the tumor or TME to facilitate tumor development, immune evasion, and migration in CRC cells. ECM proteins can also contribute to metastasis by interacting with cancer cells and aiding their migration. Furthermore, ECM proteins can create a conducive environment for cancer cells to survive and expand [16]. Tumor cells, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), and TAMs all exhibit high expression of PD-1 and PD-L1 receptors and ligands during tumor-directed immune cell activation, which aids in the growth and spread of tumors [17]. PGE2/EP4, TGF-β, and Notch-1 are the most important signaling pathways affecting TME and exert their role on cancer cells by different mechanisms. PGE2/EP4 and TGF-β pathways promote the expression of PD-L1 on malignant cells, while the Notch-1 pathway inhibits T lymphocyte proliferation and activation. Both pathways can modulate cancer progression [18, 19]. Targeting the TME offers promising therapeutic avenues, mainly through anti-angiogenic agents such as VEGF inhibitors and immunomodulatory approaches like immune checkpoint inhibitors. However, the effective targeting of the TME remains challenging due to its intrinsic heterogeneity, the development of therapeutic resistance, and the adaptive capacity of tumors. Strategies aimed at reversing hypoxia and reprogramming cancer cell metabolism are also being investigated, focusing on disrupting the function of CAFs and the ECM [20].

Despite these advances, tumors can remodel their microenvironment to evade treatments, underscoring the critical need for combination treatments that target both the cancer itself and its supportive TME. Understanding the molecular mechanisms that drive TME-mediated tumor promotion is indispensable for creating individualized therapies based on the particulars of each patient’s TME. Such personalized approaches aim to limit metastasis, overcome treatment resistance, and improve the efficacy of existing therapies. Targeting the TME can potentially boost patient outcomes and enhance quality of life by reducing treatment-related side effects [21]. As a result, the TME is a pivotal factor in CRC progression, and its modulation is crucial for developing durable and effective therapies. With ongoing research focused on the molecular drivers of the TME, personalized treatments that disrupt these pro-tumor pathways could lead to significant advances in patient management [22]. Additionally, emerging therapeutic strategies involving non-conjugated antibodies and ADCs are showing potential to improve CRC treatment outcomes by simultaneously targeting cancer cells and modifying the TME [23]. These novel approaches may provide a synergistic effect, enhancing the overall efficacy of CRC treatment and further advancing the field of cancer therapy.

Non-conjugated mAbs

Due to ability of mAbs to target disease-specific molecules with minimal off-target effects, they have become pivotal across diverse therapeutic areas [24, 25]. To reduce the immunogenicity associated with mAbs and enhance their therapeutic potential, hybridoma technology was developed in 1975. This method entails combining myeloma cells with antibody-producing B cells to form hybridoma cells, which can live indefinitely and consistently generate mAbs. Cultured hybridomas provide a robust source of mAbs, enabling large-scale production for clinical applications [26]. As medicine advances towards personalized therapies, mAbs have become invaluable in treating immune-mediated disorders, infectious diseases, and various cancers. These magic bullets, often used in conjunction with other therapeutic agents, enhance overall treatment efficacy and are integral to modern treatment regimens. Their versatility continues to expand therapeutic options, underscoring their essential role in targeted disease management and personalized medicine [27, 28]. Muromonab-CD3 was the initial mAb approved for clinical application, developed to prevent kidney transplant rejection by targeting CD3 receptors on T cells. mAbs are named using a standardized nomenclature comprising three elements: a prefix (indicating the target or disease), a substem (reflecting species origin or antigen), and the universal suffix “-mab”. For instance, in clinical use, bevacizumab (“beva-” for biological/anti-VEGF, “-zu-” for humanized, “mab”), trastuzumab (“tras-” for tumor, “-zu-” for humanized, “mab”), and cetuximab (“ce-”/“tu-” for tumor, “-xi-” for chimeric, “mab”) adhere to these schemes, making the nomenclature clinically relevant. Table 1 gives further examples, including hypothetical names and meanings for each component in approved and experimental monoclonal antibodies. These standardized naming conventions improve scientific communication by distinguishing mAbs based on their antigen targets, origin, and structural characteristics [29, 30].

Depending on whether the mAbs are conjugated or unconjugated (also known as naked mAbs or monospecific mAbs), mAbs can be classified as conjugated, unconjugated, or bispecific. The most widely utilized mAbs for cancer therapy are naked or unconjugated, which act independently. By attaching to antigens present on cancerous cells, these mAbs can elicit several responses: They can strengthen the body’s natural defenses against cancerous cells, attract immune cells to promote apoptosis, target immune checkpoints, or block signals essential for cancer cell proliferation. Certain mAbs are linked with drugs or toxins to transport cytotoxic substances to cancer cells precisely, improving treatment accuracy and reducing adverse effects [29, 31].

Despite their potential, mAb therapies can lead to several adverse effects as following: (1) immune responses, from mild hypersensitivity to severe reactions to anaphylaxis; (2) greater vulnerability to infections due to immunosuppressive effects; (3) platelets and thromboembolism disorders; (4) possible onset or aggravation of autoimmune disorders such as lupus, thyroid issues, and autoimmune colitis; (5) increased risk of secondary cancers from immune modulation; (6) skin conditions such as dermatitis; (7) cardiotoxicity that can appear as either cardiomyopathy or heart failure; and (8) cytokine release syndrome, also known as cytokine storm, causes widespread inflammation and may damage organs [32]. Additionally, the effectiveness of mAbs treatments can be restricted by the need for a steady presence or consistency of target molecules on tumor cells. Variability in antigen expression or mutations can reduce mAb effectiveness. A recently discovered resistance mechanism to antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) involves downregulating cell surface proteins critical for forming immune synapses and killing target cells in response to cetuximab and trastuzumab administration. Tumor cell adaptations like these confounders can diminish mAb efficacy, underscoring the importance of continued research into overcoming resistance pathways to optimize mAb therapies [33].

Mechanisms of action of Non-conjugated mAbs

Gaining insight into how these antibodies attack cancer cells has transformed cancer treatment approaches, leading to a more manageable toxicity level compared to traditional chemotherapy. These processes involve disrupting tumor cell survival pathways, hindering tumor expansion by blocking tumor angiogenesis, avoiding programmed cell death, and bypassing immune checkpoints [29].

Direct tumor cell killing

This mechanism requires the direct action of antibodies, which can be agonists or antagonists, leading to the induction of apoptosis, the delivery of a toxic agent in drug-conjugated forms, and the adjustment of cellular activity by attaching to an antigen that can transmit signals within the cell [24, 29]. This capacity to interrupt interactions and absorb receptors in cancer treatments has paved the way for encouraging strategies in halting cancer-causing cellular signaling [34]. There are several ways in which direct killing can occur, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The first is ligand blocking, which is the process by which growth factors and other circulating ligands are bound by mAbs. MAb prohibits these ligands from engaging with their particular receptors on the surface of cancer cells by blocking their passage. The downstream signaling pathways that are necessary for the growth and survival of tumor cells are successfully blocked by this action. Because of this, the cancer cells are unable to get the signals they require to proliferate and endure, which ultimately results in their demise (Fig. 1A) [35, 36].

The other mechanism is abrogation of signal transduction, a direct process where antibodies target the cancer cell itself to induce its destruction. In which the cancer cell with specific receptors on its surface, such as the CD20 antigen. mAbs are shown binding directly to these receptors, an action that physically blocks the receptor to prevent the activation of pro-growth signaling pathways, and it flags the cell for destruction by the immune system. This interaction is highlighted as inducing apoptotic signals within the cancer cell, ultimately leading to its programmed cell death (Fig. 1B) [37, 38]. Finally, the direct delivery of cytotoxic drugs to tumor cells is made possible by conjugated monoclonal antibodies, or ADCs. A potent drug or toxins are attached to these mAbs, causing the death of the cancer cells upon binding and internalization while minimizing damage to healthy cells (Fig. 1C) [39].

Immune-mediated tumor cell killing

In this case, the mAb influences the immune system by engaging with its Fc regions and stimulating Fc receptors (FCRs) on multiple immune cells, resulting in the death of tumor cells (35). MAbs promote tumor cell death by enhancing various immune mechanisms [29, 34, 40] as illustrated in Fig. 2. These routes include: modulation of the immune checkpoint molecules like PD-L1, PD-1, and CTLA-4 (Fig. 2A) [41], induction of Complement-Dependent Cytotoxicity (CDC) (Fig. 2B) [42, 43], Antibody-Dependent Cellular Phagocytosis (ADCP) (Fig. 2C) [44], induction of Antibody-dependent Cellular Cytotoxicity (ADCC) (Fig. 2D) [45], and improving the presentation of antigens and activating T cells by directly engaging with antigen-presenting cells or interacting with Fc receptors (Fig. 2E) [46].

Through activating these immune pathways, mAbs can greatly improve the immune system’s ability to identify and eliminate cancerous cells. Crucially, a lot of cancers could evade from immune surveillance, like downregulating antigen presentation or upregulating immune-inhibitory signals, which let them spread uncontrolled even when the immune system detects them [47]. A potent way to address tumors that may otherwise evade immune surveillance is immune-mediated killing, which complements the direct actions of mAbs. Thus, mAb-mediated tumor cell killing is crucial in immunotherapy for various cancers, from lymphomas to solid tumors.

Nonconjugated mAbs in colorectal cancer

A growing number of studies have explored the potential use of mAbs for CRC therapy. Nonconjugated mAbs target cancer cells directly and are made to attach to certain compounds on the outside of cancerous cells, preventing these cells from growing and surviving. This binding causes the cancer cells to die without affecting healthy cells.

Antiangiogenesis

Angiogenesis is crucial for the growing process of both normal and cancerous cells by delivering essential nutrients and oxygen [48]. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), is a strong, specialized regulator of angiogenesis that is expressed at higher levels in a variety of human malignancies, including CRC [49]. The members of the VEGF family include PIGF or the placental growth factor, the viral counterpart VEGF-A and VEGF-E, and VEGF-D, VEGF-C, and VEGF-B. VEGF-A promotes angiogenesis and enhances vascular permeability and includs four different isoforms: VEGF121, VEGF165, VEGF206, and VEGF189. VEGF-B controls migration, ECM disintegration, and cell adhesion. A role for both VEGF-C and VEGF-D is lymphangiogenesis [50]. The receptor tyrosine kinases VEGF receptor 1 (VEGFR-1, also named flt-1) and VEGF receptor 2 (VEGFR-2, also named flk-1) are bound by VEGF-A [49, 50] and also by VEGFR-2 and VEGFR-3, VEGF-D, and VEGF-C. Therefore, even after blocking VEGF-A, VEGF-D, and VEGF-C may continue to activate VEGFR-2 pathways. Primary colonic cancers and lymph node metastases express VEGF-C and VEGFR-2, indicating VEGF-C signaling through this route [50]. VEGF is targeted as a new method for treating solid tumors to suppress angiogenesis [49].

Bevacizumab

A recombinant, humanized immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) monoclonal antibody that targets human VEGF is called bevacizumab (Avastin®). It was generated using a 93% human and 7% murine antihuman VEGF monoclonal antibody (muMAb VEGF) [49, 51]. Bevacizumab binds to soluble VEGF and, via steric blocking, hinders it from coming into contact with the vascular endothelial cells’ surface receptors [49, 52]. Murine anti-VEGF mAbs and Bevacizumab have no direct impact on tumor cell proliferation in vitro, suggesting they do not explicitly target malignant cells [49].

Evidence shows that combining fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy with bevacizumab, with or without irinotecan or oxaliplatin, for first- and second-line treatment of metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) leads to higher response rates, longer time to disease progression, and extended median survival [49, 50]. When administered with chemotherapy regimens like FOLFOX (oxaliplatin, 5-fluorouracil + folinic acid) or XELOX (oxaliplatin + capecitabine), bevacizumab boosts overall survival and progression-free survival much more than chemotherapy alone. According to the NO16966 clinical study, bevacizumab was associated with a 2.3-month improvement in overall survival and a 1.7-month increase in median progression-free survival [53]. By comparison, sequential therapy (fluoropyrimidine + bevacizumab, with irinotecan for disease progression) with combination therapy (fluoropyrimidine + irinotecan + bevacizumab), the XELAVIRI study examined the most suitable initial therapeutic approach for mCRC. According to the results of their experiment, sequential therapy is not worse than the time to strategy failure (TFS) (HR (Hazard Ratio), 0.86; 90% CI (Confidence Interval), 0.73–1.02). Patients with RAS/BRAF wild-type tumors benefited greatly from upfront combination therapy. However, RAS-mutated cases did not. Overall survival (OS) outcomes were similar, even though the sequential arm had less clinical toxicity. These findings highlight the significance of molecular stratification in maximizing bevacizumab-based first-line treatments for mCRC [54].

The BEVAMAINT-PRODIGE 71 study (FFCD 1710) showed that, in comparison to fluoropyrimidine alone, bevacizumab increased progression-free survival (PFS) when added to a fluoropyrimidine. However, there was barely any effect on OS. The study also confirmed that using bevacizumab in the treatment’s maintenance phase prevents tumor recurrence after discontinuing antiangiogenic therapy. The maintenance of treatment after disease progression correlates with extended OS. However, using fluoropyrimidine alone is considered a better-tolerated and more economical choice, as bevacizumab increases the possibility of unfavorable outcomes such as thromboembolic events and gastrointestinal perforation. Such results emphasize the importance of balancing efficacy, safety, and cost when selecting treatment strategies [55]. In real-world metastatic CRC cohorts, bevacizumab biosimilars, such as bevacizumab-awwb, have demonstrated equivalent safety and efficacy to the original drug, leading to increased uptake and cost-effectiveness in frontline and maintenance regimens [56].

Several clinical trials assessed the efficacy and safety of combining TAS-102 (trifluridine/tipiracil hydrochloride) with bevacizumab as third-line treatment in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer. The C-TASK FORCE study evaluated the efficacy and safety of this combination as third-line treatment in mCRC patients who were resistant or intolerant to conventional chemotherapies (irinotecan, oxaliplatin, fluoropyrimidine-based regimens), VEGF-targeted agents, EGFR inhibitors (for wild-type KRAS tumors), with no prior exposure to regorafenib. Results were promising; median progression-free survival (PFS) by investigator assessment was 5.6 months and 3.7 months by central evaluations, and the median overall survival (OS) was 11.4 months [57]. However, the C-TASK FORCE study enrolled patients receiving second or third-line treatment. Thus, TAS-CC3, a phase 2 trial, specifically studied these patients receiving this combination as third-line treatment. It should be noted that 56% of patients had RAS mutations. Patients with RAS wild-type tumors demonstrated significantly longer PFS and OS than RAS-mutated cases (PFS: 5.4 months vs. 2.6 months; OS: 12.2 months vs. 9.3 months). Nevertheless, the OS in the C-TASK FORCE trial exceeded that observed in the TAS-CC3 study, which may be attributable to enrolled patients receiving second-line treatment [58]. Trifluridine–tipiracil (TAS-102) plus bevacizumab (Beva) was assessed as a third-line treatment for patients with refractory metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) in the more recent phase 3 SUNLIGHT research. In addition to showing improvements in progression-free survival and quality of life maintenance without new safety signals, the trial showed that TAS-102 plus bevacizumab significantly improved overall survival when compared to TAS-102 alone (median OS of 10.8 months vs. 7.5 months), with a hazard ratio of 0.61. As a result, it is now considered the standard of care in this context [59].

Of note, preclinical research has determined that antiangiogenic compounds, such as bevacizumab, may result in a number of adverse consequences, including gastrointestinal perforation, arterial thromboembolic events (ATEs), bleeding, wound healing issues, hypertension, and proteinuria [50, 51, 60].

Ramucirumab

Ramucirumab is an entirely human IgG1 monoclonal antibody that blocks VEGFR2 activation and signaling by specifically binding its extracellular domain with a high affinity (KD = 50 pM) [61]. Compared to the natural VEGF-A ligand, ramucirumab shows a noticeably greater binding affinity for the VEGF-binding epitope on the extracellular domain of VEGFR-2 [62]. Unlike other agents targeting the VEGF signaling cascade, ramucirumab specifically targets a defined epitope on VEGFR2 extracellular domain, causing complete blockade of all VEGF ligand binding (VEGF-A/C/D) to this clinically validated target [61]. More complete target inhibition and high specificity in combination may lead to a more complete angiogenesis blockade through synergistic pathway inhibition [62]. The local concentration of targeted ligands may affect the efficacy of bevacizumab and aflibercept; however, because ramucirumab binds to the extracellular domain of VEGFR2, it seems to have less of an impact [61].

Verdaguer et al. were shown that after ramucirumab therapy, VEGF-A level elevated almost instantly and remained high until the subsequent treatment. This elevation was attributed to ramucirumab’s competitive displacement of natural VEGF-A, enabled by higher binding affinity for VEGFR2. VEGF-A rate remained high as ramucirumab was present. Conversely, the quantities of soluble VEGFR1 and VEGFR2 reduced right after ramucirumab therapy [61]. When paired with fluorouracil, leucovorin, and irinotecan, ramucirumab demonstrated exceptional performance as a second-line treatment for patients with mCRC in the phase III randomized, placebo-controlled clinical research RAISE. In this study, nearly half of the patients had malignancies with mutant KRAS. When compared to placebo/FOLFIRI, ramucirumab/FOLFIRI therapy dramatically increased PFS and OS in wild-type individuals [61, 63]. Commonly reported adverse reactions following ramucirumab use are grade 3 hypertension and abdominal pain. Deep vein thrombosis, headache, nausea, vomiting, elevated blood alkaline phosphatase, neutropenia, proteinuria, and dyspnea are also among other grade 3–5 side events [61, 63].

EGFR pathway

EGFR is a transmembrane receptor with tyrosine kinase activity that is also known as erb-B1 or HER1. It is expressed in various cell types and is essential for the growth of cells and division. Also, it is associated with several types of cancers, and its expression is considered as a potential therapeutic target [64, 65]. HER receptors are membrane-bound glycoproteins, including HER3, HER2, HER4, and EGFR (HER1). These receptors have three parts: an extracellular region that binds ligands, an intracellular area with tyrosine kinase activity, and a membrane-spanning part affixing the cell to the receptor. When ligands bind to the extracellular domain, they cause the creation of homodimers (between monomers of the same receptor) or heterodimers (between the attached receptor and other HER family members), subsequently inducing tyrosine kinases, triggering a cascade of cellular pathways that regulate adhesion, apoptosis, angiogenesis, motility, and cell proliferation [66]. The c-erb-B1 proto-oncogene, which is found on chromosome 7q22, encodes the 170 kDa transmembrane glycoprotein known as HER1 (EGFR). TGF-α, amphiregulin, EGF, heparin-binding EGF, epiregulin, betacellulin, and NRG2-α are the ligands that have been discovered [65]. Various tumor cell lines, including 25–80% colorectal carcinomas, have overexpression of EGFR [64].

The administration of mAbs that target the extracellular domain of the EGFR has proven to be an effective strategy for inhibiting natural ligand binding [65, 67]. Murine antibodies were used to create M528 IgG, M579 IgG, and M225 IgG, which block EGFR activation. M225 was determined to have the strongest anti-EGFR activity upon further analysis. However, human anti-mouse antibodies (HAMA) emerged in patients treated by M225, despite the trials’ effectiveness. Consequently, M225 was altered to synthesize C225, a human-murine chimeric antibody containing an IgG1 Fc isotype [67].

Cetuximab

Extremely specific for EGFR, cetuximab (Erbitux®) is a recombinant human/mouse chimeric monoclonal antibody. Preclinical studies have shown antitumor activity against various cancer cell lines [64, 66]. etuximab’s antitumor activity occurs via different pathways. cetuximab hinders cell cycle progression during the G1 gap phase by increasing the expression of p27kip1. Changing the Bax to Bcl-2 ratio and raising apoptotic caspases also triggers cancer cell apoptosis. Additionally, cetuximab downregulates the synthesis of growth factors like amphiregulin (AR), TGFα, and cripto (CR −1), as well as angiogenic factors such as IL-8, VEGF, and essential fibroblast growth factor. Additionally, cetuximab has been shown to prevent invasion and migration of malignant cells, possibly by preventing adhesion molecule production and activity like matrix metalloproteinase-9. In addition, a variety of cellular reactions are triggered by cetuximab, increasing the tumor’s sensitivity to chemotherapy and radiotherapy [64, 68].

It was demonstrated that the combination of FOLFIRI and first-line cetuximab treatment reduced the likelihood of mCRC progression. The cetuximab group’s showed considerably extended progression-free survival and a higher response rate than the placebo group’s [69]. KRAS mutations occur in 36–46% of CRC cases and CRC patients with KRAS have poor efficacy to cetuximab treatment. Nevertheless, recent studies have demonstrated that cetuximab accelerates RSL3-induced ferroptosis by triggering p38 MAPK and inhibiting the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway. This discovery could assist in developing of a therapy plan including cetuximab and ferroptosis for patients with KRAS mutant CRC. This will propose a novel method of treatment for KRAS mutant CRC patients, in which iron and reactive oxygen species cause a form of cell death called ferroptosis. The Nrf2/HO-1 pathway’s inhibition by cetuximab will likely lead to increased ferroptosis, which could benefit these patients [70]. Furthermore, Zhang et al. reported that treatment of CRC cells with cetuximab increases the expression of E-cadherin while decreasing the expression of PLCB3 and β-catenin. The WNT signaling pathway is essential for tissue repair and homeostasis. The majority of colorectal cancers show excessive WNT pathway activity. The primary outcome of WNT pathway activation is β-catenin-mediated gene transcription. Accordingly, combining cetuximab with the si-PLCB3-1 and Wnt activator further intensifies the inhibition of CRC proliferation, migration, and invasion [71].

Among the adverse events associated with cetuximab, cutaneous toxicity and hypersensitivity reactions were more common. Other adverse consequences of cetuximab include hypomagnesemia, diarrhea, and asthenia (with or without accompanying hypocalcemia) [64, 66].

Panitumumab

With a high affinity for binding specifically to the extracellular region of EGFR on both healthy and neoplastic cells (approximately 8-fold greater affinity compared with cetuximab), panitumumab (Vectibix®) is a recombinant, fully human IgG2 monoclonal antibody antagonist of the EGFR that inhibits its activation and intracellular signals [72–74]. After traditional chemotherapy (irinotecan, oxaliplatin, and fluoropyrimidines) failed to treat EGFR-positive mCRC, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMEA) authorized panitumumab use as a monotherapy [75].

In the randomly allocated, open-label, multicenter, phase III PRIME trial, patients with wild-type KRAS tumors who received panitumumab + FOLFOX4 had a significantly longer median progression-free survival (primary outcome) than those who received FOLFOX4 alone [76, 77]. Despite its efficacy in people with wild-type KRAS, panitumumab does not provide clinical benefit to patients with KRAS mutations in mCRC [78, 79]. Proposed mechanisms for panitumumab’s antitumor activity include receptor internalization-induced downregulation of EGFR expression, EGFR signaling pathway blockage that induces apoptosis, cell cycle stop, autophagy, and angiogenesis suppression [75–77]. Although both panitumumab and cetuximab are anti-EGFR mAbs, their safety profile are mainly different; this difference may result from cetuximab being a chimeric IgG1 and panitumumab being a completely human IgG2 [72]. Murine sequences introduce the risk of hypersensitivity reactions, and human anti-mouse antibodies can neutralize cetuximab with subsequent administrations [74].

Genetic factors underlie the tumor-specific response, and mCRCs react differently to drugs that target EGFR. Monotherapy with cetuximab or panitumumab only works in 10% to 20% of mCRCs. Recently published data indicate that therapeutic response to EGFR inhibition is determined by oncogenic activation of downstream signaling pathways like BRAF, KRAS, PIK3CA, and PTEN. anti-EGFR treatments are more likely to be effective in CRCs that do not exhibit oncogenic changes in these four genes, known as “quadruple negative.” Collective data indicate that evaluating BRAF, PIK3CA/PTEN, and KRAS mutations may help identify mCRC patients who do not benefit from anti-EGFR therapy. Additionally, detecting the copy quantity of the EGFR gene and assessing EGFR gene polymorphisms and receptor ligand mutations may predict clinical outcomes and patient response rates. Another possibility is mutations in other key components of EGFR-dependent signaling (e.g., AKT1, MAPK) or in tyrosine kinase receptors other than EGFR, which provide alternative pathways for survival and/or proliferation [80, 81].

Despite its substantial effects, panitumumab administration led to some complications. In ≥ 20% of cases, dermatologic toxicities (pruritus, erythema, dermatitis acneiform, skin exfoliation, fissures, and rash) were the most often reported side effects. Beyond cutaneous effects, other frequent adverse events associated with panitumumab monotherapy include: paronychia, electrolyte abnormalities (hypomagnesemia), and GI toxicities (diarrhea, nausea, vomiting), along with fatigue. However, severe dermatologic toxicity, pulmonary embolism, and pulmonary fibrosis are the most clinically important side effects linked to panitumumab treatment [73, 76, 77].

HER2 pathway

Anti-EGFR monoclonal antibodies, especially cetuximab and panitumumab, are commonly used in tumor-targeted therapy for metastatic colorectal cancer. Unfortunately, mutations in NRAS, KRAS, or BRAF trigger alternative signals that circumvent EGFR signaling inhibition and decrease the effectiveness of anti-EGFR antibodies (primary resistance). According to studies, patients with KRAS wild-type metastatic colorectal cancer resistant to anti-EGFR antibodies may benefit from treatment by targeting HER2 [82]. HER2 changes can co-occur with primary resistance, which mutations in NRAS, KRAS, PIK3CA, and other genes may drive. However, it has not been confirmed how this may affect the efficacy of HER2-targeted treatment in individuals with malignant colorectal cancer that is HER2-positive [83]. HER2 amplification or overexpression was observed in 2–6% of patients overall, and in around 5% of individuals with metastatic disease and RAS wild-type (WT) tumors [83, 84]. Overexpression of HER2 is an initial molecular mutation that remains during the development of tumors and supports production of both activated homo- and hetero-dimers [82, 85]. While HER2 amplification is a well-established negative prognostic factor in certain clinical trials, other studies report no significant association between HER2 amplification and clinical results [84].

Trastuzumab

A recombinant humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody called trastuzumab (Herceptin®) targets the extracellular domain of the HER2 receptor (ErbB-2) [86]. Trastuzumab attaches to the HER2 extracellular domain and hinders HER2 extracellular domain division and consequently activation of the receptor, inhibits HER2 dimerization, and accelerates endocytic degradation to downregulate the HER2 receptor. There are diminished homo and heterodimers that may trigger signals when there are fewer HER2 receptors available. Trastuzumab may impair cells’ repair of damaged DNA after radiation or chemotherapy. In addition, trastuzumab opposes the activation of vascular endothelial growth factor and other angiogenic factors by HER2. Additionally, trastuzumab induces ADCC and causes complement activation [85, 86].

Tucatinib was approved by the FDA on January 19, 2023, as a combination treatment with trastuzumab for mCRC that is HER2-amplified/RAS wild-type and resistant to chemotherapy [84]. In the context of preclinical colorectal cancer models, monotherapy using either anti-HER2 antibodies or HER2 tyrosine kinase inhibitors has demonstrated limited efficacy. Conversely, favorable outcomes were achieved when a tyrosine kinase inhibitor (lapatinib) and an antibody (pertuzumab or trastuzumab) were combined. Although the pertuzumab-lapatinib combination lacks clinical approval and has an incompletely characterized toxicity profile, the trastuzumab-lapatinib dual blockade remains a standard regimen for HER2-positive breast cancer. The HERACLES trial evaluated the effectiveness of trastuzumab and lapatinib in patients with HER2-positive, KRAS exon 2 (codons 12 and 13) wild-type mCRC after standard treatments failed. The findings show that in HER2-positive mCRC patients who are not improving with therapy, trastuzumab and lapatinib together are both beneficial and well tolerated [82]. Trastuzumab’s short-term adverse effects are often modest and easy to manage. Nevertheless, trastuzumab therapy carries a significant risk of cardiotoxicity. Other adverse events are infusion-related reactions, which may present with systemic symptoms (fever, chills), respiratory compromise (dyspnea, bronchospasm, hypoxia), and cardiovascular manifestations (hypotension) [84, 86].

Pertuzumab

One of the main processes for activating various signaling pathways involves HER dimerization, which is considered a promising target for developing novel drugs [87]. For over 15 years, trastuzumab was the only HER2 antibody on the market. However, it failed to treat cancers lacking gene amplification or overexpression. Also, some individuals, primarily those with HER3 overexpression, have initial resistance to trastuzumab or will acquire secondary resistance mechanisms. By binding to the HER2 receptor’s ECD IV, trastuzumab prevents the ligand-independent heterodimers (HER3, HER2-HER3, as well as HER2–HER1 and HER2–HER4) and the spontaneous creation of homodimers (HER2–HER2). Regretfully, trastuzumab has little effect in blocking heterodimerization, especially with regard to the strongest HER2 − HER3 dimer [87, 88]. As a result of these findings, a novel type of HER2-targeted treatment was developed [87].

The first antibody from a brand-new family of medications known as dimerization inhibitors is pertuzumab (Perjeta®) [87]. A recombinant humanized monoclonal antibody called pertuzumab attaches itself to the HER2 extracellular dimerization domain II and prevents ligand-induced HER2 heterodimerization [87, 88]. Trastuzumab blocks the signaling pathway without affecting dimerization, so pertuzumab’s mechanism of action complements that of trastuzumab. Furthermore, pertuzumab’s anticancer effect as an antibody is linked to ADCC’s stimulation of the immune system, particularly NK cells [87].

A phase I trial conducted with pertuzumab has shown promising results when used in conjunction with other agents, including cytotoxic drugs (capecitabine and docetaxel) and targeted therapies (EGFR inhibitors, Trastuzumab-DM1) [87]. The effectiveness of administering drugs against VEGF, the crucial component of colorectal cancer, is reported by many phase III studies. However, because EGFR inhibition causes HER2 − HER3 heterodimers and HER3, the use of anti-VEGF medications eventually leads to primary resistance, including HER3 overexpression. Regardless of EGFR blockade, HER2 − HER3 initiates the MAPK pathway, which makes pertuzumab an effective medication to overcome heterodimer-mediated anti-EGFR resistance. The effectiveness of treating patients with cetuximab-resistant mCRC with a combination of pertuzumab and cetuximab has been examined in a phase II/I trial [87]. Because the two medications’ toxicity profiles overlapped, the trial was abruptly terminated [87, 89]. Targeted medicines in advanced malignancies with actionable genetic changes are examined by the TAPUR Study. It was indicated that pre-existing genetic alterations like the amplification of ERBB2 in colorectal tumors are related to primary and secondary resistance to anti–EGFR therapies, and increased occurrence of central nervous system metastases [90]. Results are presented from CRC individuals treated with trastuzumab plus pertuzumab (T + P) who had ERBB2 amplifications or ERBB2/3 mutations. This work highlights the need for more investigation by showing that T + P is efficacious in ERBB2-amplified CRC but fails to display antitumor activity in CRC caused by mutations in ERBB2/3 [91]. Since HER2-targeted therapies are effective in treating HER2-positive breast and gastric cancers, they hold promise for treating HER2-positive metastatic mCRC. HER2 overexpression or amplification was seen in 2–6% of individuals with stage 3/4 CRC. Preclinical research shows that HER2 monotherapies (such as pertuzumab, trastuzumab, and lapatinib) are not very successful, while combination treatments (such as trastuzumab + lapatinib) are more effective. The HERACLES phase II research found that 30% of patients with HER2-positive, KRAS wild-type mCRC who received trastuzumab with lapatinib responded. An ORR of 30% and a median PFS of 4.7 months were reported by the phase II HERACLES study, which assessed dual HER2 blockade with trastuzumab plus lapatinib. Biomarker criteria were HER2 amplification (IHC 3 + or 2 + with FISH positivity) and KRAS wild-type status. Dual HER2-targeted treatment (trastuzumab and pertuzumab) is well-tolerated and successful, especially in KRAS wild-type patients (40% overall response rate), according to the MyPathway phase 2a research. For patients with HER2-amplified mCRC who have received significant pretreatment, this makes it a viable choice [83].

Along with the cardiac safety concerns, rash, tiredness, diarrhea, and nausea were the most frequent side effects seen. Most of them were mild to moderate in severity when administered in a phase II context with either pertuzumab monotherapy or trastuzumab/pertuzumab [88].

Immunotherapy

Numerous mechanisms have been developed by tumors to suppress and evade the immune system, such as (1) signaling issues that cause T-cell proliferation to decline, (2) inducing Tregs and MDSCs, (3) diminished tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes’ cell-mediated cytotoxicity, (4) inadequate antigen presentation due to lack of co-stimulatory molecules in tumor cells or down-regulation of MHC class I molecules, and (5) raised tolerogenic molecule levels in the tumor [92].

Anti-CTLA4; ipilimumab

T cell activation is finalized through a secondary co-stimulatory signal, where CD28 on T cells binds to B7-2 (CD86) and B7-1 (CD80) molecules on APCs. This occurs after the T cell receptor (TCR) recognizes an antigen bound to the MHC on the APCs [92, 93]. CTLA-4 is elevated in activated effector T-cells after T-cell activation. As a negative regulator, CTLA-4 counteracts the stimulatory effects of CD28 on T-cell responses. In fact, when it comes to binding to the B7 complex, CTLA-4, a homolog of CD28, outcompetes CD28 and shows a higher affinity for B7 family members. T-cell activation is inhibited by CTLA-4 interactions with CD86 and CD80 due to the disruption of IL-2 production and cell cycle progression. Structurally, CTLA-4 resembles other Ig family co-receptors like ICOS and CD28, featuring an IgV domain in its extracellular region. This stalk promotes disulfide-linked dimerization, a transmembrane portion, and a cytoplasmic tail. In inactive T cells, CTLA-4 localizes to intracellular compartments, including endosomes, the trans-Golgi network, lysosomal vesicles, and secretory granules, while CD28 is predominantly expressed on the plasma membrane. When it comes to binding to B7 molecules, CD28 exhibits monovalent behavior, whilst CTLA-4 shows a bivalent binding capability. These results show that compared to CD28, CTLA-4 has a higher affinity for B7 molecules [92]. Immunosuppressive mechanisms of CTLA-4 include the followings: it displaces B7 molecules from CD28, preventing the stimulatory effects of CD28 signaling; it disrupts TCR signaling by interacting with PP2A and the tyrosine phosphatase SHP2 through its cytoplasmic tail; it activates AKT and PI3K, which inactivates the pro-apoptotic BAD protein while boosting levels of anti-apoptotic Bcl-xL and Bcl-2; it shortens the time between T cells and APCs by increasing T cell motility and decreasing lipid raft expression, which hinders the formation of immunologic synapses; and when expressed on Tregs, CTLA-4 interacts with CD86/CD80 on APCs, causing reverse signaling that upregulates IDO, depletes tryptophan (causing tryptophan starvation), and elevates kynurenine levels, ultimately promoting T cell apoptosis [92, 93]. “In summary, understanding CTLA-4’s pivotal role in restraining T-cell activation led to the proposition that blocking CTLA-4 might enhance antitumor immune responses mediated by CD4+ and CD8+ effector T cells [93].

For clinical use, two monoclonal antibodies that target CTLA-4, tremelimumab and ipilimumab, have been developed. A completely human IgG1k monoclonal antibody that targets CTLA-4 is called ipilimumab (Yervoy®) [92]. According to the CheckMate 142 study, the US Food and Drug Administration authorized nivolumab to be used either in combination with ipilimumab or alone in patients with mismatch repair-deficient or microsatellite instability-high (dMMR/MSI-H) mCRC that advanced following oxaliplatin, fluoropyrimidine, and irinotecan treatment. The complete response rate was 13%, the disease control rate was 84% (95% CI, 70.5 to 93.5), and the objective response rate was 69% (95% CI, 53 to 82). Positive clinical results were seen regardless of baseline demographic and tumor characteristics, including the existence of a BRAF or KRAS mutation. In the first-line treatment of dMMR/MSI-H mCRC, the nivolumab plus low-dose ipilimumab regimen demonstrated robust efficacy, favorable clinical outcomes, and excellent tolerability [94].

For up to four doses, ipilimumab is given intravenously (IV) as a 1 mg/kg dosage every 3 weeks to individuals with dMMR/MSI-H mCRC [95]. The half-life of ipilimumab plasma was found to be around 15 days [92, 93]. Immuno-related adverse effects (irAEs), or increased immune reactions against healthy tissues, are the main cause of ipilimumab side effects seen in clinical studies. Itching and Rashes, vitiligo, diarrhea and colitis, endocrine diseases affecting the pituitary, adrenal, or thyroid glands, uveitis, and hepatitis are among the most common adverse drug reactions (irAEs) [92].

The nivolumab-ipilimumab combination showed high efficacy in treating CRC, according to a meta-analysis and systematic review, evaluating the effectiveness and safety of ipilimumab, pembrolizumab, and nivolumab as monotherapies or in combination. High objective response rates (ORR) and enhanced OS and PFS served as indicators of this. Additionally, there was no discernible difference in the incidence of adverse events (AEs) between ipilimumab plus nivolumab treatment (IPI + NIVO) and pembrolizumab in combination with chemotherapy, or between pembrolizumab monotherapy and IPI + NIVO. However, NIVO + IPI is more likely to cause treatment-related grade ≥ 3 adverse events than pembrolizumab monotherapy. Even so, the heterogeneity observed in some results suggests the need for further research and investigation [96]. When used in conjunction with nivolumab and temozolomide (TMZ), the ipilimumab exhibits encouraging outcomes in the mCRC patients with stable microsatellites (MSS). Despite the fact that MSS-mCRC tumors are usually immunologically “cold” and resistant to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), the MAYA study reported a 45% response rate for MGMT-methylated MSS-mCRC patients treated with TMZ in conjunction with ipilimumab and nivolumab. Furthermore, in vitro studies show that TMZ renders MSS-CRC cells more susceptible to ipilimumab-mediated T-cell cytotoxicity, regardless of MGMT status. These results challenge the traditional limitations of ICI in this patient cohort and suggest that ipilimumab in combination with TMZ may expand therapeutic options for MSS mCRC [97].

Anti-PD-1

A checkpoint molecule involved in immune response regulation is the transmembrane receptor known as PD1 [98, 99]. PD1 binds PDL2 and programmed cell death protein one ligand 1 (PDL1). Together with PD1, they are both transmembrane proteins. Activated CD8+ and CD4+ T, B, NK cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells all express PD1. PDL1 is found on B, T, dendritic, and Tregs. INF-γ can also cause PDL1 to be expressed on epithelial cells and other non-hematopoietic cells. PDL2 expression is frequently limited to IL-4 and INF-γ activation, especially in macrophages and dendritic cells. APCs and activated T cells can provide inhibitory signals when PDL1 binds to their CD80. Regardless of PD1, PDL2 binds to RGMb and sends T cells inhibitory signals. As with CTLA-4/CD28, PDL2 and PDL1 can interact with another co-stimulatory molecule to generate T-cell signals [98]. In microsatellite instability colon cancer, PDL1 is mostly expressed on myeloid cells, including monocytes and dendritic cells. When PD1 on T-cells and PDL1 on APCs interact, T cells are transformed into induced Tregs, which subsequently inhibit the activity of cytotoxic T-cells. Because PDL1 is a transmembrane protein, reverse signaling via it allows tumor cells to survive CTL-induced death [98]. Therefore, preventing the development of the PD1: PDL1 complex is an essential immunotherapeutic cancer therapy [98, 99]. One crucial predictor and biomarker of the tumor cells response to PD1 inhibition is the expression level of PDL1. A more active pre-existing anti-tumor immunity may be indirectly demonstrated by increased PDL1 expression. PDL1 expression in cancerous cells rises as pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as INF-γ, are secreted by activated T lymphocytes as they invade the tumor. However, there are a number of challenges in using PDL1 expression as a biomarker for PDL1-blocking cancer therapy, including its uneven distribution across different tumor regions, its dynamic nature over time, and the challenge of distinguishing between surface expression, which is therapeutically significant, and intracellular expression, which is therapeutically insignificant. Additionally, the impact of PDL1 expression on myeloid cells and APCs may be unexpected [98].

Pembrolizumab

The anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody pembrolizumab (Keytruda®) (IgG4 kappa isotype) works by competitively inhibiting PDL1 binding to PD1 [98, 99]. Pembrolizumab has no cytotoxic activity because it binds to PD1 without activating complement or involving Fc receptors [98]. Pembrolizumab (200 mg every 3 weeks) or chemotherapy (5–fluorouracil–based treatment, with or without bevacizumab or cetuximab, every 2 weeks) was given to 307 patients with metastatic MSI–H–dMMR colorectal cancer in an open-label phase 3 study to evaluate the efficacy of pembrolizumab. As a first-line treatment for MSI–H–dMMR mCRC, the results demonstrated that pembrolizumab led to a considerably better progression-free survival than chemotherapy and fewer treatment-related side events [100]. Considering that PDL1 expression only happened in MMR-deficient tumors [98].

Immune-mediated skin adverse events (bullous pemphigoid, toxic epidermal necrolysis, exfoliative dermatitis and Stevens-Johnson syndrome), immune-mediated endocrinopathies (thyroid disorders hypophysitis, type 1 diabetes), immune-mediated pneumonitis, immune-mediated hepatitis/hepatotoxicity, colitis [101], elevated alanine aminotransferase, exhaustion, diarrhea [102], and neutropenia [103] are among the clinically adverse reactions of pembrolizumab. Key nonconjugated monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) used in colorectal cancer

Nivolumab

Nivolumab (Opdivo®), a highly selective, fully humanized IgG4 monoclonal antibody, inhibits Programmed Death-1 (PD1) by hindering the interaction between PD1 and its ligands (PDL1/PDL2), thereby stimulating cytokine production and T-cell proliferation [104]. According to new research, patients with dMMR/MSI-H mCRC (approximately 4–5% of cases) form a unique biomarker-defined subgroup with a poorer response to conventional chemotherapy and shorter overall survival compared to those with proficient MMR (pMMR) [105]. The assessment of MMR/MSI status is a validated biomarker for identifying patients who are likely to respond to anti-PD1 checkpoint inhibitors. Studies indicate a high concordance rate of 92% between MMR testing via IHC and MSI testing via PCR [106]. Based on the CheckMate 142 trial, the U.S. FDA approved nivolumab, either alone or combined with ipilimumab, for patients with MSI-H/dMMR metastatic colorectal cancer after failure of conventional chemotherapy with fluoropyrimidine, oxaliplatin, and irinotecan [107].The primary biomarker in the phase II CheckMate-142 trial, which examined nivolumab monotherapy or in combination with ipilimumab, was dMMR/MSI-H status. The combination arm of the trial achieved an ORR of 55% and a 12-month PFS rate of 71% [108]. Many studies evaluated the efficacy and safety of nivolumab monotherapy or combination with chemotherapy or other Immune checkpoint inhibitors.

The CheckMate 142 trial is an ongoing, multicenter, phase 2 study evaluating checkpoint inhibitors in patients with recurrent or metastatic dMMR/MSI-H colorectal cancer. Its open-label design includes multiple treatment arms, administering nivolumab as a single agent and combined with ipilimumab [104] Based on the CheckMate 142 trial, suggested that nivolumab is a promising therapeutic option for patients with previously treated dMMR/MSI-H mCRC. The study also found that the combination of nivolumab and ipilimumab was well tolerated and showed encouraging efficacy [105]. Notably, clinical benefit was observed across all patient subgroups, including those with or without Lynch syndrome and those with KRAS or BRAF mutations [106].

The CheckMate 8HW trial is a phase 3, multinational study randomizing patients with MSI-H/dMMR metastatic colorectal cancer to receive nivolumab plus ipilimumab, nivolumab alone, or standard chemotherapy [109]. Across all treatment lines, the nivolumab plus ipilimumab demonstrated superior progression-free survival over nivolumab alone with confirmed MSI-H/dMMR metastatic colorectal cancer. According to the results, median progression-free survival was not reached with nivolumab plus ipilimumab, and it was 39.3 months with nivolumab. Of note, in an updated analysis and a minimum follow-up of 16.7 months, the combination of nivolumab and ipilimumab continued to demonstrate a progression-free survival benefit over chemotherapy (54.1 months vs. 5.9 months) [110].

In a phase Ib/II trial, the combination of ONC201 and nivolumab was evaluated in patients with microsatellite stable (MSS) CRC that had progressed after standard treatment. ONC201 is a small molecule that downregulates AKT and ERK signaling pathways while simultaneously activating the TRAIL pathway. Although preclinical data suggested potential synergy between ONC201 and checkpoint inhibitors like nivolumab, the clinical trial failed to demonstrate efficacy; the combination was well-tolerated but no objective tumor responses were observed in patients with treatment-refractory MSS CRC [111]. Nivolumab is typically administered intravenously every two weeks. While landmark trials used a weight-based dose of 3 mg/kg, the current recommended fixed dose for colorectal cancer is 240 mg. Treatment continues until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity occurs [104]. The most common adverse event of nivolumab administration in solid tumors is rash and pruritus among more than 20% of patients. Other frequent events include infections and peripheral edema. Less common but clinically important reactions, such as ventricular arrhythmia, iridocyclitis, infusion-related reactions, and neuropathy, were observed in fewer than 10% of patients, often accompanied by laboratory abnormalities including elevated liver enzymes, hyponatremia, and hyperkalemia [106].

Drug-conjugated mAbs

The advent of mAbs has fundamentally changed the paradigm of oncology treatment by precisely targeting tumor-associated antigens. Nevertheless, monotherapy with mAbs often proves inadequate, possibly due to a comparatively lower cytotoxic efficacy against neoplastic cells compared to conventional chemotherapy. Therefore, A groundbreaking approach known as antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) has been created to address the incompatibility between mAbs and cytotoxic agents, thereby enhancing the therapeutic index [112]. ADCs typically consist of monoclonal antibodies that specifically target tumors and are linked to a cytotoxic agent via a carefully engineered chemical compound, allowing for precise targeting and high potency. In addition, pairing the cytotoxic agent with a sizable hydrophilic antibody reduces its non-specific absorption into cells lacking the target antigen, consequently boosting the therapeutic effectiveness of the ADC [112]. ADCs allow for directly administering high-potency drugs to cancer cells, thereby reducing collateral damage to healthy tissues and limiting systemic adverse effects. Early efforts in this field involved conjugating mAbs with conventional chemotherapy drugs like methotrexate, doxorubicin, and cisplatin. However, these first-generation ADCs faced significant challenges, including severe toxicity and limited therapeutic efficacy, leading to disappointing clinical outcomes in many patients. These shortcomings prompted the development of second-generation ADCs, which were engineered to address the limitations of earlier designs, enhancing both safety and therapeutic effectiveness [113]. Research into the factors underlying the failure of first-generation ADCs revealed several critical criteria for success. A significant requirement is the conjugated drug’s non-toxic nature at circulating plasma concentrations until it is delivered to and activated inside the target tumor cells. Furthermore, the cytotoxic payload must exhibit potency superior to that of the unconjugated antibody alone [114, 115].

Numerous ADC drugs have received approval globally for solid malignancies and hematologic cancers, and over 100 ADC candidates are presently undergoing clinical testing at different phases [112]. In the upcoming years, ADCs are predicted to supplant conventional chemotherapies as they herald in a new age of targeted cancer treatment with an expanding variety of targets and indications. The antibodies used for the production of ADCs are often IgG, with a focus on the IgG1 subclass. Off-target toxicity is minimized since the optimal antigens are broadly distributed in cancer cells and infrequently found in normal cells. Antibodies are conjugated to therapeutic payloads via innovative linkers that have various properties that can be strategically manipulated for therapeutic applications [116]. In the case of CRC, monoclonal antibodies target specific signaling pathways in tumor growth and survival, which is mentioned in the previous Section [12].

Mechanisms of action for drug-conjugated mAbs

Combining the precise targeting of monoclonal antibodies with the potent cytotoxicity of conventional chemotherapeutics, ADCs represent a revolutionary advancement in cancer treatment. ADCs have revolutionized the therapeutic paradigm by providing a potent balance between safety and efficacy in contemporary cancer treatment, explicitly delivering targeted payloads to cancer cells while preserving healthy tissues [117]. These agents work by interfering with multiple processes, including providing cytotoxic payloads to tumor cells, activating immune responses, and disrupting tumor-promoting signaling pathways [118]. Their therapeutic efficacy is enhanced through distinct yet related mechanisms, including specific antigen binding and uptake, immune-mediated cytotoxicity, and inhibition of signal transduction. Below, we present a comprehensive and integrated explanation of ADC mechanisms. Paul Ehrlich’s “magic bullet” theory, which was developed more than a century ago, served as the inspiration for the therapeutic legacy of ADCs, which represent a revolutionary approach to cancer treatment. ADCs, which are designed to be precisely directed “biological missiles,” provide oncologists with the exact tools they need to target tumor cells while minimizing harm to healthy organs. ADCs expand the therapeutic options by combining the powerful cell-killing potential of small molecules with the precise targeting of monoclonal antibodies, resulting in more effective therapy with fewer side effects [119–122]. Using a tailored delivery method, ADCs deliver powerful anticancer medications straight to cancer cells. After intravenous injection, these compounds circulate in the blood and reach tumor tissues by taking advantage of the long half-life of the monoclonal antibody. The antibody in the ADC binds specifically to antigens on the surface of cancer cells and causes the ADC-antigen complex to be taken up into the cell by the process of endocytosis. Inside the cell, this complex follows the endolysosomal pathway, and the type of bond (cleavable or noncleavable) determines where and how the drug is released. Cleavable linkers allow for drug release in the endolysosomal compartment through specific mechanisms like enzymatic cleavage, hydrolysis, or reduction, enabling diffusion to neighboring cells via the bystander effect. In contrast, non-cleavable linkers provide greater stability in circulation but require complete antibody degradation in the lysosome for payload release, limiting off-target effects. When the released payload can cross membranes or penetrate cells, it may trigger a bystander effect that boosts the effectiveness of the ADC. Additionally, this bystander effect from the drugs can modify the tumor microenvironment, potentially amplifying the ADCs’ ability to destroy cancer cells. Once released, the cytotoxic drug attacks intracellular components such as microtubules (in antimitotic drugs) or DNA (in genotoxic agents), eliminating cancer cells while causing the least amount of harm to healthy areas (Fig. 3B) [122–124].

ADCs are a revolutionary approach in cancer therapy that enables the specific delivery of drugs to cancer cells by the targeted attachment of cytotoxic agents to monoclonal antibodies, thereby minimizing side effects on healthy tissues. In addition to the medication’s direct anticancer effects, these compounds also activate various immune responses, including ADCP, ADCC, and CDC. These immune mechanisms are mediated through the Fc region of the antibody, which allows interaction with effector immune cells and the complement system [125, 126]. In some ADCs, the Fab region of the antibody is able to specifically bind to antigenic epitopes on the outside of tumor or virus-infected cells. At the same time, the Fc region of the antibody interacts with Fc receptors (FcR) on the surface of killer immune cells (including NK cells, macrophages, and other effector cells). This dual mechanism results in direct cytotoxic effects against target cells (Fig. 3C).

Furthermore, the antibody portion of ADCs is capable of acting not only as a drug carrier by specifically binding to an antigenic epitope on cancer cells, but may also block downstream signaling pathways of the receptor. This dual property can simultaneously inhibit the transduction of tumor growth signals and deliver the cytotoxic drug to cancer cells in a targeted manner (Fig. 3D). A prominent example of this mechanism is T-DM1, which binds to the HER2 receptor on cancer cells, not only releasing its cytotoxic cargo, but also disrupts key cell signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK by preventing the formation of HER2/HER1, HER2/HER3, or HER2/HER4 heterodimers. This dual interference leads to suppression of cell proliferation, reduced tumor cell survival, and ultimately induction of apoptosis [127, 128].

In the following section, we will explore ADCs in depth, focusing on their emerging applications across various cancer types to illustrate their transformative role in modern oncology.

HER2 antibody-drug conjugate

In clinical oncology, anticancer strategies targeting HER2 can selectively eliminate tumor cells while minimizing collateral damage to adjacent normal tissues. The HER2 gene’s amplification and/or overexpression can initiate a series of dysregulated cellular processes, including as aberrant differentiation, proliferation, carcinogenesis, and metastasis, mediated by interconnected signaling pathways like MAPK and PI3K/Akt and pathway activation [129]. Breast cancer, Gastric, Cholangio, Colorectal, and urothelial carcinoma are among the tumor types that exhibit HER2 amplification or aberrant expression; HER2-targeted therapies may be highly beneficial for treating these conditions [130–132]. RC48, T-DXd, and T-DM1 are three ADCs that target HER2 that have been authorized one after the other.

Individuals with metastatic breast cancer that is HER2-positive have previously had taxane and trastuzumab, either alone or in combination, can now get T-DM1, a first-in-class ADC composed of trastuzumab connected to the tubulin inhibitor DM1 via a non-cleavable linkage. T-DXd consists of three main components: the antibody trastuzumab, a cleavable linker, and the anticancer drug droxtecan, which is a topoisomerase I inhibitor. Clinical studies have confirmed that T-DXd is highly effective in individuals with advanced or metastatic HER2-positive breast cancer who have shown resistance to initial treatments, and is used as an effective treatment option in later stages of the disease [133]. Urothelial carcinoma and advanced or metastasized HER2-positive gastric cancer can be treated with RC48, an ADC made up of a humanized anti-HER2 monoclonal antibody (hertuzumab) coupled to the microtubule inhibitor monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) via a cleavable linker [119]. Preclinical research has demonstrated that in HER2-positive breast cancer and gastric cancer, RC48 has noticeably more antitumor activity than T-DM1. Consequently, it is imperative to investigate ADCs targeting HER2 in isolation and/or in combination with other therapeutic modalities for treating HER2-positive solid tumors [134].

Liu et al. evaluated the efficacy of HER2-targeting ADC RC48 as a monotherapy and combined with gemcitabine (GEM) for treating HER2-positive advanced CRC. HER2 was overexpressed in 74% of CRC patient samples, and RC48 demonstrated potent dose-dependent tumor inhibition in HER2-positive CRC cell lines (IC50: 1.55–3.79 µg/mL), inducing apoptosis by downregulating key survival proteins (C-MYC, MCL1, BCL2) and cell cycle arrest (M/G2 phase). In vivo, RC48 resulted in tumor growth inhibition (TGI) rates of 50.2% in HCT116 xenografts and 88.2% in COLO205 xenografts, with further confirmation of tumor regression and survival benefits in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models. Combination therapy with GEM significantly enhanced antiproliferative effects, reducing tumor burden and inducing apoptosis through modulation of several signaling pathways, including p53, PI3K-AKT, Foxo, and MAPK. In CDX and PDX models, RC48 + GEM achieved TGI rates of 85.8–93.4%, with decreased Ki-67 expression and increased apoptosis, while maintaining a favorable safety profile. These findings highlight RC48 as a promising therapeutic strategy for HER2-positive CRC, particularly in combination with GEM, offering a potential approach to overcoming chemotherapy resistance and warranting further clinical investigation [135].

The phase II HERB trial reported that trastuzumab deruxtecan, a HER2-targeted antibody–drug conjugate, achieved clinically meaningful antitumor activity and sustainable responses in patients with HER2-positive biliary tract cancers, including intrahepatic and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, suggesting its potential as an effective therapeutic strategy in this hard-to-treat population [136]. Comprehensive reviews of recent evidence emphasize that HER2-directed antibody–drug conjugates, particularly trastuzumab deruxtecan, represent a promising novel treatment approach for cholangiocarcinoma, providing encouraging efficacy signals and expanding the therapeutic landscape beyond conventional chemotherapy [137].

Drug-conjugated mAbs in colorectal cancer

Fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan

A drug of the ADC group, which is used in the treatment of cancers caused by the increase of HER2, is Fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan, called deruxtecan, which includes a topoisomerase I inhibitor called deruxtecan and a human monoclonal antibody against HER2 [138]. Its metabolism may be divided into two sections: CYP3A4 primarily metabolizes Dxd, whereas catabolism pathways break down the HER2 IgG1 antibody into peptides and tiny amino acids. It has a half-life of 5.7 days and a daily clearance of 0.42 L [139]. Pulmonary complications, especially interstitial lung disease, have been shown by this drug [140].

Fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan shows its antitumor effects in cancers with increased HER2 levels, such as breast, lung, ovarian, and colorectal cancer. Recent results suggesting that certain drugs, including deruxtecan, may have improved antitumor activity in cancers with compromised DNA repair pathways strengthen the case for the use of DNA-damaging ADCs in molecular subtypes with compromised DNA repair pathways. DESTINY is a series of ongoing Phase III/II studies that are being used to examine this medication. Such studies are necessary to assess the translatability from preclinical and phase I studies to proof of concept within the clinical setting. Topoisomerase I inhibitor-based ADCs in combination with immuno-oncology therapies have also shown additive effects in preclinical models. This has subsequently initiated clinical investigations powered by this combination to improve clinical outcomes [141].

A 78-year-old female with metastatic colorectal cancer who had a HER2 L726I mutation but no HER2 amplification or overexpression showed an impressive clinical response to Fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan in a recent case report. This case highlights the potential effectiveness of HER2-directed treatments even in the absence of HER2 amplification, emphasizing the importance of comprehensive genomic testing in metastatic cancers. The patient’s significant improvement in disease burden and tumor markers after treatment underscores the need for further clinical tests to explore the therapeutic potential of Fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan in patients with uncommon HER2 mutations [142]. This finding aligns with the ongoing DESTINY trials, which aim to validate the clinical benefits of HER2-targeted therapies across various cancer types, including those with rare genetic alterations. The effectiveness of Fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan in treating HER2-expressing mCRC is further supported by the DESTINY-CRC01 research, a phase II experiment. In this study, 53 patients with HER2-positive malignancies (ISH-positive and IHC2 + or IHC3+) demonstrated an objective response rate of 45.3% after a median follow-up of 27.1 weeks [143]. Based on HER2 expression, the phase II DESTINY-CRC01 trial evaluated trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd) in three cohorts. Cohort A (HER2-high) had a median PFS of 6.9 months and an ORR of 45.3%; biomarkers needed to be HER2 IHC 3 + or 2+/ISH + and RAS/RAF wild-type [144]. These results highlight the significance of HER2-targeted therapy for this patient population by demonstrating that trastuzumab deruxtecan has promising and long-lasting efficacy in HER2-positive mCRC that is resistant to standard treatments [143].

Notably, additional promising ADCs are being actively studied for treating colorectal cancer, including T-DM1 (targeting HER2) and Sacituzumab govitecan (targeting Trop-2). Due to their early-stage development and the depth of ongoing research, these agents are discussed in detail in this review’s dedicated “Future Perspectives” section. Emerging modalities: bispecific antibodies

Bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) are a novel type of therapeutic agent that recognizes two different antigens or epitopes at the same time [145]. This dual-targeting capability enables innovative mechanisms of action that go beyond traditional monoclonal antibodies, particularly in engaging the immune system to combat cancer [146]. In the setting of CRC, especially in MSS tumors, the low response to traditional immunotherapies is a significant obstacle [147]. BsAbs present a possible method of overcoming this resistance by forcefully rerouting and activating immune effector cells within the TME [148].

Mechanisms of action bispecific antibodies in CRC

The most clinically developed method of BsAb use in solid tumors is immune cell engagement, usually by binding TAAs to cancer cells and CD3 to T cells [149]. Consequently, polyclonal T-cells that are activated by this immunologic synaptic connection destroy tumor cells with potent, focused lysis [150]. Cibisatamab (CEA-TCB, RO6958688) is a BsAb that targets CD3 and Carcinoembryonic Antigen (CEA), a glycoprotein that is considerably excessively expressed in colorectal cancer. It is a prime example pertinent to CRC [151]. Its particular 2:1 structure (monovalent for CD3, bivalently for CEA) allows T-cells to crosslink to tumor cells in a steady manner, showing a strong anticancer effect in preclinical models [152]. A phase I clinical trial of NCT02324257 and another with NCT02650713 both demonstrated antitumor activity when administered to patients with advanced solid tumors with CEA+, including CRC. However, they also brought to light issues like cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and on-target, off-tumor toxicity because of low-level CEA expression in normal tissues [153, 154]. The second mechanism, called Tumor-Associated Antigen Targeting, does not directly engage immune cells, unlike the aforementioned mechanism. Rather, these bispecific antibodies block signals essential for tumor proliferation and survival by simultaneously targeting two distinct epitopes on the surface of cancer cells. As a result, this approach is able to more powerfully hinder signaling pathways, something that a single-specific antibody alone cannot do [155, 156].

Zenocutuzumab (MCLA-128) serves as an example of this mechanism. This bispecific antibody, by simultaneously binding to HER2 and HER3 receptors, prevents dimer formation (pairing) and activation of downstream signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT. Although this drug is not specific to colorectal cancer, its main mechanism conceptually demonstrates dual targeting of tumor antigens [157]. Other BsAb methods outside of T-cell engagers are being investigated. Compared to the combined use of single antibodies, bispecific antibodies such as cadonilimab (AK104), which simultaneously bind to PD1 and CTLA-4, block two immune inhibitory pathways simultaneously. This approach can result in more efficient immune activation and a more favorable safety-efficacy profile (therapeutic index) [158, 159]. The next mechanism is Payload Delivery, in which bispecific antibodies act as smart carriers. For example, the triple antibody TF2 simultaneously targets the CEA antigen on cancer cells and a hapten (HSG). This feature enables its application in a pre-targeting approach for radioimmunotherapy and diagnostic imaging (immuno-PET) in colorectal cancer, creating a promising theranostic platform [160, 161].

For colorectal cancer, BsAbs employing the immune cell engagement mechanism (e.g., anti-CEAxCD3) hold significant potential to overcome the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and elicit responses in traditionally immunotherapy-resistant, microsatellite stable (MSS) CRC.

Challenges and limitations

Despite notable advances in CRC treatment, fundamental challenges such as drug resistance remain a primary obstacle to optimizing therapeutic efficacy. One major limitation to the effectiveness of anti-EGFR treatments, such as cetuximab and panitumumab, is inherent and acquired resistance. This resistance is often driven by mutations in KRAS, most commonly present in codons 12 and 13 and rarely 61/146, which cause constitutive activation of RAS proteins. As KRAS is a pivotal downstream effector in the EGFR signaling pathway, hindering the upstream receptor is ineffective at stopping the downstream signaling caused by the mutant RAS protein, rendering anti-EGFR monoclonal antibodies unsuccessful in patients with KRAS-mutant CRC [162, 163]. Furthermore, patients with initial wild-type KRAS tumors may develop secondary resistance due to tumor heterogeneity or acquired mutations during therapy [164, 165].

Beyond KRAS, tumors with mutations in NRAS, BRAF, or PIK3CA/PTEN also frequently exhibit resistance, and anti-EGFR therapy is primarily beneficial for patients with a wild-type RAS status [162, 163]. Additional genetic mechanisms confer resistance, including amplifications of BRAF, HER2, and MET and mutations in PIK3CA/PTEN, which trigger bypass signaling pathways that circumvent EGFR blockade [166–168]. From a receptor-centric perspective, resistance to anti-EGFR therapies is further explained by secondary mutations (e.g., EGFR T790M/C797S) and compensatory signaling through pathways such as PI3K/AKT and RhoA-mTOR; counter-strategies being explored include HER2/EGFR depletion via HSP70-mediated lysosomal degradation or the use of biparatopic antibodies to restore sensitivity [169].

Additional avenues of escape for CRC cells are provided by the upregulation of HER3 and the stimulation of alternative growth factor receptors, such as IGF1R [170]. strategies for dealing with resistance include dual blockade of HER2 with antibodies (trastuzumab + pertuzumab) and ADCs (T-DM1, Fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan) for HER2-amplified CRC, or combining anti-EGFR agents with inhibitors of other pathways (e.g., BRAF or MEK inhibitors, particularly in BRAF-mutant CRC) [171, 172]. Additionally, anti-EGFR antibodies can be paired with anti-VEGF therapies like bevacizumab and ramucirumab or with conventional chemotherapy regimens like FOLFIRI and FOLFOX to simultaneously target multiple systems and boost tumor control [173, 174]. New strategies involve examining ADCs consisting of sacituzumab govitecan (Trop-2 targeting) or HER3-targeted ADCs (patritumab deruxtecan) that may circumvent resistance related to upstream signaling, as well as bispecific antibodies (e.g., zanidatamab targeting two HER2 epitopes) for resistant tumors [175–177]. However, the efficacy of ADCs can be modest in broader or refractory CRC populations, as highlighted by the phase I/II IMMU-132-01 trial where sacituzumab govitecan (SG), a Trop-2-targeting ADC, produced an objective response rate (ORR) of only 11% in substantially pretreated metastatic CRC patients, underscoring enduring issues like antigen loss, impaired internalization, and efflux-mediated payload resistance that limit ADC durability [178].

The challenges of antibody-based therapies extend beyond specific resistance mechanisms to more general limitations. While antibodies are cornerstone treatments, they face issues such as high production costs, limited half-life, and the potential to trigger immune-related adverse reactions or bind to healthy cells expressing the target antigen, leading to toxicity. Furthermore, resistance can occur due to the downregulation or loss of the target antigen on cancer cells. ADCs, which conjugate cytotoxic drugs to antibodies, introduce additional complexities. The expression levels of target antigens vary between patients, necessitating a personalized medicine approach to maximize effectiveness. The large size of ADCs compared to free drugs can reduce their extravasation from vessels and lead to improper distribution within tumor tissue, a problem exacerbated by impediments like the binding-site barrier and the blood-tumor barrier [179]. The effectiveness of an ADC is also influenced by the drug-to-antibody ratio and the specific binding site on the antibody [180]. In relation to this, another challenging issue is the limited types of conjugation chemistries and linkers available to enable the controlled attachment of a well-defined number of therapeutic agents to specific positions on antibodies [181]. The increased clearance caused by the high hydrophobicity of some drug-antibody combinations [182], and the critical need to control the level of unconjugated antibodies post-production, as these can compete with ADCs for antigen binding and reduce overall drug efficacy [183]. Resistance to ADCs has also been observed, where cancer cells increase resistance by removing surface antigens or coating them with substances like mucin or hyaluronan [184]. To overcome this, novel strategies such as raising replication stress with CHK1 inhibition or targeting PARG to cause excessive PARylation and DNA lesions are being explored; this DDR-stress approach synergizes with DNA-damaging payloads (e.g., topoisomerase inhibitors in ADCs like sacituzumab govitecan) in preclinical models, showing potential to restore sensitivity in resistant cells [185].

The monitoring and management of drug resistance increasingly rely on advanced diagnostics. Liquid biopsy, particularly the identification of new resistance mutations (e.g., in KRAS, BRAF) by circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analysis, permits real-time monitoring and prompt therapy changes, while thorough genetic profiling (e.g., of KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, HER2, MET, PIK3CA, PTEN, and EGFR copy number) enables optimal therapy selection [186–188]. Similary, new biomarker tests may improve CRC post-treatment surveillance plans. In detecting recurrence in HPV-related cancers, a recent proof-of-concept study on high-risk HPV (HPV-HR) DNA testing showed better sensitivity (92.31%) and negative predictive value (97.44%) than PET/CT imaging, indicating that it is a cost-effective substitute to cut down on needless imaging, expenses, and radiation exposure. This strategy should be investigated in HPV-associated CRC subtypes (such as anal squamous cell carcinoma), even though it has been primarily tested in cervical/vaginal cancers. It can be used with ctDNA monitoring to enhance future surveillance programs and customize follow-up care [189]. As predictors of response or resistance, functional indicators such as protein expression, pathway activation status, and characteristics of the tumor immune microenvironment are also being actively studied [190, 191].

Looking forward, research is concentrating on advanced drug delivery systems like PLGA-conjugated nanocarriers to enhance payload delivery and circumvent resistance [192, 193]. A recent preclinical study on a nanoscale fluoropyrimidine prodrug (FdUMP) demonstrated superior antitumor activity against CRC liver metastases compared to conventional 5-FU, achieving significant tumor regression with minimal systemic toxicity and supporting its integration as a refined backbone for combinations with ADCs or monoclonal antibodies [194]. Future plans also involve the continued development of multi-specific antibodies and next-generation ADCs. Although many of these approaches are still in phase I/II trials and not yet common clinical practice, they offer encouraging avenues for CRC treatment. Ultimately, addressing drug resistance in CRC, especially resistance to anti-EGFR therapy, will require a multifaceted strategy integrating precision diagnostics, novel drug modalities, rational combinatorial therapies, and personalized medicine, all underpinned by ongoing research into molecular mechanisms and thorough clinical evaluation.

Future perspectives

To address the limitations of current treatments, ongoing research is focused on developing next-generation therapies, many of which are in the nascent stages of clinical investigation. These emerging strategies, which are currently supported by preliminary data from early-phase or smaller-scale studies and have not yet entered standard clinical practice, nonetheless signal a promising new frontier in CRC treatment. A primary focus of new research is to overcome established mechanisms of drug resistance. For instance, overexpression of HER3 has been identified as a way tumors can evade EGFR-targeted therapies. To counter this, novel ADCs like patritumab deruxtecan (HER3-DXd) are being developed [195]. This agent, which targets HER3 explicitly, is currently in early-stage clinical trials. Preliminary findings suggest it may offer a new therapeutic avenue for patients with resistant tumors, though larger, more definitive studies are required to confirm its efficacy and safety profile. Innovation in antibody engineering is giving rise to novel formats with unique therapeutic potential. Bispecific antibodies, which can simultaneously bind to two different targets, are a prime example. Zanidantamab, a bispecific antibody that targets two distinct epitopes on the HER2 protein, has shown encouraging preliminary results in other HER2-expressing solid tumors. Its evaluation in CRC is still in the early phases, but it represents a promising strategy to achieve a more comprehensive blockade of oncogenic signaling pathways [196]. Beyond drugs themselves, research into advanced delivery systems aims to enhance targeting further and reduce toxicity. Technologies such as poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA)-conjugated antibody nanocarriers are being explored in preclinical and translational settings [197]. These platforms offer a biodegradable and the biocompatible method for achieving controlled, sustained release of therapeutic payloads directly at the tumor site [192]. While this research is still foundational, it holds the potential to refine the precision of antibody-based therapies in CRC significantly.

In addition to direct targeting techniques, newer tactics such as viral cytokine delivery present encouraging paths to enhance ICI and antibody responses in colorectal cancer. A recent review highlights gene-delivered IL-2 via oncolytic viruses (e.g., vaccinia virus vvDD-IL2-RG), which enhances intratumoral T and NK cell activity, increases IFN-γ and granzyme B levels, and synergizes with mAbs/ICIs like anti-PD1 to achieve up to 90% tumor eradication in preclinical colon cancer models while limiting systemic toxicities such as capillary leak syndrome through localized expression. Membrane-bound IL2 variants further improve safety by reducing serum leakage, suggesting potential for combination with nonconjugated mAbs (e.g., cetuximab) or ADCs to overcome immunosuppressive TMEs in MSS CRC [198]. Furthermore, ADCs hold particular promise for addressing the unmet need in MSS (microsatellite stable) CRC, a population that typically responds poorly to conventional immunotherapies. By targeting specific surface antigens (e.g., Trop-2, HER2), ADCs can exert a potent immune-independent mechanism of action, offering a viable therapeutic strategy for this challenging patient subgroup [199].

The progression of these and other early-stage concepts into rigorous clinical trials will be critical to fully realizing their potential and continuing to improve outcomes for patients. To treat solid tumors, the first antibody-directed chemotherapy to be licensed is Ado-Trastuzumab emtansine, an antibody-conjugated medication that contains anti-HER2 human IgG. A stable thioether linker covalently binds this medication to approximately 3.6 microtubule inhibitors, including DM1, a derivative of maytansine [200]. Emtansine, also known as DM1, exhibits an antitumor effect by interacting with tubulin and hindering microtubule assembly [201]. As discussed, trastuzumab interferes with HER2-related activities. By binding to the HER2 ectodomain, this drug disrupts the activities of HER2/HER3 in cancer cells [202]. Cell cycle arrest brought on by trastuzumab happens during the G1 phase [203]. Additionally, trastuzumab buildup in HER2 + tissues draws NK cells, initiating the lysis of cancer cells bound to mAb [204]. The entire family of ADC treatments could benefit from a deeper understanding of the processes that drive off-target toxicity caused by T-DM1. However, the preclinical research is not sufficient to predict the full range of possible toxicities; these studies may provide invaluable information to elucidate the mechanisms responsible for major adverse events [205]. Certain research indicates that T-DM1 hinders megakaryocyte differentiation via a pathway that does not rely on HER2 but depends on Fcγ RIIa. This is supported by evidence showing that the Fcγ RIIa receptor-blocking antibody, anti-CD32, prevents T-DM1 from binding to the cell surface and being internalized. (130). Hepatotoxicity, which can result in considerable elevations of liver enzymes, such as aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT), is a known adverse reaction for T-DM1 [206].

Clinical trials indicate that T-DM1 has greater effectiveness and safety advantages over conventional chemotherapy due to its antibody-mediated targeting. Clinical trials have also proven its efficacy in treating HER2 + patient malignancies previously receiving trastuzumab treatment. For instance, the T-DM1/pertuzumab combination may prevent HER3 ligand activation. substantial therapeutic benefit accompanied by a certain safety profile has only been exhibited for the first ASC (T-DM1) at the advent of an exciting new era in ADC development. This strategy could lead to the design of additional tumor-specific antigen-targeting ADCs for other cancer types [207]. A 2020 study by Sandhu et al. provides evidence for T-DM1’s effectiveness in a specific metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) case. The patient, who had a KRAS mutation and HER2 amplification and had progressed on standard therapies, showed significant clinical improvement and tumor shrinkage after starting T-DM1. However, the disease eventually progressed in the liver after six cycles. Analysis suggested that this resistance was linked to a decrease in HER2 amplification, indicating the emergence of clones that no longer expressed the drug’s target [208]. Sacituzumab govitecan is a novel ADC created by linking the humanized monoclonal antibody hRS7 IgG1k, which binds to the human trophoblast cell-surface antigen 2 (Trop-2), with SN-38, the active form of the chemotherapy drug irinotecan, a topoisomerase I inhibitor [178]. Many epithelial malignancies, including breast cancer, overexpress human Trop-2, a transmembrane protein that transduces calcium signals. In comparison to the comparable healthy tissue, Trop-2 is involved in the regulation of several intracellular signaling pathways, including those related to cancer cell migration and proliferation [209]. Trop 2, which is present in many epithelial malignancies, is the target of sacituzumab [210]. The safety and effectiveness of this innovative ADC are encouragingly demonstrated by the outcomes of the NCT01631552 Phase I/II clinical study of sacituzumab govitecan (IMMU-132) in patients with GI malignancies, such as GC, esophageal (EAC), pancreatic (PC), and CRC.

In a clinical study, Sacituzumab govitecan (IMMU-132) demonstrated promising anticancer activity in patients with treatment-resistant colorectal cancer. Evidence included significant tumor shrinkage in two patients and a prolonged time to progression in another. The therapy also exhibited a manageable safety profile with primarily mild to moderate side effects. While these results suggest it is a potential treatment option for advanced disease, further research is necessary to confirm its efficacy [211].

Conclusion

Unconjugated monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) have fundamentally altered the therapeutic landscape for colorectal cancer (CRC), establishing a new paradigm in targeted oncology. Unconjugated mAbs like bevacizumab and cetuximab have proven effective by inhibiting critical tumor growth pathways, such as those mediated by VEGF and EGFR, while also leveraging the immune system through mechanisms like antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC). Complementing this, ADCs such as trastuzumab deruxtecan and sacituzumab govitecan represent a significant advancement, enabling the direct delivery of potent cytotoxic agents to cancer cells, thereby maximizing therapeutic efficacy and minimizing systemic toxicity.

Clinical trials have validated the success of these approaches, particularly in patient populations with specific biomarkers like HER2-positive or Trop-2-expressing tumors, offering tangible improvements in disease control and overall survival even in heavily pretreated patients. However, significant challenges persist. The clinical utility of these agents is often limited by the development of drug resistance, driven by mechanisms such as KRAS gene mutations or downregulation of target antigens like HER2. Furthermore, tumor heterogeneity and the management of treatment-related adverse events, including neutropenia and hepatotoxicity, remain critical hurdles that underscore the need for continued research and therapeutic innovation.
