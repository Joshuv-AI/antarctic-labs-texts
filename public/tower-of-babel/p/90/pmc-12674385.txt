Integrated analysis of intestinal microbial community and muscle transcriptome profile in rabbits
Authors: Shengbo Meng, Shanshan Xing, Huifen Xu, Jing Li, Yixuan Jiang, Hui He, Hanfang Cai, Ming Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntestinal microbial community plays an important part in maintaining health and skeletal muscle development in livestock. This study is the first of its kind in the world. In order to better understand the relationship between gut microbiota and gene expression in skeletal muscle of rabbits, caecum contents and longissimus dorsi tissues of rabbits at 0 d (S1), 35 d (S2) and 70d (S3) were collected and subjected for 16S rRNA sequencing and transcriptome sequencing. Our results showed that, among three groups of rabbits, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were the dominant phyla at the phylum level, while Akmansia, Bacteroides and Ruminobacter were the dominant genera at the genus level, and the relative abundance of Akmansia and Bacteroides increased firstly and then decreased from 0 d to 70 d. By analyzing the transcriptome sequencing data, we identified 2866, 2446 and 4541 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in S1 vs S2, S2 vs S3 and S1 vs S3 groups, respectively. Finally, we performed correlation analysis between gut microbiota and the expression levels of muscle development-related genes of rabbits at 0 d and 70 d. Compared with 0 day old rabbits, in 70 day old rabbits Acinetobacter and Cronbacter with decreased abundance, and Ruminococcaceae_UCG-014 and Ruminococcus_1 with increase abundance is beneficial to caecum health in rabbits. These results will lay a foundation for further re-searches about the relationship between caecum microflora and muscle development in rabbits.Keywords: Microbiome, transcriptome, gene–microorganism interactions, rabbits, muscle

Introduction

There is a mass of microbial communities colonized in mammalian intestine. The intestinal microflora of domestic rabbits is composed of different microorganisms including bacteria, archaea and fungi. Among them, bacteria are the most representative type.1 The intestinal flora of rabbits changes a lot throughout their life span from birth to adulthood and is influenced by various factors (diet, age, host phylogeny, intestinal morphology and so on).2 In 2016, Li et al. explored the relationship between the gut microbiota and diet and environmental microbes in Rattus norvegicus.3,4 In 2017, Li et al. discovered the relationship between intestinal microbial composition and host phylogeny.5 In 2018, Velasco-Galilea M investigated microbiota changes throughout the rabbit gut, including primarily caecum and faecal microbiota.6 In 2022, Liu et al. found that alfalfa powder may affect the health of the body by changing the intestinal flora of rabbits, thereby improving meat quality.7 Some researchers have found that the addition of low-nicotine tobacco changed the microbial composition of rabbit caecum and regulated the metabolic pathway of microbial metabolism.8 This provides an alternative strategy for improving rabbit health.

Skeletal muscle accounts for 40% of the total body weight, and has an effect on economic value health of animals.9,10 In humans, the gut microbiota can influence host health by co-evolving with the host and meet mutually beneficial biochemical and biological needs.11 Also, malnutrition and metabolites can affect the intestinal microbiome by affecting muscle metabolism in mice and rats.12,13 Few studies indicated that the intake of probiotics brought about changes in skeletal muscle development and metabolism in mouse.14 In 2013, Bindels firstly pointed out the possible existence of a gut microbiota-muscle axis in animals.15 Subsequently, studies showed that restoration of symbiotic Escherichia coli is effective in preventing skeletal muscle atrophy in a mouse model of chronic intestinal inflammation.16 Yan et al. demonstrated that intestinal microbes could transfer the fibrous characteristics and lipid metabolism characteristics of skeletal muscle from pigs to germ-free mice, which provided new evidence for the existence of intestinal microbiome-muscle axis.17 Subsequent studies have also shown that gut microbiota played a role in controlling the size, quality and function of skeletal muscle.18,19 Recently, Ren et al.20 confirmed that intestinal flora plays an important role in the regulation of human muscle metabolism, and confirmed the existence of muscle axis from a clinical perspective for the first time. However, the interaction between the gut microbiota and skeletal muscle development in rabbits is still unknown.

Moreover, the epigenome comprising different mechanisms e.g., DNA methylation, chromatin remodelling, histone tail modifications, chromatin microRNAs and long non-coding RNAs, interact with environmental factors like nutrition, pathogens, climate to influence the expression profile of genes and the emergence of specific phenotypes.21–23 Multi-level interactions between the genome, epigenome and environmental factors might occur.24,25 Furthermore, numerous lines of evidence suggest the influence of epigenome variation on health and production.26–28 The expression of eukaryotic genes is temporarily and multidimensionally controlled.29 One of the basic activities in domestic animals is the study of genes and proteins related to economic traits and their study at the cellular or chromosomal level.30 This suggests that differences in the surface epigenome can affect the health and productivity of the organism.

Herein, the present study is aimed to explore the regulatory mechanism of muscle growth and development by caecum microbiota. We compared the gene expression of longissimus dorsi and intestinal microflora community of caecum content among different ages (0, 35 and 70 d) of rabbits by RNA-seq sequencing and 16S rRNA sequencing, respectively. And the correlation between caecum microbiota abundance and expression level of muscle development-related genes was revealed. Our results will make a foundation for further studies about the mechanism of intestinal microorganisms on animal meat performance.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and sample collection

A total of nine healthy female New Zealand White rabbits at 0 d (S1), 35 d (S2), and 70 d (S3) were randomly selected. (0 day is a day of birth, 35 days is the weaning period, 70 days is the slaughter period.) These rabbits were provided by Henan Chunying Biotechnology Science and Technology Co., Ltd (Zhengzhou, China). New Zealand white rabbits were raised in an open double-corridor commercial farm with good temperature control and ventilation devices. The feed used in this experiment is a rabbit-specific pellet feed prepared by the company, and its composition and nutritional level are shown in Table S1. Rabbits were fed twice a day. During the feeding period, they were free to eat and drink water without receiving antibiotics and anticoccidial drugs. Before slaughter, all rabbits were fasted for 24 h with free access to water. After slaughter, we immediately collected about 1 g of caecum content from 5 cm far away from the end of the ileum, and collected the longissimus dorsi on both sides of the spine from the third to the seventh rib and stored in liquid nitrogen until use.

16S rRNA extraction and sequencing

In order to detect the microbial flora changes among different stages of rabbits, microbial genomic DNA was first extracted from caecum contents using HiPure Stool DNA Kits (faecal DNA extraction kits) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The V3–V4 region of 16S rRNA was amplified using bar code specific primers with di-luted genomic DNA as template.31 The sequence of primers are as follows: 341F: (CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG); 806R: (GGACTACHVGGGTATCTAAT). The reaction was consisted of 30 cycles with a total reaction volume of 50 µL, and the PCR (polymerase chain reaction) products was purified using Agencourt AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). The final DNA libraries were sequenced on the HiSeq-2500 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).32,33

Analysis of 16S rRNA gene sequencing data

Firstly, primers and joint sequences were removed from the original FASTQ file, and then the clean reads were merged into original tags by FLSAH (version 1.2.11).34 Clean tags were searched against the reference database using the UCHIME algorithm.35 All chimeric labels were removed, and the valid labels obtained were used for further analysis. Valid labels were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) with ≥97% similarity using USEARCH (version 9.2.64).36 The label sequence with the highest abundance was selected as the representative sequence within each cluster. Intergroup VENN analysis was performed with the R language Venn Diagram package (version 1.6.16) to identify common and unique OTUs.37

Microbial diversity and functional prediction analysis

The community diversity was evaluated by Chao1, ACE, Shannon and Simpson diversity indices at intergroup levels. The diversity index was calculated using QIIME (version 1.9.1), and then the data visualization was plotted using the R package ggplot2.38 Finally, the Tukey HSD test was used to draw the box plot of α diversity index, and the difference between each two groups was visually displayed. We evaluated the adequacy of the sequencing volume which can indirectly reflect the species richness of the samples by plotting Shannon dilution curves for each index. Different groups were analysed using LEfSe software (version 1.0).39 The Bray–Curtis distance matrix and the weighted UniFrac distance matrix were used to calculate the β diversity and visualized in the form of principal coordinate analysis (PCA) using the R language ggplot2 package.40 The possible microflora function was predicted by annotating OTUS paths in Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database using Tax4Fun.41 Using SPSS, test of significance was performed using one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s test for multiple comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant when probability value (p) <0.05.

RNA extraction, cDNA library construction and Illumina sequencing

Trizol was used to extract the total RNA from the longissimus dorsi of rabbits. The first strand of cDNA was synthesized in the M-MuLV reverse transcriptase system with random oligonucleotides as primers. Then the RNA strand was degraded by RNaseH, and the second strand of cDNA was synthesized. The purified double-stranded cDNA was subjected for terminal repair. A tail and the sequencing adapter were added. About 200 bp cDNA was screened by AMPure XP beads and amplified. PCR products were purified by AMPure XP beads and the library was finally obtained. The library preparation was sequenced on Illumina Hiseq 2500 platform.

RNA-seq data analyses

In order to ensure data quality, we first use FASTP40 to perform quality control on raw reads and filter low-quality data, and finally obtain clean reads. Clean reads were mapped to rabbit ribosome database using the short reads comparison tool Bow-tie2,42 and the reserved unmapped reads were used for subsequent transcriptome analysis. HISAT2 software was used for comparative analysis of reference genomes. Stringtie was used to reconstruct the transcripts based on the HISAT2 comparison results and RSEM was used to calculate the expression levels of all genes in each sample.43 Based on the expression information, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to investigate the distance relationship among different samples using the idea of dimensionality reduction with R package. The EdgeR and DESeq2 software were used to analyse the differential expression the three groups,44 p value and FDR (false discovery rate) value were then calculated. Genes with FDR < 0.05 and |log2 FC| > 1 were identified as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and KEGG pathway analysis were used to forecast the main biology and molecular features of DEGs.

Statistical analysis

Pearson algorithm was used to calculate the correlation between microbial abundance and differential gene expression, and the value was between −1 and 1, with statistical differences only between groups. The p values were corrected using FDR, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

OTU clustering and taxonomy analysis

After quality screening, 968,875 valid tags were obtained (Table S2), and a total of 1,142,798 pairs of readings were obtained from the 16S rRNA gene sequencing. After sequence filtering and chimera removal, 1,135,493 high quality reads were obtained. All valid contigs were delineated into OTUs using 97% sequence similarly thresholds, 605, 711 and 882 OTUs were obtained from S1, S2 and S3, respectively. A total of 1203 OTUs were identified, including 341 core OTUs which were present in all three groups. In addition, 159, 128 and 262 unique OTUs were found in S1, S2 and S3, respectively (Fig. S1). The saturation curves shown in the Shannon–Wiener curves indicated that each sample has a coverage of more than 90% (Fig. 1a), suggesting that the sequencing depth is sufficient for diversity assessment. PCA showed that the samples in the group are gathered together, but the samples between the groups are far apart (Figs. 1b and S2). S1 were far away from the other two groups, indicating that the gut microbial structure of the rabbits at 0 d was significantly different from rabbits at 35 and 70 d. According to the data in Table S3, we calculated different indices to analyse the alpha diversity of samples. As shown in Fig. 1c, Chao1 and ACE indices differed between each two of the groups, and the difference between S1 and S3 was the greatest (p < 0.01). However, Simpson index and Shannon index were not significantly different among them (p > 0.05).

Overview of intestinal microflora of three different ages of rabbits. (a) Shannon–Wiener curves, principal coordinate analysis (PCA) performed based on the mean-centred values of unweighted UniFrac matrix (b) and alpha diversity index (c) of three groups of rabbits.

Comparison of intestinal microbiota diversities of different ages of rabbits

At phylum level, Firmicutes was the dominant flora among the three groups (Fig. 2a), which had high proportion in S2 (41.73%) and S3 (66.95%). Followed by Bacteroidetes, they accounted for 7.40%, 34.74% and 15.67% in S1, S2 and S3 group, respectively. At the genus level, the dominant bacteria among three groups were Acinetobacter (25.76%), Akkermansia (19.91%) and Bacteroides (16.71%), respectively. The relative abundance of Akkermansia and Ruminococcaceae_NK4A214 in S3 group were 12.63% and 11.84%, respectively, while the relative abundance of Bacteroides in S1, S2 and S3 groups were 16.71%, 2.16% and 2.54%, respectively. Ruminococcaceae_NK4A214_group accounted for 11.84% in S3 group, but it accounted for less than 5% in the other two groups (Fig. 2b).

As shown in Fig. 2c, a total of 20 biomarkers with statistical differences were identified by LEfSe analysis. At the phylum level, relative abundance of Actinobacteria and Cyanobacteria were significantly different among the three groups. The relative abundance of Bacteroidetes in S2 group was higher than that in S1 and S3 group. At the genus level, when compared S1 with each of the other groups, Acinetobacter was the top different genus. The relative abundance of DNF00809, Hydrogenoanaerobacterium and Ruminococcaceae_UCG-005 were the highest in S3 group.

In order to assess potential functional changes in the microbiota, we used the PICRUSt and KEGG databases to predict metabolic pathways and compare the differences in microbial functions. Compared with S1 group, pathways of carbohydrate metabolism, cofactors and vitamin metabolism, and amino acid metabolism were enriched in S2 and S3 groups of rabbits. The abundance of bacteria related to terpenoids, polyketones metabolism, and energy metabolism in S1 was significantly less than those in S2 and S3 (p < 0.05). There was only one metabolic pathway, digestive system was enriched when compared S2 with S3 groups (Fig. S3, Welch’s T test, p < 0.05).

Differential gene expression in longissimus dorsi of different ages of rabbits

The longissimus dorsi of rabbits was collected and used for transcriptome sequencing. In general, a total of 8,754,496,467 raw reads and 8,641,605,000 clean reads were obtained. GC content was 56.22% and Q30 was 95.40% (Table S4). The results of PCA showed that the samples within the group were relatively concentrated, and the samples in different group were highly dispersed (Fig. 3a). Based on RNA-seq data, 8809, 7675 and 7347 genes were detected in S1, S2 and S3 groups, respectively (Fig. 3b). When compared to S1, 2866 DEGs were identified in S2, including 493 up-regulated genes and 2373 down-regulated genes. When compared to S2, 2446 DEGs were identified in S3, including 1104 up-regulated genes and 1342 down-regulated genes. When compared to S1, 4541 DEGs were identified in S3, including 1001 up-regulated genes and 3540 down-regulated genes (Fig. 3c). The difference of transcriptional changes between S1 and S3 were the greatest.

KEGG enrichment analysis showed that signalling pathways related to muscle growth and development, such as ECM–receptor interaction, PI3K-Akt signalling pathway, MAPK signalling pathway, and regulation of actin cytoskeleton were significantly enriched in the DEGs when compared S1 with S3 (Fig. 3d). For the DEGs in S1 vs. S2 or S2 vs. S3, Focal adhesion, FoxO signalling pathway, PI3K-Akt signalling pathway and regulation of actin cytoskeleton were enriched (Fig. S4a,b). Also, we found that the main pathways involved in the biological process category were cellular process, single-organism process and metabolic process by GO annotation. ‘Myofibril’, ‘microtubule cytoskeleton’ and ‘myosin complex’ were significantly enriched in the Molecular function. ‘Actin filament-based process’ and ‘develop-mental process’ were significantly enriched in the category of biological processes, and ‘myosin binding’ and ‘actin binding’ were significantly enriched in the category of molecular functions (Figs. 3e and S4c,d). Then, based on these differential GO annotations and KEGG pathways, highly expressed genes including CDKN1A, HSPA8, COL1A1, MYL2, CACNG1, and MEF2C were eventually screened in S1 and S3 groups, implying that they may play a key regulatory role in muscle development of rabbits.

To verify the accuracy of transcriptome sequencing results, RT-qPCR (quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction) was carried out to detect the expression level of five DEGs, which were randomly selected in the three groups. As shown in Fig. 3g, compared to S1 and S2, MYOC and IGFN1 gene were significantly up-regulated in S3 group (p < 0.001), while MYH3, ACTC1 and FBN2 genes were significantly down-regulated in both of S2 and S3 (p < 0.001). The qRT-PCR (quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction) results were consistent with the sequencing data (Fig. 3f,g), indicating that the sequencing data were accurate and reliable.

Relationship between DEGs in longissimus dorsi and intestinal flora

Correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationship between intestinal flora and muscle development-related genes in S1 vs. S3 group. As shown in Fig. 4a, the relative abundance of Acinetobacter and Subdoligranulum decreased with age, whereas the relative abundance of Ruminococcus_UCG-014 and Ruminococcus_1 increased with age. We visualized the expression pattern of four (two up-regulated and two down-regulated) randomly selected differentially expressed representative genes, from genes included in the muscle development-related pathways (Fig. 4b). When compared to S1, the expression levels of CACNG1 and HSPA8 increased in S3, but COL1A1 and MYL2 showed a downward trend. Using Pearson correlation, we found that there were 19 significant gene–microorganism correlations in the intestine (Table S5).

We also visualized the correlation between bacterial abundance and longissimus dorsi gene expression (Fig. 4c). There was significant positive correlation between Ruminococcaceae_NK4A214_group and MYLK2 (r = 0.94), Ruminococcaceae_UCG-014 and HSPA8 (r = 0.94) (p < 0.01). Ruminococcaceae_NK4A214_group and Ruminococcus_1 were negatively correlated with COL4A1 (r = −0.94) and HSPB1 (r = −0.94) (p < 0.01), respectively. Based on Fig. 4c, we noticed that these microorganisms had certain correlation with various muscle development-related genes, such as Acinetobacter and Subdoligranulum, which were significantly positively correlated with muscle development-related genes (p < 0.05). However, bacteria such as Ruminococcace_UCG-014, Ru-minococcus_1, Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group were significantly negatively correlated with those genes (p < 0 .05). It is worth noting that there are completely positive correlations among several bacteria and DEGs (r = 1), such as the correlation between Acinetobacter and HSPB1, Subdoligranulum and COL4A1 and Subdoligranulum was completely negatively correlated with MYLK2 (r = −1).

Discussion

Composition and functional analysis of caecum microflora of rabbits at different ages

Consistent with previous study, our study reconfirmed that Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were the dominant phyla in the caecum microbiota of rabbits.45 Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes are reported to be tightly involved in fat deposition and storage.46,47 Rabbits before weaning are mainly in the stage of muscle growth and development, and adult rabbits (from weaning to marketing age) are mainly in the stage of fat deposition and hypertrophy. Our results showed that Cyanobacteria were hardly detected in S2 (35 d) and S3 (70 d) group, but the relative abundance of Cyanobacteria was as high as 29.08% in S1 (0 d) group. Cyanobacteria plays an important role in obligate anaerobic fermentation and the synthesis of vitamin B and K.48 Such a high abundance of Cyanobacteria in new-born rabbits (S1) is speculated to be conducive for improving the immunity of young rabbits. Proteobacteria showed the highest abundance in new-born rabbits and decreased with the increase of age, this is consistent with the previous results, which showed that Proteobacteria had the highest abundance in piglets and began to decrease after 1 month of birth.49

From birth to weaning and then adult stage, the relative abundance of Akkermansia and Bacteroides increased firstly and then decreased, these two kinds of species are mainly involved in vitamin metabolism, such as biotin and retinol.50,51 It is reported that Akkermansia is tightly related to intestinal inflammation and the relative abundance of Akkermansia in the intestinal tract of mice increased under low levels of inflammation.52 Therefore, high abundance of Akkermansia can help decrease the occurrence of inflammation in rabbits. Bacteroides is an anaerobic gram-negative bacterium that play an important role in the degradation of plant polysaccharides and amino acid fermentation.6 Functional enrichment analysis showed that these bacteria were mainly enriched in metabolisms of carbohydrate, cofactors, vitamins and amino acid, this is consistent with the functions of Akkermansia and Bacteroides.

Transcriptome analysis of longissimus dorsi of rabbits at different ages

The transcriptional factors in regulating muscle growth and development in rabbits were explored by RNA-seq. After that, the KEGG pathway was enriched by bioinformatic analysis. During these three periods, ECM–receptor interactions, PI3K-Akt, MAPK and focal adhesion patch signalling pathways were significantly enriched, suggesting an important role of these pathways in regulating muscle growth and development in rabbits.

ECM (Extracellular matrix) signalling pathway is involved in maintaining tissue morphology and regulating cell proliferation and differentiation, it also plays an indispensable role in various cellular responses such as transcription, inflammation, proliferation and differentiation.53 Focal adhesion kinase is a non-receptor tyrosine kinase that is highly overexpressed in hypertrophic skeletal muscle, this signalling pathway is essential for wound healing and regeneration of damaged muscles by contributing to cell migration and adhesion.54,55 PI3K-Akt signalling pathway is one of the important intracellular signalling pathways, and its activation induces skeletal muscle hypertrophy. A previous study found that skeletal muscle-specific Akt overexpressing mice became hypertrophic, and a similar study demonstrated that the expression level of Akt was elevated in hypertrophied skeletal muscle.56,57 Recent study proved that IGF-1/Akt can inhibit muscle atrophy-inducing cytokine and muscle growth inhibitor signalling by inhibiting NF-κB and Smad pathways, respectively.58 Based on these differential GO annotations and KEGG pathways, the highly expressed DEGs that may affect the development of skeletal muscle were identified, such as CDKN1A, COL1A1, LAMB2 and MEF2C were eventually screened in S1 and S3 groups.

CDKN1A is a cell cycle regulatory protein downstream of p53 gene, which is in-volved in the regulation of cell differentiation, senescence and death.59 In the muscle atrophy model induced by hindlimb suspension, CDKN1A expression was increased and decreased after reloading.60 It has also been reported that CDKN1A can promote muscle atrophy by decreasing the expression of spermidine oxidase.61 COL1A1 regulates focal adhesion, PI3K-AKT, and ribosomal pathways through its interaction with MSTN.62 LAMB2 involved in the regulation of skeletal muscle development in ducks.63 MEF2C is a well-defined transcription factor involved in multiple steps of skeletal muscle development, including myoblast differentiation and fusion, as well as various processes that regulate skeletal muscle homeostasis after birth.64,65

All these studies have identified that the DEGs have important functions in skeletal muscle development. Therefore, the DEGs in S1 and S3 may have an important regulatory effect on the mechanism of skeletal muscle development.

Interactions between muscle development-related genes and gut microbiota composition

Recent advance has demonstrated that intestinal microbiota plays an important role in regulating muscle metabolism.20 In the present study, we measured the potential associations between the abundance of caecum microbiota and transcriptional expression of muscle development-related genes during these stages. The results showed that the abundance of Ruminococcus_1 and Ruminococcaceae_UCG-014 was significantly increased in S3 group relative to S1 group, while Acinetobacter and Subdoligranulum were significantly decreased. Researchers have found that Acinetobacter may be a conditional pathogen caused by individuals ingesting contaminated food in the environment.66,67 This may be the reason why young rabbits have low resistance and are prone to disease. Ruminococcaceae_UCG-014 and Ruminococcus_ 1 are butyrate-producing bacteria and can promote butyrate production and assist the lambs by digesting their food effectively, stimulate intestinal wasting capacity.68 In this study, the relative abundance of Ruminococcaceae_UCG-014 and Ruminococcus_1 were significantly increased during the marketing period relative to the birth period, which suggested an important role of these bacteria in maintaining intestinal health of rabbits.

Furthermore, IGF1, MYLK2, HSPB1, MYL2 and other genes related to muscle growth and development were screened between S1 and S3 group. IGF-1 is a well-known growth factor in regulating muscle development.58 MYLK2 exists in skeletal and cardiac muscle and activates actin contraction through myosin, and has been proved to be involved in skeletal muscle development in pigs.69 MYL2 is composed of three major isoforms encoded by three different genes and involved the differentiation of goat skeletal muscle satellite cells.70 HSPB1 (also known as Hsp27) is a member of the small heat shock protein (sHSP) family that provides protection for cells and tissues including skeletal muscle.71 We found that Acinetobacter and Subdoligranulum were positively correlated with most of the selected genes, and negatively correlated with MYLK2 and CACNG1. This prompts us the different microbiota may affect the DEGs expression in longissimus dorsi, and then control skeletal muscle development. However, how these microbiota works in this biological process needs further researches.

Conclusions

In summary, we analysed caecum microbiota and longissimus dorsi gene expression in New Zealand white rabbits at different growth stages. The signal pathways related to muscle development that DEGs enriched in the three stages were analysed and dis-cussed. Furthermore, we observe changes in the microbiome, and an increase in butyrate-producing bacteria that may contribute to the maintenance of caecum health and muscle development. Finally, our study provides a heat map of the interactions between intestinal microbiota and host genes in S1 and S3 group, and reveals the longissimus dorsi gene–caecum microbiota interaction. These results may lay a certain foundation for further exploration of the causal relationship between them.
