Bridging the Gap: A Pilot Study Using Artificial Intelligence to Make Plastic Surgery Research Accessible
Authors: Rebeka A. Dejenie, Bryce K. Gantt, Malory M. Alexis, Sierra M. Shockley, Katherine Foote, Monet S. Woolfolk, Derek Banyard, Paris D. Butler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Nearly 90% of Americans face health literacy challenges, limiting their ability to understand complex medical information. In plastic and reconstructive surgery, much of the literature exceeds recommended readability levels, creating barriers to patient education, including disparities-focused research. This study evaluated whether large language models (LLMs) can generate accurate and patient-accessible summaries of such research.Methods:Eight disparities-related plastic surgery articles from PubMed were input into 4 LLMs: ChatGPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro, Grok 3, and DeepSeek-V3, using a standardized prompt to simplify the text to a sixth- to eighth-grade reading level. Generated summaries were assessed using 4 readability metrics (Flesch Reading Ease [FRE], Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level [FKGL], Simple Measure of Gobbledygook Index, and Gunning Fog Index) and were reviewed by a physician for accuracy.Results:Grok generated the most readable summaries, achieving average scores between the seventh- and ninth-grade reading levels (FRE M = 63.06, SD =1.80; FKGL M = 7.69). It significantly outperformed the other models across all metrics (FRE P = 0.001; FKGL P = 0.003; Simple Measure of Gobbledygook P = 0.034; Gunning Fog Index P = 0.007). ChatGPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek showed moderate improvements but did not achieve statistically significant differences from the original articles (P > 0.05), with average grade levels between the 10th and 12th grades.Conclusions:Grok demonstrated superior readability while preserving accuracy, making it the only LLM to meet health literacy benchmarks. Other models fell short, underscoring a gap in artificial intelligence tools. Enhancing LLM performance could promote access to surgical literature and empower diverse patient populations through enhanced health communication.

Takeaways

Question: Can artificial intelligence improve the readability of plastic surgery research in diverse patient populations to promote accessibility and equity?

Findings: Grok was the only language learning model to significantly enhance readability across all metrics, producing summaries at a seventh- to ninth-grade level, whereas other models, including ChatGPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek, did not show statistically significant improvements from the original text.

Meaning: Our findings suggest that artificial intelligence has the potential to bridge gaps in health literacy, but current models often fall short, especially in making disparities-focused research understandable, underscoring the urgency of developing more inclusive and equitable tools for patient education.

INTRODUCTION

Health literacy, as defined by the Institute of Medicine, refers to an individual’s ability to obtain, process, and understand fundamental health information and services necessary to make appropriate health-related decisions.1 Yet nearly 90% of Americans face challenges with health literacy, and over half read below a sixth-grade level, significantly impacting their ability to comprehend basic health recommendations.2,3 Low health literacy has been linked to a wide range of adverse outcomes including increased mortality, increased rates of hospitalization, and increased rates of chronic disease.4,5 Additionally, recent literature has revealed that patients with lower health literacy had poorer postoperative recovery, an increased likelihood of postoperative complications, and overall lower health-related quality of life.6,7 This underscores the widespread literacy challenges that intersect with healthcare access.3 This disconnect poses serious challenges, especially in fields such as plastic and reconstructive surgery, where research is often written at a university reading level and serves as a barrier to patients who wish to engage with research to better understand their medical conditions or treatment options.8–19 As patients increasingly seek out information online, the readability of healthcare content has become critical to patient education, engagement, and equity in care delivery.

The American Medical Association (AMA) and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) recommend that patient materials be written at reading levels equivalent to the sixth and eighth grades, respectively.20 In recent years, rising public interest in medical research has driven efforts to enhance accessibility, such as through lay summaries and graphical abstracts.21,22 However, many peer-reviewed articles, including those related to health disparities, continue to use complex language and concepts that exclude the average reader.9,20 Despite growing public demand for accessible health information, the complexity of these articles remains a barrier.

Emerging technologies, such as large language models (LLMs), offer a promising solution to bridge this gap. Models such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, and DeepSeek have shown potential in simplifying complex content.14,23 However, little is known about how well these tools translate disparities-focused research in plastic surgery into accurate, readable summaries that meet health literacy standards. Therefore, there remains a critical gap in evaluating their utility in patient education. To address this deficit, we conducted a pilot study evaluating the ability of 4 LLMs to simplify disparities-related plastic surgery research into content accessible at a sixth- to eighth-grade reading level using validated readability metrics and expert review for medical accuracy. By ensuring clarity, readability, and precision, these tools have the potential to empower patients with a better understanding of the field, including its advancements, risks, and benefits in plastic and reconstructive surgery. Improving the accessibility of these resources could enable patients to engage with recent data and make more informed decisions about their care, and potentially improve clinical outcomes while reducing health disparities.

METHODS

Study Design

This study evaluated and compared the performance of 4 LLMs, ChatGPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro, DeepSeek-V3, and Grok 3, in simplifying disparities-related plastic and reconstructive surgery research to a sixth- to eighth-grade reading level. The primary outcomes were readability and accuracy of the generated summaries.

Data Collection

First, a comprehensive PubMed search was conducted to identify peer-reviewed articles addressing disparities affecting patients of color in plastic and reconstructive surgery. Inclusion criteria required articles to address demographic factors in their patient populations, originate from different subspecialties, have full text accessible online, be written in English, and be published within the last 15 years. From this, 8 retrospective cohort studies were randomly sampled.24–31 The selected studies encompassed various subspecialties within plastic surgery, including craniofacial (25%), hand (12.5%), and breast reconstruction (62.5%).

Accounts were created for GPT4-o, Gemini 1.5 Pro, Grok, and DeepSeek. All LLMs were accessed in February of 2025 on a private browsing window on Google Chrome (version: 131.0.6778.266). Each LLM received an identical prompt to generate summaries of the articles: “Please rewrite the following text to be readable at a sixth-to eighth-grade reading level. Do not include information not contained in the original text, and do not exclude information contained within the original text.”32,33 The 8 selected articles were then uploaded into each LLM individually in PDF format.

After generating the summaries, each was reviewed for readability by multiple researchers. Readability was assessed via 4 scoring systems: the Flesch Reading Ease (FRE), Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL), Gunning Fog (GF) Index, and the SMOG (Simple Measure of Gobbledygook) Index—4 widely recognized tools for evaluating readability.34,35 All readability scores were calculated using the online tool WebFx.36 The FRE and FKGL calculate readability based on average sentence length and word length, whereas the SMOG Index analyzes longer texts and identifies words with 3 or more syllables. The GF Index estimates the years of formal education a reader needs to understand the text on the first reading, based on sentence length and complex word use. (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays an image displaying the different readability metrics used and the formulas to calculate them, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E697.)

It is important to note that although these readability formulas provide standardized metrics, they evaluate linguistic simplicity rather than true patient comprehension. In addition to readability, each summary was reviewed by a physician for medical accuracy and contextual reliability. The physician reviewer was blinded to the source of each summary. A Likert scale was used to evaluate the summaries across 3 domains: medical accuracy, completeness, and overall quality.

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all readability scores. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of data distributions. Based on these results, paired tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used where appropriate to compare readability scores. One-way analysis of variance and post hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were performed to examine differences across all 4 models. Additionally, χ2 analyses were conducted to compare the distribution of readability categories among models. Given the small sample size (n = 8), we conducted post hoc power analyses using Cohen d at an α value of 0.05 with 80% power.

RESULTS

Descriptive statistics revealed notable differences in readability between models. (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays the readability scores calculated from each artificial intelligence–generated summary, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E698.) Grok produced the most readable summaries overall, with the highest average FRE score (M = 63.06, SD = 5.97) and the lowest average grade-level scores across all 3 metrics: FKGL (M = 7.69, SD = 1.58), SMOG Index (M = 8.09, SD = 1.16), and GF Index (M = 9.16, SD = 1.79). In contrast to all automated summaries generated by the LLMs, the original articles were the least readable, with a low average FRE score (M = 37.02, SD = 13.66) and high grade-level estimates: FKGL (M = 11.10, SD = 2.65), SMOG (M = 10.06, SD = 1.94), and GF (M = 12.46, SD = 1.89). (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 3, which displays the mean and SD of the readability scores, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E699.)

ChatGPT (FRE M = 45.16), Gemini (FRE M = 45.17), and DeepSeek (FRE M = 47.49) provided moderate improvements in FRE score compared with the original text but maintained average grade-level scores between 10th and 11th grades across all metrics. Paired t tests were conducted to compare the readability scores of each language model to the original articles across all 4 metrics. (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 4, which displays paired t test results comparing readability scores between language models and original articles, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E700.) Grok was the only model to demonstrate statistically significant improvements in multiple domains. Specifically, Grok showed a significantly higher FRE score compared with the original text (P = 0.0013) and significantly lower grade-level estimates for FKGL (P = 0.0239) and GF (P = 0.025). The improvement in SMOG score for Grok approached significance (P = 0.0742). In contrast, ChatGPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek did not show statistically significant differences from the original articles in any readability metric (P > 0.05), indicating minimal improvement in accessibility. One-way analysis of variance revealed significant differences in readability scores across models for all 4 metrics: FRE (P = 0.0004), FKGL (P = 0.0056), SMOG (P = 0.0331), and GF (P = 0.0059). Post hoc pairwise comparisons using Bonferroni-corrected Wilcoxon tests indicated that Grok consistently outperformed other models. (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 5, which displays post hoc Wilcoxon test results with Bonferroni correction comparing Grok to other models across readability metrics, where the P values reflect the Bonferroni correction, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E701.)

Grok had significantly higher FRE than ChatGPT (P = 0.0062), DeepSeek (P = 0.0186), Gemini (P = 0.0466), and the original articles (P = 0.0016). It also had significantly lower FKGL scores than ChatGPT (P = 0.027) and Gemini (P = 0.038), though differences compared with the original were not statistically significant. Grok also scored significantly lower than ChatGPT on the SMOG Index (P = 0.011), whereas differences in GF scores compared with Gemini (P = 0.063) and the original (P = 0.054) approached significance.

χ2 tests revealed significant differences in the distribution of readability grade-level categories across models for all 3 metrics. (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 6, which displays χ2 test results comparing the distribution of grade-level readability categories across models, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E702.)

Grok was the only model that consistently generated a higher number of summaries in the recommended sixth- to eighth-grade range, whereas other models and the original articles primarily produced content in the 11th-grade level or higher. These findings reinforce the superior performance of Grok in producing summaries aligned with recommended health literacy standards.

Post hoc power analyses confirmed that the study was underpowered to detect small or moderate differences in readability scores, given the limited sample size (n = 8). Only large effects (d ≥ 1.2) could be detected at 80% power, which explains why Grok demonstrated significant improvements across several metrics, whereas ChatGPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek may not have reached significance despite potential moderate effects.

The evaluation of each LLM, ChatGPT, DeepSeek, Gemini, and Grok, across 3 metrics (accuracy, completeness, and overall quality) revealed distinct performance differences. Grok consistently ranked highest in all metrics, demonstrating superior accuracy, completeness, and overall quality. ChatGPT followed with strong performance in accuracy and overall quality but slightly lower scores in completeness. Additionally, DeepSeek and Gemini exhibited similar results, with DeepSeek performing the lowest across all metrics. These findings underscore Grok’s robust performance and highlight the relatively lower performance of DeepSeek compared with the other LLMs.

DISCUSSION

The findings of this study demonstrated significant differences in the ability of LLMs to produce readable and accessible summaries of plastic and reconstructive surgery research focused on disparities, with Grok emerging as the most effective tool. As the AMA and NIH recommend materials be written at sixth- to eighth-grade reading levels, Grok produced summaries with the highest average FRE, and the lowest grade-level estimates across the FKGL, SMOG, and GF indices. These metrics indicate that Grok may produce summaries that are significantly more readable than original articles and other LLMs. Meanwhile, the widely used ChatGPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek showed moderate improvements over the original articles, but their readability remained at a 10th- to 11th-grade reading level.

Beyond readability, Grok demonstrated superior performance in accuracy, completeness, and overall quality, outranking ChatGPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek, with DeepSeek performing the weakest. These findings suggest that Grok can simplify complex research while preserving essential information, potentially alleviating current concerns about the reliability of LLMs in medical research. By aligning with the recommended AMA and NIH readability guidelines, Grok offers a promising solution to empower patients with a better understanding of advancements, risks, and treatment options, potentially improving informed decision-making and health outcomes.

The broader utility of LLMs in medical research strongly supports their adoption in fields such as plastic and reconstructive surgery. Recent literature has found that LLMs are being used to generate patient education materials and improve doctor–patient communication, demonstrating their potential application in medicine.37 These examples, particularly in translating complex medical content into accessible language, align with the ability of Grok to produce readable summaries and suggest that LLMs may be used to make health information more inclusive, especially in specialized fields such as plastic and reconstructive surgery. Additionally, LLMs can help address language barriers among patients and their providers. Studies show that LLMs can accurately translate scientific articles into multiple languages while preserving key details.38 For non-English-speaking patients, this ability could broaden access to health education, overcoming the dominance of English use in medical literature. Expanding LLM functionality to produce multilingual summaries at recommended reading levels could further improve health literacy and ensure that language does not serve as a barrier to patient understanding of complex surgical concepts, thus improving clinical outcomes and reducing health disparities.

This study has several limitations. First, this study focused on disparity-focused research in plastic and reconstructive surgery articles, and Grok performance may vary in other medical domains. Additionally, the included studies were heavily skewed toward breast reconstruction (62.5%), which may bias results toward this subspecialty and limit generalizability to other areas of plastic surgery. Future research should explore the effectiveness of Grok across diverse medical texts and include a more balanced distribution among subspecialties. Another key limitation of this study is its small sample size (n = 8), which substantially limits statistical power and generalizability. Post hoc power analyses confirmed that only large effects could be reliably detected, meaning the study may have missed moderate but clinically meaningful differences. Accordingly, nonsignificant results for ChatGPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek should be interpreted with caution. As this was a pilot study, future studies with larger and more representative samples are needed to confirm these findings, improve statistical power, and determine whether moderate differences between models translate into meaningful improvements in accessibility. Additionally, although 8 retrospective cohort studies were randomly selected from a PubMed search based on defined inclusion criteria, this process may not fully capture the breadth of disparity-focused research in plastic and reconstructive surgery. Our study did not account for the differing accessibility of peer-reviewed literature, which targets specialized readers, compared with patient education materials designed for broader audiences, which warrants further exploration. To minimize bias, future studies should include multiple expert reviewers representing different plastic surgery subspecialties and establish interrater reliability metrics. Notably, our reviewer was a surgeon; however, multireviewer validation would enhance the robustness and reproducibility of medical accuracy assessment. Furthermore, although readability was assessed with established formulas (FRE, FKGL, SMOG, GF), these metrics have not been specifically validated for medical content or culturally diverse populations. Future work should evaluate whether these scores truly reflect patient comprehension, particularly among underserved groups.

It is also important to consider the cost associated with using LLMs. ChatGPT and Gemini offer basic memberships for around $20 per month, with premium tiers reaching up to $200. Similarly, Grok costs approximately $30 per month, with higher pricing for expanded access, whereas DeepSeek operates on a token-based model. Although all platforms provide limited free versions, financial and technological barriers may restrict access for patients with limited resources, making affordability an important consideration in their potential use.

A significant limitation of this study lies in the rapid evolution of LLMs. The models used at the time of analysis may differ significantly within a short time and can quickly become outdated. To strengthen methodological rigor, we explicitly document model version and access dates for reproducibility (all models were accessed in February 2025). Given the pace of LLM updates, we recommend future investigations adopt version-tracking frameworks and recurring evaluation intervals to monitor performance drift and maintain longitudinal relevance. Additionally, our study did not compare all LLMs, and there were variations in access, as Grok and DeepSeek were free versions, whereas ChatGPT and Gemini had paid subscriptions, which could influence performance and user experience and provide variations that may affect outcomes. Furthermore, LLMs are susceptible to hallucinations and can generate incorrect information, which poses a risk in medical contexts where accuracy is essential.39 To address this, future studies should implement systematic hallucination-detection and validation frameworks. Examples include dual independent physician review, automated factual-consistency scoring, and cross-referencing outputs with verified clinical sources. Establishing such quality-control pipelines will be critical for ensuring the safety and reliability of LLMs in medical communication. Assessing patient comprehension and preferences for LLM-generated content, including terminology, could help optimize its practical impact.

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrated that Grok significantly improves the accessibility of disparities-focused plastic and reconstructive surgery research by generating summaries that meet the AMA and NIH recommended sixth- to eighth-grade reading level. Among the evaluated LLMs, Grok was the only model to consistently produce readable content while maintaining clinical accuracy and completeness. These findings position Grok as a promising tool for translating complex research into patient-friendly language, an essential step toward reducing health literacy gaps. As LLMs continue to evolve, their strategic use in patient education has the potential to promote health equity, empower self-advocacy, and transform how medical knowledge is shared with diverse patient populations.
