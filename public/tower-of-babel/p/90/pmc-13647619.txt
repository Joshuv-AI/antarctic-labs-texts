PGPR-induced regulation of Zn and Fe transporters in wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) uncovered through integrated genome-wide analysis and functional validation
Authors: Faiza Maqbool, Rubab Zahra Naqvi, Raheela Rehman, Imran Amin, Ifrah Imran, Asma Imran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractZinc (Zn) and iron (Fe) deficiencies affect more than two billion people globally, particularly in cereal-dependent regions where wheat, despite its high consumption, provides inadequate micronutrient levels. Conventional interventions such as genetic modification and mineral supplementation remain costly, unevenly accessible, and insufficient for large-scale nutritional improvement. Agronomic biofortification using plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) offers a promising yet underexplored alternative, especially for regulating metal homeostasis genes in wheat. This research integrates multi-season field trials of Zn-biofortified Akbar-19 and the local cultivar Khaista-17, conducted under reduced fertilizer conditions with PGPR consortia. Afterwards, genome-wide analyses including phylogenetic relationships, promoter elements, gene interaction networks, expression profiles, and conserved domains/motifs of the TaNAS (19 genes), TaNAAT (6), TaDMAS (3), and TaVIT (31) gene families was performed. This was followed by transcriptional expression (qPCR) of six candidate genes in wheat grown under hydroponic Zn/Fe stress in the presence of PGPR. Field evaluation showed that PGPR inoculation boosted yield by 15–18% and increased grain Zn/Fe by 15–20% in Akbar-19 and 25–28% in Khaista-17, consistently outperforming fertilizer-only controls across both seasons. The genome-wide analyses exhibited the phylogenetic relationship of wheat TaDMAS, TaNAAT, and TaNAS genes with barley, while TaVIT and TaVTL genes with rice and maize. Promoter analyses of these genes showed an enrichment of stress-responsive cis-elements, such as IDE1/2, ZDRE1/2, IRO2-binding sites, and metal-responsive elements suggesting coordinated regulation of micronutrient chelation, uptake, and homeostasis. qPCR results confirmed PGPR-induced upregulation of NAS1, NAS6, NAS9, NAAT2, DMAS1, and VIT2 under Zn/Fe stress, with stronger induction in Khaista-17. Overall, the results show that PGPR modulate metal‑transporter gene networks and improve micronutrient biofortification in wheat, providing a genotype‑responsive and sustainable approach to address micronutrient deficiency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09415-8.Keywords: Micronutrient, Biofortification, Genome-wide analysis, Wheat, Nutrient transporter, Zn and Fe homeostasis, Rhizobacteria nutrient mobilization, Plant growth promoting rhizobacteria

Background

Zinc (Zn) and iron (Fe) deficiencies are persistent global health challenges, affecting over two billion individuals worldwide. According to the World Health Organization, approximately 30% of the global population is at risk of zinc deficiency [1]. Additionally, Fe deficiency remains the primary cause of anemia, impacting nearly 40% of pregnant women and 43% of children under the age of five [2]. These deficiencies are most widespread in Southeast Asia, Africa, and the Eastern Mediterranean, where limited dietary diversity and restricted access to supplementation make populations particularly vulnerable [3]. Despite the well‑established importance of these micronutrients for health, large segments of the global population especially in the developing countries still fail to meet recommended dietary allowances (RDA). Furthermore, dietary diversification and supplementation remain out of reach for many [4]. Hence, sustainable agricultural approaches are increasingly recognized as key to tackling micronutrient deficiencies at population scale [5, 6].

Wheat, the second staple, with a global output of 799 million tonnes in 2024-25 [7], plays a vital role in daily nutrition, by contributing ≈ 24% of total caloric intake, 33% of protein, and 3.5% of fat [8]. Globally, wheat contributes an estimated 6.49 mg of Fe and 4.69 mg of Zn per capita per day. However, intake levels are substantially lower in Asia. In South‑East Asia, wheat provides only 2.60 mg Fe and 1.88 mg Zn per capita, while South Asia shows slightly higher values at 6.72 mg Fe and 4.85 mg Zn [9]. The standard reference concentration in wheat grain declared by the US Department of Agriculture is 36 mg kg− 1 Fe and 26 mg kg− 1 Zn. To meet this nutritional needs, HarvestPlus has set the breeding target of 30 mg kg− 1 for Zn and 59 mg kg− 1 for Fe [10]. Despite its dietary significance, wheat does not sufficiently meet the RDAs for Fe and Zn [11]. This underscores the need for agronomic interventions emphasizing sustainable biofortification strategies in wheat [5]. A biologically driven approach based on soil microbiota, particularly rhizosphere and endophytic microbes with the ability to mobilize the soil metals, improve the plant nutrient uptake, and regulate the expression of plant transporters responsible for Zn and Fe distribution within grain has been reported recently [12, 13].

Under micronutrient deficiency, wheat acquire Fe and Zn through Strategy II which is a chelation-based mechanism involving phytosiderophores (PS), primarily mugineic acid (MA) and nicotianamine (NA). PS are secreted into the rhizosphere via the MA transporter TOM1, where they chelate Fe³⁺ and Zn²⁺ to form soluble complexes that are subsequently taken up by the roots. After Fe and Zn are released into the root cytosol, PS molecules are released into the rhizosphere for further chelation [14]. Triticum aestivum nicotianamine synthase (TaNAS), nicotianamine aminotransferase (TaNAAT), and deoxymugineic acid synthase (TaDMAS) are the core genes for PS biosynthesis, and their expression is modulated by the relative availability of Fe³⁺, Fe²⁺, and Zn²⁺ [15]. NA also functions as an intracellular chelator and transporter of Fe, Zn, Mn, and Cu, and serves as a precursor for deoxymugineic acid (DMA) synthesis in wheat and other graminaceous plants [16]. Although, the expression of PS-biosynthetic genes under micronutrient deficiency has been reported, their regulation in response to plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) remains poorly understood.

Genome-wide analyses revealed 21 TaNAS, 6 TaNAAT, and 3 TaDMAS genes in wheat exhibiting high sequence homology with their barley counterparts. Comparative expression analyses in wheat and barley demonstrate a conserved regulatory pattern, marked by strong induction of NAS, NAAT, and DMAS under Fe-deficient conditions and clear repression under Fe-sufficient conditions [15]. Similarly, under combined Zn–Fe deficiencies, these genes are upregulated to maintain the metabolite flux required for phytosiderophore biosynthesis in wheat. In bread wheat, 2′-DMA is synthesized via the methionine salvage pathway which is initiated with methionine being converted to S-adenosylmethionine (SAM). NAS then utilize three SAM molecules to generate one molecule of nicotianamine (NA) which is subsequently converted into a 3′′-ketointermediate by NAAT. DMAS catalyzes the final reduction step to produce DMA, completing the biosynthetic route essential for Fe and Zn acquisition [17].

Although, the response of transporter gene to micronutrient stress has been well characterized, genome-wide analyses that integrate stress-responsive expression with microbe-mediated nutrient mobilization remain limited. This knowledge gap constrains the development of sustainable strategies for crop micronutrient biofortification and soil health improvement. We hypothesized that PGPR inoculation will help in uptake of micronutrients and the expression of micronutrient transporters. The present study evaluated the PGPR-inoculation response of wheat genotypes under field conditions and a comprehensive assessment of the TaNAS, TaNAAT, TaDMAS, and TaVIT gene families, phylogeny, promoter features, gene structure, conserved domains, expression dynamics, and interaction networks. These analyses collectively advance our understanding of transporter functions in micronutrient acquisition and homeostasis. Furthermore, transcriptional profiling of NAS, NAAT, DMAS, and VIT genes in wheat shoots under controlled conditions demonstrates that PGPR inoculation modulates their expression in response to Fe and Zn deficiencies, likely by enhancing micronutrient availability in the rhizosphere.

Bacterial strains

The bacteria used in this study were previously isolated from rhizosphere of wheat and rice from different regions in Pakistan. Isolation was performed using the standard serial dilution plating technique [18]. The glycerol stocks of bacteria (R14A, RZ-7, ER-29, 5D, K-1) were obtained from the laboratory collection and revived on LB plates. These strains were previously characterized for PGPR traits identified through 16 S rRNA gene sequence analysis (Table 1).

Qualitative assay for plant growth-promoting traits

The bacteria were tested for various plant growth-promoting traits, including zinc and phosphate solubilization, nitrogenase activity, siderophore production, and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) production. The zinc solubilization was tested on tris-minimal agar supplemented with D-glucose and insoluble zinc oxide (ZnO; 1 g L− 1) equivalent to 0.1% Zn, as zinc source. Freshly grown bacterial cultures were spot-inoculated in triplicate with a sterile loop on the Zn-solubilization media plates. Plates were incubated at 30 ± 2 °C for 7 days, and the zinc solubilization was observed as a clear halo area around the colonies. The colony and halo zone diameters (mm) were recorded to determine the zinc solubilization index (ZSI) [19]. Phosphate solubilization was assessed by spot inoculation of pure culture on the petri plates containing Pikoviskaya medium with 0.5% tricalcium phosphate (TCP) as the insoluble phosphorus source. The plates were incubated at 30 ± 2 °C for 6–7 days and halo zones around the colonies indicated phosphate solubilization. The solubilization index (SI) was calculated using the formula by D Paul and SN Sinha [20]. The potential of bacteria to fix nitrogen was analyzed qualitatively by bacterial growth on a semi-solid nitrogen-free medium [21]. The chrome azurol S (CAS) agar assay, as described by B Schwyn and J Neilands [22], was used to test siderophore production. CAS agar plates spot-inoculated with freshly grown bacterial isolates were incubated at 30 ± 2 °C for 5 days. The presence of yellow-orange halo zones around colonies indicated siderophore production. The colorimetric assay was used to quantify IAA production [23]. The isolates were inoculated into Luria Bertani (LB) broth supplemented with 0.1% tryptophan as an IAA precursor and incubated at 28 °C for 7 days at 140 rpm. After incubation, the cultures were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. Subsequently, 100 µL supernatant was added to 100 µL of Salkowski’s reagent and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 20 min. The appearance of pink-to-purple color exhibited the presence of IAA or its metabolites. The bacterial strains were maintained on nutrient agar slants and stored at 4 °C until further use.

Formulation of bacterial consortium

To formulate the bacterial consortium, bacterial strains were subjected to in vitro compatibility assay as described [24]. Each bacterial strain was spot-inoculated onto LB agar plate, with the other strains spread across the plates, followed by incubation at 28 ± 2 °C for 72 h. The absence of an inhibition zone between colonies indicated compatibility. The individual strains were then cultured in LB broth at 28 ± 2 °C for 24 h. The strain were mixed in 1:1 ratio to form the consortium (109 CFU mL− 1). The bacterial consortium (30%) was inoculated into sterilized carrier material (sugarcane waste/ filter mud) to formulate a biofertilizer. This biofertilizer was used to coat the wheat seeds before sowing by simply wetting the seeds with water sprinkler and dusting the biofertilizer with constant shaking till a thin and uniform layer of biofertilizer is coated on to the seed.

Field-based assessment of wheat in response to nutrient stress and PGPR application

Field location and soil physicochemical properties

A field experiment was conducted to assess the crop over two consecutive wheat seasons (2022–2023, 2023–2024). The experimental field is located in Faisalabad, Punjab, Pakistan (31º 25’ N latitude and 73º 05’ E longitude) at an altitude of 184 m. The climate of the region is semi-arid (steppe), with cold, dry winters and average low temperatures around 4 °C, and hot summers with high temperatures usually above 40 °C especially during May and June. The baseline features of the soil at experimental site were EC = 0.84 dS m− 1, pH = 7.8, loamy in texture, organic matter = 1.08%, and low DTPA- extractable Zn = 0.42 mg kg− 1. The fertilizer doses applied (per hectare) was 130 kg N, 83.8 kg P, and 67.5 kg K. The crop was sown during November 25–28 and harvested during May 17–20 in both years.

Experimental design and treatments

The following five treatments were evaluated for the experiments:

I.F100: 100% fertilizer, i.e., recommended doses of N, P, and K.

F100: 100% fertilizer, i.e., recommended doses of N, P, and K.

II.F70: 70% fertilizer of the recommended doses of N, P, and K.

F70: 70% fertilizer of the recommended doses of N, P, and K.

III.F70 + Zn/Fe: 70% NPK fertilizer + Zn (22 kg ZnSO4.7H2O) + Fe (8 kg FeSO4.7H2O) ha-1.

F70 + Zn/Fe: 70% NPK fertilizer + Zn (22 kg ZnSO4.7H2O) + Fe (8 kg FeSO4.7H2O) ha-1.

IV.F70 + B: 70% NPK fertilizer + Bacterial consortia.

F70 + B: 70% NPK fertilizer + Bacterial consortia.

V.F70 + B + Zn/Fe : 70% NPK fertilizer + Bacterial consortia + Zn (22 kg ZnSO4.7H2O) + Fe (8 kg FeSO4.7H2O) ha-1.

F70 + B + Zn/Fe : 70% NPK fertilizer + Bacterial consortia + Zn (22 kg ZnSO4.7H2O) + Fe (8 kg FeSO4.7H2O) ha-1.

Two wheat genotypes, Akbar-19 and Khaista-17, were selected for these experiments. Akbar-19 is a high-yielding, rust-resistant wheat variety, known for improved tillering and medium height. It is a biofortified, zinc-enriched wheat variety. Khaista-17 is a tall, high-tillering, heat-stress-tolerant, low-Zn wheat variety. The seeds were obtained from Ayub Agriculture Research Institute (AARI) and the Department of Plant Breeding and Genetics at the University of Agriculture, Faisalabad. The experiment was arranged in a two-factorial randomized complete block design with 2 wheat genotypes and five treatments as factors, following three replicates per treatment. The plot size per replicate was 2m2 and wheat was sown using the dibbler method. The basal dose was applied before sowing as: full Zn, P, and K, 1/3 N were applied to the soil as per wheat crop recommendations. The remaining 2 doses of N were applied during the first and second irrigations. Bacterial-coated seeds were prepared one hour before sowing.

Morphological and agronomic attribute evaluation

During the vegetative stage, the plants were uprooted to evaluate morphological traits, including shoot fresh weight, root fresh weight, number of tillers, shoot length, and root length. At physiological maturity, plant height was recorded manually from the ground level to the apex of the spike using a meter rod. After harvesting, the number of fertile tillers, spike length, and spike weight alongwith the total biomass, grain weight, and 1000-grain weight were measured to assess crop performance and yield potential. Data was collected from three plants per replicate, with three replicates conducted per treatment.

Grain nutrient analysis

Grains at physiological maturity were collected and packed in labelled polythene zipper bags. The grains were then dried in the oven at 60± 2 °C in airtight conditions for 72 h. A stainless-steel grinder was used to finely grind the wheat grains. To analyze the nutrients, 1 g of each sample was weighed and digested with 10 mL of a di-acid mixture (HNO3:HClO4) in a 9:4 ratio at 300 °C using a digestor until the solution became colorless. The digested samples were transferred to a 100mL volumetric flask, and the volume was adjusted to 100mL with distilled water. The diluted samples were subjected to phosphorus (P) quantification using a spectrophotometer (Camspec M-350 double-beam UV visible spectrophotometer, United Kingdom) and to Zn and Fe analysis using atomic absorption spectrophotometry (Agilent AA240FS, Fast sequential atomic absorption spectrophotometer), along with standards [25].

Statistical analysis

Two-way ANOVA was used to analyze the data, with two factors (wheat genotype and treatment). The pairwise comparisons were used to compare the means of the main effects (genotype and treatment) and the interaction effects (genotype x treatment) and to test them using the least significant difference (LSD) test a significance level of 5%. The Pearson correlation analysis (was performed utilizing IBM SPSS Statistics 20 software to evaluate the relationships among agronomic traits. The resulting correlation matrix was manually visualized as a heatmap using Microsoft powerpoint, employing conditional formatting with a three-color scale to delineate the strength and direction of associations. The asterisk included to the heatmap indicates the level of significance (p ≤ 0.05).

Genome-wide analysis

The protein sequences of the TaDMAS, TaNAAT, TaNAS, and TaVIT gene families in wheat, rice, barley, maize, soybean, and Arabidopsis were obtained from the NCBI, Phytozome, and Ensembl Plants databases.

Phylogenetic analysis

To infer the phylogenetic tree, MEGA 11 software was used, and further annotation and visualization was performed using iTol software to understand the evolutionary relationships among the TaDMAS, TaNAAT, TaNAS, and TaVIT gene families in wheat, rice, barley, maize, soybean, and Arabidopsis. Phylogenetic relationships were inferred from a bootstrap consensus tree, which was considered accurate and credible.

Promoter analysis

The promoter analysis was performed to annotate and visualize the cis-regulatory sequences within 1.5 kb promoters of TaNAS, TaNAAT, TaDMAS, and TaVIT genes. All the genes were annotated using Geneious Prime. The cis-acting elements included the TATA-Box (TATA), iron deficiency-responsive elements (IDE1 and IDE2), zinc deficiency responsive element (ZDRE-1 and ZDRE-2), Iron-related transcription factor -binding sequence (IRO2-BS), and metal-responsive elements (MRE). These elements are involved in the regulation of genes related to iron and zinc metabolism.

The annotation of cis-acting elements within the 1.5 kb promoter regions of the TaNAS, TaNAAT, TaDMAS1 and TaVIT genes included six key promoters: TATA-Box (TATA), IDE1 (5′-ATCAAGCATGCTTCTTGC − 3′), IDE2 (5′-TTGAACGGCAAGTTTCACGCTGTCACT-3′), ZDRE-1 (5′- ATGTCGACAT-3′), ZDRE-2 (5′- ATGTCGACAC-3′), IRO2- binding sequence (5′-CACGTGG-3′) and MRE-like element (5′-TGCAGGC-3′). Furthermore, the IDE1 and IDE2 elements, which are responsive to iron-deficiency, were annotated within the 1.5 kb promoter regions of genes exhibiting 60% sequence similarity. ZDRE 1 and ZDRE 2 elements were identified with no more than one mismatch to the consensus sequence (RTGTCGACAY) [27]. Additionally, analyses revealed the presence of core iron-related transcription factor-binding sites (IRO2-BS) and metal-responsive elements (MRE-like element), within the 1.5 kb promoter regions, exhibiting 100% similarity identity.

Gene interaction and pathways analysis

The protein sequences of TaDMAS, TaNAAT, TaNAS, and TaVIT, retrieved from Ensemble Plants, NCBI, and Phytozome, were subsequently fed into STRING 11.0 to construct the interaction network of the selected genes. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the pathway interactions among the studied gene families, an online tool, Shiny GO vo.8, was used. The retrieved gene IDs of the selected genes were uploaded to this web server to generate a pathway interaction dot plot.

Gene structure and domain analyses

For gene structure analysis, the genomic and CDS sequences were retrieved from databases and analyzed using the online Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS). For domain analysis, the retrieved protein sequences of selected genes were uploaded to the Conserved Domain Database (CDD-NCBI), and the resulting data were further annotated using TB Tools-II software.

Conserved motif analysis

The conserved motifs of protein sequences was predicted using MEME (Multiple Expectation Maximization for Motif Elicitation) suite (version 5.5.5) web server. The motifs width was set to range from 5 to 200 amino acids, with the number of motifs specified as 10 for TaDMAS, TaNAAT and TaNAS, and 12 motifs for TaVIT.

In-silico expression analysis

For in-silico expression analyses, a total of 31 TaVIT, 19 TaNAS, 6 TaNAAT, and 3 TaDMAS genes were selected. The wheat expression browser, exp Vip was used to retrieve the expression values in the form of TPMs. The extracted data were used to generate an expression heatmap using TBtools-II software. The absolute values were used to illustrate the developmental and tissue-specific expression patterns.

PGPR-mediated expression analysis of Zn and Fe transporters in wheat

Set up of hydroponic experiment

A hydroponic experiment was conducted to evaluate the effects of inoculation on Zn and Fe transporters in wheat plants under Zn and Fe-deficient conditions in a greenhouse environment at NIBGE, Faisalabad. Seeds were surface sterilized using 2% sodium hypochlorite for 10 min, followed by successive rinses (5–6 times) with sterile distilled water. The broth cultures of five PGPR strains were grown separately to reach 109 CFU mL− 1. The sterilized seeds were primed with PGPR by soaking the seeds in a PGPR consortium for 30 min. Control seeds were treated with sterile nutrient broth. Both inoculated and uninoculated seeds were germinated on moist, sterile filter paper, under a relative humidity of 70–80% and day/night temperature of 25 °C /20°C in a greenhouse setting. After four days, the wheat seedlings were shifted to a Phytabox with a volume of 350 mL, filled with half-strength Hoagland solution.

The experiment was arranged in a two-factorial completely randomized design (CRD). A total of three replicates were maintained for seven treatments. Twenty seedlings per Phytabox were grown under a daily temperature range of 25 ± 2 °C during the day and 20 ± 2 °C at night, with a photoperiod of 14-h light and 10-h darkness. The detailed treatments for the hydroponic experiment are listed in Table 2. The pH of the Hoagland solution was maintained at 6.5 ± 0.5 before its inoculation into the Phytabox and aeration was maintained to ensure supply of dissolved oxygen. The Hoagland solution for each treatment was changed twice weekly. The plants were harvested four weeks after cultivation in a hydroponic system, and the three replicates from each treatment were used for expression studies of Zn and Fe transporter genes.

Validation of qPCR for expression of Zn and Fe transporter genes in the shoot of contrasting wheat genotypes

The expression of genes (NAS1, NAS6, NAS9, NAAT2, and DMAS1) and vacuolar iron transporter genes (VIT2) involved in phytosiderophore production pathway, which potentially play roles in Zn and Fe chelation and transport, were selected for validation via RT-PCR under various treatments of Fe and Zn-deficiency conditions. Total RNA was extracted from shoot samples, followed by DNase I treatment (using Thermo Scientific) to remove genomic DNA contamination. The complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg (1 µg) of total RNA using the high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Applied Biosystems™) and an oligo (dT) primer. PCR was performed in 96-well plates using the PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems™). The gene-specific primers for Zn and Fe transporter genes (Table 3) facilitated the assessment of differential gene expression in selected wheat varieties. The Actin gene was used as an endogenous reference for normalizing the results. Three independent biological replicates, each with three technical replicates, were maintained for each cDNA sample. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method and the statistical differences of the mean differences were evaluated using Tukey’s test (P < 0.05 & P < 0.01).

Field assessment of wheat genotypes

Comparative analysis of treatment and genotype effect on morphological and vegetative traits

A noticeable genotypic and treatment-dependent response was observed across all studied traits, indicating a differential response of genotypes to fertilizer treatments and bacterial inoculation (Table 4). During the vegetative stage, both Akbar-19 and Khaista-17 genotypes were notably affected by reduced NPK fertilization, with or without Zn supplementation. In the 2022-23 season, Akbar-19 exhibited significant improvement in SFW (30.6 g), RFW (3.3 g), NT (14 tillers), SL (83.3 cm), and RL (19 cm) under F70 + B + Zn/Fe, closely followed by F70 + B treatment, suggesting a synergistic effect of bacterial inoculation on plant biomass and shoot development. Notably, both treatments outperformed the control F100 in terms of effectiveness. Similar responses were observed in Khaista-17, where F70 combined with B and Zn/Fe resulted in higher tillers (12 tillers), SFW (30.2 g), RFW (3.0 g), SL (77.3 cm), and RL (17.8 cm). In (2023–24), Akbar-19 consistently reproduced the genotype-treatment response, achieving the highest SFW (30.5 g), RFW (3.3 g), NT (13 tillers), SL (80.3 cm), and RL (17 cm) under F70 + B + Zn/Fe, thereby confirming the seasonal reliability of this treatment. Likewise, Khaista-17 produced the highest NT (12 tillers), SFW (29.4 g), RFW (3.2 g), SL (75.7 cm), and RL (16 cm) under the same treatment. Over two consecutive years, F70 + B + Zn/Fe and F70 + B demonstrated superior efficacy compared to other treatments, resulting in significant enhancements in early vegetative growth as well as yield traits. Conversely, F70 alone resulted in reduced trait expression, highlighting the importance of bacterial and micronutrient synergy for robust morphological growth under reduced fertilizer application.

The F70 + B treatment yielded results as observed for other traits, whereas F70 with or without Zn underperformed, highlighting the significance of synergistic effect of microbial and nutrient inputs in maximizing crop productivity. Generally, Akbar-19 produced higher PDW and GW compared to Khaista-17, which exhibited higher TGW, thereby indicating a genotype-specific response.

Phylogenetic analysis of TaDMAS, TaNAAT, TaNAS, and TaVIT

The phylogenetic tree elucidates the relationship of the wheat TaDMAS gene family with its relative species, primarily Oryza sativa, Hordeum

vulgare, and Zea mays. Sequences of these genes were obtained and integrated from the NCBI and Ensemble Plants database. Three DMAS genes were identified in wheat. A phylogenetic tree based on the neighbor-joining (NJ) method was constructed, revealing that TaDMAS1 genes are most closely related to barley (HvDMAS1), followed by rice (OsDMAS1) and maize (ZmDMAS1), indicating a shared evolutionary lineage (Fig. 4-a). The bootstrap consensus tree demonstrated that TaDMAS genes exhibit significant similarity to those of the related species, suggesting the conservation of gene function. For instance, TaDMAS1 is positioned closer to HvDMAS1, implying that the functions of these proteins are conserved for the synthesis of deoxymugineic acid (DMA), which is involved in zinc and iron chelation and transport.

Phylogenetic analysis of (a) TaDMAS (Deoxymugienic acid synthase) and (b) TaNAAT (Nicotianamine aminotransferase) by MEGA 11 using the neighbor joining (NJ) method and further differentiated using the iTol tool

The positive correlation values of the analyzed wheat DMAS genes with barley indicate that these genes have remained syntenic and perhaps, functional across these species. This conservation is crucial for the role of DMAS genes in the biosynthesis of phytosiderophores, which facilitate plants in chelate micronutrients such as zinc and iron. Within the analyzed gene set, the extent of evolutionary conservation suggests that DMAS adopted while maintaining their functions including micronutrient regulation.

The phylogenetic analysis of the TaNAAT gene family revealed six NAAT genes in wheat (Fig. 4-b). These genes were compared with the homologous genes in rice, barley, and maize. The evolutionary tree demonstrated three major clades within the NAAT gene family, suggesting the occurrence of gene duplication events.

It was ascertained that the identified NAAT genes shared considerable homology with the cereal crop species, revealing evolutionary conservation. For instance, TaNAAT1 closely grouped with HvNAAT-B and OsNAAT1, whereas TaNAAT2 showed relatedness to HvNAAT-A. The presence of distant species in the phylogenetic tree clearly indicates that one NAAT gene family diversified and evolved into other functional families. This implies that wheat has undergone duplication during its evolution, as evidenced by divergent clades in the NAAT phylogenetic tree, which facilitate the plant’s adaptation to various environmental stressors through optimization of iron and zinc chelation and transport mechanisms. These diversified configurations within NAAT gene family experienced functional shifts in response to different selective pressures. Additionally, homeologs TaNAAT1 and TaNAAT2 were closely related to HvNAAT-B and HvNAAT-A., respectively, suggesting the functional similarity among these genes.

The phylogenetic analysis of NAS genes included gene families from rice (OsNAS), barley (HvNAS), maize (ZmNAS), soybean (GmNAS), and Arabidopsis (AtNAS) gene families. The analysis identified three distinct clades of NAS genes that shared significant sequence homology among the proteins. Clade I comprised 3 TaNAS genes, Clade II included NAS genes from soyabean and Arabidopsis, and Clade III consisted of 16 TaNAS genes as well as NAS genes from barley, rice, and maize, which were further subdivided into 5 subgroups based on the bootstrap support as illustrated in Fig. 5. The phylogenetic tree indicated that wheat NAS genes were more similar to HvNAS, suggesting an evolutionary conservation between wheat and barley NAS genes. Clade I contained 3 homeologs TaNAS genes (TaNAS9-A, TaNAS9-B, TaNAS9-D), which were closely related to the barley NASHOR2 gene. Within, Clade III subgroup 1 comprises two TaNAS genes (TaNAS1-A and TaNAS1-B) which are most closely related to HvNAS5-1 and HvNAS5-2 from barley. Subgroup 2 included three TaNAS genes (TaNAS2-A, TaNAS2-D1, TaNAS2-D2) closely associated with HvNAS1. Subgroup 3 contained 5 TaNAS genes (TaNAS3-A, TaNAS3-B, TaNAS4-A, TaNAS4-D, TaNAS5-B), showing high similarity to HvNAS2 and HvNAS3. Subgroup 5 contains 6 TaNAS genes (TaNAS6 and TaNAS7) with 4 orthologous barley genes. The NAS gene family in wheat has undergone considerable evolutionary diversification, with the emergence of a subdivision within Clade III that further refines the functional specificity of its members, as depicted in Fig. 5.

The phylogenetic analysis of VIT and VTL protein sequences from wheat, rice, maize, soybean, and Arabidopsis categorized the sequences into two distinct clades: vacuolar iron transporter (VIT) and vacuolar iron transporter-like (VTL). The VIT gene family in wheat comprises 31 genes which are further subdivided into 8 VIT and 23 VTL sequences. Two major clades of VIT and VTL genes indicate that functional differentiation occurred within the gene family. The VIT genes are involved in cellular sequestration of iron (Fe) and manganese (Mn), thereby facilitating their transport to the seed vacuole. Conversely, the role of VTL genes seems to be distinct, potentially involving the transport and accumulation of metals, predominantly cations. The extensive presence of VIT and VTL genes in wheat clearly indicates that this family of micronutrient transporters is primarily responsible for micronutrient storage and serve as essential regulator of micronutrient homeostasis.

Gene structure and domain analysis of TaDMAS, TaNAAT, TaNAS, TaVIT genes

For a detailed understanding of gene structure, we performed GSDS analysis of TaDMAS, TaNAAT, TaNAS, and TaVIT. TaDMAS gene size ranged from 2150 bp (TaDMAS1-D) to 2614 bp (TaDMAS1-B) with three exons and two introns. TaNAAT gene sizes ranged from 3154 bp (TaNAAT2-B) to 4235 bp (TaNAAT1-D). The TaNAAT genes showed a similar gene structure with seven exons and six introns. TaNAS gene sizes ranged from 993 bp to 3555 bp, with TaNAS7-A2 being the shortest and TaNAS1-B the longest. All TaNAS genes have one exonic region, with CDS lengths varying from 843 bp (TaNAS1-A) to 1155 bp (TaNAS6-D) (Fig. 10a).

Based on gene structure analysis, the VIT gene family was classified into two subfamilies. VIT exhibit a similar distribution pattern concerning the number of exons and introns, whereas VTL consist of a single exon with no introns, as illustrated in Fig. 10c. The size of TaVIT genes ranged from 2173base pairs (bp) to 4034 bp, with TaVIT-2D being the shortest, and TaVIT3-7 A the longest. Conversely, the size of VTL gene extends from 778 bp to 3806 bp within the homoeologs of TaVTL5-2 A. The coding sequences (CDS) length for VIT varies from 557 bp to 747 bp, while for VTL, the CDS region mainly ranges from 549 bp to 810 bp, except for TaVTL5-2A-3 which has the shortest size of 378 bp.

All TaDMAS genes were identified to contain Aldo/keto reductase superfamily conserved domain (PF00248, IPR018170) along with the NADP-dependent oxidoreductase domain (IPR023210) (Fig. 10b). These domains suggest a role in the biosynthesis of cellular nitrogen compounds and siderophores (mugineic acid), thereby facilitating iron acquisition. The TaNAAT genes exhibit similar domians, including the aminotransferase, class I and II superfamily (IPR004839) and the Beta-eliminating lyase (PF01212) domain, with the exception of the TaNAAT2-A gene, which additionally contains GH20-hexosaminidase superfamily domain (pfam00728) as shown in (Fig. 10b). The former domain indicates TaNAAT’s involvement in nicotianamine aminotransferase biosynthesis and the metabolism of carboxylic acids and organonitrogen compounds. The latter domain is associated with catalytic activity, specifically hydrolysis of glycosidic bonds. For TaNAS, the predominant domain was Nicotianamine synthase (PF03059), followed by the methyltransferase_25 superfamily (IPR029063) reported in 15 genes, and the TaNAS9-B gene uniquely containing the MTS domain. Moreover, The domain analyses suggest their role in Nicotianamine and siderophore biosynthesis as well as transcriptions factor ILR3-like response, which includes positive regulation of Fe homeostasis and plays a significant role in plant response under Fe starvation conditions. Furthermore, all VIT and VTL genes have a typical VIT-1 or CCC1- family (Ca2+-Sensitive Cross-complementer) (IPR008217) domain, as shown in Fig. 10d. The domain is mainly involved in maintaining cellular iron and manganese ion homeostasis and facilitating transport to vacuoles or mitochondria. The VTL genes are predominantly engaged in zinc ion transport.

Protein motif analysis of TaDMAS, TaNAAT, TaNAS, and TaVIT genes reveals the presence of conserved domains

Using the MEME tool, motif analysis was performed for TaDMAS, TaNAAT, TaNAS, and TaVIT. The motif analysis for DMAS illustrated the presence of 9 motifs in DMAS, with motifs 1, 2, 3, 8 and 9 being completely conserved throughout DMAS and are associated with the aldo/keto reductase domain involved in oxidoreductase activity. The motif analysis for TaNAAT identified 10 conserved motifs of which seven motifs (Motifs 1–7) possessed functional domains involved in pyridoxal phosphate binding (related to methionine metabolism) and the tyrosine catabolic process. Additionally, the motif analysis for TaNAS identified 5 conserved motifs across all NAS genes. Motif 6,7,8 and 9 were present in all NAS proteins, with certain exceptions such as motif 6 and 8 were absent in TaNAS1; motif 7 was absent in TaNAS1 and TaNAS6-A; motif 10 was absent in TaNAS1 and TaNAS2, and motif 9 was absent in TaNAS9-D. Predicted motif 1–5 in TaNAS possess similar functional roles related to nicotianamine biosynthesis, which serves as a precursor for plant siderophore production (Fig. 11-b).

Motif analysis performed using the MEME tool identified 12 conserved motifs within wheat VIT proteins. All TaVIT proteins possess motifs 1 to 4, 6, 9, 10, except for 3 genes that lack certain motifs TaVIT2-5D, TaVIT3-7A and TaVIT3-7D. Motifs 9 and 10 were specific to TaVIT, with the exception that motif 10 is absent in TaVIT3-7A. This pattern suggests that TaVIT2 genes play a significant role in the storage and transportation of iron during grain filling, facilitating iron accumulation as the grain matures (Fig. 12a).

Discussion

Modulation of yield and micronutrient partitioning via PGPR-mediated biofortification

Cereals, especially wheat, possess an inherently low Zn and Fe content, which limits their capacity to meet human dietary zinc requirements, especially in regions characterized by marginal soils and high prevalence of micronutrient deficiency [29]. Chemical fertilizer applications are less efficient in boosting grain micronutrient concentrations due to high pH and calcareous nature of soils that promote immobilization of these chemicals [30]. PGPR significantly improve nutrient solubilization and absorption in the soil thus contribute to enhance crop productivity [31]. The present study demonstrates the potential of PGPR under zinc and iron-limiting conditions to mobilize and translocate these micronutrients in wheat and their likely contribution to produce biofortified grains. This approach presents a sustainable strategy to improve Zn and Fe uptake, nutrient use efficiency, nutritional value, and crop yield [32, 33].

The present study showed the efficacy of designed bacterial formulation to improve productivity and micronutrient fortification, in two wheat genotypes, Akbar-19 (Zn-biofortified) and Khaista-17 (Low-Zn) under field conditions. Both genotypes demonstrated a positive response to bacterial consortia under reduced fertilizer application (F70) in both growing seasons for most of the morphological and yield related traits. Interestingly, the F70 + B + Zn/Fe treatment performed better than F100 (control), indicating the synergistic effects of bacterial inoculation combined with micronutrient supplementation on observed plant traits and nutrient content. It further emphasizes that bacterial inoculation improves the efficiency of applied fertilizer, thereby accelerating plant metabolic enzyme activities and ultimately improving yield. These results further validate that Zn-solubilizing bacteria along with chemical fertilizers enhanced Zn uptake and yield of wheat in Zn-deficient soils [12, 34, 35, 36]. The genotype response was found variable in the present study where the Akbar-19 exhibited improved plant height and spike weight compared to Khaista-17, indicating inherent genetic variability in nutrient utilization efficiency and responsiveness to biofertilization.

A significant positive correlation observed between yield components, morphological traits, and grain nutrient contents (P, Zn, Fe) shows that bacterial inoculation improved not only the growth (morphological, agronomic traits) but also the nutrient solubilization and its fortification into the edible part. Earlier studies showed that combining bacterial inoculation with supplemental inputs (e.g., foliar nano‑Zn) consistently improves nutrient uptake, stimulates growth in cereals and sugarcane, and ultimately enhances yield [37, 38].

Akbar-19 is reported as a high yielding variety demonstrating consistent stability over several years showing up to 7.05% increase than other varieties in trials at multiple locations across Pakistan [39]. Similarly, the yield performance of Khaista-17 has also been tested across various locations in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa [40]. Present study shows that both Akbar-19 and Khaista-17 exhibited improved performance under bacterial and zinc-amended reduced NPK regimes, thereby suggesting rhizobacterial-assisted enhanced nutrient use efficiency and potentially higher yields.

PGPR-mediated enhanced grain nutrient uptake (P, Zn, and Fe) across both genotypes demonstrate efficient nutrient absorption and translocation from soil to grain. Of both genotypes, Akbar-19 exhibited the maximum grain Zn and Fe (40.2 and 48.2 mg/kg, respectively). The data confirms the previous findings [41, 42, 43] which demonstrate that nutrient-solubilizing bacteria can enhance the Zn and Fe content of wheat and rice grains. It further affirms the role of bacterial inoculants in the solubilization and mobilization of soil nutrients, ultimately enhancing the nutritional quality of the crop.

The study was further extended to include genome-wide analysis to get an insight into the genetic mechanisms responsible for micronutrient fortification in wheat, along with molecular study that validates the genotype-specific nutrient transporter response across both genotypes.

Phylogenetic and regulatory insight of strategy II micronutrient (Zn and Fe) acquisition genes

Micronutrients, mainly Zn and Fe, are important for crop physiological processes and these processes are severely affected under nutrient-deficient soil conditions, ultimately impacting crop productivity [44]. The reduced nutrient content in cereal crops, mainly wheat, is primarily due to stringent soil conditions that influence grain yield. Graminaceous plants have adopted a unique strategy to mitigate deficiencies of these vital nutrients. In Wheat, the strategy II nutrient uptake mechanism is commonly used to absorb Zn and Fe from the rhizospheric zone into the plant roots, where phytosiderophore exudation mediated by mugineic acid (MA) family proteins facilitates the chelation of these nutrients forming complexes such as Zn2+-MA and Fe3+-MA [17]. In wheat, the gene families TaNAS, TaNAAT, and TaDMAS are integral component of phytosiderophore biosynthesis pathway [15, 45]. Furthermore, VIT gene is involved in metal homeostasis and serves as promising candidate for essential micronutrient biofortification in cereal grains. Specifically, in wheat, the role of VIT and VTL-like protein from the MFS gene family are crucial for the mobilization and translocation of Zn and Fe to foliar tissues and seeds [46].

The studies have identified NAS, NAAT, DMAS and VIT gene families in wheat but, a comprehensive genome-wide analysis of their phylogenetic relationships, promoter analysis, gene interaction and pathway studies, gene structure, domain and motif characterization are yet to be reported. This gap was undertaken in the current study which elucidates the phylogenetic relationships of wheat with several relative species, including rice, barley, maize as well as Dicot/legume (soyabean), and model plant (Arabidopsis). Phylogenetic analysis revealed a close relationship of wheat and barley DMAS, NAAT, and NAS genes, indicating potential evolutionary and functional similarities between wheat and barley. Furthermore, as reported earlier [47], the phylogenetic analysis of TaNAAT revealed that wheat TaNAAT1 and TaNAAT2 genes have undergone duplication in the diploid wheat ancestor following divergence, likely adapting for roles in nutrient chelation and transport. The phylogenetic tree of the NAS genes family indicated the presence of multiple clades and subgroups, reflecting substantial genetic and physiological diversity within this gene family. Furthermore, the TaNAS gene family in wheat comprises a higher number of genes relative to other species, suggesting their extensive involvement in metal ion regulation influenced by the mineral content of the soil. This finding validates a recent study [48], which indicated that gene duplication events have contributed to the expansion of the NAS gene family in wheat, supporting the concept of subfunctionalization.

In contrast to NAS, NAAT and DMAS, the phylogenetic analysis of wheat VIT and VTL demonstrated the evolutionary divergence showing close clustering with rice and maize VIT/VTL. A high number of VTL genes in wheat is attributable to multiple homologous regions, supporting the previous findings [46] which reported the occurrence of duplication events of VTL genes in wheat. Notably, the highest count of VTL genes was observed on chromosome 2, which is linked with quantitative trait loci (QTL) associated with fortifying Zn and Fe contents in grains. This suggests the involvement of TaVTL in the localization of Zn and Fe within seed vacuoles; however, their role beyond metal transport and storage remains unspecified. These phylogenetic analysis provides a structural foundation for understanding the evolutionary and functional relationships between homologous protein sequences across different species.

Present study demonstrates six cis- regulatory elements to be associated with zinc and iron stress including the Iron deficiency responsive elements (IDE1 and IDE2), Zinc deficiency responsive elements (ZDRE-1 and ZDRE-2), Iron-related transcription factor -binding sequence (IRO2-BS), and Metal-responsive elements (MRE). The promoter analysis (Table 5) revealed significant counts of IDE1, IDE2, ZDRE-1, and ZDRE-2 in TaNAAT, TaDMAS, and TaNAS, as reported earlier [49] confirming the presence of IDE1 and IDE2 in the promoters of TaNAAT, TaDMAS, and HvNAAT. This suggests the presence of binding sites responsive to Fe deficiency that are involved in Fe homeostasis [50]. The studies have shown the presence of ZDREs in the promoter regions of ZIP transporter genes in wheat, rice and barley, as well as the binding of specific transcription factors to these promoters under Zn deficiency, thereby confirming their role in Zn deficiency responsive gene regulation [27,51]. The presence of IDE1, MRE, and IRO2 in the promoter region of TaVIT has been reported earlier [46] which aligns with our study, indicating the vital role of TaVIT in maintaining metal ions homeostasis and modulating its gene expression in response to changes in metal ion levels, primarily Zn and Fe.

Gene structure analysis indicated that all 3 TaDMAS genes are positioned on chromosome 4 with nearly almost similar sequence lengths. Whereas, TaNAAT genes are located on chromosome 1, with a little variation in sequence length. The structural analysis of NAS genes reveals its distribution across all chromosomes except chromosome 1 and 7. Furthermore, TaVIT are situated on chromosomes 2, 4, 5 and 7 with significantly varying sequence lengths observed for both TaNAS and TaVIT genes.

The regulatory networks of DMAS, NAAT, NAS, and VIT proteins in wheat, exhibited substantial protein-protein interactions (PPI) with p-value of less than1.0e-1, indicating marginal interaction among proteins from different gene families. Protein-protein interaction prediction revealed the role of TaNAS in methionine metabolism and nicotianamine synthesis, as well as the role of TaNAAT genes in nicotianamine aminotransferase synthesis [16]. Moreover, TaDMAS genes are essential for siderophore synthesis (nicotianamine (NA)) and for iron regulation. The TaVIT genes demonstrate retained functionality similar to the CCC1 domain in yeast that is associated with vacuolar iron storage and redistribution. Moreover, VTL proteins predominantly mediate Zn sequestration into vacuoles, further reinforcing the functional coherence of this transporter set. These results are consistent with earlier transcriptomic studies [17], which reported significant regulation of these genes under Zn and Fe-deficient conditions, thereby, reinforcing the potential of these proteins as targets for phytosiderophore biosynthesis. This suggests enhanced nutrient uptake under stress conditions, facilitating metal ion sequestration within organelles, and maintaining metal homeostasis. Additional in‑depth research is needed to validate the functions of these genes and to clarify their interaction dynamics within broader regulatory pathways.

The gene expression analysis provided further insights into the tissue-specific developmental regulation of NAS, NAAT, DMAS, VIT, and VTL genes in wheat, revealing variability in gene activity across various tissues and developmental stages. A prominent expression observed in TaNAS genes in roots suggests that NA serves as precursor to phytosiderophore (DMA), which is released from plant roots into the rhizosphere to chelate Fe3+. Generally, Clade III TaNAS genes play key role in root-specific NA synthesis during vegetative as well as reproductive stages. On contarary, Clade I TaNAS9 exhibits shoot-specific NA production throughout the grain filling stage and notably at 12-days post- anthesis (dpa). Similar predominant expression of TaNAS genes in seedling root, leaves, shoot and at reproductive stages in anther and seeds is reported earlier [48], suggesting tissue specific expression pattern.

The expression of TaDMAS across different tissues and grain development stages predicts its role in long-distance nutrient transport and its loading into the wheat grain. A similar trend has been reported in rice and maize DMAS genes based upon the phylogenetic relationships and expression profiling [52]. The expression analysis of TaNAAT depicted variations in regulation, where TaNAAT2 was observed being expressed in most tissues and grain development stages, primarily the root, spike, and grains, while TaNAAT1 exhibited root-specific expression. The results aligns with barley HvNAAT genes [47], which are orthologs of TaNAAT, both showing an enhanced expression in anther and grain tissues, indicating a significant role in anther development and reproduction. Root-specific TaNAS, TaNAAT, and TaDMAS genes are expressed during vegetative growth as active iron uptake genes; however, others also involved in seed development potentially through chelation and redistribution of iron to nourish the embryo and endosperm [47].

Expression profiling of TaVIT1 and TaVIT2 demonstrated higher transcript accumulation in the roots, shoot, and spike. During the grain filling stage, higher transcript levels were observed in the aleurone layer 20 days after anthesis (dpa) in TaVIT2 and TaVTL5. It has been previously reported that TaVIT1 can be a good candidate for enhanced absorption and accumulation of Fe and Zn in the grain [53, 54] while the endosperm specific expression of TaVIT2 and TaVTL5 is already established [55, 56]. The studies also explained that iron is captured from the large flux of iron to the embryo’s scutellum through the endosperm adjacent to the scutellum and was retained there 20 days post anthesis [55, 56]. The TaVIT2 genes are linked to the grain and regulated to facilitate iron sequestration within seeds.

Overall, the expression analysis indicated major physiological events associated with specific gene families including NAS, NAAT, DMAS essential for the uptake and chelation of micronutrients (Zn and Fe) in early plant growth and their redistribution to maturing seed supported by VIT activity, which is crucial for nutrient fortification within seeds. These analyses provide potential molecular targets for functional genetic research aimed at understanding micronutrient homeostasis and biofortification in wheat.

The TaNAS1, TaNAS6, TaNAS9, TaNAAT2, TaDMAS1 and TaVIT2 genes were selected based on their responsiveness to Zn/Fe stress, as identified through genome-wide analysis (GWA) performed in this study as well as the previously reported studies [17, 57, 58].

Additionally, the enhanced expression of TaNAAT2 and TaDMAS1 under Fe & Zn limiting conditions in the shoot of inoculated plants suggests increased production of DMA for micronutrient acquisition and translocation within wheat. This is in accordance with the previous findings [61], which reported enhanced expression of SbNAS2 and SbDMAS2, resulting in increased PS release from roots in response to arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) inoculation under Fe-deficient conditions in sorghum relative to the uninoculated treatment suggesting that NA is transcriptionally upregulated to facilitate Fe uptake via chelation and is involved in the transport of Fe within roots and shoots. In our study, the Zn-efficient wheat variety Akbar-19 exhibited significantly enhanced expression levels of genes involved in phytosiderophore biosynthesis and metal chelation specifically NAS, NAAT, and DMAS in comparison to the Zn-inefficient cultivar under zinc-deficient conditions, suggesting an evolved molecular mechanism for Zn mobilization and uptake in Akbar-19.These findings align with previous reports by [17], where the Zn and Fe-efficient wheat cultivar Narmada 195 demonstrated enhanced expression of phytosiderophore biosynthesis genes compared to the inefficient cultivar PBW 502 under nutrient-limiting conditions. An upregulation of Vacuolar-Iron transporter-like (VTL) and VIT under Fe and Zn surplus and deficient conditions has been reported validating the active participation of these gene in long distance Zn and Fe transport and its translocation from vegetative tissues to the wheat grain [46,17,62]. Our results demonstrate enhanced regulation of TaVIT2 in inoculated plants relative to the control and uninoculated plants under Zn and Fe stress conditions which suggests that the application of bacterial consortia improve uptake of Fe and Zn, thereby facilitating the transport of excess iron into the vacuole to maintain the cellular nutrient balance, which is further redistributed based on iron demand within the plant.

Conclusions

This study systematically evaluated the synergistic effects of PGPR and micronutrient-based integrated nutrient management (INM) strategies under field conditions, focusing on their agronomic and molecular impacts across consecutive cropping seasons. The multi-season trials confirmed the reliability, reproducibility, and field-level consistency of the treatment outcomes. Two wheat genotypes Akbar-19 (a Zn-biofortified variety) and Khaista-17 (a local cultivar) were cultivated under identical nutrient regimes to assess genotype-specific responses. Both varieties exhibited a genotype-independent improvement in agronomic traits, whereas noticable differences in nutrient uptake dynamics highlighted genotype-specific sensitivities, particularly in micronutrient assimilation.

A distinctive aspect of this research is its molecular investigation of wheat transporter genes involved in zinc (Zn) and iron (Fe) chelation, transport, and homeostasis. The study examined the expression profiles of these transporters under nutrient-deficient conditions, comparing PGPR-inoculated and uninoculated treatments. Notably, PGPR inoculation significantly upregulated all targeted transporter genes, thereby facilitating improved micronutrient uptake and translocation. This led to enhanced Zn and Fe accumulation in wheat grains across both cultivars.

By integrating agronomic performance metrics with genome-wide analysis and gene expression analysis, this research bridges a critical gap in understanding the mechanistic basis of nutrient acquisition in wheat under microbial intervention. It introduces a molecular dimension to field-based agronomic studies, an approach rarely employed in conventional nutrient management research, thereby providing valuable insights into the regulation of micronutrient transporters and their role in sustainable crop biofortification.
