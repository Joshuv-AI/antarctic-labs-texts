Baseline Optical Coherence Tomography Biomarkers Associated with the 2-Year Development of Macular Atrophy or Fibrosis in Neovascular Age-related Macular Degeneration
Authors: Chiara Olivieri, Giacomo Perin, Xuenan Zhuang, Giovanni Neri, Guglielmo Parisi, Paola Marolo, Pasquale Viggiano, Francesco Boscia, Michele Reibaldi, Enrico Borrelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate baseline structural OCT biomarkers associated with the development of macular atrophy or fibrosis in treatment-naïve eyes with exudative neovascular age-related macular degeneration (AMD) managed with a treat-and-extend (T&E) anti-VEGF regimen.DesignA retrospective, cohort study.ParticipantsEighty-nine eyes of 89 patients with newly diagnosed exudative neovascular AMD, treated with a T&E regimen and followed for 24 months. Eyes with evidence of macular atrophy or fibrosis at baseline were excluded.MethodsStructural OCT scans were assessed at baseline for morphological features including, macular neovascularization (MNV) subtype and presence of subretinal fluid, intraretinal fluid (IRF), subretinal hyperreflective material, hyperreflective foci, and subretinal drusenoid deposits. Two masked graders independently evaluated each scan, with adjudication for discrepancies. Cox regression models were used to determine baseline predictors of macular atrophy and fibrosis over 24 months.Main Outcome MeasuresDevelopment of macular atrophy or fibrosis during 24 months, expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsDuring follow-up, 28 eyes (31.5%) developed macular atrophy and 12 eyes (13.5%) developed macular fibrosis, with 10 eyes showing both complications. Multivariate Cox regression identified baseline IRF (HR, 6.07; 95% CI, 2.23–16.48) and type 3 MNV (HR, 4.38; 95% CI, 1.63–11.73) as independent predictors of macular atrophy, whereas type 1 MNV was protective (HR, 0.23; 95% CI, 0.09–0.61). Type 2 MNV emerged as the strongest predictor of macular fibrosis (HR, 7.28; 95% CI, 1.51–35.10).ConclusionsBaseline OCT biomarkers, particularly IRF and type 3 MNV, strongly predict macular atrophy, while type 2 MNV predicts fibrosis in eyes with exudative neovascular AMD treated with a T&E regimen. Type 1 MNV may confer protection against atrophy. These exploratory findings underscore the value of comprehensive baseline OCT assessment for anticipating long-term structural outcomes and guiding clinical management.Financial DisclosuresProprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.Keywords: Anti-VEGF, Fibrosis, Atrophy, Neovascularization, Age-related macular degeneration

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is the leading cause of vision loss in individuals over the age of 65, and its prevalence is expected to increase with the aging population.1, 2, 3 Although the early and intermediate stages of AMD typically have a limited effect on visual acuity, about 10% of cases may progress to the advanced exudative neovascular form, which is often associated with substantial visual impairment.4

Intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy has dramatically improved both treatment outcomes and visual prognosis in patients with exudative neovascular AMD. Timely diagnosis and early initiation of therapy, together with continuous treatment without interruption during at least the first 2 years, are critical for achieving favorable visual outcomes.5,6 Current treatment strategies for neovascular AMD include fixed-interval dosing as well as flexible regimens tailored to individual patients, the latter introduced to help reduce patient burden. The two most widely adopted flexible approaches are the pro re nata (or “as-needed”) regimen and the treat-and-extend (T&E) strategy. The T&E regimen is proactive as treatment is administered at every visit regardless of disease activity, while the interval between injections is adjusted according to the disease status. Evidence supports the superiority of T&E over pro re nata, as two recent systematic reviews with meta-analyses demonstrated that T&E provides better visual outcomes than pro re nata across both clinical trials and clinical practice settings, and with different anti-VEGF agents.7,8

Even with appropriate and effective treatment, neovascular AMD can still be complicated by macular complications that compromise visual outcomes. Among these, macular fibrosis and macular atrophy are the most common and may often coexist.9 The term macular atrophy has been suggested as a more appropriate term to describe areas of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and photoreceptor loss when macular neovascularization (MNV) is present, while the term geographic atrophy is reserved for cases without MNV.10,11 The first description of macular atrophy arising in treated neovascular AMD came from an exploratory analysis of the 2-year cumulative incidence and related risk factors in the Comparison of Age-related Macular Degeneration Treatments Trials (CATT), where patients received anti-VEGF therapy with ranibizumab or bevacizumab.12 The SEVEN-UP study—a long-term follow-up of a multicenter cohort initially treated intensively with ranibizumab—highlighted macular atrophy as a key anatomical determinant of visual outcomes.13

Fibrosis, in contrast, reflects an exaggerated wound-healing response of the neovascular complex. It is driven by inflammation, cytoskeletal remodeling, RPE transdifferentiation into a mesenchymal phenotype, and fibrocyte recruitment, ultimately leading to collagen deposition and fibrotic scar formation.14,15 Similar to macular atrophy, macular fibrosis was explored in the CATT through an analysis of its 2-year cumulative incidence and associated risk factors.16 The presence of fibrosis is strongly associated with poorer visual acuity and reduced retinal sensitivity in affected patients.17

Structural OCT is the current reference standard for the diagnosis and monitoring of exudative neovascular AMD. It enables precise identification of MNV subtypes based on specific morphological features, and allows localization of exudation, which may present as intraretinal fluid (IRF), subretinal fluid (SRF), or subretinal hyperreflective material (SHRM).18 In addition, structural OCT can detect important biomarkers associated with the development of atrophy, such as intraretinal hyperreflective foci (HRF)19 and subretinal drusenoid deposits.20, 21, 22 Finally, it is highly sensitive for the detection of macular atrophy and fibrosis.23, 24, 25

Although no effective therapies are currently available for these vision-threatening complications, there is hope that new treatments will emerge in the near future. Moreover, identifying the risk factors associated with their development may offer important insights into their underlying pathophysiology.26,27

Several studies have investigated OCT biomarkers associated with the development of macular complications in neovascular AMD. Analyses from large clinical trials, including the CATT and HARBOR studies, identified baseline features such as IRF, SRF, and MNV subtype as potential predictors of macular atrophy or fibrotic scar formation.28,29

However, many of these studies were conducted within randomized clinical trial settings with predefined treatment protocols and selected patient populations. In contrast, real-world studies evaluating multiple baseline OCT biomarkers simultaneously and their association with both macular atrophy and fibrosis in patients managed with a T&E regimen remain limited. Therefore, further evidence from real-world cohorts is needed to better characterize the predictive role of baseline OCT features in this setting.

Therefore, in this retrospective 2-year analysis, we examined the association of demographics, clinical characteristics, and baseline structural OCT findings with the development of macular atrophy or fibrosis in treatment-naïve eyes with exudative neovascular AMD managed with a T&E regimen.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study was approved by the University of Turin institutional review board and adhered to the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

Subjects

In this study, we included subjects aged ≥50 years with a confirmed diagnosis of exudative neovascular AMD in ≥1 eye, identified from the medical records of the medical retina clinic at the Città della Salute e della Scienza Hospital, University of Turin, Italy. Patients were enrolled in the initial study cohort if exudative neovascular AMD was documented at the baseline visit—defined as the time of diagnosis by the treating physician (author E.B.). The diagnosis was established using multimodal imaging, including structural OCT and swept-source OCT angiography, and, in selected cases with uncertain findings, dye angiography. Finally, exclusion criteria at baseline visit included: (1) history of previous ocular surgeries, including anti-VEGF injections; and (2) history or evidence of other retinal and optic nerve disorders. Moreover, eligible patients were required to have been treated with a T&E regimen following a loading phase of anti-VEGF therapy, with a minimum of four OCT scans per year over a 2-year period (24 months) after the baseline visit. The cohort meeting these inclusion and exclusion criteria comprised 134 of the 435 patients with exudative neovascular AMD in our database (Fig 1).

Structural OCT imaging at baseline and follow-up visits was performed using the Heidelberg Spectralis HRA + OCT device (Heidelberg Engineering). Spectral-domain OCT volumetric scans (20 × 20°, 49 B-scans, 16 ART mean) were centered on the fovea. A minimum signal strength of 25 was required to the OCT images to be included, as recommended by the manufacturer.30 At each visit, patients also underwent a complete ophthalmic evaluation, including best-corrected visual acuity assessment and dilated fundus examination. As described above, baseline imaging additionally included swept-source OCT angiography (Plex Elite, Zeiss) and, in selected cases, dye angiography with the Heidelberg Spectralis HRA + OCT device.

OCT Grading

Structural OCT images were initially reviewed by an experienced certified grader (E.B.) to confirm that inclusion and exclusion criteria were met. In addition to the criteria mentioned above, eyes with evidence of macular atrophy or fibrosis at baseline were excluded. Final eligible eyes (n = 89, Fig 1) were then independently assessed for qualitative features by two masked graders (C.O. and G.P.). Follow-up visits were evaluated for the development of macular atrophy and fibrosis. Baseline OCT scan was used as the reference.

Based on previous studies,31 a lesion was considered present if the grader had >90% confidence that it was identifiable in ≥1 B-scan. Graders subsequently compared their assessments, and disagreements were resolved through discussion and open adjudication in order to reach an agreement for each assessment. In cases where consensus could not be reached, the final decision was made by the certified grader (E.B.).

The presence of macular atrophy and/or fibrosis was assessed on structural OCT images both at baseline—to exclude affected eyes from the final cohort—and at follow-up visits to identify the development of these complications. Their presence was defined as follows.

oMacular atrophy: graded as present when complete RPE and outer retinal atrophy was identified. According to the Classification of Atrophy Meetings group, complete RPE and outer retinal atrophy is defined as an area of choroidal hypertransmission measuring ≥250 μm in diameter, accompanied by RPE and photoreceptor degeneration.10

Study Outcomes and Statistical Analysis

The primary outcome was the development of macular complications, defined as the occurrence of either macular atrophy or fibrosis at any follow-up visit. These two outcomes were analyzed separately, and for each, univariate Cox regression models were first applied, followed by multivariate models, to evaluate whether baseline structural OCT features and other clinical/demographic variables (including age, sex, and number of anti-VEGF injections) were associated with the risk of developing complications. The total number of anti-VEGF injections during follow-up was included as an adjustment variable to account for potential differences in treatment exposure among patients. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis omitting this variable confirmed the robustness of biomarker associations. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated. Patients who remained free of macular complications throughout follow-up were censored at their final assessment (24-month visit).

To account for potential multicollinearity, formal diagnostics (phi coefficients and Variance Inflation Factor) were performed. As severe collinearity was identified in a full model, a three-model strategy was adopted to ensure low Variance Inflation Factor values (Supplementary Table S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). The first model included IRF and type 2 MNV, along with all other covariates, but excluded type 1 and type 3 MNV. The second model included type 1 and type 2 MNV with all other covariates, excluding IRF and type 3 MNV. The third model included type 3 and type 2 MNV, with all other covariates, but excluded IRF and type 1 MNV.

All statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (version 20.0; SPSS Inc.). A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

From the initial cohort of 135 participants initially included in the study, 89 were finally included in our analysis (Fig 1). Table 1 summarizes the demographics and clinical characteristics of this study cohort. Overall, mean best-corrected visual acuity was 0.6 ± 0.5 logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (LogMAR) at baseline and 0.5 ± 0.4 LogMAR at the final follow-up visit (P = 0.1 with paired sample t-test). Final best-corrected visual acuity was 0.8 ± 0.7 LogMAR in eyes that developed macular atrophy, 1.2 ± 0.7 LogMAR in those that developed fibrosis, and 0.4 ± 0.4 LogMAR in eyes without either fibrosis or macular atrophy (P < 0.001). The average number of anti-VEGF injections over 2 years administered was 14.7 ± 6.2. Out of 89 eyes, 82 were treated with aflibercept 2 mg, and 7 eyes with ranibizumab.

Among the 89 participants (43 males and 46 females) with exudative neovascular AMD at baseline, 28 eyes (31.5%) developed macular atrophy and 12 eyes (13.5%) developed macular fibrosis within 24 months. Among those who developed macular fibrosis, 10 eyes developed also macular atrophy. The unweighted k values for intergrader repeatability were 0.80 (80/89) for detection of macular atrophy and 0.75 (78/89) for detection of macular fibrosis. The mean time to complication onset was 10.3 months for macular atrophy and 9.2 months for fibrosis.

Prevalence of Baseline Risk Factors for Development of Macular Complications and Repeatability of Grading

At baseline, SRF was detected in 74 eyes (83.1%), while IRF and SHRM were detected in 30 (33.7%) and 46 (51.7%) eyes, respectively (Table 1). Intraretinal HRF were observed in 84 eyes (94.4%), while subretinal drusenoid deposits were graded to be present in 54 eyes (60.7%). Regarding MNV subtypes, 62 cases (69.7%) were classified as type 1, 6 cases (6.7%) as type 2, and 21 cases (23.6%) as type 3.

Intergrader repeatability showed unweighted κ values of 0.93 (86/89) for SRF, 0.87 (83/89) for IRF, 0.64 (73/89) for SHRM, 0.78 (79/89) for MNV type, 0.91 (85/89) for HRF, and 0.73 (77/89) for subretinal drusenoid deposits. Agreement was reached for all discrepancies after adjudication between graders.

Cox Regression Analysis of Baseline Risk Factors for the Development of Macular Atrophy

In the univariate Cox regression analysis, several baseline factors were associated with the 2-year risk of developing macular atrophy. Increased risk was observed in eyes with IRF (HR, 10.75; 95% CI, 3.90–29.61; P < 0.001), HRF (HR, 3.32; 95% CI, 1.12–9.89; P = 0.031), type 3 MNV (HR, 7.34; 95% CI, 3.05–17.64; P < 0.001), and with older age (HR, 1.09; 95% CI, 1.02–1.16; P = 0.009). By contrast, the presence of SRF (HR, 0.17; 95% CI, 0.07–0.40; P < 0.001), type 1 MNV (HR, 0.12; 95% CI, 0.05–0.30; P < 0.001), and a higher number of anti-VEGF injections over 2 years (HR, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.84–0.98; P = 0.013) were protective.

Subsequently, a multivariate Cox regression analysis was conducted to assess the independent contribution of each factor while adjusting for the others (Table 2, Fig 2). In this model, IRF (HR, 6.07; 95% CI, 2.23–16.48; P < 0.001) and type 3 MNV (HR, 4.38; 95% CI, 1.63–11.73; P = 0.003) emerged as the strongest independent predictors of macular atrophy. Conversely, type 1 MNV remained protective (HR, 0.23; 95% CI, 0.09–0.61; P = 0.003). Notably, type 2 MNV also emerged as a significant predictor of macular atrophy, although its effect was less robust and reached significance only in one of the models tested (Table 2). Cox Regression Analysis of Baseline Risk Factors for the Development of Macular Fibrosis

In the univariate Cox regression analysis, several baseline factors were associated with the 2-year risk of developing macular fibrosis. Eyes with type 2 MNV had a significantly higher risk (HR, 6.87; 95% CI, 1.85–25.47; P = 0.004). Conversely, type 1 MNV (HR, 0.19; 95% CI, 0.06–0.62; P = 0.006) and a greater number of anti-VEGF injections over 2 years (HR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.79–0.99; P = 0.013) were protective.

Discussion

In this longitudinal study, we investigated baseline predictors of macular complications in patients with treatment-naïve exudative neovascular AMD managed with a T&E regimen. We identified significant risk factors associated with the development of macular atrophy or macular fibrosis within 24 months of follow-up. Demographic variables, including age and gender, were not significantly associated with progression to these complications in the multivariate analysis. Although a higher number of anti-VEGF injections during the 2-year period appeared to exert a protective effect, reducing the likelihood of macular atrophy or fibrosis, this association did not persist in the multivariate regression analysis. Instead, structural OCT biomarkers emerged as more relevant predictors of long-term outcomes.

Macular atrophy is a frequent complication in patients with exudative neovascular AMD and can markedly compromise visual acuity.13 In our cohort, 31.5% of eyes developed macular atrophy within 2 years of diagnosis and treatment initiation. This rate is consistent with findings from our recent systematic review and meta-analysis, which reported a cumulative incidence of 11% at 1 year, increasing to 29% at 2 years.45 In the latter meta-analysis, a subgroup analysis revealed a higher incidence of macular atrophy in patients with type 3 MNV, supporting its role as a risk factor for atrophy development. This observation is consistent with the findings of Staurenghi et al,46 who retrospectively evaluated structural OCT features in neovascular AMD eyes from the HARBOR trial and demonstrated that type 3 MNV significantly increased the risk of macular atrophy at 24 months. In the present study, we confirmed these results in a real-world cohort treated with a T&E regimen, where type 3 MNV similarly emerged as a significant risk factor for macular atrophy. One possible explanation for the increased incidence of macular atrophy in eyes with type 3 MNV could be more severely impaired choriocapillaris in these cases.47,48 In addition, other mechanisms that may contribute to atrophy in type 3 MNV include the collapse of serous pigment epithelial detachments, which are frequently observed in this subtype, and mechanical injury to the RPE caused by neovessels that often extend into the RPE or sub-RPE space.40,49

In contrast, our analysis identified type 1 MNV as a protective factor against the development of atrophy. This finding is consistent with emerging evidence from imaging and histology studies suggesting that type 1 MNV may exert a protective effect on the RPE. In 2004, Grossniklaus and Green50 first proposed a potential protective role of type 1 MNV. Based on histological evidence, they suggested that type 1 MNV may mimic the morphology of native choriocapillaris and support the overlying RPE and outer retina. A more recent clinicopathologic correlation study has reinforced this theory.51 In the latter study, an elderly woman with untreated nonexudative type 1 MNV underwent postmortem examination after 9 years of multimodal imaging. The MNV had remained stable within a shallow, irregular pigment epithelial detachment, and histology showed vessels resembling the choriocapillaris that traversed multiple breaks in Bruch membrane, exhibited VEGF-driven fenestrations, and preserved the overlying RPE and outer retina. Imaging studies in geographic atrophy further corroborate this protective role, showing that eyes with nonexudative MNV exhibit slower atrophy progression.52,53

It is important to note that SRF has often been suggested as protective against the development of macular atrophy. In the HARBOR trial,29 eyes with SRF at baseline had a lower risk of atrophy over 24 months compared with those without, a finding consistent with the Inhibition of VEGF in Age-related choroidal Neovascularisation and CATT trials.27,54 However, this protective effect remains debated, as SRF is frequently associated with type 1 MNV, raising the possibility that the MNV subtype rather than SRF itself may underlie the protection. This is further supported by observations in type 3 MNV, where the presence of SRF reflects a more advanced disease stage and is associated with a poorer prognosis. In our study, SRF emerged as a protective factor in the univariate analysis but did not retain significance in the multivariate model, suggesting a less prominent role compared with other baseline biomarkers. Conversely, in agreement with previous study,29,55,56 the presence of IRF at baseline was a significant risk factor for the development of macular atrophy in our real-world cohort treated with a T&E regimen. Intraretinal fluid may disrupt retinal architecture, ultimately resulting in irreversible tissue damage and atrophy.57,58

In our study, 13.5% of patients developed macular fibrosis within 2 years, an incidence slightly lower than the 8.9 cases per 100 person-years recently reported in the literature.59 This modest reduction may be explained by the proactive treatment strategy we employed (i.e., T&E), in which patients were treated regardless of OCT signs of exudation. Such an approach likely minimized disease activity fluctuations, which are recognized risk factors for the development of fibrosis.17,60 In our study, the only baseline factor significantly associated with the development of fibrosis in the multivariate analysis was the presence of type 2 MNV, in line with previous studies that have identified this as a recognized risk factor.17,60 An important clinical consideration emerges here. Although fibrosis is typically visualized as SHRM on structural OCT, we found that the presence of SHRM at baseline was not a risk factor for subsequent fibrosis within 2 years. While this may appear counterintuitive, it is essential to recognize that SHRM is a nonspecific OCT feature that can result from various underlying entities, including type 2 MNV, fibrin, subretinal exudation, vitelliform material, hemorrhage, subretinal drusenoid deposits, and fibrosis itself.61 Given this heterogeneity, it is not surprising that baseline SHRM did not predict fibrosis, whereas type 2 MNV—a more specific marker—was confirmed as a significant risk factor.

Notably, the presence of type 2 MNV at baseline was also associated with an increased risk of developing macular atrophy, although this association was weaker than that observed for IRF or type 3 MNV. This finding is not unexpected for two main reasons. First, by definition, type 2 MNV originates from the choroid and grows above the RPE after breaching Bruch’s membrane, inherently causing mechanical disruption of the RPE. Second, fibrosis is frequently accompanied by atrophy;62 since type 2 MNV is a recognized risk factor for fibrosis, it may indirectly contribute to the development of atrophy as well.

Our study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, this was not a large multicenter trial, and patients were not monitored at strictly predefined intervals. Nevertheless, all patients were managed with a T&E regimen, which inherently requires regular examinations to adjust treatment intervals, thereby partially mitigating this limitation. Importantly, assessing progression in eyes with exudative neovascular AMD within a real-world setting provides clinically relevant insights that can help inform risk counseling for macular complications. Another limitation is that we evaluated mostly baseline biomarkers, which did not allow us to investigate longitudinal biomarkers such as fluid fluctuations. Additionally, the total number of anti-VEGF injections was included as a covariate despite representing a post-baseline variable, and therefore, its interpretation should be considered exploratory. However, this was consistent with our study aim of identifying predictive baseline OCT features.

Moreover, the relatively small sample size and the limited number of eyes with certain MNV subtypes, particularly type 2 MNV, may have reduced the statistical power of the multivariate analyses; therefore, these findings warrant confirmation in larger prospective studies. Furthermore, the OCT acquisition protocol (49 B-scans over a 20° × 20° volume) resulted in relatively wide interscan spacing, which may have reduced sensitivity for detecting small focal structural changes, such as early atrophic or fibrotic alterations.

Strengths of the study include the use of standardized data collected over 2 years of follow-up and the evaluation of each image by two independent, masked graders, ensuring robust and reliable data.

In conclusion, in patients with exudative neovascular AMD, baseline OCT biomarkers—particularly IRF, type 2 MNV, and type 3 MNV—emerged as strong predictors of macular atrophy, with type 2 MNV also representing a significant risk factor for fibrosis over a 2-year period. Conversely, type 1 MNV appeared to confer a protective effect against atrophy. These results highlight the value of comprehensive baseline OCT evaluation, not only to guide immediate treatment decisions but also to anticipate long-term structural outcomes. Larger, prospective multicenter studies with standardized imaging and extended follow-up are required to confirm these findings.
