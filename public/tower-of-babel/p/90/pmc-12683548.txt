Early life elevations of methionine oxidation and ornithine track and predict cystic fibrosis structural lung disease

Introduction

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a genetic disorder caused by variants impacting the expression, stability and function of the CF transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) protein [1]. Insufficient CFTR anion channel activity leads to multiorgan disease, including airway obstruction, chronic inflammation and recurrent lung infections, culminating in progressive pulmonary decline [2, 3]. From the earliest stages of postnatal life, CF lungs progressively develop structural lung disease (SLD) driven by neutrophilic inflammation [4]. Neutrophils entering CF airways undergo transcriptional and functional reprogramming, including enhanced primary granule exocytosis, releasing myeloperoxidase (MPO) and neutrophil elastase (NE) into the airway lumen [5, 6]. In the airways, MPO and NE are robust predictors of CF lung disease severity and progression but may become measurable only after significant disease has occurred [7, 8]. Paradoxically, CF neutrophils recruited to CF airways actively restrict their bacterial-killing capacity, leading to chronic colonisation [9].

Inflammation, infection and SLD remain at abnormally high levels in people with CF even after the initiation of CFTR modulators, although improvements from pre-treatment baseline have been noted in multiple studies [10, 11, 12, 13]. Nevertheless, disease persists, and better monitoring and treatment of early-stage inflammatory pathology remains an unmet clinical need [14]. Unravelling pathways in lung inflammation offers valuable insights, potentially leading to new targets for anti-inflammatory therapies. However, assessing and monitoring airway inflammation in CF is still not common practice, as practical implementation is hampered by the complexity of analysis and a lack of consensus about the most sensitive biomarkers. Identifying biomarkers capable of sensitively predicting early CF SLD may be critical for optimising care and developing individualised treatment strategies [15].

Surveillance efforts in CF care programmes aim to slow or prevent progressive lung damage, targeting the earliest disease manifestations [5]. These initiatives have focused on SLD and airway fluid sampling to identify correlative and causative relationships between molecular, cellular and structural changes [16]. Metabolomics, the study of the small molecules of bioenergetics and cell signalling and their byproducts, is of increasing interest in CF research [17]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that CF-associated lung inflammation is correlated with the differential abundance of specific amino acids, phospholipids, ribonucleotides and oxidation products in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid [18]. However, more research is needed to establish these metabolites as sensitive monitoring tools to track and predict early CF SLD.

Here, we applied mass spectrometry (MS)-based metabolomics to BAL fluid from very young children with CF and tested the relationships of metabolites with SLD and BAL neutrophils, MPO activity and NE activity. We used the Perth–Rotterdam Annotated Grid Morphometric Analysis for CF (PRAGMA-CF) to quantify SLD, cytological determination of BAL leukocytes to quantify neutrophils, and assays of MPO and NE activities. Ultimately, metabolites selected by cross-sectional two-site analysis were applied in a third, longitudinal cohort to assess their potential as predictive biomarkers of future bronchiectasis, compared with conventional biomarkers of CF lung disease, such as NE.

Methods

Human subjects

The study was performed at CF care centres located at Emory University/Children's Healthcare of Atlanta (Emory/CHOA, Atlanta, USA), the Erasmus MC/Sophia Children's Hospital (Rotterdam, The Netherlands) and the Royal Children's Hospital (Melbourne, Australia) as part of the Integrated Monitoring Platform for Early Disease Events in Cystic Fibrosis (IMPEDE-CF; IRB00097352), the Inflammatory Markers in Broncho-Alveolar Lavage Fluid for Lung Disease in Infants with Cystic Fibrosis (I-BALL; ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT02907788) and the SYNERGY-PRESERVE sub-study of the Australian Respiratory Early Surveillance Team for Cystic Fibrosis (AREST-CF; Human Research Ethics Committee Approval Number 25054) programmes, respectively. BAL, chest computed tomography (CT) and clinical information were prospectively collected during clinically stable periods.

BAL collection

At all sites, BAL was performed under general anaesthesia after overnight fast. One mL of sterile saline per kg of body weight was instilled and recovered via a bronchoscope three times in the right middle lobe. A fourth rinse was performed in the most diseased lobe as judged by the bronchoscopist according to the chest CT, or in the lingula if no such lobe could be identified. BAL fluid was centrifuged to remove cells and then stored at −80 ˚C until further analysis. Additional details are given in the supplementary information.

Chest CT

At Emory/CHOA, chest CTs with full inspiratory and expiratory breath holds were performed under sedation using a SOMATOM Flash scanner (Siemens). Free-breathing chest CT scans were acquired without anaesthesia using a SOMATOM Force ultra-fast scanner (Siemens) at the Erasmus MC/Sophia Children's Hospital and the Royal Children's Hospital. For both Emory/CHOA and Erasmus/Sophia sites, the PRAGMA-CF scoring was used to quantitatively determine total lung disease (%Dis), including bronchiectasis, bronchial wall thickening and mucus plugging [16]. CT scans from both sites were scored by a single blinded experienced observer.

Longitudinal cohort

At the Royal Children's Hospital, inspiratory CT scans from children aged 9 years were reviewed by three clinicians, and scans were deemed as showing “no bronchiectasis” if all three reviewers agreed on the classification. Additional details are given in the supplementary information.

Metabolomics

Metabolites were extracted from cell-free BAL fluid by mixing one volume of the fluid with two volumes of extraction solvent (50% acetonitrile, 50% methanol, and internal standards), vortexing and incubating on ice for 30 min prior to centrifugation at 20 000 g and 4 °C for 10 min. Pooled quality controls (QCs) were generated from samples, and Standard Reference Material 1950 (SRM 1950; MilliporeSigma) was used as a reference control. Samples were processed in a randomised fashion to avoid bias. The extract was injected into iHILIC-p Classic (HILICON) columns prior to ionisation. Data were acquired using a Q Exactive High Field Orbitrap MS (ThermoFisher) in full scan for positive and negative modes. Metabolites were annotated, normalised and quantified using Compound Discoverer 3.3 (ThermoFisher). Reference standardisation was performed by comparison with SRM 1950 [19]. Additional details are given in the supplementary information.

Neutrophil effector protein assays

MPO activity was quantified by Amplex Red oxidation following immunocapture [20]. The results were calibrated using MPO from human neutrophils (Millipore). NE activity was measured using the FRET-based NEmo-1 probe (Sirius Fine Chemicals SiChem GmbH), except for PRESERVE samples assayed by conventional spectrophotometry [14]. Data were acquired using a SpectraMax iD3 and SoftMax Pro version 7.0.3 (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). Samples were assayed with duplicate or triplicate technical replicates.

Statistics

Linear mixed-effects models, p-value calculation and false discovery rate adjustments by the method of Benjamini–Hochberg were carried out in R (version 4.3.1). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and curves were performed on GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.3). Additional details are given in the supplementary information.

Results

Subject demographics

Subject clinical characteristics for the Emory/CHOA and Erasmus MC/Sophia Children's Hospital cohorts are shown in table 1. The former cohort had significantly more male participants, while the latter had a higher rate of reactive antibiotic use. 64 out of the 67 Erasmus subjects were receiving prophylactic antibiotic treatment. In addition, the latter cohort was generally older with a wider age range, but this difference was not statistically significant. There were no other significant differences. None of the listed variables was found to significantly influence or correlate with %Dis (table 1), and the use of lumacaftor–ivacaftor in a subset of patients did not appear to affect clinical outcomes such as airway neutrophils (supplementary table S1). Using principal component analysis, we did not observe global metabolome variation by study site (supplementary figure S1A). Using a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) that accounted for site as a covariate, 13 drugs, excipients and endogenous metabolites were identified as unequally distributed (supplementary figure S1B). These included N4-acetylsulfamethoxazole and sulfamethoxazole (antibiotics), paracetamol (anti-inflammatory), and vanillin, galactitol and saccharin (excipients), which could be explained by distinct treatment regimens at each site. Other metabolites that varied by site were endogenous, namely acylcarnitines 8:0, 12:1 and 18:0, glycochenodeoxycholic acid, lysophosphatidylethanolamine 22:6 and tyrosine. The basis of site differences in these metabolites is unclear but may be related to the aforementioned differences in antibiotic use or other treatment differences.

Comparison of BAL metabolites from right middle lobe and lingula or most diseased lobe

A comparison of BAL metabolite content between the right middle lobe (RML; main study sample) and the lingula or the most diseased lobe by paired t-test revealed no statistically significant differences. Additionally, of the 208 BAL fluid metabolites detected, 146 (70.2%) showed positive correlations across both matrices (p<0.05, q<0.10), indicating highly comparable metabolomes across lung regions in early CF (supplementary figure S2). All subsequent analyses were conducted exclusively on the RML samples to maintain matrix consistency.

BAL metabolites associate with SLD and neutrophilic inflammation

Of the 84 collected BAL samples, 78 (Emory n=10, Erasmus n=68) had matching PRAGMA-CF scored chest CTs and were used to determine which metabolites associated with %Dis, a robust measure of total SLD. We focused on endogenous metabolites for this analysis, ignoring drugs and excipients (194 total). Using an LMM, 28 metabolites were selected as associated with %Dis, controlling for patient age (p<0.05 and q<0.10; figure 1 and supplementary figure S3). The most significant was N,N,N-trimethyllysine (TML; p=9.4×10−11, q=0.000145), and most metabolites increased in abundance as %Dis increased. A similar approach was undertaken to identify metabolites associated with BAL neutrophils (%PMN) and macrophages (%Mac; n=83 each), as well as MPO (n=79) and NE (n=44) activities. 35 metabolites were significantly associated with %PMN and 34 were significantly associated with %Mac as determined by LMM, controlling for age (figure 2), with 32 exhibiting opposing relationships across the two cell types (unsurprising considering that the pathogenesis of CF includes neutrophil dominance at the expense of macrophages) [21]. Forty-four metabolites were associated with MPO and 28 were associated with NE activities in BAL (figure 2; detailed heatmaps are in supplementary figures S4 and S5). Taken together, metabolites associated with %PMN, MPO and NE overlap substantially and often correlate inversely with those associated with %Mac.

We did not include %Mac in this analysis because it was consistently anti-correlated with %PMN. 10 metabolites were identified as significantly associated with all four variables: arginine, eicosadienoic acid, threonine, lysine, methionine, MetO, N-acetylmethionine (NAcMet), TML, ornithine and phenylalanine (figure 3a). Significant metabolites from each variable list were submitted for KEGG pathway analysis (figure 3b), which shows associations with arginine and proline metabolism (arginine, ornithine), methionine and cysteine metabolism (methionine, MetO, NAcMet), carnitine biosynthesis pathway (TML), lysine biosynthesis (lysine), and glycine, serine and threonine metabolism (threonine). We also tested whether these 10 metabolites, the percentage of methionine oxidation (%OxMet) and the arginine–ornithine ratio (AOR) correlated with the same metabolites and ratios measured in matched blood (n=46 matched sample sets from a subset of the donors described in table 1) and whether they performed as well as their BAL counterparts in the LMM for association with %Dis. Although NAcMet showed modest trends of correlation between blood and BAL and %Dis association, none of the metabolites tested passed FDR thresholds (supplementary table S3). Thus, blood-based analysis could not replace that of BAL.

BAL metabolites associated with neutrophil-driven SLD are impacted by pulmonary exacerbations and change with age

To determine whether the detected metabolites are affected by disease severity, we analysed the panel of 10 metabolites from figure 3a and compared their concentrations in study subjects with those in CF subjects experiencing pulmonary exacerbations (PE) or with disease controls (DC) from the Emory/CHOA aerodigestive clinic (figure 4a). Demographic information for PE and DC cohorts is shown in supplementary tables S4 and S5, respectively. All metabolites showed a significant increase in PE compared with CF or DC groups. We also analysed %OxMet and the AOR as important ratios giving insight into specific reaction mechanisms in vivo and negating any impact of variable BAL dilution [20, 22]. %OxMet increased with PE, and was lower in DC than in CF, while AOR was lower in PE than in either CF or DC. Additionally, 9 out of 10 of these metabolites increased with CF patient age (the exception was NAcMet, which decreased), as did %OxMet, while AOR decreased with age (figure 4b).

Elevated MetO, %OxMet, NAcMet and ornithine predict later-life bronchiectasis

We sought to assess whether the metabolites selected by LMM could predict the development of bronchiectasis in later childhood. We analysed a longitudinal cohort of 28 well-matched individuals enrolled in the AREST-CF programme, called PRESERVE, who either did or did not exhibit bronchiectasis by age 9 years (table 2). We analysed BAL fluid collected at 6 months to 2 years, averaging across repeated visits before conducting receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, and compared the performance of the previously selected metabolites (except eicosadienoic acid, not detected in the longitudinal cohort) with well-established biomarkers of CF lung disease, i.e. NE and MPO activities, interleukin-8 (IL-8), and %PMN in BAL (figure 5; n=12 who developed bronchiectasis and n=13 who did not). Subject visits from the same study aged 3–6 years were analysed in the same way to compare performance at an older age (n=14, both groups). Metabolite abundances in the BAL samples were not impacted by variable durations of storage at −80 ˚C (supplementary table S6).

At ages 0–2 years, NE activity, MPO activity, IL-8 and %PMN were not able to predict future bronchiectasis (figure 5a–d). However, MetO, %OxMet, NAcMet and ornithine were sensitive predictors (p<0.05, AUC>0.7; figure 5e–h; further details in supplementary table S7). Combining MetO and ornithine improved sensitivity (AUC=0.79, p=0.0123; figure 5i). Within the same cohort at ages 3–6 years, MetO, %OxMet, ornithine and NAcMet remained sensitive predictors of future bronchiectasis (p<0.05; figure 5n–q), but conventional biomarkers (%PMN, IL-8, MPO and NE) were also sensitive predictors of bronchiectasis at age 9 years. Cut-off threshold values for each tested variable are given in supplementary table S8. Except arginine, all metabolites tested predicted bronchiectasis development if assessed at 3–6 years (supplementary figure S6). Finally, within ages 3–6 years, the combination of MPO and NE activities was the best predictor of future bronchiectasis (figure 5r).

Discussion

In the current study, we identified 10 metabolites in early CF BAL fluid robustly associated with SLD and neutrophilic inflammation: arginine, eicosadienoic acid, lysine, methionine, MetO, NAcMet, ornithine, phenylalanine, threonine and TML. These metabolites were selected by consensus with models predicting chest CT-scored SLD, BAL neutrophils, and BAL fluid activities of MPO and NE, and thus reflect robust relationships with pathological neutrophil activity. We followed up by analysing BAL of a separate cohort of well-matched children who either did or did not exhibit bronchiectasis at age 9 years, and found that MetO, NAcMet and ornithine measured at ages 0–2 years sensitively predicted the age 9 bronchiectasis outcome. Bronchiectasis at age 9 years is expected to be less reversible than that occurring earlier in life, and lack of bronchiectasis at this age in CF indicates preserved lung health. The combination of MetO and ornithine was more sensitive than either alone. By contrast, NE activity, %PMN and IL-8 were poor predictors when measured at ages 0–2 years, although they were sensitive at ages 3–6 years, as were all other tested metabolites except arginine.

A previous study by Sly et al. demonstrated that detectible BAL NE activity was the most sensitive risk factor for bronchiectasis development from 3 months to 3 years of age, and the most sensitive risk factor for bronchiectasis persistence from 3 months to 1 or 3 years of age [23]. Thus, it may seem surprising that in the current study we found NE activity at ages 0–2 years to be a poor predictor of bronchiectasis at age 9 years. Important differences in our study include its outcome (future bronchiectasis was assessed much later in life), the analysis of NE as a continuous rather than a binary variable, the use of ROC to evaluate biomarker potential, and a smaller sample size. Interestingly, Horati et al. also found that NE activity was not a sensitive predictor of bronchiectasis progression in toddlers with CF [24]. Most importantly, the results of the current study have identified that specific metabolites related to inflammatory processes – MetO (and ratio %OxMet), NAcMet and ornithine – may at certain periods of development be even better predictors of bronchiectasis than the effector proteins associated with them.

This study highlights the strong potential of BAL fluid metabolites for tracking and predicting future bronchiectasis in very young people with CF. Notably, MetO and %OxMet were the best single variable predictors of future bronchiectasis. MetO and %OxMet have received considerable attention in recent years as potential early CF disease biomarkers, arising predominantly from MPO-derived hypochlorous acid (HOCl), which has the potential to cause irreversible macromolecular damage [20, 25–29]. %OxMet may have increased clinical robustness considering its resilience against variable dilution of BAL. Considering the overall high methionine abundance in CF airways, free methionine is likely to be a major source of MetO, but it may also arise in proteins and be liberated by proteolysis [30, 31]. NAcMet, another predictor of bronchiectasis, is anticipated to arise from protease activity owing to high N-terminal methionine acetylation [32, 33]. Ornithine, another predictor of bronchiectasis, is a product of arginase activity, which decreases nitric oxide by limiting nitric oxide synthase activity, and also increases polyamines, including spermine, which can limit bronchial relaxation [34–38]. Ultimately, combining MetO and ornithine yielded the best prediction of bronchiectasis, perhaps because this combination captures two independent axes of airway metabolic disturbance: (chlorinating) oxidative stress and disrupted arginine metabolism.

Many observations in this study align with the metabolic activities of granule-releasing, immunomodulatory and metabolically active (GRIM) neutrophils in early CF [7]. These include the multiple metabolite products of MPO, NE and ARG1 activities noted above, as GRIM neutrophils actively exocytose these molecules into the lumen [39]. Interestingly, BAL fluid adenosine diphosphate (ADP) was associated with %Mac, and inversely with %PMN, suggesting that it may reflect relative airway macrophage rather than neutrophil relative content and function [40].

Our data suggest that the transition from infancy to early childhood sees a metabolic shift in the CF lungs as disease becomes established. This predominantly sees metabolites associated with neutrophil-driven pathology increasing over time, as in the cases of MetO and ornithine, although NAcMet decreases (perhaps owing to diminished anti-proteolytic N-acetylation). Because such pathology is associated with SLD manifestations that are difficult to resolve, the utility of sensitive, early-stage biomarkers to predict neutrophil-driven lung damage could be high. In the era of increasing use of highly effective CFTR modulators, the metabolites identified in this study may aid clinicians in judging the effectiveness of and need for modulators and additional interventions. Although not yet available, several are in early trials [41].

Limitations

One key limitation of BAL biomarker studies is the invasive nature of BAL collection, especially in the light of alternatives such as induced sputum (IS) and exhaled breath condensate (EBC). However, BAL fluid metabolites have consistently proven to be the most sensitive and reliable indicators of disease progression, particularly in children with CF in whom neutrophil-driven lung disease is not yet fully established [42–46]. Future studies could determine whether these biomarkers function as well in IS or EBC. If not, while longitudinal BAL remains invasive and impractical, our study suggests that a surveillance bronchoscopy in the first years of life could offer important clinical insight into early inflammation and disease risk. Our study also suggests that multilobe sampling may be unnecessary, considering that RML and lingula BAL metabolites were highly correlated.

Another important limitation is that most of the people with CF in our study were CFTR modulator naïve at the time of sample collection, and those using modulators were using lumacaftor–ivacaftor, which is no longer the first-line drug in class. Future studies should attempt to replicate the current findings in people of a similar age with CF using state-of-the-art modulators. Yet another cohort limitation was a lack of healthy control data for comparison, as BAL fluid from healthy children is not readily available for research. The DC cohort in the current study represents various levels of CFTR-independent airway pathologies, not a disease-free baseline.

Finally, our metabolomics technique under-reports hydrophobic molecules and polyamines owing to issues of poor or extreme chromatographic retention, respectively [47, 48]. This limited our ability to analyse such molecules in the current study.

Conclusions and future directions

We identified BAL metabolites that tracked early CF SLD and neutrophilic inflammation in a cross-sectional cohort of young children across two CF centres. A subset of these metabolites (MetO, ornithine, NAcMet and the ratio %OxMet) sensitively predicted bronchiectasis in an independent longitudinal cohort of CF infants and toddlers. These metabolites outperformed conventional biomarkers, including NE activity. Further studies are needed to validate these metabolites as prognostic tools and to explore their translation into less invasive specimens. Future research should also focus on developing targeted anti-inflammatory strategies based on the pathways from which these biomarkers are hypothesised to originate, namely HOCl production by MPO, NE proteolysis and ARG1 activity.
