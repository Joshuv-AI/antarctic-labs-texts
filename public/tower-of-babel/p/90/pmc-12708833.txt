Repurposing levothyroxine for managing metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease
Authors: Leonidas H Duntas, Stergios A Polyzos, Ulrike Gottwald-Hostalek, Bogumila Urgatz, Paul M Yen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is an entity closely linked to obesity and/or insulin resistance, projected to affect about half of the global adult population by 2040. New therapeutic strategies that are both effective and affordable are needed to address this impending public health crisis.MethodsA narrative review was conducted to identify studies supporting the use of levothyroxine and thyroid hormone analogs in treating MASLD and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH).ResultsClinical studies have identified clear pathophysiological links between low (or low-normal) thyroid function and increased risk of hepatic steatosis. Of the two main thyroid hormone receptor (THR) isoforms, THRα and THRβ, the latter primarily mediates the metabolic effects of thyroid hormones in the liver, which could greatly benefit patients with MASLD. Recently, selective analogs of hepatic THRβ receptors have been developed to treat MASLD, with early clinical data demonstrating an effective profile. However, due to the increasing prevalence of MASLD in both developed and developing countries, the high cost of branded drugs may hinder their widespread use.ConclusionsAlthough the development of thyromimetics mainly aims to prevent activation of THRα in other tissues, there is evidence supporting the efficacy and safety of low-dose levothyroxine (LT4) therapy for treating MASLD. This review aims to systematically explore the potential of LT4 therapy as an alternative approach to managing MASLD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12020-025-04451-0.Keywords: TSH, Levothyroxine, Thyromimetics, Metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease, Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, Resmetirom

Introduction

Obesity has significantly increased globally over the past few decades, particularly characterized, by abdominal obesity and insulin resistance [1]. Individuals with obesity are at a higher risk of developing cardiometabolic diseases associated to metabolic syndrome (MetS). These conditions include arterial hypertension, atherogenic dyslipidemia, and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [2]. Ectopic fat deposition in key tissues involved in metabolic balance, such as the liver, pancreas, heart, and muscles, can frequently occur in patients with MetS. In the liver, fat accumulation can lead to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), and its more severe form, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), characterized by steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis [3, 4]. MASH is a serious condition requiring medical attention because its progression can lead to cirrhosis, liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma in some patients [5, 6]. The clinical and economic burdens of MASLD and MASH significantly impact vulnerable populations and healthcare systems; therefore, substantial efforts have been made to find ways to prevent or reverse these conditions [7]. The overlap in prevalence between MASLD/MASH and hypothyroidism, along with their similar pathophysiologic features in the liver, has sparked new research avenues in recent years [8, 9]. This has led to the development of innovative drugs that mimic thyroid hormone (TH) action in the liver by selectively targeting thyroid hormone receptor β (ΤΗRβ) with minimal side effects on tissues such as the heart and bone, which express high levels of THRa [10]. Recently, the effectiveness of resmetirom, a selective THRβ agonist, in treating metabolic-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) and liver fibrosis was demonstrated in a 52-week trial [11]. Resmetirom was well tolerated by patients and significantly adds to the options available for treating MASH. It can be used in combination with other medications to optimize metabolic factors, such as glucagon-like peptide-1 and hydroxymethylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase inhibitors [12]. However, further studies are needed to evaluate these combination therapies. On the downside, resmetirom is expensive, costing around $450,000 per year, and its widespread use could place a heavy burden on healthcare systems in both developed and developing countries [13]. Consequently, there is a need for efficient, low-cost alternatives for treating MASH. The synthetic thyroid hormone levothyroxine (LT4) has been successfully used as the primary treatment for hypothyroidism since the latter half of the 20th century [14, 15]. Moreover, it is both effective and affordable, making it a potential alternative therapy for MASH.

This review summarizes the latest research in this field and evaluates the relative advantages and disadvantages of using novel thyromimetics, as well as the potential application of “low-dose LT4” for treating MASLD/MASH.

Methodology

Search strategy

This is a narrative review that employs a structured search strategy. Publications of interest were identified through a PubMed search using the following terms: “metabolic dysfunction associated with steatotic liver disease,” “non-alcoholic fatty liver disease,” “metabolic associated fatty liver disease,” along with “thyroid,” “hypothyroidism,” and “levothyroxine.”

Preference was given to randomized, controlled trials, meta-analyses, and larger observational studies. References in identified publications and the authors’ reference collection were also sources of information.

Terminology

The terms MASLD and MASH have largely replaced the older terms ‘non-alcoholic fatty liver disease’ (NAFLD) and ‘non-alcoholic steatohepatitis’ (NASH). NASH was differentiated from NAFLD by the presence of more severe inflammation and signs of hepatocyte injury (such as ballooning, with or without hepatic fibrosis). The term MASLD has gained popularity in recent discussions. However, it is important to note that these terms do not refer to exactly the same diagnoses. NAFLD and NASH are primarily diagnoses of exclusion, indicating fatty liver disease in individuals without significant alcohol consumption or other causes of secondary fatty liver. In contrast, MASLD and MASH are diagnosed using more objective criteria, which will be described later.

The term “MetALD” is used for those with MASLD who also have increased alcohol consumption (20–50 g/d for women; 30–60 g/day for men). Steatosis in the setting of alcohol consumption above those levels is termed “alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD). The updated nomenclature is widely supported, non-stigmatizing, and enhances clarity, awareness, and patient identification [16].

For consistency and clarity, we will use the terms MASLD and MASH throughout this review, unless the context requires the specific use of one of the alternative terms. MASLD identifies a more consistent group of patients with a steatotic liver resulting from metabolic dysfunction and provides a valuable framework for comprehensive, patient-centered management strategies to enhance health outcomes.

Diagnosis of MASLD

According to a recent consensus report of the American Diabetes Association, MASLD is defined as the presence of steatotic liver disease with at least one metabolic risk factor such as obesity, arterial hypertension, prediabetes, high triglycerides, low high-density lipoprotein (HDL)-cholesterol, or type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), with minimal or no alcohol consumption (< 20 g/d for women; < 30 g/d for men) [17].

However, to accurately determine MASH progression, liver biopsy is considered the gold standard for providing histological evidence of MASH by detecting ballooning and determining the fibrosis stage in patients at higher risk of steatohepatitis and/or advanced fibrosis.

In a longitudinal observational cohort study conducted in Sweden, 959 adults with biopsy-defined MASLD were included. Each patient had a documented fibrosis stage (ranging from F0 to F4) and an age-stratified FIB-4 score (classified as low, indeterminate, or high) at baseline. The study assessed healthcare resource utilization (HCRU) and costs related to MASLD by disease stage, using both biopsy-defined staging and the FIB-4 score to evaluate fibrosis severity. The results showed a positive link between fibrosis severity and both HCRU and costs in MASLD patients. This indicates that the FIB-4 score may serve a similar role as biopsy in assessing MASLD severity and has comparable predictive ability for identifying patients with high HCRU [18].

The diagnosis of MASLD/MASH may soon become sophisticated and possibly more accurate by applying omics technology and artificial intelligence [19]. Expanding diagnosis and treatment for the MASLD population with moderate-to-advanced fibrosis will prevent severe liver disease and death, while also reducing HCRU.

Prevalence of MASLD and MASH and their global health and economic burden

A 2016 meta-analysis reported a global prevalence of MASLD in 32% of adults [20], while another meta-analysis published in 2022, which included data from more than 10 million individuals, estimated a global prevalence of MASLD at 38.8% [21]. The prevalence was lower in non-overweight/non-obese subjects (5.4%) compared to overweight individuals (29.4%; note that this category included overweight subjects without specifically categorizing obese individuals). Another meta-analysis from the same year (based on 2.7 million people) found an “astonishingly high” prevalence of MASLD of 51.7% among overweight or obese subjects [22]. These analyses highlight the link between MASLD and obesity/insulin resistance, as previously mentioned. Cross-sectional data from China collected between 2010 and 2011 revealed a MASLD prevalence of 40.3% in adults aged 40 and older, indicating that the prevalence of MASLD increases with age [23]. In contrast, a meta-analysis found a high prevalence of T2DM at 26.2%, along with an incidence rate of 26.9 cases per 1,000 person-years among individuals with MASLD [24]. The prevalence of MASH is roughly one-tenth that of MASLD in the general population, reaching about 50% among patients with MASLD. Importantly, the prevalence of both MASLD and MASH is rising rapidly. One study predicted that by 2040, more than half of the global population (55%) will have MASLD, representing a threefold increase since 1990 and a doubling since 2020 [25].

The health care resource utilization and medical cost of MASH severity, were analyzed in a cohort study in a real-world setting, based on Fibrosis-4 Index (FIB-4) score [7]. Cardiovascular (CV)- and MASH-related costs were calculated by disease severity, and all-cause hospitalization was significantly higher in the high FIB-4 cohort when compared with the low FIB-4 reference [7].

Increasing awareness, screening, and diagnosis, along with introducing pharmacologic therapies that stop fibrosis progression, are essential to achieve health and economic benefits for these patients [26].

Adverse impact of MASLD on cardiovascular risk and disease

An analysis of 11 studies involving 11,995,994 patients with NAFLD and MASLD found that both conditions are linked to higher risks of cardiovascular and all-cause mortality [27]. However, the likelihood of cardiovascular death seems to be greater in patients with MASLD compared to those with NAFLD, indicating that the use of MASLD criteria may help identify more individuals at risk.

Data from 7,761 participants in the Third National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) indicated that individuals with MASLD face a higher risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality compared to those with NAFLD [28]. Over a median follow-up of 23 years, MASLD was linked to a 17% increased risk of all-cause mortality and heightened cardiovascular mortality risk. The study suggests redefining NAFLD as MASLD and viewing it as part of a broader multi-system disease involving obesity, diabetes, high blood pressure, and high cholesterol, potentially improving mortality risk prediction.

The impact of MASLD on CV risk depends on the stage of the disease. MASLD has also been associated with an increased risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD) [29].

A nationwide health screening involved 9,584,399 participants aged 40–64, categorized by NAFLD and MASLD. The prevalence rates were 28.0% for NAFLD and 37.3% for MASLD [30]. Participants without prior cardiovascular disease were followed for a median of 10.1 years, and both conditions were linked to a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular events. The progression from simple hepatic steatosis to cirrhosis is low, but once MASH develops, cirrhosis occurs in 20% of cases, making MASH a major risk factor for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [31]. The prevalence of HCC has increased by 20% since 1980 and is expected to rise further [32].

Association of MASLD/MASH with hypothyroidism

Despite some findings that overt or subclinical hypothyroidism (OH and SCH, respectively), are associated with a constellation of cardiometabolic risk factors closely resembling those associated with MetS, inconsistencies remain [33].

Hypothyroidism and MASLD often coexist [34], and the link between hypothyroidism and MASLD exists independently of other risk factors [35, 36]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of twenty-six studies involving 61,548 participants indicated that patients with MASLD/MASH had significantly higher serum thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels compared to controls [37]. However, the statistical results were inconsistent among the subgroup analyses for SCH and hypothyroidism. Notably, an increase in TSH levels—even within the normal reference range—was identified as an independent risk factor for MASLD. This increase may also influence the progression of liver fibrosis, even when FT4 levels remain normal. Multivariate regression analysis showed that NAFLD was statistically significantly associated with hypothyroidism (odds ratio (OR) 1.38, 95% confidence interval (CI), 1.17–1.62) and the grade of hypothyroidism in a dose-dependent manner [37].

Interestingly, another meta-analysis based on data from 14 studies involving 7,191 NAFLD patients and 30,003 controls found no significant association between MASLD and either OH or SCH [38]. Additionally, there was no significant difference in TH levels between participants with and without MASLD. Ethnic differences may also be a factor to consider among various influences.

Nonetheless, SCH with MASLD, is associated with increased odds of MetS, insulin resistance, and coronary artery calcification, independent of potential confounders [39]. Hypothyroidism is more prevalent among patients with MASLD, and MASLD is also more common in those with hypothyroidism [40].

A study analyzing data from 360 U.S. hospitals over five years (2015–2020) included 37.648.180 individuals aged 18 and older and reported 2,320 cases of MASLD, or 6.16 cases per 100.000 [41]. Of these, 520 patients (22.4%) had hypothyroidism. While both genders were equally affected, individuals aged 18 to 65 and Caucasian patients were at higher risk. The odds ratio for MASLD in hypothyroid patients was 1.587, indicating a significantly increased risk. The prevalence of SCH is approximately twice as high in patients with MASLD. Patients with MASLD also tend to have, on average, 26% higher (TSH) levels [41]. Patients with MASLD are more likely to experience obesity and other components of metabolic syndrome, such as T2DM and high blood pressure. The findings suggest that metabolic status, age, gender and race can be taken into account when assessing SCH in individuals with MASLD. Thyroid function appears to be linked to the risk of MASLD and MASH, even in euthyroid individuals [8].

Other meta-analyses also reported that MASLD is closely associated with the presence of SCH and/or OH. A total of 12 cross-sectional and 3 longitudinal studies involving 44,140 individuals found an association between OH and SCH with MASLD, independent of age, sex, body mass index, and other common metabolic risk factors [9]. However, the study design (meta-analysis of observational studies) did not allow for establishing causality. In an update of this study, 24 cross-sectional and 4 longitudinal studies with aggregate data on approximately 76.5 million individuals were identified [42]. Primary hypothyroidism, defined as LT4 replacement therapy, SCH, or OH, is associated with an increased risk of prevalent MASLD (n = 24 studies; random-effects odds ratio 1.43, 95% CI 1.23 to 1.66; I²=89%). Although LT4 replacement treatment may not indicate primary hypothyroidism, the findings of this extensive data analysis from the reevaluation study suggest a heightened risk of MASLD associated with thyroid dysfunction.

Low thyroid function in MASLD is likely an independent predictor of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Among the spectrum of thyroid function, mild SCH showed the highest hazard ratios for mortality [43]. Increased circulating interleukin-27 (IL-27) in hypothyroidism has been proposed as a potential pathophysiological link between hypothyroidism and MASLD [44]. Serum TSH levels are frequently increased in severe obesity; thus, reduced thyroid function may be yet another pathophysiological link between obesity and MASLD.

Moreover, liver steatosis and fibrosis have been associated with circulating leptin. In a cross-sectional study, 63 of 86 patients (73.3%) followed for weight management had liver steatosis, and 17 (19.8%) had liver fibrosis [45]. The mean leptin levels were 22.3 ± 14.1 ng/mL, while BMI and waist circumference measured 36.7 ± 7.2 kg/m² and 114.5 ± 16.4 cm, respectively. The results suggest that circulating leptin concentrations are independently associated with hepatic fibrosis and may promote hepatic fibrosis in individuals with a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² [45]. However, there are no longitudinal studies.

In an observational study that utilized prospectively collected liver biopsies and clinical data from youth enrolled in two pediatric clinical trials within the Nonalcoholic Steatohepatitis Clinical Research Network (NASH CRN), researchers investigated the relationship between TSH levels and the histological characteristics of MASLD in young individuals [46]. They compared the TSH levels of youth aged 18 years and younger with MASLD to those of healthy controls using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). The findings revealed that TSH levels were positively associated with an increase in liver steatosis over time (p = 0.03). Additionally, SCH was linked to the presence of borderline or definite MASH on histological examination at baseline (p = 0.03), as well as changes in liver fibrosis over time (p = 0.01) [46]. This connection between TSH levels and the severity of steatosis in young individuals suggests that TSH may play an independent role in MASLD and supports previously reported data in adults [37].

Mechanisms of disease and the thyromimetics

Brief overview of thyroid signaling

We reference some expert reviews that cover this topic in detail [47–49].

The circulating levels of thyroid hormones – and their effects on target tissues – are regulated by a complex series of overlapping feedback loops. T3 binds to intracellular THRs, which primarily reside in the nucleus and regulate the transcription of multiple target genes involved in the pleiotropic actions of TH on metabolism [50]. Two principal TH receptor isoforms, THRα and THRβ, are expressed in human tissues [51]. Each tissue type expresses varying amounts of these two THRs isoforms [52]. The THRα1 isoform is mainly expressed in the heart, adipose tissue, brain, and bone [53]. It plays important roles in cardiac, adipose, and bone development, and regulates CV and heart rate [53]. On the other hand, THRβ1 is predominantly expressed in the liver and kidney and mediates many of the metabolic actions of THs in those tissues [54]. THRβ is essential for the metabolism of lipids and carbohydrates in the liver [55]. Disruption of THRβ signaling can result in metabolic disorders, which may contribute to the development of MASLD and its progression to MASH and cirrhosis [56]. Emerging evidence suggests a connection between MASLD and subclinical intrahepatic hypothyroidism, characterized by relatively low levels of hepatic T3 while maintaining normal circulating thyroid hormone (TH) levels [57]. In euthyroid individuals with MASH, there is a decreased conversion of prohormone T4 to bioactive T3 and an increased conversion of T4 to the inactive metabolite reverse T3 [58]. Consequently, reduced levels of T3 result in impaired intrahepatic THRβ signaling, a state of intrahepatic hypothyroidism, which promotes progression of MASH. Hepatic THRβ activation leads to metabolically beneficial effects in the liver including mitochondrial fatty acid uptake and β-oxidation, mitochondrial biogenesis, increasing surface low-density lipoprotein-receptor (LDLR)-density and lowering of circulatory LDL-cholesterol [58].

Insulin resistance is linked to obesity and diabetes, causing lipolysis in fat tissue and raising serum FFA levels. These FFAs, along with those from the diet or produced in the liver, are transformed into triglycerides and stored in liver cells [59]. When T3 activates THRβ, it boosts lipid turnover and β-oxidation of FFAs via selective autophagy, a process that removes triglycerides from lipid droplets [60]. As a result, the increase of FFA oxidation enhances mitochondrial function, which reduces liver fat content and may help lessen hepatic steatosis [61]. TH decreases liver inflammation and fibrosis in animal models of MASH, via autophagy of inflammasomes and reduction of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress [62]. Moreover, in hepatic steatosis, DIO1 expression may initially increase to maintain intrahepatic T3 levels. However, as MASH advances, DIO1 decreases and DIO3 increases, leading to “intrahepatic hypothyroidism” despite normal circulating T3 and T4 levels [62]. Notably, patients with mutations in the THRβ gene that reduce TH binding affinity also experience higher levels of hepatic steatosis and more liver fibrosis compared to controls [63].

Use of thyromimetics and LT4 for the treatment of MASLD/MASH

These preclinical and clinical findings stimulated the design and development of liver- and THRβ-selective thyromimetics for MASH. Their tissue- and isoform-selectivity theoretically reduces the risk of cardiac and bone toxicity since these latter tissues primarily express THRa. In this line of evidence, resmetirom, was reported to be effective in reducing fibrosis in MASH and in the phase 3 evaluation it was shown that randomisation to resmetirom doubled the number of patients with resolution of MASLD and improvement of fibrosis as compared to those treated with placebo (29.9% vs. 9.7%) [11, 64]. The results were confirmed by a meta-analysis of pooled data from four placebo-controlled, randomised studies of resmetirom [65, 66]. The highest dose of resmetirom reduced hepatic fat content by almost 40% (measured by liver biopsy and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)) in patients with MASH, compared to the placebo group and also decreased triglycerides, liver enzymes, and serum FT4 levels. There were no significant safety concerns, although gastrointestinal side effects appeared to be more common (≥ 10% ) in the resmetirom group compared to placebo at < 12 weeks of treatment [66]. Several other thyromimetics in earlier stages of development also have had beneficial effects in preclinical models of MASLD/MASH [56, 67]. In addition to TH effects on the liver mediated by hepatic THRβ, preclinical data suggest that TH may inhibit absorption of lipids from the gut or affect the gut microbiome in the setting of MAFLD to reduce hepatic lipotoxicity [68].

The understanding that intracellular TH concentrations are low in MASLD and that the activation of THR is reduced, opens up new opportunities for intervention using TH [69]. Recently, a single-arm study involving 20 male subjects with T2DM and MASLD evaluated the effects of LT4 on hepatosteatosis [70]. LT4 was titrated to maintain TSH levels within the low-normal range (0.34–1.7 mIU/L) for 16 weeks, with a mean LT4 dose of 18.75 µg/day. The primary outcome of the trial, intrahepatic lipid content, was reduced in 75% of the patients, with a mean reduction of 12%. %. A post-hoc analysis of a randomized controlled trial found that 15 months of LT4 treatment in a group of 330 individuals with SCH and TSH levels > 10 mIU/L resulted in a reduction of the prevalence of MASLD from 48% to 24%, compared to a control group that did not receive LT4 treatment [71]. It is important to note that there was no significant reduction in the prevalence of MASLD among the 330 subjects with SCH and serum T4 levels less than 10 mIU/L. However, within a subpopulation of these subjects who had dyslipidemia at baseline, a significant reduction in MASLD prevalence was observed [71] (Table 1).

Pros and cons of using thyromimetics and LT4 for the treatment of MASLD/MASH

Virtually every cell in the body is responsive to TH; however, the relative expression of THR isoforms varies considerably among different tissues. The rationale for developing liver- and/or THRβ-selective activators for the treatment of MASLD was to achieve liver- and THRβ-selectivity. Thyromimetic compounds may mimic T3 in the liver, potentially preventing or treating MASH while minimizing adverse effects on bones and the heart, which primarily express thyroid hormone receptor alpha (THRα) [72]. Additionally, selective THRβ activators may inhibit binding with lower affinity to THRs, whose activation in hepatic stellate cells (HSC) is thought to be crucial for the development and progression of hepatic fibrosis.

Interestingly, low and medium concentrations of T3 can reduce inflammation and oxidative damage in cases of acute alcoholic liver injury, inhibit HSC activation, and delay the onset and progression of liver fibrosis in mice [72].

In an animal study, applying a acute alcoholic liver injury model in mice, low to moderate (25 µg/Kg -50 µg/Kg) T3 administration inhibits the phosphoinositide 3-kinase/serine-threonine kinase (PI3K/AKT) and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathways, increases the levels of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), and restores liver autophagy [73]. The activation of HSC into matrix-producing myofibroblasts can be prevented by antagonizing soluble factors (e.g., integrins, cytokines), stimulating nuclear receptor signaling, and TH administration [73]. In advanced fibrosis, cell therapy with reparative macrophages may accelerate repair by deactivating HSC. TRα and TRβ expression is downregulated in the liver during hepatic fibrogenesis in humans and mice [74]. TRα and TRβ expression decreases in the liver during hepatic fibrogenesis in both humans and mice. TRα is the predominant isoform found in hepatic stellate cells (HSC). In vitro studies show that T3 inhibits the TGFβ-induced expression of fibrogenic genes in HSC and disrupts wound healing by modulating TGFβ signaling, an effect that relies on the presence of TRα [74].

These actions highlight significant differences between thyromimetics and traditional TH. Moreover, it is essential to note that high concentrations of T3 (100 µg/Kg) may have the opposite effect.

LT4 is currently one of the most-prescribed drugs worldwide, and its utility and safety profile are well-established after seven decades of use and accumulating experience. The main safety considerations for LT4 relate to its risk of iatrogenic thyrotoxicosis due to overtreatment. However, this is rare when treatment is given alone or in combination with T3 by expert hands. In the clinical study on the use of LT4 treatment for hepatic steatosis, very low doses of LT4 were administered [70]. The median maintenance dose was 18.75 µg, with a range from 12.5 µg every two days to 87.5 µg daily. This dosage effectively lowered TSH levels to the lower end of the reference range (0.34–1.70 mIU/L) and was adequate to stimulate the expression of the DIO1 gene in the liver [70, 75]. No serious adverse events were reported during the study; however, three patients discontinued treatment due to grade-related adverse effects 1 (71).

Nevertheless, caution is necessary, as low LT4 treatment by activating THRα may cause adverse effects on the heart, bones, and gut. Monitoring patients and adjusting the LT4 dosage accordingly is recommended.

Another important consideration is that LT4 is a relatively inexpensive drug, making it a potentially valuable low-cost alternative to thyromimetics for treating MASH. Its use could be particularly beneficial for MASH patients in developing nations who may not afford thyromimetics therapy. Furthermore, widespread adoption of LT4 could lead to significant cost savings for healthcare systems in both developing and developed countries, addressing a critical issue related to managing conditions with high prevalence, such as MAFLD.

Recent advancements in LT4 formulations and the use of precision dosing, patient-centered algorithms, a broader range of tablet strengths, and enhanced chemical stability support accurate dosing and maintenance of TSH-guided LT4 therapy [76]. If LT4 can effectively lower TSH to near the lower end of the reference range without causing side effects, it could be useful in preventing and reducing the progression of MASLD and MASH.

Given that DIO1 expression is downregulated in advanced MASH, it may be interesting to consider T3 or T3/T4 combination therapies in patients with late-stage MASH. Currently, there is a lack of clinical data on LT4 and/or LT3 therapy in large patient populations with MASLD or MASH, highlighting the need for controlled trials to evaluate the efficacy of this treatment approach. If its effectiveness is confirmed, it will be crucial to establish the appropriate LT4 dosage to minimize or eliminate the need for titration.

Conclusions

The selective activation of hepatic THRβ offers a promising approach for treating MASLD and MASH. However, while LT4 shows some efficacy in treating MASLD, concerns remain about its effectiveness for MASH [11, 75]. Identifying which patients will respond positively to these treatments is essential, and non-invasive markers of TH action in the liver may be useful for monitoring therapy.

The activation of HSCs plays a crucial role in the progression of hepatic fibrosis. A promising therapeutic approach for treating hepatic fibrosis focuses on slowing the proliferation of activated HSC and enhancing their removal from the injured liver tissue. Innovative strategies for achieving this clearance may involve the use of autophagy inhibitors, pro-apoptotic agents, and senescence inducers specifically targeting activated HSC. The projected rise in MASLD cases by 2040 raises concerns about the prohibitive costs of branded drugs for many patients. Developing liver/THRβ-selective agents is necessary to avoid unwanted activation of THRα in tissues, and using low-dose LT4 might be a potentially useful and cost-effective alternative. Further research is required to confirm the efficacy and safety of LT4 for MASLD and MASH treatment.
