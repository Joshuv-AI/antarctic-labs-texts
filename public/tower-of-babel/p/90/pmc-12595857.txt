Investigation of methylation profiles in Silver–Russell syndrome to explore episignatures
Authors: Kaori Hara-Isono, Takanobu Inoue, Akie Nakamura, Tomoko Fuke, Kazuki Yamazawa, Keiko Matsubara, Maki Fukami, Tsutomu Ogata, Tomoko Kawai, Masayo Kagami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpisignatures are disease-specific, genome-wide DNA methylation patterns identified in more than 100 genetic syndromes caused by mutation of genes related to epigenetic modifiers. The utility of episignatures as a disease diagnostic tool has been demonstrated in recent years. However, studies evaluating episignatures in imprinting disorders (IDs) are very limited. We hypothesized that identifying episignatures in Silver–Russell syndrome (SRS), one of the representative IDs, could lead to the development of a new diagnostic tool for SRS. We investigated methylation profiles in patients with different molecular etiologies to clarify the presence or absence of DNA methylation episignatures in SRS.ResultsWe conducted genome-wide methylation analysis (GWMA) using the HumanMethylationEPIC array in 27 patients consisting of five groups based on molecular etiologies satisfying clinical diagnostic criteria: hypomethylation of H19-differentially methylated region (DMR) (n = 9), maternal uniparental disomy chromosome 7 (n = 11), IGF2 variant (n = 3), PLAG1 variant (n = 2), and deletions including KCNQ1OT1:TSS-DMR (n = 2). We compared the methylation levels of CpGs (β values) between each group and control group (94 pediatric samples) and extracted aberrantly methylated CpGs satisfying the following criteria: (1) Bonferroni-corrected p value < 0.05, and (2) the absolute value of ∆β (|∆β|), the difference between the average β value of each ID group and controls at each probe, was above 0.1. Based on the extracted aberrantly methylated CpGs, we examined (1) aberrantly methylated patterns, (2) aberrantly methylated regions, and 3) CpG-enriched genes and their functions, shared by multiple groups. Although several CpGs were commonly hypermethylated or hypomethylated in two of the five groups, the number of CpGs was too small to form characteristic methylation patterns. Several aberrantly methylated regions, including disease-responsible DMRs of SRS, were identified in each group, but no regions common in multiple groups were identified. Previously reported aberrantly methylated regions other than disease-responsible DMRs were not identified. In addition, we found no CpG-enriched genes or their functions common in multiple groups.ConclusionsGWMA in SRS patients with different molecular etiologies identified no characteristic episignatures. Only methylation changes of CpGs within disease-responsible DMRs, not genome-wide methylation changes, occur in some SRS etiologies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-025-01993-y.

Background

Episignatures are disease-specific, genome-wide DNA methylation patterns identified in genetic syndromes mainly caused by abnormalities in genes involved in epigenetic regulatory mechanisms, such as histone modification and chromatin remodeling [1]. For example, episignatures were identified in Kabuki syndrome caused by dysfunctions of lysine methyltransferase 2D (KMT2D) and lysine demethylase 6 A (KDM6A) [2], Sotos syndrome and Rauch–Steindl syndrome caused by dysfunction of histone H3 lysine 36 methyltransferase [3, 4], and Coffin-Siris and Nicolaides-Baraitser syndromes caused by disruption of the BAF chromatin remodeling complex [5].To date, nearly 70 distinct episignatures for more than 100 Mendelian disorders have been reported, and these episignatures are used as biomarkers for diagnosis of neurodevelopmental syndromes [6]. A previous study assessing the diagnostic utility of episignatures showed that 312 (18.7%) of 1667 patients with one or more phenotypes of disorders having episignatures showed positive results [6]. Moreover, episignatures have been used to determine the pathogenicity of the variants of unknown significance (VUS). For example, Kawai et al. reported a patient with Kabuki syndrome carrying both a nonsense variant in KDM6A, defined as deleterious, and a missense variant in KMT2D, defined as VUS. Hierarchical clustering analysis based on the 149 episignature probes for Kabuki syndrome demonstrated that the KDM6A variant detected in this patient was responsible for Kabuki syndrome phenotype [7].

Imprinting disorders (IDs) are clinical syndromes caused by abnormal expression of imprinted genes, which show parental origin specific expression patterns [8]. The differentially methylated region (DMR) has a parental origin specific DNA methylation pattern and regulates the expression of the imprinted gene. Silver–Russell syndrome (SRS) is one of the representative IDs characterized by prenatal and postnatal growth disturbances. The Netchine-Harbison clinical scoring system (NH-CSS) is recommended as clinical diagnostic criteria for SRS by an international consensus statement [9] and consists of prenatal and postnatal growth failure, relative macrocephaly at birth, protruding forehead, feeding difficulty, and body asymmetry [10]. Furthermore, patients with SRS often show additional clinical features such as triangular face and fifth finger clinodactyly. The patient with four or more of the six criteria in NH-CSS meets the SRS clinical criteria, and the patient with three of the six criteria and one of the additional clinical features is classified as clinical suspicion of SRS with the recommendation to take a molecular test for SRS. The major molecular etiologies of SRS are hypomethylation of the H19/IGF2:IG-DMR (50%) (H19LOM) and maternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 7 (UPD(7)mat) (10%) [9]. In addition, copy number variants on the 11p15.5 region (< 3%) and pathogenic variants of CDKN1C, IGF2, PLAG1, and HMGA2 genes cause SRS [11]. However, more than 50% of patients with SRS have unknown genetic causes, reflecting the genetic heterogeneity and non-specific clinical features of SRS.

Although methylation defects in disease-responsible DMRs are one of the main etiologies of IDs, studies evaluating episignatures in IDs, including SRS, are very limited. Aref-Eshgni et al. established a support vector machine model using the genome-wide array-based methylation analysis data [12]. This model accurately classified 14 different syndromes with distinct episignatures, such as Kabuki syndrome, Sotos syndrome, and CHARGE syndrome, and distinguished them from healthy controls. On the other hand, this model classified IDs, including SRS (n = 64), Angelman syndrome (n = 14), Prader-Willi syndrome (n = 7), and Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome (n = 9) into the same category as healthy control subjects [12]. Although this study demonstrated the specificity of other known diseases with episignatures compared to IDs, genome-wide episignatures in SRS or other IDs were not directly investigated. Moreover, this study did not assess the diagnostic criteria or molecular etiologies of SRS in the included cases. Another study demonstrated clinical validation of a genome-wide methylation analysis (GWMA) for molecular diagnosis in 28 patients with IDs, including a single SRS patient with UPD(7)mat [13]. As shown in Table 1, GWMA in patients with SRS, including H19LOM, UPD(7)mat, and unknown etiologies, identified several novel aberrantly methylated regions other than H19/IGF2:IG-DMR, for example, hypermethylation of the RB1:Int2-DMR [14], hypomethylation of the CpG sites located at the 5′UTR of the OSBPL5 gene [15], and hypomethylation of the promoter of the HOXA4 gene [16]. Muurinen et al. examined more than 450,000 CpG sites in 44 cases with SRS and detected a hypomethylated region in the promoter of the HOXA4 gene in 55% of cases. Furthermore, 44% of 39 cases with severe growth restriction of unknown etiology also showed the hypomethylation at the same region; the authors hypothesized that HOXA4 plays an important role in regulating human growth [16]. However, previous studies performing GWMA in SRS patients adopted original criteria other than the NH-CSS as diagnostic criteria for SRS and included many patients with unknown etiologies. In addition, these studies focused on the aberrantly methylated regions, not the episignatures, in SRS, and the detected regions were inconsistent. Previously, we examined aberrantly methylated regions in 16 SRS cases with H19LOM using the HumanMethylation450 BeadChip and found no aberrantly methylated region other than disease-responsible DMRs [17]. Considering this result, we extended our investigation to SRS patients having different molecular etiologies, analyzing a great number of CpGs using the HumanMethylationEPIC array, and evaluating the existence or absence of episignatures as well as aberrantly methylated regions. Here, we report the results of GWMA using the HumanMethylationEPIC BeadChip array (Illumina, San Diego, CA) obtained from 27 patients with SRS consisting of five different molecular etiologies and diagnosed based on NH-CSS. We hypothesized that if the specific episignature for SRS was identified, we could propose a new diagnostic tool for SRS. To investigate the presence or absence of DNA methylation episignatures in SRS, we conducted GWMA in patients with SRS classified into five groups based on the molecular etiology and examined 1) aberrantly methylated patterns consisting of commonly hypermethylated or hypomethylated CpGs in multiple groups, 2) aberrantly hypermethylated or hypomethylated regions common in multiple groups, and 3) CpG-enriched genes and their functions based on the extracted aberrantly methylated CpGs shared by multiple groups.

Results

Subjects

We included 27 patients with SRS consisting of (1) nine patients with H19LOM caused by epimutations (H19LOM group), (2) eleven patients with UPD(7)mat (UPD(7)mat group), (3) three patients with IGF2 variants consisting of two patients with missense variants (p.Cys70Tyr and p.Cys71Arg) and one patient with a frameshift variant (p.Leu37Glnfs*31) (IGF2 variant group) [18–20], (4) two patients with PLAG1 variants consisting of a mother and her child with the same missense variant (p.Arg197*) (PLAG1 variant group) [18], and (5) two patients with microdeletions affecting the paternally inherited KCNQ1OT1:TSS-DMR (Kv-DMR del group) [21]. The molecular etiologies and clinical characteristics of patients are shown in Supplementary Table S1. All patients other than Patient 25 (the mother of Patient 24) had four or more NH-CSS features or three NH-CSS features plus triangular face and/or short 5th clinodactyly. Patient 25 with a PLAG1 pathogenic variant did not have sufficient clinical information. As normal controls, we used methylation data of 16 healthy pediatric controls [22] and those of 78 pediatric controls obtained from public databases (GSE180534, GSE193730).

GWMA

We carried out GWMA using the HumanMethylationEPIC BeadChip array (Illumina, San Diego, CA) featuring 850,000 CpGs across the human genome. We first compared the methylation levels (β values) in all filtering passed CpGs between all 27 patients with SRS and the controls, followed by comparisons between each group and the control group, using multivariate linear regression modeling adjusting for cell variations in blood, sex, and batch effects as previously reported [23]. Then we calculated the difference between the average β value of each group and the control group at each probe site (∆β). We defined the aberrant methylated CpGs with Bonferroni-corrected p value < 0.05, the absolute value of ∆β (|∆β|) above 0.1, and specificity to patients with SRS. The comparison between all 27 patients with SRS, irrespective of molecular etiology, and the controls revealed no common aberrantly methylated CpGs specific to SRS. The comparisons between each group and the control group showed 287, 1273, 402, 1530, and 676 aberrantly methylated CpGs in the H19LOM group, UPD(7)mat group, IGF2 variant group, PLAG1 variant group, and Kv-DMR del group, respectively.

Aberrantly methylated patterns in patients with SRS having different molecular etiology

To examine the presence or absence of aberrantly methylated patterns common in different molecular etiologies for SRS, we investigated commonly hypermethylated or hypomethylated CpGs among the five groups. The number of extracted aberrantly hypermethylated or hypomethylated CpGs in each group are shown in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S2. No CpGs were commonly hypermethylated or hypomethylated among all five groups. Only one CpG was commonly hypermethylated in the H19 LOM and UPD(7)mat groups and PLAG1variant and Kv-DMR del groups. Three CpGs were commonly hypomethylated in the PLAG1 variant and UPD(7)mat groups and in the PLAG1 variant and IGF2 variant groups, and six CpGs were commonly hypomethylated in the PLAG1 variant and Kv-DMR del groups. These findings showed that the numbers of commonly hypermethylated or hypomethylated CpGs in two of the five groups were too small to form characteristic methylation patterns. Regarding each group, detected aberrantly methylated CpGs were localized only in disease-responsible DMRs and did not form an episignature in the H19LOM, UPD(7)mat, or Kv-DMR del groups. To evaluate the utility of GWMA for the genetic diagnosis of SRS, we examined the methylation levels of CpGs within disease-responsible DMRs associated with SRS in the H19LOM, UPD(7)mat, and Kv-DMR del groups. We confirmed alteration in disease-responsible DMRs in each group, namely, hypomethylation of H19/IGF2:IG-DMR, IGF2:Ex9-DMR, and IGF2:alt-TSS-DMR in the H19LOM group, hypermethylation of GRB10:alt-TSS-DMR, PEG10:TSS-DMR, and MEST:alt-TSS-DMR in the UPD(7)mat group, and hypermethylation of KCNQ1OT1:TSS-DMR in the Kv-DMR del group (Supplementary Fig. 1). In addition, in the UPD(7)mat group, we compared the aberrantly hypermethylated CpGs identified in this study with those detected in a patient with UPD(7)mat in the study by Aref-Eshghi et al. [13] (Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S2). We identified aberrantly methylated CpGs at the same SRS-associated imprinted loci (GRB10, PEG10, and MEST loci) as those detected in a patient with UPD(7)mat reported by Aref-Eshghi et al. [13]. We also found aberrantly methylated CpGs at additional loci on chromosome 7, including known regions with allele-specific methylation (RPS2P32, SVOPL, and HTR5A loci). Regarding GRB10 and MEST loci, the regions with aberrantly hypermethylated CpGs were expanded by approximately 1 kb due to the greater number of probes in the HumanMethylationEPIC array compared with the HumanMethylation450 BeadChip used in the report by Aref-Eshghi et al. [13].

Aberrantly methylated regions in patients with SRS having different molecular etiology

We examined commonly hypermethylated or hypomethylated regions among the five groups. In this regard, we evaluated whether extracted aberrantly methylated CpGs in each group constituted aberrantly methylated regions, i.e., three or more consecutive aberrantly methylated CpGs within 1000 bp from transcript start sites of genes or known imprinted DMRs. As shown in Fig. 2, several aberrantly methylated regions, including known imprinted DMRs, were identified in each group, except for the IGF2 variant group. In the H19LOM group, only disease-responsible DMRs, such as H19/IGF2:IG-DMR and IGF2:Ex9-DMR, were identified. In the UPD(7)mat group, five regions (except for the vicinity of the TNNI3 gene) were located on chromosome 7. Several aberrantly methylated regions other than disease-responsible DMRs were identified in the PLAG1 variant group (vicinity of VWA5B1, ANKMY1, LRTM2, ZNF300, and CD93 genes), and the Kv-DMR del groups (vicinity of the HLTF gene). We identified no common aberrantly methylated regions in multiple groups. To validate our results, we compared the actual methylation levels (β values) of the HOXA4 promoter region of all patients in our study and all cases in Muurinen’s study [16] using publicly available data (GEO accession number: GSE104451). Gene enrichment analysis based on aberrantly methylated CpGs

We further performed gene enrichment analysis based on the extracted aberrantly methylated CpGs in each group, as follows: 287, 1273, 402, 1530, and 676 CpGs in the H19LOM group, UPD(7)mat group, IGF2 variant group, PLAG1 variant group, and Kv-DMR del group, respectively.

First, we evaluated the distribution of aberrantly methylated CpGs per gene to examine whether CpGs are enriched for specific genes (Fig. 3). Each CpG was annotated to the gene with the closest transcript start site using the Homer annotation tool [24]. Consistent with the results of methylation analysis, aberrantly methylated CpGs were enriched in H19 and IGF2 (hypomethylation) in the H19LOM group, PEG10 and MEST (hypermethylation) in the UPD(7)mat group, and KCNQ1OT1 (hypermethylation) in the Kv-DMR del group. Several genes had enriched more than five aberrantly methylated CpGs in each group, but genes with enriched CpGs commonly among multiple groups were not observed. Next, to evaluate the gene function shared by multiple groups, we performed gene ontology analysis using the Genomic Regions Enrichment Annotation Tool (GREAT) [25] and examined the SRS phenotype-related or pathogenesis-related terms common in multiple groups (Table 2). In the UPD(7)mat group, 24 of 945 hypermethylated CpGs were related to cranial skeletal system development and 12 of 328 hypomethylated CpGs were related to embryonic digit morphogenesis. In the Kv-DMR del group, 47 of 127 hypermethylated CpGs were related to cell–cell signaling. No terms were identified in the H19LOM and IGF2 variant groups. Although 28 hypomethylated CpGs in the UPD(7)mat group and 26 hypermethylated CpGs in the PLAG1 variant group were related to anterior/posterior pattern specification, SRS phenotype-related or pathogenesis-related terms common in multiple groups were not identified. Discussion

This study may be the first trial to evaluate the DNA methylation episignature in patients meeting SRS clinical diagnostic criteria with different molecular etiologies using GWMA across the human genome. No common aberrantly methylated CpGs were identified either in the comparison between all SRS patients and the controls, or in the comparisons between each genetic etiology group and the controls. Therefore, we detected no SRS-characteristic aberrantly methylated patterns or regions and demonstrated the absence of SRS-characteristic episignature. A previous study identified the episignatures for each of 14 syndromes with known disease-episignatures and evaluated their specificity against both healthy controls and IDs [12]. However, it did not directly assess episignatures in patients with IDs, including SRS. Moreover, the clinical features related to NH-CSS and molecular etiologies in 64 patients with SRS were not described. Our study included patients meeting SRS clinical diagnostic criteria and having known genetic causes of SRS showed consistent results. Therefore, it is very likely that SRS has no disease-specific episignature. We speculate that IDs, including SRS, have methylation changes only in CpGs within disease-responsible DMRs, whereas disorders caused by the dysfunction of genes regulating epigenetic mechanisms have genome-wide methylation changes that form episignatures. In addition, our study detected no episignature in each molecular etiology for SRS. This suggests that no genome-wide methylation changes specific to the molecular etiology occur in SRS.

To evaluate the diagnostic potential of GWMA for SRS, we examined the methylation levels of CpGs within disease-responsible DMRs associated with SRS in the H19LOM, UPD(7)mat, and Kv-DMR del groups. We confirmed alterations in disease-responsible DMRs in these groups, suggesting the potential utility of GWMA for detecting known SRS-associated methylation alterations in imprinted regions. Furthermore, in the UPD(7)mat group, we identified aberrantly methylated CpGs at the same loci reported by Aref-Eshghi et al. [13] as well as the other loci on chromosome 7 with allele-specific methylation, supporting the applicability of GWMA for the diagnosis of UPD(7)mat. Regarding GRB10 and MEST loci, the aberrantly methylated regions detected in our study were expanded by approximately 1 kb due to the greater number of probes in the HumanMethylationEPIC array compared with the HumanMethylation450 BeadChip used in the report by Aref-Eshghi et al. [13].

Results of exploring aberrantly methylated regions in SRS revealed several notable findings. First, there was no aberrantly methylated region in SRS shared by different molecular etiologies. The previous three studies conducting GWMA for patients with SRS reported several commonly hypermethylated or hypomethylated regions other than H19/IGF2:IG-DMR among SRS patients, but results were inconsistent (Table 1). Previous studies included a large number of cases with unknown etiologies, used different diagnostic criteria for SRS other than NH-CSS, and obtained methylation data using a previous type of array, the HumanMethylation450K BeadChip, targeting 450,000 CpGs. Furthermore, analysis methods and models used to extract aberrantly methylated CpGs in GWMA were varied among reports (Table 1). In our study, we included the patients with molecularly diagnosed SRS meeting NH-CSS criteria. Moreover, using the latest version of the array, the HumanMethylationEPIC BeadChip, we obtained methylation levels of 850,000 CpGs and utilized multivariate linear regression modeling according to the current recommendation for the methods for GWMA [23]. Considering these facts, we believe that our study provides the most robust results compared to previous reports. Regarding the HOXA4 promoter region, most patients in Muurinen’s study showed hypomethylation of the HOXA4 promoter region, whereas several patients in our study showed hypomethylation or hypermethylation of the same region (Supplementary Table S3). We consider that this inconsistency occurred due to the differences in clinical diagnostic criteria of SRS and definitions of aberrantly methylated CpGs between the two studies. Second, consistent with our previous study evaluating SRS patients with H19LOM [17], no aberrantly methylated regions other than disease-responsible DMRs were identified in the H19LOM group. In this study, we expanded the cohort to include SRS-associated molecular etiologies other than H19LOM, evaluated a greater number of CpGs using the HumanMethylation-EPIC array, and increased the number of controls with publicly available data. On the other hand, we found several novel aberrantly methylated regions other than disease-responsible DMRs in other groups, such as the vicinity of the TNNI3 gene in the UPD(7)mat group, the vicinity of VWA5B1, ANKMY1, LRTM2, ZNF300, and CD93 genes in the PLAG1 variant group, and the vicinity of the HLTF gene in the Kv-DMR del group. However, no associations were found between these genes and SRS phenotype or pathogenesis.

In our study, we found specific genes enriched in CpGs with aberrant methylation involving disease-responsible DMRs in the H19LOM, UPD(7)mat, and Kv-DMR del groups, but did not identify genes enriched for aberrantly methylated CpGs common in multiple groups. These results also show that no genome-wide methylation changes occur in patients with SRS, but methylation changes confined to disease-responsible DMRs occur in the H19LOM, UPD(7)mat, and Kv-DMR groups. Although several genes were enriched for aberrantly methylated CpGs in each group, there was no association between these genes and SRS phenotype or pathogenesis. In addition, we could not obtain gene ontology terms which related to SRS phenotype or pathogenesis by GREAT analysis, suggesting that aberrantly methylated CpGs did not have functions related to SRS phenotype and pathogenesis. Although both hypomethylated CpGs in the UPD(7)mat group and hypermethylated CpGs in the PLAG1 variant group were related to the anterior/posterior pattern specification, we considered that this result is not particularly meaningful, because the direction of the extracted CpGs was opposite despite being of the same phenotype.

Our study has some limitations. First, our study focused only on the methylation patterns of genomic DNA from leukocytes because current analysis for episignatures is limited to peripheral blood where large reference databases are available. In addition, in cases with SRS caused by H19LOM and UPD(7)mat, methylation abnormality occurs in a mosaic manner. Therefore, it is possible that episignatures are masked due to the presence of mosaicism in these two groups. Examination in other tissues may show more promising methylation changes. Further investigation and establishment of reference datasets in other tissues such as buccal cells or fibroblasts will be needed to identify the episignature even in the presence of mosaicism. Second, the sample size in our study was insufficient to detect low-level methylation changes. We included a small number of SRS patients, in particular, patients with IGF2 and PLAG1 genetic defects due to the low frequency of these etiologies. A multicenter collaboration to increase the number of cases with rare etiologies may reveal episignatures in patients with SRS.

Conclusions

In this study, we identified no characteristic episignatures for SRS in patients who met SRS clinical diagnostic criteria and had different molecular etiologies. No genome-wide methylation changes, but only methylation changes of CpGs within disease-responsible DMRs occur in some SRS etiologies.

Methods

Participants

We included 27 patients with SRS consisting of five groups based on molecular etiologies and 16 healthy child controls. All patients had four or more NH-CSS features or three NH-CSS features plus triangular face and/or short 5th clinodactyly, except for Patient 25 (mother of Patient 24 with the same rare PLAG1 variant). We also used genome-wide DNA methylation data using white blood cells from 16 healthy pediatric controls [22] and 78 healthy controls aged 2 to 15 years in a public database (GEO accession numbers: GSE180534 and GSE193730).

The etiologies of patients were confirmed by molecular analysis. Patients classified into the H19LOM group had hypomethylation of the H19/IGF2:IG-DMR without copy number change detected by methylation-specific multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification analysis (MS-MLPA, MRC Holland, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). Patients classified into the UPD(7)mat group had hypermethylation of PEG10:TSS-DMR, MEST:alt-TSS-DMR, and/or GRB10:alt-TSS-DMR by MS-MLPA or pyrosequencing-based methylation analysis and confirmed UPD(7)mat by microsatellite analysis for chromosome 7 using samples from patients and their parents, as previously reported [26]. Patients classified into the IGF2 and PLAG1 variant groups had each variant detected by whole-exome sequence or a custom-made HaloPlex Target Enrichment System (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) for genes involved in the development of SRS and growth failure, as reported previously [18–20]. Patients classified into the Kv-DMR del group had hypermethylation of the KCNQ1OT1:TSS-DMR and deletions encompassing all four probes within the KCNQ1OT1:TSS-DMR detected by MS-MLPA, as reported previously [21], suggesting the deletions were considered to be present on the paternal allele. Subsequently, we determined the extent of deletions by array comparative genomic hybridization analysis.

DNA methylation analysis

Methylation analysis was carried out on 27 patients with SRS and 16 controls using the HumanMethylationEPIC BeadChip array featuring approximately 850,000 methylation sites across the human genome. Genomic DNA extracted from peripheral blood of patients was treated with bisulfite using the EpiTect plus DNA bisulfite kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). We subjected bisulfite-treated DNA to the array and scanned it by the Illumina iScan system to obtain methylation data as IDAT files.

Bioinformatics analysis for methylation data

We processed the methylation data using the ENmix (v.1.30.3) packages in R-4.1.3. and calculated methylation levels of each CpG site as β values. Briefly, we performed data preprocessing for background and dye-bias correction, between-array normalization and probe-type bias adjustment. Then we performed quality control to identify and exclude low quality probes or samples and outlier samples. Subsequently, we excluded probes on sex chromosomes and finally included 846,231 probes for the analysis. Cell proportions in blood were estimated by the Houseman method [27]. Surrogate variables for batch effects and unknown experimental confounders were estimated by ENmix using intensity data for non-negative internal control probes. After data processing, we first compared β values of CpGs between all 27 patients with SRS and the controls, followed by comparisons between each group and the control group, using multivariate linear regression modeling adjusting for estimated blood cell variations and sex. Estimated surrogate variables were also added as covariates in regression analysis to adjust for experimental variation. Subsequently, we extracted aberrantly methylated CpGs satisfying the following criteria: 1) Bonferroni-corrected p value < 0.05, and 2) the absolute value of ∆β (|∆β|), the difference between the average β value of each group and controls at each probe, was above 0.1. A comparison between all 27 patients with SRS and the control group identified 21 hypermethylated and 25 hypomethylated CpGs. Because |∆β| in these CpGs was also above 0.1 in three cases with syndromes other than SRS (Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome [n = 2] and pseudohypoparathyroidism type 1b [n = 1]) having no SRS clinical features (score of 0/6 NH-CSS) [22] on the same experimental batch, we filtered out these CpGs. Consequently, there were no common aberrantly methylated CpGs specific to SRS. We also removed the above 21 hypermethylated and 25 hypomethylated CpGs in the comparison between each group and the control group.

We explored episignatures based on extracted aberrantly methylated CpGs. First, we extracted aberrantly methylated CpGs common in multiple groups and examined characteristic methylation patterns. Second, we examined aberrantly methylated regions common in multiple groups satisfying the following criteria: 1) ≥ 3 consecutive aberrantly methylated CpGs within 1000 bp from transcript start sites of genes, or 2) ≥ 3 consecutive aberrantly methylated CpGs within known imprinted DMRs defined by Monk [28] and Joshi [29]. Third, we conducted gene enrichment analysis. We evaluated the distribution of aberrantly methylated CpGs per gene using the Homer annotation tool [24] and explored CpGs enriched in specific genes. In addition, we performed gene ontology analysis using GREAT [25] to examine the phenotype-related or pathogenesis-related terms common in multiple groups. In brief, GREAT identified genes close to extracted CpGs with aberrant methylation levels, computed gene ontology terms using the binominal test for the input genomic regions, and extracted gene ontology terms classified into three aspects, namely, molecular function, cellular component, and biological process.

We evaluated the methylation levels of CpGs within SRS-associated DMRs in the H19LOM, UPD(7)mat, and Kv-DMR del groups. In particular, in the UPD(7)mat group, we compared the aberrantly hypermethylated CpGs identified in this study with those detected in a patient with UPD(7)mat in the study by Aref-Eshghi et al.[13].

We compared β values of the HOXA4 promoter region in our study with those in Muurinen et al. [16] using publicly available data (GEO accession number: GSE104451). Briefly, we extracted CpGs in the HOXA4 promoter region of all patients in both studies, calculated ∆β of each CpG, and evaluated whether CpGs within the HOXA4 promoter region were hypermethylated (∆β > 0.1) or hypomethylated (∆β < ‒0.1) in each patient.
