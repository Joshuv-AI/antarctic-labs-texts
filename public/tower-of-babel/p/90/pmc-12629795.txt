FLT3L-based drug conjugate effectively targets chemoresistant leukemia stem cells in acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Dengyang Zhang, Yao Guo, Zhiyong Peng, Yan Xiao, Zhiguang Chang, Liuting Yu, Yuming Zhao, Qi Zhang, Lingling Ma, Shuping Li, Chi-Kong Li, Kam Tong Leung, Zhizhuang Joe Zhao, Chun Chen, Yun Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryAcute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a heterogeneous malignancy with poor prognosis due to relapse and chemotherapy resistance. FLT3 mutations promote AML and predict adverse outcomes. As most AML cells express FLT3, it represents a promising therapeutic target. In this study, we develop FL-Fc-DM1, a FLT3-targeted conjugate linking FLT3 ligand-Fc to DM1. FL-Fc-DM1 demonstrates potent anti-leukemic activity in vitro, ex vivo, and in both cell line- and patient-derived xenograft models. Notably, it effectively targets cytarabine-resistant AML cells by promoting cell cycle entry and inducing apoptosis. FL-Fc-DM1 also significantly reduces functional leukemia stem cell frequency, as demonstrated by limiting dilution transplantation assays. The therapeutic efficacy can be further strengthened by BH3 mimetics. Importantly, toxicity assessments in a humanized mouse model show limited impact on normal human hematopoiesis at therapeutic doses. Our findings suggest that FL-Fc-DM1 is a promising candidate for AML treatment, even for cell cycle-arrested or slow-cycling chemo-resistant AML cells.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a heterogeneous hematopoietic malignancy driven by multiple genetic mutations.1 Current first-line treatments for AML include cytarabine, daunorubicin, and idarubicin.2 However, the non-selective nature of cytotoxic chemotherapy results in substantial adverse effects and frequent relapse, with a 5-year survival rate of only 30%–35%.2 To improve selectivity, antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) utilize antigen-antibody interactions to achieve intracellular delivery of cytotoxic payloads via receptor-mediated internalization.3 Gemtuzumab ozogamicin (GO) is the first ADC approved for CD33-positive AML, and multiple other ADCs are currently being evaluated in clinical trials.4,5 FLT3 is a key oncogene in AML, with gain-of-function mutations such as internal tandem duplication (FLT3-ITD) and tyrosine kinase domain (FLT3-TKD) alterations contributing to leukemogenesis and disease progression.6,7,8 To address this, small-molecule FLT3 tyrosine kinase inhibitors have been developed and clinically applied for the treatment of AML patients harboring these mutations.9,10,11 Given that FLT3 is a cell surface receptor expressed in 70%–100% of AML cells in patients, it represents an attractive target for ADCs.12 Several FLT3-targeted therapeutic antibodies and ADCs have been developed, including IMC-EB10,13 20D9-ADC,5 and ASP1235.14,15 These agents have shown promising preclinical activity, and both IMC-EB10 (NCT00887926) and ASP1235 (NCT02864290) have been evaluated in clinical trials. Although the phase 1 trial of ASP1235 was terminated for sponsor-related reasons, 14% (6/43) of patients with relapsed/refractory AML achieved composite complete remission, and an additional 19% (8/43) achieved partial remission.16 These findings support the continued development of FLT3-directed therapeutics in AML.

Leukemia stem cells (LSCs) are a subset of leukemia cells with self-renewal capacity that generate the heterogeneous leukemia cell population. These cells are often quiescent, exhibiting slow cell cycling.17,18 After chemotherapy, surviving AML cells can adopt senescence-like or quiescent-like states, both characterized by slow or arrested cell cycle progression, rendering them insensitive to treatment.19,20,21,22 Under less stressful conditions, these cells can repopulate the leukemia and drive relapse.18,19,23 Strategies to eliminate or counteract these cells include senolytic therapies,24,25 induction of cell-cycle re-entry,26,27 and targeting specific vulnerabilities of LSCs.28 The cell-cycle re-entry approach, mostly explored with granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) mobilization, showed promise preclinically but limited efficacy in clinical trials.29

FLT3 ligand (FL) is a hematopoietic growth factor that promotes proliferation and differentiation of FLT3-expressing cells.30,31 As FLT3 is highly expressed in most AML cells, FL offers a selective targeting strategy for therapeutic delivery. However, the clinical use of FL is limited by its short half-life in vivo. To address this, we fused FL to an IgG4 fragment crystallizable (Fc) domain, which prolongs serum half-life via neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn)-mediated recycling and reduces renal clearance.32 The IgG4 Fc domain also exhibits minimal antibody-dependent and complement-dependent cytotoxicity,33,34 supporting a more controllable therapeutic approach.

In this study, we developed a long-acting ligand-drug conjugate, FL-Fc-DM1, by linking FL-Fc to the cytotoxic agent DM1, a potent inhibitor of microtubule assembly. FL-Fc-DM1 exerts its activity by promoting cell cycle entry in AML cells, including bulk populations, slow-cycling LSCs, and chemotherapy-arrested cells, followed by G2/M phase-specific apoptosis induced by DM1. We comprehensively evaluated its efficacy in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo, and further investigated its activity in cytarabine-resistant and slow-cycling AML cell populations.

To generate the ligand-drug conjugate, rhFL-Fc was conjugated to DM1 via an uncleavable sulfo-SMCC linker. SDS-PAGE analysis showed a modest increase in molecular weight for FL-Fc-DM1 compared to FL-Fc, under both reducing and non-reducing conditions (Figure 1C). Additionally, ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) revealed a distinct shift in elution time between FL-Fc and FL-Fc-DM1 (Figure 1D), confirming successful conjugation of the cytotoxic payload. Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis revealed an average of 10 DM1 molecules conjugated per FL-Fc molecule (Figure S1B).

To evaluate whether FL-Fc-DM1 retains the biological activity of FL, we treated serum-starved MV-4-11 (FLT3-ITD mutant) and THP-1 (FLT3-WT) cells with 100 ng/mL FL-Fc-DM1 and collected samples at different time points for immunoblotting analysis. FL-Fc-DM1 induced phosphorylation of FLT3 and its downstream effectors ERK and AKT (Figure 1E), at levels comparable to stimulation with 100 ng/mL unconjugated FL-Fc.

UPLC and LC-MS analysis confirmed that unconjugated FL-Fc constituted less than 10% of the total protein in the FL-Fc-DM1 preparation. To verify that the observed signaling was primarily mediated by the conjugated form, we compared 100 ng/mL FL-Fc-DM1 with 10 ng/mL FL-Fc. At this lower concentration, FL-Fc induced only minimal phosphorylation of FLT3, ERK, and AKT (Figure S1C), supporting that the signaling activity of FL-Fc-DM1 was predominantly due to the ligand-drug conjugate.

The specificity of FL-Fc-DM1 was assessed using a panel of HCD-57-derived cell lines engineered to express either murine or human FLT3 (HCD-57/mFLT3-WT, HCD-57/hFLT3-WT, and HCD-57/hFLT3-ITD), along with parental HCD-57 cells.10,35,36 FL-Fc-DM1 selectively induced cytotoxicity in HCD-57/hFLT3-ITD and HCD-57/hFLT3-WT cells, but had minimal effect on HCD-57/mFLT3-WT or parental HCD-57 cells (Figure 1F), indicating specificity for human FLT3. Binding affinity measurements further confirmed that human FL binds human FLT3 with high affinity (KD = 4.63 × 10−8 M), but exhibits much lower affinity for murine FLT3 (KD = 1.38 × 10−5 M) (Figure 1G; Figure S1D). Together, these results demonstrate that the FL-Fc-DM1 conjugate retains the functional activity of FL-Fc and exhibits high specificity for human FLT3-expressing cells.

FL-Fc-DM1 promotes cell cycle progression to enhance DM1-mediated cytotoxicity

To evaluate whether FL-Fc-DM1 retains the cell cycle-promoting activity of FL,30 we first performed cell cycle analysis in AML cells treated for 24 h. Flow cytometry revealed that FL-Fc-DM1 significantly increased the percentage of cells in both the S and G2/M phases compared to unconjugated DM1. While DM1 alone caused G2/M accumulation, it did not promote S-phase entry. In contrast, FL-Fc alone significantly elevated the S-phase population without affecting G2/M accumulation (Figure 1H). Consistently, FL-Fc-DM1 treatment induced hyperphosphorylation of retinoblastoma (RB) protein, a key regulator of the G1/S transition, further supporting its role in promoting cell cycle progression (Figure 1I).

To confirm these findings, we established MV-4-11 and THP-1 cell lines stably expressing Clover-hGEMININ(1–100), a fluorescent S/G2/M phase reporter from the FUCCI system. After 24 h of treatment, FL-Fc-DM1 significantly increased the proportion of fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-positive cells compared to control or DM1 alone, indicating enhanced transition into S/G2/M phases in both FLT3-WT and FLT3-ITD backgrounds (Figure 1J).

Colony-forming unit (CFU) assays further showed that FL-Fc-DM1 markedly reduced AML colony numbers compared with control or DM1 alone (Figure 1K). Co-treatment with FL-Fc and DM1 also suppressed colony formation more effectively than DM1 alone, consistent with FL-Fc facilitating S-phase entry, thus sensitizing cells to DM1-induced G2/M arrest. This is supported by a greater increase in S-phase cells in the FL-Fc+DM1 group than with DM1 alone (Figure 1H).

Collectively, these data indicate that FL-Fc-DM1 exerts its cytotoxic effect by driving AML cells into the S and G2/M phases, where DM1 is most effective. A schematic illustration of the FL-Fc-DM1 structure is shown in Figure 1L.

As FL binding triggers receptor internalization,37 we examined whether FL-Fc-DM1 retains this capability. Flow cytometry showed that a 1-h treatment with either FL-Fc-DM1 or FL-Fc resulted in a comparable reduction in surface FLT3 levels, indicating that FL-Fc-DM1 promotes receptor-mediated internalization as effectively as FL-Fc (Figure 2D; Figure S2A).5

Cell cycle analysis showed that FL-Fc-DM1 arrested AML cells at the G2/M phase (Figure 2E). In THP-1 cells (FLT3-WT), over 90% of cells were in G2/M after 48 h of treatment, accompanied by 28% cell death—suggesting apoptosis occurred predominantly in mitosis (Figure 2F). In contrast, MV-4-11 (FLT3-ITD) cells showed only 12% in G2/M but 72% cell death at 48 h, indicating apoptosis occurred throughout G1, S, and G2/M phases (Figure 2F).

The extent of G2/M arrest was dependent on FLT3 mutation status, drug dose, and treatment duration. In THP-1 cells treated with 1 nM FL-Fc-DM1, the G2/M population rose from 22% at day 1 (Figure 1H) to 92% at day 2 (Figure 2F). In MV-4-11 cells, however, the G2/M population decreased from 18% to 14% over the same period, due to widespread apoptosis. Lower concentrations (0.2 nM) delayed cell death and allowed clearer detection of G2/M arrest in MV-4-11 cells by day 2 (Figure 2E). Overall, the increase in S and G2/M phases was dose-dependent but plateaued at higher concentrations (Figure S2B).

FL-Fc-DM1 reduced total FLT3 and FLT3-ITD protein levels in both THP-1 and MV-4-11 cells (Figure 2G), and activated the p53 signaling pathway. Notably, phospho-AKT levels initially increased upon FL-Fc-DM1 stimulation (1 h; Figure 1E), but declined after 24 h in MV-4-11 cells (Figure 2G), likely due to FLT3-ITD reduction. This effect was not observed in THP-1 cells (Figure 2G), which are FLT3-independent.38,39

FL levels are known to rise after chemotherapy and can reduce the efficacy of FLT3 inhibitors.40,41 This elevation might theoretically compete with FL-Fc-DM1 for receptor binding. However, the addition of exogenous FL at its physiological peak concentration (20 ng/mL) did not significantly impair the cytotoxic activity of FL-Fc-DM1 (2 nM) in any of the AML cell lines tested (Figure S2C), suggesting that elevated FL levels are unlikely to compromise its therapeutic efficacy.

FL-Fc-DM1 targets cytarabine-resistant AML cells

To test whether FL-Fc-DM1 could overcome this phenotype, we analyzed cell cycle profiles following co-treatment with Ara-C and FL-Fc-DM1. After 24 h, S and G2/M populations increased significantly compared to Ara-C alone (Figure 3E), with higher FL-Fc-DM1 doses inducing more pronounced progression than lower doses (Figure S2B). A 72-h co-treatment also reduced β-galactosidase activity (Figure S3D) and led to substantial cell death (Figure S3E). In Ara-C-pretreated THP-1 cells (ΔAra-C), FL-Fc and FL-Fc-DM1 both suppressed β-gal activity, suggesting that FL-Fc component promotes cell cycle re-entry, thereby disrupting senescence-like state (Figure 3F). In surviving AML cells pretreated by Ara-C, FL-Fc-DM1 further inhibited cell viability (Figure 3G), and induced significant cell apoptosis (Figure 3H). These results demonstrate that FL-Fc-DM1 can reverse cytarabine-induced senescence-like state by promoting cell cycle re-entry and inducing apoptosis in otherwise quiescent AML cells. Notably, FL-Fc-DM1 was more effective in AML cells without Ara-C pretreatment, likely because Ara-C-induced senescence-like state reduces cell proliferation, thereby limiting the susceptibility of AML cells to cell cycle-dependent killing by FL-Fc-DM1.

FL-Fc-DM1 sensitizes AML cells to BH3 mimetics

We hypothesized that this MCL-1 upregulation may confer a survival advantage and thus tested the combination of FL-Fc-DM1 with the MCL-1 inhibitor AZD-5991. In a CFU assay using AMLZD08 cells, FL-Fc-DM1 dramatically reduced clonogenic potential compared to other treatment groups (Figure 5D). Clonogenic efficiencies were approximately 0.081% (control), 0.056% (DM1), 0.095% (FL-Fc), 0.074% (FL-Fc + DM1), and 0.0003% (FL-Fc-DM1). Flow cytometry further confirmed that higher FL-Fc-DM1 concentrations induced significant apoptosis (Figure 5E).

Cytarabine treatment of primary AML cells elevated β-galactosidase activity, indicative of a senescence-like phenotype (Figure 5F).19 FL-Fc-DM1 treatment reduced β-gal activity in cytarabine-pretreated cells (Figure 5G). Importantly, FL-Fc alone produced a comparable reduction, suggesting that this effect is primarily driven by FL-mediated cell cycle re-entry. In fact, conjugation to DM1 may slightly diminish the β-gal reduction due to additional cytotoxic stress, as also observed in AML cell lines (Figure 3F). Finally, in AMLTX01 cells pretreated with cytarabine, FL-Fc-DM1 promoted cell cycle progression as evidenced by increased S/G2/M populations (Figure 5H).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that FL-Fc-DM1 retains functional activity in patient-derived AML cells, promoting cell cycle re-entry, suppressing senescence-like state, and inducing apoptosis even under chemotherapy-induced quiescence.

To determine whether the therapeutic benefit was uniquely attributable to FL-Fc-DM1, HCD-57/hFLT3-ITD xenograft mice were treated with vehicle, FL-Fc, DM1, or FL-Fc-DM1. Results from spleen size, spleen weight, bone marrow EGFP+ cell percentage, and survival demonstrated that DM1 had only a modest effect, FL-Fc alone had no impact, while FL-Fc-DM1 induced robust anti-leukemic responses (Figures S5E–S5H).

To evaluate off-target effects, the same treatment groups were applied to mice engrafted with parental HCD-57 cells (which express murine FLT3). In this model, none of the treatments—including FL-Fc-DM1—produced significant changes in leukemia burden or survival (Figures S5E–S5H), confirming that FL-Fc-DM1 selectively targets human FLT3-expressing cells in vivo.

In terms of safety, only mice receiving FL-Fc-DM1 in the HCD-57/hFLT3-ITD group gained weight during the experiment, while all other groups exhibited progressive weight loss (Figure S5I). Liver and kidney function parameters (alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], albumin, globulin, and blood urea nitrogen [BUN]) in the FL-Fc-DM1 group remained within baseline levels recorded before leukemia cell injection, whereas other groups showed significantly elevated ALT and AST, consistent with liver damage due to leukemic infiltration (Figure S5J).

FL-Fc-DM inhibits primary AML cells and prolongs survival in PDX models

The procedure for establishing the patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model and drug administration schedule is outlined in Figure S6A. In the initial efficacy study, both vehicle- and FL-Fc-DM1-treated mice were euthanized when the control group reached humane endpoints (moribund state and elevated peripheral blood hCD45+ cells). Morphological assessment revealed splenomegaly and increased spleen weight in the control group, whereas spleens from FL-Fc-DM1-treated mice were near-normal in size and weight (Figure S6B). Flow cytometric analysis of white blood cells from spleen and bone marrow revealed significantly reduced hCD45+ populations in the FL-Fc-DM1 group, demonstrating potent in vivo anti-AML activity (Figure S6C). H&E staining of spleen sections from control animals revealed dense infiltration of leukemic blasts with deeply basophilic nuclei, while FL-Fc-DM1-treated spleens showed more heterogeneous and morphologically normal cell populations (Figure S6D). Immunohistochemistry confirmed widespread hCD45+ cell infiltration in control spleens, which was markedly diminished in the FL-Fc-DM1 group. These results collectively indicate that FL-Fc-DM1 effectively targets primary AML cells in vivo.

To further validate its therapeutic potential, additional efficacy experiments were performed in two PDX models (AMLTX02 and AMLTX03), with four treatment arms: vehicle (control), FL-Fc, DM1, and FL-Fc-DM1 (Figure 6E). Across these models, FL-Fc alone showed negligible anti-leukemic activity, while DM1 exhibited modest but variable inhibition. In contrast, FL-Fc-DM1 consistently reduced leukemic burden, suppressed hCD45+ cells in spleen and bone marrow, and extended survival (Figures 6F–6H). Survival analysis confirmed that FL-Fc-DM1 significantly prolonged lifespan in both models (Figure 6I).

Given the clinical relevance of FLT3-ITD mutations in AML, which are associated with poor prognosis and frequent relapse,50,51 we evaluated FL-Fc-DM1 in a newly established FLT3-ITD PDX model (AMLTX04) developed in our recent work.52 Treatment with FL-Fc-DM1 markedly reduced leukemic burden and improved survival in this model as well, demonstrating efficacy against FLT3-ITD-mutant AML (Figures 6F–6I). Together, these data support the broad therapeutic potential of FL-Fc-DM1 against both wild-type and FLT3-ITD AML in clinically relevant in vivo models.

FL-Fc-DM1 targets leukemia stem cells and impairs leukemia-initiating capacity

LSCs are a major contributor to therapy failure and disease relapse due to their quiescent or slow-cycling phenotype, which renders them resistant to conventional chemotherapies.53,54 Given that FL-Fc-DM1 promotes cell cycle progression via FL-Fc and induces apoptosis through G2/M arrest via DM1, we investigated its potential to target LSC populations in vivo.

We first analyzed LSC-associated surface markers in primary AML PDX samples. In AMLTX01, the proportion of hCD34+CD38− cells decreased from 5.58% in the control group to 0.32% following FL-Fc-DM1 treatment (Figure S6E). Similarly, in AMLTX02, hCD34+CD117+ cells declined from 2.35% to nearly undetectable levels, and in AMLTX03, hCD34+CD38− cells dropped from 1.52% to 0.052%. These results indicate that FL-Fc-DM1 effectively reduces the frequency of phenotypic LSCs.

To determine whether even short-term FL-Fc-DM1 exposure can impair leukemia-initiating activity in vivo, two AMLTX03 PDX mice with high hCD45+ engraftment were treated with vehicle or a single dose of FL-Fc-DM1 (3 mg/kg). After 24 h, spleen cells were harvested and transplanted into irradiated NCG-M mice (Figure 7C). Prior to treatment, these mice exhibited 51.7% and 54.8% human CD45+ cells in peripheral blood, respectively (Figure 7D). Two weeks post-transplantation, the FL-Fc-DM1 group exhibited significantly lower hCD45+ levels in peripheral blood and reduced spleen size and weight (Figure 7E). Kaplan-Meier analysis confirmed a survival advantage in the FL-Fc-DM1-treated group (Figure 7F), demonstrating that even brief exposure to FL-Fc-DM1 can impair leukemia-initiating activity.

Finally, we conducted a limiting dilution transplantation experiment using primary AML cells (AMLTX04). Cells were treated ex vivo with either vehicle or FL-Fc-DM1 (20 nM) for 24 h and injected into irradiated NCG-M mice at doses of 1 × 104, 1 × 105, or 1 × 106 cells per mouse (Figure 7G). Ten weeks post-cell injection, hCD45+ cell population in the bone marrow were measured by flow cytometry (Figure 7H). The relevant data were subjected to ELDA online analysis (https://bioinf.wehi.edu.au/software/elda/).55 The estimated LSC frequency in the control group was 1 in 14,242 cells (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1 in 54,839 to 1 in 3,699), whereas the frequency in the FL-Fc-DM1 group was markedly reduced to 1 in 1,670,427 cells (95% CI: 1 in 6,667,180 to 1 in 418,517), indicating an approximately 117-fold depletion of functional LSCs following treatment. Collectively, these results demonstrate that FL-Fc-DM1 effectively targets LSCs in vivo, supporting its therapeutic potential in targeting AML LSCs.

FL-Fc-DM1 shows minimal hematopoietic toxicity at therapeutic doses

Since FLT3 is also expressed on normal human hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), potentially leading to on-target off-tumor toxicity, we further evaluated the safety of FL-Fc-DM1 on HSCs both ex vivo and in vivo.

For the ex vivo CFU assay, enriched CD34+ cells from healthy donor bone marrow and AML patient samples were plated in MethoCult medium at 1 × 103 cells per well (Figure S7A). After 14 days, colony numbers were counted. FL-Fc-DM1 (10 nM) had no significant effect on colony formation by healthy donor CD34+ cells, whereas it markedly suppressed colony formation from AML-derived CD34+ cells (Figure S7B). Flow cytometry revealed higher FLT3 expression in AML-derived CD34+ cells compared to healthy counterparts (Figure S7C), indicating increased sensitivity of AML progenitors to FL-Fc-DM1 and relative sparing of normal HSCs.

To validate these findings in vivo, we used a humanized NSG-M mouse model (HuHSC-NSG-M) reconstituted with healthy human CD34+ cells. A single dose of FL-Fc-DM1 (3 mg/kg) caused no significant change in body weight over 10 days (Figure S7D). Flow cytometric analysis of blood and bone marrow showed that FL-Fc-DM1 did not impair either murine CD45+ cells or the human hematopoietic compartment, including T cells (hCD3, hCD4, and hCD8), B cells (hCD19), myeloid cells (hCD33), and natural killer cells (hCD56). Interestingly, a slight increase in hCD3+ T cells was observed following treatment (Figures S7E–S7G). Peripheral blood counts confirmed that neither total leukocytes nor multilineage hematopoiesis were adversely affected in either species (Figure S7H). Collectively, these data indicate that FL-Fc-DM1 selectively targets AML stem and progenitor cells while exerting minimal toxicity toward normal human hematopoietic cells.

Discussion

We developed FL-Fc-DM1, a FLT3-targeted conjugate linking recombinant FL-Fc to the microtubule inhibitor DM1. DM1 was chosen for its well-defined ability to induce G2/M arrest, hypothesized to be especially effective when combined with FL-driven cell cycle entry. Targeted delivery overcomes DM1’s systemic toxicity and lack of specificity as a free drug. FL-Fc-DM1 showed potent activity across FLT3 wild-type and mutant AML models, including cytarabine-resistant, senescence-like cells and LSCs, highlighting its potential to overcome relapse and drug resistance.

For a therapeutic drug, the ability to promote cell cycle progression may initially seem counterintuitive, as it contrasts with the classical paradigm that favors cell-cycle arrest as a strategy for cancer treatment.56 However, in the context of an ADC carrying DM1, this cell cycle-promoting activity is synergistic with the mechanism of the cytotoxic payload. Clinically, therapy-induced cell-cycle-arrested populations, such as senescence-like cells, diapause-like cells, and quiescent or slow-cycling LSCs, are major contributors to disease persistence and relapse.19,21,27,28,29,43 In addition to chemotherapy, agents such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors and therapeutic antibodies including GO have been shown to induce G0/G1 phase arrest.20,57 GO is currently the only FDA-approved ADC for AML. In this study, we observed that FL-Fc-DM1 was able to promote cell cycle re-entry into the S phase in these Ara-C-induced senescence-like cells, likely through FL/FLT3-mediated hyperphosphorylation of RB protein, both in senescence-like cells and in ex vivo primary AML cells. FL-Fc-DM1 also demonstrates excellent efficacy against LSCs, which has a slow-cycling phenotype. In contrast, GO shows limited efficacy against CD33+CD34+ leukemia cells due to their quiescent G0 state, as previously reported.57 By promoting cell cycle progression in LSCs, FL-Fc-DM1 enhances drug susceptibility and demonstrates a distinct therapeutic advantage.

A similar approach has been used with G-CSF in CAG/CLAG/FLAG regimens for AML, where G-CSF drives quiescent AML cells into cycle, sensitizing them to cytotoxic drugs.58,59 G-CSF plus cytarabine can also eliminate AML stem cells in murine models,27 consistent with our in vitro finding that FL-Fc and DM1 strongly inhibited AML cells. A concern is whether forcing cycle entry might promote proliferation without sufficient apoptosis. This risk is mitigated by DM1’s high potency, the strong efficacy of FL-Fc-DM1 even at low conjugation ratios (Figure S7I), and the use of a non-cleavable linker that confines DM1 release to target cells, limiting off-target effects and prolonged FLT3 signaling. Based on pharmacokinetic (PK) data for the anti-FLT3 ADC ASP1235 (half-life 5–7 days; plasma >240 nM at 3.2 mg/kg), FL-Fc-DM1 at 3 mg/kg is expected to maintain drug levels well above its effective concentration, though formal PK evaluation is warranted.

FL-Fc-DM1 exhibits not only high target specificity, as demonstrated in vitro using HCD-57 cell models, enriched human CD34+ hematopoietic stem and AML cells, and in vivo in NOG mice, but also a favorable safety profile, highlighting its translational potential. This was observed across multiple systems: ex vivo in human enriched CD34+ populations, in vivo in NOG mice (two doses at 5 mg/kg over 14 days), NSGS and NCG-M mice (three doses at 3 mg/kg over 60–120 days), and in a human CD34+-reconstituted NCG-M (HuHSC-NCG-M) mouse model (one dose at 3 mg/kg for 10 days). FL-Fc-DM1 demonstrated minimal cytotoxicity toward normal human CD34+ HSCs, the mouse hematopoietic system, and the human immune-reconstituted blood system. Moreover, the anti-FLT3 ADC ASP1235 showed a manageable safety profile in early clinical evaluation, with ocular toxicity as a notable adverse event. Systemic adverse events observed with ASP1235 overlapped with those of GO, while the ocular toxicity is considered a class effect associated with ADCs carrying tubulin-disrupting payloads.16 Taken together, these findings suggest that FL-Fc-DM1 has the potential for an acceptable and manageable safety profile in future human clinical trials.

FL lacks a complex tertiary structure, possessing only three intra-chain disulfide bonds and limited glycosylation. This structural simplicity supports the feasibility of large-scale (or industrial-scale) production of FL-Fc using CHO cells, such as through the LONZA GS Gene Expression System, which is already widely employed for manufacturing approved therapeutic antibodies. In our report, FL-Fc achieved 92.7% purity by SDS-PAGE, and this purity can be further enhanced using hydrophobic interaction chromatography and ion-exchange chromatography for secreted recombinant proteins.

To advance this drug candidate toward clinical application, several critical steps must be undertaken. These include securing financial and institutional support, establishing robust preclinical research collaborations, identifying reliable manufacturing partners, and addressing intellectual property and regulatory requirements. Moving forward, we propose a translational roadmap for early-phase clinical development, encompassing preclinical toxicology studies in non-human primates, evaluation of pharmacokinetics and biodistribution, and identification of predictive biomarkers such as FLT3 expression to guide patient selection. These steps will be essential for optimizing trial design and ensuring the safe and effective translation of FL-Fc-DM1 into clinical settings.

Although FL-Fc-DM1 alone has shown strong efficacy against bulk AML cells, cytarabine-induced senescence-like AML cells, and LSCs, we anticipate that this drug candidate may be effective as monotherapy or in combination with standard chemotherapies for patients with FLT3-expressing AML, particularly those with unresponsive, relapsed, or refractory disease.

Limitations of the study

This study demonstrates the efficacy and safety of FL-Fc-DM1 in AML models harboring wild-type or mutant FLT3, but several limitations remain. First, the current FL-Fc preparation contains ∼7.3% impurities, which may affect efficacy and immunogenicity, and further purification is needed to improve protein quality. Second, mechanistic insight could be strengthened by live-cell tracking of cell cycle dynamics and by broader validation in diverse primary AML PDX models, including cytarabine-treated relapsed cases. Third, the CDX and PDX models used lack functional adaptive immunity, which limits evaluation of immune-mediated effects, and testing in immunocompetent models would be informative. Fourth, safety assessments in humanized mice were short term, and longer-term studies are needed to evaluate potential impacts on normal hematopoiesis. Addressing these issues will refine mechanistic understanding and support clinical translation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

THP-1 (human cell line, male), MV-4-11 (human cell line, male), and HEK 293T (human cell line, female) cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection; Molm-13 cells (human cell line, male) were sourced from the DSMZ open collection (Germany); HEK 293F (human cell line, female) cells were purchased from Invitrogen; and the murine erythroid leukemia HCD-57 cell line was maintained in our laboratory. THP-1/clover-hGEMININ cells were generated by transducing THP-1 cells with a recombinant lentivirus encoding the clover-hGEMININ(1–110) fusion protein in the pCSII-bsr-6013x vector. After plating in MethoCult H4230 semi-solid medium, single green fluorescent clones were isolated by microscopy, expanded through two rounds of selection, and clones with stable fluorescence and doubling times of 24–36 h were used for experiments. Parental and modified THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37°C with 5% CO2, diluted 1:3 at 1.5–1.8 × 106 cells/mL. Similarly, MV-4-11 cells were transduced with the same lentiviral construct, cloned in MethoCult medium, and fluorescent clones with stable growth characteristics were expanded and maintained in Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium (IMDM) with 10% FBS under standard conditions. Molm-13 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 with 10% FBS, and HCD-57 cells were maintained in IMDM supplemented with 20% FBS and 5 ng/mL EPO. To generate HCD-57 derivatives expressing FLT3 variants (human FLT3-ITD, mouse FLT3-WT, human FLT3-WT), cells were transduced with retroviruses in the pMSCV-GFP vector, selected in IMDM without EPO, cloned in MethoCult medium, and expanded. Resulting clones were validated by fluorescence, FLT3 expression via flow cytometry, and growth rate, and cultured under variant-specific conditions with appropriate FL or EPO supplementation, diluted 1:3 at 1.5–1.8 × 106 cells/mL. HEK 293T and 293F cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM with 10% FBS at 37°C with 5% CO2, with medium refreshed every two days and passaged at ∼95% confluence. HEK 293F cells were adapted to suspension culture in 293Freestyle medium for protein expression, maintained below 3 × 106 cells/mL in spinner bottles at 100–120 rpm. All cell lines used in this study were authenticated by GENEWIZ (Suzhou, China) using short tandem repeat profiling, except for HCD-57, a non-commercial line maintained in our laboratory. All cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination by using the Mycoplasma qPCR Detection Kit (Takara, Japan).

Enriched CD34+ human cells

Bone marrow (BM) samples were obtained from three healthy donors and three AML patients who provided written informed consent using a protocol approved by the Ethics Committee of The Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (approval number: KY-2024-200-01), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. CD34+ hematopoietic progenitor cells were isolated from the BM of healthy donors or AML patients using the CD34 MicroBead Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-046-702) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The isolated CD34+ cells were resuspended in culture medium for subsequent colony-forming assays. Cells from the same donor/patient were used simultaneously for both control and experimental conditions. Therefore, no sample allocation to experimental groups was performed. The reported gender and age for healthy donors are as follows: HEDZD01 (female, 13), HEDZD02 (male, 11), HEDZD03 (male, 10). The reported gender and age for patients with AML are as follows: AMLTX01 (male, 7), AMLTX03 (male, 13) and AMLTX04 (male, 11). Gender information was based on donor’s self-report. Samples from both male and female donors/patients were included in this study, but the results were not analyzed by sex. Therefore, potential sex-specific differences could not be evaluated and should be acknowledged as a limitation of this work.

Patient-derived primary Acute myeloid leukemia cells

Primary AML cells were obtained from BM samples of five AML patients who provided written informed consent using a protocol approved by the Ethics Committee of The Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (approval number: KY-2024-200-01), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Cells were thawed and cultured in SFEM II complete medium (Stemcell Technologies), supplemented with StemSpan CD34+ Expansion Supplement (Stemcell Technologies), 1 μM UM729 (Stemcell Technologies), and 10 ng/mL FL (Peprotech). Cultured cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and used for ex vivo assays. Cells from the same patient were used simultaneously for both control and experimental conditions. Therefore, no sample allocation to experimental groups was performed. The reported gender and age for patients with AML are as follows: AMLZD08 (male, 51), AMLTX01 (male, 7), AMLTX02 (female, 3), AMLTX03 (male, 13) and AMLTX04 (male, 11). Gender information was based on donor’s self-report. Samples from both male and female patients were included in this study, but the results were not analyzed by sex. Therefore, potential sex-specific differences could not be evaluated and should be acknowledged as a limitation of this work.

Mouse models

Animal experiments were performed following protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees and Ethics Committees of TOP Biotechnology (approval number TOP-IACUC-2023-0134) and GemPharmatech Laboratory (protocol GPTAP20250604-1), in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Female 7-week-old NOG mice (NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Sug/JicCrl) were purchased from the Vital River Laboratory and used in the relevant experiments. Male and female 9-week-old NSG-SGM3 mice (NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl Tg(CMV-IL3,CSF2,KITLG)1Eav/MloySzJ) were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory and used in relevant experiments. Female 7-week-old NCG-M mice (NOD/ShiLtJGpt-Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22Rosa26em1Cin(hCSF2&IL3&KITLG)/Gpt) were obtained from GemPharmatech Laboratory and used in the relevant experiments. Female 10-to 14-week-old HuHSC-NCG-M mouse model was constructed and provided by GemPharmatech Laboratory (facility RRID: SCR_017239). Human HSCs (CD34+ HSCs) from a healthy 25-year-old female donor (blood type B, RhD+) were transplanted into NCG-M mice (GemPharmatech Laboratory) for immune system reconstruction. Recipient mice were intravenously engrafted with 5×104 enriched CD34+ cells following irradiation at 0.8 Gray. Peripheral blood samples were collected at weeks 6, 10, and 14 post-engraftment for immunophenotyping by flow cytometry. Successful humanization was confirmed by reconstitution of human immune cell populations including hCD45+ (∼70% of peripheral blood leukocytes), hCD3+ (43%), hCD19+ (47%), hCD33+ (5.5%), hCD4+ (43%), and hCD56+ (0.75%). All mice were housed in SPF barrier facilities with 100% HEPA-filtered air and a minimum of 10–15 air changes per hour. Environmental conditions were maintained at 22 ± 2°C with 55% ± 5% relative humidity and a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on from 08:00 to 20:00) at a light intensity of 150–300 lux. A maximum of five mice were housed per cage, which were routinely monitored for cleanliness and adequate food and water supply.

Method details

Extraction of primary cells from Acute myeloid leukemia patients

Patient-derived primary AML cells (AMLZD08, AMLTX01, AMLTX02, AMLTX03 and AMLTX04) were isolated from BM aspirates of primary AML patients (Table S1). Briefly, in a 15 mL centrifuge tube, added 3 mL of Ficoll-Paque media (Cytiva) to the tube bottom, then mixture (8 mL) of 4 mL of anticoagulant-treated BM samples and 4 mL of HBSS was carefully layered onto the Ficoll-Paque media solution. The sample was centrifuged at 400 × g for 35 min at 18°C (the brake was turned off). The mononuclear cell layer was carefully collected and washed with at least 3 volumes of HBSS. Blood white cells extraction from mouse model

The red blood cell lysis buffer (RBC buffer) (0.15 M NH4Cl, 10 mM KHCO3, 0.1 M EDTA) was prepared, filter-sterilized and stored at 4°C–8°C. Samples (peripheral blood, spleen blood and BM) of mice were treated with anticoagulant. Blood samples were mixed with RBC buffer at a ratio (v/v) of 1:10. The mixed blood sample in a centrifuge tube were rotated on ice for 5 min. Sample was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min at 4°C. White blood cell pellet was resuspended in pre-cold HBSS and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The final white blood cells were resuspended in appropriate solutions for flow cytometry analysis or ex vivo experiments.

Transcriptome analysis

In the experiment including control, Ara-C and Δcytarabine+FL-Fc-DM1: For the control group, AML cells (THP-1, MV-4-11, Molm-13) were cultured for 4 days; In the Ara-C group, AML cells were treated with Ara-C (20 μM for THP-1, 5 μM for MV-4-11, 1 μM for Molm-13) for 4 days; In the FL-Fc-DM1 treatment group, AML cells were treated by Ara-C (20 μM for THP-1, 5 μM for MV-4-11, 1 μM for Molm-13) for 3 days and then treated with FL-Fc-DM1 (5 nM for THP-1, 2 nM for MV-4-11, 2 nM for Molm-13) for 24 h in the absence of Ara-C. In the experiment of comparison between vehicle and FL-Fc-DM1: For the control group, AML cells (THP-1, MV-4-11, Molm-13) were cultured for 24 h without any drug treatment; For the FL-Fc-DM1 group, AML cells (THP-1, MV-4-11, Molm-13) were cultured for 24 h under FL-Fc-DM1 (1 nM and 3 nM for THP-1, 0.5 nM and 1.5 nM for MV-4-11, 1 nM and 3 nM for Molm-13) treatment for 24 h. Total RNA was extracted by using the Trizol method. RNA quality was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) and checked using RNase-free agarose gel electrophoresis. Total mRNA was enriched by Oligo(dT) beads. Then, the enriched mRNA was fragmented and reverse-transcripted into cDNA with random primers. Second-strand cDNA was synthesized by DNA polymerase I. The cDNA fragments were purified with the QiaQuick PCR extraction kit (Qiagen, Venlo, The Netherlands), end repaired, poly(A) added, and ligated to Illumina sequencing adapters. The ligation products were size-selected by agarose gel electrophoresis, PCR amplified and sequenced using Illumina HiSeq2500 by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. (China). Reads obtained from the sequencing machines, including raw reads were further filtered by fastp. Reads containing adapters, containing more than 10% of unknown nucleotides, and low-quality reads containing more than 50% of low-quality bases were removed. rRNA-mapped reads were also removed. The paired-end clean reads were mapped to the human reference genome using HISAT2.2.4. The mapped reads of each sample were assembled using StringTie v1.3.1 in a reference-based approach. The TPM method was used to eliminate the influence of different gene lengths and sequencing data amount on the calculation of gene expression. RNAs differential expression analysis was performed by DESeq2 software between two different groups (and by edgeR between two samples). The genes/transcripts with the parameter of false discovery rate below 0.05 and absolute fold change ≥2 were considered differentially expressed genes/transcripts. DEGs were subjected to enrichment analysis by using the KEGG database. GSEA analysis were performed on total genes against specific gene signatures from MSigDB or published data as shown in the STAR Methods section using GSEA software.19 RNA-seq data are available at GEO under accession number GEO: GSE270338, GSE300846, GSE300847, GSE300849, GSE300904 and GSE300905.

Expression and purification of recombinant human FL-Fc

Human FL mRNA was extracted from THP-1 cell using Universal RNA Extraction Kit (Takara), and cDNA was obtained using PrimeScript RT enzyme kit (Takara). Primers used to obtain FL ectodomain gene were shown in the STAR Methods section. The PCR product of FLecto and pINFUSE-hIgG4-Fc2 were both enzyme (EcoR I, Bgl II) cut and ligated according to manufacturer’s protocol. The recombinant pINFUSE-FL-Fc Plasmid used for transfection was extracted using endo-free plasmid extraction kit (Omega Bio-tek). PEIMAX transfection reagent was prepared at 1mg/mL according to provider’s instruction (Polysciences). For transfection, cell density was 1-2×106 cells/mL with >95% viability. The transfection mixture of PEIMAX and plasmid was drop-wise added into cell culture. This cell culture was maintained in spinner bottle (Bellco, 1965–61015) at a speed of 100–120 rpm. Three days post-transfection, cell culture supernatant containing recombinant FL-Fc was collected by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was purified on a Protein-A Sepharose column (Cytiva, USA). The target protein was eluted under 0.5 M glycine (pH 3.0) and neutralized with phosphate sodium buffer (pH 8.0). Recombinant hFL-Fc was further purified on a Sephacryl S-200 column (Cytiva, USA). The recombinant human FL-Fc (rhFL-Fc) protein was stored at −80°C for further use.

Generation of FL-Fc-DM1

Purified FL-Fc (adjusted to 1 mg/mL in 80 nM phosphate sodium buffer, pH 7.0) and DM1 were conjugated via chemical linker sulfo-SMCC. Sulfo-SMCC powder was dissolved in ultrapure water to make up the stock solution (4.8 mg/mL) immediately before use. Sulfo-SMCC and FL-Fc (molar ratio: 15:1) were combined and reacted for 0.5 h. The solution of the reaction mixture (FL-Fc-SMCC) was changed to PE buffer (80 nM phosphate sodium buffer, pH 6.0, 1 mM EDTA) via an ultrafiltration tube (cutoff value 10 kDa). After ultrafiltration, molar ratio (15:1) of emtansine (10 mM in dimethylacetamide) and FL-Fc-SMCC were combined and reacted for 1 h. The reaction product (FL-Fc-SMCC-DM1, abbreviated as FL-Fc-DM1) was processed by ultrafiltration (cutoff value 10 kDa) to remove unconjugated DM1. Reaction product FL-Fc-DM1 was analyzed by SDS-PAGE. Sample was also analyzed by LC-MS/MS. Sample was loaded on Ultimate 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and separated on ACQUITY UPLC Protein BEH C4 Column (300 Å, 1.7 μm, 2.1 mm × 50 mm). Mobile phase: A: 0.1% formic acid in water; B: 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. Total flow rate: 0.3 mL/min. LC linear gradient: from 5% to 100% B for 10 min, from 100% to 100% B for 3 min, from 100% to 5% B for 1 min, from 5% to 5% B for 1 min. Mass spectrometry: AB SCIEX TripleTOF 5600 Mass Spectrometer, Mass range: m/z 600–4000. For other assays, molar ration of (5:1, and 3:1) of emtansine (10 mM in dimethylacetamide) and FL-Fc-SMCC were combined and reacted for 1 h, the dialyzed FL-Fc-DM1 (via ultrafiltration) product were measured using UV/Vis spectroscopy64 and used for specific assays.

SDS-PAGE analysis

Samples were mixed with reducing/unreducing SDS-PAGE sample loading buffer and heat-treated at 95°C for 5 min. Samples were separated on SDS-PAGE gel. After electrophoresis, the PAGE gel was immersed in Coomassie brilliant blue R250 staining buffer (0.1% m/v Coomassie Brilliant Blue R250, 50% methanol, 10% Glacial acetic acid, 40% water) and rotated in a rotator for 2 h. The PAGE gel was then de-colored in a de-coloring buffer (50% methanol, 10% Glacial acetic acid, 40% water).

Immunoblotting analysis

Samples on the PAGE gel were transferred to 0.45 μm PVDF membrane (Millipore). Membrane was incubated with blocking buffer (5% w/v BSA in TBST) for 1 h at room temperature. After three times of washing with TBST, the membrane was incubated with a primary antibody (1:2000 dilution in blocking buffer) overnight at 4°C on a rotator. After washed three times by TBST, the membrane was incubated with an anti-mouse/anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:2000 dilution in blocking buffer) at room temperature for 1 h. After washed three times by TBST, the membrane was immersed in the SuperSignal West Pico PLUS solution (Thermo Scientific, USA), and the fluorescence was captured using ChemiDoc system (BioRad, USA).

Cell viability assay

For cell viability assay, 3 × 104 cells were plated in each well in a 96-well plate with complete culture medium. For the drug groups, different concentrations of drugs were added to the corresponding wells. The plate was incubated for 3 days in a 37°C incubator with 5% CO2. Viable cells were either calculated using trypan blue staining with a cytometer or measured by cell counting kit-8 method.

β-galactosidase assay

For measurement using the β-galactosidase assay kit (Beyotime, China), cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and then incubated overnight at 37°C in darkness with the working solution containing 0.05 mg/mL X-gal. Cells were washed once and then resuspended in PBS before being visualized under a microscope. For measurement using C12-FDG regent, C12-FDG was added to the cell culture at a final concentration of 33 μM. Cells were protected from light and incubated for two hours at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After incubation, cells were washed, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed on CytoFlex LX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, USA). The data were analyzed via FlowJo software (FlowJo, USA).

Cell cycle analysis

For cell cycle analysis using flow cytometry, collected cells were washed with PBS and resuspended in 1 mL of 70% ethanol overnight at −20°C. After washed with pre-cold PBS and resuspended in PI staining buffer (0.1% v/v Triton X-100, 0.1 mg/mL DNase-free RNase, 20 μg/mL propidium iodide in PBS), the cells were analyzed by FACS. For cell cycle analysis using the Fluorescent Ubiquitination-based Cell Cycle Indicator (FUCCI) system, the MV-4-11/clover-hGEMININ and THP-1/clover-hGEMININ cells were used and treated with FL-Fc-DM1 for 24 h. Cells of different groups were collected, washed with pre-cold PBS, and then analyzed on a CytoFlex LX flow cytometer.

Cell apoptosis assay

For apoptosis analysis using FACS, cells (1 × 106) were treated with different drugs for different time. Collected cells were washed with PBS and resuspended in Annexin V staining buffer (BioLegend, USA). APC-annexin V was added to the cells and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. The cells were then incubated on ice, supplemented with PI (final concentration 20 μg/mL), and analyzed on a CytoFlex LX flow cytometer.

Cell membrane protein marker assay

Cells were collected, washed twice with pre-cold flow cytometry staining buffer (BD bioscience), and then stained with fluorescein-conjugated primary antibody. The cells were incubated on ice for 0.5 h. After washed with pre-cold flow cytometry staining buffer for two times, cells were resuspended in pre-cold flow cytometry staining buffer and analyzed on a CytoFlex LX flow cytometer.

FLT3 internalization assay

Cells (1 × 106) were plated in each well of a 12-well plate and cultured in complete culture medium. Corresponding wells were added with different drugs (0.2, 1, 2 nM of rhFL-Fc or FL-Fc-DM1) and were incubated for 1 h at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were collected, washed twice with pre-cold flow cytometry staining buffer, and then stained with APC-conjugated anti-FLT3 primary antibody on ice for 30 min. After washed with pre-cold flow cytometry staining buffer, cells were analyzed on a CytoFlex LX flow cytometer. Flow data were analyzed by FlowJo VX software (USA). Direct flow cytometry was conducted on THP-1 and Molm-13 cells. Indirect flow cytometry was conducted on MV-4-11 cells using an anti-FLT3 antibody and a secondary Alexa Fluor(R) 647 Goat anti-mouse IgG antibody.

Colony formation assay

Cells (2 × 103 per well in 24-well plate for THP-1 and MV-4-11, 1 × 105 per well in 6-well plate for AMLZD08, 1 × 103 per well in 12-well plate for enriched BM CD34+ cells from AML patients, 1 × 103 per well in 12-well plate for enriched BM CD34+ cells from healthy donors) were resuspended in appropriate culture medium supplemented with different drugs. The cell solution was mixed with MethoCult H4230 and plated in cell culture plate. The plate was incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 10–12 days. Colony number was counted under an inverted microscope.

Cell signaling assay

The bioactivity of FL-Fc-DM1 was assayed in THP-1 and MV-4-11 cells. Cells (2 × 106 cells per well) were starved overnight by culturing in basic culture medium (RPMI 1640 for THP-1, IMDM for MV-4-11) without FBS in a 12-well plate. After overnight culture, either rhFL-Fc (100 ng/mL or 10 ng/mL) or FL-Fc-DM1 (100 ng/mL) in basic culture medium was added to the corresponding wells. Cells were collected at different time points (10 min, 30 min, 60 min). The cell pellet was resuspended in RIPA solution containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail and sonicated briefly. The proteins in the cell lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE and analyzed by immunoblotting with anti-p-FLT3, FLT3, p-AKT, AKT, p-ERK1/2, ERK1/2 and β-ACTIN primary antibodies.

Binding affinity assay

The binding affinity between FLT3 (human and mouse) ectodomain and rhFL was determined using the streptavidin (SA) sensor chip on a Biacore 8k (Cytiva). The biotinylated human FL ectodomain (Thr27-Pro185) (Cat#FLL-H82E6, ACRO BIOSYSTEMS, expressed in HEK 293) was loaded onto an SA chip, which was washed and transferred to wells containing different concentrations of recombinant mouse FLT3 ectodomain (Cat#FL3-M52H6, ACRO BIOSYSTEMS, expressed in HEK 293) or recombinant human FLT3 ectodomain (Cat#FL3-H52H6, ACRO BIOSYSTEMS, expressed in HEK 293). The association and dissociation of the recombinant FLT3 ectodomain with rhFL were measured. Binding kinetic parameters (ka and kd) and affinity (KD) were calculated using Biacore insight evaluation software.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired Student’s t test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used. Statistical analyses were conducted using OriginPro 2025 (Version 10.2.0.196, OriginLab, USA). Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated with OriginPro software and analyzed by the log rank test. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: ns, not significant; ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001.
