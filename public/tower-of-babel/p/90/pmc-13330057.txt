Delpacibart etedesiran improves the molecular pathology of myotonic dystrophy type 1 in the phase 1/2 MARINA study
Authors: Tanya T Kwan, Qingying Meng, Nathaniel Delos Santos, Nicholas E Johnson, Charles A Thornton, Barbora Malecova, Rob Burke, Li-Jung Tai, Arthur A Levin, Husam S Younis, W Michael Flanagan, Yiming Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1) is a rare, autosomal dominant, progressive neuromuscular disease caused by the expansion of CTG repeats in the DM1 protein kinase (DMPK) gene. Expanded CUG repeats in the mutant DMPK transcript bind and sequester splicing factors, especially in the muscleblind-like (MBNL) family, leading to misregulated alternative splicing of mRNA, which is the main driver of DM1 pathology. We designed an antibody-oligonucleotide conjugate (AOC), delpacibart etedesiran (del-desiran), to deliver a DMPK-targeting small interfering RNA to muscle via transferrin receptor 1-mediated cell internalization. Del-desiran reduced mutDMPK mRNA expression, increased estimated levels of functional MBNL, and corrected disease-associated mis-splicing in a dose-dependent manner. The extent of increase in functional MBNL was associated with consistent trends toward muscle function improvement, confirming del-desiran’s mechanism of action and supporting its development as a treatment for DM1. The translation of its pharmacodynamic activity from preclinical models (patient-derived myotubes and Macaca fascicularis [cynomolgus monkeys]) to patients with DM1 demonstrates del-desiran’s consistency across species and reveals the potential broad utility of the AOC platform in further neuromuscular diseases.Keywords: del-desiran, myotonic dystrophy 1, DMPK, mis-splicing, MARINA, MBNL

Introduction

Myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1) is a rare, progressive neuromuscular disease, with an estimated prevalence of 9.27 cases per 100,000 individuals.1 The disease affects multiple organ systems and is characterized by muscle hyperexcitability (myotonia), progressive muscle weakness, cardiac arrhythmia, insulin resistance, and other systemic manifestations.2 DM1 is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by expanded CTG repeats (>50 repeats) in the DM1 protein kinase (DMPK) gene.2,3 The length of these repeats is generally positively correlated with disease severity and inversely correlated with age at onset.4,5,6

The DMPK mRNA expressed from the expanded mutant allele accumulates in nuclear foci and sequesters mRNA regulatory proteins, most notably splicing factors in the muscleblind-like (MBNL) family (MBNL1, MBNL2, and MBNL3).2,7 MBNL proteins function in skeletal and heart muscle, among other tissues, where they regulate the developmentally appropriate alternative splicing of hundreds of transcripts involved in terminal muscle differentiation.8 MBNL sequestration leads to widespread defects in alternative splicing, or “mis-splicing,” affecting many transcripts with roles in myotonia and skeletal muscle contraction/relaxation that are clinically relevant to DM1, including ion channels/transporters (CACNA1S, CLCN1, ATP2A1), muscle structural components (BIN1, DMD, LDB3), signaling proteins (INSR, CAMK2B), and others.8,9 DM1-related mis-splicing can be quantified by integrating the splicing patterns of MBNL targets or disease-associated splicing events into composite splicing biomarkers, such as [MBNL]inferred.9,10,11,12,13,14

Currently, no approved therapies are available for DM1, and treatment strategies focus on supportive care.15 Multiple therapeutic approaches—including small-molecule inhibitors, gene therapies, and antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs)—have been investigated without yielding an approved treatment.16,17,18,19 An ASO targeting DMPK demonstrated limited efficacy in the clinic likely due to poor muscle delivery and/or insufficient potency.11 Recently, antibody-oligonucleotide conjugates (AOCs) have shown promise in the targeted and effective delivery of oligonucleotides into muscle tissue compared with naked oligonucleotides, effectively modulating gene expression.20 Delpacibart etedesiran (del-desiran, AOC 1001) is a small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting DMPK mRNA conjugated to a humanized anti-transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) antibody. Del-desiran is designed to reduce DMPK expression in muscle tissue, thus releasing sequestered MBNL proteins and correcting the mis-splicing underlying DM1 pathogenesis. Given the promising safety and efficacy profile of del-desiran in the MARINA phase 1/2 study (NCT05027269),21 del-desiran is currently being evaluated in a confirmatory global phase 3 trial as a disease-modifying therapy for patients with DM1 (NCT06411288).

We previously reported an initial assessment of DMPK mRNA reduction and mis-splicing correction in patients with DM1 treated with del-desiran, confirming target engagement and molecular efficacy.21 Here, we present a biomarker analysis demonstrating an in-depth molecular pharmacodynamic (PD) profile of del-desiran in patients with DM1, including its ability to modulate the molecular basis of the disease by restoring MBNL activity and correcting mis-splicing. We also demonstrate reduction of the expanded mutant DMPK (mutDMPK) isoform and concordant improvement in downstream molecular and functional outcomes. Importantly, improvement in molecular biomarkers of the disease generally correlated with improvement in muscle function in patients with DM1, providing confirmatory evidence of the mechanism of action of del-desiran and supporting its continued development as a treatment for DM1.

Results

Del-desiran treatment in preclinical models demonstrates its ability to reduce DMPK mRNA levels, CUG-containing nuclear foci, and mis-splicing

Myoblasts derived from patients with DM1 that harbor an expanded mutDMPK allele22 were used to evaluate the pharmacological activity of the siRNA component of del-desiran (siDMPK) in vitro. These models exhibit hallmarks of DM1 molecular pathology, including the presence of DMPK nuclear foci and mis-splicing. Treatment of myoblasts with siDMPK markedly reduced DMPK mRNA in both the nucleus and the cytoplasm by approximately 60% and 80%, respectively (Figure 1A), suggesting reduction of the mutDMPK mRNA sequestered in the nuclear compartment. Consistent with these results, siDMPK treatment also led to a ∼50% decrease in the number of CUG-containing foci in the nucleus of treated DM1 myotubes (Figure 1B). siDMPK treatment corrected hundreds of mis-splicing events in DM1 myotubes, while showing no effect on these events in healthy cells that only express wild-type DMPK (wtDMPK) (Figure 1C), consistent with expectations that the reduction in mutant but not wtDMPK affects splicing and results in its improvement.

Given that del-desiran is cross-reactive to cynomolgus monkeys, its PD profile was preclinically evaluated in this species. A single intravenous (i.v.) infusion of del-desiran (0.6–6 mg/kg siRNA dose) produced dose-dependent reductions in total DMPK mRNA 4 weeks post-dose in multiple skeletal muscles, with maximal reduction achieved at 2 and 6 mg/kg (tibialis anterior 74.9% and 81.8%, quadriceps 82.1% and 86.4%. and gastrocnemius 78.0% and 89.8% reduction, respectively) (Figure 1D). Importantly, a single dose of 6 mg/kg siRNA of del-desiran in cynomolgus monkeys resulted in substantial reduction of total DMPK mRNA of up to ∼90% in a broad panel of skeletal muscles including diaphragm (Figure 1E). Del-desiran was also active in the heart and the gastrointestinal tract (mixed cell types), with ∼75% and ∼45% mean reductions, respectively (Figure 1E). These data support effective delivery of del-desiran to muscle and robust reduction of DMPK expression across a broad panel of muscle groups.

MBNLinferred is a biomarker of mis-splicing and disease severity in patients with DM1

Previous reports have shown that MBNL levels correlate with mis-splicing,9,12,13 and that restoration of MBNL function can reverse DM1 phenotypes in preclinical models.10 We therefore sought to infer the levels of functional, non-sequestered MBNL proteins ([MBNL]inferred) in baseline and treatment-phase tibialis anterior (TA) biopsies collected as part of the MARINA (NCT05027269) study,21 using bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and a previously published approach9 (Figures S1 and S2).

At baseline, DM1 participants showed significantly reduced levels of [MBNL]inferred compared with healthy control muscle (mean [SD], 0.354 [0.172] versus 0.884 [0.058] respectively, p < 0.0001, Figure 2A). [MBNL]inferred was not significantly associated with CTG repeat length measured in blood (Figure S3A) or Muscular Impairment Rating Scale scores (Figure S3B), two independent measures of disease severity.23 Baseline [MBNL]inferred showed strong correlation with two other mis-splicing metrics—a 22-gene splicing composite score that has been used previously to quantify mis-splicing in DM111 (r = −0.925, p < 0.0001, Figure S3C), and a 4-gene muscle-enriched splicing score (ATP2A1, BIN1, CACNA1S, CLCN1) (r = −0.932, p < 0.0001, Figure S3D).21 Interestingly, both correlations tend to plateau along the axis of the two mis-splicing scores, suggesting that the [MBNL]inferred may have a better dynamic range and sensitivity in capturing mis-splicing changes in individuals with more severe disease.

There was no significant difference in mean [MBNL]inferred values across the treatment groups at baseline, although, on average, functional MBNL levels were lower in the 4 mg/kg group, which also had a more severe disease phenotype21 (Figure S3E). We also observed a positive correlation between [MBNL]inferred and ankle dorsiflexion (ADF) strength at baseline (r = 0.577, p = 0.0004, Figure S3F), consistent with previous publications.9,13 This relationship with [MBNL]inferred was also apparent for additional clinical measures of muscle strength (total quantitative myometry testing [QMT]: r = 0.567, p = 0.0006, Figure 2B, lower QMT: r = 0.447, p = 0.0091; Figure S3G; upper QMT: r = 0.561, p = 0.0007; Figure S3H) and mobility (10-m walk/run test [10MWRT]: r = −0.408, p = 0.0184) (Figure 2C), although no significant correlation was observed between [MBNL]inferred and myotonia (video hand opening time [vHOT]) at baseline (data not shown). Together, these data support the utility of [MBNL]inferred as a biomarker of disease severity in patients with DM1 and its use to evaluate the molecular efficacy of del-desiran in the MARINA trial.

Del-desiran treatment results in dose-dependent increase of MBNLinferred and accumulation of correctly spliced isoforms in patients with DM1

Del-desiran treatment led to a dose-dependent increase in [MBNL]inferred levels in treatment-phase biopsies, approaching statistical significance at the 4 mg/kg dose compared with placebo (mean fold change from baseline [FC], 2.02 versus 0.86, respectively, nominal p = 0.0336; adjusted p = 0.0540) (Figure 3A). We also observed an increase in [MBNL]inferred at the 2 mg/kg dose (mean FC, 1.42), while the 1 mg/kg cohort experienced little change (mean FC, 1.03).

Importantly, del-desiran-mediated correction of mis-splicing resulted in increased expression of the correctly spliced isoforms of genes affected in DM1, including genes associated with ion transport (ATP2A1, RYR1, CACNA1S, CLCN1), muscle structure (LDB3, DMD, BIN1), calcium signaling (CAMK2B, CAMK2G), transcription (NFIX, MEF2C), RNA splicing (MBNL1, MBNL2), and other critical functions important for normal muscle function24 (Figure S3). On average, the expression of these correctly processed isoforms increased in a dose-dependent manner (mean log2FC: 1 mg/kg, 0.13; 2 mg/kg, 0.21; 4 mg/kg, 0.32; placebo, −0.24, Figures 3B and S4) and approached statistical significance at the 4 mg/kg dose compared with placebo (nominal p = 0.0265; adjusted p = 0.1230) (Figure 3B). The mean expression changes of these correctly spliced isoforms significantly correlated with the changes in [MBNL]inferred levels in patients (r = 0.872, p < 0.0001, Figure 3C), suggesting that, upon degradation of mutDMPK, del-desiran increases functional MBNL and normalizes the downstream expression of genes that are important for muscle function.

Del-desiran treatment corrects the mis-splicing disease signature in patients with DM1

We identified 412 splicing events that were significantly altered in DM1 muscle compared with normal TA muscle (DM1 disease signature, Figures 4A and 4B). Patients with lower [MBNL]inferred scores appeared to have more disrupted disease signature, confirming the link between [MBNL]inferred and the broad mis-splicing characteristic of the disease (Figure 4B). Genes in this mis-splicing disease signature were enriched for pathways involved in muscle structure, contractility and development, and cytoskeletal organization and morphogenesis, attesting to their role in the pathophysiology of DM1 (Figure 4C). A broad shift toward improvement across the whole disease signature was observed after treatment of 2 and 4 mg/kg del-desiran, evidenced by PSI increase for exons abnormally skipped in DM1 and PSI decrease for those abnormally included in the disease (Figure 4D). As expected, the patients with the largest disease signature corrections were those with the greatest increases in [MBNL]inferred score, reinforcing that the impact of MBNL is not limited to the events used to derive the score, but encompasses global splicing (Figure 4D). On average, little change of the disease signature was observed in the placebo cohort.

There were 93 events that were defined as the top corrected events with the largest improvement following del-desiran treatment (p < 0.05, mean |ΔPSI|>0.05, Figures 5A and 5B). Among these events were genes known to be essential for normal muscle function and relevant to DM1 pathology, including muscle relaxation (CLCN1),25 muscle development (NFIX,26 MEF2C27), muscle structure (LDB3,28 ABLIM129), and RNA splicing factors (MBNL1, MBNL210). Importantly, the sequences of the top corrected events were enriched for MBNL binding motifs (e.g., GC-rich 4-mers, including well-defined YGCY motifs, such as CGCT and CGCC) present in the exon and/or nearby genomic locations, which further provides evidence for MBNL1-dependent reversal of mis-splicing (Figure 5C).12 The top corrected events were enriched for pathways important for muscle biology, such as actin binding, contractile fiber, I-band, and actomyosin structure organization, which were misregulated in the disease signature, suggesting that del-desiran-corrected events contribute to global improvement in muscle structure and function (Figure 5D). Quantification of splicing correction (mean PSI improvement) within the top 20 pathways that were enriched both within the disease signature and the top corrected events revealed improvement at the 2- and 4-mg/kg groups compared with the 1-mg/kg group, while no change on average was observed in the placebo group (Figure 5E). Consistently larger correction in pathways that directly relate to muscle biology, e.g., contractile fiber, I-band, muscle cell development, was observed in the 4- versus the 2-mg/kg group (Figure 5E).

In summary, del-desiran-mediated increases in inferred functional MBNL levels are associated with global correction of disease-relevant mis-splicing and point to improvement in downstream muscle biology in patients with DM1.

Del-desiran-mediated splicing correction correlates with improvements in functional endpoints in patients with DM1

We previously reported that del-desiran led to improvements in multiple functional endpoints in patients with DM1, including myotonia, QMT, and 10MWRT.21 We therefore evaluated the relationship between changes in [MBNL]inferred and the improvements in these measures of muscle function. A statistically significant correlation was evident between early changes in [MBNL]inferred (in treatment-phase biopsies) with later changes (3 months post-last dose) in muscle strength (total QMT, r = 0.3612, p = 0.0499, Figure 6A) myotonia (vHOT, r = −0.4250, p = 0.0216, Figure 6B), and a trend with mobility (10MWRT, Figure 6C). Indeed, participants who experienced early [MBNL]inferred increase showed a consistent mean improvement in these metrics of muscle function, with myotonia (vHOT) reaching significance compared with patients who experienced no improvement in [MBNL]inferred (p = 0.0265) (Figures 6D–6F). These data link the increase of free, non-sequestered, MBNL proteins to muscle function improvement, providing mechanistic evidence for the early signs of clinical improvements in del-desiran-treated patients with DM1.

An association between mutDMPK reduction, MBNLinferred release, and improvement of clinical outcomes in a patient treated with del-desiran

Quantification of mutDMPK in muscle biopsy is technically challenging as it is difficult to distinguish from the wild-type transcript using readily available methodology. However, a single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) in the DMPK gene (rs527221) is present in a minority of DM1 patients and may be used to detect allele-specific expression to evaluate both mutant and wild-type DMPK isoform expression levels.30

A single subject in the 1-mg/kg cohort of the MARINA trial harbored this SNP. In this patient, mutDMPK constituted 36.7% of DMPK transcripts containing exon 10 at baseline (Figure 7A). Total DMPK reduction in this patient 6 weeks post-dose was 48.8%, while mutDMPK and wtDMPK showed 31.4% and 70.0% reduction, respectively (Figure 7B). mutDMPK reduction was associated with concurrent correction of mis-splicing evidenced by a 1.3-fold increase from baseline in [MBNL]inferred (Figure 7C). Importantly, this patient also experienced improvements in myotonia 6 weeks post-dose (3.03 s decrease in vHOT, Figure 7D). Although representing a single case, these data provide direct evidence demonstrating the ability of del-desiran to reduce mutDMPK in the clinic, leading to increased functional MBNL levels and subsequent improvement of muscle function.

Discussion

Del-desiran demonstrated robust correction of the pathogenic mechanism in patients with DM1, including mutDMPK reduction, increase in MBNL activity, mis-splicing improvement, and production of properly processed transcripts. This PD effect in patients with DM1 builds on potent preclinical activity observed in both DM1 cell lines and cynomolgus monkeys.

The connection between MBNL proteins and muscle function has been studied in multiple preclinical models,9,10 supporting the central role of the sequestration of MBNL by mutDMPK in disease pathogenesis. Indeed, in a single del-desiran-treated patient harboring a rare SNP in the DMPK gene that allows for the specific quantification of the mutant CUG-containing transcript, decrease in mutDMPK mRNA was associated with increase in functional MBNL. In addition, consistent with previous reports,9 we observed a significant correlation between functional MBNL levels ([MBNL]inferred) and multiple measures of muscle function at baseline. Analysis of post-treatment samples provides evidence that increases in functional MBNL levels translate to better clinical outcomes in strength, myotonia, and mobility. Muscle strength correlates with MBNL activity both at baseline and post-treatment, suggesting a more direct link between MBNL levels and the underlying biology of muscle strength. Mobility, measured by 10MWRT, is a more complex body performance metric that integrates strength, coordination of multiple muscles, gait, balance, and other functions. Thus, it is not surprising that there is a weaker correlation of 10MWRT with MBNL levels at baseline, and a more scattered pattern between their post-treatment changes. There is also a clear correlation between myotonia changes and [MBNL]inferred changes post-treatment that confirms the relevance of alternative splicing to the regulation of muscle relaxation and supports that splicing correction plays a major role in the observed improvements in this measure of muscle health.

A limitation of this study is that a single muscle was chosen for biopsy, and we therefore cannot confirm splicing corrections throughout the body. While the splicing defects and inferred MBNL levels measured in the TA muscle may not fully reflect the disease biology and symptoms elsewhere in the body, the associations between increase in MBNL activity in the TA and improvements in whole-body muscle function supports broad molecular corrections across different muscle groups. This conclusion is consistent with the preclinical data showing DMPK reduction in all tested muscles (skeletal, smooth, and cardiac) following del-desiran treatment in cynomolgus monkeys, confirming that broad systemic delivery was achieved.

Another limitation is the relatively small number of biopsies and functional assessments available in this study. Our analysis revealed a temporal delay between molecular improvement and the consequent clinical changes, which is plausible given the disease mechanism. However, with a single treatment-phase biopsy and a small sample size, we cannot determine the dynamics of the biomarker changes or the optimal timing to study their relationship with muscle functions. Thus, while the increase of inferred MBNL levels in treatment-phase biopsies provides a proof-of-mechanism for the clinical efficacy of del-desiran in patients with DM1, to fully understand the dynamic relationship between splicing biomarkers and muscle functions requires pre-specified longitudinal analysis in a larger study.

In summary, biomarker analysis in the MARINA study (NCT05027269)21 confirms robust PD activity of del-desiran, including target engagement (mutDMPK knockdown) and downstream molecular response (functional MBNL increase and global improvement of splicing). Notably, although this trial was not powered for definitive clinical efficacy, the parallel improvement in multiple clinical outcomes provides encouraging signs of clinical benefit consistent with biomarker changes. Our data bridge the gap from preclinical proof-of-concept to clinical activity, establishing del-desiran as a promising disease-modifying therapy for DM1, currently under evaluation in the phase 2 MARINA-OLE (NCT05479981) and phase 3 HARBOR (NCT06411288) clinical trials.

Materials and methods

Study design

The MARINA clinical study (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT05027269) was a randomized, phase 1/2 double-blinded, placebo-controlled two-part trial to evaluate the safety, tolerability, and efficacy of del-desiran in patients with DM1.21 Part A administered a single dose of 1 mg/kg of del-desiran or placebo (saline); part B was a nested single and multiple ascending dose design with cohorts receiving three doses of 2 or 4 mg/kg of del-desiran or placebo at approximately 6-week intervals. Both parts included a 3-month follow-up (Figure S1). The study drug was administered intravenously; dose in mg reflects the siRNA component of del-desiran. The trial was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Council for Harmonisation guidelines for Good Clinical Practice and with approval from local institutional review boards. For detailed trial design, inclusion/exclusion criteria, randomization, statistical analysis plan, and protocol, refer to the main trial publication.21

TA muscle tissue biopsies were collected from participants at baseline, during the treatment phase of the trial (∼6 weeks post-single dose/43 days on study for the 1 mg/kg cohort or ∼6 weeks post-second dose/92 days on study for the 2- and 4-mg/kg cohorts) and during the recovery phase of the trial (∼3 months post-single dose/92 days on study for the 1-mg/kg cohort or ∼3 months post-third dose/183 days on study for the 2- and 4-mg/kg cohorts) (see Figure S1 for a study schema). Tissue samples were flash frozen and stored at −80°C. Healthy volunteer TA biopsy samples (n = 5) were used as controls for RNA-seq analyses. All clinical sample testing was performed by Precision for Medicine in a CLIA-certified environment. All successfully extracted and sequenced treatment phase samples are included in the current analysis (Figure S2). Transcription data quality control (QC) and analysis are described in the relevant sections.

RNA extraction from patient biopsy samples

RNA extraction was performed using a Direct-Zol 96 kit (cat. no. R2055, Zymo Research, Irvine, CA). RNA QC and quantification was performed using a Qubit and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA). Samples with RNA integrity number scores <4 were excluded from further analysis. The following input amounts were used for downstream assays: 100 ng for bulk RNA-seq and 10 ng for mis-splicing-targeted RNA-seq.

Bulk RNA-seq

RNA samples were subjected to library preparation with the Illumina Stranded Total RNA Prep, and ligation with a Ribo-Zero Plus kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Derived libraries were qualified with the Agilent Fragment Analyzer 5300 (Agilent) and quantified with a Qubit fluorometric assay. All libraries passed QC with acceptable yield and expected library smear size. Libraries were normalized, pooled, denatured, and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) with an S2 flow cell, at 2 × 150 bp according to the manufacturer’s protocol, resulting in a median of 148.7M pair-end reads per sample.

RNA-seq raw FASTQ files from MARINA patient and healthy control biopsy samples were aligned to human GRCh38 genome with Spliced Transcripts Alignment to a Reference (STAR) software.31 Gene-level and transcript/isoform-level expression were quantification by RNA-Seq by Expectation-Maximalization (RSEM) software,32 and differential gene expression was assessed by R package Limma.33 Splicing analysis was performed using replicate multivariate analysis of transcript splicing (rMATS).34 Detection in at least 80% samples was required to include splicing events in downstream analysis. Correctly spliced isoforms of genes of interest were defined as the total expression of all isoforms (CPM) that contain (PHTF2, SPAG9, CAMK2B, MYO9A, CLASP1, ARHGEF10L, CAPZB, CACNA1S, SORBS1, SLAIN2, CLK4, SOS1, PPP3CC, BEST3, MACF1, BIN1, KIF13A, NUMA1, MBNL2(1), ANK2, CACNA2D1, VPS39, AKAP13, INSR, IMPDH2, MAPT, MYBPC1, RYR1, ATP2A1, GFPT1, OPA1, DMD, and CCPG1) or do not contain (COPZ2, NFIX, PHKA1, MEF2C, DNM1L, LDB3, MBNL2(2), GOLGA4, CAMK2G, MBNL1, MXRA7, and CLCN1) the exons listed in Table S1.

MBNL inference

To infer MBNL for each sample, we used a method derived from Wagner et al.9 For baseline patient samples and healthy controls, we identified 31 splice events from our sufficiently observed splice events (detection in at least 80% of samples), corresponding to 31 out of 46 splice events used to infer MBNL by Wagner et al., after liftover to hg38 coordinates. Only sufficiently observed splice events with a 100% exact match of lifted-over coordinates between hg19 and hg38 were included. As per Wagner et al., we used a Bayesian model fitting a sigmoid curve between inferred MBNL for sample and PSI (percent spliced in) for each corresponding splice event observation. From this Bayesian fitting process, we derived the PSI versus MBNL curve parameters PSI min, PSI max, EC50, slope, and sigma for each splice event (Figure S5). We then used a weighted least-squares method to estimate an inferred MBNL value for each sample that best fit the derived parameters and observed splice event PSI values. The weights prioritized smaller PSI residuals for splice events with lower sigma parameters. We confirmed that the [MBNL]inferred scores calculated in this way showed a strong correlation to scores calculated using the previously reported curve parameters by Wagner et al. (Figure S6).

DM1 disease signature and del-desiran top corrected event signature analyses

To define a DM1 disease signature, we compared Ψ values for baseline MARINA samples versus healthy control samples using a t test, selecting splice events with average PSI difference between DM1 and healthy samples |ΔPSI | > 0.1. We then corrected p values after application of previous filters, and selected splice events with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.0001 (that also pass previous filters) as the disease response signature, composed of 412 unique splice events. A heatmap was used to visualize the changes in each treated sample’s PSI for each splice event, relative to their paired baseline sample. Row-wise, genes were ordered by mean PSI difference between healthy and DM1 baseline; column-wise, samples were ordered from highest to lowest [MBNL]inferred score.

To determine significant reversal of the disease signature by treatment (top corrected events), we performed the following comparisons of PSI values using a t test: 1 mg/kg del-desiran treatment-phase biopsy versus baseline; 2 mg/kg del-desiran treatment-phase biopsy versus baseline; 4 mg/kg del-desiran treatment-phase biopsy versus baseline. For each comparison, we extracted the subset of the disease signature events that fulfilled the following criteria: |average ΔPSI | > 0.05 between comparison groups; sign of ΔPSI for comparison between groups opposite of sign of ΔPSI for baseline DM1 versus healthy; p < 0.05.

Pathway enrichment analysis was performed by accessing the over-representation of disease signature events genes or top corrected events genes against the Gene Ontology terms in the Human Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, v.7.5.1)35 using the hypergeometric test. Significant enrichment was defined as Padj < 0.001. The network relationship of the enriched pathways was visualized by Cytoscape.36

MBNL motif enrichment was performed for both inclusion and exclusion events following the published method with some modifications.12 Briefly, 4-mer motifs were enumerated, and their occurrences across five different regions—including 250 bp downstream of the upstream exon, 250 bp upstream and downstream of the skipped exon (SE), the entire SE itself, and 250 bp upstream of the downstream exon—were counted and compared against the total number of motifs in that region for both significant (N = 93 top corrected events) and non-significant events (N = 10,000). The observed ratio of a given motif in significant events against the ratio of that motif in background splicing events was defined as the odds ratio and plotted for motif enrichment. Each SE was assigned an enrichment score corresponding to the highest enrichment score across the 5 examined regions. The top 30 4-mers with highest enrichment scores are shown.

DMPK allele-specific expression analysis

Human rs527221 located in exon 10 of DMPK is an informative Bpm1 polymorphism.37 The Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) was used to identify (HaplotypeCaller) and quantify (ASEReadCounter) the SNP from RNA-seq data.38 The DMPK gene consists of both rs527221-containing and non-containing isoforms, and DMPK allele-specific expression was calculated from the former pool only. Briefly, expression of either the mutant (G) or wild-type (C) allele was obtained by multiplying their observed polymorphism percentage with the total expression of rs527221-containing isoforms.

Mis-splicing targeted sequencing

A targeted RNA-seq assay was used to quantify the PSI of alternative exon splicing events listed in Table S1 using multiplexed gene-specific primers specific to the regions outside the exon of interest. The SMART-Seq v.4 PLUS Kit (cat. no. R400753, Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan) was used for library preparation. First-strand cDNA synthesis was conducted using the SMART method (Switching Mechanism at 5′ End of RNA Template), followed by eight cycles of long-distance PCR. Multiplex PCR (MP-PCR) was performed in two pools—pool A (17 cycles; ATP2A1, BIN1, CAPZB, CCPG1, CLASP1, CLCN1, DMD, GOLGA4, MBNL2, OPA1, and SOS1) and pool B (19 cycles; ANK2, BEST3, CACNA1S, CAMK2B, GFPT1, INSR, KIF13A, MBNL1, NFIX, RYR1, and VPS39) to account for different expression levels of the targets. MP-PCR products were subjected to 3× Agencourt AMPure XP (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA) bead purification, then underwent PCR with index primers. Libraries were purified, quantified, and normalized, and equal mass of each library was pooled for sequencing. Pooled libraries were sequenced on the NextSeq 500 system (Illumina) with the NextSeq 500/550 Mid Output kit v.2.5 (Illumina; 300 cycles) to generate an average of 3–4 million paired-end reads per sample per run. One percent of PhiX library (Illumina) was spiked in the pool to serve as sequencing QC.

Targeted RNA-seq bioinformatic analysis was conducted by Shannon Genomics. Briefly, RNA-seq raw FASTQ files were aligned to a customized reference human genome (GRCh38), which contained the sequences of all the variants to which the exon-included and exon-excluded reverse-transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) amplicons should align. For each of the 22 splice events (k), PSI was defined as PSI_k = ΣCount of exon-included amplicons/(ΣCount of exon-included + ΣCount of exon-excluded amplicons). Normalized or scaled PSI was further calculated as scaled_PSI_k = (PSI_k – PSI.healthy_k)/(PSI.DM1.95_k – PSI.healthy_k), where PSI.healthy_k was the median PSI from healthy individuals (N = 25), and PSI.DM1.95_k was the 95th percentile PSI from the most severely affected patients with DM1 (N = 172) (DMCRN dataset, Dr. Charles Thornton) for event k. The composite score was then calculated by averaging the normalized PSI values across all 22 splice events. The 4-gene muscle-enriched score was calculated by averaging the normalized PSI values of ATP2A1, BIN1, CACNA1S, and CLCN1.

Functional endpoint assessment

Functional endpoints were assessed as described in the primary analysis of the MARINA study data.21 In short, QMT was measured as the force (kg) transduced at the joint of six muscle groups (hand grip, elbow flexion, elbow extension, knee flexion, knee extension, and ADF), which was then converted to percent predicted normal using normative data from a healthy population.39 Total QMT reflects the average of all six muscle groups, while lower QMT includes knee flexion, knee extension, and ADF only. vHOT was assessed by a timed video recording of the subject holding a fist for 3 s and then opening their hand as quickly as possible. The 10MWRT measures the time it takes a subject to walk or run 10 m. Correlation analysis between biomarkers and functional metrics at baseline were performed using Pearson correlation. Comparisons of functional metrics change 3 months post-last dose between subgroups at treatment phase ([MBNL]inferred increase or [MBNL]inferred decrease) were performed using two-sided t test.

Cell lines used for in vitro experiments

Immortalized human myoblast cell lines,40 used for analysis of DMPK reduction in the nucleus versus cytoplasm and in vitro mis-splicing correction, were obtained from MyoBank, affiliated with EuroBioBank (authorization ref. AC-2019-3502). The DM1 immortalized human myoblasts22 had 2,600 CUG repeats. The control immortalized human myoblast cell line was derived from the fascia lata of a 20-year-old healthy male donor (AB1167C20FL). Myotubes, used for CUG foci staining analyses, were differentiated from M65779 primary myoblasts derived from a patient with DM1 (male, 27 years old, 1,300 CTG repeats), obtained from CHU de Québec-Université Laval, Canada. Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) and OptiMEM (Life Technologies) following standard procedures.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractionation of DM1 myoblasts

Immortalized myoblasts derived from patients with DM1 were transfected 24 h after plating with DMPK siRNAs at 5 nM final concentration and harvested 3 or 4 days post-transfection. Cells were fractionated using methods reported previously.41 Fractions were collected in TRIzolol for RNA isolation using a Direct-zol-96 RNA isolation kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA) and purified RNA was converted to cDNA using a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA). cDNA was analyzed by qPCR using the TaqMan Fast Universal Master Mix II (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and TaqMan probes (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in duplicates. The target gene DMPK expression was normalized to a reference gene (PPIB) and the percentage of target mRNA expression in treatment samples was determined relative to the control treatment using the 2–ΔΔCt method.42 In all fractions, HPRT and MALAT1 expression was measured to ensure proper fractionation.

Nuclear foci detection by fluorescent in situ hybridization and immunofluorescence

Primary human skeletal muscle myoblasts from a donor patient with DM1 (M65779) were cultured on MyoScreen plates (CYTOO, Grenoble, France) and induction of differentiation was performed as described previously.43 Differentiating myotubes were transfected with DMPK siRNAs at 1 nM final concentration. Myotubes were fixed with formalin 7–9 days post-treatment. Fluorescent in situ hybridization was performed using a Cy3-labeled (CAG)5 oligonucleotide probe (PNABio, Thousand Oaks, CA) as described previously,44 but with the following modification: cells were permeabilized for 15 min with 0.5% Triton in phosphate-buffered saline. Nuclei were visualized with Hoechst 33342 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Imaging was performed with the Operetta HCS platform (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA) using a 40× objective. A total of 81 fields were acquired per well. Image processing and analysis were performed using dedicated algorithms developed on the Acapella High Content Imaging Software (PerkinElmer) by CYTOO (Grenoble, France). RNA foci were detected specifically in the myotube nuclei and quantified using a threshold applied to the Cy3 channel. Images were generated using ImageJ software.

In vitro alternative splicing

For the alternative splicing analysis in immortalized human myotubes, immortalized myoblasts were plated on a Matrigel-coated 96-well tissue culture plate. The next day, myoblasts were transfected with the specified concentrations of DMPK siRNA; 48 h post-transfection, differentiation of myoblasts was induced. Myotubes were collected 4 days post-differentiation with TRIzolol. RNA was isolated as described above. RNA-seq libraries were prepared at Genewiz using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA). The libraries were sequenced at Genewiz using a 2 × 150 bp paired-end configuration on a HiSeq instrument (Illumina). RNA-seq analysis was performed by Monoceros Biosystems. In short, RNA-seq raw FASTQ files were aligned to the human GRCh38 genome with STAR software.31 Splicing analysis was performed using rMATS.34 Significantly mis-spliced events were defined as follows: events significantly differentially spliced in DM1 siDMPK (5 nM) versus mock treatment were defined as average PSI difference > 20% and FDR < 5% (rMATS); events significantly different between the DM1-mock versus healthy-mock conditions were defined as adjusted p < 0.05, ANOVA with Tukey HSD post hoc test.

Animal treatment, tissue collection, and analysis

All animal studies were conducted following protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, in accordance with the regulations outlined in the United States Department of Agriculture Animal Welfare Act as well as the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.”45 Naive male cynomolgus monkeys (approximately 2–4 years old) were dosed as described by a single i.v. infusion over 30 min. Monkey tissue biopsy or necropsy samples were collected at specified times, flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, and homogenized in 1 mL cold TRIzolol using a FastPrep-24 (MP Biomedicals, Irvine, CA). RNA isolation and RT-qPCR analysis were performed as described above. Ct values for PPIB and CD63 were averaged for each sample, forming a composite reference gene Ct value, which was used in ΔΔCt calculations.

Statistical analysis

Throughout the manuscript, group comparisons were performed using two-sided t tests; p values of less than 0.05 were considered significant. Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson correlation; p values of less than 0.05 were considered significant. Global transcriptomic and splicing analyses were analyzed as described in their respective sections within the materials and methods.
