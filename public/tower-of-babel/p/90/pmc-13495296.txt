Catalysts of music performance anxiety: a qualitative exploration of undergraduate students through the person-environment-occupation framework
Authors: Mustafa Cemali, Burcu Semin Akel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile music performance anxiety is traditionally analyzed through individual psychological traits, there is a scarcity of research adopting holistic frameworks that integrate person, environment, and occupation dimensions. This study utilizes a qualitative approach to examine the multi-dimensional catalysts of performance anxiety among undergraduate music students within the Person–Environment–Occupation (PEO) framework.MethodsSemi-structured face-to-face interviews were conducted with 26 conservatory students. Data were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using a content analysis approach within the PEO framework. Findings were organized into a hierarchical structure of categories and subcategories.ResultsResults indicate that performance anxiety is a multifaceted phenomenon. At the person level, anxiety is driven by perfectionist tendencies and cognitive distortions (fear of negative evaluation). The environmental dimension highlights the impact of suboptimal acoustics, pedagogical attitudes, and competitive institutional climates. The occupation dimension identifies insufficient preparation and the high-stakes nature of solo debuts as primary stressors.ConclusionMusical performance anxiety emerges from the synergistic interaction of personal, environmental and occupation-related factors. Introduction

Musical performance is a complex endeavor requiring the integration of technical mastery, cognitive control, and emotional regulation. Beyond technical proficiency, musicians must function within evaluative environments while maintaining physiological and psychological stability [1, 2]. When these multidimensional demands exceed an individual’s adaptive capacity, the resulting condition is termed Music Performance Anxiety (MPA)- a multifaceted construct encompassing cognitive, autonomic, and behavioral symptoms [3]. The literature consistently reports a high prevalence of MPA, affecting approximately 30–60% of musicians, with particularly pronounced effects among undergraduate students [4]. Importantly, MPA extends beyond the immediate performance context, contributing to performance avoidance, impeded academic progression, and diminished long-term vocational satisfaction [5].

To date, research on MPA has been largely dominated by reductionist, person-centered paradigms. Existing studies primarily examine internal psychological constructs—such as trait anxiety, perfectionism, and self-efficacy-commonly assessed through quantitative psychometric instruments [6, 7]. While these approaches provide valuable correlational insights, they often overlook the contextual and situational dynamics of the performance setting [8]. In practice, performance anxiety is shaped by multiple environmental and task-related factors, including stage conditions, training context, the presence of teachers or audience members, whether the performance is solo or ensemble-based, and the rehearsal and preparation process [8, 9]. Musical performance is therefore not an isolated phenomenon but emerges from the dynamic interaction among individual characteristics, the physical and social environment, and the specific demands of the performance task [10].

Within the literature, MPA has predominantly been investigated using quantitative methodologies, which have contributed substantially to identifying anxiety levels and their associations with individual variables [11, 12]. However, quantitative approaches may be limited in capturing how performance anxiety is experienced in situ, the contexts in which it intensifies, and the mechanisms through which contextual factors shape these experiences [7, 13]. Although qualitative studies on MPA do exist, their number remains relatively small. Moreover, existing qualitative research has largely focused on subjective experiences and personal characteristics, typically examining anxiety from a predominantly person-centered perspective [9, 14]. Consequently, studies adopting a holistic lens that simultaneously considers environmental conditions and the structural characteristics of the performance occupation remain scarce [14].

Despite the increasing theoretical focus on holistic and contextual approaches in psychology [15], a significant proportion of studies on music performance still rely on person-centred explanations. However, performance is not merely the result of personal characteristics, but rather a dynamic process shaped by environmental conditions and the structural features of the occupation. Therefore, to understand the multidimensional nature of performance, personal characteristics, environmental conditions, and the structural requirements of the occupation need to be considered together [11]. In this context, performance is best understood through the interaction of person, environment, and occupation dimensions as conceptualized within the PEO framework, which emphasizes the dynamic relationship among these dimensions rather than considering them in isolation [16, 17]. Within this framework, the person dimension refers to the individual’s emotional, cognitive, and personal characteristics; the environment dimension encompasses the physical, educational, social, and cultural contexts in which performance occurs; and the occupation dimension refers to meaningful and goal-directed musical occupations, including the structural, technical, performance-related, and task-specific demands associated with musical performance, as well as factors related to occupational participation, professional continuity, employment opportunities, and future career development [17]. Occupations are not merely outcomes; they represent meaningful forms of participation that shape how performance is experienced, what demands arise, and how individuals respond to these demands. When personal characteristics, the physical and social environment, and the structural and technical features of the occupation are considered together, a more holistic understanding of the performance experience is possible [9, 18].

However, it has been found that there is a lack of literature examining music performance anxiety from the perspectives of the person, environmental and occupation dimensions together [9]. As far as we know, no research has examined performance anxiety from the perspective of these three dimensions using a qualitative approach. Therefore, the present study aims to examine the multifaceted nature of music performance anxiety by considering the person, environmental, and occupation-specific factors that influence it.

Method

Study design

This qualitative descriptive study was conducted at the Hacettepe University State Conservatory to investigate the multidimensional catalysts of music performance anxiety. Ethical approval was granted by the Hacettepe University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (GO 17/04, 28.02.2017). The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [19].

Participants and sampling

A purposive sampling strategy [20] was employed to recruit undergraduate music students from the Hacettepe University State Conservatory. This sampling strategy was used to recruit participants who could provide in-depth accounts of severe music performance anxiety. Participants were required to be aged 18–30 and exhibit high levels of MPA, defined as a score of ≥ 105 on the Turkish version of the Kenny Music Performance Anxiety Inventory (K-MPAI), a standardized measure of music performance anxiety [21]. Students with a diagnosed psychiatric disorder or those who did not complete the interview were excluded. Of the 86 students screened, 29 met the inclusion criteria. Three students declined to participate; thus, 26 students completed the study. Sample size was guided by the principle of data saturation [22]. No new themes emerged after the 21st interview; recruitment continued until the 26th interview to ensure thematic redundancy and analytic depth.

The final sample consisted of 26 undergraduate students aged 18–30 years (mean age: 21 ± 1.57 years). Of the participants, 23.1% (n = 6) were first-year, 23.1% (n = 6) second-year, 42.3% (n = 11) third-year, and 11.5% (n = 3) fourth-year students. Females comprised 57.7% (n = 15) of the sample and males 42.3% (n = 11).

Data collection

Participants first provided demographic information, including age, gender, and department. Both quantitative and qualitative tools were employed to identify factors associated with performance anxiety in undergraduate music students. Quantitative data were collected using the K-MPAI to determine anxiety levels, while qualitative data were obtained through a semi-structured interview form designed to explore students’ experiences, perceptions, and environmental influences.

Kenny Music Performance Anxiety Inventory (K-MPAI)

K-MPAI was developed in 2004 by Kenny et al. to assess performance anxiety in musicians [23]. K-MPAI consists of 25 items and five sub-dimensions: Negative Performance Perception, Psychological Vulnerability, Somatic Anxiety, Self-Control, and Physiological Vulnerability. Each item is rated on a six-point Likert scale (0 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). The total score ranges from 0 to 150, with participants scoring 105 or higher being classified as having a high level of music performance anxiety. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the K-MPAI was conducted by Tokinan et al. in 2013. The findings demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.94, supporting the use of the scale for assessing music performance anxiety in Turkish populations [24].

Semi-structured interview form

The interview form was developed through a multi-stage process. The semi-structured interview guide was developed specifically for this study and has not been previously published or adapted from an existing instrument. First, relevant national and international literature was reviewed [23–26]. A preliminary pool of 122 questions was generated by the researchers. After removing repetitive or overlapping items, the form was refined to 29 questions, including four main questions and 25 sub-questions. The full English version of the interview guide is provided as Supplementary File 1. This approach is consistent with qualitative research practices, where data collection instruments are developed in a flexible and context-specific manner to capture participants’ experiences in depth [19, 27].

To enhance the content validity and clarity of the instrument, the interview guide was reviewed by an expert in qualitative research, who evaluated the questions in terms of relevance, comprehensiveness, and clarity. Based on the expert’s feedback, minor revisions were made to improve the wording and structure of the questions.

In addition, a pilot study was conducted with three participants prior to the main data collection process to assess the comprehensibility and applicability of the interview guide. Based on feedback from the pilot interviews, minor revisions were made to improve the wording, sequencing, and flow of the questions. The pilot interviews were not included in the final analysis.

The interview guide explored factors influencing performance anxiety across person, environment, and occupation dimensions [9, 16]. Accordingly, the interview guide was structured around the three dimensions of the PEO framework. While the overarching dimensions were established through the framework and guided the structure of the interview, the subcategories within each dimension emerged inductively from participants’ responses during the analysis process. Within this framework, the person dimension addressed awareness of anxiety, triggering situations, emotional and cognitive responses, personality characteristics, self-confidence, and future expectations. The environment dimension included physical conditions of the performance venue, teacher–student relationships, peer context, and audience presence. The occupation dimension focused on characteristics of the musical piece, technical performance demands, stage experience, rehearsal routines, practice strategies, and pre-performance preparation.

Interviews were conducted in a quiet, private setting within the students’ classrooms. Data collection took place between March 2017 and June 2017. Participants received information about the study purpose, confidentiality procedures, and audio recording, and written informed consent was obtained prior to participation. Interviews lasted between 20 and 65 min and were audio-recorded with permission. All interviews were conducted face-to-face by the first author. The interviewer followed the order of the main questions while using probing questions to deepen responses when necessary. The researcher also established rapport and clarified that participation would not affect academic standing to ensure honesty and reflexivity. In addition, the interviewer had prior experience in qualitative research and maintained reflexive awareness throughout the study process. Efforts were made to minimize the influence of the researcher’s professional background and assumptions during data collection and analysis.

Data analysis

Sociodemographic data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 26.0 and summarized using frequency (n), percentage (%), and mean ± standard deviation values. Qualitative data were analyzed using a content analysis approach within the framework of the Person–Environment–Occupation framework [28, 29]. While the overarching dimensions of person, environment, and occupation were conceptually considered during the development of the interview guide and interpretation process, the subcategories within each dimension emerged inductively from participants’ responses. The analysis process involved the systematic examination, coding, and organization of data into meaningful structures. To this end, the audio recordings were transcribed verbatim into Microsoft Word and checked for accuracy. The transcripts were read line by line, and initial coding was performed by the first researcher.

Within each framework dimension, the resulting codes were grouped based on similarities and differences within the data and combined to form subcategories. The subcategories were structured by considering the patterns, repetitions, and semantic relationships emerging from the data.

To enhance the reliability of the analysis process, independent coding was conducted by the second researcher, and the coding framework and the established category structures were reviewed jointly by both researchers. Disagreements between the researchers regarding coding and category interpretation were discussed in detail and resolved through consensus by revisiting the original transcripts, comparing interpretations, and refining the coding framework where necessary. This iterative process contributed to the consistency and credibility of the analysis. An inter-rater reliability coefficient (e.g., Cohen’s kappa) was not calculated, as the study adopted a consensus-based qualitative analysis approach.

The resulting subcategories were organized and interpreted within the pre-established dimensions of person, environment, and occupation to explain the factors influencing performance anxiety. Direct quotes from participants were included to support the findings, and frequency distributions were reported to demonstrate the distinctiveness of the categories.

Discussion

The findings of this qualitative study indicate that music performance anxiety in students is a multidimensional phenomenon emerging from the interaction of personal, environmental, and occupation-related factors, rather than a single causal source. Participants described experiencing anxiety not only in relation to individual psychological characteristics but also in connection with the physical and social performance context and the structural demands of the occupation. Within the person dimension, anxiety proneness, perfectionism, fear of mistakes, and expectations of negative evaluation were prominent. Within the environmental dimension, stage and hall conditions, teacher attitudes, peer relationships, and family expectations emerged as influential. At the occupation level, solo performance, first-time stage experience, insufficient preparation, and concerns regarding professional future were particularly salient. The findings also indicate that person-, environment-, and occupation-related factors do not operate independently but interact dynamically in shaping performance anxiety. Individual characteristics such as emotional sensitivity or self-confidence may be influenced by environmental conditions, including audience presence and teacher attitudes, as well as by the structural demands of the performance occupation. Similarly, occupation-related factors such as technical difficulty and preparation processes may interact with both personal and environmental dimensions. This interconnected structure suggests that performance anxiety should be understood as a multidimensional phenomenon emerging from the interaction of these components, rather than being explained solely through isolated individual characteristics. In this respect, evaluating music performance anxiety through the combined interaction of person-, environment-, and occupation-related dimensions may provide a broader contextual understanding of performance experiences.

Existing literature demonstrates that MPA is associated with personality traits. Individuals with perfectionistic tendencies and anxious dispositions tend to report higher anxiety levels [30]. Kaleńska-Rodzaj showed that negative pre-performance emotional states and pessimistic affective patterns are linked with MPA and perceived audience pressure [31]. Similarly, research on young adult musicians indicates that emotional profiles dominated by fear or shame are associated with lower performance satisfaction, whereas emotion regulation and self-efficacy beliefs shape performance experience [32]. The present findings support this body of evidence while extending it by showing that MPA may be influenced by multiple personal characteristics rather than a single trait. Participants associated anxiety with a broad constellation of characteristics-including anxiousness, perfectionism, obsessive and pessimistic tendencies, irritability, impulsiveness, aggressive attitudes, low self-esteem, and emotional sensitivity-suggesting that multiple personal tendencies may contribute to anxiety in different ways. These results further indicate that personal characteristics may interact with environmental pressures and occupation-related demands, thereby shaping how performance situations are perceived and experienced by individuals. These findings should be interpreted within the descriptive and qualitative nature of the study, and no causal or comparative conclusions can be drawn regarding the relationships among different personality characteristics. Accordingly, the present findings may contribute to a broader understanding of how individual characteristics shape performance experiences within specific environmental and occupation-related contexts.

There is also strong evidence linking MPA with cognitive processes. Musicians have been shown to report increased negative thoughts after performances, particularly in evaluative audience contexts [33], and anticipatory negative evaluation has been identified as a key determinant of anxiety experience [34]. Cognitive correlates of MPA include intrusive thoughts, reduced self-confidence, and concentration difficulties [35]. Consistent with these findings, participants in the present study described anxiety through diverse cognitive processes, including fear of mistakes, concern about negative evaluation, worry about forgetting lyrics or notes, comparison with peers, and doubts about performing under pressure. These results suggest that MPA arises from the interaction of multiple concurrent cognitive appraisals rather than a single maladaptive belief. Furthermore, these cognitive processes appear to be shaped not only by individual interpretations but also by environmental evaluation contexts and the structural demands of performance situations. Accordingly, the present findings support a more multidimensional understanding of cognitive experiences related to music performance anxiety.

The performance environment represents another important contextual determinant. Prior studies indicate that acoustic characteristics, lighting, spatial configuration, and stage setup can influence musicians’ anxiety and performance perception [36]. Research conducted in conservatory settings has similarly identified acoustics and physical performance conditions as environmental contributors to MPA [37, 38]. The present findings reinforce this evidence, with participants frequently associating anxiety with hall acoustics, stage flooring, orchestra placement, lighting, and general venue conditions. These results emphasize that spatial and ergonomic characteristics of performance environments are not peripheral but may directly shape the performance experience. The findings further suggest that environmental conditions may influence how individuals perceive performance demands and manage emotional responses during performance situations. In this respect, performance anxiety appears to emerge not only from individual characteristics but also from the interaction between the performer and the surrounding performance environment. Regular acoustic and ergonomic assessment of performance spaces, along with targeted environmental improvements, may therefore contribute to reducing anxiety-provoking conditions.

Teacher attitudes also emerged as a significant factor. Previous research has shown that teacher behaviors influence stage preparation, anxiety experience, and perceived self-efficacy in music education contexts [39, 40]. Teacher interaction styles have likewise been linked to students’ emotional and cognitive responses to performance situations [41]. Consistent with this literature, participants reported that teachers’ gestures, facial expressions, gaze, distracting behaviors, destructive criticism, excessive expectations, or pressure-inducing attitudes increased anxiety. Notably, indifferent or ambiguous teacher attitudes were also perceived as anxiety-provoking. These findings underscore the central role of pedagogical climate and teacher–student interaction in shaping performance experiences. Increasing educators’ awareness of supportive communication styles, constructive feedback practices, and anxiety-sensitive pedagogical approaches may help reduce students’ performance anxiety.

The social environment on music performance anxiety is also addressed. A study has shown that parental, teacher, and peer support are associated with MPA, and that perceived peer support may reduce anxiety through its effects on emotional regulation and self-efficacy [42]. Social evaluation, particularly by peers or audiences, has also been linked to heightened stress responses [43]. The current findings align with this literature, indicating that competitive peer environments, comparison pressures, perceived peer evaluation, and unsupportive social climates increase anxiety. Family expectations, perceived support levels, and the presence of musicians in the family were also identified as influential sociocultural factors. These findings suggest that social environments may shape not only external performance pressure but also individuals’ emotional interpretations and self-evaluative processes during performance situations. These results highlight the importance of fostering supportive and collaborative educational climates. Encouraging constructive peer feedback, strengthening peer support systems, and increasing family awareness may contribute to reducing anxiety.

Occupation-related and professional-process factors also played a decisive role. Previous research suggests that preparation quality, performance structure, and repertoire difficulty influence anxiety levels [44, 45]. Performances requiring solo execution, first-time performance of a piece, and technically demanding repertoire have been identified as common anxiety triggers [45]. The present findings confirm and extend this evidence. Participants associated anxiety with insufficient preparation, time pressure, ineffective practice strategies, solo performance demands, first-time stage experience, and technical difficulty. Additionally, vocational concerns—such as limited job opportunities, competitive professional environments, and uncertainty about career prospects—were reported as anxiety-related factors. These findings indicate that MPA is not confined to the performance moment but develops dynamically through interactions among preparation processes, structural performance demands, professional expectations, perceived social evaluation, and individual cognitive vulnerability. Accordingly, the present findings may highlight the potential importance of preparation processes, gradual performance experiences, and performance-related skill development in understanding music performance anxiety.

The impact of a piece’s structural characteristics and performance style on performance anxiety is emphasized in recent studies. Ryan and colleagues, in their qualitative study with adolescent pianists, reported that performances requiring solo execution increased performance anxiety; anxiety was experienced at a more pronounced level, particularly in works performed for the first time and those involving high technical difficulty. The study also noted that expectations of making mistakes and forgetting in memorized performances were among the significant triggers of pre-performance anxiety [45]. Papageorgi has shown that characteristics specific to the performance task influence the intensity and form of performance anxiety [9]. This study indicates that solo performances intensify anxiety by increasing perceived responsibility, while group performances can create a context that reduces anxiety for some musicians but increases it for others due to pressure to conform and be evaluated [9]. The current study shows that factors related to the work and performance process are decisive in the experience of performance anxiety. Participants identified solo performance, performing a work or piece for the first time, the technical difficulty level of the work, and the fear of memorization/forgetting as the main factors that increase performance anxiety. Additionally, some participants stated that they could experience anxiety in performances with a choir or orchestra due to the pressure of evaluation and conformity. When these findings are considered together, it is thought that music performance anxiety is not solely related to individual characteristics; rather, it is a context-sensitive experience that arises in interaction with the structural demands of the piece and the form of the performance. Accordingly, the present findings may contribute to understanding how performance structure, technical demands, memorization requirements, and performance format interact in shaping music performance anxiety experiences.

Recent literature further emphasizes that MPA is linked to musicians’ perceptions of professional future. Studies have shown that employment uncertainty and competitive career environments negatively affect performance focus and anxiety levels among music students [8]. Similarly, perceiving performance as an indicator of professional competence can intensify pressure, particularly in early-career musicians [5]. The present findings support this perspective, showing that perceived limited employment opportunities, restricted academic or orchestra positions, insufficient societal recognition of the profession, and high competition contribute to performance-related anxiety. The perception that performance outcomes may shape future career trajectories appears to amplify performance pressure. These findings suggest that music performance anxiety may extend beyond the immediate performance context and become intertwined with professional identity, future expectations, and perceptions of occupational continuity. In this respect, professional uncertainty appears to interact with both individual concerns and performance-related demands in shaping anxiety experiences.

This study has several limitations. First, the findings are based on participants’ self-reports, which may be influenced by the subjective nature of individual perceptions and experiences. Second, the sample consisted only of university-level music students. This limits the generalizability of the findings to a broader population of musicians, such as professional musicians, younger age groups, or individuals from different educational contexts. The way performance anxiety is experienced can vary depending on the level of professional experience and the educational process. Third, all participants were selected from a single institutional and cultural context. Since music education, performance evaluation processes, and pedagogical approaches can differ across cultures, this may limit the transferability of the findings to different cultural contexts. Fourth, the diverse musical fields (e.g., opera, instrumental fields, etc.) of the participants limited the in-depth examination of field-specific performance anxiety dynamics. Future studies with more homogeneous samples and field-specific comparisons may be beneficial. From a methodological perspective, the lack of triangulation among data sources (e.g., observation, performance evaluations, or different data collection methods) can be considered another limitation of the study. Furthermore, it is possible to further strengthen the analytical link between qualitative findings and broader theoretical frameworks. Finally, the fact that the data was collected in 2017 may limit the relevance of the findings to the current context, considering the digitalization, distance learning applications, and technological developments in music education and performance environments in recent years. However, the present study demonstrates that music performance anxiety should not be evaluated solely through individual characteristics, but rather through the interaction of person-, environment-, and occupation-related dimensions, thereby providing a comprehensive framework for understanding performance anxiety despite evolving educational and performance conditions. In particular, the limited number of studies addressing music performance anxiety within such a multidimensional framework increases the conceptual relevance of the present findings. Furthermore, factors such as perfectionism, fear of negative evaluation, teacher attitudes, environmental conditions, and preparation processes appear to remain important components influencing music performance experiences despite changing performance contexts. Therefore, the present findings may provide a useful reference point for future comparative studies conducted across different periods and evolving performance environments.

Future studies are recommended to be conducted with broader samples encompassing different age groups and professional levels (students, professional musicians, etc.), in diverse cultural and institutional contexts, and to utilize multiple data collection methods such as observation, performance evaluation, and quantitative measurements. Moreover, re-examining music performance anxiety, particularly in post-pandemic digital and hybrid performance environments, and comparatively examining different periods will make significant contributions to the field.

Conclusion

This qualitative study demonstrates that music performance anxiety is a multidimensional phenomenon arising from the interaction of personal characteristics, cognitive processes, environmental conditions, occupation-related demands, and professional expectations. Participants’ experiences indicate that anxiety unfolds as a dynamic process extending from preparation stages to performance situations and concerns about future career outcomes.

These findings underscore the importance of multidimensional assessment frameworks in educational and performance-support contexts. Educators and professionals working with musicians should consider personality traits, cognitive patterns, perceptions of the performance environment, and occupation-related demands when developing strategies to support students experiencing music performance anxiety.

In educational settings, incorporating structured content on performance anxiety awareness, coping strategies, and gradual performance exposure into conservatory and music training programs may be beneficial. Future research should employ mixed-method designs to examine MPA across personal, environmental, and occupation dimensions, and should develop and evaluate intervention programs targeting these factors to enhance practical applications in the field.
