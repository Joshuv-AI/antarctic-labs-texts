Imperatorin restores behavioral and recognition memory deficits induced by REM sleep deprivation in male rats
Authors: Mohammad Mojtaba Ayati, Maryam Eslami, Batool Ghorbani Yekta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMany studies have shown the deleterious effects of rapid-eye movement (REM) sleep deprivation (SD) on cognitive functions and synaptic plasticity markers. On the other hand, imperatorin (IMP, extracted from the fruits of Angelica dahurica and Peucedanum praeruptorum) may exert significant pro-cognitive and neuroprotective effects. The present study aimed to explore the potential therapeutic effects of IMP on behavioral and molecular changes induced by REM SD, before or after training in the novel object recognition (NOR) memory test. REM SD was done for 24 h and IMP was orally administered at the dose of 100 mg/kg (immediately after REM SD). Locomotion, anxiety-like behavior, and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and cAMP response element binding (CREB) levels were also assessed in the prefrontal cortex. The results showed that REM SD impaired NOR memory, reduced locomotor activity, increased anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors, with more severe effects of the post-train REM SD. Both pre- and post-train REM SD reduced BDNF and CREB mRNA expression levels in the prefrontal cortex. IMP could restore and increase BDNF and CREB expression levels, respectively, only in pre-train REM SD rats, and reduce behavioral impairments caused by pre- or post-train REM SD. In conclusion, imperatorin attenuated REM SD–induced recognition-memory and behavioral abnormalities, whereas its molecular effects were more selective and were limited to BDNF and CREB mRNA expression changes in the prefrontal cortex, particularly in the pre-train REM SD condition. These findings suggest that BDNF/CREB-related transcriptional modulation may contribute to, but may not fully explain, the behavioral effects of imperatorin.Keywords: Rapid-eye movement (REM), Sleep deprivation (SD), Imperatorin (IMP), Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), CAMP response element binding (CREB)Subject terms: Diseases, Neuroscience

Introduction

Sleep is a fundamental physiological process characterized by reduced mental and physical activity with essential neural mechanisms, involved in modulating memory1. Accumulating evidence indicates that REM sleep contributes to memory processing and emotional regulation, and that REM sleep disruption can impair both cognitive and affective domains. In rodents, REM sleep deprivation (REM SD) has been repeatedly associated with deficits in recognition-related tasks, including novel object recognition (NOR), as well as increased anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors. Importantly, NOR performance depends on intact encoding and consolidation of object representations, and the memory domain assessed depends on the retention interval; a 24-h delay is commonly used to evaluate long-term recognition memory2.

At the molecular level, sleep loss alters synaptic plasticity signaling cascades, including pathways converging on cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB) and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), which are widely implicated in synaptic consolidation and memory-related plasticity. Sleep deprivation has been shown to downregulate components of CaMKII–CREB–BDNF signaling in the prefrontal cortex, supporting the plausibility that impaired plasticity signaling contributes to behavioral phenotypes after REM SD. In addition to hippocampal contributions, the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) participates in recognition memory processes and interacts with hippocampal networks during novelty discrimination, making it a mechanistically relevant region for assessing plasticity-related markers in recognition-memory paradigms3.

Imperatorin (IMP) is a naturally occurring furanocoumarin reported to exert neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects in preclinical models. Prior studies indicate that IMP can mitigate experimentally induced cognitive impairments and modulate neuroinflammation-associated mechanisms, suggesting that IMP may counteract REM SD–related memory and behavioral disturbances. Based on these converging lines of evidence, we investigated whether IMP attenuates REM SD–induced recognition-memory impairment and behavioral alterations, and whether such effects are associated with changes in CREB and BDNF expression in the prefrontal cortex4.

One of the most critical roles of sleep is the consolidation of long-term memory. Adequate and high-quality sleep maintains metabolic balance, cardiovascular function, and emotional stability, and also contributes to overall physical and psychological well-being5,6. Sleep broadly consists of two main alternating phases: rapid-eye movement (REM) sleep and non-REM (NREM) sleep. These stages alternate cyclically throughout sleep, while each stage plays distinct physiological and cognitive roles. REM sleep is particularly involved in synaptic plasticity, neural reorganization, memory consolidation, and emotional processing7,8. Meta-analyses on sleep deprivation (SD) have demonstrated that both total SD and REM SD are significant risk factors for memory function9. It has been shown that REM SD impairs neuronal plasticity, reduces hippocampal neuronal arborization, reduces synaptic plasticity, increases anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors, and leads to memory and learning deficits10,11. Moreover, REM SD impairs novel object recognition (NOR) memory performance in rats12,13. However, the exact molecular and neurobiological mechanisms remain unclear14–16. Among the proposed mechanisms, two key signaling pathways have been implicated: brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and cAMP response element-binding (CREB) protein, which are essential for memory and learning17.

BDNF is a well-characterized neurotrophic factor in both the peripheral nervous system (PNS) and central nervous system (CNS), notably in the hippocampus and cortex regions. It plays a crucial role in long-term memory formation, neural development, synaptic plasticity, sleep regulation, and learning process during sleep18. Based on recent studies, BDNF plays a key role in sleep regulation, particularly in REM sleep. Decreased BDNF expression levels in the hippocampus and cortex has been linked to insomnia and cognitive dysfunction19–22. During REM sleep, mature BDNF protein binds to its high-affinity receptor, Tyrosine kinase B (TrkB), which leads to the activation of downstream signaling pathways including phospholipase C-ɣ (PLC-γ), CREB, and phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase (PI3-K)23–25. CREB is a transcription factor expressed mainly in the amygdala and hippocampus, which plays a major role in learning, memory formation, and neuronal survival process26–28. It can bind the cAMP Response Element (CRE) and modulate intracellular signals like cyclic AMP and BDNF26,29. BDNF/TrkB signaling activates PLC-γ and calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase, and then, by phosphorylating CREB (p-CREB) upregulates the expression level of genes involved in memory formation. Subsequently, p-CREB increases BDNF gene transcription and induces a positive internal loop, which BDNF further activates TrkB. Ultimately, these cascades mediate synaptic plasticity and memory process during REM sleep30,31. Evidence from rodent models has shown that disruption of both BDNF and CREB expression in different brain regions leads to impaired cognitive functions and the mood state6,32–35. However, new approaches encourage using plant-derived adjuvant treatments with significant neuroprotective properties such as crocin20,36–38, curcumin39–41, naringenin42,43, and imperatorin (IMP, (9-[(3-methyl-2-buten1-yl) oxy]−7 H-furo[3,2-g] chromen-7-one)44–46.

IMP is a metabolite that can cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and CNS. IMP is extracted from the fruits of Angelica dahurica and Peucedanum praeruptorum, with the potential pro-cognitive and neuroprotective effects47–49. It has various pharmacological activities such as pro-cognitive, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anticonvulsant, and anxiolytic effects45,46,50,51. Additionally, IMP upregulates the function of GABA-A receptor (related to anxiolytic effects) and modulates the BDNF/CREB pathway (related to pro-cognitive effects), all of which are involved in modulating synaptic plasticity, the mood state, and memory formation46,52. Moreover, many studies have shown that IMP increases the expression of neurotrophic factors such as BDNF and TrkB and increases the phosphorylation of downstream effectors such as CREB46,53. These findings support that IMP not only promotes neuronal survival and synaptic strengthening, and restores cognitive functions including learning and memory, but also alleviates behavioral disturbances caused by REM SD45. However, there is no evidence on the potential pro-cognitive and neuroprotective effects of IMP in sleep deprived rats.

According to mentioned findings, the present study aimed to investigate the efficacy of IMP in behavioral and memory impairments induced by different protocols of REM SD in male rats, with a focus on BDNF and CREB expression levels.

Materials and methods

Animals

Forty-eight adolescent male rats (150–160 g, 5–6 weeks old) were allocated for the present study. The rats were housed six per big Plexiglas cage (50 × 50 × 30), and a 12 h:12 h light/dark cycle (lights on at 7:00 h) and stable temperature (22 ± 1 °C) were provided. Each experimental group had six male rats. All the rats were born and bred in Cognitive Neuroscience Lab, Medicinal Plants Research Center, Institute of Medicinal Plants, ACECR, Karaj, Iran, and had free access to food and water. All the tests were performed during the light hours (9:00 a.m. to 3:00 p.m.). To minimize biological and experimental variability, animals were age- and weight-matched, maintained under standardized housing conditions, randomly assigned to experimental groups, and behavioral/molecular assessments were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation. Our experimental protocol was designed in accord with National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals54 and with Medicinal Plants Research Center, Institute of Medicinal Plants, ACECR guidelines. All experimental protocols were approved by Research Ethics Committees of Laboratory Animals - Islamic Azad University, Iran (Approval ID: IR.IAU.AEC.1404.022). All methods are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines.

Preparing Imperatorin (IMP)

Imperatorin (IMP) was prepared at the Institute of Medicinal Plants (ACECR, Karaj, Iran). IMP was administered orally (gavage) at 100 mg/kg. The selected dose of IMP (100 mg/kg) was based on previous studies demonstrating neuroprotective and pro-cognitive efficacy without toxicity50,55. The IMP dose (100 mg/kg, p.o.) was selected based on prior preclinical studies reporting central nervous system-relevant behavioral and molecular effects of imperatorin in rodent models, with acceptable tolerability at oral doses in this range. Additionally, imperatorin has been reported to mitigate experimentally induced cognitive impairment and to modulate neuroinflammation-associated mechanisms, supporting its evaluation against REM SD–related behavioral and recognition-memory deficits45.

IMP used in this study was prepared at the Institute of Medicinal Plants (ACECR, Karaj, Iran) from cultivated Angelica dahurica. Imperatorin (IMP) used in the present study was isolated from cultivated Angelica dahurica at the Institute of Medicinal Plants, Academic Center for Education, Culture and Research (ACECR, Karaj, Iran), following standard phytochemical purification procedures as previously described. The identity and purity of IMP were confirmed by a combination of gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. GC–MS analysis showed a dominant chromatographic peak at a retention time of approximately 2.39 min. The mass spectrum exhibited a prominent fragment ion at m/z 202, consistent with the characteristic fragmentation pattern of imperatorin, while compound identity was further supported by library matching and confirmed by ^1H-NMR and ^13 C-NMR analyses. Library search analysis confirmed the compound as 7 H-Furo[3,2-g][1]benzopyran-7-one, 9-[(3-methyl-2-butenyl)oxy] (C₁₆H₁₄O₄; CAS No. 482-44-0). Structural integrity was further verified by 1H-NMR and13 C-NMR spectroscopy, showing spectra fully consistent with reference data and no detectable extraneous signals. Based on chromatographic and spectroscopic homogeneity, the purity of IMP was estimated to be > 95%, which is appropriate for in vivo pharmacological studies. The GC–MS chromatogram, EI mass spectrum, and ^1H/^13 C-NMR spectra are provided in the Supplementary Information. No wild plant collection was performed for this study; therefore, no collection permits or licenses were required56. Formal identification of the plant material was undertaken within the Institute. The plant material was identified at the Institute of Medicinal Plants (ACECR, Karaj, Iran).

REM SD

Rapid eye movement sleep deprivation (REM SD) was induced using the modified multiple-platform (flowerpot) method (Tajhiz-Gostar Omid Iranian Co., Tehran, Iran). This paradigm is widely used for selective REM sleep deprivation in rodents because REM-related postural muscle atonia leads to loss of balance and contact with water, thereby interrupting REM sleep57–59. This apparatus included a black water tank (90 × 50 × 50 cm) with several circular platforms with a diameter of 7 cm. The tank was filled with water (25–26 °C), while the water was 1 cm below all the platforms. Rats were placed on the small platforms positioned above water. The water level was maintained approximately 1 cm below the upper surface of the platforms, allowing limited movement between platforms when necessary. During REM sleep, loss of postural muscle tone causes the animals to contact the water and awaken, thereby interrupting REM sleep57–59. Although animals had to maintain postural balance on small platforms and occasionally contacted water during REM onset, the water level was adjusted to 1 cm below the platform surface to prevent continuous swimming and excessive physical fatigue. Moreover, Water temperature was maintained at 25–26 °C throughout the deprivation period. Previous studies have demonstrated that this protocol does not induce significant long-term metabolic exhaustion when applied for 24 h59,60. When the rats fell asleep and the REM phase started, the rats fell into the water and woke up (due to REM-induced muscle relaxation). All REM SD procedures were conducted in a temperature-controlled room (22 ± 1 °C) consistent with the housing conditions. Animals were monitored continuously during the REM SD period. This process deprived the rats of the REM phase. In sham-REM SD groups, the rats were placed on the platforms with a larger diameter (15 cm) where they had a normal sleep23,61–64. As previously quantified, the modified multiple-platform method effectively suppresses REM sleep, although some reduction in slow-wave sleep may also occur; therefore, this limitation is acknowledged in the interpretation of the findings59. REM SD was done for 24 h. REM SD (and sham-REM SD) was done before or after the learning phase of the novel object recognition memory test.

Experimental timeline

Adolescent rats underwent 24 h REM sleep deprivation (REM SD) or sham-REM SD. Immediately after sleep manipulation, animals received saline or imperatorin either before (pre-train) or after (post-train) the habituation and familiarization phases of the NOR task. Memory retention was assessed 24 h later using the NOR test, followed by the open field test (OFT)65 1 h later and a single-session forced swim test (FST) 1 h thereafter as described in the sections “Novel object recognition (NOR) test”–“RT-qPCR”. The experimental design is illustrated in Supplementary Figure S3.

Novel object recognition (NOR) test

Novel object recognition (NOR) is a recognition-memory task in rodents; with a 24-h retention interval, it is commonly used to assess long-term recognition memory. The NOR procedure followed the classical one-trial object-recognition paradigm originally described for rats and later adapted for long-term recognition memory assessment in rodents65. NOR test included two sessions. In session one (habituation phase) which lasted ten minutes, the rat was habituated to an open-field Plexiglas arena (45 cm × 45 cm × 45 cm), with no objects throughout the arena. One hour later, the session two (familiarization phase) was done which lasted ten minutes. In this phase, each rat was put in the arena which had two identical objects that were placed in opposite corners. In session three (test session, 24 h after the session two), the memory test was performed. During the test session, one familiar (F) object was replaced by a novel object (N) that was different in shape and color, and the rat was free to explore the arena for ten minutes. Both objects were cleaned with 1% acetic acid between each test for each rat to ensure the absence of any olfactory cues22. Note that, most studies use a one-day interval between the familiarization phase and the habituation phase. However, the present study used a 1-h interval based on our previous studies12,22. The difference in the exploration time of N and F objects was calculated using the discrimination index (DI) as: (time N − time F)/(time N + time F)66.

Open field test

The open field test (OFT) (Tajhiz-Gostar Omid Iranian Co, Tehran, Iran) was performed according to classical open-field methodology as originally described by Hall and later critically reviewed and standardized for behavioral interpretation by Walsh and Cummins67,68. The OFT was used to assess spontaneous locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior. The apparatus consisted of a clear Perspex arena (30 × 30 × 40 cm) with the floor divided into 16 equal squares67,68. Locomotor activity was the number of crossings from one square to another during 5 min. The time spent in the four middle squares indicated anxiety-like behavior, the more time spent, the less anxiety-like behavior69,70. This test was done 1 h after the NOR test.

Forced swim test

The forced swim test (FST) was performed according to the rat behavioral-despair paradigm originally described by Porsolt et al. 71. In the present study, a single-session variant of the test was used, and immobility and climbing were quantified during a 5-min test period. This protocol was a single-session forced swim test (FST) (i.e., without a 24-hour pre-swim habituation/training session). FST included a cylindrical transparent container (50 cm height, 20 cm diameter) that was filled with water (20–22 °C) up to 2/3 of it. Each rat was placed individually in the cylinder71. During 5 min, the duration of immobility and climbing was measured. We measured immobility (depressive-like behavior) and purposeful mobility (climbing) in this test72, and we measured climbing but not swimming because climbing is a purposeful swimming that is done in order to get out of the cylinder. However, many studies only measure immobility, as a depressive-like behavior73–76, or immobility and swimming without climbing77. The duration of immobility was considered as depressive-like behavior. This test was done 2 h after the OFT.

RT-qPCR

Immediately after the FST, the rats (n = 3 in each group) were decapitated. Prior to decapitation, animals were deeply anesthetized with ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg, i.p.) to ensure complete loss of reflexes and absence of pain perception. Decapitation was then performed as a secondary physical method, followed immediately by pithing to ensure rapid destruction of brainstem activity and confirmation of death, in accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 revised edition). Brain tissues were rapidly dissected on ice for molecular analyses, and remaining carcasses were disposed of according to institutional biosafety and biomedical waste management regulations78. The prefrontal cortex of each rat was isolated on ice, and snap-frozen into liquid nitrogen before disposal at − 80 °C until further processing. The prefrontal cortex was selected because prefrontal subregions are implicated in object recognition memory consolidation/retrieval control and are sensitive to sleep-loss–related disruption of mnemonic control processes4,79,80. Then, total RNA was extracted from 100 mg of the prefrontal cortex by Qiazol (Qiazol lysis reagent, USA) in a sterilized RNase-free tube. NanoDrop ND-100 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) evaluated the concentration and purity of RNA by the ratio of the absorbance at 260 nm and 280 nm (A 260/A 280). RNA was converted into complementary DNA (cDNA) by DNase I first strand synthesis system for RT-PCR (Fermentase, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Real-time PCR reactions were done using Takara SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (2X conc.) in a final volume of 20 µl on StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). qPCR experiments were conducted and reported in accordance with MIQE guidelines to ensure transparency and reproducibility81. For qPCR, 2 µL of synthesized cDNA template (used directly, without dilution) was added to each reaction (final reaction volume: 20 µL), consistent with transparent qPCR reporting practices82. The annealing temperature optimized for primers pairs was 64 °C. For quantification of target gene, standard curve method was applied. All the samples were loaded in duplicate and the mean data were used for further analysis. The specificity of PCR products was verified by observing a single peak in melting curve analysis38,83. Representative amplification plots and single-peak melting curves for the analyzed genes are provided in the Supplementary Information. The target genes assessed by real-time PCR were BDNF and CREB, and GAPDH was used as the reference gene to normalize target gene expression; primer sequences are provided below.

[GAPDH: F 5’ GGAGTCTACTGGCGTCTTCAC 3’, R 5’ ATGAGCCCTTCCACGATGC 3’;

BDNF: F 5’ TGGATGAGGACCAGAAGG 3’, R 5’ AGAAAGAGCAGAGGAGGC 3’;

CREB: F 5’ CAGGGAGGAGCAATACAG 3’, R 5’ GCACTAGAATCTGCTGTCC 3’].

Experimental groups

The study consisted of eight groups (n = 6):

Sample sizes for behavioral experiments were selected based on established practices in rodent models of stress and sleep deprivation, where group sizes of 5–8 animals are commonly used to achieve reliable behavioral phenotyping6,84,85. Molecular analyses were conducted on biologically independent samples obtained from the same experimental cohorts. In addition to conventional statistical testing, standardized effect sizes (Cohen’s d and Hedges’ g) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated to provide a quantitative estimate of the magnitude of gene expression changes, independent of sample size.

Group 1- Sham-REM SD/saline pre-train group: The adolescent rats underwent sham-REM SD for 24 h and received saline (0.5 mL/rat, p.o.) immediately after sham-REM SD, before the learning session.

Group 2- Sham-REM SD/saline post-train group: The adolescent rats only received saline (0.5 mL/rat, p.o.) immediately after sham-REM SD, after the learning session.

Group 3- Sham-REM SD/IMP pre-train group: The adolescent rats underwent sham-REM SD for 24 h and received IMP (100 mg/kg, p.o.) immediately after sham-REM SD, before the learning session.

Group 4- Sham-REM SD/IMP post-train group: The adolescent rats underwent sham-REM SD for 24 h and received IMP (100 mg/kg, p.o.) immediately after sham-REM SD, after the learning session.

Group 5- REM SD/saline pre-train group: The adolescent rats underwent REM SD for 24 h and received saline (0.5 mL/rat, p.o.) immediately after REM SD, before the learning session.

Group 6- REM SD/saline post-train group: The adolescent rats underwent REM SD for 24 h and received saline (0.5 mL/rat, p.o.) immediately after REM SD, after the learning session.

Group 7- REM SD/IMP pre-train group: The adolescent rats underwent REM SD for 24 h and received IMP (100 mg/kg, p.o.) immediately after REM SD, before the learning session.

Group 8- REM SD/IMP post-train group: The adolescent rats underwent REM SD for 24 h and received IMP (100 mg/kg, p.o.) immediately after REM SD, after the learning session.

Pre-train and post-train groups were designed as independent experimental cohorts because REM SD was applied at distinct temporal windows relative to memory encoding. Using the same animals for both paradigms would introduce carryover effects and confound interpretation of timing-specific REM SD impacts on acquisition versus consolidation processes.

Statistical analyses

For analyzing data, we used SPSS software (V.26). Also, for evaluating statistical differences between groups, all three variables (REM SD,

imperatorin, pre/post) were analyzed using three-way ANOVA. Post-hoc Tukey test was also used to assess significant differences between groups. Data were expressed as mean ± S.D. The significant statistical level was also as P < 0.05. A three-way factorial ANOVA was used because the design included three fixed factors: REM SD condition (sham vs. REM SD), treatment (saline vs. IMP), and timing (pre-train vs. post-train). This approach allows estimation of main effects and interaction terms (e.g., REM SD × treatment, REM SD × timing, and REM SD × treatment × timing), which is necessary to determine whether IMP effects differ as a function of deprivation timing relative to learning. Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons where appropriate, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To complement p-value based analyses, effect sizes (Cohen’s d) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated for the main behavioral and molecular comparisons, and achieved post-hoc statistical power was estimated based on the observed effects.

Results

NOR memory

The effects of all three variables were measured using three-way ANOVA that showed: the effect of REM SD (F1,40 = 105.48, P < 0.001), imperatorin (F1,40 = 41.82, P < 0.001), pre or post (F1,40 = 48.63, P < 0.001), REM SD*imperatorin (F1,40 = 32.56, P < 0.001), REM SD*pre or post (F1,40 = 52.23, P < 0.001), imperatorin*pre or post (F1,40 = 3.61, P > 0.05), and REM*imperatorin*pre or post (F1,40 = 1.30, P > 0.05). Post hoc Tukey showed that pre-train REM SD impaired NOR memory performance (P < 0.001), while post-train REM SD showed a more significant impairment effect on NOR memory performance compared with pre-train REM SD (P < 0.001). Imperatorin restored NOR memory deficit in pre-train REM SD rats, while attenuated it in post-train REM SD rats (P < 0.001, compared with both post-train REM SD and respective sham-REM SD) (Fig. 1). Overall, REM SD impaired long-term recognition memory in NOR, whereas IMP improved discrimination performance in both pre- and post-train REM SD paradigms.

Locomotor activity

The effects of all three variables were measured using three-way ANOVA that showed: the effect of REM SD (F1,40 = 75.82, P < 0.001), imperatorin (F1,40 = 38.03, P < 0.001), pre or post (F1,40 = 25.51, P < 0.001), REM SD*imperatorin (F1,40 = 22.48, P < 0.001), REM SD*pre or post (F1,40 = 27.43, P < 0.001), imperatorin*pre or post (F1,40 = 10.19, P < 0.01), and REM*imperatorin*pre or post (F1,40 = 10.59, P < 0.01). Post hoc Tukey showed that pre-train REM SD slightly decreased locomotor activity (P < 0.01), while post-train REM SD showed a more significant effect on locomotor activity compared with pre-train REM SD (P < 0.001). Imperatorin in both REM SD rats restored locomotor activity (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2). In summary, REM SD reduced locomotor activity, and IMP partially normalized locomotion depending on the timing condition.

Anxiety-like behavior

Post hoc Tukey analysis showed that REM SD reduced center time in both pre-train and post-train saline-treated rats, indicating increased anxiety-like behavior (P < 0.001). This effect was more severe in the post-train REM SD group. Imperatorin restored time spent in the middle squares in both REM SD groups (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

BDNF and CREB expression levels in the prefrontal cortex

Discussion

The present study investigated the potential therapeutic role of imperatorin on behavioral, molecular, and memory functions. We showed that REM SD impaired NOR memory, reduced locomotor activity, increased anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors (reduced climbing and increased immobility), with more severe effects of the post-train REM SD. Although the sequence of behavioral testing may influence anxiety-related measures, the novel object recognition task in the present study was preceded by a habituation session designed to minimize novelty-induced stress. Therefore, NOR was not considered a high-stress task in this protocol. Nevertheless, the potential influence of task order on anxiety-like behaviors cannot be completely excluded and should be considered when interpreting open field outcomes.

Both pre- and post-train REM SD reduced BDNF and CREB expression in the prefrontal cortex. Regarding brain-region selection, we focused on the prefrontal cortex because converging evidence supports a critical contribution of medial/ventromedial prefrontal circuitry to consolidation and control of recognition memory, including object recognition paradigms4,79. Moreover, sleep deprivation can disrupt prefrontal mechanisms involved in memory control, providing a mechanistic basis for assessing cortical plasticity markers (BDNF/CREB) in this region under REM SD conditions80. REM sleep loss robustly affects prefrontal-dependent executive and affective regulation, and prefrontal BDNF/CREB signaling represents a key molecular substrate of activity-dependent synaptic plasticity and stress-cognition coupling86–89. Although hippocampal and amygdala circuits are critically involved in learning and emotional memory, the prefrontal cortex is particularly vulnerable to REM sleep disruption and plays an essential role in top-down control of recognition memory. The observed normalization of BDNF and CREB mRNA expression in pre-train REM SD animals suggests that imperatorin may influence transcriptional processes related to prefrontal plasticity when administered proximal to memory encoding; however, in the absence of protein-level validation, this interpretation should be considered preliminary90. Nevertheless, given the established contribution of the hippocampus and amygdala to recognition and stress-related memory, fmolecular analyses were limited to mRNA expression2,91. expression and were not complemented by protein-level measurements. Therefore, the observed changes in BDNF and CREB transcripts should not be interpreted as direct evidence of functional activation of BDNF/TrkB/CREB signaling in the prefrontal cortex. In particular, the biological effects of BDNF depend primarily on mature BDNF protein availability and TrkB receptor engagement, which cannot be reliably inferred from BDNF mRNA expression alone. Likewise, CREB function is regulated mainly at the post-translational level, especially through phosphorylation of CREB, and thus altered CREB mRNA levels do not by themselves demonstrate CREB activation. Accordingly, our molecular findings should be interpreted as transcription-level associations that may be related to the behavioral effects of imperatorin, rather than definitive evidence of the underlying functional signaling mechanism. Future studies should incorporate protein-level assays such as mature BDNF, TrkB, total CREB, and phosphorylated CREB measurements to determine whether the transcriptional changes observed here are translated into biologically functional signaling events.

Measurement of circulating corticosterone would have further helped distinguish sleep-loss effects from nonspecific stress responses; accordingly, this issue is now discussed as a study limitation rather than a resolved mechanism45,92,93. Also, IMP could restore BDNF/CREB signaling pathway in the pre-train REM SD condition, and reduced the behavioral impairments caused by pre- or post-train REM SD. Based on our results, IMP showed restorative effects on behavioral functions in both pre- and post-train REM SD groups.

However, increased BDNF/CREB expression levels induced by IMP were only observed in the pre-train REM SD rats. Importantly, IMP restored BDNF and increased (but not restored) CREB expression levels in the pre-train REM SD rats.

We intentionally used adolescent rats because adolescence represents a neurodevelopmental stage characterized by ongoing maturation of prefrontal–limbic circuitry and heightened vulnerability to sleep loss–related cognitive and affective dysregulation. Therefore, evaluating imperatorin in this age window may provide translationally relevant insight into sleep-deprivation susceptibility during adolescence, while future studies should directly compare adolescent versus adult cohorts94. The present study highlighted the therapeutic potential of IMP in the alleviation of behavioral dysfunctions induced by REM SD, although BDNF and CREB upregulation may not be the main underlying mechanism.

Evidence has shown the vital role of REM sleep in the modulation of cognitive processes including memory consolidation. It has been revealed that the physiology of REM sleep is tightly related to normal memory function in the brain95. REM phase of sleep has a vital role in mediating hippocampal neural differentiation96. It has been reported that offline learning during the REM phase of sleep seems to be important for shaping how memory representations relate to one another, either by pushing memories closer together or pulling them apart96. REM sleep is also critically involved in processing emotional memories97. Previous research has noted that increased REM sleep during nighttime sleep predicts a better performance in later procedural tasks in humans98. It has also been declared that REM sleep restores prefrontal memory control80. Also, rats after sleep with enhanced REM phase show a better memory retention and behavioral performance in memory-related tests99. Therefore, any disruption in the REM phase may lead to cognitive and memory dysfunctions. Our findings are related to previous studies that highlighted the critical role of REM SD in the impairment of cognitive and emotional behaviors, as well as memory performance such as novel recognition memory12,100,101. Previous studies have shown that different durations of REM SD disrupt memory consolidation and cognitive functions, and lead to mood disturbances102–105. Specifically, short-term REM SD significantly impairs the maintenance of synaptic plasticity in CA1 area of the rats via affecting the maintenance of hippocampal long-term potentiation (LTP), which has a pivotal role in mediating learning and memory106. Additionally, REM SD disrupts contextual fear memory consolidation and reduces CA3–CA1 LTP, supporting the hypothesis of a close relationship between REM SD and memory formation107. It has also been shown that 48 h REM SD potently impairs NOR memory in male rats via affecting synaptophysin and glycogen synthase kinase-3 beta (GSK-3beta) expression levels in the prefrontal cortex12. A recent study has also shown that REM SD can restore PTSD-related memory (freezing behavior) as much as extinction train in male rats108. Other study has shown that REM SD via decreasing BDNF mRNA expression levels leads to impaired passive avoidance memory in male rats6.

Previous study has shown that pre-train REM SD impairs various phases of memory including recall and extinction in both contextual and auditory fear conditioning tasks, while pre-test REM SD prevents the extinction of the learned task in rats, highlighting that REM SD may interfere with acquisition, recall, and extinction, but not necessarily with the consolidation of emotional memory109. Notably, these impairments are often associated with increased anxiety-like behaviors and elevated locomotor activity, which shows that behavioral disturbances may be closely linked to disrupted learning and memory function110. Previous study has shown that both pre- and post-train paradoxical SD significantly lead to memory impairments in mice, emphasizing the important role of the time interval between the training and the test111. It has also been reported that memory refinement is primarily related to REM sleep, given evidence of bidirectional synaptic plasticity in the REM phase and the results of selective REM SD studies7. Paradoxical REM SD also leads to a reversible decrease in hippocampal LTP, extracellular signal-regulated kinases/mitogen-activated protein kinase (ERK/MAPK) pathway activation, and glutamate receptor protein expression levels in the dorsal hippocampus, suggesting the potential cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying the impairment effect of paradoxical REM SD on certain hippocampal-dependent forms of memory consolidation112.

In fact, studies on synaptic plasticity following REM SD protocols have shown deficits in memory consolidation, particularly within spatial and emotional learning. These findings support the notion that REM SD impairs memory partially via reducing the expression of two keys plasticity-related proteins in the brain, including BDNF and CREB113,114. Interestingly, anxiety responses to REM SD appear to vary depending on the timing of deprivation. In some cases, anxiety increased during post-train REM SD, while in others, it may decrease. These fluctuations in anxiety are closely linked to memory performance, emphasizing the dynamic interplay between sleep and cognitive process such as attention and emotional regulation.

On the molecular level, evidence has indicated that REM SD suppresses critical pathways involved in synaptic plasticity. For instance, REM SD reduces the phosphorylation of cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), CREB, and MAPK in the hippocampus, thereby impairing molecular pathways involved in memory function115. Chronic REM SD (14 days) in female rats also induces manic- and OCD-like behaviors and impairs passive avoidance memory, via reduction in hippocampal BDNF expression levels35. Moreover, another study has highlighted sex-dependent changes in BDNF expression levels following REM SD, showing that males have greater behavioral disruptions compared to females108. Additionally, extended REM SD (8–48 h) also downregulates the expression of some key genes and proteins (which related to synaptic plasticity) such as BDNF, synapsin I, CAMKII, and CREB, specifically in the CA1 area of the hippocampus, which are vital for long-term memory formation116. In support of these findings, two studies have shown that REM SD in mice reduces protein kinase A (PKA) activation and CREB phosphorylation in the brain, leading to the impairment of recognition memory consolidation117. A recent study has also shown that chronic REM SD downregulates BDNF, synaptophysin, and CREB mRNA expression levels, leading to the induction of cognitive deficits118. The present study also showed that both pre- and post-train REM SD decreased both BDNF and CREB mRNA expression levels.

As previously mentioned, IMP shows a potential pro-cognitive and neuroprotective role47–49. IMP also exhibits neuroprotective effects through suppression of pro-inflammatory markers and reduction of oxidative stress and inflammation in the hippocampus51,119. Previous studies have demonstrated that treatment with IMP can restore both behavioral dysfunctions and the underlying molecular changes. It has been noted that IMP can counteract scopolamine-induced memory deficits and induce antioxidant effects in a mouse model of scopolamine-induced memory deficit48. Additionally, studies have shown the neuroprotective effects of IMP in a mouse model of lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced memory deficits via increasing BDNF mRNA expression levels and attenuating neuroinflammation45,120. IMP also exerts neuroprotective effects on cerebral ischemia both in vitro and in vivo, while these effects are associated with the anti-apoptotic properties of IMP53. Furthermore, IMP can ameliorate learning and memory impairments in prenatally-stressed female offspring rats via upregulating BDNF/TrkB and ERK/CaMKIIα/CREB signaling pathways46. A previous study has also reported that IMP attenuates depressive-like behaviors in prenatally stressed offspring rats via improvement of serotonergic signaling in the hippocampus and the frontal cortex121. However, there is no evidence on the potential therapeutic effect of IMP on behavioral and molecular changes induced by REM SD.

For the first time, the present study showed that imperatorin attenuated several REM SD-induced behavioral abnormalities and was associated with changes in BDNF and CREB mRNA expression in the prefrontal cortex. However, because these molecular data were limited to transcript levels, the study does not establish that functional BDNF/TrkB/CREB signaling is the primary mechanism underlying the behavioral effects of imperatorin, particularly given that behavioral improvement was also observed in conditions where molecular rescue was less evident.

Although imperatorin is widely reported to modulate oxidative stress and neuroinflammatory pathways, these mechanisms were not directly assessed in the present study. Previous studies have shown that imperatorin can reduce oxidative stress markers and pro-inflammatory cytokines while improving BDNF-related outcomes in experimental models of cognitive impairment45,92,93. Therefore, the behavioral improvements observed here are compatible with, but do not directly confirm, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, only one dose of imperatorin was examined, precluding dose–response interpretation.

Second, molecular analyses were limited to mRNA expression in the prefrontal cortex, and no protein-level validation was performed. This is a major limitation because transcript abundance alone is insufficient to establish functional BDNF/TrkB/CREB signaling. Mature BDNF protein, TrkB receptor engagement, total CREB, and especially phosphorylated CREB should be assessed in future studies to determine whether the present transcriptional changes correspond to biologically active synaptic plasticity mechanisms.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study showed that REM SD impaired recognition-memory performance and altered behavioral outcomes in male rats, whereas imperatorin attenuated several of these abnormalities. The molecular findings were limited to BDNF and CREB mRNA expression changes in the prefrontal cortex, with more evident effects in the pre-train REM SD condition. These transcriptional findings may be associated with the behavioral effects of imperatorin, but they are not sufficient to demonstrate functional BDNF/TrkB/CREB signaling. Future studies should incorporate dose–response evaluation, protein-level analyses of mature BDNF, TrkB, total CREB, and phosphorylated CREB, hippocampal assessment, oxidative/inflammatory biomarkers, corticosterone measurement, and additional cognitive paradigms to strengthen mechanistic interpretation.
