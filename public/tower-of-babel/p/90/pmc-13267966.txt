Intravenous Atropine in Reducing Reperfusion Arrhythmias, Conduction Abnormalities, and Hypotension in Inferior ST-elevation Myocardial Infarction Undergoing Primary Percutaneous Coronary Intervention
Authors: Mohamed I. Rashed, Islam M. Bastawy, Ahmed L. Mohamed, Mohamed G. Elewa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesInferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction is frequently complicated by vagally mediated bradyarrhythmias and hypotension during reperfusion, contributing to increased morbidity. The Bezold–Jarisch reflex has been implicated in this hemodynamic instability; however, evidence supporting prophylactic atropine remains limited. The study evaluated whether intravenous atropine administered immediately before reperfusion reduces clinically meaningful bradyarrhythmias, hypotension, early sustained ventricular arrhythmias, and inpatient major adverse cardiovascular events in patients undergoing primary percutaneous coronary intervention.MethodsIn this prospective single-center, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, adults presenting within 12 h of symptom onset with inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction were randomized 1:1 to intravenous atropine (1 mg) or placebo immediately before guidewire crossing or balloon inflation. Of 158 patients randomized, 150 patients were included in a prespecified modified intention-to-treat analysis, excluding 8 patients with angiographic no-reflow. The primary endpoint was a composite of hypotension and symptomatic bradycardia following reperfusion. Secondary endpoints included the individual components of the primary endpoint, sustained ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation, and inpatient major adverse cardiovascular events.ResultsThe primary endpoint occurred in 17.3% of atropine-treated patients and 49.3% of placebo-treated patients (relative risk 0.35, 95% CI 0.20–0.61), corresponding to an absolute risk reduction of 32.0% (95% CI 17.81–46.19) and a number needed to treat of 3 (p < 0.001). Atropine significantly reduced hypotension and symptomatic bradycardia. Sustained ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation occurred in 2.7% vs 13.3% of patients. No serious atropine-related adverse events were observed.ConclusionsProphylactic atropine significantly reduces hypotension and bradyarrhythmias and may also reduce early ventricular arrhythmias and inpatient major adverse cardiovascular events in this population.Clinical trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT07268586, retrospectively registered December 2025.

Introduction

Inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), most commonly resulting from occlusion of the right coronary artery (RCA), is frequently complicated by bradyarrhythmias, hypotension, and atrioventricular (AV) conduction disturbances. These conduction abnormalities occur more often in inferior than anterior STEMI, reflecting the involvement of AV nodal tissue and autonomic influences on cardiac conduction [1].

Reperfusion of the inferoposterior myocardium can trigger reflex autonomic responses. The Bezold–Jarisch reflex, a vagally mediated cardioinhibitory reflex characterized by hypotension, bradycardia, and vasodilation, has been described in the setting of myocardial reperfusion, particularly after restoration of coronary flow in inferior infarctions, thereby contributing to reperfusion-associated hemodynamic instability [2].

Intravenous atropine is currently the first-line treatment for symptomatic bradycardia or hypotension that develops during an acute myocardial infarction. Temporary pacemaker insertion should be considered for patients with high-degree AV block or for patients whose symptoms do not improve despite receiving atropine [3]. There have been no randomized trials evaluating the efficacy of pre-reperfusion intravenous atropine for preventing bradyarrhythmias and hypotension in inferior STEMI patients undergoing primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI); available evidence is retrospective and observational [4]. As a result, its efficacy remains unknown, despite the frequency and potential severity of these events.

To address this gap, we conducted a prospective, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial evaluating whether prophylactic intravenous atropine administered immediately before reperfusion reduces the incidence of clinically significant bradyarrhythmias, hypotension, and early reperfusion ventricular arrhythmias in the inferior STEMI population undergoing primary PCI.

Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and oversight

This prospective, single-center, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study was conducted between August 2025 and February 2026. The study protocol was evaluated and approved by the local research ethics committee (FMASU R49/2025), in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. All patients provided written informed consent. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07268586) in December 2025, after study initiation but before completion of recruitment and prior to data analysis, due to administrative delays. All study procedures were conducted according to the pre-specified protocol. A CONSORT flow diagram of patient enrollment, randomization, follow-up, and analysis is provided in Fig. 1.

2.2. Intervention and control

Intravenous atropine at a dose of 1 mg was given to patients who were randomly assigned to the intervention group just before reperfusion, which is defined as balloon inflation or restoration of coronary flow. Intravenous normal saline was administrated to patients in the control group as a placebo at the same time and in the same volume relative to the procedure. To preserve blinding of patients, treating physicians, and outcome assessors, study drugs were produced in identical syringes. The placebo group did not receive prophylactic atropine; however, as is standard during PCI, rescue atropine administration was permitted if clinically needed for symptomatic bradycardia or hypotension. The preservation of blinding and treatment allocation was unaffected by such rescue treatment.

2.3. Patient population

Adults ≥18 years presenting within 12 h of symptom onset with inferior STEMI (≥1 mm ST-segment elevation in II, III, and aVF with reciprocal ST depression in I and aVL and angiographic confirmation of the culprit lesion) were eligible [5].

2.4. Exclusion criteria (pre-randomization)

Patients were excluded if they underwent rescue PCI after fibrinolytic therapy; had mechanical complications; demonstrated spontaneous reperfusion (Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) flow grade 3); had prior permanent or temporary pacemaker implantation; had a documented history of bradycardia (<50 beats per minute), hypotension (systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg), or ventricular tachycardia with a definitive cause unrelated to the disease under study; had previously undergone coronary artery bypass graft surgery; presented with cardiogenic shock or pulmonary edema consistent with Killip class III–IV; had known contraindications to atropine; or declined or were unable to provide informed consent.

2.5. Post-randomization handling

Patients who experienced a no-reflow phenomenon post-randomization were excluded. No-reflow was defined as a final TIMI flow grade ≤2 after revascularization. These patients were excluded due to the distinct pathophysiological mechanisms of no-reflow that may confound the assessment of vagally mediated reperfusion reactions.

2.6. Randomization and blinding

Patients were randomized 1:1 to receive either intravenous atropine 1 mg or a placebo using a computer-generated sequence. Allocation was concealed using a sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by an investigator not involved in patient recruitment. Patients, clinicians, and outcome adjudicators were blinded.

2.7. Endpoints

2.7.1. Primary endpoint

The primary composite endpoint consisted of clinically significant vagally mediated reperfusion reactions following angiographically successful reperfusion, defined as either hypotension (systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg, or requiring pharmacologic or fluid support) and symptomatic bradycardia, characterized by a heart rate under 50 bpm with associated symptoms necessitating intervention. This composite was selected to capture clinically meaningful hemodynamic instability attributable to acute parasympathetic activation during reperfusion.

2.7.2. Secondary endpoints

Secondary endpoints included the individual components of the primary endpoint, as well as the incidence of sustained ventricular tachycardia (VT) or ventricular fibrillation (VF) requiring immediate intervention after reperfusion [6] and inpatient major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), defined as a composite of acute heart failure, cardiovascular mortality, non-fatal stroke, or recurrent myocardial infarction [7]. Secondary endpoints were supportive and exploratory.

2.7.3. Safety endpoints

Safety endpoints consisted of atropine-related side effects during hospitalization until discharge, including sinus tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, nausea and/or vomiting, urinary retention, flushing, dry mouth, and blurred vision [4]. Serious adverse events, including anticholinergic toxicity, were monitored prospectively.

2.8. Follow-up and outcome assessment

All endpoints were prospectively defined, continuously monitored from the time of the index PCI until discharge from hospital, and evaluated by a blinded clinical events clinicians unaware of treatment allocation. The primary endpoint was assessed during the periprocedural period, defined as the time from flow restoration and reperfusion through the immediate post-procedural phase. Secondary arrhythmic outcomes, in-hospital MACE and safety outcomes were assessed from the time of the procedure until hospital discharge. No post-discharge or long-term follow-up outcomes were assessed in this study.

2.9. Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated assuming a reduction in hypotension from 40% in the placebo group to 18% in the atropine group. Hypotension was expected to represent the most frequent component of the composite primary endpoint, based on conservative estimates derived from prior observational data [4]. With a two-sided α level of 0.05 and 90% power, a minimum of 69 patients per group was required.

2.10. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate, and were compared using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as counts (percentages) and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Patients who developed angiographic no-reflow following stent deployment were excluded from the primary analysis, which was carried out in a predetermined modified intention-to-treat population. To assess the robustness of the results, a full intention-to-treat analysis with all randomized patients was carried out as a predetermined sensitivity analysis. Effect estimates are presented as relative risks (RR) with 95% confidence intervals, along with absolute risk reduction (ARR) and number needed to treat (NNT). A prespecified multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to evaluate the independent association between atropine administration and the primary endpoint, adjusting for clinically relevant baseline variables (age, sex, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, known coronary artery disease (CAD), and culprit vessel) (RCA versus non-RCA) regardless of their univariate statistical significance. The results are shown with corresponding p-values and 95% confidence ranges, and all statistical tests were two-tailed. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value of less than 0.05.

Results

3.1. Patient flow and baseline characteristics

Out of 300 screened patients, 158 were randomized (79 per group) (Fig. 1). Eight patients experienced angiographic no-reflow after stenting and were excluded from the modified intention-to-treat population, leaving 150 patients for the primary analysis (75 per group). No patients crossed over, withdrew, or were lost to follow-up during the inhospital study period. Baseline clinical, angiographic, and procedural characteristics were well balanced between the two groups (Tables 1 and 2).

3.2. Primary endpoint

The primary composite endpoint of clinically significant reperfusion reactions (hypotension and symptomatic bradycardia) occurred in 13 patients (17.3%) in the atropine group compared with 37 patients (49.3%) in the placebo group (relative risk [RR] 0.35, 95% CI 0.20–0.61; absolute risk reduction [ARR] 32.0%, 95% CI 17.81–46.19%; number needed to treat [NNT] 3; p < 0.001). This reduction was primarily driven by lower rates of hypotension and symptomatic bradycardia (Table 3). No patients experienced both hypotension and symptomatic bradycardia simultaneously. No high-grade atrioventricular conduction abnormalities requiring intervention were observed.

In multivariable-adjusted analysis, prophylactic atropine remained independently associated with a lower risk of the primary endpoint (adjusted odds ratio 0.21; 95% CI (0.09–0.45); p < 0.001). Diabetes mellitus showed a trend toward increased risk but did not reach statistical significance after adjustment (adjusted odds ratio 2.05; 95% CI 0.96–4.37; p = 0.064) (Table 4).

3.3. Secondary endpoints

Sustained ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation occurred infrequently. In the primary analysis, 2 patients (2.7%) in the atropine group and 10 patients (13.3%) in the placebo group experienced these events (RR 0.20, 95% CI 0.05–0.88; ARR 10.67%, 95% CI 2.15–19.18%; NNT 9; p = 0.031) (Table 3). All events were documented during the periprocedural or early post-reperfusion period using continuous telemetry monitoring, either intra-procedurally or during early intensive care unit observation; no late in-hospital arrhythmic events were observed. These events were evaluated by a blinded clinical events clinicians. Given the low number of events, these findings should be considered exploratory.

3.5. Safety outcomes

Atropine was well tolerated, with no significant increase in adverse events, including sinus tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, nausea or vomiting, urinary retention, flushing, dry mouth, or blurred vision (Table 6). No serious adverse events, such as tachyarrhythmias or anticholinergic toxicity, were observed.

3.6. Sensitivity analysis

The full intention-to-treat analysis, including all randomized patients, with and without angiographic no-reflow, demonstrated consistent reductions across endpoints, confirming the robustness of the treatment effect. In this analysis, hypotension occurred in 11.4% of the atropine group versus 27.8% of the placebo group (RR 0.41, 95% CI 0.20–0.83; ARR 16.46%, 95% CI 4.34–28.57%; NNT 6; p = 0.009). Symptomatic bradycardia occurred in 10.1% versus 29.1% (RR 0.35, 95% CI 0.17–0.73; ARR of 18.99%, 95% CI 6.96–31.01%; NNT 5; p = 0.003). The composite endpoint occurred in 21.5% versus 57.0% (RR 0.38, 95% CI 0.24–0.60; ARR 35.44%, 95% CI 21.25–49.63%; NNT 3; p < 0.001). Sustained VT/VF occurred in 3.8% versus 15.2% (RR 0.25, 95% CI 0.07–0.85, ARR 11.39%, 95% CI 2.43–20.36%; NNT 9; p = 0.03) (Table 7).

Discussion

In this prospective, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, prophylactic intravenous atropine administered immediately before reperfusion in inferior STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI significantly reduced the incidence of clinically meaningful hypotension and symptomatic bradyarrhythmias, with a favorable safety profile. The magnitude of the observed effect was substantial, corresponding to an ARR of 32% and a NNT of 3. Moreover, although based on a small number of events, the observed reduction in early sustained VT and VF raises the hypothesis that modulation of vagal reflex pathways may influence electrophysiological stability during reperfusion. In addition, there was a trend toward lower inpatient MACE, primarily driven by fewer episodes of acute heart failure, suggesting a possible association between improved hemodynamic stability and early clinical outcomes, although mechanistic conclusions cannot be established from this trial.

Pathophysiological rationale

Inferior myocardial infarctions often provoke marked autonomic responses during reperfusion because of reflex activation of cardiac vagal afferents [2]. The Bezold–Jarisch reflex is a well-recognized cardioinhibitory and vasodepressor reflex characterized by bradycardia, hypotension, and vasodilation triggered by mechanoreceptors in the inferoposterior myocardium during reperfusion of ischemic tissue [2]. This phenomenon has been well described in patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) undergoing PCI, where sudden restoration of flow to the right coronary artery (RCA) territory elicits an abrupt parasympathetic surge with accompanying hypotension and bradycardia [2]. Although the Bezold-Jarisch reflex was described decades ago, it remains clinically significant. Early recognition of the role of vagal reflexes provides the mechanistic basis for targeting parasympathetic tone to prevent hemodynamic instability.

Clinical management of bradyarrhythmias and atropine use

Current clinical practice and guideline recommendations for ACS recognize atropine as the first-line therapy for symptomatic bradycardia with hemodynamic compromise (heart rate <50 bpm with hypotension), particularly in inferior MI, where vagal tone is pronounced [8]. Intravenous atropine (0.5–1.0 mg) may be repeated to achieve a therapeutic effect, and alternative chronotropic agents (e.g., epinephrine, dopamine) are recommended if bradycardia is refractory [8]. However, while atropine’s reactive use in response to symptomatic bradyarrhythmias is broadly accepted, evidence for prophylactic atropine before reperfusion has historically been limited. To our knowledge, this study represents one of the first randomized, placebo-controlled clinical evaluations of prophylactic intravenous atropine before reperfusion in inferior STEMI.

Reperfusion arrhythmias and electrophysiological considerations

Reperfusion itself is a pro-arrhythmic process. Consensus documents [9] and registry data [10] indicate that reperfusion arrhythmias such as accelerated idioventricular rhythm, non-sustained VT, and sustained VT and VF are commonly observed in the early PCI period. The majority of reperfusion arrhythmias are transient and clinically benign. However, sustained VT and VF during the reperfusion period are associated with increased short-term mortality and hemodynamic instability and often require immediate treatment [10].

Our findings suggest that a reduction in the number of sustained VT and VF events with the use of prophylactic atropine may also influence the development of arrhythmogenic triggers through a possible modulation of autonomic balance or through the reduction of the frequency of abrupt alterations in myocardial refractoriness at the time of reperfusion, although our study was not sufficiently powered to test for a primary endpoint of ventricular arrhythmias. Regardless, our observations have clinical significance and require additional mechanistic and larger-scale studies.

Inpatient MACE

Although the decrease in total inpatient MACE did not reach statistical significance (5.3% vs 14.7%; p = 0.093), the lower incidence of acute heart failure among patients indicates a clinically meaningful trend. The presence of this potential benefit suggests that maintaining hemodynamic stability post-reperfusion may reduce any early cardiac decompensation and provide a potential secondary benefit of targeted vagolytic therapy. Larger multicenter trials will be needed to establish whether prophylactic atropine consistently reduces MACE and subsequent cardiovascular outcomes in a hospital setting.

Safety and contemporary guidelines

The current ACS and arrhythmia management guidelines emphasize supportive care for bradyarrhythmias and do not recommend prophylactic antiarrhythmic therapy in patients without documented rhythm abnormalities [3,8]. In this context, the present trial shows that induced, targeted vagolytic prophylaxis is efficacious and safe, as there were no increases in arrhythmias or anticholinergic adverse events. Thus, judicious use of atropine, with risk stratification, can be safely combined with reperfusion protocols in high-risk patients for vagally mediated reactions.

Comparison with Prior Literature

In a retrospective observational study, Hou et al. reported that prophylactic intracoronary atropine (0.5–2 mg) was associated with lower rates of bradycardia, hypotension, ventricular arrhythmias, and temporary pacemaker insertion in inferior STEMI patients undergoing primary PCI, with more reductions observed in the higher-dose subgroup [4]. In contrast, our prospective, randomized, double- blind trial suggests that a fixed 1 mg intravenous dose administered immediately before guidewire crossing significantly reduced a prespecified composite of hypotension and symptomatic bradycardia, with an absolute risk reduction of 32% (NNT = 3), as well as early sustained reperfusion ventricular arrhythmias. Although direct comparisons between intracoronary and intravenous routes are not possible given pharmacokinetic differences and the absence of head-to-head testing, our findings indicate that systemic administration at a standard 1 mg dose is sufficient to achieve clinically meaningful vagal modulation during reperfusion. These data suggest that clinically meaningful vagal modulation may be achieved through systemic administration, although the relative efficacy of intracoronary versus intravenous routes remains unknown, warranting further multicenter evaluation to define optimal dosing strategies.

Limitations and future directions

The findings of this single-center study do not necessarily generalize to other clinical practice settings and populations. In addition, excluding patients treated for cardiogenic shock or advanced heart failure (Killip class III–IV) may further limit its applicability to these patients. This study did not evaluate dose–response relationships between atropine and clinical outcomes beyond the periprocedural period. Overall, additional, larger trials are needed to replicate these findings, evaluate possible long-term effects, and extend to other patient populations, including those with complex hemodynamic profiles. The use of a modified intention-to-treat analysis, although prespecified and supported by sensitivity analyses, may introduce selection bias and should be considered when interpreting the effect size. Additionally, the relatively small number of ventricular arrhythmia and MACE events limits the statistical power to detect differences in these secondary outcomes.

Conclusion

Prophylactic intravenous atropine administered immediately before reperfusion in patients with inferior STEMI undergoing primary PCI significantly reduces clinically meaningful hypotension and symptomatic bradyarrhythmias and is well tolerated. These findings support further evaluation of targeted vagolytic prophylaxis in larger multicenter randomized trials.
