Surgical relevance of a pedicled omental flap for the treatment of a recurrent giant mycotic left ventricular pseudoaneurysm: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Pan Dan, Tristan Ehrlich, Yihua Liu, Francesco Ferraro, Élodie Phamisith, Juliette Piccoli, Tiski-Dabini Boudjou, Giuseppe Lauria, Daniel Grandmougin, Nguyen Tran, Juan-Pablo Maureira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMyocardial necrosis following acute myocardial infarction may result in three major mechanical complications: papillary muscle rupture, free-wall rupture, and ventricular septal rupture (VSR), all of which are associated with devastating hemodynamic consequences and may compromise short-term survival. Although the incidence of these complications has markedly declined with the widespread adoption of early percutaneous coronary interventions, the surgical management of VSR remains highly demanding and requires substantial expertise. Moreover, the optimal timing of surgery remains controversial, with partially conflicting recommendations, as North American guidelines for STEMI advocate urgent VSR closure regardless of hemodynamic status, while European guidelines recommend early surgery only in cases of refractory cardiogenic shock or severe right ventricular dysfunction. The occurrence of a VSR followed by a delayed FWR several months later remains exceptionally rare and reflects specific myocardial rupture patterns.Case presentationWe report the case of a 55-year-old caucasian male patient with a history of multiple surgical procedures for ventricular septal rupture, followed by a delayed FWR complicated by a giant pseudoaneurysm. Three months later, the patient was urgently referred to our department for recurrence of a giant mycotic pseudoaneurysm caused by Propionibacterium acnes, which was successfully treated using a large pericardial patch reinforced with a transdiaphragmatic pedicled omental flap.ConclusionsThis case, characterized by severe and complex complications involving infectious mechanisms with technically demanding surgical management, provided an opportunity to review the literature regarding these challenging issues and to underscore the potential role of the omental flap as an effective immunological adjunct to address Propionibacterium acnes devastating infections.

Introduction

Post-infarction ventricular septal rupture (VSR) is a rare but particularly dreaded complication that occurs early after myocardial infarction [1, 2].

Along with left ventricular free-wall rupture (FWR) and papillary muscle rupture (PMR), it represents one of the three major mechanical complications of the acute phase of myocardial infarction [3].

Whereas free-wall and papillary muscle ruptures generally mandate emergent surgical repair, the optimal therapeutic strategy and timing of surgery for VSR remain controversial [4, 5].

Surgical management of these mechanical complications is associated with a high in-hospital mortality rate [6–8]. Repair of a necrotic ventricular septum associated with a left-to-right shunt remains a major technical challenge related both to the critical hemodynamic conditions of the patient and to the technical difficulties posed by the friable tissue of the infarcted area. Suturing a prosthetic patch to freshly infarcted septal myocardium requires complex surgical techniques and adherence to technical principles, among which the presence of sufficiently fibrotic septal tissue is essential to ensure durable suture anchorage.

This is achieved by debridement of necrotic septal tissue prior to patch fixation.

Numerous clinical series have shown that when surgical repair can be deferred for 2–3 weeks after the onset of VSR, both survival and repair durability are significantly improved, owing to progressive fibrotic scarring that allows reliable suture anchorage. In contrast, early repair, most often dictated by refractory hemodynamic deterioration despite optimal mechanical circulatory support with an intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) [9], Impella [10], and/or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) [11] is associated with high mortality and a substantial rate of technical failure, mainly related to patch suture dehiscence. Such failures may result in recurrence of a significant left-to-right shunt (Qp/Qs > 1) or in hemolysis caused by high-velocity leakage through a small dehiscence [12]. These recurrences frequently necessitate either complex reoperation or, when anatomically feasible, percutaneous closure using an Amplatzer occluder [13].

The concomitant occurrence of VSR and left ventricular FWR is rare but particularly devastating [14]. As reported by Mann [15], this association is related to distinctive patterns of septal injury characterized by specific anatomical features. In contrast, the occurrence of a FWR several months after post-infarction VSD repair has not been reported in the literature and may involve specific alterations in myocardial wall tension following ventriculotomy closure.

We report an exceptional case of a caucasian male patient who underwent multiple consecutive surgical procedures for post-infarction VSR, sternal wound infection, and delayed left ventricular FWR complicated by a giant ventricular pseudoaneurysm, followed by recurrence of a large mycotic pseudoaneurysm caused by Propionibacterium acnes, a pathogen known for its marked virulence.

Ultimately, this situation was a complex infectious challenge that was managed with a transdiaphragmatic pedicled omental flap, intended to provide both a mechanical and an immunological barrier to protect the surgical repair and fill the infected neocavity. This approach successfully addressed a catastrophic scenario using a straightforward yet remarkably effective technique. At more than 24 months of follow-up, the patient remains free of infection, with complete anatomic restoration confirmed by serial imaging. Supported by a review of the literature, this exceptional case underscores the enduring efficacy of the omental flap, highlights the importance of meticulous surgical planning, and draws attention to the often-underestimated virulence of Propionibacterium acnes.

Case presentation

A 55-year-old caucasian male patient was referred to our institution for a recurrent large mycotic pseudoaneurysm of the diaphragmatic wall of the left ventricle. Twenty-two months earlier, he had undergone emergency surgery for repair of a post-infarction VSR complicated by cardiogenic shock despite initial hemodynamic support with IABP and veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO). Coronary angiography revealed distal occlusion of a collateral branch of the posterior descending artery (PDA), not amenable to revascularization. As hemodynamics rapidly deteriorated, postponing surgery was deemed unsafe, and salvage repair was undertaken. The septal rupture was successfully closed with a Dacron patch following debridement of infarcted tissue, and the ventriculotomy closure was reinforced with Teflon strips.

The postoperative course was relatively uneventful, considering the patient’s severely compromised preoperative hemodynamics. Three months later, the patient required reoperation for a 6 mm residual hemolytic shunt caused by localized patch dehiscence due to a ruptured stitch as percutaneous closure was considered unsuitable because of anatomical considerations. The ventriculotomy was re-closed and reinforced with two Teflon strips. The subsequent postoperative course was complicated by a sternal wound infection with painful instability, necessitating sternal osteosynthesis, drainage, and a 6-week course of antibiotics to treat local contamination by Propionibacterium acnes, identified by microbiological analysis of tissue samples.

Three months later, the patient developed a subacute onset of neurological symptoms, like transitory imbalance and weakness of the left arm. A few days earlier, he had experienced spontaneous, self-limiting episodes of heart rate irregularity, likely due to paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. However, on admission, the ECG showed sinus rhythm. While CT imaging was unremarkable, cerebral MRI revealed a superficial right Sylvian stroke. Anticoagulation therapy was then initiated with heparin and subsequently transitioned to fluindione. The patient’s neurological symptoms resolved completely, and he was discharged with a recommended INR target of 2–3 for stroke prevention.

Three months later, the patient experienced a sudden transient episode of chest pain that resolved spontaneously, followed a few days later by progressively worsening dyspnea. Due to the severity of his symptoms, he was admitted to our hospital.

Thoracic CT imaging revealed a large pseudoaneurysm (12 × 13 cm) of the diaphragmatic wall of the left ventricle with significant intra-aneurysmal blood flow. Laboratory tests demonstrated anemia (7.2 g/dL) with an INR of 5.5.

Based on the patient’s history of post-infarction VSR repair via repeat ventriculotomy, prior sternal wound infection with Propionibacterium acnes, imaging findings, and over-anticoagulation, we hypothesized that the pseudoaneurysm had resulted from dehiscence of the ventriculotomy sutures, potentially of infectious origin, despite preoperative apyrexia, as Propionibacterium acnes had been previously identified during the sternal wound infection.

A redo procedure was planned, and a tailored surgical strategy was devised to minimize the risk of catastrophic bleeding. The patient was placed on axillo-femoral cardio-pulmonary bypass (CPB) with right axillary artery cannulation and percutaneous femoral venous cannulation, and cooled to 20 °C. Following the onset of ventricular fibrillation, a resternotomy was safely performed.

The ascending aorta was then clamped, and anterior cold blood cardioplegia was administered. After placement of a left ventricular vent via the right superior pulmonary vein, careful dissection of dense adhesions between the diaphragm and the pseudoaneurysm was initiated. This step was technically challenging, and the pseudoaneurysm was subsequently entered.

After evacuation of blood and multiple thrombi, which were sent for bacteriological analysis, the ventriculotomy closure was found to be intact. However, an unexpected 3 cm ventricular rupture was identified 2 cm from the previous ventriculotomy closure (Fig. 1C). The myocardial tissue surrounding the rupture was notably fibrotic.

Following debridement of the tear edges, the rupture was circumferentially reinforced with interrupted 2–0 felt-pledgeted sutures (Fig. 1D) and closed with a large circular Dacron patch (Fig. 1E).

The postoperative course was uneventful. A postoperative thoracic CT scan confirmed a successful anatomic restoration of the diaphragmatic wall of the left ventricle (Fig. 1F).

Bacteriological analysis of the clots confirmed the presence of Propionibacterium acnes, a pathogen known to exhibit potential virulence in the presence of indwelling material. Following multidisciplinary consultation with infectious disease specialists, the patient was discharged on an 8-week antibiotic regimen (2 weeks of intravenous therapy followed by 6 weeks of oral therapy), despite the absence of overt infection. He remained afebrile, and white blood cell counts, including total WBCs, neutrophils, monocytes, and lymphocytes, remained within normal limits.

Unfortunately, 2 months after completion of antibiotic therapy, and despite being afebrile with normal laboratory values, the patient developed recurrent symptoms, including sudden chest pain and acute dyspnea. At this stage, the diagnosis of a mycotic pseudoaneurysm was clear, likely caused by dehiscence of the Dacron prosthetic patch secondary to an indolent local infection with Propionibacterium acnes, known to produce multiple virulence factors that facilitate tissue damages.

Given the presence of Propionibacterium acnes and indwelling intrapericardial materials, the decision was made to completely remove all infected prosthetic material and reconstruct the diaphragmatic wall of the left ventricle using a heterologous pericardial patch overlaid with a pedicled omental flap, intended to provide mechanical support and immunological protection to reduce local bacterial burden.

Owing to the significant risk of hemorrhage during chest reentry, the patient was cooled to 20°C on left axillary–femoral CPB. Resternotomy and cardiac dissection were performed under ventricular fibrillation. Superior and inferior venous cannulation was then established to optimize venous drainage, as the femoral venous cannula could not be adequately positioned in the right atrium due to displacement of the inferior vena cava by the giant pseudoaneurysm. Cardiac arrest was achieved with antegrade cold blood cardioplegia, and left ventricular venting was achieved via a cannula placed through the superior pulmonary vein.

The pseudoaneurysm was carefully entered, and all clots were removed from the neocavity created by the downward displacement of the left diaphragm. The cavity was thoroughly irrigated with saline to mobilize residual thrombus and subsequently with 2% dilute povidone–iodine. A hemicircumferential dehiscence of the Dacron patch was identified, as initially suspected, and completely excised along with all felt-pledgeted sutures. Debridement of a large area of the ventricular diaphragmatic wall was then carried out, and an ovoid-shaped, glutaraldehyde-treated heterologous pericardial patch was anchored using felt-pledgeted sutures. In addition, the Teflon strips previously used to reinforce the ventriculotomy closure were removed and replaced with two heterologous pericardial strips.

A 5 cm subxiphoid laparotomy was then performed to harvest a large pedicled omental flap, which was mobilized and tunneled into the pericardial cavity through a diaphragmatic defect. Care was taken to avoid twisting the flap and to preserve its vascular supply. Follow-up

At 24 months after discharge, the patient remains clinically well, with no evidence of recurrent infection. Serial transthoracic echocardiographic assessments demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 50%, with persistent akinesia of the diaphragmatic wall.

Discussion

The challenge of cardiac rupture

Cardiac rupture following acute myocardial infarction remains a feared complication associated with life-threatening outcomes. It encompasses a spectrum of mechanical injuries, including VSR, FWR, PMR. In the absence of surgical intervention, prognosis is exceedingly poor, with reported in-hospital mortality exceeding 90%. However, surgical management carries a substantial risk, with mortality rates ranging from 23 to 45% [6–8, 16].

Management of patients with VSR is surgically extremely demanding and requires a close collaboration among cardiac surgeons, intensivists, cardiologists and anesthesiologists to determine the optimal timing of surgery thereby facilitating surgical repair and reducing operative mortality [12, 17–19].

The overall incidence of cardiac ruptures has declined from more than 3% before the era of early reperfusion and thrombolytic therapies to less than 0.5% [2]. Nevertheless, its true incidence is likely underestimated, as autopsy series report rates as high as 11% [20–22].

Therefore, while the widespread use of percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty (PTCA) has directly contributed to rendering cardiac rupture, particularly VSR, a truly rare pathophysiological entity, it has also led to reduced surgical exposure that should be carefully acknowledged in the management of these complex situations.

The combination of a VSR and a FWR, also referred to as a ventricular double rupture (VDR), is rare, and these two complications often occur nearly simultaneously. Yalonetsky [14] identified only 17 cases between 1980 and 2015. Pathological studies suggest that VDR arises from specific anatomical patterns of myocardial rupture, which Mann et al. [15] classified as true double rupture (TDR) and junctional rupture (JR), as illustrated in Fig. 6. TDR is characterized by tears in both the ventricular septum and the free wall, whereas JR involves a rupture localized at the septal-free-wall junctional area.Fig. 6Illustration showing the classification by Mann [15] of mechanisms leading to ventricular double rupture

Notably, five exceptional cases of triple ventricular rupture (TVR), involving the interventricular septum, left ventricular free wall, and papillary muscles, have been reported in the same patients [23–27].

In the context of ventricular double rupture (VDR), our case was unique, as the onset of FWR was delayed relative to VSR. We, therefore, hypothesized that excessive parietal tension following repeat ventriculotomy closure represented the most likely underlying mechanism of this delayed cardiac rupture.

Residual leaks after VSR repair are typically related to patch dehiscence, with reported incidences ranging from 10% when surgery is moderately delayed to 25% in cases of early intervention [28, 29].

Surgical timing is, therefore, a major determinant of both mortality and residual septal recanalization. Early surgery clearly increases technical difficulty, as stitches must be anchored in necrotic tissue despite thorough debridement, whereas delaying surgery for 10–21 days allows fibrous healing and improves suture strength [2, 12, 19].

Our case highlighted the importance of this determinant, as salvage surgery was required in the setting of rapidly worsening hemodynamic instability despite IABP and VA-ECMO support. For symptomatic residual leaks after VSR repair, transcatheter closure with an Amplatzer occluder can be effective in selected patients [30]. Unfortunately, anatomical imaging considerations rendered this approach unsuitable in our patient due to a high risk of device malposition.

The virulence of propionibacterium acnes

Despite persistent apyrexia and normal blood tests throughout the postoperative course and follow-up, we remained concerned about Propionibacterium acnes detected within thrombi from the pseudoaneurysm, given its established role in severe infections, particularly with prosthetic material. This organism has long been considered of low pathogenicity, regarded both as a ubiquitous member of the skin flora and a common contaminant [31]. However, recent evidence led to consider P. acnes as a major cause of indolent but serious infections involving cardiac indwelling devices, prosthetic joints, and ventricular shunts [31–35].

Propionibacterium acnes infections usually originate from cutaneous contamination at the time of surgery. Duffy et al. [36] demonstrated that deep-dwelling skin flora, including P. acnes and Staphylococcus epidermidis, can survive standard preoperative skin preparation, as immunofluorescence identified bacteria throughout all skin layers, suggesting that the true dermal bioburden is likely underestimated.

Furthermore, Propionibacterium acnes is a commensal organism of the upper respiratory tract, which may contribute to an increased risk of infection following cardiac surgery [31, 37]. Severe tissue damage associated with P. acnes infection is attributed to its well-established ability to produce virulence factors, including cytolysins with β-hemolytic activity [38]. In addition, P. acnes harbors multiple virulence genes involved in local inflammatory responses, chronic infection, and biofilm formation [39, 40].

Biofilms are highly organized microbial communities embedded within an extracellular matrix composed of polysaccharides, proteins, and extracellular DNA [41], which confer resistance to many antibiotics [42, 43]. Accordingly, biofilm formation in Propionibacterium acnes infections represents a major clinical challenge, particularly with respect to diagnosis and treatment.

The sudden development of a second giant pseudoaneurysm, occurring 2 months after completion of an 8-week course of antibiotic therapy, was highly suggestive of recurrent Propionibacterium acnes infection, resulting in Dacron patch anastomotic dehiscence with contained hemorrhage into the neocavity, as confirmed by imaging and surgical exploration.

At this stage, considering the pathogenic profile and virulence of Propionibacterium acnes [38–41, 44], previous antibiotic therapy had failed to sterilize the large pre-existing neocavity, which had to be regarded as a potential abscess. Urgent and effective intervention was, therefore, required to address this life-threatening condition. Given the uncertainty regarding complete removal of infected tissue and thrombotic material, prolonged postoperative antibiotic therapy alone was unlikely to achieve definitive sterilization.

Although the use of pedicled omental flaps is not, per se, a novel concept given their well-established wound-healing and antimicrobial properties [45–47], the mechanisms underlying these effects remained poorly understood for decades.

The transposition of the omentum as a pedicled flap is a technique, successfully applied and widely advocated across multiple surgical specialties, including cardiac, thoracic, gastrointestinal, gynecological, urological, reconstructive, and even veterinary surgery [45].

Omental flap transposition applies to a broad spectrum of clinical situations, including management of severe mediastinal infections after cardiac surgery [46–48], reconstruction of chest wall defects [49–54], coverage of infected or irradiated tissue [55, 56], protection of vascular anastomoses [57], digestive colonic sutures [58], genitourinary reconstructions [59], treatment of lymphoedema [60] and lymphoceles [61], repair of esophageal, bronchial, tracheal, pancreatic, fistulae [62–66], breast reconstruction [67], and obliteration of residual abscess cavities [68, 69].

Promising cardiac applications have recently emerged, including management of LVAD-related infections that require prospective evaluation [70, 71].

The omentum’s ability to reach diverse thoracic regions and its low failure rate make it a reliable reconstructive option. In specific scenarios, such as esophageal, bronchial, tracheal, or cervical fistulae, its reach may be limited by pedicle length, a constraint that can be addressed through complete mobilization along the gastroepiploic vessels or by free transplantation with microvascular anastomosis.

Donor-site complications such as hemorrhage, incisional hernia, seroma and infection have also been reported. Infections notably concern extra-abdominal applications of omentoplasty like breast reconstruction. In addition, a case of unexplained delayed splenic rupture has been identified in the literature [79].

In retrospect, it is legitimate to question whether prophylactic omentoplasty should have been considered during treatment of the first pseudoaneurysm. The delayed cardiac rupture identified intraoperatively was unexpected, leading to underestimate a potential infectious mechanism and to consider the neocavity as non-infected. Furthermore, since the median incubation period of Propionibacterium acnes in blood cultures is usually 7 days (range 5–14 days) [35], it likely contributed to masking an underlying indolent local infection at the time the first prosthetic Dacron patch was implanted.

Conclusion

The present case, supported by a narrative review of the literature, supports the role of the omental flap as a valuable therapeutic adjunct, acting in synergy with antibiotic therapy in highly challenging infectious situations and highlights the insidious virulence of Propionibacterium acnes, characterized by an indolent course and diagnostic pitfalls that may lead to underestimation of its clinical severity.
