Probiotic-based self-crosslinking bio-coating for the treatment of colitis by increasing probiotic colonization
Authors: Shang Shi, Boqing Li, Ying Zhang, Zhiqin Li, Yingzi Cui, Chunlei Ma, Xiaolin Zhang, Sisi Liu, Bo Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Probiotic-based therapies have great potential to treat inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Still, their success is limited by difficulties in ensuring survival, colonization, and targeted effects within the harsh environment of the gastrointestinal tract. This study presents a bio-coated probiotic strategy for treating colitis in mice, using self-crosslinking mussel adhesive protein (Map) to form a protective layer around Bifidobacterium longum (BL). Because Map contains abundant lysine residues that confer positive charges, it interacts electrostatically with negatively charged alginate (Alg) to form the BL@Map@Alg formulation. Compared to uncoated BL, BL@Map@Alg increased survival under oxygen exposure by 19.6-fold, showed a 76.8-fold increase in resistance to simulated stomach acid, and exhibited a 6.2-fold boost in intestinal colonization. It also demonstrated superior ROS scavenging ability, decreasing ROS levels by 41.1 %. In a mouse colitis model, BL@Map@Alg reduced weight loss, colon shortening, and inflammatory cytokines compared to untreated colitis. It also helped restored intestinal barrier function by increasing the levels of tight junction proteins ZO-1 and claudin-1, which were diminished in DSS-treated mice. 16S rRNA sequencing showed that BL@Map@Alg lowered Escherichia abundance from 27.5 % to 0.042 % while increasing Akkermansia from 0.73 % to 9.3 %. These results highlight the potential for BL@Map@Alg to enhance probiotic therapy by improving survival, colonization, and gut microbiota, offering a promising strategy for personalized and precision medicine.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which includes Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, is an immune-driven disorder characterized by chronic intestinal inflammation, and its global prevalence is increasing [[1], [2], [3]]. Current treatments, such as anti-inflammatory drugs (e.g., aminosalicylates, corticosteroids), immunosuppressants, and antibiotics, aim to control harmful bacterial infections and regulate the immune system [4]. However, these therapies often face limitations like variable effectiveness, poor drug absorption, and significant systemic side effects, which compromise their long-term safety and therapeutic benefits.

Probiotic-based therapies provide a promising alternative for managing inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), owing to their diverse therapeutic effects on gut health. Probiotics help maintain gut homeostasis by sticking to the intestinal lining, blocking harmful pathogens, and producing bioactive compounds [[5], [6], [7]]. Probiotics such as Lactobacillus [8,9], Bifidobacterium [[10], [11], [12]], Bacillus [13,14], and Akkermansia muciniphila [15,16] have shown significant therapeutic potential in preclinical and clinical studies, especially in animal models of colitis. These probiotics enhance intestinal mucus production to protect epithelial cells, reinforce the mucosal barrier, and improve antioxidant defenses to reduce oxidative stress [[17], [18], [19]]. In addition, probiotics can modulate the immune response, attenuate inflammation, and stimulate the release of beneficial metabolites and anti-inflammatory cytokines [20,21]. However, despite the substantial therapeutic promise of probiotics, the application of probiotics remains hindered by several challenges. A major limitation is their low bioavailability, defined as the ability of probiotics to survive the harsh conditions of the gastrointestinal environment and reach the target site in sufficient quantities [22]. The acidic stomach environment, bile acids, and digestive enzymes pose significant barriers to the survival and efficacy of orally administered probiotics [23,24]. These harsh conditions can greatly decrease probiotic viability, limiting their therapeutic benefits. Furthermore, many probiotics struggle to effectively adhere to the intestinal mucosa, a critical step for exerting their beneficial effects in the gut [25,26]. Without proper adhesion, probiotics may not be able to outcompete harmful bacteria or stimulate the production of beneficial metabolites and immune responses.

Recently, functional surface encapsulation has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance their therapeutic potential [[27], [28], [29]]. One approach involves the nanoencapsulation of probiotics with an anti-inflammatory agent using a polysaccharide responsive to the gastrointestinal microenvironment, showing potential in colitis treatment [30]. Another approach utilizes the unique properties of silk fibroin, which self-assembles into a nanocoating on bacterial surfaces, significantly improving the colonization efficiency of E. coli [26]. Despite these advancements, significant challenges persist, especially for anaerobic probiotics, which lack antioxidant mechanisms to withstand oxidative stress [5]. Research on universal coatings for anaerobic bacteria remains limited, with several critical issues needing resolution to enhance therapeutic efficacy. First, coating reaction conditions must be sufficiently mild to avoid damaging the probiotics caused by chemical agents. Second, the process should be completed swiftly to prevent probiotics from dying in oxygen-rich environments. Third, coatings must endure the harsh acidic and enzymatic conditions of the stomach while degrading in the intestine to restore probiotic activity. Despite recent advances in probiotic encapsulation, anaerobic probiotics continue to struggle with poor intestinal colonization and vulnerability to oxygen exposure during transit. Therefore, enabling them to simultaneously gain acid resistance, survive oxygen exposure, and improve intestinal colonization is essential for their effective use in IBD therapy.

Among these strategies, mussel adhesive protein (Map) has gained increasing attention due to its strong underwater adhesion, biocompatibility, and ability to form stable coatings [31]. Derived from mussels, Map contains catechol and amine functional groups, which enable strong interactions with both organic and inorganic surfaces [32]. Its self-crosslinking properties allow the formation of a continuous coating under mild conditions without harming probiotic viability [33]. These unique features make Map an ideal candidate for surface functionalization of sensitive probiotics. Alginate (Alg), a naturally occurring anionic polysaccharide extracted from brown seaweed, possesses carboxyl groups that confer pH-responsive behavior, allowing stability in acidic gastric environments and controlled degradation in the intestine [34]. Moreover, Alg has inherent anti-inflammatory activity and serves as a protective matrix that promotes mucosal adhesion and targets inflamed areas of the intestine [35]. The combined properties of Map and Alg provide a synergistic platform for engineering functional probiotic coatings.

In this study, we developed a novel therapeutic strategy using functionalized probiotics (BL@Map@Alg) with synergistic effects. On the one hand, Map molecules self-crosslink via their oxidized and unoxidized DOPA groups on the biological surfaces; on the other hand, Map carries numerous lysine residues with positive charges, enabling electrostatic interactions with the probiotics to form a continuous protective coating (Fig. 1A). Simultaneously, Map adsorbs the negatively charged alginate, an anti-inflammatory molecule, facilitating the accumulation of probiotics at sites of inflammation, thereby enabling a synergistic therapeutic effect. In the gastric environment, sodium alginate undergoes ion exchange (Na+ replaced by H+), resulting in reduced solubility that protects the probiotics. Upon reaching the intestine, H+ in alginate is again exchanged with Na+, which increases solubility and facilitates the gradual exposure of the underlying adhesive layer. The negative charge of alginate further directs the probiotics to inflammation sites, while mussel adhesive protein (Map) adheres to the intestinal surface through hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, and coordination with metal ions (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, both Map and alginate confer anti-inflammatory properties to the probiotics. Our findings indicate that BL@Map@Alg effectively alleviates colitis by reducing pro-inflammatory factors, strengthening the intestinal barrier, and modulating the gut microbiota (Fig. 1C). Materials and methods

Materials

MRS was purchased from Hi-tech Industrial Park Hope Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China). Mussel Adhesive Protein (Map) and FITC-Alg were obtained from Qiyue Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Xian, China). Fluorescein Isothiocyanate (FITC), Propidium Iodide (PI), and potassium phosphate buffer were obtained from Macklin Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Rhodamine B NHS ester was purchased from MCE Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Alginate (Alg) and Lipopolysaccharides (LPS) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Co., Ltd. (USA). The LDH Release Assay kit and Nitric Oxide Assay kit were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Dextran Sulfate Sodium (DSS) was obtained from MP Biomedicals Co., Ltd. (USA). ZO-1 and Claudin-1 antibodies were purchased from Proteintech Group, Inc. (Wuhan, China). DyLight 488/549 and Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG were obtained from Abbkine Scientific Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Diamidine Phenyl Indole (DAPI), Hoechst 33342, Alcian Blue Stain Kit (pH 2.5) and Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) assay kit were acquired from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). DMEM and Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) were obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Simulated Gastric Fluid (SGF) and Simulated Intestinal Fluid (SIF) were purchased from Phygene Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Fuzhou, China). ELISA assay kits for MPO, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β were sourced from Wuhan Adanti Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Bacterial and cell culture

Bifidobacterium longum (BNCC185354) was obtained from BeNa Culture Collection. The bacteria were grown in MRS broth supplemented with cysteine (0.5 g/L) at 37 °C under anaerobic conditions (85 % N2, 10 % CO2, 5 % H2), using an anaerobic microaerobic culture system (ANOXOMAT MARK II, Netherlands). RAW 264.7 macrophage cells and Caco-2 cells obtained from Binzhou Medical University, were cultured in complete medium at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 atmosphere. The complete medium consisted of DMEM, fetal bovine serum (FBS), and penicillin/streptomycin in a volume ratio of 89:10:1.

Preparation and characterization of BL@Map@Alg

Bifidobacterium longum (BL) was cultured in MRS medium at 37 °C for 72 h under anaerobic conditions. After incubation, the bacteria were harvested by centrifugation (6000 rpm, 5 min) and washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). To prepare BL@Map, 2 mg/mL of Map was mixed with the bacterial suspension in K+ buffer and vortexed for 5 min to achieve a homogeneous solution. The mixture was then incubated for 10 min under anaerobic conditions. After incubation, the solution was centrifuged to collect the bacterial precipitate. The bacterial pellet was resuspended in a 10 mg/mL sodium alginate solution, vortexed for 5 min to form BL@Map@Alg. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were acquired using a JEM-1400Plus transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Japan), and hydrated particle sizes and zeta potential were measured using a Zetasizer NanoZS90 instrument (Malvern, U.K.). FTIR spectra were obtained using a Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrometer (Bruker, Germany).

In vitro simulation of gastrointestinal microenvironment

BL, BL@Map, and BL@Map@Alg (2 × 108 CFU) were incubated anaerobically (37 °C, 85 % N2, 10 % CO2, 5 % H2) in simulated gastric fluid (SGF, pH 2.0) containing pepsin for 1 h and 2 h. They were also incubated in simulated intestinal fluid (SIF, pH 6.8) containing trypsin for 2 h and 4 h. The bacterial suspensions were then serially diluted, plated on coated plates, and incubated for 72 h before colony counting. After incubation in both SGF and SIF, the bacteria were centrifuged (6000 rpm, 5 min). The resulting precipitate was collected, resuspended in PBS, and 10 μL of the suspension was placed onto a copper grid for morphological observation using transmission electron microscopy (TEM).

Protective effect of bio-coating against oxygen exposure

BL, BL@Map, and BL@Map@Alg (2 × 109 CFU/mL) were resuspended in PBS and serially dilute for initial plate counting (0 h). The remaining suspensions were incubated under aerobic conditions at 37 °C for 24 h, then by plate counts were performed to bacterial viability.

Assessment of BL@Map@Alg adhesion in vivo

The BL suspension was first centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 5 min, then washed three times with PBS (pH 7.4) to collect the bacterial cells. To label the BL, 100 μL of Rhodamine B NHS (100 μg/mL) was added to the bacterial suspension, and the bacteria were washed three times with PBS to remove excess dye. Next, BL@Map and BL@Map@Alg were synthesized using the previously described method. For in vivo imaging, BL, BL@Map, and BL@Map@Alg (2 × 109 CFU/mL) were orally administered to mice. At 4, 8, 24, and 48 h post-administration, the mice were analyzed with an in vivo optical imaging system (IVIS Spectrum, PerkinElmer) to monitor the distribution and accumulation of the labeled bacteria in the body.

Mucosal adhesive capability of BL@Map@Alg

The BL suspension was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 5 min, then washed three times with PBS (pH 7.4) to collect the bacterial cells. To label the BL, 100 μL of FITC solution (100 μg/mL) was added, and the bacteria were washed three times with PBS. Next, BL@Map and BL@Map@Alg were synthesized using the previously described method. Fresh mice colon tissues were collected and incubated with FITC-labeled BL, BL@Map, and BL@Map@Alg at 37 °C for 2 h. After incubation, the colon tissue was washed twice with PBS and analyzed using an in vivo optical imaging system (IVIS Spectrum, PerkinElmer). The average radiance efficiency was quantified using Living Image software.

LDH cytotoxicity assay

RAW 264.7 cells and Caco-2 cells (5 × 103) were cultured in a 96-well plate at 37 °C for 12 h. After incubation, the complete medium was replaced with basal medium, and PBS, BL (1 × 104 CFU), BL@Map (1 × 104 CFU), and BL@Map@Alg (1 × 104 CFU) were added to the respective wells. Cell supernatants were collected at 2, 4, 8, and 12 h for LDH cytotoxicity analysis. In each case, 120 μL of supernatant was mixed with 15 μL of cellular LDH release solution, followed by 60 μL of LDH assay solution to measure enzyme activity at 490 nm. The plate was then covered with foil to protect it from light and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. LDH release was measured using a microplate reader, and the percentage of LDH release was calculated as follows:LDHrelease(%)=ExperimentalLDHrelease(OD490)/MaximumLDHrelease(OD490)

Cellular RT-qPCR analysis

The RAW 264.7 cells (5 × 104) were seeded in a 24-well plate and incubated 12 h at 37 °C. In addition to the blank group, the experimental groups were supplemented with different media containing LPS (1 μg/mL) (fresh medium, fresh medium containing BL (1 × 106 CFU/mL), fresh medium containing BL@Map (1 × 106 CFU/mL), and fresh medium containing BL@Map@Alg (1 × 106 CFU/mL)). After culturing for 12 h, the medium was aspirated and cells were washed three times with PBS, then 1 mL of Trizol and left for 10 min at room temperature, followed by 200 μL of chloroform was added and left to stand for 5 min at room temperature. The cell suspension was then centrifuged at 12000 g, 4 °C for 15 min. The supernatant was aspirated and an equal volume of isopropanol-acetone was added, and the supernatant was left for 10 min at room temperature before conducting additional centrifugation at 12000 g, 4 °C for 15 min, and then 20 μL DEPC was added after washing with anhydrous ethanol. RT-qPCR was performed with SPARKscript II RT Plus Kit and 2 × SYBR Green qPCR Mix (SparkJape). The primer list was shown in Table S1.

Determination of intracellular ROS

The Caco-2 cells (5 × 104) were seeded in a 24-well plate and incubated for 12 h at 37 °C. In addition to the blank group, the four experimental groups were supplemented with different media containing H2O2 (200 μM) for 2 h. Then, four experimental groups were treated with PBS, BL, BL@Map and BL@Map@Alg. After 6 h incubation at 37 °C, the medium was aspirated and cells were rinsed with PBS. The fluorescence probe DCFH-DA (10 μM) was added and incubated for 30 min. Cells were then rinsed with PBS, and stained with Hoechst 33342 for 5 min. Intracellular ROS was determined by detecting the fluorescence of DCF (λex = 488 nm, λem = 525 nm) using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Animal models of colitis

The mice (specific-pathogen-free C57BL/6, female, 6 weeks old) were purchased from Jinan Pengyue Laboratory Animal Breeding Company (Jinan, China; license key: SCXK [Lu] 20140007). The C57BL/6 mice were acclimatized for 7 days under the standard rearing conditions (light/dark cycle for 12 h, temperature 25 °C) before all experiments. The 30 mice were equally randomized into five groups, and each group had six mice. The five groups were named as control, DSS, BL, BL@Map and BL@Map@Alg groups. The mice were given 3 % DSS orally for 7 days in all four groups except the control group, while the control group was provided with free drinking water for 7 days. Next, the control and DSS groups were administered PBS by gavage (300 μL/mouse/day) for 9 days, while BL, BL@Map, and BL@Map@Alg (2 × 108 CFU/mouse/day, suspended in PBS) were administered by gavage for 9 days. After the treatment, the open field test was performed to assess the behavioral activity of the mice in each group.

All procedures complied with Chinese laws and regulations (Animal Use and Care Committee of Shandong Agricultural University and Binzhou Medical University) and followed internationally recognized principles and guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

H&E staining and biochemical marker assays

The colon, liver, and kidney tissues were fixed in paraformaldehyde (4 %) for 24 h. After embedding in paraffin, colon tissue sections (4 μm) were baked at 60 °C for 2 h. The sections were deparaffinized twice with xylene for 15 min each, followed by dehydration in absolute ethanol, 95 %, 85 %, and 75 % ethanol for 5 min each. The sections were then washed with distilled water for 5 min, stained with hematoxylin for 8 min. The sections were differentiated with 1 % acid alcohol for 3 s, washed with water for 10 min, and stained with eosin for 6 min. Dehydration was performed sequentially in 85 %, 95 %, and absolute ethanol for 5 min each, followed by two washes with xylene for 5 min each. Finally, the sections were mounted with neutral resin and examined under a light microscope (Olympus, Japan).

After the mouse blood was placed at room temperature for 2 h, it was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected for analysis. The biochemical markers tested included ALT, AST, DBIL, TBIL, CREA, UREA, and UA. Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd, performed the testing.

Immunofluorescence staining

The colon tissues were cut into frozen sections (10 μm), then goat serum was used to block them for 1 h at 37 °C. The blocking solution was aspirated to add primary antibodies ZO-1 (diluted 1:100), claudin-1 (diluted 1:1000), and incubated at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS three times for 5 min each, Dylight 488/549 Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 2 h. Finally, the sections were washed three times with PBS, incubated with DAPI for 5 min at room temperature. Finally, the sections were sealed with an antifade mounting medium. In addition, images of the sections were analyzed using a fluorescence microscope, and fluorescence signals were quantified with ImageJ software.

ELISA analysis

The levels of MPO, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β in the colon were determined using an ELISA assay kit (Wuhan Adanti Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). The colon from each mouse was homogenized in ice-cold PBS (pH 7.4) with an electric homogenizer. The homogenates were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C and collected the supernatant. Add the supernatant to a microplate coated with antibodies specific for TNF-α、IL-6 and IL-1 β, and then add a micro coupling reagent. After incubation, the optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader. A standard curve was used to calculate the concentration of MPO, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β.

16S rRNA sequencing assay

First, the feces of mice were collected and frozen in liquid nitrogen. Then, DNA from feces was extracted and the 16S rDNA V34 region was amplified to construct the sequencing library. Finally, the range and concentration of the library were examined using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and 16S rRNA sequencing was performed using a sequencer (MGISEQ-2000). Forward primer: 5′-ACTCCTACGGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’; Reverse primer: 5′-GGACTACHVGGGGTWTCTAAT-3’.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

The BL probe was designed and synthesized by GeWei Biotechnology. This FISH probe design used the silva138.1 database and selected bacterial 16S rRNA small subunit sequences for its design. The BL probe was labeled with Texas Red fluorescence. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was performed using a commercial bacterial FISH detection kit (Gewei Biotechnology, Shanghai). Fluorescence images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM880, Germany).

Statistical analysis

All results are shown as the mean ± standard error of the mean. Differences between multiple groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed by GraphPad Prism 9 (version 9.5.0).

Results and discussion

Preparation, characterization, and functional validation of the bio-coated probiotic BL@Map@Alg

Bifidobacterium longum (BL), a key probiotic in the human gut [36], plays a critical role in maintaining gut health by modulating gut microbiota composition and exerting anti-inflammatory effects [37]. Given these properties, BL was chosen as the model probiotic for functional modification to develop a synergistic treatment for colitis.

As shown in Fig. 2A, BL was vortexed in K+ phosphate buffer (0.2 M, pH 5.5) containing Mussel Adhesive Protein (Map) for 10 min. Map molecules self-crosslinked through both oxidized and unoxidized dopamine groups, forming a continuous bio-coating on the surface of BL. The lysine residues in Map facilitated interaction with the negatively charged alginate (Alg), promoting the formation of the BL@Map@Alg complex. TEM analysis further confirmed the structural integrity of the coatings. Compared to BL, both BL@Map and BL@Map@Alg exhibited a distinct outer shell (Fig. 2B). To improve the coating morphology observed by TEM, we further optimized the concentrations of Map and Alg. Increasing the concentration of Map resulted in gradually more complete surface coverage. When Alg was used at 10 mg/mL, the coating was smooth and uniform; however, a higher concentration (20 mg/mL) produced a thicker but disordered layer (Fig. S1). This phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that alginate concentration directly influences polymer chain networking and rheological properties. Based on these observations, 2 mg/mL Map and 10 mg/mL Alg were selected as the optimal conditions for bio-coating formation. The hydration particle size distribution revealed differences among the three groups, with values of 2.45 ± 0.08 μm for BL, 2.84 ± 0.29 μm for BL@Map, and 3.16 ± 0.24 μm for BL@Map@Alg (Fig. 2C). Importantly, the zeta potential measurement showed that the values of BL, BL@Map and BL@Map@Alg were −37.1 ± 0.79, – 26.6 ± 0.68 and −33.6 ± 1.23 mV (Fig. 2D). The zeta potential increased significantly increased after Map coating, likely due to the abundance of positively charged lysine residues in Map [38]. The subsequent decrease in zeta potential was due to the carboxyl groups carried by Alg [39], which introduced additional negative charges on the surface. This enhanced surface electronegativity may facilitate the targeted interaction of the probiotic with inflamed cells. FTIR results showed that the absorption band at 1600 cm−1, which corresponds to the aromatic C=C stretching of DOPA in Map, was observed in both BL@Map and BL@Map@Alg but was absent in BL. Moreover, the C–H stretching bands at 2914 and 2846 cm−1were only observed in BL@Map@Alg (Fig. S2). In addition, fluorescence labeling provided further confirmation of the bio-coating. Propidium iodide (PI) labeled BL, while fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) labeled Map and Alg. To further assess the impact of the coating on bacterial growth, growth curve analysis was conducted. Notably, the coated BL entered the logarithmic phase faster than the uncoated group (Fig. S4), suggesting that the bio-coating not only preserves viability but may also enhance bacterial growth dynamics. Consistently, live/dead staining demonstrated that the coating had no effect on bacterial viability (Fig. S5). The enhanced growth rate of BL in the presence of the bio-coating is encouraging, suggesting that the coating creates a supportive environment conducive for bacterial proliferation. Moreover, the coated BL maintained excellent viability after two months of refrigerated storage, indicating excellent long-term stability (Fig. 2E–S6). Notably, the coating strategy was also successfully applied to other probiotic strains, including Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum and Bacteroides vulgatus (Fig. S7), suggesting the versatility and adaptability of the bio-coating platform. This adaptability highlights the potential of this coating strategy as a universal delivery platform for various anaerobic probiotics in therapeutic applications.

In vitro protective effect under gastrointestinal and oxygen exposure conditions

The viability of Bifidobacterium longum (BL) after exposure to the harsh gastrointestinal environment is essential for its successful colonization in the intestine [40]. The gastrointestinal tract presents significant challenges to probiotics, including the highly acidic gastric environment and the relatively more neutral intestinal conditions. To simulate these conditions in vitro, artificial gastric fluid (SGF, pH 2.0) was used to replicate the acidic environment of the stomach, assessing the protective ability of the functionalized bio-coating. Artificial intestinal fluid (SIF, pH 6.8) was used to simulate the intestinal environment and evaluate the proliferation capacity of the bacteria.

As shown in Fig. 3A, S8A, TEM images indicated that after 1 h of incubation in SGF, BL exhibited shrinkage, and after 2 h, a decomposition phenomenon was observed, likely due to the combined effect of the strong acidity and gastric proteases. This led to membrane damage and the intracellular molecule degradation, ultimately leading to bacterial death [41]. In contrast, BL@Map@Alg showed relatively intact overall morphology despite some loss of coating, suggesting that the bio-coating offers protection under acidic conditions. Consistently, SEM analysis after 2 h of incubation in SGF revealed morphological changes similar to those observed by TEM (as indicated by the red arrow), further confirming the protective effect of the bio-coating (Fig. S10). Furthermore, after incubation in SIF for 2 h and 4 h, the bacteria in all groups exhibited normal morphology, with BL@Map and BL@Map@Alg retaining their coatings relatively well, indicating the stability of the bio-coating in a neutral environment (Fig. 3A–S9A). These findings were further validated through plate colony counting experiments, which showed that BL@Map@Alg had higher survival rates after 1 h of anaerobic incubation in SGF, with significant differences compared to BL. After 2 h of incubation in SGF, a large amount of BL@Map@Alg remained viable, while BL showed almost complete loss of viability (Fig. S8B). The survival rate of BL@Map@Alg increased by 76.8-fold compared to BL (Fig. 3B), demonstrating the exceptional protective effect of the bio-coating within 2 h, which aligns with the time required for probiotics to pass through the stomach during gastric emptying. In addition, SEM images demonstrated that in SIF, BL retained an overall intact morphology but exhibited surface collapse (as indicated by the green arrow), whereas BL@Map and BL@Map@Alg maintained uniform and intact structures without obvious structural damage (Fig. S10). Moreover, the proliferative capacity of BL, BL@Map, and BL@Map@Alg was evaluated in SIF, and no significant differences were observed among the groups (Fig. 3C–S9B). In addition to gastric and intestinal conditions, TEM analysis was also performed under pathological weakly acidic conditions (pH 5.5) and under extreme alkaline stress (pH 10). Under pathological pH, BL@Map@Alg maintained an intact morphology, highlighting the stability of the coating in disease-relevant environments. In contrast, under extreme alkaline conditions at pH 10, uncoated BL ruptured, whereas BL@Map@Alg, although showing partial coating disruption, preserved the overall bacterial morphology (Fig. S11). These findings emphasize the significance of the bio-coating in supporting probiotic survival during gastrointestinal transit. The coating maintained structural integrity under acidic conditions and remained stable in the intestine, preserving bacterial viability. As a strict anaerobe, BL is highly sensitive to oxygen exposure, which can compromise cell membrane integrity, disrupt redox balance, and lead to cell death [42]. However, during probiotic processing, storage, and gastrointestinal transit, temporary oxygen exposure is often unavoidable. To evaluate the protective effect of the bio-coating against oxidative stress, BL, BL@Map, and BL@Map@Alg were exposed to atmospheric oxygen for 24 h, followed by anaerobic plate counting to assess viability. As shown in Fig. 3D and E, uncoated BL showed a sharp decrease in viable counts from 2.34 × 109 to 5.7 × 106 CFU/mL, indicating a severe loss of viability. In contrast, BL@Map@Alg maintained a relatively high viable count of 1.13 × 108 CFU/mL. The survival rate of BL@Map@Alg was 19.6-fold higher than that of uncoated BL, demonstrating that the functionalized coating significantly mitigated oxidative damage during aerobic exposure. These results further support the multifunctional protective role of the bio-coating under oxidative stress conditions.

Biocompatibility is a critical aspect of applying functionalized probiotics in human or animal health [29]. To be safely and effectively used as therapeutic agents, probiotics must interact harmoniously with biological systems without causing adverse effects. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) is a widely used biomarker for assessing cell membrane integrity [41]. When cell membranes are damaged, LDH is quickly released into the culture medium, making it a reliable indicator of cytotoxicity. RAW 264.7 macrophage cells, known for their pivotal role in immune defense and clearance, are particularly sensitive to foreign substances or potentially harmful materials [46]. Therefore, evaluating the cytotoxicity of BL@Map@Alg using LDH release assays in RAW 264.7 cells provide important information about the biocompatibility of the functionalized probiotics. As shown in Fig. 4E, LDH release rate of RAW 264.7 cells were time-dependent within 12 h. Importantly, the BL@Map@Alg group showed no significant increase in LDH release compared to the PBS group, with statistical analysis confirming no significant difference (P > 0.05). Caco-2 cells, which closely resemble differentiated intestinal epithelial cells in both structure and function, were used to evaluate the biocompatibility of BL@Map@Alg. We found that BL@Map@Alg did not cause significant cytotoxicity toward Caco-2 cells within 12 h of exposure (Fig. 4F). This result aligns with the LDH release data from in RAW 264.7 macrophages and further supports the good biocompatibility of BL@Map@Alg. This lack of cytotoxic response suggests that BL@Map@Alg is well-tolerated by both immune cells and intestinal epithelial cells, and does not induce significant cytotoxicity, providing strong evidence for its safety in potential oral delivery applications.

In order to further assess whether the bio-functionalized BL@Map@Alg could effectively mitigate the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines from macrophages, we established an inflammatory model using RAW 264.7 macrophage cells. The cells were induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 1 μg/mL) to simulate an inflammatory response. Nitric oxide (NO) production was measured as a key marker of inflammation. The results demonstrated a significant increase in NO content (28.9 ± 1.46 μM) following LPS stimulation, confirming the successful induction of the inflammatory model (Fig. S13). To further investigate the effect of the BL@Map@Alg treatment on pro-inflammatory cytokine release, we measured the relative expression levels of three key inflammatory cytokines: TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, using RT-qPCR. These cytokines are pivotal in the inflammatory cascade and serve as reliable biomarkers for assessing inflammation. Our data indicated that BL@Map@Alg significantly reduced the release of all three pro-inflammatory cytokines compared to the LPS-stimulated group (Fig. 4G, H, I), suggesting that BL@Map@Alg effectively suppresses the inflammatory response. These results highlight the potential of the bio-coating in modulating macrophage activation and reducing inflammation, a critical step in treating inflammatory diseases. This suggests that BL plays a major role in inhibiting the expression of pro-inflammatory factors by RAW264.7 cells, likely through its intrinsic immunomodulatory properties that prevent macrophage overactivation and suppress cytokine secretion.

To further evaluate the antioxidant potential of BL@Map@Alg, we used H2O2-stimulated Caco-2 cells as in vitro models of acute cell injury to investigate its ability to scavenge intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS). As shown in Fig. 4J and K, ROS levels significantly increased upon H2O2 exposure (P < 0.0001), indicating oxidative stress. Notably, the BL@Map@Alg group exhibited the strongest ROS-scavenging capacity, with 41.1 % reduction in ROS levels compared to the BL group. The antioxidant enhancement is likely attributable not only to the improved viability of the coated probiotics, but also to the intrinsic redox-regulating properties of Map and Alg [47,48]. This dual-action mechanism may offer synergistic protection against oxidative and inflammatory damage in the intestinal epithelium. Considering the close interplay between oxidative stress and inflammation in intestinal pathophysiology, the superior ROS-scavenging ability of BL@Map@Alg may further contribute to its overall anti-inflammatory efficacy.

Evaluation of preventive and therapeutic effect on DSS-induced colitis in mice

It has been shown that the characteristics of DSS-induced colitis in mice closely resemble those observed in humans, including weight loss, bloody stool, ulceration and epithelial cell loss [49]. Based on this similarity, we investigated the in vivo therapeutic effects of BL@Map@Alg in a mouse model of DSS-induced colitis. Mice were given DSS (3 wt%) in their drinking water for 7 days. Starting on day 4, mice were treated with BL, BL@Map, and BL@Map@Alg by gavage daily for 7 days (Fig. 5A). Mice given DSS exhibited diarrhea and bloody stools to varying degrees, with body weight beginning to decline significantly from the third day. By day 7, mice in the DSS, BL, and BL@Map groups lost 27.4 %, 22.9 %, and 22.6 % of their body weight, respectively. Notably, the BL@Map@Alg group exhibited the greatest protection, with only a 16.2 % reduction in body weight. After stopping DSS treatment, mice in the BL, BL@Map, and BL@Map@Alg groups exhibited gradual recovery of their body weight (Fig. 5B). In addition, DSS treatment significantly shortened colon length, which was notably restored in the BL@Map@Alg-treated group (Fig. 5C and D). Myeloperoxidase (MPO) levels, which indicate neutrophil infiltration and tissue inflammation, increased by 39.3 pg/mL in the DSS group compared to the control group. Treatment with BL@Map@Alg significantly reduced MPO levels by 33.1 pg/mL (Fig. 5E), indicating its efficacy in mitigating neutrophil-mediated inflammation. Additionally, pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, key mediators of the inflammatory response in colitis, were significantly lower in the BL@Map@Alg-treated group compared to other treated groups. Cytokine levels in the BL@Map@Alg group were 527.7 pg/mL, 105.8 pg/mL, and 74.0 pg/mL for TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, respectively, the lowest among all experimental groups (Fig. 5F-H). However, despite the significant reduction, cytokine levels in the BL@Map@Alg group remained higher than those in the control group. This could be attributed to the incomplete recovery of the mucosal layer, which retained some residual inflammatory infiltration. These findings indicate that while BL@Map@Alg treatment significantly attenuates the severity of DSS-induced colitis, it may not completely reverse the damage to the mucosal barrier in the short term.

Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (ECN) is a well-characterized probiotic strain that has been widely used in clinical practice, particularly for the management of inflammatory bowel diseases and maintenance of intestinal health [51]. To exclude the interference from the bio-coating itself, we used the ECN as a positive control. ECN@Map@Alg treatment in mice with colitis showed comparable effects to BL@Map@Alg on body weight recovery and colon length, but the inflammatory cytokine levels differed slightly. The TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β levels in the ECN@Map@Alg group were 692.2 pg/mL (P < 0.01), 113.5 pg/mL (P > 0.05), and 67.0 pg/mL (P > 0.05), respectively (Fig. S14). Therefore, the differences in inflammatory cytokine levels may be attributed to the different bacterial strains used, rather than the bio-coating itself.

To further assess the effects of treatment on disease-related behavioral function, we conducted an open-field test, analyzing representative locomotor trajectories and quantifying total distance traveled. Results showed that DSS group exhibited markedly reduced activity and significant wall-clinging behavior, indicating disease and anxiety manifestations. In contrast, mice in the BL@Map@Alg group showed significantly restored locomotor activity, reduced wall-clinging behavior, and a greater distribution of movement trajectories in the central area (Fig. 5I). Furthermore, compared to the DSS group, the total distances in the BL, BL@Map, and BL@Map@Alg groups reached 1.06-fold, 1.88-fold, and 2.76-fold, respectively (Fig. 5J), with the recovery in BL@Map@Alg approaching that of the control group. Additionally, we separately quantified CD86 and CD206 in colonic tissue to assess mucosal innate immunity. Results show that, relative to control, DSS increased CD86 from 2.48 ng/mL to 4.09 ng/mL and decreased CD206 from 8.04 ng/mL to 4.87 ng/mL. Following BL@Map@Alg treatment, CD86 decreased to 2.61 ng/mL and CD206 increased to 8.47 ng/mL, indicating a shift from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 polarization and restoration of mucosal immune homeostasis (Fig. 5K and L). Overall, BL@Map@Alg confers multidimensional efficacy in the DSS colitis model, highlighting its potential as a multifunctional therapeutic platform.

In parallel, the expression of tight junction proteins such as ZO-1 and claudin-1 is markedly downregulated, leading to increased epithelial permeability and impaired barrier integrity. In addition, we performed immunofluorescence analysis to investigate the localization and expression levels of ZO-1 and claudin-1 in intestinal epithelial cells, aiming to further elucidate the therapeutic mechanism of BL@Map@Alg. As shown in Fig. 7D–F, the results revealed a significant decrease in the expression of both ZO-1 and claudin-1 in the DSS-treated group, consistent with DSS-induced disruption of intestinal barrier function. This reduction in tight junction protein expression highlights the compromised integrity of the intestinal epithelium in DSS-induced colitis, a hallmark of IBD pathophysiology. Treatment with BL@Map@Alg significantly restored the expression of ZO-1 and claudin-1. Specifically, the expression levels of ZO-1 and claudin-1 in the colon tissues of mice treated with BL@Map@Alg were 2.03-fold and 1.71-fold higher, respectively, compared to the DSS group (Fig. 7E–G). Importantly, there was no statistically significant difference in the expression levels of ZO-1 and claudin-1 between the BL@Map@Alg group and the control group (P > 0.05), indicating that the bio-coating formulation was able to restore barrier function to baseline levels, similar to healthy, untreated mice. Further analysis of the localization of ZO-1 and claudin-1 proteins revealed their predominant localization at the outer cell membranes of the intestinal epithelial cells, especially within the mucosal layer (Fig. 7D–F). This membrane localization is critical for the function of tight junctions, as it ensures the sealing of intercellular spaces and prevents the infiltration of harmful substances into the intestinal mucosa. The restoration of ZO-1 and claudin-1 expression, along with their proper membrane localization in BL@Map@Alg-treated mice, suggests that the bio-coating formulation not only enhances the expression of these tight junction proteins but also re-establishes the integrity of the intestinal barrier at the cellular level. These results strongly support the role of BL@Map@Alg in restoring intestinal barrier function in DSS-induced colitis. By upregulating the expression of ZO-1 and claudin-1 and promoting their proper localization at the tight junctions, BL@Map@Alg can effectively mitigates DSS-induced intestinal barrier disruption. This restoration of the mechanical barrier likely reduces intestinal permeability, preventing the entry of pathogenic microorganisms and antigens into the mucosa, thereby mitigating immune activation and inflammation [[56], [57], [58]].

Further analysis of specific bacterial genera revealed important shifts in the relative abundance of pathogenic and beneficial microorganisms. Escherichia, a genus containing pathogenic species that produce toxins and contribute to intestinal inflammation and diarrhea, was significantly reduced after BL@Map@Alg treatment. Conversely, Akkermansia, a beneficial genus within Verrucomicrobiota, exhibited a substantial increase following BL@Map@Alg treatment, thereby contributing to improved gut health by enhancing intestinal barrier integrity, reducing inflammation, and modulating the immune response [60]. To further explore the effects of BL@Map@Alg on the gut microbiota, principal component analysis (PCA) and principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) were performed to assess the overall microbial composition among different treatment groups. The results showed distinct differences between the groups, with the BL@Map@Alg group closely resembling the control group in microbial diversity and composition (Fig. S16). Conversely, the BL@Map@Alg group showed improved pathways related to translation and digestive system, indicating restored microbial activity and enhanced host-microbiota interactions in the digestive system. These findings are promising, as they demonstrate that the bio-coating formulation has the potential to restore normal intestinal function by promoting the growth of beneficial bacteria and suppressing the colonization of pathogenic microorganisms.

Beyond its effects on the overall gut microbiota, we also aimed to examine the colonization of BL in the mouse intestine following bio-coating treatment. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) experiments using Texas Red-labeled BL probes revealed that the bio-coating significantly enhanced the colonization of BL in the intestinal tissues (Fig. 8E). This indicates that the bio-coating not only protects BL from the harsh conditions of the gastrointestinal tract but also facilitates its adherence to the intestinal epithelium, allowing it to establish a more stable and long-term presence in the gut. To quantitatively assess the effect of the bio-coating on BL colonization, a standard curve was generated using RT-qPCR targeting the gyrA gene of BL (Fig. S17). Quantitative analysis revealed a 6.2-fold increase in BL colonization in the intestines of BL@Map@Alg-treated mice compared to untreated mice (Fig. 8F). This further confirms that the bio-coating significantly enhances BL colonization and persistence in the gut, which is crucial for its long-term therapeutic effects. Effective probiotic colonization is critical for the sustained release of beneficial metabolites, such as SCFAs, which contribute to intestinal health and help to maintain the gut barrier [61,62]. Thus, enhanced BL colonization facilitated by the bio-coating plays a pivotal role in the long-term efficacy of BL@Map@Alg treatment.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed a rapid and efficient bio-coating strategy for probiotics, demonstrating its potential for oral treatment of colitis in mice. In vitro studies revealed that the Map coating conferred ultra-high adhesion to probiotics, along with enhanced tolerance to gastrointestinal fluids and oxygen, as well as excellent biocompatibility. Meanwhile, the bio-coated probiotic effectively inhibits the expression of inflammatory factors in inflammatory cells. In vivo, bio-coated probiotics synergistically diminished colitis in mice by enhancing colonization, modulating the gut microbiota, repairing the intestinal barrier, and reducing the secretion of inflammatory factors. This simple and versatile bio-coating strategy holds promise as a universal approach for anaerobic probiotics, enabling personalized probiotic-mediated therapy and precision medicine. It also facilitates the discovery and application of bioactive agents leveraging probiotic properties. Importantly, further clinical investigations are warranted to explore the long-term efficacy and safety of this formulation in human patients.
