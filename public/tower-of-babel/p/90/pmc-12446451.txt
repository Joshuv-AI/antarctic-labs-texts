Diagnostic and prognostic roles of circulating EVs circ_PPAPDC1A in ICIs resistance in NSCLC
Authors: Fang-wen Zou, Yi-fang Tang, Lei-yi Zhang, Shun Xu, Shi-Ze Yang, Wen-Ya Li, Zheng-hua Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractICIs resistance presents a significant challenge in clinical practice. Circulating extracellular vesicles circRNAs are promising biomarkers for cancer diagnosis and treatment. This study aimed to determine the oncogenic role of circulating extracellular vesicles circ_PPAPDC1A in ICIs resistance diagnosis and prognostic analysis of NSCLC. We used a co-culture system of NSCLC cells and T cells, and a mice xenograft assay, to evaluate the effect of circ_PPAPDC1A on anti-PD-1 antitumor activity. A multicenter cohort study of 40 patients analyzed the associations between extracellular vesicles circ_PPAPDC1A and ICIs resistance. In vitro, circ_PPAPDC1A reduced the sensitivity of NSCLC cells to ICIs by promoting proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis. In vivo, circ_PPAPDC1A promoted NSCLC progression and induced CD8+ T-cell exhaustion. Clinical samples showed circ_PPAPDC1A upregulation in NSCLC patients with ICIs resistance, high diagnostic efficiency (AUC = 0.98), and higher expression associated with shorter PFS. Circulating extracellular vesicles circ_PPAPDC1A may serve as a predictive biomarker for ICIs resistance diagnosis and prognostic analysis.Subject terms: Tumour biomarkers, Cancer therapy

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is currently one of the most prevalent and deadliest malignancies globally1. Over 70% of NSCLC patients are already in advanced stages at the time of diagnosis, and first-line chemotherapy regimens based on platinum compounds have an efficacy rate of only 20%-30%, often leading to treatment failure2,3. While the in-depth study of lung cancer gene profiling and the rapid development of targeted therapies have ushered in the era of precise targeted treatment for NSCLC4–6, more than 70% of NSCLC patients with non-driver mutations do not benefit from targeted therapy. In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors, with programmed death 1/ligand 1 (PD-1/L1) as a representative, have achieved success in various tumors, including non-small cell lung cancer7,8, significantly improving the five-year survival rate for advanced NSCLC patients from less than 5% to 31.9%9. The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has brought about a revolutionary change in the diagnosis and treatment and significantly improved the survival rates of advanced NSCLC patients.

However, like other anti-tumor drugs, almost all NSCLC patients who initially respond to ICIs eventually experience disease progression or relapse after a period of immune therapy, leading to the development of ICIs resistance10. Currently, there is no unified standard for the definition and classification of ICIs resistance. An expert consensus led by A.J. Schoenfeld11 divides the diagnostic criteria for ICIs resistance into several components, including treatment type, depth of remission, duration of remission, and treatment continuity, which is the current mainstream definition of ICIs resistance. This definition differs from the SITC definition of immune therapy resistance12 and is limited to advanced NSCLC. Notably, it differs from the SITC definition in the following ways11: 1) Objective remission after ICIs treatment, excluding disease stability; 2) Disease progression within six months after the last ICIs treatment; 3) No need for imaging confirmation of progression after previous remission. Currently, there is a lack of highly sensitive biomarkers for ICIs resistance in clinical practice, primarily relying on imaging examinations. However, imaging examinations often lag significantly behind the actual development of resistance, and the presence of pseudo-progression in tumors renders imaging assessment of ICIs resistance of limited value.

Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) are a category of RNA transcribed from the genome but not encoding proteins13,14. They exert biological functions at the post- transcriptional level. Currently, several ncRNAs have been engineered as therapeutics for clinical applications in disease diagnosis and treatment. Circular RNA (circRNA) is a more recently discovered type of ncRNA molecule formed by back-splicing of precursor RNA transcripts, resulting in a covalently closed circular structure lacking the 5’-cap and 3’-poly(A) tail. Importantly, circRNAs are resistant to RNA exonucleases and are not easily degraded15,16. They play a crucial regulatory role in genome stability and the development of tumors. Differing from linear ncRNA molecules like miRNAs, circRNAs exhibit features such as widespread distribution, structural stability, and sequence conservation. Consequently, circRNAs are currently regarded as promising molecular markers and therapeutic targets in the field of cancer diagnosis and treatment17,18.

Extracellular vesicles are a type of vesicular body found in bodily fluids, characterized by a phospholipid bilayer membrane structure, formed through the fusion of cell membranes and multivesicular bodies (MVB)19. Extracellular vesicles can be released by various cell types, including B cells, T cells, dendritic cells, epithelial cells, stem cells, and tumor cells. Due to their small size, they can effectively evade phagocytosis by monocytes and macrophages20. Furthermore, extracellular vesicles can freely traverse vascular barriers and the extracellular matrix, making them widely distributed in bodily fluids such as blood, urine, breast milk, cerebrospinal fluid, and serous cavity fluids. They serve as crucial carriers for intercellular communication, influencing various biological processes and participating in the circulation of biomolecules21. Tumor-derived circulating extracellular vesicles are small membrane vesicles released by tumor cells into the extracellular microenvironment, serving as critical mediators for intercellular communication within the tumor microenvironment. circRNA is the primary component carried by tumor-derived extracellular vesicles and serves as the most crucial and efficient vehicle for circRNA intercellular transfer. Furthermore, an increasing body of research has also revealed that tumor-derived extracellular vesicles circRNA exhibits a dual role, acting as both an extracellular vesicles carrier for functional delivery and a regulator of circRNA epigenetic control22. As a result, it stands as one of the most promising biomarkers and therapeutic targets in current cancer diagnostics and treatment23.

Tumor- derived extracellular vesicles carry a wealth of biomacromolecules, including nucleic acids (DNA, mRNA, ncRNAs, etc.), proteins, lipids, and other biological entities, primarily sourced from parent tumor cells. These molecules are enveloped and protected by the extracellular vehicles (EVs)’s lipid membrane, maintaining their original stability and biological functionality23,24. Among these, circular RNAs (circRNAs) are the principal components carried by tumor-derived extracellular vesicles, representing the most significant and efficient vehicles for circRNA intercellular transfer. Moreover, an increasing body of research has unveiled the dual nature of tumor-derived extracellular vesicles circRNAs, demonstrating their roles in both extracellular vesicles functional transfer and epigenetic regulation25. They exhibit high stability, strong specificity, abundant content, non-invasive detectability, and repeatability26. As a pivotal constituent of the tumor microenvironment, tumor-derived extracellular vesicles circRNAs play a crucial role in tumor development, progression, and resistance processes. They are currently regarded as the most promising biomarkers and therapeutic targets in the realm of cancer diagnosis and treatment27.

Our preliminary study28 revealed high expression of circ_PPAPDC1A in Osimertinib resistance NSCLC patients, implicating it as one of the significant contributors to Osimertinib resistance. Five NSCLC patients were enrolled in the study, and they underwent second-line standard treatment after developing resistance, which involved ICIs immunotherapy combined with pemetrexed and carboplatin chemotherapy. Clinical practice indicated a negative correlation between circ_PPAPDC1A expression and the response to ICIs. We hypothesized that circ_PPAPDC1A might be associated with ICIs resistance. To validate our hypothesis, we established cell models with circ_PPAPDC1A overexpression and knockdown, along with a co-culture system with T cells to investigate the effect of circ_PPAPDC1A on ICIs sensitivity and its influence on CD8+T cells. To further substantiate the potential value of circ_PPAPDC1A as an early diagnostic biomarker for resistance, we initiated a multicenter cohort study to observe the correlation between circulating extracellular vesicles circ_PPAPDC1A and ICIs resistance, as well as its impact on survival prognosis.

Results

Higher circ_PPAPDC1A expression is associated with a poorer response to ICIs

In our preliminary study, we collected tissue samples from five pairs of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients before and after acquiring resistance to Osimertinib. Using high-throughput Arraystar circRNA microarray analysis, we identified differentially expressed circRNAs associated with Osimertinib resistance and established a differential expression profile for these circRNAs. The cluster heat map (Fig. 1A) presented the top ten most upregulated and downregulated circRNAs in Osimertinib-resistance NSCLC.

Establishment of a co-culture system of NSCLC cells and T cells

Lentiviral vector-mediated circ_PPAPDC1A overexpression (circ_PPAPDC1A OE) and circ_PPAPDC1A short hairpin RNA (shRNA) (sh-circ_PPAPDC1A) and corresponding negative control (NC) (OE-NC/sh-NC) were synthesized and were constructed by Genomeditech (Shanghai, China). The circ_PPAPDC1A OE and sh-circ_PPAPDC1A lentiviral vectors, along with their corresponding NC lentiviral vectors (OE-NC/sh-NC), were transfected into A549 and SK-MES-1 cells, respectively. This transfection was performed using the Lipofectamine3000TM assay (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The blank lentiviral vectors were utilized as negative controls. The transfection efficiency of each group was confirmed through RT-qPCR (P < 0.05; Fig. 2A). CD8+ T cells obtained from six healthy donors were co-cultured with A549 and SK-MES-1 cells at a 1:1 ratio for 24 h, successfully establishing a co-culture system of NSCLC cells and CD8+ T cells for subsequent experiments (Fig. 2B).

circ_PPAPDC1A promotes cell proliferation and clonogenesis in vitro

To investigate the impact of circ_PPAPDC1A on the sensitivity of NSCLC cells to ICIs, cells from each group were exposed to various concentrations of Pembrolizumab (0 µM, 0.25 µM, 0.5 µM, 0.75 µM, 1.0 µM) in vitro. The results showed a significant increase in cell viability in the circ_PPAPDC1A OE group. In contrast, the sh-circ_PPAPDC1A group exhibited a significant decrease in cell viability compared to its corresponding NC group (P < 0.05; Fig. 2C). Subsequently, soft agar colony formation assays were employed to assess the influence of circ_PPAPDC1A on cell clonogenesis after treatment with 0.5 µM Pembrolizumab for 48 h. The results demonstrated a significant increase in the number of cell colonies in the circ_PPAPDC1A OE group and a significant decrease in the sh-circ_PPAPDC1A group compared to their respective NC groups (P < 0.05; Fig. 2D). These findings suggest that in the T-cell co-culture system, circ_PPAPDC1A can reduce the sensitivity of NSCLC cells to ICIs, thereby promoting cell proliferation and clonogenesis of NSCLC cells in vitro.

circ_PPAPDC1A promotes cell invasion and migration in vitro

Additionally, Matrigel Invasion and Wound Healing assays were utilized to evaluate the impact of circ_PPAPDC1A on cell invasion and migration after treatment with 0.5 µM Pembrolizumab for 48 h. The results demonstrated a significant increase in both cell invasion and migration activities in the circ_PPAPDC1A OE group. Conversely, the sh-circ_PPAPDC1A group exhibited a significant reduction in both cell invasion and migration activities compared to their corresponding NC group (P < 0.05; Fig. 3A, B). These results suggest that in the T-cell co-culture system, circ_PPAPDC1A can diminish the sensitivity of NSCLC cells to ICIs, thereby promoting cell invasion and migration of NSCLC cells in vitro.

circ_PPAPDC1A inhibits cell apoptosis in vitro

Furthermore, Annexin V-FITC/PI Staining and Caspase-3 assays were utilized to assess the effect of circ_PPAPDC1A on cell apoptosis after a 48 h treatment with 0.5 µM Pembrolizumab. The results indicated a significant reduction in the apoptosis rate in the circ_PPAPDC1A OE group, while a significant increase was observed in the sh-circ_PPAPDC1A group compared to their respective control groups (P < 0.05; Fig. 4A).

A Annexin V-FITC/PI Staining and (B) Caspase-3 assays. n = 3. P < 0.05, **P < 0.01. OE OverExpression, shRNA short hairpin RNA, NC negative control.

Caspases, a family of proteolytic enzymes characterized by cysteinyl-aspartic acid proteases, play a pivotal role in apoptosis. Activated Caspases can cleave specific intracellular proteins, ultimately triggering the process of programmed cell death. Our Caspase-3 assays provided further evidence of circ_PPAPDC1A inhibitory effect on cell apoptosis. These assays demonstrated a noteworthy decrease in Caspase-3 activity in the circ_PPAPDC1A OE group, while a significant increase was observed in the sh-circ_PPAPDC1A group (P < 0.05; Fig. 4B). These findings indicate that in the T-cell co-culture system, circ_PPAPDC1A can reduce the sensitivity of NSCLC cells to ICIs, thereby inhibiting apoptosis in NSCLC cells in vitro.

circ_PPAPDC1A promotes NSCLC progression in vivo

To assess the impact of circ_PPAPDC1A on the effectiveness of in vivo anti-PD-1 therapy, a xenograft experiment was conducted using C57BL/6 mice. The mice were randomly divided into four groups using a double-blind approach and received weekly intravenous injections of an anti-PD-1 antibody through the tail vein. After 4 weeks, the animals were euthanized and xenograft samples were collected, and tumor sizes were calculated using the formula: volume (mm3) = [width2 (mm2) × length (mm)]/2. A comparative analysis with the control group revealed significant increases in the tumor growth rate, tumor weight, and tumor volume in the circ_PPAPDC1A OE group, while these parameters notably decreased in the sh-circ_PPAPDC1A group (P < 0.05; Fig. 5A–C).

Additionally, RT-qPCR assay validated a notable upregulation of circ_PPAPDC1A in xenograft tumors of circ_PPAPDC1A OE group and a significant downregulation in the sh-circ_PPAPDC1A group (P < 0.05; Fig. 5D). Ki-67 staining assays showed a significant increase in the percentage of Ki-67-positive cells in the circ_PPAPDC1A OE group and a decrease in the sh-circ_PPAPDC1A group (P < 0.05; Fig. 5E). Furthermore, TUNEL apoptosis assays performed with xenograft tumor samples demonstrated that circ_PPAPDC1A overexpression inhibited apoptosis in A549 cells, whereas circ_PPAPDC1A silencing promoted apoptosis in SK-MES-1 cells in vivo (P < 0.05; Fig. 5F). These findings align with the in vitro results, suggesting that circ_PPAPDC1A can reduce the sensitivity of NSCLC cells to ICIs, thus promoting NSCLC progression in vivo.

circ_PPAPDC1A induces CD8+ T-cell exhaustion in vivo

To investigate the role of circ_PPAPDC1A in the tumor immune response, we established stable cells with circ_PPAPDC1A OE and circ_PPAPDC1A-shRNA using lentiviral vectors and implanted them subcutaneously into C57BL/6 mice. All mice received weekly intravenous injections of an anti-PD-1 antibody via the tail vein. After 4 weeks, all mice were anesthetized, and aseptic procedures were followed to collect both serum and xenograft samples. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis was conducted on subcutaneous tumors to assess CD4 and CD8 expression. Flow cytometric analysis was performed to study immune cell populations within the subcutaneous tumors.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis of the subcutaneous tumors showed no significant difference in CD4 expression among the groups (P > 0.05; Fig. 6A, B). However, overexpression of circ_PPAPDC1A was associated with reduced numbers of CD8+ cells, whereas knockdown of circ_PPAPDC1A resulted in an increase in CD8+ cell counts (P < 0.05; Fig. 6C) and a negative correlation between circ_PPAPDC1A expression and CD8 cell numbers was observed in the subcutaneous tumors (P < 0.05; Fig. 6D). Additionally, flow cytometric analysis of immune cells within subcutaneous tumors showed that circ_PPAPDC1A overexpression significantly reduced the rate of CD8+ T cells and IFN-γ+ cells. Conversely, knockdown of circ_PPAPDC1A significantly increased the rate of CD8+ T cells and IFN-γ+ T cells (P < 0.05; Fig. 6E/G). A negative correlation between circ_PPAPDC1A expression and CD8 + T cells and IFN-γ+ cells rate was observed in the subcutaneous tumors (P < 0.05; Fig. 6F/H).

Furthermore, we conducted quantitative analyses of various cytokines and chemokines in the serum using Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) to assess the secretion function of CD8+ T cells. Interestingly, ELISA results indicated that immune-boosting factors, such as CXCL9, CXCL10, IFN-γ, and IL-2, were significantly decreased in mice with circ_PPAPDC1A overexpression, while immune inhibitory factors, like TGF-β and IL-1β, were activated. In contrast, CXCL9, CXCL10, IFN-γ, and IL-2 were significantly activated, and TGF-β and IL-1β were significantly decreased in mice with circ_PPAPDC1A knockdown (P < 0.05; Fig. 6I, J). Therefore, our findings suggest that circ_PPAPDC1A promotes the progression of NSCLC by inducing CD8+ T-cell exhaustion.

circ_PPAPDC1A existed in circulating extracellular vesicles and derives from NSCLC cells

In the clinical validation of circ_PPAPDC1A expression among NSCLC patients resistant to ICIs, considering the challenges associated with obtaining tumor tissue samples, we opted to use extracellular vesicles as an alternative medium for sample collection. Extracellular vesicles were selected for their ease of sample acquisition, remarkable stability, strong specificity, non-invasive detection, and robust reproducibility. It’s worth noting that extracellular vesicles can originate from various cell types. To confirm whether circ_PPAPDC1A exists in circulating extracellular vesicles and originates from NSCLC cells, we established cell models with circ_PPAPDC1A overexpression and circ_PPAPDC1A knock-down for subsequent experiments. Extracellular vesicles in the cell culture medium (CCM) of each group were isolated using the ExoQuick method, and the total RNA from extracellular vesicles and CCM was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit. The results from the RT-qPCR assay showed that extracellular vesicles circ_PPAPDC1A was significantly upregulated in the circ_PPAPDC1A OE group and significantly downregulated in the sh-circ_PPAPDC1A group (P < 0.05; Fig. 7A), indicating that extracellular vesicles circ_PPAPDC1A can be secreted by NSCLC cells. Moreover, circ_PPAPDC1A expression levels in extracellular vesicles were nearly equivalent to those in CCM (P > 0.05; Fig. 7B), implying that extracellular circRNA_102481 is contained within extracellular vesicles and circ_PPAPDC1A is primarily secreted into CCM in the form of extracellular vesicles.

To confirm whether circ_PPAPDC1A is secreted through packaging into extracellular vesicles, we examined the extracellular circ_PPAPDC1A expression level after treatment with ribonuclease (RNase). As demonstrated in Fig. 7C, circ_PPAPDC1A in CCM was minimally affected by treatment with RNase alone, but its levels significantly decreased when treated with RNase and Triton X-100 (which can dissolve lipids and disrupt membrane structures) simultaneously (P < 0.05; Fig. 7C), suggesting that extracellular circ_PPAPDC1A is protected by a membrane rather than being directly secreted into the extracellular medium. These findings suggest that circ_PPAPDC1A is predominantly secreted by cancer cells via extracellular vesicles.

Circulating extracellular vesicles circ_PPAPDC1A serve as biomarker for ICIs resistance diagnosis and prognostic analysis

A total of 40 NSCLC patients who received ICIs treatment were included in our study. Serum circulating extracellular vesicles were extracted from these 40 patients both before and after the development of ICIs resistance by Total EVs Isolation Kit. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to observe the morphology of the EVs. And the expression of surface proteins CD63 and CD81 was detected by Western blot. Under TEM, the EVs appeared as light gray, round or oval structures of varying sizes and uneven distribution (Supplemental Fig. 2A), with diameters ranging from 40 to 180 nm (Supplemental Fig. 2B). The Western blot results show that the EVs marker proteins CD63 and TSG101 are highly expressed in EVs both before and after ICIs resistance, while the negative marker protein Calnexin is not detected in EVs either before or after ICIs resistance (Supplemental Fig. 2C). RT-qPCR analysis revealed a significant upregulation of circulating extracellular vesicles circ_PPAPDC1A in NSCLC patients with ICIs resistance (P < 0.05; Fig. 8A, B) compared to the matched extracellular vesicles with ICIs sensitive. Additionally, in order to assess the diagnostic value of circulating extracellular vesicles circ_PPAPDC1A in predicting ICIs resistance in NSCLC patients, we employed ROC curve analysis. Statistical analysis demonstrated that circulating extracellular vesicles circ_PPAPDC1A showed high diagnostic efficiency for ICIs resistance, with an AUC of 0.98 (P = 0.0001; Fig. 8C).

The association between circulating extracellular vesicles circ_PPAPDC1A and clinicopathological variables was analyzed using Fisher’s exact and χ2 tests. The median expression of circulating extracellular vesicles circ_PPAPDC1A was used as the cutoff value (presented in Fig. 8D). The results showed no significant correlation between the expression of circulating extracellular vesicles circ_PPAPDC1A and clinicopathological characteristics such as age, smoking status, histopathology, and pathological grading (P > 0.05; Fig. 8E). Furthermore, we used the Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank test to assess the progression-free survival (PFS) duration in patients with higher and lower circulating extracellular vesicles circ_PPAPDC1A expression. The results indicated that higher expression of circulating extracellular vesicles circ_PPAPDC1A was associated with a shorter PFS (6.15 mo. vs. 9.25 mo., P = 0.019; Fig. 8F). These findings suggest that circulating extracellular vesicles circ_PPAPDC1A may serve as a potential predictive biomarker for diagnosing ICIs resistance and for prognostic analysis in NSCLC patients.

Discussion

The mechanisms underlying of ICIs resistance are exceedingly complex. Ultimately, the development of resistance is attributed to various factors that result in the inability of the host immune system to activate innate immune responses. These factors include ineffective antigen presentation, aberrant T-cell activation, and the presence of inhibitory receptors and exhaustion in tumor-infiltrating T cells29. Intrinsic factors within the tumor30,31 encompass genetic mutations occurring in tumor cells, either inherently or as a consequence of therapy, which can disrupt the recognition and presentation of neoantigens and the activation of T cells. Mutations in genes such as JAK1 and JAK2, PTEN loss, and aberrant activation of the MAPK signaling pathway can all lead to abnormal IFN-γ and MHC expression levels, resulting in an unresponsive state to immunotherapy. External factors within the tumor microenvironment (TME)32,33 primarily pertain to the immune suppressive elements in the TME. Regulatory T cells (Tregs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), M2-type macrophages, and immunosuppressive molecules, as well as the activation of alternative immune checkpoint pathways like LAG-3, TIGIT, VISTA, TIM-3, all serve to inhibit the anti-tumor immune response, consequently affecting the effectiveness of ICIs in combating cancer34.

In current clinical practice, several common predictive biomarkers are used for lung cancer immunotherapy (ICIs), including programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1), tumor mutation burden (TMB), microsatellite instability (MSI)/mismatch repair (MMR), the immune microenvironment, serum markers, and genetic profiling, among others34. However, an efficient and sensitive biomarker for the accurate identification of ICI resistance remains elusive. As a result, we primarily rely on imaging techniques, such as CT/PET-CT scans and MRIs, to assess patient condition. However, this dependency on imaging-based methods comes with significant limitations32. Firstly, imaging examinations often lag behind the actual development of resistance because they typically only reflect changes in tumor size and do not capture alterations at the molecular level. Secondly, pseudo-progression, where tumors appear to grow on imaging even when patients remain responsive to ICIs, is a common occurrence, leading to misdiagnoses and unnecessary therapeutic interventions. As a result, although imaging plays a crucial role in cancer management, its diagnostic value in determining acquired resistance to ICIs is severely restricted. Therefore, there is an urgent need to discover biomarkers that are more sensitive and specific. These biomarkers would enable the earlier detection and monitoring of resistance, thereby enhancing treatment decision-making and improving patient survival rates34.

Tumor-derived circulating extracellular vesicles are small membrane vesicles released by tumor cells into the extracellular microenvironment, serving as critical mediators for intercellular communication within the tumor microenvironment. They play essential regulatory roles in tumor initiation, growth, invasion, and metastasis19,23. Tumor- derived extracellular vesicles carry a wealth of biomacromolecules, including nucleic acids (DNA, mRNA, ncRNAs, etc.), proteins, lipids, and other biological entities, primarily sourced from parent tumor cells20. These molecules are enveloped and protected by the EVs’s lipid membrane, maintaining their original stability and biological functionality20,23. Among these, circular RNAs (circRNAs) are the principal components carried by tumor-derived extracellular vesicles, representing the most significant and efficient vehicles for circRNA intercellular transfer. Moreover, an increasing body of research has unveiled the dual nature of tumor-derived extracellular vesicles circRNAs, demonstrating their roles in both extracellular vesicles functional transfer and epigenetic regulation27. They exhibit high stability, strong specificity, abundant content, non-invasive detectability, and repeatability22. As a pivotal constituent of the tumor microenvironment, tumor-derived extracellular vesicles circRNAs play a crucial role in tumor development, progression, and resistance processes. They are currently regarded as the most promising biomarkers and therapeutic targets in the realm of cancer diagnosis and treatment26.

Studies35,36 have shown that tumor-derived extracellular vesicles circRNA regulation is a crucial regulatory network in the tumor microenvironment. It exhibits significant expression abnormalities in various solid tumors, including breast cancer, colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, liver cancer, bladder cancer, among others, and is involved in the initiation, development, and progression of these cancers. Current research23,37,38 also suggests that tumor-derived extracellular vesicles circRNA can play roles as oncogenes and tumor suppressors in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), participating in the progression of NSCLC and processes such as drug resistance. As a result, they serve as important diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for NSCLC. However, there is currently a lack of research regarding the relevance of tumor-derived extracellular vesicles circRNA in non-small cell lung cancer immune escape and ICIs-induced resistance. Exploring the predictive value of tumor-derived extracellular vesicles circRNA as a biomarker in ICIs-induced resistance remains a gap in current research.

The findings of our study shed light on the potential role of circ_PPAPDC1A and circulating extracellular vesicles as crucial players in the development of ICIs resistance in NSCLC. circ_PPAPDC1A (circRNA ID: hsa_circ_100696) originates from exons 3, 4, and 5 of the PPAPDC1A gene (NM_001030059), situated on chromosome 10, and spans 280 base pairs. While circ_100696 has limited existing research, some insights have been gathered from previous studies. William R39 noted that circ_100696 is abundantly expressed and conserved in human fibroblasts, with an association with ALU repetitive sequences. Agnieszka40 also reported that circ_100696 is abundantly and highly conserved, with dynamic expression in the mammalian brain. The coding gene for circ_PPAPDC1A is Phosphatidic Acid Phosphatase Type 2 Domain Containing 1A (PPAPDC1A), which is located on human chromosome 10q26.12. PPAPDC1A plays a crucial role in the activation of PKC and TPK, contributing to lipid metabolism and cell signal transduction pathways. Current research41 has shown that PPAPDC1A is highly expressed in various tumors and functions as an oncogene, promoting tumor initiation and development. However, no prior research has explored the role of circ_PPAPDC1A and the PPAPDC1A gene in ICIs resistance in NSCLC. The encoding of circ_PPAPDC1A by the PPAPDC1A gene may be one of the essential mechanisms through which it exerts its oncogenic effects. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report detailing the oncogenic role of circ_PPAPDC1A in NSCLC with ICIs resistance.

Additionally, the in vitro and in vivo experiments presented substantial evidence to suggest that circ_PPAPDC1A plays a pivotal role in diminishing the sensitivity of NSCLC cells to ICIs. This resistance mechanism primarily involves the promotion of cell proliferation, invasion, and migration, while concurrently inhibiting apoptosis. These findings elucidate the intricate interplay between circ_PPAPDC1A and the immune microenvironment within NSCLC, ultimately resulting in reduced ICI efficacy. Moreover, the in vivo mice xenograft model underscored the adverse impact of circ_PPAPDC1A in promoting NSCLC progression and inducing CD8+ T-cell exhaustion42,43. Its main manifestation is the reduced density of CD8+ TILs. Additionally, the numbers of CD8+ T cells and IFN-γ+ cells were diminished, and immune-boosting factors, such as CXCL9, CXCL10, IFN-γ, and IL-2, exhibited significant decreases in mice with circ_PPAPDC1A overexpression. In contrast, immune inhibitory factors like TGF-β and IL-1β were activated. This phenomenon suggests that circ_PPAPDC1A may create an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, thus preventing the immune system from mounting an effective antitumor response. The induction of T-cell exhaustion is a well-recognized barrier to successful cancer immunotherapy, and our study provides additional insight into one of the potential mechanisms underlying this phenomenon. Furthermore, the clinical analysis of EVs samples from NSCLC patients confirmed that circulating extracellular vesicles containing circ_PPAPDC1A are significantly upregulated in patients with ICIs resistance. Furthermore, these extracellular vesicles demonstrated high diagnostic efficiency, as evidenced by the substantial area under the curve (AUC) value. The association between increased extracellular vesicles circ_PPAPDC1A expression and shorter PFS emphasizes the clinical relevance of this biomarker. These results highlight the potential utility of circ_PPAPDC1A as a predictive biomarker for ICIs resistance diagnosis and prognostic analysis.

The identification of circ_PPAPDC1A as a key factor in ICIs resistance may have significant implications for the treatment of NSCLC. It implies that targeting circ_PPAPDC1A or modulating its effects may be a promising approach to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs in NSCLC patients. Additionally, the use of circulating extracellular vesicles as a biomarker may allow for the early identification of patients who are likely to develop resistance to ICIs, enabling more personalized treatment strategies. This study opens the door to further research avenues. Future investigations could focus on the underlying molecular mechanisms by which circ_PPAPDC1A exerts its effects and explore potential therapeutic interventions to counteract its influence. Moreover, larger-scale clinical studies are warranted to validate the diagnostic and prognostic value of circulating extracellular vesicles in a broader patient population.

However, there are several limitations to our study. First, our study included only 40 patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), resulting in a relatively small sample size. Additionally, the screening criteria for enrolled patients did not adequately account for various other clinical and treatment-related factors, which may have introduced bias into the analysis. Second, the median value was used as the cutoff to analyze the impact of circ_PPAPDC1A expression on clinicopathological features and prognosis; this cutoff has not been validated in an independent cohort, which may affect the generalizability of the results. Third, due to the difficulty in obtaining tumor tissue specimens, our study relied primarily on circulating extracellular vesicles (cEVs), and we were unable to assess PD-L1 expression levels (TPS score) or baseline CD8 + T-cell infiltration in matched tissue samples. This represents an important gap that requires further investigation. Fourth, our cohort consisted entirely of patients with stage IVb NSCLC, with a relatively high proportion of brain metastases (35%), which differs from the patient characteristics in landmark trials such as KEYNOTE-189 (where brain metastases occurred in 17.8% of patients in the Pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy arm). These cohort-specific features may have contributed to differences in survival outcomes compared to other studies, introducing potential bias. Fifth, our animal experiments utilized a subcutaneous tumor model rather than an orthotopic model. We acknowledge that orthotopic models more accurately simulate the in vivo tumor microenvironment and immune responses, and the use of subcutaneous models may have affected the reliability of results related to CD4+ and CD8 + T cells and associated immune factors. Sixth, and most notably, the molecular mechanism by which circ_PPAPDC1A mediates T-cell inhibition—including its downstream genetic or molecular targets in immune cells—remains underexplored. This limits the mechanistic depth of our current findings. To address these limitations in future work, we plan to: 1) expand the sample size and implement more stringent patient screening and stratification to minimize confounding factors; 2) validate the optimal cutoff value for circ_PPAPDC1A expression in larger, independent cohorts; 3) prioritize the collection of tumor tissue specimens to investigate PD-L1 expression, CD8 + T-cell infiltration, and their potential correlation with circ_PPAPDC1A levels; 4) establish orthotopic tumor models to better elucidate the role of circ_PPAPDC1A in T-cell exhaustion; 5) extend our investigations to explore the impact of circ_PPAPDC1A on T-cell exhaustion in human subjects; and 6) conduct in-depth mechanistic studies to identify the downstream genetic and molecular targets of circ_PPAPDC1A in immune cells, thereby clarifying its role in mediating T-cell inhibition.

In conclusion, our study provides valuable insights into the mechanisms of ICIs resistance in NSCLC. For the first time, we have identified that circ_PPAPDC1A reduces the sensitivity of NSCLC to ICIs by inducing T-cell exhaustion. Circulating extracellular vesicles circ_PPAPDC1A serve as a predictive biomarker for ICIs resistance diagnosis and prognostic analysis. These findings have the potential to impact the development of more effective treatment strategies for NSCLC patients and improve the clinical management of ICIs resistance. Further research in this area may lead to novel therapeutic approaches and improved patient outcomes.

Methods

Cells

We selected ICIs-sensitive A549 and SK-MES-1 NSCLC cell lines without dominant driver gene mutations such as EGFR or ALK for our study. The A549 and SK-MES-1 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). A549 cells refer to a human lung adenocarcinoma cell line with wild-type EGFR and K-ras mutant, while SK-MES-1 is a human lung squamous cell carcinoma cell line with no dominant driver gene mutations. Both of these cell lines are sensitive to ICIs. Cell lines were cultured in RPMI 1640 with 10% FBS and 100 μL/mL penicillin and streptomycin.

Clinical extracellular vesicles samples

The corresponding circulating extracellular vesicles from forty NSCLC patients before and after ICIs resistance were collected from The Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University, and approved by the institutional review board of Medical Ethics Committee of The Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (approval No. LYEC2024-0089) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants signed informed consent after told study details. All forty NSCLC patients without driver gene mutations received first-line treatment, including ICIs immunotherapy combined with platinum-based chemotherapy. Clinical efficacy assessment (RECIST 1.1)44 for apparent disease progression (PD) was comprehensively considered to ICIs resistance. The methods and procedures for patient circulating extracellular vesicles collection comply with the relevant regulations and requirements of the Medical Ethics Committee in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was obtained from all patients. Patient confidentiality was strictly maintained. All patient data were anonymized, and only de-identified information was used for analysis. Follow-up data were ended by March 2023, and the median follow-up time was 10 months (range 4–18 months). The basic clinical and pathological data of the patients are detailed in Supplemental Table I. The chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables. The Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank test were used to evaluate PFS. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression models were fit to assess the association between PFS and patient characteristics. Extracellular vesicles from the serum of NSCLC patients and culture medium were isolated using ExoQuick Exo-some Precipitation Solution according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Extraction of extracellular vesicles and observation by transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Circulating extracellular vesicles in serum (6 mL) were isolated by the ExoQuick method. Centrifuge the sample at 3000 × g for 15 min and take the supernatant. After filtering with 0.22-mm PVDF filter, add the precipitant, mix and shake, store in a refrigerator at 4 °C for 30 min, and centrifuge at 1500 × g for 5 min. Discard the supernatant Precipitate in 100 ML PBS and place in a constant temperature refrigerator at 80 °C. Extracellular vesicles were collected from the pellet and re-suspended in PBS. About 10-20 μL sample was dropped on the carbon grid for 1 min. The droplet was sucked off with filter paper and contrasted with 2% uranyl acetate. Images were obtained with TEM. The size distribution and concentration of exosomes were analyzed by nanoparticle-tracking analysis. The extracellular vesicles-related marker protein of CD63 and TSG101 were detected by western-blotting assay.

Cell transfection

Lentiviral vector-mediated circ_PPAPDC1A overexpression (circ_PPAPDC1A OE) and circ_PPAPDC1A short hairpin RNA (shRNA) (sh- circ_PPAPDC1A) and corresponding negative control (NC) (OE-NC/sh-NC) were synthesized and were constructed by Genomeditech (Shanghai, China). Cell transfection was performed using Lipofectamine3000TM reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, cells were seeded in 6-well plates, and a mixture of Lipofectamine3000TM (5 μL) and oligonucleotides (0.75 μg) was added for an 8-hour incubation. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with fresh complete culture medium and incubated for an additional 24 h. Cells were then harvested for further experiments.

Reverse transcription-quantitative (RT-q) PCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol® reagent and reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT-PCR Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Custom-synthesized primer sequences were used for circRNAs, miRNA, and mRNA. GAPDH served as the internal reference for circRNAs and mRNA, while U6 was the internal reference for miRNA. The thermocycling conditions included an initial step at 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles at 94 °C for 15 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 70 °C for 30 s. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCq method.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cells were seeded at 2000 cells/well in a 96- well plate and treated with Pembrolizumab (0.0–1.0 μM) for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed by measuring the absorbance at 492 nm after incubation with CCK-8 solution, and the formula Cell Viability = (Exp. group OD490-Blank control OD490) / (Neg. control OD490 - Blank control OD490) × 100% was used. The experiment was repeated three times.

Soft Agar Colony Formation Assay

Cells were embedded in 0.7% agarose on top of a 1.2% agarose layer in culture medium. Pembrolizumab (0.5 μM) was added every 3 days. After 14 days, visible colonies were stained with crystal violet, and colony efficiency was calculated as (Amount of Clones/Number of Planted Cells) × 100%. The experiment was repeated three times.

Matrigel Invasion Assay

All cells (5 × 104) were digested and then resuspended. Cells in each group were transferred to the upper cross-pore compartment (without FBS) and lower cross-pore compartment (containing 20% FBS). The cells in the upper chambers were removed by a swab. All cells fixed and stained with crystal violet (2.5%) were counted and imaged by a microscope (Nikon).

Wound Healing Assay

Cell migration was evaluated by measuring the movement of cells within a created gap using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip. Following the scratch, cells were washed twice and cultured in media containing 0.1% FBS to minimize the impact of cell proliferation. The progress of wound closure was documented at 0 and 48 h using a ×5 objective lens.

Annexin V-FITC/PI Staining Assay

Cell apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V- Fluorescein Isothiocyanate (FITC)/Propidium Iodide (PI) Apoptosis Detection Kit from BD Biosciences, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were exposed to 10 mM TAM or ethanol as a control for 48 h, stained with FITC and PI, and subsequently subjected to analysis through fluorescence-activated cell sorting using a FACS Calibur instrument from BD Biosciences. The data pertaining to cell apoptosis were processed and analyzed using FlowJo 7.6.1 software.

Western-blot Assay

Proteins extracted from extracellular vesicles or cells (50–80 μg) using RIPA lysis buffer were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were initially blocked with a 5% non-fat milk blocking buffer for 1 h in a horizontal position. Subsequently, they were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and then with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the membranes were scanned using an Odyssey scanner, and the protein band intensity was analyzed using Odyssey software.

CD4+ and CD8+ T cells Detection

CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells were obtained from cell supernatant by positive selection using magnetic cell separation according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Supplemental Table II) and detected by Immunohistochemical (IHC) (Supplemental Table III) and Flow cytometry (Supplemental Table IV).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was used to measure the concentrations of CXCL9, CXCL10, IFN-γ, IL-2, TGF-β, and IL-1β. The measurements were conducted using ELISA kits (Supplemental Table V) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Mice xenograft anti-PD-1 therapy in vivo

Experiments involving C57BL/6 mice received approval from our hospital’s Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee. The mice were housed in a pathogen-free environment. A total of 2 × 106 A549 or SK-MES-1 cells were subcutaneously implanted into the left flank of six-week-old C57BL/6 mice. The experiments were randomly assigned into four groups: OE-NC (A549), circ_PPAPDC1A OE (A549), sh-NC (SK-MES-1), and sh-circ_PPAPDC1A (SK-MES-1), and the investigators were blinded to the group assignment. Mice received intravenous injections of an anti-PD-1 antibody through the tail vein on a weekly basis. The anti-PD-1 antibody for mice was obtained from Bio X Cell (West Lebanon, NH, USA). Animals were euthanized either when tumors reached a maximum size of 1000 mm3, after 4 weeks, or before reaching a moribund state. The day of the first administration of therapy was considered as day 1. Survival time was defined as the period from the first therapy date to the date of sacrifice. Tumor sizes were calculated using the following formula: volume (mm3) = [width2 (mm2) × length (mm)]/2. The study was approved by the institutional review board of Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of The Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (approval No. 20231011).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed by SPSS V.24.0 software (SPSS). Variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or as number (percentage). The continuous variables were analyzed using Student’s t-test or One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni post-hoc analysis. The chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables. The Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank test were used to evaluate PFS. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression models were fit to assess the association between PFS and patient characteristics. All statistical tests were two sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
