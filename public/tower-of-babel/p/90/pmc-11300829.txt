Mechanism of arsenic removal using brown seaweed derived impregnated with iron oxide biochar for batch and column studies
Authors: Satesh Kumar Devrajani, Zubair Ahmed, Naveed Ahmed Qambrani, Sania Kanwal, Uma Maheswari Sundaram, Nabisab Mujawar Mubarak
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Water contaminated with arsenic presents serious health risks, necessitating effective and sustainable removal methods. This article proposes a method for removing arsenic from water by impregnating biochar with iron oxide (Fe2O3) from brown seaweed (Sargassum polycystum). After the seaweed biomass was pyrolyzed at 400 °C, iron oxide was added to the biochar to increase its adsorptive sites and surface functional groups, which allowed the binding of arsenic ions. Batch studies were conducted to maximize the effects of variables, including pH, contact time, arsenic concentration, and adsorbent dosage, on arsenic adsorption. The maximum arsenic adsorption efficiency of 96.7% was achieved under optimal conditions: pH 6, the adsorbent dosage of 100 mg, the initial arsenic concentration of 0.25 mg/L, and a contact time of 90 min. Langmuir and Freundlich’s isotherms favored the adsorption process, while the kinetics adhered to a pseudo-second-order model, indicating chemisorption as the controlling step. Column studies revealed complete saturation after 200 min, and the adsorption behavior fits both the Adams–Bohart and Thomas models, demonstrating the potential for large-scale application. The primary mechanism underlying the interaction between iron-modified biochar and arsenic ions is surface complexation, enhanced by increased surface area and porosity. This study highlights the significant contribution of iron-modified biochar derived from macroalgae as an effective and sustainable solution for arsenic removal from water.

Introduction

The arsenic contamination of water sources is significantly contributed through natural means like flooding, soil erosion, volcanic eruptions, and weathering of arsenic-containing minerals, as well as from human-caused activities such as uncontrolled agricultural, industrial, metallurgical, and mining practices1. This contamination is widespread globally, and in many developing nations, people still rely on arsenic-contaminated tube wells as their primary drinking water source. Despite the groundwater being safe apart from its arsenic content, the presence of this toxic substance poses severe health risks, including cancer, cardiovascular and skin disorders, and neurological damage2,3. To mitigate these risks, the WHO has set a stringent limit of 10 μg/L for allowable arsenic levels in potable water.

Arsenate and arsenite are the inorganic forms of arsenic found in natural waters. Arsenite is easily transformed into Arsenate in aerobic environments due to its thermodynamic instability. Thus, developing a dependable, effective, and affordable treatment method for eliminating arsenic from water and wastewater is crucial4.

Traditional methods of arsenic removal, including coagulation, oxidation, membrane filtration, reverse osmosis, ion exchange, etc., are effective but have restrictions regarding high production and maintenance costs, sludge generation, and energy requirements. Moreover, due to its uncomplicated, cost-effective, and self-renewing properties, the widespread utilization of biochar for adsorption purposes has become increasingly prevalent. The pyrolysis of organic matter produces biochar, a porous, carbon-rich material that has demonstrated promise as an economical, environmentally benign, and adsorbent for removing heavy metals5. The favorable conditions for the uptake of arsenic are provided by specific surface area and unique surface chemistry of biochar6.

Arsenic adsorption using various adsorbents made from sawdust, peanut shells, sugar cane, marine algae, wastewater sludge, and dairy manure has been thoroughly studied over the past decade and has demonstrated a significant potential for adsorption of heavy metals in water and wastewater7–9. Various algae species, collectively called “Seaweed/Macroalgae,” can be found in coastal areas or water bodies like oceans, lakes, and rivers. And serve essential roles as a food source and potential habitat for various marine organisms. Many countries, including Singapore, China, Japan, South Korea, and Vietnam, also consume macroalgae as a diet. Moreover, macroalgae possess versatile applications beyond their culinary use. They can be utilized to produce biochar for adsorption purposes. This potential makes macroalgae/seaweed a promising and sustainable option for addressing water treatment challenges and other industrial applications10. Commercial biochars have been used for contamination removal but have still not been identified as effective because arsenic species lack distinct surface functional groups. To address this, improved adsorption capabilities for removing pollutants, including arsenic, have been introduced through impregnation or surface modification of biochar derived from seaweed.

Iron is crucial in magnetic biochar because it imparts magnetic properties, facilitating its separation from water after adsorption processes. Combining biochar with magnetic materials, especially iron oxide (Fe2O3), has gained popularity for introducing iron alterations to biochar. This approach addresses challenging adsorbent recovery and simple loss during water treatment11. The physicochemical properties of biochar are modified by adding magnetic species, which makes magnetic biochar an effective and appropriate choice for eliminating contaminants. It combines the advantageous properties of both biochar and magnetic materials12. Several experiments have been conducted to improve separation after usage by modifying adsorbents with magnetic material to address this drawback13.

Nevertheless, to highlight the adsorption capacity of biochar, many magnetic biochar studies have been skewed for the analysis of organic components and anionic heavy metals. At the same time, there are few studies about the adsorption of cationic heavy metals. As a result, it is critical to assess how magnetic loading affects the capacity of the biochar to adsorb cationic heavy metals and to identify the ideal manufacturing parameters for magnetic biochar to maximize biochar recovery after usage and remove cationic heavy metals.

This study aims to produce a novel kind of magnetic biochar from abandoned marine macroalgae, namely Sargassum polycystum, a brown seaweed commonly found in Pakistan. The surface of the biochar is impregnated with iron oxide (Fe2O3) particles to give it magnetic traits. The study aims to eliminate arsenic, a cationic heavy metal. In particular, the objectives are to: (1) develop a biochar from brown seaweed/macroalgae (Sargassum polycystum) and impregnate it with iron oxide; (2) assess the physiochemical characteristics as well as the adsorption capacity of the iron oxide impregnated biochar by optimizing the adsorption influencing parameters.

Materials and method

Chemicals and reagents

A 1000 ppm standard solution (analytical grade, Sigma-Aldrich) was used to prepare the arsenic solution, and 0.1 M nitric acid (reagent grade, ≥ 65%, Fisher Scientific) and 0.1 M NaOH (reagent grade, ≥ 97%, Merck) were used to adjust the pH of the solutions. Iron sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO4·7H2O, analytical grade, ≥ 99%, Sigma-Aldrich) and Iron chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O, analytical grade, ≥ 99%, Sigma-Aldrich) were used to modify the biochar.

Analysis

An inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP-MS, PerkinElmer Nexion 350, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to detect arsenic. Hydrochloric acid (HCl) and ultra-pure nitric acid (HNO3) were used to digest the sample, which was then microwave-digested. Operating parameters included an RF power of 1400 W, a 15 L/min (argon) plasma gas flow rate, a 1.0 L/min (argon) auxiliary gas flow rate, and a 0.9 L/min (argon) nebulizer gas flow rate. Utilizing a cyclonic spray chamber and micromist nebulizer, the sample was introduced at a 0.5 mL/min rate. The sub-ppt range, or low ppt for method detection limits, was where the detection limits were found. There was no use of a chromatographic column. Accurate measurement was guaranteed by blanks, duplicates, spiked samples, and calibration curves with arsenic concentrations ranging from 0 to 200 ppb.

Synthesis of brown seaweed biochar

The process of preparing biochar and its iron modification is illustrated in Fig. 1. Brown seaweed/macroalgae (Sargassum polycystum) collected from Manora Island, Karachi, was first washed with distilled water and then allowed to dry for 7 days naturally. Subsequently, the seaweed was oven-dried for 3 h at a temperature of 120 °C. After the crushing of the dried seaweed, it was passed through a 600-µm sieve. The resulting material was loaded into a stainless-steel reactor and placed inside a muffle furnace for pyrolysis at a temperature of 400 °C for 2 h. During the pyrolysis, at the flow rate of 3.5 L/min, the nitrogen was purged into the reactor to prevent ignition14,15. This process yielded the brown seaweed carbonized biochar.Figure 1Synthesis and iron modification of the biochar.

Synthesis and iron modification of the biochar.

Iron modification of brown seaweed biochar

To create the magnetizing solution, 20 g of biochar or biomass were suspended in 200 mL of distilled water, then 11.1 g of FeSO4·7H2O and 20 g of FeCl3·6H2O were added to 600 mL of distilled water. To bring the pH of the mixed suspension down to about 10, NaOH (10 M) was added dropwise. After 1 h of boiling the mixed suspension at 80 °C, the biomass or biochar was removed using a magnet. The biomass, or biochar, was dried for 12 h at 70 °C after being rinsed three times with distilled water and ethanol16.

Characterization and surface morphology of biochar (BC) and iron modified biochar (Fe-BC)

Different physicochemical properties of the biochar and iron-modified biochar were analyzed to understand their role in adsorption. JEOL (Tokyo, Japan) scanning electron microscopy (SEM) examined the surface morphology of raw and iron-modified biochar. The BET surface area (SBET) was measured using an automated nitrogen adsorption analyzer (Micromeritics ASAP 2020N, USA). Energy dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis was also done to evaluate the elemental composition of the biochar and biochar treated with iron oxide. Similarly, the infrared spectra of raw biochar and iron-treated biochar were also acquired using a Shimadzu FTIR Spectrometer, operating in the 400–4000 cm−1 wavelength range with a resolution of 8.0 cm−1.

Batch experiments for the removal of arsenic

The batch adsorption experiments were performed using falcon tubes with a volume of 50 mL of arsenic solution at 25 °C and stirred at 150 rpm. Then, the samples were filtered using 0.45 μm pore size syringe filters once they achieved equilibrium. The concentration of arsenic ions was analyzed using ICP-MS. Under different conditions such as the adsorbent dosage (20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 mg), initial arsenic concentrations (0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, and 0.25 mg/L), pH (2, 4, 6, 8, and 10) and contact time (30, 60, 90, 120, and 150 min), the effectiveness of Fe-BC adsorbing arsenic was investigated. All the experimental analyses were done in triplicates. The adsorption kinetic and isotherm studies were also carried out at room temperature.

Fixed bed column study

A 25 cm long by 2.5 cm diameter was utilized for the column adsorption experiment. Iron oxide-modified brown seaweed biochar was added to the column up to a height of 5.0 cm as an adsorbent. The purposeful placement of glass wool and plastic mesh at the base and top of the column was to prevent any adsorbent material loss during the process.

The 2 mg/L arsenic solution was gradually added to the column by a peristaltic pump operating at a 5 mL/min rate. The solution flowed continuously from top to bottom. The concentration ratio quickly climbed to 1.0, which was the breakthrough point, and the samples were taken at 20-min intervals. The column bed was now considered useless, and regeneration was necessary for adsorption17. The experiment continued until the adsorbent bed capacity and the amount of arsenic was reached.

A breakthrough curve shows how influent and effluent concentrations (Ct/Co) relate to one another over time. To comprehend the dynamic features of the column and optimize operational design, the shape of the breakthrough curve and the saturation period are essential. The breakthrough curve's steepness also gives crucial information on how much of the adsorbent bed can be utilized effectively18.

Results and discussion

Characterization

Morphology and specific surface area

The SEM analysis investigated biochar (BC) and iron-modified biochar (Fe-BC) surface morphology. The inherent characteristics of biochar, such as its irregular and smooth structure, were observed in Fig. 2a, previously observed by19. However, after modifying the biochar with iron sulphate and iron chloride salts, the surface became noticeably rougher and heterogenous than raw biochar, as shown in Fig. 2b, which indicates the presence of clusters formed by the deposition of iron compounds on the biochar. This phenomenon was attributed to magnetic attraction and surface tension, as explained by20.Figure 2Scanning electron microscopic images of brown seaweed derived raw biochar (a) and iron-modified biochar (b).

Scanning electron microscopic images of brown seaweed derived raw biochar (a) and iron-modified biochar (b).

The surface area was analyzed for biochar and iron-modified biochar using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) technique. The specific surface area of macrolagae derived raw biochar and iron-modified biochar was observed to be 0.52 m2/g and 168.29 m2/g. The pore volume of raw and iron-modified biochar was determined as 0.013 cm3/g and 0.746 cm3/g.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopic (FTIR) analysis

The FTIR analysis was done to examine the presence of different functional groups on the surface of biochar and iron-modified biochar, as shown in Fig. 3. The observed peaks in the FTIR spectra for both BC and Fe-BC were similar regarding their wavenumbers. Regarding the spectrum of raw BC, the peaks identified by adsorption bands in the 3200–4000 cm−1 range represent the spectrum of O–H and N–H, which can be associated with hydroxyl and amino groups21. Additionally, the peaks at approximately 1782 and 1627 cm−1 suggested the existence of C=O and C–O groups, which are associated with carboxyl and aromatic compounds22. A notable difference was observed in Fe-BC, where the broad O–H absorption band indicated a higher abundance of OH groups than raw BC, suggesting successful iron integration. Moreover, the new adsorption bands at 620 and 817 cm−1 in the Fe-BC spectrum correspond to Fe–O peak vibration stretches of Fe2O3 and Fe3O4. This confirms that iron was successfully integrated into the biochar surface during the chemical modification. These FTIR analysis findings suggest that chemical modification significantly impacts the biochar's properties. The intensity of functional groups was increased after modification on the surface of biochar, indicating that the chemical modification process created new active sites, potentially enhancing arsenic adsorption on the surface of biochar.Figure 3FTIR spectra of brown seaweed-derived biochar and biochar modified with iron oxide.

FTIR spectra of brown seaweed-derived biochar and biochar modified with iron oxide.

Batch adsorption study

Effect of pH

Since hydrogen ions highly favor heavy metal ions, the pH of the solution can influence how well ions bind to the adsorbent, which determines the availability of binding sites on the adsorbent surface24. This research investigated the impact of pH on arsenic using brown seaweed-derived iron-modified biochar. The arsenic adsorption efficiency was examined by adjusting a pH range of 2–10. The findings are represented in Fig. 4. According to the observations, the maximum arsenic adsorption efficiency of 88% was achieved at the pH of 6, 120 min equilibrium time, arsenic initial concentration of 0.25 mg/L, and adsorbent dose of 100 mg. Figure 4. illustrates that the arsenic adsorption efficiency improved from 60 to 88% with the increase of pH from 2 to 6 and decreased from 88 to 66% with the increase of pH from 6 to 10. This trend of increasing adsorption efficiency at pH 6 was consistent with previous investigations by15,23, who also found that the optimal pH for arsenic removal was around 6–8, and the most efficient removal occurred at pH 6.Figure 4Influence of pH levels on arsenic removal using the biochar modified with iron oxide.

Influence of pH levels on arsenic removal using the biochar modified with iron oxide.

The current investigation revealed that the pH levels and the surface groups on the adsorbent influence the binding of metal ions. The exceptional arsenic adsorption capacity of brown seaweed-derived iron-modified biochar at a slightly acidic pH of 6 can be attributed to the electrostatic interaction between the positively charged surface of the biochar and the negatively charged arsenic species (H2AsO4−). At lower pH levels, the presence of H+ ions promoted hydroxylation processes that enhanced the attraction of arsenic ions to the adsorbent sites, leading to improved arsenic adsorption efficiency25. Conversely, as the pH increases from 6 to 10 and the solution becomes more alkaline, the surface of the macroalgae-derived biochar modified with iron oxide becomes negatively charged due to the deprotonation of functional groups. This change in surface charge repels the negatively charged arsenate ions, reducing the electrostatic attraction and decreasing adsorption efficiency.

The biochar surface is positively charged, and the zeta potential of both biochars, raw and iron-modified biochar, decreased with increasing the pH, as shown in Fig. 5. Due to the prevalence of –OH groups, the biochar surface repelling negatively charged arsenic species. As the pH increased, the zeta potential of the biochars became progressively more negative. This trend benefits the electrosorption process, as the increasingly negative potential enhances the biochar's ability to absorb heavy metals through electrostatic attraction14.Figure 5Zeta potential of BC and FeBC.

Zeta potential of BC and FeBC.

Effect of adsorbent dosage

The adsorbent dosage used is a decisive factor that can have a great impact on the adsorption of arsenic onto the adsorbent material. In the experiments, the influence of the amount of adsorbent used on the effectiveness of removing arsenic using iron-modified biochar was investigated using different quantities of the adsorbent ranging from 20 to 100 mg. The results indicated that higher amounts of adsorbent led to better arsenic adsorption efficiency, and the results are illustrated in Fig. 6. The maximum arsenic adsorption efficiency of the adsorbent was achieved 96.7% at the adsorbent dose of 100 mg. The results show that increasing the brown seaweed-derived iron-modified adsorbent dose enhances the number of active binding sites that attract and bind arsenic ions. The number of active sites increases when more biochar is added to the solution, allowing arsenic to be adsorbed onto the biochar26. These results are consistent with the findings of23.Figure 6Effect of iron-modified adsorbent doses on arsenic adsorption.

Effect of iron-modified adsorbent doses on arsenic adsorption.

Effect of initial arsenic concentration

The adsorption efficiency is also significantly influenced by initial arsenic concentration in a solution. The research investigated how an adsorbent behaves at different arsenic initial concentrations (0.05–0.25 ppm). It was discovered that the arsenic removal efficiency was increased by increasing the initial arsenic concentrations. Specifically, higher initial concentrations of arsenic resulted in a greater capacity of the adsorption sites to bind with arsenic particles, leading to a higher overall removal efficiency of 88.9% of arsenic from the solution, as shown in Fig. 7. At lower initial concentrations of arsenic, the surface of the biochar treated with iron oxide had a higher density of adsorption sites relative to the number of arsenic particles present27,28. This abundance of adsorption sites facilitated a higher probability of successful collisions between the arsenic particles and available sites, which led to the more effective adsorption of arsenic in the solution.Figure 7Influence of different initial arsenic concentrations on arsenic removal using the biochar treated with iron oxide.

Influence of different initial arsenic concentrations on arsenic removal using the biochar treated with iron oxide.

Effect of contact time

The arsenic contact time significantly influenced the biochar surface adsorption process, which was identified as a significant factor. The effectiveness of arsenic removal was investigated in relation to the length of contact between an adsorbent material and a solution containing 0.25 mg/L of arsenic. The contact period varied from 30 to 150 min to observe adsorption efficiency variations. The maximum arsenic removal efficiency was observed at 91.5% at the contact time of 90 min. The study revealed a notable enhancement in arsenic removal efficiency from 68.3 to 91.5% when the contact time between the arsenic solution and the adsorbent material was extended from 30 to 90 min, as illustrated in Fig. 8. However, beyond 90 min, the arsenic removal capacity of iron-treated biochar did not show any further improvement. This observation is consistent with the work of29, who also found an optimum adsorption time of 90 min for arsenic removal.Figure 8Influence of contact time on arsenic removal using the biochar treated with iron oxide.

Influence of contact time on arsenic removal using the biochar treated with iron oxide.

Furthermore, it aligns with the study conducted by30, which identified that over time, the available binding sites for arsenic decrease, indicating a reduction in the removal rate competition of remaining metal ions. Similarly, the study of31 also identified similar results, supporting the notion that beyond a certain contact time, the efficiency of arsenic adsorption reaches a plateau. The investigation demonstrates that the removal of arsenic by iron-modified biochar is impacted by contact time, with an initial increase in adsorption efficiency up to 90 min, after which the adsorbent material eventually reaches a point where it can no longer efficiently bind further arsenic molecules as the adsorption process progresses. Because fewer competing metal ions are in the solution and the accessible binding sites on the adsorbent become saturated, there is a plateau in the adsorption capacity, which reduces the effectiveness of further arsenic removal29–31.

Equilibrium isotherm

Studying adsorption isotherms is crucial for analyzing and comprehending how pollutants interact with the biochar surface32. To explore the adsorption behavior of brown seaweed-derived iron-modified biochar, the data was fitted using two popular equilibrium isotherm models: Freundlich and Langmuir20 state that the Langmuir isotherm model relies on the supposition that sorption takes place in a monolayer fashion on a uniform surface composed of identical sites, each with the same activation energy. Conversely, the Freundlich model is commonly utilized to depict chemisorption on a heterogeneous surface. Table 2 presents the correlation coefficients and constants for the Freundlich and Langmuir adsorption isotherms, while Fig. 9a,b display the corresponding R2 values of 0.99 and 0.93, respectively. These results indicate that with the highest adsorption capacity (qmax) of 0.08 mg/g, the Langmuir equilibrium model is better suited to explain the adsorption of arsenic on brown seaweed biochar treated with iron oxide. The Langmuir isotherm suggests that the arsenic adsorption on the surface of biochar forms irreversible connections involving a homogeneous layer of dissolved molecules, and the energy level of adsorption remains constant across the entire surface. However, the Freundlich isotherm model also showed better adsorption, suggesting the heterogeneous adsorption layer of dissolved molecules. Adsorption kinetics

The adsorption kinetics study aimed to investigate the adsorption kinetics of adsorbate molecules over time and understand the mechanisms involved in the adsorption process of arsenic on brown seaweed-derived iron-modified biochar34. This study examined arsenic adsorption kinetics using three widely used kinetic models: the pseudo-first-order, pseudo-second-order kinetic, and infraparticle diffusion kinetic models. In the first-order kinetic model context, the rate at which a solute is removed is directly linked to changes in the quantity of adsorbent and the saturation concentration used during the process. Several previous studies35 also have supported this assumption. Conversely, the 2nd order kinetics suggests that chemical adsorption may have an impact, which implies the chemical interactions between the adsorbate and the adsorbent likely control the process. The infraparticle diffusion model describes the mechanism and rate at which adsorbates move from the bulk solution phase into the interior of adsorbent particles. Table 2 shows the Pseudo-first-order, Pseudo-second-order, and infraparticle diffusion kinetic model parameters. The good fitness of a model is described by the value of the correlation coefficient (R2). It can be seen that the value of R2 of pseudo-first-order is less than pseudo-second-order kinetics, as shown in Fig. 10a–c. It shows that the infraparticle diffusion model and pseudo 1st order are not appropriate for predicting the adsorption kinetics of arsenic on brown seaweed-derived iron-modified biochar. The R2 value of Pseudo 2nd order fitted well (R2 = 0.9995), which means that chemisorption occurs between the functional groups of the absorbent and the adsorbate. The findings of this investigation are consistent with those of28, confirming that the pseudo 2nd order kinetic model provides the best fit for the kinetics of arsenic adsorption, consistent with their research.Figure 10Arsenic adsorption kinetic models Pseudo-first-order kinetics (a), Pseudo-second-order kinetics (b), and infraparticle diffusion model (c).

Column study breakthrough curve

To ascertain if the adsorbent would be suitable for commercial application, the BC-Fe was evaluated in a fixed-bed column experiment. To completely utilize the packed adsorbent at room temperature, the artificially contaminated arsenic water was poured down the bed from top to bottom. A bed mass of around 8 g, BC-Fe, and a constant influent arsenic concentration of 2 ppm were used to study the breakthrough curve. A 5 mL/min influent flow rate was selected.

As seen in Fig. 11, the breakthrough curve rises quickly, suggesting that arsenic was adsorbed before the breakthrough because many adsorbent sites were accessible. Because there were fewer binding sites available for arsenic to attach to in the column's bottom layers, the adsorption showed18. As the arsenic solution passed through the column's higher layers, the adsorbent was quickly absorbed (Ct/Co = 0). As the higher layers progressively get saturated and the column becomes saturated (Ct/Co = 0), adsorption occurs in the adsorbent's unadsorbed layers36. When the ratio of effluent concentration to influent concentration (Ct/Co = 0) was 0.99, or very close to 1, the saturation time was chosen. Thus, 200 min was noted as the saturation time. This guarantees that the column's adsorption capacity was saturated and that a sizable section of the adsorbent bed was used37. Practically, it means that a significant portion of the available adsorption sites are already occupied, indicating that the system has reached a stage where more adsorbate molecules have less chance to connect to them.Figure 11Column study breakthrough curve.

Arsenic adsorption mechanism

According to the findings of our analysis and other research, the predominant mechanism underlying the interaction between iron sulfate heptahydrate (FeSO4·7H2O) and iron chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O) with arsenic ions is surface complexation. This entails the creation of complexes between the metallic oxide and arsenic ions at the surface level, facilitating their bonding and removal. Therefore, the removal effect of arsenic was influenced by additional factors, including ionic attraction and the formation of metal-containing nodules on the surface. When using modified biochar treated with FeSO4·7H2O and FeCl3·6H2O, increased surface area and porosity enhanced adsorption efficiency. According to the findings of characterization analysis, a consequence of the iron oxide that was successfully loaded showed that the biochar can adsorb arsenic was significantly enhanced. The rate-limiting step in the pseudo-second-order model and Langmuir isotherm is typically considered to be the chemical sorption of arsenic on Fe-BC; this suggests that arsenic was most likely adsorbed on the surface of Fe-BC through chemical interaction17.

The brown seaweed-derived biochar treated with iron oxide has surface functional groups like amino (N–H), carboxyl (C=O), aromatic (C–O), and hydroxyl (O–H). These groups are essential for drawing in and holding onto arsenic ions. Through electrostatic forces, the positively charged metal ions in the biochar treated with iron oxide interact with the arsenic species, which are negatively charged45. Because arsenic ions are negatively charged, this attraction is especially strong for them. Arsenic ions and iron ions on the adsorbent's surface can combine to form complexes. The complexation leads to stable complexes on the surface through the coordination of iron ions in the adsorbent with arsenic ions. The functional groups on the adsorbent surface can form stronger chemical bonds, like covalent bonds, with the arsenic species15. This chemisorption improves the stability of the arsenic adsorption onto the adsorbent. Their presence can aid ion exchange reactions between arsenic ions and different metal ions on the adsorbent's surface. Arsenic adsorption results from this process, which replaces less strongly bound ions on the adsorbent surface with arsenic ions. Arsenic ions can diffuse into the interior of biochar due to its porous nature. Because this pore diffusion provides more sites for arsenic binding, it may increase the total adsorption capacity46. Arsenic ions can react with iron oxides and hydroxides on the adsorbent surface to form insoluble precipitates. The process of precipitation helps to remove arsenic from the water (Fig. 14).Figure 14Potential arsenic adsorption mechanisms on iron-modified biochar surface23,47.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this research has successfully demonstrated the promising potential of brown seaweed (Sargassum polycystum) derived biochar impregnated with iron oxide as an efficient and eco-friendly adsorbent material for the adsorption of arsenic from synthetic water. The characterization techniques confirmed the successful loading of iron onto the biochar's surface and shed light on the underlying mechanisms of arsenic removal. Batch adsorption experiments revealed a remarkable arsenic removal efficiency of 96.7% at the pH of 6.0 and the adsorbent dosage of 100 mg for the arsenic concentration of 0.25 mg/L, with Langmuir and Freundlich’s isotherms supporting the favorable adsorption process. Kinetic studies further demonstrated that the removal process suggested pseudo-2nd-order kinetics, highlighting chemical adsorption as the rate-limiting step. Moreover, breakthrough curve analysis in a column under consistent conditions showed the suitability of the Thomas and Adam–Bohart models, with full saturation occurring after 200 min, resulting in an adsorption capacity of 1.64 mg/g. These findings underscore the potential of brown seaweed-derived biochar treated with iron oxide as a promising and sustainable solution for addressing arsenic contamination in water, which holds significant implications for water purification technologies and environmental remediation efforts. Further research on real water samples and the regeneration potential of the biochar will provide valuable insights into its practical application and feasibility on a larger scale.
