Subjective smell‐ and taste loss in diabetes—A questionnaire‐based study on 47 011 individuals with diabetes and matched controls
Authors: Alexander Wieck Fjaeldstad, Kasper Norman, Lasse Bjerg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTaste and smell disorders are more common in individuals with diabetes, particularly among those with low insulin sensitivity or central obesity. These disorders may affect glycaemic control by altering dietary habits. This study aimed to investigate self‐reported taste and smell dysfunction in individuals with diabetes and explore associations with clinical and behavioural factors.MethodsWe examined self‐reported taste and smell disorders in 47,011 individuals with or without diabetes using data from the Health in Central Denmark (HICD) cohort. In total, eight questions on subjective smell and taste function were included in the questionnaire.ResultsParticipants with type 2 diabetes reported significantly more qualitative and quantitative chemosensory complaints than matched controls. In contrast, no significant differences were found between individuals with Type 1 Diabetes and controls. Smoking was consistently associated with increased complaints across all groups, while HbA1c levels showed no correlation with taste or smell disorders. Obesity, particularly in women with diabetes, was linked to higher symptom reportingConclusionsIndividuals with type 2 diabetes report more chemosensory complaints than controls. Smoking and obesity, but not HbA1c, were associated with a higher prevalence of symptoms. As subjective rating tends to underestimate true prevalence, future research should include objective testing to clarify how chemosensory disorders affect dietary behaviour and diabetes management.Keywords: body mass index, gustation, olfaction, olfaction disorders

BACKGROUND

Diabetes is associated with both macrovascular (e.g., cardiovascular disease) and microvascular complications (e.g., neuropathy, nephropathy, and retinopathy), driven by hyperglycaemia, low‐grade inflammation, and dietary factors. 1 While these well‐established complications have been extensively studied and addressed in diabetes‐specific clinical guidelines, recent studies indicate that taste and smell disorders are overlooked traits that may also be impaired in patients with diabetes. 2

Not only do individuals with diabetes have a higher prevalence of clinically evaluated smell loss than individuals without diabetes (22% vs. 15%, diabetes type not specified), 2 , 3 but this association is also stronger in those with low insulin sensitivity. 4 Olfactory dysfunction is often referred to as a complication of diabetes, due to vascular affection, 5 neuropathy, 6 or insulin resistance. 4 Olfactory disorders are associated with other diabetes‐related complications, including peripheral neuropathy and retinopathy. 7 , 8 However, olfactory dysfunction has also been suggested as a factor that complicates glucose metabolism, 8 underlining the complexity of the relationship between the chemical senses and diabetes. Individuals with Type 2 diabetes have also been found to have a reduction in the ability to taste, and this reduction was associated with central obesity. 9

While smell contributes to food pleasure, the relationship is complex and individual‐specific. Generally, a diminished sense of smell is known to reduce the quality of life, for example, social life ratings and the pleasure of food are reduced. 10 , 11 Similarly, smell loss is associated with altered eating habits and severe challenges with cooking. 12 Chemosensory disorders have recently been associated with poorer diet quality and variation in dietary intake. 13 Especially relevant for individuals with diabetes, the chemosensory dysfunction may interfere with adherence to optimal diets 13 and contribute to disturbances in the nutritional state and insulin regulation. 14

Currently, no national or international diabetes guideline includes an assessment of smell and/or taste function. 15 This may be a natural consequence of the sparse knowledge within the field, as highlighted in a recent review on smell loss in diabetes. 16 More knowledge is needed on taste and smell disorders in diabetes to elucidate patient‐reported taste and smell problems and implications on diabetes‐friendly diet compliance. While several studies have reported a higher incidence of taste and smell disorders in persons with diabetes, it is currently unknown if this corresponds with increased subjective ratings of chemosensory disorders. As unawareness of smell deficits has been well documented in the general population, 17 , 18 , 19 this may also apply to the subpopulation with diabetes.

This large‐scale questionnaire study aims to investigate and compare self‐reported taste and smell disorders in individuals with diabetes and matched controls. Specifically, it aims to clarify if the higher occurrence of smell and taste disorders in the literature is reflected in the subjective assessment of the senses. Furthermore, we will investigate the cross‐sectional associations between taste loss, smell loss, glycaemic control, and weight.

METHODS

Study design and population

We conducted a cross‐sectional study based on data from the Health in Central Denmark (HICD) cohort. The HICD cohort is an ongoing health survey initiated in 2021 that combines data from questionnaires, nationwide laboratory databases, health databases, and national registries. 20 Diabetes was classified using a predescribed algorithm, using four types of inclusion events: ICD‐10 codes E10 to E14, redeemed glucose‐lowering medications ATC‐code A10, except Saxenda, HbA1c values ≥48, and attendance at a diabetes‐specific podiatrist service. Classification as either type 1 diabetes or type 2 diabetes was based on patterns of medication use and hospital diagnoses. The positive predictive value of this algorithm is reported to be 0.943 for type 1 diabetes and 0.875 for type 2 diabetes, respectively. 21 Among the 1.3 million citizens in the Central Denmark Region, 45 507 individuals between 18 and 75 years of age were identified as having diabetes. 21 A regional reference population (matched control group) without diabetes was matched by sex, age, and municipality of residence. In total, the HICD questionnaire was distributed to 90 854 individuals through the national digital mailbox with a 57.1% response rate. For a detailed description of the cohort design and participant inclusion, see Bjerg et al. 22 In this study, we excluded individuals who did not respond to either the smell‐ or taste‐related questions or where covariates were not available for further exploratory analysis (including medical prescriptions, comorbidities, and sociodemographic data).

Taste and smell questionnaires

Questions on subjective taste and smell function and details on taste and smell dysfunction were included in the HICD questionnaire. Due to restrictions on the length of the questionnaire, questions were limited to four related to the sense of smell and four to the sense of taste (Supplementary Table S1). The phrasing and selection of questions were based on the previous work from the Global Consortium for Chemosensory Research, where leading researchers in the field designed similar questionnaires for online distribution during COVID‐19. 23 , 24 In line with these studies, the rated smell and taste disorders were based on the previous 14 days to avoid recall bias. The subjective function was rated on a sliding scale, for example, from ‘No sense of smell’ to ‘excellent sense of smell’ as a continuous rating scale from 0 to 100 allows for a more granular level of data to be recorded. Complaints of qualitative disorders were recorded as categorical variables and are referred to as smell distortions or taste distortions (see smell and taste questions in Supplementary Table S1). Smell and taste dysfunctions were classified as the occurrence of a qualitative sensory disturbance and/or a subjective assessment of chemosensory function below 80 on the sliding scale from 0 to 100. To assess the robustness of our results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis and re‐analysed the data using cut‐off values of 50 for subjective quantitative chemosensory dysfunction (Supplementary Table S5). Smell and taste loss were defined as an isolated quantitative reduction of function without qualitative complaints. In summary, the following terminology for reduced taste and smell function is used throughout the manuscript: loss is defined as a quantitative reduction, distortion is defined as a qualitative alteration, and dysfunction is defined as a combined term encompassing both qualitative and/or quantitative alterations of smell and/or taste.

Demographics and health behaviour

Smoking status was classified as either a current smoker or a non‐smoker. HbA1c measures were retrieved from the Regional laboratory database. HbA1c was categorised into 4 levels: HbA1c <48 mmol/mol, 48–53 mmol/mol, 53–58 mmol/mol and HbA1c >58 mmol/mol. A small proportion of individuals (<10%) did not have an HbA1c measure. Using the grand mean for individuals with diabetes and individuals without diabetes, respectively, we imputed the HbA1c level in the individuals with missing values. A complete case analysis excluding participants without HbA1c measures was conducted to ensure the reliability of imputation. We estimated body mass index (BMI) as appropriate using self‐reported height and weight. We retrieved information on comorbidity from the Danish National Patient Registry. Prior rhinosinusitis was defined as an ICD‐10 coding of J300–J304, J310, and J320–J329, up to 10 years prior to inclusion.

Statistics

Descriptive statistics were used to present distributions of characteristics of the study population. The statistics were tabulated for Type 1 diabetes and Type 2 diabetes, and their matched controls, respectively. While continuous variables were presented as means (SD), categorical variables were presented as numbers (%) of individuals. Group comparisons were done using the Chi‐squared test.

Using matched controls without diabetes as the reference group, we determined the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence intervals for the presence of smell and/or taste dysfunction in both individuals with Type 1 diabetes and those with Type 2 diabetes, separately for men and women. We used logistic regressions for group comparison and to evaluate the impact of well‐known available factors (HbA1c, smoking and BMI). In Model 1, we did not adjust (crude model); in Model 2, we adjusted for age, smoking, HbA1c, and BMI (Table 4). To further investigate the effect of confounding, we conducted a sensitivity analysis adjusting for origin, educational level, lipid‐lowering medication, glucose‐lowering medication, Charlson comorbidity index, and Chronic rhinosinusitis in each model (Supplementary Table S4).

Ethics

As the study was based on questionnaire and registry data, the study did not require regional Ethical Committee approval (Danish Committee Act, Section 10, Subsection 1). The storage of data was registered in the internal register of research projects in the Central Denmark Region (reg. no. 1‐16‐02‐165‐20), which is under the approval of the Danish Data Protection Agency.

DISCUSSION

In this study, involving 21,801 individuals with diabetes and 25,210 matched controls, we found that individuals with Type 1 diabetes showed no signs of increased complaints of taste and smell dysfunction, as these complaints were significantly lower than those of the controls. Contrarily, individuals with Type 2 diabetes had significantly more complaints of taste and smell dysfunction compared with controls. This included complaints of both subjective quantitative disorders (taste and smell loss) and qualitative disorders (distorted and phantom smell/taste).

For all individuals with diabetes and controls, smoking was associated with a significantly higher risk of taste and smell dysfunction after adjusting for age, HbA1c, and BMI. Obesity (BMI >30 kg/m2) was associated with a higher risk of taste and smell dysfunction in female individuals both without diabetes and to an even larger extent in women with diabetes (type 1 and type 2), which was also the case for underweight (BMI <20 kg/m2) female individuals with type 2 diabetes.

Comparison with previous studies

There is currently a limited amount of research, primarily consisting of small studies on diabetes and smell dysfunction, where many do not differentiate between types of diabetes. 16 The largest study on diabetes and taste/smell dysfunction to date was based on the NHANES 2013–2014 data, where 428 patients with self‐reported diabetes were compared with 2776 controls and tested with the 8‐odour NHANES Pocket Smell test and the NHANES Tongue Tip and Whole‐mouth taste test. 2 While the prevalence of smell dysfunction was higher in patients with diabetes compared with controls (22% vs. 15%, p <0.001), no differences in taste dysfunction were found (11% vs. 12%, p = 0.286).

In the most recent meta‐analysis, the pooled data from studies with clinically evaluated smell loss revealed that individuals with diabetes had an almost 60% increased risk of smell dysfunction. 25 In the current study, only individuals with Type 2 diabetes had an increased risk of complaints of smell and taste dysfunction, while individuals with Type 1 diabetes did not.

The inconsistency between complaints of taste/smell dysfunction and measured taste/smell dysfunction has been well studied, with 0.5%–3.4% of healthy individuals who subjectively reported having a normal sense of smell having no functional sense of smell when tested. 26 This underreporting is similarly reflected in the general population, where the prevalence of smell loss is increased two to three‐fold from subjective assessments to smell testing. 27 In contrast to quantitative disorders, there is currently no gold standard for testing qualitative chemosensory disorders. 28

Possible effects of quantitative taste and smell loss on diet and weight

The effects of smell loss on weight changes are not homogeneous. Smell loss has been associated with a decreased calorie intake in both humans and mice with diabetes. 2 , 29 , 30 However, some patients with smell loss experience weight gain, 12 , 30 highlighting the differences in metabolic consequences and coping strategies following smell loss. The sense of smell is the most important food‐related sensory contributor, as it is responsible for perceiving all aromas. 31 If a meal is deprived of sensory stimulation, it produces insufficient cues for satiation and can lead to a higher overall calorie intake. 32 As such, this may interfere with adherence to dietary recommendations for managing diabetes and impact patient outcomes. 14

Although taste bud density has been negatively associated with HbA1c levels, 33 little is known about the associations between taste dysfunction, diabetes, and diet.

In this study, we identified increased odds of smell/taste dysfunction in underweight (BMI <20 kg/m2) and obese (BMI >30 kg/m2) female individuals with diabetes after adjusting for HbA1c levels. While obesity is a risk factor for Type 2 diabetes, the presence of an association between BMI and smell/taste loss in both Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes is noteworthy, highlighting the necessity to study these groups independently. Furthermore, smell and taste loss may be treated in some cases with medicine, 28 or on a broader scale, smell function can be improved with standardised olfactory training. 34 Negative effects on food and dietary habits in patients with smell disorders can also be alleviated by tailored interventions that focus on food and cooking. 35 As such, the identification of smell and taste dysfunction in individuals with diabetes may have both clinical and personal positive effects. 36 , 37 However, no current studies on dietary interventions in diabetes have included smell and taste testing, as this is currently not standard practice clinically or in research.

Possible effects of qualitative taste and smell disorders on diet and weight

In Type 2 diabetes, complaints of all types of qualitative taste and smell dysfunction (changed taste/smell or phantom taste/smell) were significantly increased, being almost twice as frequent as in the control group. While a reduced sense of smell or taste can reduce the perceived pleasure of food, quantitative disturbances can trigger signs of danger during eating, such as rotten aromas or metallic taste. 38 Still, the effects on diet and weight vary greatly between individuals, as some continue to gain weight despite severe daily qualitative disturbances. 12

HbA1C

As the glycaemic variation is associated with micro‐ and macrovascular complications in diabetes, the possible association with smell function has been highlighted as an important knowledge gap. 16 Irrespective of diabetes type, there were no significant associations between increased HbA1c levels and subjective smell or taste dysfunction, neither before nor after adjusting for age, BMI, and smoking. While the underlying mechanism of chemosensory dysfunction in diabetes remains unknown, the study design cannot establish causality. We found no evidence of an association between subjective complaints and HbA1c. This may suggest that HbA1c does not accurately capture long‐term glycaemic variability, cumulative exposure, or microvascular complications—factors that may be more relevant to chemosensory dysfunction.

Smoking

For both individuals with and without diabetes, current smoking was associated with a significantly increased risk of subjective smell or taste dysfunction. We found that the association between smoking and smell loss was more pronounced in men with diabetes compared with controls, while women had more similar associations in diabetes and controls. The role of smoking in smell loss remains controversial due to the lack of larger intervention studies on smoking cessation and smell loss. 28 Smoking is generally assessed to have negative consequences on smell function and may increase the risk of smell loss 39 and taste loss. 40 While the common finding of taste loss in smokers has been shown to improve after smoking cessation, 41 smoking cessation has currently only been shown to improve smell loss after a shorter duration of smoking, 42 indicating that the underlying mechanisms of sensory loss may differ between these senses. 5

Strengths and limitations

The main strengths of this study are the sample size, the matched control groups, and the combination of questionnaires with several nationwide registries. Of the 51,854 participants of the HICD cohort, 90.7% responded to the smell and taste questionnaires and had data on covariates for exploratory analysis (See Supplementary Table S7).

The main limitation of the study is that taste and smell function was self‐reported and not measured. The threshold of <80 for sensory loss was based on previous studies using similar scales in large‐scale chemosensory surveys, such as the ODoR19, the commonly used SNOT‐22 questionnaire for sino‐nasal disease and the Smell‐Qx for olfactory disorders. While practical, this cut‐off has not been validated in chronic metabolic conditions and may not accurately reflect clinically significant loss of function. Therefore, additional exploratory analysis was conducted with the threshold cut‐off of 50, which increased the strength of the identified associations. For all groups, the subjective assessment of chemosensory function may lead to an underestimation of smell loss, as participants may not be aware of a reduced sense of smell or misclassify a smell loss as a taste loss. 19 The findings of this study can therefore highlight the awareness of taste and smell dysfunction in diabetic individuals, but not assess the prevalence of measured dysfunction.

As several diseases are associated with chemosensory dysfunction, the additional exploratory analysis, which included the Charlson Comorbidity Index, reduced the group differences and widened the confidence intervals. Further analysis of the underlying associations between additional covariates and taste or smell dysfunction is beyond the scope of the current study.

Due to restrictions on the length of the questionnaire, we did not include direct measurements of dietary behaviour or clinical outcomes, limiting the interpretation of potential downstream effects of smell and taste dysfunction.

The retrospective design of the study can only reveal possible associations and does not give insights into the underlying causality between diabetes and any taste and/or smell dysfunction.

The list of potential contributing factors for smell and taste dysfunction is extremely long and heterogeneous, ranging from sino‐nasal or oral diseases to trauma, infections, medications, various systemic diseases, excessive alcohol consumption, neurodegenerative diseases, peripheral and central neurological diseases. 28 As the design of the current study does not reveal causality, some of these factors associated with diabetes may affect the findings of the study. However, if these or other factors are associated with diabetes and increase the prevalence of smell and taste dysfunction in diabetic individuals, this does not alter the potential clinical relevance of addressing taste and smell dysfunction in diabetic individuals.

As expected, there were some differences between those who answered the questionnaire and those who did not. Non‐response bias and underrepresentation of ethnic minorities or lower socioeconomic status groups may limit the external validity of the study.

Implications for future research and clinical practice

The discrepancy between measured and self‐reported smell and taste dysfunction in diabetes 20 emphasises the need for increased focus on these diabetes‐related complications in future research and clinical practice. This includes investigations into the possible effects of dietary changes in patients with diabetes and taste or smell dysfunction, and the potential need for patient care and guidance.

CONCLUSION

In this study, which included 21 801 individuals with diabetes (2637 with Type 1 diabetes) and 25 210 controls, there was an increased proportion of taste and smell complaints (qualitative and quantitative) in individuals with Type 2 diabetes compared with controls, after adjustment for BMI, HbA1c, gender, and smoking. Type 1 diabetes was not associated with significantly increased complaints of taste or smell dysfunction. Subjective taste and smell dysfunction were associated with smoking and obesity in women. No associations were found between HbA1c levels and chemosensory dysfunction. The lower prevalence of subjective taste and smell loss compared with the prevalence in studies with tested function warrants clinical attention and the need for psychophysical testing of taste and smell function.

More research is needed to investigate the measured taste and smell dysfunction in relation to diabetes type, possible effects of taste and smell dysfunction on diet, potential negative effects on diabetes, and clinical implications.
