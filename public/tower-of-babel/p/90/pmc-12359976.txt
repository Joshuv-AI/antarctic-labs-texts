Simvastatin inhibits 20-Hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid formation: docking and in vitro assay
Authors: Yazun Jarrar, Mansour Almansour, Mansour Al-Sayed Ahmad, Belal O. Al-Najjar, Amin A. Al-Doaiss, Sireen Abdul Rahim Shilbayeh, Su-Jun Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction20-Hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (20-HETE), a metabolite of arachidonic acid catalyzed mainly by cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes CYP4A11 and CYP4F2, plays a role in cardiovascular homeostasis. Elevated levels of 20-HETE are associated with hypertension. Accordingly, 20-HETE is a potential target for therapeutic interventions.AimsThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of commonly prescribed statins simvastatin, atorvastatin, and rosuvastatin on 20-HETE formation in human liver microsomes and to conduct molecular docking simulations with CYP4A11 and CYP4F2.MethodsHuman liver microsomes were used to assess 20-HETE formation inhibition. Molecular docking simulations were performed using predicted structures of CYP4A11, CYP4F2, and CYP4F3B. The binding affinity of simvastatin was evaluated based on lowest energy of binding (LEB) values, and interactions with the heme group were analyzed.ResultsIn vitro assay showed that simvastatin, but not other tested statins, inhibited the formation of 20-HETE in the human liver microsome with IC50 value of 10 µM and in a competitive mechanism as shown in the kinetics of the Lineweaver-Burk plot. Using in silico tools, simvastatin showed a significant inhibitory effect on CYP4A11, CYP4F2, and CYP4F3B with LEB values of -11.06, -9.93, and − 11.55 kcal/mol, respectively, indicating a stronger interaction compared to the known inhibitor HET0016. Simvastatin interacted predominantly with multiple hydrogen bonds and with the heme group of CYP4A11.ConclusionsSimvastatin effectively inhibits the formation of 20-HETE most probably through interaction with CYP4A11, CYP4F2, and CYP4F3B, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic agent for managing conditions associated with elevated 20-HETE levels. Further clinical investigations are needed to explore its implications in cardiovascular diseases associated with elevated 20-HETE levels.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40360-025-00976-2.

Introduction

20-Hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (20-HETE) is an arachidonic acid metabolite produced in humans through the cytochrome P450 (CYP) 4 family, mainly CYP4A11 and CYP4F isoforms [1]. These CYP4 enzymes are highly expressed in the liver and kidneys [1]. In addition, they are expressed in platelets, the brain, and the heart [2]. 20-HETE plays a major role in cardiovascular homeostasis by increasing platelet aggregation [3], causing vasoconstriction, inducing inflammation [4] and cell division, and decreasing insulin secretion from the pancreas [5]. Several studies have reported that elevated levels of 20-HETE are harmful to the cardiovascular system and associated with diseases such as hypertension and heart failure [3, 4, 6]. Furthermore, increased expression of 20-HETE-synthesizing CYPs has been observed in NSAID- [7] and doxorubicin-induced cardiovascular toxicity [8]. Accordingly, several reviews suggest that 20-HETE is a potential target for the treatment of cardiovascular diseases, particularly hypertension [9, 10].

Statins, which inhibit 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase, reduce endogenous cholesterol formation and are used in the treatment of hypercholesterolemia. Statins are classified into short-, intermediate-, and long-acting drugs [11]. The most commonly used statins are simvastatin, atorvastatin, and rosuvastatin [12]. In addition to their beneficial effects on the lipid profile, statins are considered to have pleiotropic activity in the treatment of cardiovascular diseases [13]. They reduce blood pressure and enhance blood supply to the heart by inducing endothelial nitric oxide synthase [14].

Statins are metabolized in the human liver by CYP450s, mainly CYP3A4 and CYP2C9 [15]. Drugs that influence these CYP450s may affect the pharmacokinetics and hence the response of simvastatin. Regarding the influence of statins on the CYP4 family, Kocarek and Reddy (1996) found that statins, specifically lovastatin, simvastatin, and fluvastatin, increase CYP4A mRNA and protein expression in cultured rat hepatocytes and in vivo in rat liver. In contrast, pravastatin had minimal impact on CYP4A expression [16].

Since 20-HETE is emerging as a novel therapeutic target for hypertension [3, 4], and statins are recognized for their cardioprotective and antihypertensive effects [13], their impact on 20-HETE formation remains unexplored. Accordingly, the current study aimed to investigate the effect of the most commonly prescribed statins on the formation of 20-HETE in human liver microsomes and to perform docking studies of the statins with the major 20-HETE-synthesizing CYPs, CYP4A11, CYP4F2 and CYP4F3B.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Arachidonic acid, 20-HETE and its isotope (as an internal control) were obtained from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Simvastatin, atorvastatin, rosuvastatin, and reduced β-nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Pooled human liver microsomes were obtained from BD Gentest (Woburn, MA, USA) to evaluate the inhibition activity of simvastatin on 20-HETE formation.

In vitro inhibition assay

To evaluate the effects of statins on 20-HETE formation, seven concentrations of simvastatin, atorvastatin, and rosuvastatin (0.1 to 1000 µM) were incubated with 15 µg of pooled human liver microsomes and 150 µM arachidonic acid under the incubation condition described previously [17]. We used human liver microsomes because CYP450 enzymes, including those that synthesize 20-HETE, are highly expressed in the liver [18]. This allows for a clear assessment of statin’s influence on 20-HETE production. Reactions were initiated by adding 10 mM NADPH and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. The IC50 value, the concentration of the inhibitor required to inhibit the enzyme function by 50%, was calculated as described previously [19]. The optimization of the incubation time and liver microsome amount is provided in supplementary Tables 1 and 2, respectively.

20-HETE level analysis

The extraction and analysis of 20-HETE were performed according to a previously published method developed by the same research center, which was also applied in the present study [20]. 20-HETE was extracted using ethyl acetate after lowering the pH of the media to 3.4 with 2 N H2SO4, and the pH was verified using a calibrated pH meter (inoLab® Benchtop Meter, Wissenschaftlich-Technische Werkstätten, Germany). The ethyl acetate was evaporated then the residue was reconstituted in 0.1 mL ethanol. 20-HETE was quantified using a liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) system with a reverse-phase column Luna C8 (150 mm × 2.0 mm, 5 m; Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA). HPLC was conducted using an Agilent 1200 series HPLC system. The mobile phases consisted of 0.5 mM ammonium formate (pH 3.3).

(A) and acetonitrile containing 0.5 mM ammonium formate (B) with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The system was coupled to an API 5500 mass spectrophotometer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) with electrospray ionization in negative ion mode. The mass transitions monitored included 20-HETE (m/z 319.4 to 301.2) and 20-HETE-d6 (m/z 325.4 to 281.4). The LC-MS method used in this study was validated in a previously published study by Shinde et al., (2012), which established the method’s accuracy, precision, and linearity for detecting arachidonic acid metabolites, including 20-HETE [20].

Molecular docking

Protein Preparation

The crystal structure of CYP4F2, CYP4A11, and CYP4F3B are not available on the RCSB Protein Data Bank (www.rcsb.org), but the are available as predicted structures on AlphaFold (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/) under the codes: AF-P78329-F1-model_v4 for CYP4F2, AF-Q02928-F1-model_v4 for CYP4A11, and AF-Q08477-F1-model_v4 for CYP4F3B.

All structures were superimposed against CYP4B1 (PDB ID: 6C94) since it’s co-crystalized with (HET0016), a compound that was proven to be a potent and selective inhibitor of 20-HETE [21], to confirm the active site coordinates and to add the heme molecule to the structure, the superimposition process was done using the “superimpose by sequence alignment” protocol in BIOVIA Discovery Studio 16., then each protein was prepared further using AutoDockTools 1.5.7 by adding polar hydrogen atoms and assigning Kollman charges.

Ligand preparation

HET0016 was obtained from the crystal structure of CYP4B1 as mentioned earlier, then it was separated from the protein and saved as “mol2” file, Simvastatin, Atorvastatin, and Rosuvastatin were obtained from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) as a 3D conformer. All compounds were minimized using employing the Universal Force Field (UFF) in Avogadro 1.2.0 software and converted into “pdbqt” format using AutoDockTools 1.5.7.

Docking simulation

After loading the ligands and calculating their Gasteiger charges, grid maps of the atom types were calculated using Autogrid4 with the grid box coordinates from the crystal structure of CYP4B1 and a unified box size of 40 × 40 × 40 points with the default grid spacing value of 0.375 Å. AutoDock4 was then utilized to execute the docking simulations with all default parameters and for 100 Lamarckian Genetic Algorithm runs. Results were analysed for conformation with the lowest energy of binding (LEB) and was visualized with the interacting/surrounding amino acids through BIOVIA Discovery Studio 16.1.

Results

Figure 1 shows the percentage of inhibition of 20-HETE formation in the human liver microsome by simvastatin, atorvastatin, and rosuvastatin in different concentrations. The results show a dose-dependent response of simvastatin against 20-HETE formation; as the statin concentration increases, inhibition rises. The calculated IC50 value is 10.4 µM. In contrast, atorvastatin and rosuvastatin did not show inhibition activity toward 20-HETE formation (Fig. 1).

The results of docking assay are presented in Table 1. Simvastatin has a high affinity toward CYP4F2 with LEB value of -9.93 kcal/mol and a better affinity toward CYP4A11 and CYP4F3B with LEB value of -11.06 and − 11.55 kcal/mol, respectively. HET0016 displayed moderate binding across the enzymes, with LEB values of -7.64, -7.78, and − 7.83 kcal/mol for CYP4F2, CYP4A11, and CYP4F3B, respectively. Rosuvastatin showed moderate binding to CYP4F3B (LEB = -7.04 kcal/mol) and no affinity toward CYP4F2 (LEB = + 3.02 kcal/mol) and only weak affinity toward CYP4A11 (LEB = -3.03 kcal/mol). While, atorvastatin demonstrated unfavorable binding profile to all the CYP enzymes, with positive LEB of (+ 38.06, + 24.33, + 26.69 kcal/mol) for CYP4F2, CYP4A11, and CYP4F3B, respectively suggesting it does not interact effectively with the active site.

Simvastatin formed hydrogen bonds with residues GLU328 and VAL397 in CYP4F2, and GLY388 and GLU321 in CYP4A11. Key hydrogen bond interactions for HET0016 were with residues GLU328 and THR332 in CYP4F2, and SER119 in CYP4A11, while other residues were also involved in binding (Table 1).

In CYP4F3B, simvastatin forms a hydrogen bond with ALA397, and interacts with several hydrophobic and aromatic residues such as TYR125, VAL395, LEU504, PHE327, LEU137, and PHE124, in close proximity to the heme group. In contrast, HET0016 forms hydrogen bonds with GLU328 and THR332, and interacts with a smaller subset of residues including VAL395, LEU137, and PHE124.

Figure 4 shows the 3D interaction that each ligand (20-HETE and HET0016) is making with CYP4F2, CYP4F3B and CYP4A11. One of the important interactions with CYP enzymes is the bond with the heme molecule, HET0016 can interact with the heme in CYP4F2 and CYP4A11, but simvastatin can only interact with the heme in CYP4A11. Both ligands interact with conserved residues critical for substrate binding (e.g., GLU328 in CYP4F2 and GLU321 in CYP4A11) and coordinate with the heme iron (highlighted in pink in Fig. 4), which is characteristic of orthosteric binding in cytochrome P450 enzymes, by competing with the natural substrate for binding to the active site.

Discussion

Simvastatin is a widely prescribed medication with multiple beneficial effects in cardiovascular diseases. It has been observed that simvastatin reduces blood pressure in hypercholesterolemic patients [22]. Accordingly, it is recommended to prescribe simvastatin to patients with elevated serum cholesterol and blood pressure [13]. However, the exact mechanism by which simvastatin reduces blood pressure is still not fully understood. Some studies have shown that simvastatin increases the levels of the vasodilator nitric oxide and decreases the vasoconstrictive effect of angiotensin II [14]. This study adds a new potential mechanism for the antihypertensive effect of simvastatin, which involves reducing 20-HETE formation through the inhibition of arachidonic acid-metabolizing CYP450 enzymes. Since 20-HETE is considered a new target for hypertension treatment [10], simvastatin may have potential in managing hypertension in patients with elevated 20-HETE levels. The results of this study enhance our understanding of the beneficial effects of simvastatin on the cardiovascular system. However, clinical studies are needed to assess the effect of simvastatin on plasma 20-HETE levels in hypertensive patients and to confirm the findings of this study.

Safe inhibitors of 20-HETE formation are currently lacking. Experimentally, HET0016 and SKF-525 A are widely used in in vitro studies [23, 24]; however, their safety for clinical use remains unclear. There is a need for safe inhibitors of 20-HETE formation, as current experimental inhibitors are primarily used in laboratory settings, and their safety for human clinical use remains uncertain. In contrast, simvastatin is widely used with an acceptable safety profile. The major unwanted side effects of simvastatin include constipation, myopathy, and elevated liver enzymes which are generally well-tolerated [25]. Thus, simvastatin is a safer candidate than experimental 20-HETE formation inhibitors.

Wu et al. reported that sesamin inhibits the production of 20-HETE in vitro, with an IC₅₀ value below 20 µM. Additionally, the same study showed that sesamin consumption significantly reduced both plasma and urinary 20-HETE levels [26]. In our study, simvastatin was identified as an inhibitor of 20-HETE production in human liver microsomes, with an IC₅₀ of 10.4 µM. Furthermore, kinetic analysis using Lineweaver-Burk plots revealed that simvastatin acts as a competitive inhibitor, as indicated by an increase in Km without affecting Vmax. This suggests that simvastatin competes with arachidonic acid for the active site of CYP4A11, 4F2 and 4F3B. Moreover, the reported maximum plasma concentration of simvastatin is approximately 14.84 µM [27], which is close to the IC₅₀ observed in our assay. This suggests a potential in vivo inhibitory effect of simvastatin on 20-HETE production. However, further clinical studies are required to confirm whether simvastatin can effectively reduce plasma 20-HETE levels in humans.

Although the kinetic data obtained from Lineweaver-Burk analysis are consistent with a competitive inhibition mechanism, we cannot conclude that simvastatin inhibits CYP4F2, CYP4A11, and CYP4F3B through the same mechanism, as this study utilized human liver microsomes rather than purified individual CYP4 enzymes.

In the human liver, CYP4A11, CYP4F2, CYP4F3B are the main CYP450s involved in 20-HETE production [28, 29]. CYP4A11 is the main enzyme in the liver for arachidonic acid metabolism, while CYP4Fs play a minor role [30]. Our in vitro assays showed that simvastatin inhibited the production of 20-HETE in human liver cells in a dose-dependent manner. Additionally, computer-model predicted that simvastatin has a higher affinity toward CYP4A11 and CYP4F3B compared to CYP4F2. However, the expression of CYP4F3B in liver is low [29]. Therefore, it is likely that simvastatin inhibits the production of 20-HETE in the liver predominantly through CYP4A11 inhibition.

We acknowledge in this study that the concentration of arachidonic acid used in the in vitro inhibitory assay (150 µM) is higher than the physiological range typically found in tissues (0.1 to 10 µM), although levels can exceed up to 500 µM under certain pathological conditions [30]. However, the selected concentration was based on established scientific protocols, which recommend using a substrate concentration near the Km value according to Michaelis-Menten kinetics [31]. Given the discrepancy between physiological and experimental arachidonic acid concentrations, caution is warranted when extrapolating these in vitro findings to human physiology. Therefore, in vivo studies are necessary to validate the relevance of our results.

The docking assays indicate differences among the statins regarding their inhibition of CYP4A11, CYP4F2, and CYP4F3B. Simvastatin is an effective inhibitor of these enzymes, whereas other commonly prescribed statins, atorvastatin and rosuvastatin, showed no potential inhibition of 20-HETE formation, which was confirmed by using in vitro inhibition assay in this study. This difference is attributed to variations in the chemical structure between simvastatin and the other tested statins. The main chemical difference in the structure of these compounds is that simvastatin has a lower molecular weight and less rotatable bonds than both atorvastatin and rosuvastatin, which makes simvastatin structurally more similar to the reported inhibitor HET0016.

There are some limitations to this study: other less commonly used statins were not tested, and no in vivo experiments were conducted to confirm docking and in vitro results. Accordingly, the results of this research encourage us to do future in vivo animal and human studies regarding the influence of simvastatin on 20-HETE levels and correlate with the cardiovascular functions. Additionally, since 20-HETE plays a role in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular diseases, further in vitro inhibitory assays of simvastatin on 20-HETE production using heart microsomes may provide valuable insights.

Conclusions

In conclusion, simvastatin is a competitive inhibitor of 20-HETE formation through the suppression of arachidonic acid-metabolizing CYP450 enzymes. This study suggests that simvastatin may reduce blood pressure by inhibiting 20-HETE formation. Further clinical studies are required to validate these findings, assess simvastatin’s effect on plasma 20-HETE levels, and determine its full cardiovascular benefits.
