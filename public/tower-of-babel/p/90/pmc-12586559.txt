Biocompatible and antibacterial co-electrospun tragacanth gum and polyaniline nanofibers for skin tissue engineering
Authors: Zahra Akbari Kheirabadi, Zahra Nazemi, Mahsa Janmohammadi, Fatemeh Radmanesh, Hamid Reza Moslemi, Mohammad Sadegh Nourbakhsh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeveloping novel nanofibrous skin substitutes using biomaterials that exhibit biocompatibility, suitable mechanical strength, and inherent antibacterial activity is essential. In this study, we fabricated polyvinyl alcohol/tragacanth gum/polyaniline (PVA/TG/PANI) nanofibrous membranes via co-electrospinning of PVA/TG and PVA/PANI. The membranes were characterized using scanning electron microscopy, contact angle measurements, swelling tests, and mechanical examination. The co-electrospun nanofibers achieved an average fiber diameter of 176.45 ± 29 nm. Contact angle measurements confirmed the hydrophilicity of these membranes, which also exhibited favorable mechanical properties, including sufficient strength and flexibility. Cytocompatibility was assessed by culturing HDF-4 cells; the co-electrospun PVA/TG/PANI membrane demonstrated the highest cell viability at 91.94%, surpassing that of PVA/TG nanofibers. Furthermore, antibacterial activity against E. coli and S. aureus was significantly enhanced in the co-electrospun nanofibers compared to the PVA/TG and PVA/PANI counterparts. Based on these findings, we propose the co-electrospun nanofibrous membrane as a promising scaffold candidate for skin tissue engineering applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22553-7.Keywords: Co-electrospinning, Nanofibrous membrane, Polyaniline, Tragacanth gumSubject terms: Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

The skin, as the largest organ of the body, serves a critical role as a protective barrier against pathogens, dehydration, external injuries, and diseases1,2. Disruption or defects in the skin can compromise tissue integrity, potentially resulting in acute or chronic wounds, which can profoundly and adversely affect individuals’ quality of life depending on injury severity3. Effective skin regeneration requires complex coordination between the epidermis and dermis, along with synergistic interactions among microenvironmental signals and migrating cells at the wound site during healing. Autografts and allografts have traditionally been preferred clinical options for treating extensive skin loss4; however, these approaches face significant limitations, including infection risk, immune rejection, and limited availability. To overcome these challenges, skin tissue engineering has emerged as a promising strategy to facilitate tissue regeneration and improve healing through scaffolds5. Engineered scaffolds provide an appropriate substrate for cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation, supporting regeneration of damaged skin. These scaffolds can be fabricated in various forms, including films, sponges, hydrogels, nanofibers, and three-dimensional printed constructs6. Among these, nanofibrous scaffolds produced via electrospinning have garnered significant attention due to their high surface-to-volume ratio and porous architecture, which closely mimic the extracellular matrix of native tissues. Additionally, this nanofibrous microenvironment promotes cell adhesion, proliferation, migration, and tissue development7.

Various synthetic and natural polymers have been employed in the preparation of electrospun nanofibrous scaffolds, considering the diverse physical, mechanical, and biological requirements8,9. Several recent studies have focused extensively on the electrospinning of carbohydrate polymer gums due to their bio-renewable nature and environmentally sustainable processing characteristics, as well as their favorable properties for promoting tissue healing. For instance, nanofibers derived from tragacanth gum (TG) have demonstrated excellent compatibility with living cells and a significant healing capacity in injured tissues, ranging from skin to periodontal damage10–15. TG is an anionic polysaccharide with a highly branched structure composed of various sugars, methoxy content, and both water-soluble and water-swellable components. This natural gum consists of linear chains of 1,4-linked α-D-galacturonic acid residues with numerous side chains comprising D-xylose, L-fucose, D-galactose, and trace amounts of D-glucuronic acid, all bonded to the main acidic backbone16. Some of these sugar components enable TG to enhance fibroblast cell adhesion and proliferation, thereby facilitating the healing process of skin defects11,17. On the other hand, these unique sugar components enable TG to mitigate bacterial growth in wound sites, where they provide an ideal environment for inflammation and microbial infection due to their warm, moist, and nutrient-rich conditions18. Nevertheless, the antibacterial efficacy of TG is constrained; thus, it is advisable to enhance its activity through the incorporation of supplementary antibacterial agents to attain desirable therapeutic outcomes16,19–21.

Polyaniline (PANI) nanofibers were applied to the TG nanofibrous membrane through the co-electrospinning method to enhance the antibacterial effects of TG. PANI is a conductive polymer that has gained considerable attention in tissue engineering due to its biocompatibility, electrical conductivity, and antibacterial properties. These characteristics broaden its potential applications in biomedical fields, including controlled drug delivery, biosensors, neural probes, and tissue engineering, particularly where infection control is critical22–25. Conductive polymers such as PANI possess the remarkable ability to mimic electrical signals present in wound environments, thereby stimulating and enhancing cellular processes such as proliferation and differentiation. This unique property can significantly accelerate the wound healing process26,27. For example, Najafian et al. focused on designing and developing electrically conductive nanofibrous scaffolds composed of tragacanth gum–polyaniline and polyvinyl alcohol. Their study demonstrated that these scaffolds significantly enhanced fibroblast cell adhesion and proliferation, suggesting their suitability for wound healing applications. This research provides valuable insights into the biocompatibility and cellular response to conductive nanofibrous materials, which are crucial factors in developing advanced wound dressings and tissue engineering constructs28,29. While PANI is a promising candidate for enhancing wound healing, it is not ideal for electrospinning on its own. To overcome this limitation, PANI is frequently blended with other synthetic polymers that impart desirable characteristics. Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) is one such polymer, offering elasticity and flexibility that are particularly beneficial for skin tissue engineering applications30,31.

To enhance the antibacterial properties of TG in wound healing applications, nanofibrous membranes of TG and PANI were created using a co-electrospinning method. Subsequently, the morphology, chemical composition, swelling capacity, and mechanical properties of the nanofibrous membranes. The cytocompatibility of all the fibers was tested with HDF-4 cells, and the antimicrobial effectiveness of the membranes was determined through growth inhibition and antibiofilm assessments.

Materials and methods

Materials

PANI, with a molecular weight of 50,000 Da was purchased from Sigma Aldrich. PVA (Mw = 72,000 Da), camphorsulfonic acid (CSA), dimethylformamide (DMF), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), and glutaraldehyde (50%, GA) were obtained from Merck. TG was sourced from Isfahan Province, Iran. Hydrochloric acid (37%, HCl) and absolute ethanol (EtOH) was obtained from Mojallali, Iran.

Electrospinning of TG nanofibrous membranes

To purify TG, 1 g of the gum was dissolved in 100 mL of distilled water and stirred for 24 h at room temperature. Next, 100 mL of a 1 M NaOH solution was added to the TG solution, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 6 h at 4 °C. The resulting solution was neutralized with HCl. Subsequently, a white precipitate was formed upon the addition of EtOH, which was collected by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 20 min at 10 °C32. The precipitate was subjected to dialysis for 48 h and dried for further analysis. The characterization of the purified TG can be found in the supplementary data (Fig. S1).

The PVA/TG nanofibrous membranes were fabricated using the electrospinning technique. First, purified TG was dissolved in distilled water to obtain a homogeneous solution. The PVA was also dissolved in distilled water at 60 °C for 3 h. The electrospinning solutions were prepared by blending the PVA solution with the TG solution in the following volume ratios of 50/50, 60/40, 70/30, and 80/20 (Table 1). The PVA/TG solution was then loaded into a 5 mL glass syringe and electrospun at a constant flow rate of 0.5 mL/h. The resulting electrospun nanofibrous membranes were crosslinked using a mixture of 25% GA and 37% HCl in a 1:3 (v/v) ratio, placed in a desiccator, and incubated in an oven at 37 °C for 24 h.

Electrospinning of PANI nanofibrous membranes

The PANI solutions were prepared by dissolving 0.1 g of PANI and 0.16 g of CSA in 10 mL of DMF for 3 days at 45 °C. The PANI solution was then sonicated using a probe sonicator for up to 30 min at a power of 20 W. Polymer solutions were prepared by blending the PVA solution with the PANI solution in volume ratios of 50/50, 60/40, 70/30, and 80/20. The voltage, flow rate, and tip nozzle-to-collector distance were set to 15 kV, 0.5 mL/h, and 10 cm, respectively. Finally, the nanofibers were crosslinked using the same method applied to the PVA/TG nanofibers.

Co-electrospinning of TG-PANI nanofibrous membranes

The PVA/TG and PVA/PANI nanofibers were fabricated using a two-nozzle electrospinning method. The PVA/TG solution in ratios of 60/40 and 80/20, as well as the PVA/PANI solution in a ratio of 80/20, were each loaded into separate syringes for individual electrospinning. The nanofibers were electrospun from different nozzles and crosslinked according to the aforementioned protocols.

Morphological evaluation

The morphologies of the nanofibers (including PVA/TG, PVA/PANI, and PVA/TG -PVA/PANI membranes) were observed using a scanning electron microscope (XL30 Philips) before and after cross-linking. Before observation, the nanofiber surfaces were coated with a gold layer. The average diameter of the nanofibers was determined using ImageJ software (version 1.54, National Institute of Health, Bethesda, ML, USA; https://imagej.net/ij/).

Chemical characterization

The chemical compositions of the PVA/TG, PVA/PANI, and PVA/TG-PVA/PANI nanofibrous membranes were evaluated in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 using a Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, 8400 SHIMADZU).

The crystalline structure of the electrospun nanofibrous membranes was investigated by X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis using a D8-Advance diffractometer (Bruker, Germany) over a 2θ range of 5°–70°.

The thermal behavior of the nanofiber membranes was investigated using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA), model STA PT 1600, Linseis (Germany), with a heating rate of 10 °C/min over a temperature range of 25–800 °C.

The thermal behavior of the nanofiber membranes was analyzed in a nitrogen atmosphere using a differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) device (model DSC-400, SANAM, Iran). The temperature was varied from 25 °C to 300 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min.

Contact angle

The hydrophilicity of the nanofibrous membranes was assessed using contact angle measurements (CA-ES10). The drop method involved placing a water droplet on the surface of each sample to measure the contact angle.

Results and discussion

Morphology of nanofibers

In this study, a series of nanofibrous membranes was produced via electrospinning of PVA/TG and PVA/PANI, as well as by co-electrospinning these materials, targeting potential applications in skin tissue engineering. PVA served as the carrier polymer, while TG primarily imparted hydrophilic properties to the formulations. PANI was incorporated to enhance antibacterial activity by inhibiting bacterial growth and biofilm formation. Initial evaluation involved SEM imaging and fiber diameter measurements. SEM images of all PVA/TG (Fig. S2) and PVA/PANI (Fig. S3) membranes were taken to select appropriate electrospinning parameters and material ratios. Parameters listed in Table 1 were systematically assessed to identify conditions producing uniform nanofibers suitable for the intended applications. Among the tested samples (S1–S11), S5 (60/40 polymer ratio) and S11 (80/20) were selected due to their highly uniform, bead-free morphology with minimal fiber entanglement. Other parameter sets exhibited minor bead formation or fiber entanglement, making them less suitable for further study (see Supplementary Fig. S2).

Based on morphological evaluations, S5 (P60/T40) and S11 (P80/T20) were chosen among the PVA/TG nanofiber samples, and P80/AN20 was selected from the PVA/PANI group. Figure 1 shows the morphology of these selected membranes, all exhibiting smooth, uniform, and bead-free fibers. Before cross-linking, the average fiber diameters were 193 ± 10.38 nm, 184.44 ± 28 nm, and 160.48 ± 27 nm for P60/T40, P80/T20, and P80/AN20, respectively. Post cross-linking, the membranes retained a smooth and uniform morphology with only minor fiber distortions. The average fiber diameters increased to 203.61 ± 56 nm, 205.24 ± 44 nm, and 253.20 ± 46 nm, respectively, consistent with expected swelling behavior during cross-linking.

Notably, the co-electrospun nanofibers also maintained uniform morphology without bead formation and exhibited fine diameters, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The average fiber diameters of co-electrospun P60/T40-P80/AN20 and P80/T20-P80/AN20 nanofibers were 142.53 ± 34 nm and 176.45 ± 29 nm, respectively. After cross-linking, fiber diameters increased to 244.07 ± 68 nm for P60/T40-P80/AN20 and 258.79 ± 59 nm for P80/T20-P80/AN20.

Chemical characterization

The chemical composition of PVA, P60/T40, P80/T20, P80/AN20, P60/T40-P80/AN20, and P80/T20-P80/AN20 nanofibers were analyzed by FTIR (Fig. 3). The characteristic peaks of PVA at approximately 3000–3500 cm⁻¹, 2900 cm⁻¹, 1720 cm⁻¹, 1082 cm⁻¹, and 835 cm⁻¹ correspond to the O–H stretching, asymmetric and symmetric CH₂ stretching, C = O stretching, C–O stretching, and C–C bond vibrations, respectively34–36. The peak observed at 1738 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the carbonyl group vibrations of TG, thereby confirming the presence of TG in the nanofibers containing this polymer37. PANI was characterized by the bands associated with quinone and benzene rings at 1580 cm⁻¹ and 1425 cm⁻¹, respectively31. Additional benzene ring peaks were detected at 745 cm⁻¹ and 690 cm−1. The C–N stretching vibration appeared at 1290 cm⁻¹, while the peak near 1017 cm⁻¹ is related to the acetal ring and ether bond formed as a result of crosslinking reactions between the hydroxyl groups of PVA and glutaraldehyde22.

FTIR spectrum of electrospun nanofibers.

The XRD patterns of the electrospun nanofibrous membranes are shown in Fig. 4. The pure PVA sample exhibits a sharp peak at around 2θ = 19.5°, indicating its semi-crystalline nature38. Upon incorporation of TG at different ratios (P60/T40 and P80/T20), the intensity of this characteristic peak decreased. This reduction in crystallinity is attributed to the disruption of intermolecular hydrogen bonding within PVA chains and the formation of new interactions between PVA and TG chains38. For the P80/AN20 membranes, the XRD pattern shows a broad peak centered around 2θ ≈ 20°, which overlaps with the characteristic peaks of both PVA and the amorphous region of PANI. The absence of distinct peaks for PANI in this blend confirms its amorphous nature, as previously reported39,40. Furthermore, the slight peak shift and reduced intensity in the combination compared to pure PVA may result from partial disruption of PVA crystallinity caused by the incorporation of PANI.

XRD patterns of electrospun nanofibers.

In co-electrospun membranes (P60/T40–P80/AN20 and P80/T20–P80/AN20), the XRD patterns exhibit slightly broader and less intense peaks around 2θ = 19–20°, indicating a further reduction in crystallinity due to the combined effects of TG and PANI. These overlapping profiles support successful blending and good interaction between the components.

TGA was performed to assess the thermal behavior of all nanofiber membrane samples (Fig. 5A). The first stage of weight loss occurred in all samples between approximately 30 and 200 °C and is attributed to the evaporation of free water absorbed within the nanofiber matrix. Notably, the P60/T40-P80/AN20 sample exhibited an earlier and more gradual weight loss in the range of 30 to 100 °C.

A) TGA and B) DSC diagrams of PVA, P60/T40, P80/T20, P80/AN20, P60/T40-P80/AN20 and P80/T20-P80/AN20 nanofibrous membranes.

The second phase of weight loss, observed between 250 and 400 °C, corresponds to the thermal decomposition of PVA, TG, and PANI. Pure PVA showed a decomposition peak at about 320 °C, whereas in P60/T40 and P80/T20 samples, this peak shifted to approximately 333–336 °C, indicating an interaction between PVA and TG that enhances thermal stability, consistent with findings by Ghorbani et al. 14. Nanofiber membranes containing PANI exhibited earlier and more intense thermal degradation at around 284 °C, reflecting the chemical instability of PANI and corroborating observations by Bhadra et al. 41.

The P60/T40-P80/AN20 membrane showed the greatest weight loss, attributed to the presence of PANI and the doping effect of CSA. Samples containing TG (P60/T40 and P80/T20) demonstrated lower weight loss, while P80/AN20 exhibited increased weight loss. By contrast, the co-electrospun P80/T20-P80/AN20 membrane displayed enhanced thermal stability and reduced weight loss, likely due to improved interactions among PVA, TG, and PANI in the integrated structure formed through simultaneous electrospinning. Overall, TG incorporation improves the thermal stability of nanofiber membranes14, whereas the presence of CSA-doped PANI reduces stability41,42. In P60/T40-P80/AN20, which contains a higher TG proportion, the lower decomposition peak temperature and increased weight loss are likely due to unfavorable CSA interactions with PVA and TG, possibly due to chemical interference between the acidic groups of CSA and the hydroxyl (-OH) groups in PVA and TG, which can weaken hydrogen bonds between molecules and also weaken the polysaccharide structure of TG through acidic interactions, reducing thermal stability. This behavior is consistent with the findings of J. Bhadra et al., who investigated the interactions based on hydroxyl groups and the effects of doping acids on thermal stability reduction41, whereas P80/T20-P80/AN20, with less TG, exhibited superior thermal stability.

Figure 5B presents the DSC thermograms of electrospun nanofibrous membranes composed of PVA, P60/T40, P80/T20, P80/AN20, P60/T40-P80/AN20, and P80/T20-P80/AN20. PVA, a thermoplastic polymer, exhibits a dehydration endothermic peak at 58 °C and a melting peak at 192 °C, consistent with literature report37. In the P60/T40, P80/T20, and P80/AN20 samples, as well as in the co-electrospun nanofibers, an endothermic peak near 100 °C was observed, attributed to dehydration, aligning with findings reported for PANI-PVA and PVA/TG blends37,41. Additionally, these samples exhibited melting peaks in the range of 170–180 °C, which are lower than the melting temperature of pure PVA. This reduction in melting temperature agrees with previously published data37. The decrease in melting point observed in TG-containing samples is likely due to the presence of swollen, insoluble TG domains that retain water and act as a plasticizer for PVA, thereby reducing its melting temperature37. Furthermore, samples containing PANI also showed decreased melting temperatures, possibly resulting from interactions between PVA and CSA-doped PANI, which diminishes the crystallinity of PVA. This effect is similar to the crystallinity decrease reported for PCL–PANI and PVA/PANI blends41,43.

Contact angle and swelling ratio

The hydrophilicity of the nanofibrous membranes was evaluated via contact angle measurements, as presented in Fig. 6A. Among the tested samples, the pure PVA membrane exhibited the highest contact angle of 55.53°, indicating the lowest hydrophilicity. In contrast, the P80/AN20 membrane demonstrated the lowest contact angle at 20.13 ± 2.4°, reflecting significantly enhanced hydrophilicity compared to pure PVA (P < 0.01). The other membranes—P60/T40 (36.67 ± 0.2°), P80/T20 (39.37 ± 4.4°), P60/T40-P80/AN20 (25.29 ± 1.9°), and P80/T20-P80/AN20 (36.27 ± 3.2°)—exhibited intermediate contact angles. Furthermore, the co-electrospun membranes displayed significantly greater hydrophilicity relative to PVA, with P60/T40-P80/AN20 showing a statistically significant difference (P < 0.01). No significant differences were detected between single-nozzle and dual-nozzle electrospun fibers, except between P80/T20-P80/AN20 and P80/AN20 (P < 0.05).

(A) Water contact angle and (B) Swelling ratio of the PVA, P60/T40, P80/T20, P80/AN20, P60/T40-P80/AN20 and P80/T20-P80/AN20 electrospun nanofibers (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ±SD, n = 3).

The hydrophilic properties of scaffolds are critical in promoting cell attachment and proliferation, thereby facilitating cellular interactions that support tissue regeneration processes in injured tissues22–24. The enhanced hydrophilicity observed in all nanofibrous membranes, relative to pure PVA as the carrier polymer, is attributed to the incorporation of TG and PANI in the fiber formulations. TG contains abundant hydrophilic functional groups, including hydroxyl and carboxyl moieties, which confer its intrinsic hydrophilicity44. Additionally, PANI has been shown to enhance the hydrophilicity of electrospun silk fibroin fibers, further confirming the hydrophilic character of this conductive polymer45.

The swelling behavior of the nanofibrous membranes correlated with the water contact angle results presented in Fig. 6B. All membranes reached swelling equilibrium after 24 h of immersion in PBS. Following this period, the water absorption percentages were approximately 516.67% for PVA, 742.96% for P60/T40, and 632.73% for P80/T20 nanofibers. The swelling ratio increased with higher TG content in the membranes; however, no statistically significant differences were observed among the samples. This outcome underscores the pivotal role of TG in facilitating water penetration into the fiber matrix, attributable to the high-water adsorption capacity of this natural gum14. Additionally, the P80/AN20 nanofibers exhibited a swelling ratio of 536.24%. The co-electrospun membranes showed comparable swelling behavior, with values of 582.43% for P60/T40-P80/AN20 and 546.58% for P80/T20-P80/AN20, and no meaningful differences were detected. Statistical analysis using one-way ANOVA confirmed the absence of significant differences among all groups.

Biodegradation

Tissue engineering aims to regenerate new tissues, with substituted scaffolds providing support at the sites of damaged tissue. To achieve successful regeneration, the degradation rate and capacity of scaffold materials must be well matched to the rate of new tissue formation to yield optimal regenerative outcomes46. Figure 7 illustrates the degradation properties of the various electrospun membranes evaluated in this study. All experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3). Statistical analyses were performed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with significance assigned at P < 0.05. The degradation rate of PVA/TG nanofibrous membranes exceeded that of pure PVA. Moreover, the degradation rate increased with higher TG content (P60/T40 > P80/T20), likely due to enhanced water absorption in membranes containing greater amounts of TG, which facilitates polymer chain dissociation. These results are consistent with the observed swelling behavior and the intrinsic hydrophilicity of TG, attributed to its hydroxyl and carboxyl functional groups.

Biodegradability of the PVA, P60/T40, P80/T20, P80/AN20, P60/T40-P80/AN20 and P80/T20-P80/AN20 electrospun nanofibers) ± SD, n = 3).

Furthermore, the co-electrospun nanofibrous membranes P60/T40-P80/AN20 and P80/T20-P80/AN20 exhibited significantly greater weight loss (60.39 ± 7% and 40.27 ± 1%, respectively) compared to P60/T40, P80/T20, and P80/AN20 membranes (19.98 ± 18%, 6.44 ± 4%, and 14.63 ± 1%, respectively) after 28 days of immersion in PBS. Specifically, P60/T40-P80/AN20 showed highly significant weight loss compared to P60/T40, P80/T20, and P80/AN20 (p < 0.0001). For P80/T20-P80/AN20, weight loss was significantly greater compared to P60/T40 (p < 0.05), P80/T20 (p < 0.0001), and P80/AN20 (p < 0.001). The highest degradation rate observed in co-electrospun membranes can be attributed to the presence of TG within the fibers, which facilitates water molecule penetration and promotes structural dissociation. These findings are consistent with previously reported studies highlighting the role of TG in enhancing the biodegradability of polymeric scaffolds46,47.

Mechanical properties

The similarity of the mechanical properties of scaffolds to those of native tissues is a critical requirement for effectively supporting the defect site and promoting successful healing. For skin tissue regeneration, a tensile strength of approximately 7.6 MPa and a modulus in the range of 0.1–0.2 MPa are considered acceptable48. The mechanical characterization results (n = 3) are presented in Fig. 8A–D. Among all samples, the P80/T20 nanofibrous membrane exhibited the highest tensile strength (14.31 ± 1.2 MPa), which was significantly greater than that of PVA (7.13 ± 0.4 MPa) (P < 0.01) and P80/AN20 (7.13 ± 0.5 MPa) (P < 0.01) membranes, while no significant difference was observed compared to the other samples. Statistical comparisons were carried out using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with significance assigned at P < 0.05. Furthermore, the P60/T40 and P80/T20 nanofibrous membranes demonstrated higher Young’s moduli (3.65 ± 1.2 MPa and 3.75 ± 1.0 MPa, respectively) than the PVA membrane (1.57 ± 0.1 MPa) (P < 0.05) (Fig. 8C).

Mechanical properties of the PVA, P60/T40, P80/T20, P80/AN20, P60/T40-P80/AN20 and P80/T20-P80/AN20 electrospun nanofibers. (A) Stress-strain curve; (B) Tensile strength; C) Young’s modulus; (D) Elongation (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ±SD, n = 3).

Interestingly, the incorporation of TG into PVA nanofibrous membranes significantly decreased the elongation-at-break of P60/T40 nanofibers (P < 0.05) (Fig. 8D). This reduction can be attributed to the highly branched and rigid structure of TG, which restricts polymer chain mobility and thus limits elongation under tensile loading. Additionally, hydrogen bonding between the hydroxyl groups of PVA and TG may further constrain the extension of both polymer chains46. In contrast, P80/AN20 nanofibers exhibited the highest elongation, indicating superior flexibility compared to other membranes. This enhanced flexibility is advantageous, as it improves scaffold adaptability to the dynamic environment of the wound site49,50. Although no statistically significant differences were observed in the elongation of co-electrospun nanofibrous membranes relative to other samples, these membranes exhibited lower elongation than P80/AN20 fibers. The reduced elongation in co-electrospun fibers may result from insufficient interactions between the two electrospun materials. Specifically, in co-electrospinning PVA/TG and PVA/PANI systems, weak hydrogen bonding appears to be the primary mode of fiber attachment, which may yield fibers with limited strength and elongation capacity under tensile stress.

Considering the physical and mechanical evaluation results, PVA, P80/T20, P80/AN20, and P80/T20-P80/AN20 nanofibrous membranes were selected for further biological investigations, including assessments of cell viability and antibacterial activity. These membranes not only exhibited acceptable mechanical properties but also demonstrated suitable handling characteristics and could be easily removed from the collector foil without tearing.

Cell viability

Wound healing is a complex biological process that requires the presence of a biocompatible scaffold at the injury site, capable of promoting cellular activity to regulate and accelerate the healing process51. PANI and TG have been reported to possess biocompatible properties that positively influence cellular signaling, proliferation, and differentiation52,53. In this study, the cell viability of nanofibrous scaffolds containing PANI and TG was evaluated using the MTT assay on days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 14 (n = 3, two-way ANOVA, P < 0.05).

The MTT assay results demonstrated significant differences in cell viability among the nanofibrous membranes at days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 14 (Fig. 9). On day 1, a significant difference was observed between the P80/T20-P80/AN20 and P80/T20 samples (P < 0.01), suggesting a positive effect of combining PANI and TG in the co-electrospun membrane. By day 3, the P80/T20-P80/AN20 membrane exhibited significantly higher cell viability compared to both P80/T20 and PVA (P < 0.01), while P80/AN20 showed greater viability than P80/T20 (P < 0.0001). Additionally, P80/T20 demonstrated significantly higher viability than PVA (P < 0.0001). No statistically significant differences were observed among the groups on day 5, likely due to cells reaching a steady state at this stage. On day 7, the co-electrospun membrane showed significantly higher viability than P80/T20 (P < 0.001) and PVA (P < 0.05), but significantly lower viability than P80/AN20 (P < 0.05), highlighting the strong biocompatibility of the AN20-based membrane. By day 14, both P80/AN20 and P80/T20-P80/AN20 samples demonstrated markedly higher cell viability compared to P80/T20 and PVA (P < 0.0001). Furthermore, P80/T20 exhibited significantly greater viability than PVA (P < 0.001).

Cell survival measurement of the PVA, P80/T20, P80/AN20 and P80/T20-P80/AN20 electrospun nanofibers on days 1, 3, 5, 7 and 14 (48 h treatment) (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001, ±SD, n = 3).

The 24 h cell viability data (Supplementary Data, Fig. S9) showed initial cell stability, while 48 h results confirmed a significant improvement in cell proliferation. PANI-containing nanofibrous membranes (P80/AN20 and P80/T20-P80/AN20) significantly enhanced fibroblast cell viability and proliferation, consistent with Najafian et al. 29, who showed that PANI scaffolds promote cell–matrix interactions and proliferation through electrical conductivity and ECM mimicry29. Similarly, the high viability of P80/T20-P80/AN20 membranes is likely due to PANI conductivity and TG biocompatibility, which stimulate cell signaling. As scaffold conductivity is known to influence cell behavior, proliferation, and even differentiation, particularly in excitable cells29,54, these membranes show considerable potential for wound healing and skin tissue engineering, although in vivo studies are necessary to confirm their performance. These findings align with existing literature reporting enhanced cell viability and proliferation on substrates containing TG and PANI for wound healing applications11,22,26.

Scratch assay

The wound healing assay plays a pivotal role across various fields, including cancer research, drug testing, and regenerative medicine, as it provides valuable insights into cellular responses during the wound healing process. This process is classically divided into four distinct phases: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. The scratch assay, a widely employed in vitro technique, simulates cell migration and proliferation by creating a controlled cell-free area, or “wound,” within a confluent cell monolayer55.

Qualitative results from the scratch assay and quantitative data on wound closure for PVA, P80/T20, P80/AN20, and P80/T20-P80/AN20 nanofibrous membranes (n = 3) are presented in Fig. 10. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare differences, with significance set at P < 0.05. In this assay, wound closure percentage, reflected by scratch width reduction, serves as an indicator of cell migration and wound healing capacity. The P80/T20-P80/AN20 nanofibrous membrane, which promotes fibroblast proliferation and migration, achieved approximately 65.78 ± 13% wound closure, representing a significant enhancement compared to PVA (15.38 ± 6.7%, P < 0.01) and P80/T20 (30.96 ± 6.8%, P < 0.05). The stimulatory effect of TG-containing nanofibers on cell migration has been previously documented55,56. and was attributed to the presence of fucose and galactose sugar moieties in the TG branches, which enhance fibroblast adhesion and spreading, thereby facilitating cell migration and wound closure55. Microscopy images of cells on P80/T20-P80/AN20 nanofibrous membranes at 24 h show a fibroblast-like morphology, possibly suggesting favorable cell-material interactions (Supplementary Fig. S10).

(A) Images of fibroblast cells during the scratch wound healing test and (B) wound closure percentage measured from the scratch assay for the PVA, P80/T20, P80/AN20, and P80/T20-P80/AN20 electrospun nanofibers (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ±SD, n = 3).

In contrast, no significant difference was observed between the P80/T20 membrane and the PVA control (P > 0.05). Additionally, the P80/AN20 membrane exhibited a cell migration rate of 44.11 ± 10%, significantly outperforming the PVA membrane (P < 0.05). These results emphasize the beneficial role of conductive polymers in enhancing the wound healing process. Although conductive polymers improved healing capacity compared to TG-containing PVA nanofibers, this difference was not statistically significant. However, the synergistic effect of TG and PANI on wound healing, anticipated in the co-electrospun membrane, was clearly demonstrated in this analysis. PANI, due to its electrical conductivity and biocompatibility, enhances cell migration and proliferation by stimulating bioelectrical signals, thereby improving the wound healing process26.

Antibacterial activity

It is well established that bacterial infections can lead to elevated levels of exudate at wound sites, which subsequently impede or delay the healing process. Therefore, the use of antibacterial substrates in wound management is crucial for effectively controlling pathogens and mitigating inflammatory responses47,49. Accordingly, the antibacterial properties of the nanofibrous membranes—namely PVA, P80/T20, P80/AN20, and P80/T20-P80/AN20—were evaluated against S. aureus (Gram-positive) and E. coli (Gram-negative). All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3). Statistical analyses were performed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with significance assigned at P < 0.05. Growth inhibition and anti-biofilm assays were conducted, with the results presented in Fig. 11. Inhibition zones were also measured, and the corresponding data are provided in the supplementary information (Figs. S4–S8), further supporting the proposed antibacterial mechanisms.

Antibacterial effect of the PVA, P80/T20, P80/AN20 and P80/T20-P80/AN20 electrospun nanofibers. (A) bacterial growth inhibition and (B) biofilm inhibition (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001, ±SD, n = 3).

Growth Inhibition

The nanofibrous membranes exhibited greater antibacterial activity against the Gram-negative bacterium E. coli than against the Gram-positive bacterium S. aureus (Fig. 11A). The P80/AN20 nanofibrous membrane demonstrated a significant increase in growth inhibition of both E. coli and S. aureus compared to the PVA and P80/T20 membranes (P < 0.001 vs. PVA and P < 0.05 vs. P80/T20 for E. coli; P < 0.01 vs. PVA and P > 0.05 vs. P80/T20 for S. aureus). This enhanced antibacterial performance can be attributed to the intrinsic antimicrobial properties of PANI in the P80/AN20 sample, which effectively suppresses the growth of both bacterial strains.

The co-electrospun P80/T20-P80/AN20 nanofibrous membranes exhibited significantly greater antibacterial activity relative to the other samples, suggesting their superior efficacy in inhibiting E. coli and S. aureus growth (P < 0.0001 vs. PVA and P80/T20; P < 0.01 vs. P80/AN20 for E. coli; P < 0.0001 vs. PVA; P < 0.001 vs. P80/T20; P < 0.05 vs. P80/AN20 for S. aureus). These results suggest the reinforcing effect of PANI on the antibacterial properties of TG, thereby demonstrating a synergistic antibacterial effect between these two polymers within the co-electrospun nanofibrous membranes. The enhanced antibacterial activity of PANI-based membranes may be attributed to its interaction with bacterial membranes, although the underlying mechanism was not directly explored in this study.

Previous studies have reported that the electrical conductivity of PANI enhances its antibacterial efficacy against both Gram-negative E. coli and Gram-positive S. aureus bacteria57,58. The antibacterial activity of PANI is proposed to occur via two mechanisms: in its base emeraldine form, PANI maintains a positive charge, enabling electrostatic interactions with bacterial membranes that disrupt membrane potential and cause cell damage; meanwhile, in the doped state, PANI can alter the local pH by releasing dopants absorbed by bacteria, disrupting intracellular environments. These dopants may react with critical cellular components such as proteins, DNA, or electrolytes, ultimately leading to bacterial cell death59. The former mechanism is likely the predominant contributor to the antibacterial activity observed in this study. These mechanisms have been reported in the literature, but were not directly examined in this study. As shown in Fig. S8, the localized inhibition beneath PANI-containing fibers supports a surface-mediated interaction mechanism, rather than diffusion-based antibacterial activity. Additionally, the notable decrease in antibacterial activity after crosslinking (Figs. S5–S7) indicates that surface accessibility and polymer chain mobility are essential for PANI activity.

Additionally, TG has been shown to exert antibacterial effects against both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria. This activity is attributed to the presence of L-sugars in tragacanth, such as L-arabinose and L-fucose, which resist microbial degradation since many microorganisms lack the enzymes required to metabolize these sugars16,60.

Taking together, while TG demonstrated some antibacterial activity, its efficacy against the tested microorganisms was limited. As expected, the antibacterial properties of this natural gum were substantially enhanced by incorporating PANI into the nanofibrous membranes. In conclusion, the simultaneous incorporation of TG and PANI into the co-electrospun nanofibrous membranes markedly improved their antibacterial performance against both Gram-negative E. coli and Gram-positive S. aureus bacteria. The observed improvement in antibacterial activity of the co-electrospun P80/T20-P80/AN20 membrane compared to P80/T20 and P80/AN20 provides qualitative support for a possible synergistic effect, although this has not been quantitatively evaluated.

Biofilm Inhibition

Bacteria find the skin injuries as an ideal substrate for biofilm formation, which contributes to chronic inflammation and hinders the wound healing process61,62. Therefore, developing effective antibiofilm strategies is crucial for managing wound healing, particularly in cases of chronic injuries59.

In this study, the antibiofilm activity of single-nozzle electrospun and co-electrospun nanofibrous membranes was evaluated against E. coli and S. aureus. As shown in Fig. 11B, the co-electrospun P80/T20-P80/AN20 membrane exhibited the highest antibiofilm effect against S. aureus. This enhanced antibiofilm property is probably primarily attributed to the presence of PANI in the nanofibrous membrane formulation, as PANI has been reported to disrupt bacterial membranes and inhibit biofilm formation through its unique antibacterial mechanisms63. In contrast, biofilm inhibition assays against E. coli showed no statistically significant differences among the samples. This observation may be due to the distinct biofilm-forming behavior of E. coli strains, which tend to form more complex and resilient biofilms, potentially requiring additional strategies for effective inhibition64.

Considering the antibacterial results obtained, all nanofibrous membranes exhibited greater antibacterial efficacy against the Gram-negative bacterium E. coli compared to the Gram-positive S. aureus in the growth inhibition assays. This difference is likely attributable to the structural variations between the cell walls of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Gram-positive bacteria such as S. aureus possess a thick peptidoglycan layer (approximately 20–80 nm), which confers increased resistance to certain antibacterial agents targeting the cell wall. In contrast, Gram-negative bacteria like E. coli have a considerably thinner peptidoglycan layer (approximately 7–8 nm) but feature an additional outer membrane absent in Gram-positive species. This outer membrane can render Gram-negative bacteria more susceptible to specific antibacterial compounds than their Gram-positive counterparts65,66.

Furthermore, the co-electrospun nanofibers demonstrated superior antibiofilm activity against S. aureus compared to single-nozzle electrospun membranes. This enhancement may be related to the combined antibacterial effects of PANI and TG, as well as their potential to disrupt biofilm formation. However, all samples exhibited comparable antibiofilm effects against E. coli, suggesting that the biofilm formation mechanisms of Gram-negative bacteria may be less influenced by the membrane components than those of Gram-positive bacteria.

In summary, the structural differences in bacterial cell walls contribute to the variability in susceptibility to the antibacterial and antibiofilm effects of the co-electrospun PANI and TG nanofibers. The possible synergistic action of these materials indicates that the co-electrospun nanofibrous membranes are promising candidates for wound healing and skin tissue engineering applications. Notably, Najafian et al. recently fabricated PANI-grafted TG (TG-g-PANI) and PVA nanofibrous scaffolds for skin tissue engineering, demonstrating excellent cytocompatibility, hemocompatibility, and favorable protein adsorption29. Given these encouraging findings alongside the antibacterial efficacy reported in the present study, our co-electrospun nanofibrous membrane merits further investigation for application in infected wound healing. Nonetheless, additional research is required to develop effective strategies specifically targeting E. coli biofilm inhibition in wound environments. The antibacterial mechanisms proposed in this study are based on literature reports and indirect observations. Further studies are needed to fully elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

In this study, PVA/TG/PANI nanofibrous membranes were developed via co-electrospinning for skin tissue engineering applications. The resulting membranes exhibited smooth and uniform fibers with tunable water absorption and hydrophilic properties. The swelling capacity of the membranes can be attributed to the hydrophilic groups present in TG and PVA. The chemical structure of the components and their interactions within the membranes were confirmed by FTIR analysis, while the thermal properties and stability were characterized using DSC and TGA. The PVA/TG/PANI membranes demonstrated adequate mechanical strength and flexibility suitable for skin tissue. Furthermore, growth inhibition tests revealed that the P80/T20-P80/AN20 nanofibrous membranes possess antibacterial activity against E. coli and S. aureus, with the most pronounced antibiofilm effect observed against S. aureus. In addition, cytocompatibility with HDF-4 cells and enhanced cell migration in a scratch assay were confirmed, demonstrating their potential to support wound healing. These results indicate that nanofibrous membranes based on PANI and TG are promising candidates for skin tissue engineering applications.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. Given that only in vitro evaluations were conducted, in vivo studies are required to confirm the biological performance. Moreover, direct mechanistic investigations of the antibacterial activity—such as live/dead bacterial staining or SEM analysis of bacterial morphology—were not performed. The enhanced antibacterial effect of co-electrospun membranes suggests a possible synergistic interaction between TG and PANI, although this must be quantitatively validated. Future studies should focus on advanced mechanistic analyses, quantitative synergy assessments, and optimization of membrane composition to maximize antibacterial efficacy and tissue regeneration.
