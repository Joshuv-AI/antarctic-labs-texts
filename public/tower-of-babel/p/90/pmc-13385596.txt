A Translational Readiness Checklist for Stem-Cell–Based Trials in Movement Disorders: A Practical Roadmap
Authors: Sahar Hemeda, József Janszky, Dávid Pintér, Norbert Kovács
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStem cell-based therapies for movement disorders show inconsistent translation due to variations in product characterisation, delivery, endpoints, safety, and follow-up. Regulatory guidance provides principles but lacks a unified trial framework. The objective was to create a practical framework that translates scientific, regulatory, manufacturing, ethical, and clinical expectations into an auditable pre–First Patient In (FPI) checklist for stem cell trials. A structured review of the literature, clinical trials, and guidance documents was conducted. Searches were performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Google Scholar, and clinical trial registries, including ClinicalTrials.gov, Japan Registry, European Union Register, and Clinical Trials Registry–India, until January 2026. Key guidance documents from the FDA, EMA, ICH, ISSCR, and selected PMDA sources were reviewed. Recurring requirements related to nonclinical evidence, manufacturing quality, ethics, trial design, safety, and follow-up were identified and translated into auditable pre-FPI checklist items. A framework was developed, structured as a universal Core Gate plus three risk-proportionate modules: Module A for pluripotent-derived neural grafts, Module B for somatic neural grafts, and Module C for mesenchymal stromal cell/secretome approaches. The Core Gate outlines the minimum pre-FPI requirements across preclinical justification, GMP release documentation, regulatory and ethics readiness, patient selection, trial design, safety oversight, trial conduct, long-term follow-up, and transparency. The modules add platform-specific requirements according to biological and procedural risks, including tumourigenicity and genomic stability assessment, immunologic monitoring, and route-appropriate biodistribution and persistence expectations. Movement disorder-specific operational elements not defined in general guidance were also incorporated, including harmonised baseline phenotyping, disease-specific endpoint menus, practical imaging and biomarker options. This framework turns fragmented guidance into a practical, auditable pre-FPI roadmap for stem cell trials in movement disorders. By combining a universal Core Gate with risk-proportionate modules, it aims to reduce protocol heterogeneity, improve cross-trial comparability, strengthen regulatory planning, and support safer, interpretable clinical translation.Keywords: translational roadmap, spinocerebellar ataxia, Huntington’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, stem-cell-based therapy, movement disorders

Introduction

Movement disorders, including Parkinson’s disease (PD), Huntington’s disease (HD), spinocerebellar ataxia (SCA), dystonia, and multiple system atrophy (MSA), are among the most debilitating neurodegenerative conditions. These disorders are characterised by a progressive decline in motor function, loss of independence, 1 and are a common cause of morbidity and cognitive impairment in older adults. 2 In 2021, an estimated 11.77 million people worldwide were living with PD, 3 whereas HD and SCA, although less common, have devastating impacts on patients and their families.4,5 Current pharmacological and surgical interventions, such as dopaminergic therapy6,7 and deep-brain stimulation, 8 offer symptomatic relief but do not halt disease progression or restore lost neuronal circuitry.

Stem cell therapy (SCT) has emerged as a promising approach for addressing neurodegenerative diseases by targeting multiple mechanisms, including neuronal replacement, neuroprotection, 9 immune modulation, and trophic support. Thus, ultimately alter the disease trajectory. 10 Various platforms, including pluripotent stem cells (embryonic and induced pluripotent), neural progenitor cells, and mesenchymal stromal cells, are being investigated. Preliminary clinical data in PD suggest the feasibility and biological activity of pluripotent-derived dopaminergic progenitors,11-13 while pilot studies in HD, MSA, and SCA indicate the safety and potential immunomodulatory benefits of mesenchymal or neural progenitor approaches.14,15 However, the translation remains inconsistent.

Many clinical trials are small, open-label, and heterogeneous in terms of cell source and delivery, and lack standardised endpoints. Long-term follow-up is rare, despite ongoing concerns regarding tumourigenicity, graft-induced dyskinesia, and immune rejection. The absence of harmonised operational standards has left the field fragmented, with enthusiasm repeatedly tempered by safety and reproducibility concerns. For instance, in PD, a recent Phase I/II trial at Kyoto University involving allogeneic iPS-derived dopaminergic progenitors (jRCT2090220384) demonstrated promising safety and ^18F-DOPA PET imaging improvements, albeit with a small sample size of seven patients and a limited duration before tapering immunosuppression. 16 Similarly, a phase 2 randomized, placebo-controlled trial of allogeneic bone marrow-derived MSCs in PD(NCT04506073), conducted within an immunomodulatory and neuroinflammation-based therapeutic rationale, reported efficacy in the three-infusion arm, good tolerability, and the need for functional potency assays to address possible batch-to-batch variability in clinical response. 17 Due to these barriers, regulatory heterogeneity between countries, variable manufacturing/characterisation of cell products, inconsistent immunological monitoring, and short or non-standardised follow-up, there is a risk that advances observed in preclinical studies may not reliably translate to patient benefits. Existing international guidance from regulatory agencies and professional societies provides essential principles for cell-based product development and clinical trial conduct, but these requirements are distributed across documents and are rarely presented as a single auditable pre-enrolment gate tailored to CNS delivery and movement disorder endpoints. Here, we operationalize these expectations into a concise Core Gate preFPI checklist with explicit documentary evidence requirements (e.g., study reports, SOPs, Certificates of Analysis, Data and Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) charter, SAP version), complemented by product-type modules (A–C) that scale platform-specific requirements and incorporate movement-disorder baseline phenotyping, imaging/biomarker menus, and neurosurgical workflow elements to improve cross-trial comparability and regulatory readiness.

Clinical and Translational Landscape of Stem-Cell Therapy in Movement Disorders

Across movement disorders, stem-cell therapy has progressed from proof-of-concept animal studies to early human trials, but the trajectory has not been uniform across diseases or cell platforms. In Parkinson’s disease, preclinical primate work by Morizane et al 18 showed that iPSC-derived neural grafts can survive in the brain and that autologous grafting provokes less immune activation than allogeneic transplantation, helping define the translational logic for dopaminergic cell replacement; this line has now reached first-in-human studies by Sawamoto et al and Tabar et al,16,19 both of which reported short-term safety together with imaging evidence consistent with graft survival and dopaminergic activity. In contrast, outside PD, the field has relied more heavily on mesenchymal or supportive cell strategies than on true circuit reconstruction: Bachoud-Lévi et al 20 found no clear clinical benefit from fetal striatal grafting in Huntington’s disease, Lee et al 21 and Singer et al. 22 reported mainly safety and possible slowing signals for MSC-based approaches in multiple system atrophy, and Jin et al 23 and Tsai et al 24 described early safety and exploratory efficacy signals in spinocerebellar ataxia. Taken together, the existing literature shows that stem-cell therapy in movement disorders is no longer purely theoretical, but remains a mixed translational landscape in which biological rationale, product class, delivery route, and disease anatomy strongly shape what early trials can realistically achieve.

Clinical development remains highly uneven across movement disorders. In Parkinson’s disease, the most current peer-reviewed anchors are the 2025 studies by Sawamoto et al. 16 Tabar et al 19 and chang et al, 25 ll of which advanced pluripotent-derived dopaminergic cell transplantation into early human trials and reported short-term safety together with imaging or exploratory clinical findings consistent with graft survival and dopaminergic activity; however, these programs remain limited by small cohorts, open-label or early-phase designs, and follow-up that is not yet sufficient to settle durability, late adverse events, or the magnitude of true clinical benefit. In Huntington’s disease, the most recent clinical program is the NestaCell series reported by Fernandes et al in 2025 26 : a phase I open-label study established safety at two dose levels, and a phase II randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial reported improvement on the UHDRS Total Motor Score; even so, the evidence is still based on a relatively small study, requires replication in independent cohorts, and does not yet resolve the broader question of whether systemic stem-cell administration can produce durable disease-modifying effects in HD. In spinocerebellar ataxia, the most up-to-date clinical anchor is the ongoing randomized, double-blind phase II Stemchymal® program (NCT06397274) 27 ; however, despite positive public updates, full peer-reviewed efficacy and safety results are still unavailable, so the field remains clinically promising but evidentially immature. In multiple system atrophy, the most current active trial is the randomized double-blind intrathecal autologous MSC study NCT05167721, 28 while the best-known published clinical anchors remain Lee et al 21 who reported slowed progression in an earlier randomized study, and Singer et al, 22 who showed that intrathecal MSC delivery was feasible and generally safe; taken together, these studies suggest biological plausibility, but they have not yet settled the optimal route, dosing frequency, durability of effect, or whether benefits can be reproduced consistently across the clinically heterogeneous MSA population. Across PD, HD, SCA, and MSA, the main challenge is no longer the absence of stem-cell activity signals, but the difficulty of interpreting and comparing those signals across highly heterogeneous studies. The current clinical record combines fundamentally different therapeutic strategies, including true neuronal replacement approaches, progenitor-based grafting, and mesenchymal or other cell products intended primarily for trophic or immunomodulatory effects, yet these are often discussed under the same translational label despite requiring different evidentiary standards. Across diseases, recurring weaknesses include incomplete product definition or refinement, uncertainty regarding the mechanism of action, small early-phase cohorts, inconsistent alignment between the proposed mechanism and chosen endpoints, variable use of biomarkers and imaging, and limited long-term assessment of durability, immune response, and late safety. As a result, even encouraging findings remain difficult to benchmark across programs, and apparent efficacy signals are not always sufficient to distinguish true biological effect from platform-specific variability, study-design limitations, or short follow-up. Taken together, these recurring cross-disease limitations indicate the need for a practical and harmonized pre-enrolment framework capable of translating broad translational expectations into consistent and auditable readiness criteria across movement-disorder stem-cell trials.

From Translational Gaps to an Operational Framework

The cross-disease clinical and translational landscape outlined above indicates that progress in stem-cell–based therapy for movement disorders is increasingly limited not only by biological uncertainty, but also by the absence of a harmonized operational structure for trial readiness. Across studies, recurring sources of heterogeneity include variable trial designs, differences in product characterization, inconsistent endpoint selection, and uneven long-term safety and follow-up practices. In this context, a standardized pre-enrolment checklist offers a practical way to reduce avoidable protocol variance, strengthen the evidentiary basis for First Patient In decisions, and improve transparency by ensuring that key scientific, manufacturing, ethical, and clinical readiness elements are predefined, documented, and independently verifiable.

Similar harmonisation frameworks have improved reproducibility and interpretability in other high-stakes areas. In clinical trials, CONSORT provides a minimum set of reporting items and a flow diagram to improve the completeness and transparency of randomised trial reporting. 37 In genomics, MIAME established minimum reporting standards for microarray experiments, enabling reliable public deposition and reuse of datasets and supporting consistent downstream analysis. 38 In oncology, standard outcome and safety frameworks such as RECIST (tumour response assessment) and CTCAE (adverse event grading) created a common language that improved comparability across trials and enabled consistent interpretation of results. 39 In cardiology, the VARC endpoint definitions similarly standardised outcomes for transcatheter aortic valve interventions, improving comparability and interpretability across studies. 40 Collectively, these precedents support the rationale for a concise, auditable pre-FPI checklist (checklist gate) for stem-cell–based trials in movement disorders, aimed at reducing heterogeneity, facilitating meaningful cross-trial comparison, and strengthening regulatory confidence.

Checklist Development and Guidance-Mapping Method

The operational checklist was developed through a structured review of published literature, registered clinical trials, and major international guidance documents relevant to stem-cell–based therapies in movement disorders. The aim was to translate principle-level scientific, regulatory, and trial-conduct expectations into an auditable pre–First Patient In (FPI) readiness framework.

Evidence Identification

Published literature and registered clinical trials relevant to stem-cell–based approaches in movement disorders were identified through structured searches of PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Google Scholar, and major international clinical trial registries (including ClinicalTrials.gov, the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials, the European Union Clinical Trials Register, and the Clinical Trials Registry–India) through January 2026. The evidence base included representative preclinical studies and registered/published clinical trials involving pluripotent stem cells, neural stem/progenitor cells, and mesenchymal stromal cell/secretome approaches in Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, multiple system atrophy, spinocerebellar ataxia, dystonia, and related movement disorders. The purpose of this evidence review was not to generate a formal systematic review or meta-analysis, but to identify recurring translational barriers, operational gaps, and representative trial features relevant to checklist development.

Guidance Identification

Major international guidance and standards relevant to stem-cell–based products and clinical trial conduct were identified through searches of official issuer websites and cross-referenced regulatory guidance pages. Core documents included the ISSCR Guidelines for Stem Cell Research and Clinical Translation (2021), relevant FDA and EMA guidance on cellular products and advanced therapy medicinal products, and ICH guidance, including E6(R3), E8(R1), and E9(R1).41-48 The detailed guidance-to-checklist mapping matrix is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Extraction and Mapping

From the literature, trial records, and guidance documents, we extracted operational expectations across six domains: (i) nonclinical evidence, including biodistribution/persistence, tumorigenicity risk assessment, and dose/route justification; (ii) quality/manufacturing, including identity, purity, viability, potency, traceability, and change control; (iii) ethics and consent; (iv) trial design and statistical planning; (v) safety oversight and monitoring; and (vi) long-term follow-up, registry planning, and transparency. These expectations were mapped to checklist domains and converted into auditable pre-FPI items by requiring pre-specified methods, acceptance criteria, and associated evidence artifacts to be filed in the Trial Master File (e.g., final study reports, SOPs, Certificates of Analysis, DSMB charter, SAP version).

Synthesis and Author-Panel Agreement

Candidate items were consolidated, de-duplicated, and refined through iterative review by the multidisciplinary author team. The final framework was structured as a universal pre-FPI Core Gate plus product-type Modules A–C to support risk-proportionate implementation across pluripotent-derived neural grafts, somatic neural grafts, and MSC/secretome approaches. The guidance-to-checklist mapping matrix is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Regulatory Alignment and Crosswalk

This checklist is intended to operationalize existing regulatory and professional guidance into an auditable pre–First Patient In (FPI) readiness gate; it does not replace jurisdiction-specific mandatory requirements. We mapped checklist domains and module-specific items to key international guidance documents, including relevant FDA and EMA guidance for cell-based products, ICH Good Clinical Practice and related trial-quality guidance, ISSCR clinical translation guidance, and selected PMDA regenerative-medicine guidance.41-48 The detailed guidance-to-checklist mapping matrix is presented in Supplementary Table S1, and the broader regulatory and guidance crosswalk is presented in Supplementary Table S2. Where expectations differ across jurisdictions or depend on product-specific risk, investigators should confirm the final evidence package and acceptance criteria through early regulatory engagement (e.g., pre-IND or scientific advice).

Operational Problem Addressed

By converting principle-level expectations into checkable pre-FPI requirements and adding disease/procedure-specific harmonisation, the framework aims to reduce protocol heterogeneity, improve interpretability across trials, and strengthen regulatory-ready planning.

The framework uses disease-specific endpoint menus rather than a single generic outcome set: for example, PD programs should predefine motor endpoints (e.g., MDS-UPDRS/UPDRS, ON/OFF state assessments, dyskinesia and medication burden) with graft-survival/target-engagement imaging where applicable; HD programs should prioritize UHDRS-based motor, functional, and cognitive measures; SCA programs should prioritize SARA-based ataxia outcomes and gait/balance measures; and MSA programs should prioritize UMSARS-based motor/autonomic outcomes, with imaging and fluid biomarkers selected according to mechanism, route, and product class.

Feasibility and Tiered Implementation

Implementation of the proposed framework entails variable resource requirements depending on product class, manufacturing complexity, regulatory setting, and institutional capacity. Major cost drivers include GMP-compliant manufacturing, preclinical safety studies, specialised delivery infrastructure, and long-term follow-up systems. While precise cost estimates vary across settings, these factors should be considered early during trial planning and funding allocation, particularly when adopting a tiered implementation approach.

However, to support adoption across academic settings and resource-variable regions, the checklist is designed to be risk-proportionate and scalable. The Core Gate defines minimum pre-FPI requirements applicable to all stem-cell–based trials, while Modules A–C add platform-specific requirements proportional to risk. To avoid creating barriers to responsible development without compromising participant safety, checklist items should be implemented using three tiers:

• Mandatory: required to protect participant safety, ensure product quality, and enable regulatory/ethics review (Core Gate plus the applicable module).

• Mandatory: required to protect participant safety, ensure product quality, and enable regulatory/ethics review (Core Gate plus the applicable module).

• Risk-based (context-dependent): required when justified by product type, route, persistence/proliferation risk, and/or regulator feedback; scientifically justified alternatives may be acceptable if documented.

• Risk-based (context-dependent): required when justified by product type, route, persistence/proliferation risk, and/or regulator feedback; scientifically justified alternatives may be acceptable if documented.

• Enhanced (recommended): desirable to maximise interpretability and cross-trial comparability when feasible (e.g., expanded biomarker panels, higher-frequency imaging, additional redundancy testing).

• Enhanced (recommended): desirable to maximise interpretability and cross-trial comparability when feasible (e.g., expanded biomarker panels, higher-frequency imaging, additional redundancy testing).

Operational Checklist Template for Stem-Cell Trials in Movement Disorders

Operational Checklist: Purpose and Scope

This checklist translates the proposed minimum standards for stem-cell–based clinical trials in movement disorders into practical steps. It is intended for use by investigators, sponsors, and oversight bodies from various disciplines before enrolling the First Patient In (“First Patient In”, FPi). Each item must be documented in the Trial Master File and independently verified for quality assurance before the trial begins. The checklist ensures readiness across scientific, manufacturing, ethical, and clinical aspects, effectively bridging the gap between promising preclinical data and consistent patient benefit. The integrated translational roadmap and overall framework are illustrated in Figure 1.

Translational roadmap for stem-cell–based trials in movement disorders. This integrated framework merges conceptual domains with operational specifics, ensuring that scientific validity and manufacturing quality are established before clinical application. Patient-level preparedness, including diagnosis, safety, immunology, and consent, is auditable and reproducible, while regulatory alignment and long-term follow-up are embedded within the trial design. The roadmap follows a sequential translational pathway: Bench → Preclinical Validation → GMP/CMC Readiness → Regulatory & Ethics Approval → Trial Readiness (site qualification, DSMB, SAP, and procedure readiness) → Checklist Gate (all Pre-FPI items must be complete) → Patient Screening/Eligibility and Baseline Confirmation → First Patient In → Long-Term Follow-Up and Registry Integration. Trial-level preclinical, manufacturing, regulatory, and operational readiness activities culminate in a mandatory pre-FPI checklist core gate. Only after successful completion of this gate should patient-specific screening, eligibility confirmation, and baseline readiness proceed to First Patient In, followed by structured long-term follow-up and registry. Standardized implementation of this framework is intended to improve cross-disease comparability, strengthen regulatory assurance, and facilitate the translation of stem-cell therapies for movement disorders from research to clinical practice.

Practical Implementation and Policy Integration

The successful implementation of the proposed checklist in real-world settings requires both top-down policy alignment and bottom-up engagement by investigators. Researchers should integrate the checklist into protocol development, ethics submissions, and grant applications to ensure trial readiness and transparency before the first patient is enrolled. Institutions and sponsors are encouraged to incorporate the checklist into their internal quality systems and use it as a gating tool for resource allocation, site qualification, and milestone release.

Regulatory authorities, such as the EMA, FDA, and PMDA, should consider formally aligning this framework with existing cell- and gene-therapy guidance to expedite consistent review processes and reduce redundancy in the evaluation of dossiers.

Journals and trial registries can enhance transparency and cross-trial comparability by requiring checklist completion prior to publication or trial registration. Professional societies and consortia play a crucial role in sustaining implementation by providing shared templates, digital-tracking platforms, and periodic audits. Establishing a global registry of checklist-compliant trials would further facilitate meta-analyses, post-market surveillance, and continuous learning loops in neuroregenerative research.

Importantly, implementation strategies should remain adaptable to low- and middle-resource centres through the use of open-access tools and regional harmonisation workshops. Collectively, these measures have the potential to transform the checklist from a procedural document into a dynamic quality framework that supports the safer, faster, and more equitable translation of stem cell therapies for movement disorders.

Conclusion

In summary, we present an operational, auditable translational readiness framework for stem cell–based trials in movement disorders, structured as a pre-FPI Core Gate checklist complemented by product-type modules. This approach is intended to reduce avoidable protocol heterogeneity, improve cross-trial comparability, and support regulatory-ready trial planning. Future work should evaluate feasibility and uptake across ongoing programs and refine the framework through multidisciplinary consensus and real-world implementation feedback.
