Comparison of rumen microbiota and meat metabolism in lambs under different feeding regimes: insights for optimizing growth performance and meat quality
Authors: Yafeng Huang, Yuan Lu, Wuyunfeng Zhu, Fen Huang, Xin Luo, Kefan Zhang, Huiqing Wei, Yuxiang Sun, Guanjun Wang, Jiahong Chen, Zijun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated effects of three feeding systems — indoor feeding (CK), artificial pasture grazing for 4 h with indoor feeding (G4H), and 8 h with indoor feeding (G8H) — on lamb growth performance, meat quality, rumen microbiota, and muscle metabolites using 16S rRNA gene sequencing and untargeted metabolomics. Pasture-grazing groups produced meat with superior quality, particularly in fatty acid (FA) profiles than CK group, and G4H lambs exhibited higher average daily gain and meat crude protein (CP) content (P < 0.05). Key rumen microorganisms, including Butyrivibrio 2 and Prevotellaceae UCG-003, as well as metabolites such as (R)-salsolinol and acrylamide, were significantly associated with meat tenderness, CP content, and FA content (P < 0.05 and |r| ≥ 0.49). Artificial pasture grazing improved meat quality by reshaping rumen microbiota and modulating metabolic pathways, presenting a preferable strategy for producing healthier lamb meat, with the G4H system offering the greatest growth performance.Keywords: Huang-huai lambs, Metabolomics, Meat quality, Rumen microbiota

Introduction

Mutton is a type of meat valued worldwide for its distinctive flavor and high nutritional value (Huang et al., 2023). With rising living standards and growing health awareness, consumer demand increasingly favors high-quality, safe, and flavorful mutton produced through sustainable and environmentally friendly responsible practices (Wang, Wang, Chen, et al., 2021). It is well known that lamb meat quality, including sensory attributes, nutritional value, and fatty acid (FA) composition, is strongly influenced by feeding systems (Gruffat et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2015). Pasture-grazed lambs typically provide health benefits due to higher levels of total n-3 polyunsaturated FAs (PUFAs) and a lower n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio (Gruffat et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2015). However, the weight gain of pasture-grazed lambs is often inferior to that of indoor-fed lambs (Huang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2015). Therefore, farmers must carefully balance feeding systems to optimize both growth rate and meat quality of lambs.

Against this backdrop, in many countries worldwide, including China and New Zealand, artificial grasslands are increasingly being used for livestock grazing (Somasiri et al., 2016; Wang, Wang, Chen, et al., 2021). Integrating artificial grasslands with forage legumes into ruminant systems or combining grazing with indoor feeding has the potential to produce healthier, naturally raised meat aligned with consumer expectations (Wang, Wang, Chen, et al., 2021). Artificial grasslands types primarily include legume-based mixtures (e.g., alfalfa-clover), grass-dominated swards, and multi-species pastures (Li et al., 2025; Somasiri et al., 2016). Compared with natural grasslands, artificial grasslands offer several advantages, including higher biomass yield, superior nutritional quality of forages, improved resource-use efficiency, and greater control over species composition. These benefits support more consistent and efficient ruminant production while advancing environmental stewardship. Studies have shown that grazing on legume-grass mixtures can enhance FA composition in ruminant meat (Li et al., 2025; Wang, Wang, Chen, et al., 2021). Therefore, establishing artificial grasslands has become a practical approach to improving the meat quality of ruminants (Li et al., 2025). In China, where mutton production is the highest worldwide, an estimated 13 million hectares of degraded grassland slopes and cropland in the south are suitable for conversion into artificial grasslands. If utilized effectively, these areas could produce up to 3 million tons of ruminant meat annually (Zhang & Li, 2012). These regions feature fragmented landholdings, high rainfall, and a high risk of soil erosion, thereby making the establishment of artificial grasslands a particularly compelling solution. Artificial pastures featuring legume-grass mixtures can revitalize degraded lands, support intensive yet sustainable grazing systems, and align with national ecological restoration initiatives. However, research on the integration of artificial grassland development and livestock production remains limited, with most studies focused in northern China, where environmental conditions differ markedly from those in the south (Huang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025; Zhang & Li, 2012). Moreover, the prevalence of numerous small pasture parcels and the imperative of ecological conservation in southern China make it difficult to implement traditional all-day or long-term grazing. Thus, modern systems based on short-duration grazing have emerged, underscoring the need for further research in these regions to explore the effects on improving growth performance and meat quality of lambs.

The rumen plays a pivotal role in ruminant nutrition, harboring diverse microbiota that regulate digestion and strongly influence both growth and meat quality. Shifts in the rumen microbiota affect the deposition of metabolites such as FAs in muscle tissue (Zhang, Han, Hou, Raza, Gui, et al., 2022). Several studies have reported that the ruminal genera Blautia and Erysipelatoclostridium were positively associated with Warner-Bratzler shear force (WBSF) and fat content, while Moryella was negatively correlated with C20:3n-6, C20:4n-6, C20:5n-3, and n-3 PUFAs but positively associated with L-carnosine and N-acetyl-L-histidine (Ma et al., 2023; Wang, Wang, Chen, et al., 2021). Untargeted metabolomic analysis using UHPLC-MS/MS has been shown to provide an almost full range of compounds contributing to meat nutritional value and flavor. Zhang, Han, Hou, Raza, Wang, et al. (2022) found that differences in meat quality among feeding systems were linked to differential metabolites (DMs) such as maltotriose, and chenodeoxycholate. Therefore, analyzing the differences in the composition of the rumen microbiota community and the correlations between rumen microbiota, muscle metabolism, and edible quality, nutritional quality, and FA composition of meat obtained under different grazing regimes on artificial grasslands can provide new insights.

Although feeding strategies are known to influence lamb growth and meat quality (Wang, Wang, Chen, et al., 2021), the metabolic links between rumen microbial activity, muscle metabolism, and the resulting quality traits remain insufficiently understood, especially in artificial grasslands. Since most studies rely on single-omics datasets or phenotypic assessments, there is a lack of integrative multi-omics data to help elucidate rumen–muscle crosstalk. This limitation constrains the lamb and meat industries' ability to optimize grazing on artificial grasslands to enhance the nutritional value of lamb meat. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to evaluate the effects of different grazing durations on artificial pastures on the lamb growth performance, FA composition, and metabolome profiles in the longissimus lumborum (LL) muscle. Additionally, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was applied to characterize changes in rumen microbiota composition underlying alterations in muscle composition. The findings discussed herein provide practical insights into optimizing artificial pasture grazing strategies to enhance both growth performance and meat quality in lamb production.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in compliance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals issued by the National Research Council. The experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of Anhui Agricultural University (Hefei, China; Approval No. AHAUXMSQ-2021083) and adhered to the Guiding Principles for Biomedical Research Involving Animals.

Artificial mixed grassland preparation

The experiment was carried out on an artificial grassland located in Dingyuan County, Anhui Province, China (32°57′ N, 117°50′ E, 71.7 m above sea level). The artificial grassland comprised four grazing plots with areas of 3014, 2680, 2881, and 2635 m2, respectively. The sown pasture consisted of white clover (Trifolium repens, 10%), alfalfa (Medicago sativa, 15%), orchard grass (Dactylis glomerata, 15%), perennial ryegrass (Lolium perenne L., 25%) and other pasture species (35%).

Animals, experimental design and sample collection

Thirty-three five-month-old male Huang-huai lambs (Ovis aries) with an average body weight (BW) of 28.76 ± 1.01 kg were randomly assigned to one of three feeding systems: indoor feeding twice daily at 8:30 and 18:30 (CK, n = 11); grazing on artificial pasture for 4 h daily plus indoor feeding twice daily at 8:30 and 18:30 (G4H, n = 11); and grazing on artificial pasture for 8 h daily (08,30–12:30 and 14:00–18:00) plus indoor feeding once daily at 18:30 (G8H, n = 11). All lambs were fed a total mixed ration (TMR) whose ingredients and chemical composition are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

The experimental period lasted 102 days from April 1, 2021 to July 12, 2021, including a 12-day adaptation period. In the G4H group, grazing occurred from 09:00 to 13:00 during the first 57 days, then shifted to 14:00–18:00 to avoid reduced feed intake caused by midday heat. Throughout the experiment, all lambs had ad libitum access to feed and water. Daily feed offered and refusals were weighed prior to next morning's feeding to determine average dry matter intake (DMI). BWs were recorded on days 0, 30, 60, and 90 before the morning feeding during the fattening phase. Average daily gain (ADG, kg/day) was calculated based on the intervals between feeding periods. A total of ten randomly selected quadrats (100 cm × 50 cm) were chosen in the artificial grasslands grazed by the animals included in the experiment, with five quadrats assigned to each group. Harvested grass was dried at 65 °C for 48 h, and then ground through a 1-mm sieve. Forage nutrient composition from the artificial grassland is shown in Supplementary Table 2. At the end of the experiment, six lambs per group were slaughtered at a commercial abattoir following a 24 h fast from food and an 8 h fast from water. Rumen fluid samples were collected postmortem, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen after being filtered through 4 layers of cheesecloth, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. The LL muscle from the left carcass side was collected within 45 min after slaughter, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. Six replicates per group were used for meat quality, ruminal microbiota, and muscle metabolite analyses. The experimental design and workflow are depicted in Fig. 1.

Meat edible quality and meat, TMR and forage nutritional analysis

Edible quality traits, including pH, WBSF, cooking loss, drip loss, and color [lightness (L*), redness (a*), and yellowness (b*)] were measured following the method proposed by Ma et al. (2023). The pH meter was determined using a digital pH meter (Testo AG, Lenzkirch, Germany) at 45 min (pH 45min) and 24 h post-mortem (pH 24h) of slaughter. The pH meter was calibrated with phosphate buffer solutions at pH 4.00 and 7.00. Measurements were conducted at 25 °C, with temperature compensation applied for accuracy. The L*, a*, and b* values were measured using a CR-410 colorimeter with 2° observer angles and the pulse xenon lamp illuminant based on the CIE 1931 standard observer, using a Φ50mm/Φ53mm aperture for 1 s, within 45 min post-slaughter. The colorimeter was calibrated with a white plate prior to each measurement. The hue angle (indicative of meat discolouration) and chroma (representing color intensity) were determined as follows: Hue angle = tan −1 (b*/a*); Chroma = (a* 2+ b* 2)0.5 (Tuell et al., 2021). For pH value and meat color parameters, each meat sample measurement was obtained in triplicate.

Three pieces (20 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm) per LL muscle were suspended in sealed plastic bags for 24 h at 4 °C, ensuring no contact with the bag. Drip loss was measured as the weight difference before and after dripping. To determine cooking loss, shear force, and textural properties, lambs were divided into three cooking batches, each containing six lambs, with one lamb randomly selected from each group. Cooking loss was calculated as the percentage change in sample weight before and after cooking relative to the initial weight. Three replicate muscle samples from each lamb were separately placed in a plastic bag and heated in a water bath at 80 °C until the internal temperature reached 75 °C. Cooked samples were then cut into strips of 30 mm × 10 mm × 1 mm (6.64 ± 0.31 g), with muscle fibers aligned longitudinally. Shear force values were measured using a C-LM3B tenderness meter (Northeast Agricultural University, Harbin, China) equipped with a 50-kg capacity strain gauge load cell at a shearing speed of 5 mm/s. WBSF was measured in triplicate, and the mean value was calculated.

Nutritional quality parameters of the meat, TMR and forage, including moisture, crude protein (CP), ether extract (IMF), and total ash content, were evaluated according to the method of AOAC (2019). Briefly, moisture content was measured using method 950.46 by drying samples in an oven at 150 ± 2 °C until constant weight. The CP content was analyzed using the Kjeldahl method (method 984.13), with total nitrogen multiplied by 6.25. The IMF was determined by petroleum ether extraction using the Soxhlet method (method 920.39). Ash content was assessed by incineration at 600 ± 10 °C in a muffle furnace according to method 920.153. The contents of NDF and acid detergent fiber (ADF) were measured following the method proposed by Van Soest et al. (1991). Each parameter was measured twice for each meat sample.

Fatty acid analysis of meat

The meat FA composition was determined using gas chromatography on an Agilent 7890B (Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and a DB-FastFAME capillary column (90 m × 250 μm × 0.25 μm; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), following the procedure described by Chen et al. (2017).

Approximately 1.50 g of meat was homogenized with 2.65 mL of a solution composed of hexane (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; 1 mL), C11:0 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; 0.66 mL), 95% ethanol (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.; 0.66 mL) and purified water (1.33 mL). The mixture was ground for 2 min at 60 Hz using an automatic rapid sample grinder (JXFSTPRP-24, Shanghai Jingxin Industrial Development Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Then, pyrogallic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; 33 mg), zeolite (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China; 33 mg), and hydrochloric acid (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China; 3.3 mL, 8.3 mol/L) were added, and the mixture was incubated at 75 °C in a water bath (HH-S4, Gongyi Yuhua Instrument Co., Ltd., Zhengzhou, China) for 40 min. Following incubation and cooling, 95% ethanol (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China; 5 mL) and a 1:1 (v/v) ether/petroleum ether solution (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China; 10 mL) were added. After vortexing for 5 min, the resulting filtrates were harvested. This extraction procedure was repeated 3 times. Evaporation of the combined filtrates to dryness was carried out using a rotary evaporator. The residues were subjected to methylation by treatment with 1.6 mL of 2% NaOH-methanol (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) and 1.4 mL of 15% boron trifluoride-methanol (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), then heated at 80 °C for 6 min. Once cooled to ambient temperature, n-heptane (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China; 2 mL) and saturated sodium chloride solution (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China; 3 mL) were added. The mixture was left to equilibrate for 5 min, and the upper (organic) phase (1.5 mL) was collected, dried with anhydrous sodium sulphate (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China; 0.6 g), and used for gas chromatography analysis.

Temperature rise procedure: 75 °C for 1 min; 50 °C/min to 200 °C, 15 min; 2 °C/min to 210 °C, 1 min; and 10 °C/min to 230 °C, 16.5 min. The injector and detector temperatures were maintained at 240 °C and 230 °C, respectively. The flow rate was 3 mL/min, and the injection volume was 1 μL. Nitrogen was used as the carrier gas (purity ≥99.999%, Shanghai Shengguang Gas Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min. FAs were identified by comparison with retention times of standards (Supelco 37 Component FAME mix, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA). The FAs peak areas were normalized by the volume of toluene (VT) and sample weight. The internal standard (C11:0) was also normalized by the VT to obtain the normalized internal standard area (C11N). The concentration of each fatty acid was then calculated by comparing the normalized peak area of the target fatty acid with that of C11N, and expressed as mg/100 g of fresh meat.

Ruminal 16S rRNA sequencing data processing and analysis

High-throughput 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed by PANOMIX Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China) to identify ruminal microorganisms. Genomic DNA was extracted from rumen fluid samples of six lambs per group using the E.Z.N.A. method according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNA quality and concentration were determined by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis and spectrophotometry (OD-1000+). The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified with the primers 341F (CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG) and 806R (GGACTACHVGGGTATCTAAT). The PCR amplifications were performed in 10-μL reactions containing 5 ng of template DNA, 2 μL of 5 × FastPfu buffer, 0.1 μM of each primer, 0.2 μL of FastPfu polymerase, and 1 μL of 2.5 mM dNTPs. Cycling conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 28 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 30 s with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were confirmed by 2% (w/v) agarose gel electrophoresis and purified using an AxyPrep. Amplicons were pooled and sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with a NovaSeq 6000 SP Reagent Kit v1.5 (Illumina).

Raw sequencing data were processed and analyzed with the QIIME software package. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered at 97% similarity. Alpha diversity was assessed by calculating Chao 1, Simpson and Shannon indices using the QIIME2 pipeline. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on unweighted UniFrac distances was employed to compare beta diversity indices among samples, and distance-based matrix analysis was used to assess differences between samples. Sequence alignment was performed using the QIIME2 feature-classifier plugin in conjunction with the SILVA and GREENGENES databases. Biomarkers with statistically significant differences were identified by LEfSe analysis using a threshold of linear discriminant analysis (LDA) > 3.0 and a P-value <0.05. Tax4Fun (version 1.0) was applied for functional prediction, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway abundances were calculated from normalized OTU tables.

Untargeted metabolomics processing and analysis of meat

Thawed muscle samples (50 mg) were extracted with 1 mL of methanol–acetonitrile–water (2:2:1, v/v/v) containing isotopically labeled standards. Samples were homogenized (35 Hz, 4 min), sonicated in ice water (5 min, repeated thrice), incubated at −40 °C for 60 min, and centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 4 °C for 15 min). Finally, supernatants (150 μL) were transferred to sample vials for LC-MS/MS analysis.

LC-MS/MS analyses were performed on a UHPLC system (Vanquish, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with an UPLC BEH amide column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) coupled to a Q Exactive HFX mass spectrometer (Orbitrap MS, Thermo Fisher). Mobile phases consisted of 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 25 mmol/L ammonia in water (mobile phase A; pH = 9.75) and acetonitrile (mobile phase B). Column and autosampler temperatures were 25 °C and 4 °C, respectively, with a 2-μL injection volume. Quality control (QC) samples, created by pooling muscle extracts, were analyzed throughout the run to evaluate system stability and data reliability (Wang, Wang, Chen, et al., 2021). The QE HFX mass spectrometer was utilized owing to its capacity to acquire MS/MS spectra in information-dependent acquisition (IDA) using the Xcalibur software (Thermo-Fisher) with the following ESI parameters: sheath gas flow rate, 30 Arb; auxiliary gas flow rate, 25 Arb; capillary temperature, 350 °C; full MS resolution, 60,000; MS/MS resolution, 7500; collision energies, 10/30/60 in NCE mode; and spray voltages, 3.6 kV (positive) or −3.2 kV (negative).

Raw MS data were converted to mzXML format with ProteoWizard and processed in the XCMS R package (version 3.2), resulting in a data matrix composed of retention time, mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) values, and peak intensities. Peak annotation was performed using the R package CAMERA, and metabolites were identified using an internal MS2 database with an annotation cutoff of 0.3. Orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was applied to identify DMs, with model quality evaluated by R2 and Q2 values to avoid overfitting (Q2 < 0, R2Y > 0.5). Significant DMs were identified based on the following criteria: variable importance in the projection (VIP) > 1 from the OPLS-DA model, and a significance level of P < 0.05 in the Student's t-test. Fold change of the metabolites was obtained by comparing the mean values between CON and G4H, CON and G8H, and G4H and G8H. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, http://www.kegg.jp/) pathway enrichment analysis was then performed using the ClusterProfiler package in R software (version 3.6.1) based on the DMs.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS v25.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Growth performance and meat quality traits were compared using one-way ANOVA, with Duncan's test for post hoc comparisons. Statistical significance was set at P ≤ 0.05, with 0.05 < P ≤ 0.10 considered a significant trend. The associations between ruminal microbial abundance, muscle metabolites, and meat quality were evaluated via Spearman's rank correlation analysis. Correlations were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05, and the absolute value of correlation coefficient (|r|) was ≥0.49. These significant correlations were subsequently visualized as interaction networks using Cytoscape (v.3.10.3).

Results

Growth performance

During the three-month fattening trial, lambs gained an average of 19 kg BW (Table 1). Feeding systems did not significantly affect BW during the first (P = 0.889), second (P = 0.672), or final months (P = 0.461) of the experiment. Similarly, no significant differences were observed in ADG during the second (P = 0.259) and final (P = 0.444) months. However, during the first month, a significant effect of feeding system on ADG was observed, ranging from 278.79 g/day (CK) to 350.76 g/day (G4H), with the G4H group exhibiting the highest ADG (P < 0.050). Over the entire trial period, feeding system significantly influenced ADG (P = 0.042), which ranged from 190.00 g/day (G8H) to 252.02 g/day (G4H). In addition, ADG was 32.64% higher (P < 0.05) in the G4H group compared to the G8H group and tended to be higher than that in the CK group (P = 0.092). In terms of DMI, values during the 0–30 d, 31–60d, 61–90d and the overall average for the trial were higher in the CK and G4H groups than in the G8H group (P < 0.001).

Meat fatty acid composition

The contents of FAs (mg/100 g fresh meat) in the LL muscle are reported in Table 3. The contents of C14:0, C16:0, and C18:1n-9 were lower in the G8H group compared with the CK group (P < 0.05), while G4H lambs displayed intermediate values. In contrast, lambs in the grazing groups (G4H and G8H) exhibited higher levels of eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA, C20:5n-3), docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, C22:6n-3), and n-3 polyunsaturated FAs (PUFAs) compared with the CK group (P < 0.05). Total saturated FAs (SFAs) and monounsaturated FAs (MUFAs) contents were unaffected by feeding system (P > 0.05), whereas total PUFA content was significantly higher in G8H lambs than in CK lambs (P < 0.05). Other individual SFAs, MUFAs, and PUFAs did not differ significantly among different feeding systems (P > 0.05). The n6/n3 PUFA ratio was significantly lower in both G4H and G8H groups than in the CK group (P < 0.01). Additionally, the MUFA/PUFA ratio tended to be higher in G8H lambs compared to CK lambs (P = 0.084).

Rumen microbiota composition

16S rRNA sequencing of rumen bacteria indicated that sequencing depth was sufficient to support microbial diversity analyses (Fig. 3a). A total of 2960 OTUs were identified across the three experimental groups, with 92, 71, and 233 OTUs unique to the CK, G4H, and G8H groups, respectively (Fig. 3b). The observed number of OTUs was significantly higher in the G4H and G8H groups compared to the CK group (Supplementary Fig. 1a; P < 0.05). Although Good's coverage value did not differ significantly among groups (P > 0.05), the values of three groups exceeded 0.99, demonstrating that the 16S rRNA sequencing data were sufficiently deep and highly reliable (Supplementary Fig. 1b). PCoA revealed a clear separate clustering of rumen microbial communities, with PCoA1 and PCoA2 explaining 16.26% and 11.75% of the variance, respectively (Fig. 3c). Analysis of similarity (ANOSIM) based on Unweighted UniFrac distances showed significant differences in community structure among feeding systems (R = 0.270, P = 0.002; Supplementary Fig. 2). Community richness (Chao 1 index) was significantly higher in G8H group than in CK group (Fig. 3d-e; P < 0.05). Observed_species and Shannon diversity indices were higher in both G4H and G8H groups compared with the CK group (Fig. 3f; P < 0.05), whereas Simpson index values did not differ significantly among groups (Fig. 3g; P > 0.05).

Discussion

In the present study, lambs in the G4H group had a higher ADG compared to those in the G8H group. This finding aligns with a previous study suggesting that restricted grazing combined with indoor supplementary fattening confers greater benefits for growth performance in growing lambs (Wang et al., 2015). The improvement in ADG may be attributed to the advantages of shorter grazing time combined with higher DMI from indoor feeding, which together provide higher levels of digestible nutrients while reducing energy expenditure from physical activity, such as walking long distances to find forage (Wang et al., 2015; Zhang, Han, Hou, Raza, Wang, et al., 2022). Another explanation is that the artificial grassland composed of grasses, white clover and alfalfa, likely maximized fresh forage intake (Wang, Wang, Chen, et al., 2021), whereby lambs under shorter grazing durations achieved greater pasture intake rates and had a higher proportion of grazing time intaking forage (Chen et al., 2013). Previous studies have similarly demonstrated that lambs grazing on natural rangelands enriched with lucerne (Medicago arborea) or alfalfa under high herbage allowance exhibit superior growth rates (Devincenzi et al., 2019).

Meat edible quality is generally evaluated by objective traits, such as pH, color, juiciness, and tenderness. pH is a critical indicator of meat freshness and the rapid decline in pH post-slaughter, which can negatively influence meat quality attributes (Huang et al., 2023; Zhang, Han, Hou, Raza, Wang, et al., 2022). In the present study, pH 45min and pH 24h values did not differ significantly among feeding regimens, suggesting that different feeding regimens did not affect physiological stress or alter glycogen reserves that influence ultimate pH, consistent with the findings of previous studies (Majdoub-Mathlouthi et al., 2013; Wang, Wang, Zuo, et al., 2021; Zhang, Han, Hou, Raza, Wang, et al., 2022). Notably, pH24h values ranged from 5.86 to 6.15, falling within the acceptable range for sheep meat (Majdoub-Mathlouthi et al., 2013), indicating minimal the risk of protein denaturation and optimal water-holding capacity (Wang et al., 2024), thus ensuring the desirable properties for processing and consumption. Meat color, a key sensory attribute, directly affects consumer preference and shelf life (Huang et al., 2023). Among color parameters, a* has the strongest correlation with consumer acceptance; values above 14.5 are typically considered acceptable by more than 95% of consumers (Zhang, Wang, Jin, Zhao, & Duan, 2022). In the present study, a* values ranged from 17.5 to 19.9 across groups, confirming the strong marketing potential of Huang-huai lambs. Regarding L*, lambs in the CK group tended to have higher values than those in the G8H group, suggesting that meat color may be influenced by activity levels associated with different feeding intensities. Since meat color is affected by fat deposition, oxidation processes and ferric myoglobin reducing capacity (Fruet et al., 2018), indoor feeding may reduce oxidation and enhance the ferric myoglobin reducing capacity due to lower physical activity (Casamassima et al., 2001; Zhang et al., 2023). In addition, pasture-grazing animals generally consume more green forage, which may increase muscle iron content and reduce L* values (Ponnampalam et al., 2020). This observation aligns with earlier studies reporting higher L* values in concentrate-fed lambs than in pasture-fed counterparts (Díaz et al., 2002; Zhang, Wang, Jin, Zhao, & Duan, 2022). It is known that cooking loss and drip loss are negatively correlated with juiciness (Huang et al., 2023). In the present study, no significant effect of feeding system was observed for either parameter, indicating that supplemented grazing did not compromise meat juiciness. These findings are consistent with those reported by Wang et al. (2015) and da Silva et al. (2019), who reported similar drip loss values for time-limited grazing with indoor feeding or supplemented grazing compared to stall feeding. Meat tenderness is negatively correlated with WBSF, which is directly influenced by IMF content (Huang et al., 2023; Zhang, Wang, Jin, Zhao, & Duan, 2022). A rapid decline in pH also causes muscle contraction, thereby reducing tenderness (Choi et al., 2005). In the present study, meat samples from lambs in the G4H group exhibited slightly lower WBSF values than those from the G8H group, indicating improved tenderness in the G4H group. This improvement may be attributed to higher ADG, a slower pH decline, and slightly higher IMF content observed in the G4H group (Zhang et al., 2023).

Meat chemical composition, including moisture, protein, IMF, and ash content, is a key indicator of nutritional quality. In the present study, no significant differences were observed in moisture, IMF, or ash content among groups, consistent with the findings reported by Wang, Wang, Zuo, et al. (2021). These results may be explained by the similar abundance of rumen Firmicutes and the Firmicutes / Bacteroidetes ratio across groups, as these microbial features and their ratio are negatively associated with meat IMF content (Guo et al., 2024). Although artificial grassland had higher NDF and ADF, no notable variation was observed in the relative abundances of these microbes, suggesting that their stability may have limited fiber-driven, microbiota-mediated regulation of muscle fat deposition (Guo et al., 2024). Nonetheless, meat from all feeding systems was classified as lean, with fat content below 5% (Bezerra et al., 2016), likely reflecting the young age of the animals employed in the study. Appropriate fat levels are known to enhance meat taste and flavor, with a range of 3.4%–4.5% generally producing a rich and fragrant profile (Sañudo et al., 2000). Herein, mean IMF values in the CK (3.54%) and G4H (3.67%) groups fall within the optimal range, indicating that these feeding regimes are effective in producing lamb meat with desirable sensory attributes, including improved taste and flavor potential. Furthermore, the feeding regime had a significant effect on protein content only in the CK and G4H groups, potentially related to muscle protein deposition in growing animals (Bezerra et al., 2016). Muscle activity plays a pivotal role in modulating energy metabolism. Reduced fat deposition favors myoprotein formation and ultimately increases muscle content (Huang et al., 2023). It can therefore be hypothesized that greater grazing activity in the G4H group relative to the CK group may have promoted carbohydrate and fat catabolism, thereby enhancing protein synthesis. Additionally, only lambs in the G4H and G8H groups exhibited protein content values >18.5%, indicative of good-quality meat (Bezerra et al., 2016). It is worth noting that underlying metabolic changes in meat tissue are the primary factors influencing meat CP content. In a study of feedlot Tibetan lambs, variations in feeding regimes altered the CP content of lamb meat, which was found to be associated with changes in key metabolites, including L-asparagine, and peroxide-free arachidonic acid (Zhang, Han, Hou, Raza, Wang, et al., 2022). In our study, (R)-Salsolinol and homoarecoline were significantly associated with meat CP content, suggesting that CP content of lamb meat could be improved by modulating the muscle metabolism in the G4H group. Collectively, the feeding pattern combining indoor feeding with limited grazing improved meat nutritional quality to some extent, positively influencing tenderness, taste, and flavor, thereby meeting both preferences and health standards of the consumers.

FA profile, a crucial indicator of nutritional value and meat flavor, also affects human health and has received increasing attention (Huang et al., 2023; Wang, Wang, Chen, et al., 2021). Numerous studies have shown that meat FA composition in lambs can be modified by feeding systems (Gruffat et al., 2019; Wang, Wang, Chen, et al., 2021), and the present study confirms this effect. SFAs, including C14:0 and C16:0, are associated with cholesterol synthesis and low-density lipoprotein accumulation, thereby increasing cardiovascular disease risk (Wang, Wang, Chen, et al., 2021). Encouragingly, the G8H group showed relatively lower C14:0, and C16:0 contents compared with the CK group, suggesting that extended grazing improves the health-related FA profile of lamb meat. This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that fresh forage-based diets can improve the favorable FA profile by reducing saturated fat content and increasing the proportion of beneficial unsaturated fats (Wang, Wang, Chen, et al., 2021). Conversely, n-3 long-chain PUFAs, particularly EPA and DHA, are recognized for their cardiovascular benefits and well-being-promoting effects (Huang et al., 2023; Wang, Wang, Chen, et al., 2021). Higher levels of EPA, DHA, and n-3 PUFAs were observed in forage-grazing groups (G4H and G8H) than in CK, aligning with previous findings on the improved FAs composition in meat from forage-grazing lambs (Gruffat et al., 2020). These differences may be attributed to higher levels of plant-derived fatty acids in fresh forage, especially rich in EPA, which enhance n-3 PUFAs deposition in grazing animals (Wang, Wang, Chen, et al., 2021). In contrast, indoor-fed lambs receiving whole-plant corn silage and concentrates accumulate fewer beneficial fatty acids. Efficient accumulation of n-3 PUFAs in lamb meat may also result from the presence of alfalfa and white clover in grazing pastures, which are rich in condensed tannins. These tannins inhibit ruminal biohydrogenation, thereby increasing the bypass of dietary n-3 PUFAs to the small intestine for absorption and subsequent deposition into muscle tissue. In addition, meat from both forage-grazing groups (G4H and G8H) reached approximately 56 mg/100 g of EPA and DHA, exceeding the European threshold (40 mg/100 g) required for lamb meat to be considered a source of n-3 PUFA (Gruffat et al., 2020). The n-6 PUFA/n-3 PUFA ratio, an important indicator of the nutritional and health values of meat, should not exceed 4 in the human diet to preserve minimal cardiovascular risk (Huang et al., 2023; Wang, Wang, Chen, et al., 2021). In the present study, the n6/n3 ratio in the G4H and G8H groups was close to 4, whereas CK lambs showed a markedly higher value (6.48). This stark contrast showed that both grazing on artificial grasslands and short-term grazing combined with TMR supplementation offer insights into providing consumers with healthier and high-quality lamb meat through development of the artificial pasture grazing industry and adjusting grazing time.

It is well known that high species diversity in the rumen is associated with greater functional redundancy (Cui et al., 2023). In the present study, the Chao 1, observed_species and Shannon indices were lower in CK sheep than in pasture-grazing sheep, consistent with earlier reports that indoor feeding reduces microbial richness and diversity (Cui et al., 2023). The greater microbial diversity observed in grazing lambs is likely a direct consequence of their more varied fodder sources on artificial pastures. The diet leads to major shifts in fiber and water-soluble carbohydrates, which provide a broad range of nutrients and require a more functionally diverse bacterial community for efficient nutrient utilization (Cui et al., 2023). As expected, rumen microbial community structure is highly responsive to diet and feeding systems (Pitta et al., 2010), as confirmed by ANOSIM. Despite these structural shifts, Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes remained the dominant phyla across the three feeding systems, indicating their essential functional and ecological roles in the rumen, which is consistent with previous studies on lambs and beef cattle (Fan et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023). Moreover, the relative abundance of the phylum Planctomycetes was lower abundance in G4H and G8H groups compared to the CK group. Although the functional role of Planctomycetes in the rumen remains poorly understood, this phylum has been linked to carbohydrate metabolism (Stewart et al., 2019). Interestingly, a similar decrease in Planctomycetes has been reported in high-yielding dairy cows compared to low-yielding cows (Mu et al., 2019), speculating a possible negative correlation between its abundance with production traits in ruminants. At the genus level, Rikenellaceae RC9 gut group, Prevotella 1 and Christensenellaceae R-7 group were dominant, consistent with previous findings in lambs (Wang, Wang, Zuo, et al., 2021). Prevotellaceae_UCG-003 tended to show a slightly higher abundance in the G4H group than the CK group. Although the difference did not meet predefined threshold for statistical significance, the increasing trend in relative abundance of Prevotellaceae_UCG-003 could potentially explain the higher ADG trend observed in the G4H group (P = 0.092). This speculation is supported by the fact that Prevotellaceae_UCG-003 is involved in cellulolysis as well as in glucose and protein metabolism, thereby improving feed utilization (Gu et al., 2024).

Untargeted metabolomics revealed that the metabolites differentiating CK and G4H groups were primarily enriched in pathways related to biosynthesis of secondary metabolites; arginine and proline metabolism; glycine, serine and threonine metabolism; biosynthesis of phenylpropanoids; and biosynthesis of alkaloids derived from the shikimate pathway. Among the different metabolites within these pathways, the G4H group showed upregulation of ornithine and downregulation of creatine compared with the CK group. Ornithine contributes to reducing the bitterness of amino acids (Song et al., 2025), while creatine, known for its bitter taste, may act as a precursor of heterocyclic aromatic amines, which are carcinogenic to humans (Janicki & Buzala, 2013). These shifts in the G4H group suggest a reduction in undesirable taste and a potential improvement in both the palatability and functional quality of meat, aligning favorably with consumer preferences. For pathways enriched in CK and G8H groups, key metabolites (−)-gossypol, pantothenic acid, and triethanolamine were down-regulated, whereas scopoletin was up-regulated in the G8H group compared with the CK group. Given that scopoletin is a potent antioxidant factor that protects against FA oxidation and enhances meat quality (Ha et al., 2021), the metabolic profile implies that meat from the G8H group exhibited potentially greater health-promoting and oxidative stability properties. In comparisons between G4H and G8H, nitrite and acrylamide were down-regulated in the G8H group. Moreover, across the three groups, ornithine was enriched in G4H, while scopoletin was increased but nitrite and acrylamide were decreased in the G8H group. Nitrite contributes to rancid flavors during thermal processing (Wójciak et al., 2019). These changes suggest that G4H meat may offer superior sensory attributes with fewer undesirable flavor attributes, making it potentially more appealing in terms of taste. In contrast, G8H meat may offer potential health advantages, due to its enhance antioxidant capacity and lower levels of harmful compounds such as nitrite.

An increasing number of studies have shown that changes in rumen microbiota are associated with muscle metabolites and may help explain mechanisms underlying meat quality in ruminants (Wang, Wang, Chen, et al., 2021; Zhang, Han, Hou, Raza, Gui, et al., 2022). The correlation analysis conducted in the presented study is consistent with this evidence, suggesting that modulation of the rumen microbial community is associated with variation in meat quality traits. Prevotellaceae UCG-003 is a host-beneficial catabolic bacterium typically involved in glucose and protein metabolism (Gu et al., 2024). The differential bacterium Prevotellaceae UCG-003 was significantly positively associated with meat C20:5n-3, C22:6n-3, n-3 PUFAs, PUFAs, and n-6/n-3 PUFA in lamb meat, suggesting that the genus by participating in glucose and protein catabolism, may improve lipid precursor supply to enhance muscle n-3 PUFAs. Prevotella 1 was positively correlated with CP. This may be attributed to the ability of Prevotella spp. to enhance degradation of ruminal protein and generation of absorbable amino acids, thereby contributing to increasing the accumulation of CP in meat (Hu et al., 2024). Butyrivibrio 2 was negatively correlated with n-3 PUFAs and PUFAs. This relationship might be attributed to rumen biohydrogenating bacteria represented by Butyrivibrio spp. detoxifying dietary PUFAs and transforming unsaturated FAs into SFAs, thus providing precursors for FA synthesis (Lourenço et al., 2010). In addition, Butyrivibrio 2 was found to be positively correlated with acrylamide, which may potentially regulate meat tenderness and FA composition via sesquiterpenoid and triterpenoid biosynthesis as well as microbial metabolism in diverse environments. Given that acrylamide may form in vivo from precursors via non-enzymatic Maillard-type reactions, future studies should further explore how metabolites generated by Butyrivibrio 2 influence blood transport, intestinal absorption, and muscle metabolism to regulate acrylamide deposition, thereby supporting the precise exploitation and application of the functional rumen bacteria to improve lamb meat quality.

Conclusions

Growth performance and meat quality in lambs were significantly influenced by different feeding regimes. The ADG and meat CP content were both increased in the G4H group. Meat from the G4H and G8H groups exhibited FA profiles more favorable for human health, characterized by higher contents of C22:6n-3, C20:5n-3, and n-3 PUFA, along with a lower n-6/n-3 PUFA ratio compared to the CK group. Key microbes (Butyrivibrio 2, Prevotella 1, Prevotellaceae UCG-003 and Lachnospiraceae NK3A20 group) and metabolites [(R)-salsolinol, nicotinic acid mononucleotide, and acrylamide] were associated with variation in meat tenderness, protein deposition, and FA synthesis. Taken together, artificial pasture grazing for 4 h with indoor feeding increased ADG, improved meat quality and favorable shifts in key metabolites alongside shifts in rumen microbial community, representing a better selection for simultaneously improving growth performance and meat quality in lamb production. Further research is needed to determine whether targeted enrichment of beneficial rumen bacteria can enhance meat quality traits, including tenderness, CP and n-3 PUFAs, as well as other bioactive compounds in lamb meat. Furthermore, future studies should examine the long-term ecological and economic sustainability of artificial pasture grazing systems, with emphasis on rational construction and management of artificial grasslands to support large-scale promotion and application in sustainable lamb production and better respond to the advocacy of artificial grazing.
