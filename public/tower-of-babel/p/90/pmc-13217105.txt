Use of nasal high-flow in emergency departments: a four-nation UK-wide survey
Authors: Cal Doherty, David Cunningham, Fiona Marie Burton
Published: 2026

Introduction

Nasal high flow (NHF) is increasingly used in the emergency department (ED) worldwide.1 2 ED-based studies show that it is both feasible to use, reduces dyspnoea, improves respiratory parameters in type-1 respiratory failure (T1RF) and is non-inferior to non-invasive ventilation (NIV) for undifferentiated respiratory failure.35 Despite growing interest, its current use in the UK for adult patients in the ED is poorly understood. Mapping current NHF use in the ED, including perceived benefits and barriers, is essential to understand its impact, implementation and guide future research.

Methods

This was an online service-level survey of all UK type-1 EDs serving adults (≥16 years old) between April and May 2025. The survey prototype underwent review from experts in NHF and survey methodology. It was piloted by nine ED clinicians from across the UK. The final 29-question survey encompassed NHF access, indications and factors impacting implementation (see online supplemental appendix 1). Participants were contacted via existing email distribution lists and publicly available contact information. Completion implied informed consent. Descriptive statistics and content analysis were used to analyse results. Survey participants have been acknowledged in online supplemental appendix 2 unless they requested anonymity.

Results

Responses were received from 122 (58%) of 211 identified EDs. ED consultants completed 73% of the surveys. Of the 122 EDs, 52% (63/122) reported having access to NHF for adult patients, although a third of these were only able to initiate NHF via consultation with other specialty teams, for example, Critical Care. Across the UK, NHF availability ranged from 40% of sites in Wales and 50% in Scotland to 86% in Northern Ireland, with England showing significant regional variation from 33% in the North East and Yorkshire to 75% in London.

Of the 63 sites with NHF access, 91% reported that their NHF devices met demand either all or most of the time, with 55% using it at least once a week. Established protocols were used at 38% of sites.

Review of free-text responses and shared protocols identified additional NHF indications, including as an alternative to NIV or invasive ventilation, warming, carbon monoxide poisoning, face-mask intolerance, sickle cell crisis, weaning from NIV and poor secretion clearance.

Most sites could receive NHF patients in their ICU (83%) or Medical High-Dependency Unit (HDU) (54%), and 75% of sites had at least one respiratory-specific area for patients on NHF. All General Surgical Wards and the majority of General Medical Wards (90%) could not receive patients on NHF.

Figure 2 shows the perceived strength of certain barriers and facilitators to NHF use in the ED. Additional barriers identified via free text included lack of organisational culture and staff awareness, interdepartmental dependency (eg, ability to initiate NHF independently), infrastructure limitations, bed availability and patient flow. Of the 122 responding sites, 74 indicated willingness to be contacted for future NHF research participation.

Discussion

This survey suggests that access to NHF in UK EDs is more limited than previously described. While Alnajada et al2 reported 95% hospital-level use in the UK, only half of UK EDs reported NHF access in this study, with marked regional variation. Consistent with prior surveys,2 6 T1RF remains the most common indication, but practice beyond this varies widely. NHF use in EDs is constrained by limited equipment, inconsistent training, unclear guidance and reliance on other specialties, highlighting a gap between hospital capability and deliverability in the ED. These findings underscore the need for standardised guidance, consistent implementation and further research to support equitable NHF use. Ongoing Royal College of Emergency Medicine (RCEM) supported work includes a scoping review of patient-reported measures, patient focus groups and chest trauma research.
