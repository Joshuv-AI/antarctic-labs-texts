Stabilization of polyacrylonitrile-based fiber with a quasi-traveling microwave applicator
Authors: Hung-Chun Hsu, Hsien-Wen Chao, Wen-Chang Huang, Tsun-Hsu Chang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study introduces a novel microwave applicator and optimized processing conditions to enhance the stability of Polyacrylonitrile (PAN)-based precursor fiber (PF). The innovative microwave applicator facilitates the propagation of the electromagnetic (EM) field akin to a quasi-traveling wave, thus circumventing standing wave nodes. This ensures a uniform thermal distribution and broadens the heating zone. Utilizing this applicator, the PF undergoes thermal stabilization in a streamlined two-step process, completing in just 13 min, a significant improvement over the conventional 90-min process. This not only saves manufacturing time, promoting energy efficient manufacturing but also aligns with the global trend towards green energy and lightweight carbon fiber-reinforced polymer matrix composites, potentially catalyzing rapid economic growth. Fiber characterization through Raman spectroscopy (RS), X-ray diffraction (XRD), differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), and complex permittivity measurements reveals that the microwave-processed fiber meets the standard of commercial stabilization fiber (SF).Subject terms: Engineering, Physics, Materials science, Condensed-matter physics, Materials for devices

Introduction

Carbon fiber (CF) is known for its high electrical conductivity, high tensile strength, high modulus, excellent creep resistance, lightweight and flexibility. Besides, CF is resistant to all other chemical species except oxidizing agents. These outstanding mechanical properties make CF reinforced materials highly attractive1–4, leading to continuous growth in the CF industry. The emphasis on applications spans across aerospace, military, construction, medical and sporting goods5–10. CF is typically produced from three main types of precursors, polyacrylonitrile (PAN), pitch and rayon. PAN is the most popular precursor because of its higher chemical stability, higher carbon yield, higher combined strength and higher modulus properties after stabilization and carbonization processes compared to other precursors2,4,11–15. To transform PAN precursor into CFs, the stabilization and carbonization processes are necessary, and graphitization is optional. The precursor will be stabilized at temperatures between 200 and 400 °C, carbonized at up to 1600 °C and graphitized at up to 3000 °C and the final weight percent of carbon above 90%4,10–20.

In the stabilization process, cyclization, dehydrogenation and oxidation are involved21, resulting in transformation of the linear chain structure of PAN-based precursor fiber (PF) into a conjugated ladder structure, which is thermally stable and might be able to withstand high temperature during carbonization11,16,21–34. The conjugated ladder structure results in the formation of a dense and rigid aromatic structure that inhibits the inward diffusion of oxygen from the sheath to the core. This might result in significant differences in oxygen content and chemical structure between the sheath and the core, known as the sheath-core structure25,35. To avoid this, the common temperature gradient of stabilization process is slow, and the process time tends to be longer consequently. In the industry, the stabilization process usually takes a minimum of 90 min, constituting 90% of the overall production time of CFs, while the electric energy cost accounts for approximately 70% of the total power consumption. In pace with technological advancements, to accommodate higher CF requirements, the more efficiency facilities to manufacture CF is an urgent demand, but time-consuming stabilization steps make commercialization difficult16,17,36–39. To save time, there are several methods that can be adapted, such as UV pre-treatment, electron beam annealing, plasma annealing, or microwave annealing (MWA). In this study, we focus on the effect of MWA on shortening the time of stabilization process.

Microwave belong to a portion of the electromagnetic (EM) spectrum with wavelengths ranging from 1 mm to 1 m, accompanied by corresponding frequencies between 300 GHz and 300 MHz. As a novel heating method, microwave have been utilized in processing a wide array of materials, including polymers, ceramics, organic and inorganic chemicals, among others40–42. Compared to conventional heating, microwave offer a unique approach to energy transfer. In conventional methods, heat is generated from an external source and then conveyed through convection, conduction, and radiation from the external to the internal part of the material. This process is relatively slow and inefficient. In contrast, microwave heating involves the conversion of EM energy directly into heat within the EM-absorbing material42. After absorbing EM wave the EM-absorbing materials transform the EM energy into heat. Dielectric loss, magnetic loss, and impedance matching are the critical factors which administer the microwave absorption efficiency of a material. In the case of non-magnetic materials, the attenuation mechanism of the microwave absorber mainly proceeds via dielectric loss. The higher dielectric loss exhibits stronger microwave absorption capability. Due to variations in the dielectric loss of different materials, MWA demonstrates selective heating characteristics, leading to efficient heating and reduced treatment time19,33,40,43–48.

Therefore, this study proposed a new quasi-travel MWA system for PF into stabilization fiber (SF) with two-annealing steps, the first annealing is 8 min and the second annealing is 5 min, for a total of only 13 min. The specimens were also characterized by scanning electric microscopy (SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), differential scanning calorimeter (DSC), Raman spectroscopy (RS) and complex permittivity. Shortening manufacturing time to decrease energy consumption in manufacturing, and reducing the cost of carbon fiber-reinforced polymer matrix composites, satisfying the demands brought about by global trends, could lead to fast economic growth.

Experimental

Materials

The 12 K (12,000 filaments per fiber bundle) PF and 12 K stabilized PAN fiber supplied by Zhejiang Jinggong Science and Technology Co., Ltd., China, are selected in this study without purification. By enhanced-field method (EFM), the permittivities of PF and SF are determined by ε′/ε0=2.73,ε″/ε′=0.0097 and ε′/ε0=4.81,ε″/ε′=0.0753 respectively49–54.

Quasi-traveling wave applicator

There are two types of common microwave cavities, resonant and non-resonant. In previous research, a TM mode resonator system operating at a frequency of 2.45 GHz was proposed to accomplish the stabilization reaction of PAN fibers during continuous feeding processes48. The feature of such resonant cavities is the concentration of EM field energy and stable, controllable field patterns. However, a drawback is the difficulty in manufacturing large and uniform heating fields, which can be addressed through a quasi-traveling wave applicator. Therefore, we propose a quasi-traveling wave applicator constructed with a 650 mm-long WR340 waveguide with one end for microwave propagating in and the other end closed, a 390 mm-long silicon carbide (SiC) cylindrical tube to provide the thermal field by absorbing microwave, Al2O3 as insulators to separate the high temperature SiC tube and the wave guide, and a couple of windows to measure the temperature of the SiC tube. The fiber enters the applicator through the entry hole on the closed end of the WR340, is exposed to the EM field inside the applicator, and then exits from the other end (shown in Fig. 1a).

(a) Schematic of the quasi-traveling wave applicator and the simulation of electric field and temperature distribution on (b,c) 390 mm with 176W microwave output power, (d,e) 260 mm with 126W microwave output power and (f,g) 130 mm SiC tubes with 107W microwave output power by HFSS.

The principle of the quasi-traveling wave applicator lies in using SiC within the applicator to absorb the energy of the propagating wave, thus forming an incomplete standing wave. Also, because of the quasi-traveling wave's traveling wave characteristics and the high thermal conductivity of SiC, it is advantageous for producing heating fields of varying lengths that are quite uniform. With high frequency structure simulation (HFSS) software, the full wave simulations are conducted, and the results from Fig. 1b,d,f show that the final EM field patterns within the applicator will appear as a superposition of standing and traveling waves, hence the name quasi-traveling wave, and the results from Fig. 1c,e,g show that to maintain the same temperature with SiC tubes of different lengths, exerting different microwave output power is available.

MWA system

The whole system components are shown in Fig. 2. The MWA system mainly consists of a 2.45 GHz microwave source and a 500 mm quasi-traveling wave applicator. The microwave is generated by the microwave source and then propagating into the applicator. An isolator is used to prevent reflected wave back to the source, and the reflected wave is directed into water to dissipate it. The power meter measures the values of the propagating and reflected waves, and an impedance matcher is used to adjust the value of the reflected wave.

Schematic of the continuous MWA stabilization system: (a) 2.45 GHz microwave source, (b) isolator, (c) power meter, (d) water cooler, (e) impedance matcher, (f) quasi-travel wave applicator, (g) weight, (h) infrared thermometer, (i) fiber roller, (j) reducing gearbox and motor.

Since the PF is a low-loss material, we introduce a 390 mm SiC tube as susceptor to generate a thermal environment for the early stabilization. Initially, the fiber is mounted on a fiber roller and is pulled into the applicator by weights and heated by microwave and a temperature gradient from SiC tube simultaneously and the fiber speed is controlled by a reducing gearbox and motor.

Stabilization

Since the fibers are enclosed in the SiC tube during the reaction in the applicator, the surface temperature of the fibers cannot be directly measured. Therefore, the high thermal conductivity SiC tube is used as a substitute for measuring. Figure 3a shows the locations on the SiC where the infrared thermometer was used to measure the temperature.

(a) The identification number of the location measured by the infrared thermometer and (b) its corresponding temperature distribution in the SiC tube varies with different average temperatures for distinct microwave output powers.

From Fig. 3b, as the output microwave power grows, the temperature distribution gets higher gradually and the average temperature on the SiC tube grows, too. As we said before, stabilization is a very exothermic reaction and releases large amount of heat, PF will oxidize severely and break while directly annealed at an average temperature above 229˚C because of the lack of enough heat-resistant trapezoidal structure. Therefore, here adopted a two-step annealing process to stabilize precursor, the first annealing temperature is selected at an average temperature of 223˚C in air for 8 min, and the second annealing temperatures are selected at an average temperature of 229˚C, 241˚C, 253˚C, 263˚C and 273˚C in air for 5 min, for a total of only 13 min. The 'O' in the specimen name O191 stands for 'One-Step', while 'T' stands for 'Two-Step', and the number 191 represents the final annealing temperature in degrees Celsius. (Table 1).

Results and discussion

Specimens

As the heating temperature increases, the color of the fibers varies from white to yellow, brown, and finally black (Fig. 4). In stabilization of PFs, a darker, blacker color indicates a higher level of stabilization, which also signifies greater microwave absorption. In the first stage of the 8-min process, the highest temperature that won't cause fiber breakage is 223 °C, resulting in a color of deep brown. Further processing of O223 fibers in a second 5-min stage yields fibers denoted as T229, T241, T253, T263, and T273, all of which appear black and are visually indistinguishable. Therefore, additional measurements are required to characterize the fibers.

X-ray diffraction

X-ray diffractometer (D8 DISCOVER, Bruker) was used over the 2θ range of 10°–40° to observe structural change during stabilization. Figure 5 shows the XRD patterns of PF and its stabilized fibers. For the PAN precursor, the characteristic peaks at 17.2° and 30° represent the linear chain structure. For the SF, a lower intensity of these two peaks and a new characteristic peaks at 25.5° which represent the conjugated ladder structure appear, indicating the disruption of orientation and crystalline structure by thermal stabilization55,56. For specimens O211, O223, T241, T263 and T273, as the processing temperature increases, due to dehydrogenation, oxidation, and cyclization reactions, the characteristic peaks at 17.2° and 30° decrease in intensity, while the peak at 25.5° increases. This shift signifies the gradual transformation of the linear chain structure into conjugated ladder structure, a more heat-resistant aromatic structure. Differential scanning calorimeter

DSC was used to study the thermal reaction during stabilization process ramped at 10 °C/min to 400 °C under air. For PF, the temperature of initiation for stabilization reaction starts at 240 °C, peaks at 290 °C and ends at 310 °C. Since critical reactions during stabilization, dehydrogenation, oxidation and especially cyclization, are mainly exothermic reactions, the enthalpy of stabilization reaction could be used to demonstrate the cyclization degree32. As the enthalpy reducing to 0, the stabilization reaction is completed and the PF turning into SF.

As can be seen from Fig. 6, the total exotherm amount of fibers gradually decreased with the increase of the heat treatment temperature during stabilization, indicating that the degree of cyclization increased. Furthermore, because the total exotherm amount of specimen O211 clearly decreased compared by PF, it can be deduced that the stabilization reaction initiates earlier than 240 °C with microwave heating. Based on the results, it can be inferred that the heat release reactions for T263 and T273 are nearing completion, meeting the criteria of SF.

Raman spectroscopy

We used a Raman microprobe with argon-ion laser to obtain the first-order RS of carbon-bearing materials span about 800–2000 cm−1 and are generally dominated by two primary peaks. The G peak, which represents a graphitic structure, located near 1580–1600 cm−1, is associated with the sp2 bonded carbon atoms and is due to the doubly degenerate E2g peak at the Brillouin zone center, whereas the D peak, which signifies a disordered structure, located near 1350 cm−1, is defect-related and arises from a complex interaction with the electron band structure57,58.

The RI represents the ratio of the intensity between the D and G peaks, and the S/C Factor records the RI values of the sheath and core. The closer the RI value is to 1, the more similar the structures are between the sheath and core.

Since the Raman microprobe can measure the amount of localized graphite structure formation, comparing the RS of the sheath and core on a single fiber can help understand whether the fibers produced in this study exhibit a sheath-core structure. Complex permittivity

The contour map offers another way to understand the stabilization of PAN fibers, by determining the complex permittivity. With enhanced-field method (EFM), HFSS simulation provides resonant frequencies and Q factors by changing the complex permittivity. By interpolation, iso-resonant frequency lines and iso-Q-factor lines are plotted with dashed lines49–54,59.

From Fig. 8, we can observe that the permittivity of O191 is like that of PAN fibers, but it starts to prepare for dehydrogenation, oxidation, and cyclization. These reactions cause changes in atomic composition, molecular structure, molecular spacing, leading to alterations in the permittivity and fiber density. Compared with the DSC data, it is evident that MWA initiates fiber stabilization at lower temperatures. The permittivity variations between O211 and O215 are dramatic. During this phase, the fiber undergoes significant color changes, indicating intense dehydrogenation, oxidation, and cyclization reactions. The permittivity of T273 is like that of SF, suggesting that this T273 may have completed the stabilization process, while T263 not yet.

Conclusion

This study presents a novel quasi-traveling wave microwave applicator to stabilize PF in a two-step process, completing the stabilization within only 13 min, saving the 85% processing time of stabilization. Notably, compared to resonant heating cavities, quasi-traveling wave applicator offers controllable applicator length and maintain a more uniform temperature distribution compared to conventional high-temperature furnaces. As a result, we can easily determine the required applicator size based on the desired fiber speed and residence time. Based on the measurements of density, XRD, DSC and complex permittivity, it can be concluded that specimen T273 is similar in characteristics to SF. The RS results also indicate uniform stabilization in both the skin and core of the fibers. Besides, the temperature of initiation for stabilization reaction reduced from 240 to 211 °C with MWA, and PF subjected to stabilization via microwave show a more thorough conversion of their linear structures.
