Preclinical Quantitative In Vivo MRI Assessment of Exogenous Surfactant Pulmonary Distribution After Clinically Relevant Intratracheal Delivery
Authors: Oumaima Marfouk, Rémy Gérard, Ghalia Kaouane, Lara Leclerc, Noël Pinaud, Michael Fayon, Sophie Périnel‐Ragey, Jérémie Pourchez, Eric Dumas De La Roque, Yannick Crémillieux
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOptimizing pulmonary exogenous surfactant delivery remains a critical challenge in neonatal care, particularly for achieving uniform distal lung deposition while minimizing airway obstruction. This study aimed to establish a preclinical imaging framework for quantitative assessment of exogenous surfactant lung distribution in vivo using contrast‐enhanced ultrashort echo time (UTE) MRI and to evaluate the impact of physiological processes on surfactant retention and localization. Six juvenile rabbits received intratracheal instillation of a gadolinium‐enhanced surfactant solution under clinically relevant conditions. High‐resolution, motion‐robust 3D UTE MRI datasets were acquired, enabling voxel‐wise quantification of signal enhancement, gadolinium concentration, and spatial surfactant lung distribution. Peripheral volume fraction, laterality index, and distality index were computed to characterize deposition homogeneity and distal penetration. In vivo measurements were compared with previously reported ex vivo data from isolated rabbit thoraces. Quantitative MRI enabled precise mapping of surfactant within peripheral alveolar regions. Forty‐two percent of the instilled dose was retained in vivo, compared with 93.5% ex vivo. Laterality indices confirmed balanced right–left distribution, while distality indices demonstrated consistent peripheral deposition. The radial UTE sequence minimized motion artifacts and enabled robust voxel‐wise quantification under physiological breathing conditions. Quantitative in vivo MRI provides a sensitive and reliable method for assessing pulmonary surfactant delivery, spatial distribution, distal penetration, and homogeneity of deposition. Comparison with ex vivo data underscores the role of physiological processes in surfactant retention. This framework supports optimization of administration strategies in preclinical models and may be extended to other intrapulmonary therapies, establishing a versatile imaging platform for translational research.

Introduction

Respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality in preterm infants, accounting for a substantial proportion of neonatal intensive care admissions and deaths worldwide [1, 2]. The disease primarily results from pulmonary immaturity and a deficiency in endogenous surfactant, a phospholipid‐protein complex essential for reducing alveolar surface tension and preventing alveolar collapse during expiration [3]. Exogenous surfactant replacement therapy has thus become a cornerstone of neonatal care, significantly improving survival rates and pulmonary outcomes in premature infants [4]. However, optimizing surfactant delivery to ensure uniform alveolar distribution while minimizing airway obstruction and injury remains a critical clinical and research challenge [5, 6].

Over the past decades, surfactant administration techniques have evolved from invasive instillation during mechanical ventilation to minimally invasive approaches, such as Less Invasive Surfactant Administration (LISA) [7, 8]. With this approach, surfactant is instilled directly into the lungs of spontaneously breathing infants via thin catheters, reducing the need for mechanical ventilation. Yet, the efficiency of surfactant distribution, particularly in reaching distal lung regions, and the impact of administration conditions and anatomical factors on final deposition patterns remain poorly understood. Addressing these gaps requires precise, non‐invasive tools capable of visualizing and quantifying surfactant distribution within the lung parenchyma.

Preclinical animal models, particularly those that closely approximate the physiological and anatomical characteristics of human neonatal lungs (e.g., compliance, alveolar development, and lung size), have been essential for investigating surfactant delivery [9, 10, 11]. Although imaging modalities such as synchrotron computed tomography (CT) and scintigraphy have provided critical insights into overall deposition patterns of surfactant [12, 13], ultrashort echo time (UTE) MRI offers a compelling in vivo approach with millimeter‐scale spatial resolution. This capability enables three‐dimensional visualization of lung anatomy and facilitates a detailed assessment of pulmonary structure [14, 15]. Furthermore, when paired with contrast agents, UTE MRI can effectively map the lung regional distribution of administered solutions by leveraging T1 shortening and signal enhancement mechanisms [16, 17, 18].

Previous studies have demonstrated the feasibility of contrast‐enhanced UTE MRI for imaging exogenous surfactant in ex vivo rabbit lungs, achieving high spatial resolution and reproducible quantification [19]. However, ex vivo conditions lack critical physiological processes, such as mucociliary transport, spontaneous breathing mechanics, alveolar recruitment, and vascular perfusion, that may significantly alter intrapulmonary surfactant distribution compared with in vivo settings. These factors may influence the spatial distribution of instilled surfactant in such a model, limiting the translational relevance of ex vivo findings.

This study aims to address current gaps in understanding exogenous surfactant lung distribution by conducting in vivo imaging in a rabbit model. UTE MRI was used to perform in vivo imaging in rabbits administered with contrast‐enhanced surfactant solution, enabling quantification of signal enhancement, gadolinium concentration, and spatial surfactant distribution across lung regions. Novel quantitative MRI indices were developed to assess the uniformity and distal extent of surfactant lung distribution. These in vivo measurements were further interpreted in light of similar quantitative analyses previously obtained in an ex vivo isolated rabbit thorax model, providing a complementary experimental framework to better characterize the influence of physiological processes on surfactant lung distribution.

Materials and Methods

Animal Models

Experiments were performed on six White New Zealand juvenile female rabbits, six‐weeks‐old and 1.2 ± 0.15 kg body weight (Charles River, Saint‐Germain‐Nuelles, France). All procedures complied with EU Directive 2010/63/EU and the ARRIVE guidelines, and were approved by the local animal welfare committee (University of Bordeaux, APAFIS no. 49689‐2024052813567785).

Anesthesia Procedure

Anesthesia was performed in three stages according to the following timeline. A first subcutaneous pre‐sedation was administered using medetomidine (Domitor, 0.35 mg·kg−1), After 10 min of stabilization, a second intramuscular injection combining ketamine (Imalgène 1000, 50 mg·kg−1) and xylazine (Rompun, 7 mg·kg−1) was given to achieve suitable anesthesia for intubation. Intubation was performed about 20 min after this second sedation phase. Before MRI acquisition, a third intramuscular injection corresponding to half the initial anesthetic dose was administered to maintain the level of anesthesia needed for the entire imaging session (approximately 30 min). At the end of the procedure, the animal was euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (Euthasol, 150 mg·kg−1) while still deeply anesthetized.

Intubation Procedure and Surfactant Administration

After anesthesia, the rabbit was intubated using a custom 3D‐printed guidance tube specifically designed according to rabbit tracheal dimensions to facilitate airway access. The distal end of the tube was equipped with a miniature LED light source, providing internal illumination to ensure clear visualization of the epiglottis during insertion (Figure 1a). The animal was positioned in supine position on a customized cradle, and the head and neck were gently extended to align the tracheal axis. The tongue was displaced laterally to expose the laryngeal opening under the optic illumination provided by the guidance tube. A Surfcath catheter (Vygon, Ecouen, France) was then introduced through the tube to the oral cavity and advanced beyond the vocal cords into the trachea, positioned approximately 1 cm above the carina. The catheter was secured bilaterally to the teeth using custom fixators to limit movement during surfactant administration and MR imaging.

(a) Intubation set‐up including the endotracheal tube with LED, the animal cradle with head fixation and the ventilator hoses. (b) Instillation of surfactant solution based on LISA protocol. (c) Positioning of the animal within the flexible MRI coil.

A total of 2.2 mL of contrast‐enhanced surfactant contrast solution, consisting of Curosurf (Chiesi Farmaceutici, Parma, Italy) mixed with 10% (v/v) of Gd‐based contrast agent Dotarem (Guerbet, Villepinte, France) was pre‐warmed to 37°C and slowly instilled at a rate of 0.25 mL·s−1 through the tracheal catheter to mimic the administration conditions of the LISA technique (Figure 1b). Following the surfactant instillation, the animal was maintained on spontaneous breathing under Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP = 6 cmH2O).

MRI Protocol

In vivo MRI acquisitions were performed using a 3 T whole‐body system (Centurian 3 T ZGO, Canon Medical Systems, Japan). The animals were positioned supine at the magnet isocenter in a 16‐channel flexible coil used as a receiver, with the integrated body coil employed for RF excitation. MRI acquisitions started approximately 10 min after surfactant instillation. Throughout scanning, animals were maintained under spontaneous breathing with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP = 6 cmH2O) to replicate clinical preterm respiratory conditions.

A 3D UTE sequence was employed (TR = 3.7 ms, TE = 96 μs, one average, acquisition time = 3 min 28 s per dataset, field of view = 11.3 × 11.3 cm2, slice thickness = 1 mm, voxel size = 0.78 × 0.78 × 1 mm3). Data were acquired in a radial pattern, with gradient delay correction applied and images reconstructed online using the manufacturer's software. To achieve variable T₁ weighting, 3D UTE datasets were acquired at four distinct flip angles (α = 5°, 8°, 16°, and 25°). The total acquisition time, including flip angle adjustments, shimming, localizers, and four 3D UTE datasets, was approximately 20 min per animal. No respiratory gating was used during the MRI acquisitions.

During MRI acquisition, the rabbits remained in supine position on a heating pad with circulating temperature‐controlled water to maintain the animal's temperature at 37°C ± 0.5°C. A neonatal ventilator located in the MRI control room was used to apply CPAP throughout the MRI protocol (Figure 1c).

Image Processing and Analysis

Segmentation and Definition of Lung Volumes

Two lung volumes were identified on the 3D UTE images acquired with a flip angle of 25°: the total lung volume (TLV) and the peripheral enhanced region (PER). The PER corresponds to lung regions, excluding the identified airways, where deposition of contrast‐enhanced surfactant was observed.

Segmentation of these lung volumes from the in vivo MRI datasets was performed manually on a slice‐by‐slice basis using the Volume Segmenter tool in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, United States).

The TLV and PER volumes were also quantified separately for the right and left lungs. Total lung volumes were denoted TLVRL (right lung) and TLVLL (left lung), while contrast‐enhanced volumes were denoted PERRL (right lung) and PERLL (left lung).

The peripheral volume fraction (PVF), expressed as %, was calculated as the ratio of contrast‐enhanced volume in the peripheral enhanced regions to the total lung volume, (1) PVF=∑PERvol∑TLVvol where vol corresponds to the volume of a voxel. The PVF metric serves as a marker of the extent of surfactant presence in the lung regions likely to include alveoli.

The PVF metric was also measured separately for the right and the left lungs by computing PVFRL (right lung) and PVFLL (left lung): (2) PVFRL=∑PERRLvol∑TVRLvol,PVFLL=∑PERLLvol∑TVLLvol

Gd3+ Concentration Mapping and Quantification Metrics

The segmented volumes were subsequently used to compute regional gadolinium concentrations and quantitative distribution metrics.

Voxel‐wise concentrations, C, were derived from changes in the longitudinal relaxation rates between pre‐ and post‐instillation datasets, R1post = R1pre + r1.C, using a relaxivity value r1 of 3 mM−1 s−1 per Gd3+ ion for the contrast agent in plasma at 3 T [20].

The longitudinal relaxation rate R1post was obtained using the analytical expression of the MRI signal amplitude S for the UTE imaging sequence [21], (3) S=A.sinα1−e−TR.R1post1−cosα.e−TR.R1post where A is a constant depending on the MRI instrumentation and on the nuclear magnetization at thermal equilibrium. The longitudinal relaxation time T1pre was set equal to 1397 ms, a literature‐reported T₁ value for healthy lung tissue at 3 T [22].

When the computed concentration value was negative, it was set to zero in order to exclude non‐physical values.

The resulting concentration maps were then used along with the segmented lung volumes (TLV, PER) to enable regional quantification of surfactant deposition. Gd3+ quantity maps, expressed in nmol unit, were derived from the concentration maps by multiplying the Gd3+ concentration in each voxel by the volume of the voxel.

To estimate the limit of detection (LOD) for the Gd3+ concentration, we followed standard definitions of detection capability. A region of interest (ROI) was placed in the myocardium, which provides a homogeneous area with no Gd3+ uptake. Concentration values within this ROI were extracted, and the mean μ and standard deviation σ were calculated. The LOD was then defined as follows: (4) LOD=μ+3σ

This metric reflects the minimum concentration distinguishable from background variability.

To assess the asymmetry in surfactant solution distribution between the right and left lungs, a laterality index (LI) was defined as follows: (5) LI=PVFRL−PVFLLPVFRL+PVFLL

Values greater than zero indicate right‐sided predominance, values less than zero indicate left‐sided predominance, and values close to zero reflect a balanced distribution.

A distality index (DI) expressed in mm was computed as follows: (6) DI=1Nvoxel∑a,bmin‖b−a‖ with a and b, respectively the voxels from the contour of the total lung volume and from the contrast‐enhanced volume. For each voxel b in the PER, the minimum Euclidean distance to any voxel a on the contour of the TLV was computed (Figure S1a,b). The distality index DI was then calculated as the mean of these minimum distances across all the N voxel contrast‐enhanced voxels. As a result, the smaller the distality index, the more distally the surfactant solution is distributed within the lungs.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). To evaluate potential asymmetries in surfactant deposition, left–right lung balance was quantified by comparing the PVF𝑅𝐿 with the PVF𝐿𝐿. Because both measurements were obtained from the same subjects, a paired t‐test was used to assess whether systematic differences existed between the two sides. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using JASP software (JASP Team (2025), JASP (Version 0.19.3)).

To assess the robustness of the segmentation of lung volume TLV and PER, two independent lung segmentations were performed by the same operators 3 months apart and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was computed for the TLV, PER, PVF, LI, and DI metrics.

Results

Contrast‐Enhanced MRI of Surfactant Solution

Typical examples of MR images acquired after instillation of the 2.2 mL contrast‐enhanced surfactant solution are shown in Figure 2a. In this image series, the contrast‐to‐noise ratio (CNR) of the contrast‐enhanced lung region relative to the chest muscle was found to be −58, 46, 147, and 264 for flip angles of 5°, 8°, 16°, and 25°, respectively.

(a) Coronal slice obtained after surfactant administration at increasing flip angle. (b) From left to right: MIP reconstruction viewed from the front of the thorax, followed by coronal, axial, and sagittal slices acquired at a flip angle of 25°. (c) Axial slices acquired 30 min apart.

The MIP (Maximum Intensity Projection), coronal, axial, and sagittal images acquired at a 25° flip angle clearly showed the presence of contrast‐enhanced surfactant solution distributed in the lungs (Figure 2b). Noticeably, no signal hyperintensity was observed in identified airways (trachea, main, and lobar bronchi) but was noticed in the esophagus and in the stomach.

The signal intensities in the lung were unchanged over the 30‐min imaging period, indicating that the surfactant and contrast agent remained co‐localized within the intrapulmonary spaces. This observation was supported by the absence of noticeable enhancement in vessels, cardiac cavities, and myocardium in the images, indicating that the contrast agent did not cross the alveolar‐capillary membrane during the imaging period (Figure 2c).

Quantitative Gd3+ Distribution

Examples of Gd3+ concentration maps derived from UTE images are shown in Figure 4a. The LOD for the Gd3+ concentration was on the order of 0.7 mM (corresponding to a LOD in a voxel of 0.4 nanomol of Gd3+) and the mean concentration across all contrast‐enhanced volumes was equal to 2.6 ± 1.6 mM.

For the same data set, Figure 4b,c shows, respectively, the distribution of the quantity of Gd3+, derived from slice‐by‐slice measurements, from right to left and from anterior to posterior regions of the lungs. The equivalent plots obtained for each of the six imaged animals are shown in the Supporting Information (Figure S2).

As expected, given the supine position of the animals during both solution instillation and MRI acquisition, the axial and sagittal views along with the ventral‐to‐dorsal distribution of Gd3+ demonstrated a preferential accumulation of the surfactant solution in gravity‐dependent lung regions.

The right‐to‐left distribution of the surfactant was quantitatively assessed with the LI. Its mean value equal to 0.07 ± 0.12 (ICC = 0.86) indicated a slight right‐lung predominance in surfactant deposition. However, no statistically significant difference was found between PVFRL and PVFLL values using a paired t‐test, with the alternative hypothesis being a difference in their mean values (p = 0.219).

The total amount of Gd3+ in the lungs obtained by summing the Gd3+ quantity over all the voxels of the contrast‐enhanced PER volume was equal to 38 ± 28 μmol, corresponding to 42% of the 89 μmol of gadolinium effectively instilled in the lung.

The distality index DI was equal to 4.4 ± 0.3 mm (ICC = 0.96), with a coefficient of variation equal to 7% indicating a limited inter‐individual variability in the distality of the surfactant distribution in the lungs. Discussion

This study demonstrates the feasibility of quantitatively assessing in vivo the pulmonary distribution of exogenously administered surfactant using contrast‐enhanced 3D UTE MRI under clinically relevant conditions. The proposed approach offers original insights into the spatial distribution, uniformity, and distal penetration of surfactant within the lung parenchyma, addressing key limitations of other imaging modalities and ex vivo approaches. Compared with scintigraphy [13], which offers lower spatial resolution and involves difficulties specific to nuclear imaging, this MRI‐based technique enables higher‐resolution visualization of surfactant distribution. While synchrotron imaging and more generally CT imaging provide excellent spatial resolution, they rely on indirect detection of surfactant [12] due to their lower sensitivity to contrast agents. In this context, this MRI approach offers a balanced combination of spatial resolution and contrast sensitivity, making it a valuable tool for non‐invasive, in vivo evaluation of pulmonary surfactant distribution.

The surfactant administration technique replicates the standard clinical procedure used in preterm infants. In fact, the recommended dosage administered to preterm infants requiring surfactant is, for the commercial surfactant used in this study, 2.5 mL/kg (corresponding to 200 mg/kg), optionally supplemented by a second dose of 1.25 mL/kg. The volume of contrast solution used in this animal model (2.2 mL for an average weight of 1.2 kg) is thus close to the one used in clinical practice [2].

The use of a contrast agent was necessary to enable MRI visualization of the surfactant solution, raising questions regarding both the co‐localization of exogenous surfactant and contrast agent and the potential impact of contrast agent addition on surfactant lung distribution. Two observations support the validity of this approach. First, no temporal redistribution or decrease in signal intensity was observed over the 30‐min imaging period, and no enhancement was detected in vascular structures, cardiac cavities, or myocardium. This indicates preserved co‐localization of surfactant and gadolinium during in vivo imaging. Second, the absence of signal enhancement in the conducting airways is consistent with the well‐documented acceleration of mucociliary clearance induced by surfactant [23], combined with the delay between surfactant administration and the acquisition of the first MRI images. Together, these two elements suggest rapid clearance of surfactant from ciliated airways and selective retention within respiratory bronchioles and alveoli, ensuring that MRI visualization is not biased by non‐colocalized contrast agents.

A central finding emerges from the comparison between in vivo measurements and previously reported data obtained in ex vivo isolated rabbit thoraces [19]. While contrast‐enhanced MRI enabled robust quantification of surfactant distribution in both experimental settings, marked differences were observed in the retained fraction of the administered solution. In vivo, only 42% of the instilled gadolinium dose was detected within the lungs, compared with 93.5% in the ex vivo isolated thorax model. This discrepancy highlights the major role of physiological clearance mechanisms present in vivo, most notably mucociliary transport, which is absent in ex vivo conditions. Importantly, despite the reduced retained fraction, MRI signal enhancement was exclusively localized to the peripheral lung regions, where alveolar structures are located and where surfactant therapeutic activity is essential.

The literature relaxivity value r1 in plasma was deemed the most appropriate for our study, given the absence of red blood cells and hemoglobin in the intra‐alveolar space. The relevance of this choice was supported by the results obtained in a previous study [19], where the amount of Gd3+ quantified by MRI in the lungs matched the actual instilled Gd3+ concentration. An underestimation of the relaxivity value by Δr1 (%) would lead to an overestimation of the concentration by Δr1/(1 + Δr1) whereas an overestimation of Δr1 would result in an underestimation of the concentration by Δr1/(1 − Δr1). For a ±10% variation in r1, this translates to an approximate change of −9% and +11% in the concentration value, respectively.

It should be noted that the concentration measurements specifically refer to Gd3+ ions. Assuming co‐localization of gadolinium and surfactant at the voxel level, these Gd3+ concentration values can serve as a surrogate for estimating the surfactant mass concentration. The surfactant used in this study has a density (or mass concentration) of 80 mg/mL. Upon mixing with the contrast agent, the surfactant concentration in the contrast‐enhanced solution decreases to 73 mg/mL, while the Gd3+ concentration reaches 45 mM. Consequently, the mean Gd3+ concentration of 2.6 mM observed in the study corresponds to a mean surfactant mass concentration of 4.2 mg/mL.

Quantitative distribution indices illustrate both the strengths and limitations of in vivo and ex vivo approaches. Laterality indices revealed balanced right–left surfactant distribution in vivo, consistent with observations made in isolated thoraces [19], with LI values obtained from the isolated rabbit thoraces slightly positive (LI = 0.03), indicating that global symmetry of delivery is preserved across experimental conditions. In contrast, direct comparison of the distality index between in vivo and ex vivo models is not straightforward due to substantial differences in lung volumes. This highlights the importance of interpreting distality metrics within a given physiological context rather than as absolute values. Nevertheless, the low inter‐individual variability of the distality index observed in vivo supports its robustness and underscores its potential as a sensitive metric for evaluating and optimizing surfactant administration strategies under clinically relevant conditions.

Although the apparent spatial resolution achieved in vivo does not reach that obtained in ex vivo isolated thorax models, where higher‐generation airways can be visualized [19], the UTE sequence demonstrated remarkable robustness to respiratory and cardiac motion [24]. Further improvements in spatial resolution could be achieved through synchronization of signal acquisition with respiratory or cardiac cycles, which may enhance the resolution in future applications.

All things considered, the ex vivo isolated thorax model, despite its inherent limitations related to the absence of perfusion and mucociliary clearance, remains a valuable experimental framework. It enables optimization of administration parameters and imaging protocols prior to in vivo studies, while also complying with ethical principles by reducing the use of live animals. However, in vivo models remain essential for capturing the influence of physiological processes on surfactant lung distribution.

The presence of gadolinium‐based contrast agents currently limits direct clinical translation of this approach, although intrapulmonary administration of such agents in humans has previously been reported [25]. The high signal enhancement observed in vivo suggests that lower contrast agent concentrations could be explored, although at the expense of detection sensitivity. More broadly, the use of MRI itself remains a major constraint for application in preterm infants and is currently restricted to a limited number of specialized clinical research centers [26, 27].

Overall, quantitative in vivo MRI assessment of surfactant lung distribution, potentially complemented by ex vivo isolated thorax experiments, represents a unique and powerful approach for evaluating pulmonary delivery of exogenous surfactant. This methodology provides a robust experimental framework for improving administration strategies, protocols, and devices, and may be extended to other intrapulmonary therapeutic interventions.

Conclusion

Contrast‐enhanced in vivo 3D UTE MRI offers a sensitive, precise, and reliable method for visualizing and quantifying pulmonary surfactant distribution, including its peripheral localization, distal penetration, and homogeneity with millimetric precision. This approach leverages meaningful quantitative indices such as PVF, LI, and distality index. Comparison of in vivo imaging with ex vivo isolated thorax models highlighted the impact of physiological processes, such as mucociliary clearance, on surfactant retention. This integrated preclinical framework establishes MRI as a versatile imaging modality for optimizing administration strategies in neonatal care. Beyond surfactant therapy, this methodology may be applied to other inhaled or instilled pulmonary agents, supporting translational research and preclinical evaluation of novel intrapulmonary therapies.
