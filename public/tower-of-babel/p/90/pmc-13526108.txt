Integration of TSPO PET and fluid biomarkers to assess neuroinflammation in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Skye Hsin-Hsien Yeh, Tsung-Hsun Yu, Chia-Ju Liu, Sung-Pin Fan, Pin-Shiuan Chen, Cheng-Hsuan Li, Mei-Fang Cheng, Chin-Hsien Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeMicroglia-mediated neuroinflammation is implicated in Parkinson’s disease (PD), but its relationship with clinical severity remains controversial. Using [18F]FEPPA translocator protein (TSPO) PET with plasma biomarkers, we examined associations among neuroinflammation, glial activation, neuronal injury, and motor and cognitive impairment.MethodsNinety participants (71 patients with PD and 19 age-matched controls) underwent TSPO PET and plasma measurements of soluble triggering receptor expression on myeloid cells-2 (sTREM2), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and neurofilament light chain (NfL). Binding potential relative to the non-displaceable compartment (BPND) was quantified across cortical and subcortical regions. Motor severity was assessed with Movement Disorder Society-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) part III and Hoehn–Yahr stage in the off state. Cognitive performance was evaluated using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). PD dementia (PDD) was diagnosed according to MDS Task Force criteria.ResultsPatients with PD had higher TSPO BPND in the basal ganglia than controls (p = 0.01), most prominent in advanced motor stage (Hoehn–Yahr ≥ 3) and PDD, particularly in the pallidum (p < 0.01). Higher MDS-UPDRS part III scores correlated with increased BPND in the bilateral putamen and frontal and parietal cortices. Patients with PDD had significantly higher pallidal BPND than those without cognitive impairment and controls (both p < 0.001). Higher pallidal BPND was associated with worse global cognitive performance (MMSE, MoCA) and correlated with plasma NfL, GFAP, and sTREM2 levels, especially in patients with advanced disease.ConclusionTSPO PET with plasma biomarkers revealed region-specific neuroinflammation associated with motor and cognitive severity in Parkinson’s disease progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00259-026-07971-7.Keywords: Parkinson’s disease, Neuroinflammation, TSPO, Dementia

Introduction

Neuroinflammation has been increasingly recognized as a key driver in the pathogenesis of Parkinson’s disease (PD) [1]. In addition to neuronal α-synuclein aggregation being a pathognomonic feature of PD, microgliosis and neuroinflammatory processes have been demonstrated in post-mortem brain tissue and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from patients with PD [2]. Beyond its intracellular accumulation, α-synuclein is released into the extracellular space, where it activates surrounding microglia and triggers inflammatory responses [3]. Activated microglia secrete pro-inflammatory mediators that exacerbate neuronal injury and promote further α-synuclein aggregation, establishing a self-perpetuating cycle that drives the progression of PD [4]. In the absence of disease-modifying therapies, neuroinflammation represents a potentially modifiable pathogenic factor. A proof-of-concept trial of myeloperoxidase inhibitor AZD3241 demonstrated reduced microglial activation and partial restoration of immune homeostasis in patients with PD [5]. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting microglia-mediated neuroinflammation to slow PD progression, particularly in patients with clear evidence of microglial activation.

The mitochondrial translocator protein (TSPO), which is predominantly expressed by activated microglia, is a well-established biomarker of neuroinflammation [6]. Positron emission tomography (PET) using radiolabeled TSPO ligands enables in vivo visualization and quantification of microglial activation, providing insights into the temporal and spatial dynamics of neuroinflammation [7]. TSPO ligands for PET can be broadly categorized into first- and second-generation tracers. The first-generation ligand [11C]PK11195, the most widely used TSPO PET tracer, has been employed in several studies to investigate neuroinflammation in PD, though results have been heterogeneous [8–11]. Discrepancies are likely attributable to the inherent limitations of [11C]PK11195, including its low signal-to-noise ratio, high nonspecific binding and limited brain penetration [12]. Second-generation TSPO ligands, such as N-acetyl-N-(2-[18F]fluoroethoxybenzyl)-2-phenoxy-5-pyridinamine ([18F]FEPPA), exhibit higher affinity for TSPO, improved brain penetration, and more favorable pharmacokinetic properties [13]. However, these ligands are influenced by a functional TSPO polymorphism (rs6971) that produces high-, mixed-, and low-affinity binding states, contributing to marked inter-individual variability in PET signals [14]. Although the findings using second-generation ligands have also been variable, meta-analyses have identified increased TSPO binding in the midbrain as a relatively consistent feature in PD [11]. Despite these advances, the precise spatial and temporal patterns of neuroinflammation throughout the course of PD remain incompletely characterized.

Triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) is a microglial surface receptor involved in phagocytosis, lipid sensing, and regulation of neuroinflammatory responses [15]. Its soluble ectodomain, sTREM2, detectable in CSF and blood, has emerged as a biomarker of microglial activation in neurodegenerative disorders. In Alzheimer’s disease (AD), CSF sTREM2 is a sensitive marker of microglial activation [16]. Recent evidence suggests that CSF sTREM2 levels are also elevated in subsets of PD patients and associated with total and phosphorylated tau levels [17]. In contrast, plasma sTREM2 shows substantial inter-individual variability [18–20]. To date, the relationship between blood sTREM2 and motor or cognition severity in PD remains largely unexplored. Given the invasiveness of CSF sampling, validating blood-based biomarkers for longitudinal assessment is critically needed. In this study, we aimed to quantitatively characterize the presence and spatial distribution of neuroinflammation in PD patients using the second-generation TSPO PET imaging and to examine its relationship with peripheral blood markers of microglial and astrocytic activation across the PD spectrum.

Materials and methods

Study participants

Patients with PD were recruited from the movement disorder clinics of National Taiwan University Hospital. PD was diagnosed according to Movement Disorder Society (MDS) clinical diagnostic criteria [21]. Patients with atypical or secondary parkinsonism syndromes, including multiple system atrophy, progressive supranuclear palsy, corticobasal ganglionic degeneration, vascular parkinsonism, and neuroleptic agent-related parkinsonism, were excluded. All participants underwent 3T brain MRI to exclude structural lesions, vascular insults, or normal pressure hydrocephalus that could mimic or contribute to parkinsonism. In addition, all patients with PD underwent dopamine transporter imaging using 99mTc-TRODAT SPECT, which confirmed presynaptic dopaminergic degeneration and further supported the clinical diagnosis. Patients who carried previously reported causative genetic mutations for PD were also excluded [22]. Neurologically unaffected controls were friends or spouses of patients with PD recruited from the same institute. This study was reviewed by the Institutional Research Board Committee (IRB number 202305030MINA). All participants provided written informed consent before being enrolled in the study.

Clinical assessment

The motor symptoms of PD patients were evaluated using the motor subscale of the MDS-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) part III and Hoehn–Yahr (H-Y) staging during the off time, which was defined by the patients being off their PD-related medications for at least 8 h. Global cognitive functions were assessed by the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), and a complete neuropsychological evaluation.

Advanced-stage PD was diagnosed as H-Y stage ≥ 3, and early-stage PD was defined as H-Y stage < 3. PD dementia (PDD) was diagnosed according to the criteria proposed by the MDS task force [23]. Briefly, all patients had a prior diagnosis of PD, followed by an insidious onset and progressive decline in cognitive function. Dementia was defined by impairment across multiple cognitive domains (attention, executive function, visuospatial abilities, and memory) of sufficient severity to interfere with activities of daily living that were not better explained by delirium, major depression, or other neurological or systemic conditions. The temporal criterion (“1-year rule”) was applied to distinguish PDD from dementia with Lewy bodies. All diagnoses were supported by a comprehensive neuropsychological evaluation using standardized test batteries administered by trained neuropsychologists.

TSPO PET imaging

[18F]FEPPA was radiolabeled using an automatic synthesis procedure as described previously [24]. All participants received 185 ± 18.5 MBq of [18F]FEPPA intravenously, followed by a 60-minute dynamic brain PET scan. PET/CT scans were performed in 3D mode using one of the following scanners: Biograph mCT Flow or Biograph Vision (Siemens Healthineers, Knoxville, TN, USA) or Discovery 710 (GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI, USA). Dynamic images were reconstructed into four frames of 30 s, nine frames of 1 min, three frames of 3 min, and eight frames of 5 min. Harmonization of standardized uptake values (SUVs) across the three PET/CT systems was performed using EQ.PET software.

Formal MRI-based partial volume correction was performed in PMOD using the region-based voxel-wise method. T1-weighted MRI images were co-registered to PET images and used to define MRI-based anatomical VOIs, and Partial Volume Correction (PVC)-corrected regional time–activity curves were used for subsequent BPND estimation. For patients with PD, PET imaging was performed in the practical “Off” medication state after dopaminergic medications had been withheld for at least 8 h. This approach was used to minimize the potential confounding effects of symptomatic dopaminergic treatment on both clinical severity assessment and PET-based TSPO quantification.

TSPO PET image analysis

All dynamic TSPO PET data were processed using PMOD software. The dynamic PET image series were realigned for motion correction and co-registered to each subject’s native structural magnetic resonance image (MRI) acquired during the same imaging session. All subsequent analyses were performed in individual MRI space.

Specific TSPO binding on PET images was quantified using the simplified reference tissue model (SRTM) to quantify the binding potential relative to a non-displaceable compartment (BPND) [25]. Because TSPO is ubiquitously expressed and no anatomically defined reference region devoid of specific binding is available, a data-driven reference region was defined using supervised cluster analysis (SCA) [26] rather than assuming minimal binding in a predefined anatomical region, such as the cerebellum.

Specifically, a voxel-wise reference tissue mask was generated using the publicly available SCA implementation described by Kouli et al. [10]. This approach identifies grey matter voxels exhibiting kinetic characteristics consistent with non-specific tracer uptake. The resulting SCA-derived mask was used as the reference region. The reference time–activity curve (TAC) extracted from this mask was then imported into PMOD and applied to subsequent SRTM modeling.

Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined on each subject’s native MR image based on the anatomical relevance to PD pathology and neuroinflammation. ROI masks were transferred to PET space for extraction of regional TACs. Regional BPND values were calculated by applying SRTM in PMOD using the SCA-derived reference TAC. To minimize partial volume effects and CSF contamination, regional TACs were corrected using grey and white matter probability maps derived from individual MRI segmentation and smoothed to PET spatial resolution.

Genotyping of the TSPO rs6971 variant

Genomic DNA was extracted from 10 mL of venous blood. The TSPO rs6971 polymorphism was genotyped using a TaqMan SNP Genotyping Assay (Applied Biosystems, P/N 4351379; Assay ID C_2512465_20; SNP ID rs6971) as described previously [27], and participants were classified as high-affinity, mixed-affinity, or low-affinity binders.

Measurement of plasma sTREM2, GFAP, and NfL

Plasma samples were collected within 3 months of the TSPO PET scan. Fasting venous blood (10 mL) was sampled and centrifuged (2500 g for 15 min) within 1 h after collection. The plasma aliquots were then stored at − 80 °C according to standardized procedures until testing. All plasma biomarkers, including sTREM2, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and neurofilament light chain (NfL), were quantified using the ultrasensitive Simoa technology (Quanterix, Billerica, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All measurements were performed in duplicate by blinded board-certified technicians, and average concentrations were calculated.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean values and standard errors of the mean, whereas categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. The characteristics were compared by applying the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, including age and treatment duration, regardless of whether the distribution was normal. Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test was applied for categorical variables. Group differences in regional TSPO BPND were assessed using one-way ANOVA as appropriate, with post hoc correction for multiple comparisons. Associations between regional TSPO BPND and clinical symptom severity scores or plasma biomarkers were evaluated using Pearson correlation analyses depending on the data distribution. Linear regression adjusted for the genotype of rs6971 polymorphism was applied where indicated. Hemispheric analyses were performed relative to the clinically more-affected side. Multiple comparisons were controlled using false discovery rate or Bonferroni correction. Significance was set at p < 0.05. GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA) was used for statistical analyses.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 90 participants, including 71 patients with PD (mean age: 70.35 ± 9.63 years) and 19 neurologically normal controls (mean age: 69.31 ± 5.51 years), were included in this study (Table 1). The PD and control groups were comparable in age, education, and rs6971 binding affinity distribution. Our cohort contained no low-affinity binders; the vast majority were high-affinity binders, with no significant difference between groups (PD: 97.2% vs. controls: 94.7%, p = 0.59).

Forty patients with PD were classified as having advanced motor symptom severity (H-Y stage ≥ 3), and the remaining patients were characterized as early-stage PD. For cognitive stratification, 36 patients were classified as PD with normal cognition (PD-NC) and 35 as PDD. As expected, patients with advanced-stage PD or PDD showed greater motor severity and poorer cognitive performance than those with early-stage PD or PD-NC (all p < 0.01; Table 1).

Region-specific changes in TSPO PET and associations with clinical severity

Patients with PD had increased TSPO BPND in basal ganglia, particularly the pallidum, compared to controls (p = 0.01; Table 1). Representative axial and sagittal images from TSPO PET revealed group-dependent differences across cortical and subcortical structures (Fig. 1).

Visual comparison of TSPO PET images from controls and patients with PD. Representative [18F]FEPPA BPND parametric images were derived from the full 0–60 min dynamic acquisition. The PD example shown was obtained from a patient with Hoehn–Yahr stage 4

To validate the SCA-derived reference tissue used for BPND estimation, reference-region volume and kinetics were compared between PD patients and controls. The SCA-derived reference clusters showed comparable spatial distribution, volume and overall TAC shape across groups, without increased reference uptake in PD. Peak SUV, late SUV, and area under the curve (AUC) in the SCA-derived reference cluster were lower in PD than in controls, with similar findings in the cerebellar comparison (Table S1 and Supplementary Figs. S1-S2), arguing against global TSPO-related reference-region contamination.

To examine whether greater disease severity in PD is associated with increased neuroinflammation, we compared regional TSPO BPND among patients with PD stratified by motor and cognitive symptom severity. With respect to motor function, patients with advanced-stage PD exhibited significantly higher TSPO BPND in subcortical regions, including the pallidum (p < 0.01) and putamen (p = 0.03), compared with those with early-stage PD; these values were also higher than those in controls (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. S3A). A more modest elevation in amygdala TSPO BPND was also observed in advanced-stage PD compared with controls (p = 0.03). Visual inspection further revealed the most apparent group differences in basal ganglia regions, particularly the putamen, pallidum, and caudate nucleus, as well as the amygdala (Supplementary Fig. S2A), highlighting preferential neuroinflammatory involvement of the basal ganglia and limbic regions in PD, especially at more advanced stages. Beyond group differences, regional TSPO BPND was associated with motor symptom severity (MDS-UPDRS part III), particularly within the striatal circuitry. In the putamen, higher MDS-UPDRS part III scores were significantly associated with increased TSPO binding in both the left (β = 0.00329, p < 0.001) and right hemispheres (β = 0.00290, p = 0.0019) (Fig. 3). Trend-level associations were observed in other subcortical regions, including the pallidum (p = 0.076) and midbrain (p = 0.072). At the cortical level, motor severity was also associated with TSPO binding in the frontal (p = 0.047) and parietal cortices (p < 0.001). Given that motor symptoms in PD are often asymmetric, particularly in the early and less severe stages, we performed an additional laterality-based analysis according to the clinically dominant motor side. This analysis showed modest side-dependent variation in selected motor-related regions, including the pallidum, striatum, putamen, and midbrain. However, we did not observe a consistent pattern of increased contralateral [18F]FEPPA BPND relative to the clinically more affected side (Supplementary Fig. S4A–B).

These findings suggest that motor symptom–related TSPO alterations are most prominent in the putamen, with secondary cortical involvement, but without a clear laterality-dependent pattern. This may indicate that neuroinflammatory changes in PD are relatively bilateral or symmetric at the stage captured in our cohort, rather than being strictly aligned with clinical motor asymmetry.

With respect to cognitive function, patients with PDD showed significantly higher TSPO BPND in the pallidum compared with other groups (all p < 0.001; Fig. 4). Beyond subcortical regions, parietal TSPO BPND was higher in PDD than in PD-NC (p = 0.04), whereas hippocampal BPND was higher in PD-NC than in PDD (p = 0.01). Other cortical regions showed smaller group differences. Representative TSPO BPND maps further illustrate distinct spatial patterns across cognitive subgroups (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Together, these findings suggest that progression to dementia in PD is associated with an expanded neuroinflammatory signature involving both the basal ganglia and higher-order cortical systems. Beyond group differences, regression analyses showed that higher pallidal TSPO binding correlated with lower global cognitive scores (MMSE: β = −0.0070, p = 0.019; MoCA: β = −0.0048, p = 0.041) (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S5). Lower cognitive scores were also associated with increased TSPO BPND in the temporal and parietal cortices and limbic–striatal regions, including the nucleus accumbens. These patterns were consistent across MMSE and MoCA measures, indicating a robust association between TSPO binding and cognitive impairment. Together, these findings suggest that cognitive variability in PD involves a distributed cortico–striato–pallidal network. Combined with the motor-related TSPO changes observed predominantly in the putamen, the results support distinct neuroinflammatory patterns underlying motor and cognitive progression in PD.

Correlations between TSPO PET signals and plasma markers

We then examined correlations between regional brain TSPO BPND and the plasma levels of sTREM2, GFAP, and NfL in both controls and patients with PD. Plasma levels of NfL and GFAP were significantly higher in PD patients than in controls (NfL, p < 0.001; GFAP, p = 0.003), whereas systemic sTREM2 levels were only modestly elevated in PD (p = 0.05, Table 1). Notably, significant positive correlations were observed between all three plasma markers and TSPO BPND in the pallidum (all p < 0.001, Fig. 6). Plasma sTREM2 levels positively correlated with TSPO BPND in the left putamen (r = 0.31, p < 0.001) and right caudate nucleus (r = 0.30, p < 0.001), whereas correlations with cortical TSPO BPND were generally weak or non-significant. These findings suggest that plasma markers of neuronal injury (NfL), astrocytic activation (GFAP), and microglial activation (sTREM2) preferentially reflect neuroinflammation within deep brain nuclei in PD.

Discussion

In this integrated study, we observed increased TSPO binding in the pallidum and putamen of patients with PD compared with controls, indicating enhanced microglia-related neuroinflammation in deep nuclei. Regional TSPO BPND showed domain-specific clinical associations: motor severity correlated most strongly with increased TSPO binding in the bilateral putamen, whereas cognitive impairment was linked to elevated binding within a distributed pallido–temporo–parietal network, including the pallidum, temporal and parietal cortices, and nucleus accumbens. These patterns suggest partially dissociable neuroinflammatory substrates underlying motor and cognitive progression in PD. In addition, pallidal TSPO binding correlated with plasma NfL, GFAP, and sTREM2, supporting a link between TSPO PET signals and peripheral markers of neuroaxonal injury and glial activation. Together, these findings support the biological relevance of TSPO PET as an in vivo index of neuroinflammation in PD and highlight its potential integration with fluid biomarkers in future longitudinal studies.

Our findings align with prior TSPO PET studies reporting increased tracer binding in PD [11]. A meta-analysis identified elevated TSPO binding in the midbrain, putamen, and parietal–occipital cortices compared with controls [11]. We additionally observed increased TSPO BPND in the pallidum, consistent with earlier studies using [11C]PK11195 that also reported elevated pallidal binding in PD [28]. Post-mortem studies likewise demonstrate reactive microgliosis in the substantia nigra, pallidum, and putamen, often colocalizing with α-synuclein pathology [29]. Given that the pallidum is a key output hub of the basal ganglia–thalamo–cortical motor circuit [30], increased pallidal TSPO binding may reflect microglial activation within a metabolically stressed motor network node. Consistent with this, motor severity was most strongly associated with TSPO binding in the putamen, with additional trend-level effects in the pallidum and midbrain and secondary involvement of frontal–parietal regions. Similar associations between basal ganglia TSPO uptake and motor severity (MDS-UPDRS part III) have also been reported using 11C-PBR28 [31].

Beyond motor function, poorer global cognition was associated with increased TSPO binding in the pallidum, temporal and parietal cortices, and nucleus accumbens, indicating involvement of a distributed cortico–striato–pallidal neuroinflammatory network. Prior studies similarly report links between neuroinflammation and cognitive decline in PD, particularly in PDD [10, 14], supporting a role for microglial activation in network-level dysfunction. The temporal and parietal cortices are key posterior association regions supporting memory, visuospatial processing, and attention and are consistently affected in PDD [32]. The nucleus accumbens and pallidum serve as subcortical hubs connecting fronto-striatal and limbic circuits involved in motivation and executive control [33, 34]. Increased TSPO binding in these regions may therefore reflect neuroinflammation within cortical–striatal–limbic circuits contributing to cognitive impairment in PD [10]. These findings suggest that neuroinflammation extends beyond motor pathways to distributed cognitive networks. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether regional TSPO PET abnormalities predict future cognitive decline and dementia risk in PD.

We also observed that TSPO binding in the pallidum and putamen positively correlated with plasma sTREM2 levels, while pallidal TSPO BPND additionally correlated with plasma GFAP and NfL. These findings support a link between central neuroinflammation and peripheral markers of glial activation and neuroaxonal injury in PD. Consistent with this concept, prior studies have reported associations between TSPO PET signals and peripheral inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-6) in PD [10]. Direct studies combining TSPO PET with sTREM2 measurements in PD remain limited. However, multimodal investigations in AD integrating TSPO PET, amyloid and tau PET, and fluid biomarkers have shown that microglial activation ([11C]PBR28 binding) closely corresponds with CSF and plasma markers such as sTREM2 and GFAP [35]. To our knowledge, prior TSPO PET studies in PD have not concurrently measured plasma sTREM2 in the same individuals. By integrating TSPO PET with plasma biomarkers, our findings suggest that regional neuroinflammatory burden in the pallidum and putamen is partially reflected by circulating sTREM2 levels, particularly in advanced-stage PD. Although α-synuclein has not been demonstrated to directly bind TREM2 [36], experimental evidence suggests functional interactions between TREM2 signaling and α-synuclein pathology [37, 38]. The observed associations may also reflect contributions from co-pathologies such as amyloid-β and tau in advanced PD or PDD. Future studies integrating α-synuclein PET with TSPO PET and fluid biomarkers will be important to clarify the spatial and temporal relationships between protein aggregation and neuroinflammation during PD progression [39]. We further observed that pallidal TSPO uptake correlated with higher plasma GFAP and NfL levels, suggesting that regional microglial activation within the basal ganglia is linked to astroglial reactivity and neuroaxonal injury. This pattern is consistent with multimodal studies in AD showing coordinated microglial–astrocytic responses reflected by peripheral GFAP levels [35]. The association with NfL also aligns with prior PET–fluid biomarker studies in atypical parkinsonian syndromes and corticobasal degeneration, where TSPO PET signals correlate with markers of axonal injury [40]. Together, these findings support the concept that basal ganglia microglial activation reflects a broader neuroinflammatory cascade involving astrocyte activation and neurodegeneration. Our results extend previous multimodal evidence by demonstrating that pallidal microglial activity is coupled with systemic biomarkers of glial activation and neuroaxonal damage, reinforcing the relevance of basal ganglia–centered neuroinflammation in PD pathophysiology.

Our study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the temporal relationship between neuroinflammation and disease progression. Longitudinal studies integrating TSPO PET, clinical trajectories, and fluid biomarkers will be necessary to determine whether early microglial activation predicts subsequent motor or cognitive decline. Second, we did not assess α-synuclein, tau, or amyloid pathology concurrently with TSPO PET, limiting insight into the proteinopathies that may trigger microglial activation. Future multimodal imaging incorporating tracers for these pathologies will help clarify their interaction with neuroinflammation in PD. Third, CSF biomarkers and inflammatory cytokine profiling were not evaluated, restricting direct comparison between central and peripheral inflammatory processes. Finally, we acknowledge the imbalance in cohort size (71 PD patients vs. 19 controls) as a limitation of this study. This disparity reflects our primary focus on characterizing the clinical and biological heterogeneity within the PD group. While the smaller control group size may limit the statistical power for some between-group comparisons, the robustness of our main findings, particularly the correlation between TSPO PET binding and cognitive scores, remains significant after adjusting for relevant covariates. Nevertheless, future studies with larger, more balanced control cohorts are warranted to validate these results.

In conclusion, we demonstrate increased TSPO binding in deep brain nuclei in PD, indicating enhanced microglia-related neuroinflammation. Region-specific inflammatory patterns were associated with motor and cognitive symptom severity. Pallidal TSPO binding also correlated with plasma NfL, GFAP, and sTREM2, particularly in advanced disease, suggesting that peripheral biomarkers reflect neuroinflammation within deep nuclei during PD progression. These findings support a role for central neuroinflammation in PD and highlight TSPO PET as a potential imaging marker for stratifying clinical heterogeneity and progression risk.
