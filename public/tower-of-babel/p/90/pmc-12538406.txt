Left atrial appendage thrombi despite oral anticoagulation in transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy patients undergoing electrical cardioversion for atrial fibrillation or − flutter

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy (ATTR-CM) is an infiltrative, progressive disorder characterized by the deposition of abnormal transthyretin protein fibrils within the myocardium, leading to restrictive cardiomyopathy [1] and a significant increase in morbidity and mortality [2]. ATTR-CM can be classified into two types: wild-type ATTR-CM, which develops in older, predominantly male individuals, and variant ATTR-CM, which is caused by a mutation in the transthyretin gene [1]. Among the challenges in managing ATTR-CM is the presence of cardiac arrhythmias, particularly atrial fibrillation (AF) and atrial flutter (AFL), which occur in up to 70 % of patients [[3], [4], [5]] and significantly increase the disease burden [6]. AF and AFL not only exacerbate symptoms [7] but also reduce patients’ quality of life and increase the risk of heart failure progression [8], adverse in-hospital outcomes [9], thromboembolic events [10], and mortality [11].

Patients with ATTR-CM require a slightly higher heart rate to maintain cardiac output. Still, they poorly tolerate rapid heart rates, e.g., in AF, due to the already stiffened ventricle by amyloid deposits. Conversely, with a higher heart rate, diastolic filling time shortens, and left ventricular output decreases, resulting in worsening symptoms. A prior study indicated that 43 % of wild-type and 33 % of variant ATTR-CM patients who were initially AF-free develop AF during the disease course [12], further complicating management. Importantly, maintaining sinus rhythm has been linked to reduced mortality in patients with ATTR-CM [11].

The pathophysiology of AF in ATTR-CM is characterized by amyloid deposition in the left atrium and atrial dilation due to left ventricular diastolic dysfunction. Therefore, even in the absence of other cardiovascular conditions, patients with cardiac amyloidosis (CA) exhibit impaired contractile function in the left atrium and its appendage compared to control groups [5,13].

Direct current cardioversion (DCCV) is a commonly employed intervention to restore sinus rhythm and rapidly improve patient symptoms. However, amyloid deposition in the myocardium poses unique challenges to successful DCCV, including altered myocardial conduction disturbances [6], increased myocardial stiffness [14], and impaired contractility [15]. Thus, the use of DCCV has been reported to be associated with highly variable success and recurrence rates in previous investigations [3,12,16,17]. In addition, a high prevalence of left atrial appendage thrombi (LAAT), even despite oral anticoagulation, with increased risk of stroke and systemic embolism has been observed in patients with ATTR-CM in prior studies [18,19], often leading to the cancellation of DCCV [18,20]. Imaging modalities, such as transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) or cardiac computed tomography (CT), are recommended for the detection of LAAT [21]. However, a recent analysis by Itzhaki Ben Zadok et al. [17] challenged this view as they reported an absence of thromboembolic events in a relatively large patient cohort who did not receive LAAT exclusion prior to DCCV.

To date, only a few small single-center studies have reported on the frequency of LAAT and DCCV cancellation rates due to LAAT formation. However, data regarding LAAT prevalence are still scarce. As light-chain amyloidosis patients were not excluded in prior studies, data regarding LAAT prevalence in an isolated ATTR-CM cohort treated with disease-modifying therapeutics specifically are sparse. Therefore, to address this gap, we report our experience with planned DCCV in ATTR-CM patients. We sought to determine DCCV cancellation rates due to LAAT formation and success rates in patients with ATTR-CM undergoing DCCV due to AF/AFL.

Methods

Setting

The present analysis was performed within the scope of a prospective cardiac amyloidosis (CA) registry at the Medical University of Vienna, Department of Internal Medicine II, Division of Cardiology, which features a dedicated CA outpatient clinic. The study was approved by the Medical University of Vienna ethics committee (#1079/2023) and implemented in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent prior to enrolment in the registry.

Eligibility criteria

For inclusion in the present analysis, consecutive patients presenting to the CA outpatient clinic between November 2014 and December 2023 were required to fulfill the following inclusion criteria: (I) definite diagnosis of ATTR-CM, either utilizing endomyocardial biopsy or the non-invasive diagnostic algorithm first described by Gillmore et al. [22], (II) evidence of AF or AFL, (III) at least one DCCV procedure, (IV) willingness to undergo structured follow-up in-clinic visits every six months.

Patients were excluded from the analysis due to (I) evidence of concomitant amyloid light chain amyloidosis and (II) insufficient documentation of the DCCV procedure.

Direct current electrical cardioversion

DCCV was performed in patients deemed eligible as per the judgment of a board-certified cardiologist. Patients with a history of long-standing persistent AF or AFL were assessed individually for symptom burden and left atrial diameter and patients were referred to undergo DCCV if deemed appropriate. DCCV was carried out in compliance with the contemporary guidelines on the management of AF by the European Society of Cardiology [23].

Oral anticoagulation

Patients diagnosed with AF or AFL were prescribed direct oral anticoagulants in appropriate dosages irrespective of the CHA2DS2-VA score, as recommended by current guidelines [24]. In patients who had previously been prescribed vitamin K antagonists, a transition to direct oral anticoagulants was offered.

Exclusion of left atrial appendage thrombus formation

As recommended by El-Am et al. [16], patients underwent either TEE or cardiac CT prior to DCCV. Patient follow-up

Structured follow-up was implemented in compliance with Garcia-Pavia et al.’s expert consensus statement on monitoring ATTR-CM [25] and the guidelines of the Austrian social security governing body for the approval of specific ATTR-CM therapy [26].

Accordingly, patients underwent extensive in-clinic evaluation every six months, which included a physical examination, an electrocardiogram, a complete blood count, and blood chemistry, including cardiac biomarkers. Additional in-clinic visits were scheduled whenever the patient noted signs and symptoms of disease progression.

Transthoracic echocardiography was performed every 12 months. Experienced operators on high-end equipment acquired images (GE Vivid E95 and Vivid E9, GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL, USA) according to current recommendations [27]. Post-hoc analysis was conducted utilizing dedicated software (EchoPAC Clinical Workstation Software, GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL, USA).

Data on mortality and cardiac hospitalizations were collected from clinical records and by regular queries to the national death registry (Statistics Austria) [28]. Extracellular volume was assessed using cardiac magnetic resonance imaging as previously described [29].

Statistics

Categorial data is presented as counts and percentages, while continuous data is presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR). Associations between clinical, laboratory, and echocardiographic parameters and LAAT formation are assessed using linear regression analysis. Statistical significance was assumed with two-sided P-values of < 0.05 and confidence intervals of 95 %. IBM SPSS Statistics 29 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) was utilized for data analyses.

Results

A total of 62 (1.35 per patient) DCCVs in 46 ATTR-CM patients were included in the analysis. On average, patients were 77.2 ± 6.5 years old and predominantly male (89.1 %). DCCV was scheduled for AF in 42 (67.7 %) and for AFL in 11 (17.7 %) cases. The remaining 9 (14.5 %) procedures were performed for atrial tachyarrhythmia. In 5 patients (10.9 %), a transthyretin variant was causative of the myocardial disease. Symptoms of heart failure defined as a New York Heart Association stage (NYHA) stage ≥ 2 were present in 35 (76.1 %) patients. Utilizing the ATTR-CM staging system by Gillmore et al.[30], an early disease stage was present in 15 (41.7 %) patients, while 15 (41.7 %) and 6 (16.7 %) patients were in an intermediate or advanced disease stage, respectively. The median CHA2DS2-VA score was 4 [range: 2–7]. The most frequently reported comorbidities were arterial hypertension (n = 29, 63.0 %), followed by chronic kidney disease (n = 28, 60.9 %), significant coronary artery disease (n = 13, 28.3 %) and diabetes mellitus (n = 9, 19.6 %). On transthoracic echocardiography, the median left atrial volume index was 42.5 cm3/m2 (IQR 35.8–52.5), and left ventricular function was impaired with a median global longitudinal strain of −9.8 % (IQR −6.4 % – -13.0 %). Amyloid load estimated utilizing the extracellular volume in cardiac magnetic resonance imaging[31] was high with a median of 53 % (IQR 45 %–60 %). A total of 62 DCCV procedures were included in the analysis, and 39 (62.9 %) were preceded by either TEE or cardiac CT for the exclusion of LAAT formation. In total, 10 (16.1 %) procedures were aborted either due to spontaneous conversion to sinus rhythm (n = 2, 3.2 % of total) or LAAT formation (n = 8, 20.5 % of patients with LAAT exclusion), including definite LAAT (n = 6; 15.4 % of patients with LAAT exclusion) or suspected LAAT (n = 2; 5.1 % of patients with LAAT exclusion). Of these 8 patients, 5 had been receiving oral anticoagulants in adequate dosage for at least three weeks prior to the procedure, including 4 patients with definite and 1 with suspected LAAT formation (Fig. 2). In these patients, dabigatran (n = 1), apixaban (n = 3), edoxaban (n = 3), and phenprocoumon (n = 1) were utilized for oral anticoagulation. In singular linear regression analysis, we did not find any significant association between the CHA2DS2-VA score, levels of C-reactive protein, N-terminal prohormone of brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate, thrombocyte counts, the use of acetylsalicylic acid, left atrial volume, extracellular volume, and the presence of LAAT.

In 52 executed DCCVs, the immediate success was observed in 48 procedures (92.3 % of executed), and 3-month sinus rhythm control was achieved in 26 (61.9 % of executed) procedures. In 6 procedures (11.5 % of executed), peri- or postprocedural complications were documented and included AFL, supraventricular extrasystoles, atrioventricular block, and intermittent short-term asystole occurring four hours following DCCV in 1 patient. Notably, no thromboembolic or ischemic events occurred in association with DCCV procedures. However, one patient who had not undergone LAAT exclusion utilizing either TEE or CT prior to DCCV suffered a cerebral stroke two months following the DCCV procedure despite continuous anticoagulation. Within the observation period of 21.4 months (IQR 10.5–37.0), 7 (15.2 %) patients died after a median of 10.7 (7.0–22.7) months. Cardiac hospitalization was recorded in 39 (84.8 %) patients after a median of 4.2 (1.6–10.0) months. There was no significant association of disease-specific therapy or NT-proBNP levels with DCCV success or LAAT formation.

Discussion

In this single-center observational study of patients with wild-type and variant ATTR-CM, we explored the outcomes of DCCV for AF/AFL at our tertiary care amyloidosis center. The study highlighted two main findings: 1) a high cancellation rate of DCCV primarily due to definite or suspected LAAT formation (20.5 % of procedures with LAAT exclusion) and 2) a high immediate success rate in restoring sinus rhythm (92.3 % of procedures). Despite the high immediate success, 3-month sinus rhythm control was achieved in only 61.9 % of executed procedures. This study fills a gap in the existing literature by being the first national investigation at a tertiary university center focusing exclusively on an ATTR-CM cohort in the modern era. We also report the first data on LAAT formation in ATTR-CM patients who receive disease-specific medication.

Prevalence of left atrial appendage thrombus in cardiac amyloidosis

While DCCV for the termination of AF/AFL is generally considered safe, accumulating research indicates a high prevalence of LAAT. Our study revealed that 20.5 % of planned DCCVs with prior LAAT exclusion had to be cancelled due to either definite or suspected LAAT, despite adequate anticoagulation in 5 cases for at least three weeks prior to the procedure as recommended by current guidelines [23]. These findings align with previous investigations in the field reporting a LAAT prevalence of 14 %–33 % in CA patients [16,18,32,33]. LAAT prevalence in CA patients is notably higher than in the general anticoagulated AF population, where it is reported at approximately 7–8 % [34,35].

In a study of 30 ATTR-CM patients, 30 % had LAAT on TEE, varying prevalence by AF type: 23 % in paroxysmal AF, 29 % in persistent AF, and 47 % in long-standing persistent AF (p = 0.16) [36]. Additional reports have highlighted the prevalence of intracardiac thrombi also in patients with sinus rhythm [37]. Importantly, our study revealed that despite adequate anticoagulation with oral anticoagulants, in 5 (12.8 %) procedures, definitive LAAT formation still occurred, albeit at a lower prevalence than in previous studies involving different cardiac amyloidosis types [16].

Current guidelines of the European Society of Cardiology recommend anticoagulation irrespective of the CHA2DS2-VA score in this population (level 1B recommendation) [24], a recommendation supported by our study and expert opinion in the field [38], advocating for prompt initiation, close monitoring, and long-term continuation of therapeutic anticoagulation. Given the high risk of LAAT formation in CA, pre-procedural imaging using TEE or cardiac CT is considered essential. Even in patients receiving adequate oral anticoagulation, LAAT exclusion is recommended according to the most recent expert consensus document on managing CA [39] and supported by various studies [16,18,20,33,40]. One study has demonstrated the absence of thromboembolic events following DCCV in many ATTR-CM patients who did not receive LAAT exclusion prior to DCCV [17]. This has sparked a discussion of whether LAAT exclusion in adequately anticoagulated patients is required. In our cohort, one in five patients did not receive sufficient doses of oral anticoagulants, most commonly due to prescription of a reduced dosage when the full dosage would have been required, typically because of bleeding risk, complications or the discretion of external physicians.

While disease-specific medication is known to delay adverse structural changes in patients with ATTR-CM [[41], [42], [43], [44]], our study was underpowered to detect a significant effect of specific therapeutics on LAAT formation.

Notably, our study included specifically only ATTR-CM patients. Light chain amyloidosis patients may present a different thromboembolic risk due to plasma cell dyscrasia, associated systemic inflammation, and prothrombotic medication [45,46].

Success and recurrence rates

DCCV remains an effective intervention for restoring sinus rhythm in patients with ATTR-CM and AF/AFL, as evidenced by our study's short-term success rate of 92.3 %. However, the three-month recurrence rate of AF/AFL was substantial, with only 26 (61.9 %) DCCVs with documented rhythm control after 3 months. Compared to a general atrial fibrillation population undergoing DCCV, this seems to be somewhat lower as a three-months AF recurrence rate of 29 %, and a one-year recurrence rate of > 40 % have been reported in previous studies [47,48]. This highlights the challenges in the long-term maintenance of sinus rhythm in this population. Previous studies have reported varying recurrence rates in CA patients post-DCCV, ranging from 61 % at 30 days to 48 %–80 % at one year [11,16,49]. This underscores the importance of long-term monitoring and optimizing treatment strategies to prevent arrhythmia recurrence and mitigate disease progression.

The stage of CA has been found to impact the effectiveness of DCCV, with earlier intervention leading to better outcomes. For instance, Donnellan et al. reported that 90 % of patients with stage 1 ATTR-CM, according to Gillmore et al.’s scoring system [30], remained free of recurrent arrhythmia at 30 days post-DCCV, in contrast to only 33 % of those with stage 3 disease [11]. Given the challenges associated with the long-term management of AF/AFL in ATTR-CM, alternative treatment modalities such as catheter ablation should be explored further.

Safety and complications

Despite its good safety profile, DCCV is not entirely without risks. Peri- or postprocedural complications, including atrioventricular block and short-term asystole, occurred in 7 (13.5 %) of our patients.

The study by El-Am et al. [16] highlighted an up to seven-fold higher complication rate among CA patients, including those with ATTR-CM and light chain amyloidosis, undergoing DCCV compared to non-CA patients. Specifically, 14 % of CA patients undergoing DCCV experienced complications compared to only 2 % in the control group (p = 0.007). Given these findings, it is crucial to approach DCCV procedures with caution, ensuring thorough patient evaluation and continuous monitoring to enhance safety and minimize risks.

Stroke risk

The study found no periprocedural stroke events during DCCV despite high LAAT prevalence, aligning with Dale et al.'s observations, who also reported no strokes within 30 days post-DCCV in their study of ATTR-CM patients [50]. The absence of these events in our and Dale et al.'s studies is notable, especially given the high risk of thromboembolic or ischemic events reported in previous literature [51,52]. However, one patient experienced a stroke two months post-procedure, highlighting the importance of careful patient selection and thorough pre-procedural imaging, including LAAT screening, to effectively minimize stroke risk. Itzhaki Ben Zadok et al. [17] have suggested that thromboembolism following DCCV may be caused primarily by thrombi formed after DCCV, not pre-existing thrombi. Therefore, anticoagulation, but not LAAT exclusion, would protect from thromboembolic events.

Recommendations for future research

Current recommendations regarding LAAT exclusion are derived from expert consensus and prevalence studies demonstrating a considerable incidence of LAAT in CA patients. However, there is currently no evidence that either TEE or CT prior to DCCV reduces thromboembolic events. Future research should, therefore, focus on providing solid evidence for or against current practice.

Study limitations

This study has some potential limitations that warrant consideration. As a major referral center for amyloidosis, there might be a selection bias towards more complex cases or those requiring direct cardioversion at our center. Furthermore, despite our center’s expertise in amyloidosis care, the size of the ATTR-CM patient cohort undergoing DCCV collected over nine years was relatively small, which might impact the generalizability of our findings. The assessment of AF/AFL recurrence relied on available electrocardiograms, Holter monitoring, or device records, possibly leading to an underestimation of the recurrence rates of the study patients. The relatively short three-month observation period for recurrence rates further limits our ability to assess the long-term success and durability of DCCV. Last, using TEE or cardiac CT to rule out LAAT formation was performed only at the treating physicians’ discretion. This resulted in some patients not undergoing LAAT exclusion, which may have excluded some frail patients, perhaps at even higher risk of LAAT. This consideration could have influenced cancellation rates and procedural outcomes. However, due to the high LAAT rate in the present study, we have since changed our standard procedure to require LAAT exclusion for every patient.

Conclusion

Despite modern oral anticoagulation strategies, LAAT was highly prevalent, with an observed occurrence rate of definitive LAAT of 15.4 % in our cohort of ATTR-CM patients scheduled to undergo DCCV. Electrical cardioversion restored sinus rhythm in most patients in the short term. While DCCV was generally safe and well-tolerated, its efficacy in maintaining sinus rhythm was not sustained over the long term. These findings underscore the challenges in managing AF/AFL in ATTR-CM patients and emphasize the need for further research to enhance treatment strategies and outcomes. These findings point out the use of TEE or CT prior to DCCV to exclude LAAT in ATTR-CM patients, even with oral anticoagulation. LAAT exclusion should be performed before DCCV, even in patients receiving adequate doses of anticoagulants.
