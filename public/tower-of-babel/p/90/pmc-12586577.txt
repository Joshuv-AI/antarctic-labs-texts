Accurate AQI forecasting in a high-altitude city using a simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM hybrid model: a case study of Lhasa, Tibet
Authors: Fangjing Xiao, Xiaomei Cui, Guibin Jiang, Duo Bu, Qiangying Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractForecasting of the Air Quality Index (AQI) in high-altitude cities is significantly challenged by nonlinear temporal dynamics, particularly due to complex interactions between atmospheric physics and pollutant transport. A hybrid forecasting model integrating a simulated Complex-Valued Optical Convolution Accelerator (CVOCA) with a Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) network is presented to improve prediction accuracy and computational efficiency. The simulated CVOCA module enables high-speed, complex-valued convolution to extract amplitude and phase features from high-dimensional AQI sequences, while the BiLSTM component captures bidirectional temporal dependencies. Performance was evaluated using daily AQI data from Lhasa (2014–2024, 4018 samples). The hybrid model achieved RMSE = 1.60, MAE = 0.93, MAPE = 1.60%, R = 0.997, and R2 = 0.996, outperforming standalone LSTM and BiLSTM models. The proposed model captures complex AQI dynamics in plateau environments and provides a scalable solution for real-time forecasting and environmental decision support.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22474-5.Keywords: AQI forecasting, CVOCA-BiLSTM hybrid model, Exceptional predictive performance, TibetSubject terms: Atmospheric science, Environmental sciences

Introduction

In recent years, the escalating frequency and intensity of large-scale wildfires worldwide have become a critical driver of atmospheric pollution, severely undermining urban environmental sustainability and endangering public health1,2. Exceedance of hazardous Air Quality Index (AQI) thresholds constitutes a significant threat to ecosystem stability3.

The Tibetan Plateau, often called the “Third Pole” performs an indispensable function in both regional and global sustainability. Nonetheless, polar and high-altitude regions constitute sensitive interfaces within the Earth’s climate system. Residents of these regions face heightened risks from amplified climate variability and increased pollutant transport4. The Tibetan Plateau’s highly variable topography causes dramatic swings in Planetary Boundary-Layer Height (PBLH), directly affecting near-surface pollutant dispersion and deposition. Studies have indicated that warming over the Tibetan Plateau can enhance vertical mixing and may influence secondary aerosol formation processes, thereby contributing to increased complexity and variability in AQI time series5. Compared with urban plains, AQI monitoring stations across the Tibetan Plateau are sparse and unevenly distributed, yielding input data with poor spatial continuity and representativeness6. Moreover, the region’s dry climate and limited pollution sources produce relatively small AQI fluctuations and overly smooth time series, reducing model sensitivity and robustness to subtle changes7. For instance, over the past decade, Lhasa’s AQI exhibited a relative standard deviation (RSD) of 30.86% and a standard deviation (SD) of 17.81, which are substantially lower than those of Beijing (RSD: 65.09%, SD: 60.34) and Shanghai (RSD: 45.32%, SD: 33.22), highlighting the markedly weaker variability in Lhasa’s air quality data. Finally, the proportions of secondary aerosols versus primary emissions vary sharply with humidity and temperature, creating complex spatiotemporal interactions that conventional models struggle to capture8. Collectively, these factors—including the plateau’s unique high-altitude boundary-layer dynamics, sparse and smooth monitoring data, and complex transboundary transport—substantially elevate the challenge of AQI forecasting on the Tibetan Plateau.

AQI forecasting methods are generally classified into two primary categories: process-driven models and data-driven models9. Process-driven models are based on the principles of atmospheric physics and chemical mechanisms. These models simulate the generation, dispersion, transport, and deposition of pollutants to predict air quality. Representative examples include chemical transport models such as the Community Multiscale Air Quality (CMAQ) model and the Weather Research and Forecasting model coupled with chemistry (WRF-Chem)10,11. These models require numerous input parameters, including meteorological fields, emission inventories, and topographic data. While process-driven models provide strong interpretability and are grounded in physical principles, they are computationally demanding and highly sensitive to the quality of initial input data, making their predictive accuracy susceptible to various uncertainties. In contrast, data-driven models focus on learning statistical patterns and latent relationships from historical datasets, without explicitly modeling the physical transport mechanisms of pollutants. This category encompasses traditional statistical methods (e.g., ARMA, SVR), machine learning algorithms (e.g., Random Forest, XGBoost), and deep learning architectures (e.g., LSTM, CNN, Transformer). Data-driven models are typically more flexible and capable of achieving higher predictive accuracy, particularly when modeling nonlinear and highly dynamic AQI patterns12–14. However, pure temporal deep learning models without feature enhancement tend to overfit “flat” patterns when data is sparse and low in noise, while also incurring high computational costs that hinder real-time forecasting15. Ensemble machine learning algorithms offer limited threshold segmentation for smoothed AQI sequences and lack adequate modeling of temporal dependencies16. Therefore, recent studies on AQI and air pollutant forecasting have demonstrated a growing interest in hybrid models, which integrate feature engineering or decomposition techniques with advanced neural networks. These hybrid models have shown superior forecasting performance and robustness across diverse environmental contexts.

However, traditional hybrid models typically combine signal decomposition techniques—such as wavelet transform, Variational Mode Decomposition (VMD), or Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD)—with unidirectional models like LSTM or SVR to process original real-valued AQI time series17,18. While these methods have demonstrated effectiveness in capturing general trends, they often fall short in responding to nonlinear fluctuations and abrupt shifts. Moreover, their performance is highly contingent upon the selection of decomposition parameters, thereby constraining their generalizability across diverse datasets and geographic regions. To effectively extract deep dynamic patterns from time series data, this study introduces a novel simulated Complex-Valued Optical Convolution Accelerator (CVOCA), built upon a complex-valued neural network architecture that preserves data integrity while enhancing phase and amplitude information capture, thereby effectively enhance the information of minor fluctuations and achieve richer hidden representations19. Subsequently, the extracted features are fed into a Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) network for deep temporal modeling of both forward and backward dependencies. Moreover, regularization strategies combined with a robust loss function mitigated overfitting to flat patterns. The use of Huber loss, dropout, and early stopping ensured that training and validation curves converged smoothly, demonstrating strong generalization rather than mechanical memorization of smoothed segments. Collectively, the simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM framework significantly enhances the model’s capability to fit complex temporal structures. With respect to predictive accuracy, robustness, and computational efficiency, the proposed model outperforms conventional hybrid approaches and exhibits significant potential for practical deployment.

This study proposes a hybrid deep learning model that integrates a simulated CVOCA with a Bidirectional LSTM to improve AQI forecasting in high-altitude cities. By leveraging simulated CVOCA’s nonlinear feature extraction and BiLSTM’s temporal dependency modeling, the framework offers improved accuracy and reliable performance under complex climatic conditions.

Materials and methods

Study area and data collection

The proposed simulated CVOCA–BiLSTM hybrid model was validated using AQI time series data from Lhasa, a representative high-altitude city on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau. As shown in Fig. 1, AQI values were calculated as the mean of six monitoring sites—Xizang University, Railway Station, Monitoring Station, Barkhor Street, Environmental Protection Agency, and Radiation Station—using historical data from the China National Environmental Monitoring centre (https://www.cnemc.cn/) and the AQI Study database (https://www.aqistudy.cn/historydata/). The dataset comprised daily AQI values for Lhasa, Shanghai, and Beijing from January 1, 2014, through December 31, 2024, yielding a total of 12,054 observations. To ensure data integrity, the series was first examined for missing entries, and any gaps were filled using linear interpolation based on adjacent values. To improve training stability, all AQI inputs were normalized to the [0, 1] interval via MinMaxScaler, and model outputs were subsequently denormalized. Following preprocessing, the dataset was partitioned into training (70%), validation (15%), and testing (15%) subsets. The convolutional layer in the simulated CVOCA model was configured with 64 filters, a kernel size of 3, and the ReLU activation function to extract localized temporal features from the AQI time series. The BiLSTM component comprised three stacked layers with 256 hidden units each and a dropout rate of 0.1, using a time step of 10 and the tanh activation function to model temporal dependencies in both forward and backward directions. The model was trained for 100 epochs with a learning rate of 0.0005, utilizing CPU-based parallel computation to enhance efficiency. The optimized network architectures and hyperparameters of all models are summarized in Table S1 (Supporting Information). Model performance was assessed using five standard metrics: root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), Pearson correlation coefficient (R), and coefficient of determination (R2). The selection of Lhasa as a case study enables a robust evaluation of the proposed model’s effectiveness and stability under high-altitude environmental conditions.

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)

The Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network, introduced by Hochreiter et al.20 incorporates an improved strategy to optimize the training of traditional Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs). By using Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT), LSTM effectively mitigates the vanishing gradient problem35. This model is particularly well-suited for handling and predicting significant events that occur with long time intervals or delays in time series data. Unlike conventional neurons, LSTM networks are composed of memory blocks connected across layers. Each LSTM layer consists of a set of recurrently connected memory blocks, which can be seen as differentiable versions of memory chips in digital computers.

Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM)

Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) was first introduced by Graves et al.21 as an enhanced version of the LSTM network, designed to handle and predict sequential patterns in time-series data. Unlike traditional LSTMs, BiLSTM consists of two independent LSTM units: one processes the input sequence in the forward direction (forward LSTM), and the other processes the sequence in the reverse direction (backward LSTM). This bidirectional structure allows BiLSTM to capture both forward and backward temporal features, adapt to delayed contamination effects, be more sensitive to anomalous fluctuations, and exhibit strong compatibility with other feature extraction modules. In previous studies, hybrid models based on LSTM networks have been widely applied to AQI forecasting tasks22–24. The innovation of this study lies in the introduction of the fast simulated CVOCA feature extraction combined with a three-layer BiLSTM model. The main distinction of this model from previous approaches is its ability to rapidly extract high-dimensional data features while capturing both forward and backward dependencies in time series data. Unlike LSTM, which is a unidirectional structure that processes data sequentially over time and is suitable for tasks where past information influences future predictions, BiLSTM, with its bidirectional structure, can simultaneously capture both past and future contextual information. This makes BiLSTM more effective in tasks that require a deeper understanding of time dependencies25. BiLSTM is particularly well-suited for exploring the relationship of each data point in both forward and backward directions across the time dimension, which is especially crucial when making long-term predictions.

Figure 3 illustrates the structure of the simulated CVOCA-LSTM model, simplified to a sequence length of 1. As shown in the diagram, the 10-time-step input is sequentially fed into the first layer of the LSTM for training. The model’s predicted output is then compared with the actual value at the future time point (t11), and the loss is calculated. This process is repeated in the second and third layers of LSTM, with the cumulative loss from the three layers being used as the overall loss of the model. During training, the model is optimized until convergence. Finally, when the loss is minimized, the data is passed into the fully connected layer (Dense) to output the prediction.

Results and discussion

Following rapid feature extraction via simulated CVOCA, a CVOCA-BiLSTM model was constructed to predict AQI values. The performance of the proposed simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM model was validated using daily average AQI data from Lhasa. To demonstrate the advantages of the proposed approach, LSTM, BiLSTM, and simulated CVOCA-LSTM—were selected for comparative analysis. The prediction results and comparative evaluation of these models are discussed in detail in the following subsections.

Model optimization

Figure 5a illustrates the learning curves of the LSTM, BiLSTM, simulated CVOCA-LSTM, and simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM models, depicting changes in training and validation loss. It is evident that all four models adopt the Huber loss function, which integrates the advantages of both mean squared error (MSE) and mean absolute error (MAE). The Huber loss not only ensures continuity and differentiability of the loss function but also leverages the gradient behavior of MSE for small errors, allowing for more precise minimization. Furthermore, its robustness to outliers makes it particularly effective in handling anomalous data. Ultimately, the training and validation loss curves converge closely, indicating good generalization. Several regularization techniques were also employed to mitigate overfitting. For example, the early stopping mechanism was applied, halting training at around the 50th epoch out of 100 iterations to prevent overtraining. Dropout was used during training to randomly deactivate a portion of the neurons, forcing the model to learn under varied structures and enhancing generalization ability. Additionally, normalization and denormalization were employed—scaling features to a uniform range improved training speed, model stability, and activation function performance, while reducing gradient-related issues. Denormalizing the predictions allowed results to be restored to their original scale, making them easier to interpret, compare, and evaluate, and more suitable for practical applications.

The training loss and validation loss of the Huber function of the four models (a), Comparison of models based on first-order (b) and second-order differences (c).

As an adaptive study, initially, two-layer LSTM and BiLSTM models were used to train the system in order to minimize computational requirements. However, this configuration did not yield satisfactory accuracy results. Accordingly, feature extraction was performed using the CVOCA module, and the network depth was increased to three layers to optimize the model structure and performance. The real part was represented by the original values, while the second-order differences (acceleration) and FFT were used as the imaginary part to represent frequency domain features. The second-order difference was chosen for its ability to enrich the feature space with higher-order temporal dynamics, improving sensitivity to local variations that may be missed with first-order changes. Experimentally, models using the second-order difference achieved lower prediction errors (e.g., reduced RMSE and MAPE) and better captured inflection points in the AQI trajectory (Fig. 5b, c). This complementary information enhanced the model’s discriminative power and robustness. This approach elevated the original time series data into a more complex feature space, enhancing the predictive capability, which resulted in outcomes closer to the expected results.

Prediction performance

The comparison of observed and predicted AQI series for Lhasa, Shanghai, and Beijing (Fig. 6) showed that the simulated CVOCA–BiLSTM hybrid model achieved the highest predictive accuracy across environments, reproducing baseline trends while preserving short-term fluctuations. In high-altitude, low-variability Lhasa, the hybrid model produced the narrowest residuals centered near zero, indicating that amplitude–phase feature enhancement and bidirectional temporal fusion preserved subtle signals and reduced systematic bias. In coastal Shanghai, it more faithfully tracked transient oscillations but exhibited greater residual variance owing to stronger local and regional variability. In highly variable Beijing, overall trend agreement remained strong, though residual tails were heavier, reflecting sensitivity to sporadic, externally driven extremes. Model comparisons indicated that simulated CVOCA mitigated non-stationarity and improved peak reconstruction, while BiLSTM reduced information loss via forward–backward fusion; together they yielded robust cross-scenario performance. In summary, the simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM model demonstrates remarkable strengths in predictive accuracy, environmental adaptability, and methodological innovation.

Model evaluation

Figure 7 presents scatter plots comparing the predicted and actual AQI values for each model, showing a progressive improvement in model performance. The proposed simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM model achieves the highest coefficient of determination, with R and R2 values of 0.997 and 0.996, respectively—significantly outperforming both LSTM and BiLSTM. This superior performance is attributed to the feature enhancement mechanism of simulated CVOCA, which markedly boosts the model’s predictive capabilities. Additionally, it is evident that the models incorporating bidirectional temporal structures, such as simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM and BiLSTM, demonstrate slightly better performance than their unidirectional counterparts. This indicates that bidirectional structures further enhance the extraction of temporal dependencies. In terms of MAPE, the standard LSTM exceeds 10%, indicating relatively large prediction errors at certain points—suggesting it may be less reliable in practical applications. In contrast, both simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM and simulated CVOCA-LSTM models achieve MAPE values below 5%, demonstrating excellent control over relative errors. The simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM model also records the lowest RMSE and MAE values, at 1.60 and 0.93, respectively—highlighting its superior overall accuracy.

Comparative analysis

Robustness and generalizability

Across evaluations in Lhasa, Shanghai, and Beijing, the simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM consistently achieved the highest AQI prediction accuracy, with R2 values of 0.996, 0.992, and 0.991, respectively, and corresponding RMSE of 1.60, 2.83, and 4.77 (Table 1; Fig. 7). These results demonstrate clear advantages over LSTM, BiLSTM, and simulated CVOCA-LSTM, particularly in capturing high-frequency fluctuations and reducing local deviations. Compared with recent approaches such as BWO-CAformer (R2 = 0.931; RMSE = 9.82) and pollutant-level process frameworks (e.g., GML), the hybrid model showed superior accuracy without relying on decomposition or preprocessing strategies. By integrating simulated CVOCA’s amplitude–phase feature enhancement with bidirectional sequence modeling, the proposed method robustly preserved both baseline levels and transient oscillations, achieving stable and generalizable forecasting across plateau and plain-city environments.

Computational efficiency and scalability

As shown in Table 2, the integration of the CVOCA module markedly enhances both the computational efficiency and scalability of LSTM and BiLSTM models. Specifically, CVOCA-enhanced models achieve approximately 2.5 × faster inference and substantially reduced training times per epoch compared to their vanilla counterparts, while maintaining moderate memory usage (< 1 GB). The inference time per sample of the CVOCA-LSTM is roughly one-third that of the baseline LSTM, and the simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM reaches an average prediction time of 0.00049 s per sample, representing a 59.3% speedup over the standard BiLSTM. Importantly, despite modest increases in total trainable parameters, both CVOCA-LSTM and CVOCA-BiLSTM exhibit significantly improved predictive performance (R2 = 0.967 and 0.996, respectively), demonstrating that the architecture effectively leverages the additional capacity without compromising efficiency. These quantitative results provide solid evidence for the high computational efficiency and scalability of the proposed framework, underscoring its potential for large-scale, high-dimensional AQI forecasting tasks.

Prediction of AQI values

Using the LSTM, BiLSTM, simulated CVOCA-LSTM, and simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM models developed in this study, we forecasted AQI levels for January 2025 in three representative cities—Lhasa, Shanghai, and Beijing. The results are shown in Fig. 8, where distinct prediction patterns emerged across these cities, reflecting their contrasting environmental contexts as well as the evolution and driving mechanisms of eco-environmental quality across different urbanization stages42.

Predictions of the AQI for January 2025 in Lhasa (a), Shanghai (b), and Beijing (c) by four models.

In Lhasa, where AQI time series are relatively smooth due to limited local emission sources and high-altitude boundary-layer dynamics, the simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM produced a stable prediction curve that closely aligned with historical variability. Specifically, for January 2025, the average AQI predicted by simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM was stable at 52.63, compared to the actual average AQI value of 53 for January 2014–2024. This makes simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM’s prediction more accurate than the other three models for January 2025, leading to the conclusion that the AQI level for the first month of 2025 falls within the “Good” category (Table 3). In contrast, the LSTM model predicted an average AQI of 45.56, indicating an “Excellent” quality level43,44, which significantly differed from the other models. While the prediction trends for pollution levels from simulated CVOCA-LSTM and simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM were similar in terms of pattern changes, the AQI levels showed some variations. This could be attributed to the bidirectional time series structure, which allows for a more accurate overall prediction of AQI values over time. The differences in AQI levels predicted by the LSTM, BiLSTM, simulated CVOCA-LSTM, and simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM models explain the variations in model accuracy and precision. In contrast, the prediction curves for Shanghai and Beijing exhibited more pronounced short-term fluctuations, consistent with their denser emission sources and stronger meteorological variability. Across both plain cities, the simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM captured these fluctuations more effectively than the other models, providing forecasts that preserved both baseline levels and transient variations. Notably, the conventional LSTM tended to underestimate AQI in all three cities, with particularly smoothed predictions in Lhasa, failing to capture the subtle dynamics of plateau environments. BiLSTM improved upon this by partially incorporating temporal dependencies, while simulated CVOCA-LSTM benefited from enhanced feature extraction but still exhibited limitations in reproducing bidirectional temporal dynamics. By contrast, the simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM consistently yielded the most balanced forecasts, simultaneously maintaining stability in plateau conditions and adaptability to the higher variability observed in plain cities.

Conclusions

This study proposes a novel simulated CVOCA-BiLSTM hybrid model for high-accuracy AQI prediction in the high-altitude environment of Lhasa, Tibet. The model integrates the ultra-fast feature extraction capability of the simulated CVOCA for high-dimensional AQI data and the bidirectional time series dependency modeling ability of the BiLSTM network. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed model achieves outstanding predictive performance, with RMSE, MAE, MAPE, R, and R2 values of 1.60, 0.93, 1.60%, 0.997, and 0.996, respectively, outperforming standalone LSTM, BiLSTM, and conventional hybrid models. The synergy between the simulated CVOCA’s capacity to process complex amplitude–phase features and BiLSTM’s bidirectional learning capability effectively addresses the nonlinear dynamics, sudden anomalies, and pollution lag effects in AQI time series. Validation using actual data from Lhasa (2014–2024) confirms the model’s robustness under the unique high-altitude setting, while additional assessments in Shanghai (coastal plain with moderate variability) and Beijing (inland plain with high variability) demonstrate its transferability across contrasting geographical and variability regimes. This research framework not only enhances the accuracy of air quality predictions but also contributes to data-driven urban environmental management, offering feasible decision support for public health protection in ecologically fragile areas. Future research will extend to multi-pollutant coupling mechanisms and regional-scale spatiotemporal dynamics to further improve the model’s general applicability.
