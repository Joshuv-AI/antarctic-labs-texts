Post‐Release Survival and Behavior of Rehabilitated Vervet Monkeys (Chlorocebus pygerythrus rufoviridis) in Malawi
Authors: Laura P. Angley, Nicholas Mikulski, Olivia Sievert, Amanda Harwood, William O. Mgoola, Christopher A. Schmitt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPublished research on primate rehabilitation and release (R&R) is limited and studies suggest that released mortality is generally high. We investigated factors affecting survival and behavior of a rehabilitated troop of vervet monkeys (Chlorocebus pygerythrus rufoviridis) released into Kasungu National Park in Malawi in 2016 by the Lilongwe Wildlife Trust (LWT). Estimated troop survival 9 months after release was 45% when dispersing‐age male disappearances were considered emigrations, which is comparable to other vervet monkey R&R. Using a combination of linear modeling, survival analysis, and social network analysis, we considered social rank, forest strata use, antipredator behavior, troop cohesion and social connectedness, and behavioral diversity across release phases as potential factors influencing survival. Our results suggest that socially connected individuals and juveniles were more likely to survive, with these factors more significant than sex, social rank, and antipredator behavior. Unexpectedly, antipredator behavior significantly decreased after release, which may be a factor linked to increased predation. Also unexpectedly, behavioral diversity decreased post‐release, likely due to a significant increase in locomotion upon release and limitations with our ethogram. The observed mortality patterns suggest that R&R troops may benefit from pre‐release training with platform feeders that encourage canopy use. When possible, individual life history data (e.g., captive‐born, orphaned) should be collected at rescue and rehabilitation stages to help inform caretakers of unique needs. LWT's extensive pre‐ and post‐release monitoring provides vital insights into the factors influencing this troop's survival. We urge other rehabilitation centers to follow this strategy to improve R&R programs.

Introduction

As interactions between human and nonhuman primates (hereafter primates) have increased due to habitat destruction (Cheyne 2009), human–wildlife conflict (Grobler et al. 2006), and illegal wildlife trafficking (Guy and Curnoe 2013), conservation strategies have shifted from the protection of pristine habitats to more hands‐on approaches for maintaining viable wild populations. Reintroduction of rehabilitated animals that had previously been removed from wild populations has become increasingly popular for the re‐establishment of locally extirpated populations and/or to place formerly captive individuals in naturalistic environments (Beck 2010; Farmer and Courage 2008; King et al. 2012; Kleiman 1989). This process can be difficult, but aids in re‐establishing local populations in viable habitats.

Rehabilitation and release (R&R) represent specific methods of reintroduction that involve the release of wild‐born, captive‐raised, and/or captive‐born individuals into viable habitats in a given species' established range. The main criterion for a successful R&R is the establishment of a self‐sustaining population in suitable habitat (Cheyne 2009; Griffith et al. 1989). R&R can be conservation‐focused, as seen in zoo‐based breeding programs (Beck et al. 1994); however, it is more commonly motivated by welfare rather than conservation goals (Baker 2002; Beck et al. 2007; IUCN/SSC 2013). Welfare‐focused release projects for primates primarily involve individuals displaced by habitat destruction, confiscated pets from the illegal wildlife trade, or young animals orphaned by human‐wildlife conflict (Cowlishaw and Dunbar 2000; Guy and Curnoe 2013). The rehabilitation stage of this process typically involves veterinary care and species‐specific training to re‐establish natural skills and behaviors needed for survival in the wild (Beck et al. 2007; Cowlishaw and Dunbar 2000; Kleiman 1989). R&R programs may be used for most species but are more complex and time consuming, and therefore more expensive, for highly social primates (Cheyne 2009).

Savanna monkeys (Chlorocebus spp.) are particularly susceptible to human conflict, since they commonly live in close association with humans across sub‐Saharan Africa and on multiple Caribbean islands (Estes 1992; Grobler et al. 2006). Often viewed as both pest and pet, vervet monkeys (Chlorocebus pygerythrus) have been well‐documented in rehabilitation centers due to the illegal pet trade, poisonings, car accidents, shootings, and domestic pet attacks (Guy et al. 2011; Guy and Curnoe 2013). Vervet monkeys in rehabilitation centers are typically housed together in troops with continuous full contact to mimic wild conspecific troop dynamics and to prepare them for release back into the wild (Grobler et al. 2006; Guy and Curnoe 2013). Vervet monkeys are classified as “Least Concern” on the IUCN Red List with a decreasing population trend (Butynski and De Jong 2022); therefore, release programs are typically driven by welfare considerations rather than conservation concerns and species‐specific guidelines for rehabilitation are generally lacking (Guy and Curnoe 2013). It is recommended that long‐term, post‐release monitoring of behavioral and ecological observations occur to document survival rates, social interactions, welfare changes, and, ultimately, to evaluate the success of R&R programs (Beck et al. 2007; Cheyne 2009; Seddon et al. 1999). Indeed, post‐release monitoring is considered an essential step in any rehabilitation program by the IUCN Reintroduction Specialist Group (IUCN/SSC 2013).

While vervet monkeys are commonly found in rehabilitation centers, published research on the outcomes of vervet monkey R&R is limited and geographically isolated (South Africa: Guy 2013; Guy et al. 2011, 2012a, 2012b; Wimberger et al. 2010; Kenya: Donaldson 2017). Extensive pre‐ and post‐release monitoring and comparisons to habituated wild control troops have demonstrated that vervet monkeys can be successfully rehabilitated and released, with some R&R troops becoming self‐sustaining breeding populations up to two generations (Donaldson 2017). Other releases, however, have reported high troop mortality rates and/or large numbers of individuals disappearing without account. Overall, these efforts have been deemed only partially successful, with poor group composition, low social cohesion, inappropriate antipredator behaviors, susceptibility to illegal hunting, and unsuitable habitat selection being cited as potential causes of failure (Guy 2013; Guy et al. 2011, 2012b; Wimberger et al. 2010). In most studies, behavioral observations and changes in troop dynamics were not formally recorded or reported during the pre‐release phase and, except for Wimberger et al. (2010) and Donaldson (2017), long‐term post‐release monitoring was generally lacking. This makes it difficult to determine the exact causes of failure in R&R, rendering it difficult to refine protocols to increase positive outcomes.

In addition to human‐related threats, such as poisonings, car accidents, and shootings, wild vervet monkeys are at high risk of natural predation due to their small size and semi‐terrestrial lifestyle (Struhsaker 1967a). The most common predators of wild vervet monkeys are snakes, eagles, baboons, and leopards (Cheney and Seyfarth 1981; Isbell et al. 2009; Willems and Hill 2009), with recent work also highlighting domesticated dogs as an emerging, human‐facilitated predation threat in semi‐urban habitats (Robira et al. 2025). Appropriate antipredator behaviors include vigilance, predator‐specific alarm calls (Seyfarth et al. 1980; Struhsaker 1967b), and seeking refuge in tree canopies (Donaldson 2017; Seyfarth et al. 1980; Struhsaker 1967b, 1967c). Navigating risks such as these is a necessary, albeit stressful, component of everyday life for primates (Barros et al. 2008; Sheriff et al. 2011). Primates selected for R&R must undergo transportation and relocation to a new environment, which is considered extremely stressful for those individuals (IUCN/SCC 2013) and can disrupt social bonds leading to group fragmentation and increased predation risk (Aguilar‐Cucurachi et al. 2010; Richard‐Hansen et al. 2000). Prolonged stress and anxiety in primates can also compromise overall health and welfare (Moberg and Mench 2000; Muehlenbein et al. 2012).

Measuring behavioral diversity is one way to describe the breadth of behaviors expressed by an individual or population, as well as the proportion of time spent engaging in each behavior. A more diverse behavioral repertoire rich in species‐specific behaviors may reflect appropriate environmental and social engagement (Swaisgood and Shepherdson 2005). Alternatively, lower behavioral diversity may reflect stereotypical tendencies or above average lethargy (Mason and Latham 2004). Preservation of a wide range of natural behaviors, such as predator avoidance and foraging, is important for promoting survivorship in rehabilitated and released individuals (Rabin 2003). To the best of our knowledge, behavioral diversity has not been used in primate rehabilitation settings but may have potential as an indicator for appropriate acclimation to wild settings post‐captivity.

To better understand the factors associated with survival in R&R primates, we studied the 2016 release of a troop of vervet monkeys rehabilitated from the illegal wildlife trade and human‐wildlife conflict (e.g., injured, orphaned) by the Lilongwe Wildlife Trust (LWT) in Malawi. Using pre‐ and post‐release data, we considered troop dynamics, dominance hierarchy, forest strata use, antipredator behavior, activity budgets, and behavioral diversity as potential factors affecting survival rates and behavior of the troop. Appropriate forest strata use by vervet monkeys is important for predator avoidance and those more proximate to others and ranked higher in the dominance hierarchy may benefit more from collective vigilance. Therefore, we hypothesized that higher social rank, forest strata use, increased social proximity, and appropriate antipredator behavior would be associated with survival. Furthermore, we hypothesized that the troop's activity budget would be diverse post‐release and show low levels of stress‐related behaviors, leading to stable troop cohesion and increased behavioral diversity measures.

Methods

Ethics Statement

The research presented here adhered to the principles for the ethical treatment of nonhuman primates of the American Society of Primatologists. Rehabilitation, release and relevant fieldwork were conducted with clearance from Malawi's Department of National Parks and Wildlife. Post‐release monitoring of vervet monkeys was conducted following the IUCN Guidelines for Reintroductions and other Conservation Translocations (IUCN 1998). Boston University ethical clearance was not required, as BU co‐authors were not involved in data collection. All intellectual property rights were within the legal requirements of Malawi.

Study Site and Subjects

This study's data were collected in 2015–2016 on a rehabilitated troop of vervet monkeys (Chlorocebus pygerythrus rufoviridis) housed at the Lilongwe Wildlife Centre (LWC; S13.97196°, E33.78391°; 1,050 m elevation) that were released into the wild in Kasungu National Park (KNP). Pre‐release data were collected at LWC, LWT's founding project. Located in the Lingadzi Namilomba Forest Reserve (1.8 km2) in the capital city of Lilongwe, LWC is Malawi's only accredited wildlife rescue and rehabilitation center. This study's rehabilitated troop was comprised of individuals brought to LWC from the illegal wildlife trade and human–wildlife conflict. The exact cause of each individual's admittance to LWC was unknown, but confiscation or surrender from the illegal wildlife trade is the majority cause of vervet monkey intakes (64% from 2020 to 2021; Lilongwe Wildlife Trust, unpublished data). Although the history of each individual may include potential factors that inform survival upon release, there was incomplete documentation available regarding how long each vervet monkey was housed at LWC prior to the pre‐release observation period or which individuals were raised in the wild or in captivity.

Post‐release data were collected in KNP (2316 km2; S13.0728°, E33.0665°; 1000–1500 m elevation; Figure 1). Located 175 km north of Lilongwe, KNP is surrounded by moderately dense subsistence farmland (Mkanda and Munthali 1994). Three main rivers flow through the park, forming an extensive river network and drainage system which intersperses miombo woodland (predominantly Brachystegia, Julbernardia, and Isoberlina spp.), seasonally wet grassland, and isolated rocky inselbergs (Davis, Stone, et al. 2021; Bhima et al. 2003). Mean annual rainfall is 780 mm, with the majority falling during Malawi's wet season between November and April (Bhima et al. 2003).

Map of Malawi showing Lilongwe and Kasungu National Park, the location of the release site (in green).

In addition to the 20 rehabilitated monkeys planned for the release, 2 infants were born during the pre‐release phase and a wild adult male (BT) from the surrounding Lilongwe Nature Sanctuary integrated himself into the troop, bringing the total troop size to 23 individuals. LWT decided not to release BT with the rehabilitant troop, as wild immigrants from the pre‐release phase have historically resulted in troop fission post‐release (LWT, unpublished data). At the time of release, the troop was comprised of 22 individuals (7 males, 15 females; Table 1). Prior to release, five adult females and three adult males were fitted with VHF radio tracking collars (Biotrack TW‐5, Dorset, UK), each weighing < 2% of the individual's body weight, for tracking purposes. Tracking collars were used primarily to locate the troop when needed for behavioral monitoring.

Rehabilitation and Release

The rehabilitated troop was formed through individual integrations over the course of about 3 years. Once deemed stable by LWC staff, as evidenced by anecdotal observations indicating low levels of aggression and frequent positive social interactions, the troop was moved to the pre‐release phase and housed in continuous full contact in a 0.5 ha open‐top enclosure at LWC for 9 months (June 2015–February 2016). The enclosure was filled with natural vegetation to promote foraging behaviors and full forest strata use. Human presence was minimized except for daily pre‐release data collection, veterinary intervention in cases of extreme injury, and two daily feedings to supplement natural foraging (09:00 and 16:00). The feedings included common fruits and vegetables, such as corn, sweet potato, and mango, which were scattered on the ground throughout the enclosure. Troop members were approved for release based on a health assessment, the stability of social interactions within the troop, and confirmed foraging of natural foods (e.g., fruits, leaves) in addition to food provisioned by LWT.

Between February 22–29, 2016, each individual was coaxed into the enclosure's night rooms and caught either via net capture or a baited trap cage (individuals > 3.9 kg) and sedated for a final health assessment and parasite treatment prior to transport to the release site. The release site was chosen based on the presence of ample food resources, permanent water sources (< 400 m away), sleeping site availability (trees > 10 m), a low number of wild troops inhabiting the area, and being far from human presence (> 5 km from permanent human settlement, i.e., village or scout camp). LWT implemented a “soft‐release” strategy, during which the troop underwent a 2‐week adjustment period (March 1–17, 2016) at the release site in a fenced enclosure (length, 25 m; width 15 m; height 2 m), followed by supplementary food provisioning of fruits and vegetables during the first few months. Feedings were slowly reduced over time and ended 8 weeks after release. A full internal release report by LWT is available in the supporting materials (Supporting Information S2: Document 1).

Data Collection

Data were collected by one trained primary observer from LWT and recorded by an assistant. A troop census took place each day of data collection. Observations were conducted every day and were divided into four 3‐h time blocks (6:00–9:00, 9:00–12:00, 12:00–15:00, and 15:00–18:00), with one morning and one afternoon time block utilized per day. Each individual was observed four times per week, once in each time block. All adults, subadults, and juveniles (> 1 yr old) were observed. During the first 4 months post‐release, the troop was monitored every day, with weekly observations decreasing by 1 day each subsequent month to encourage dishabituation. Individuals that were not seen for at least 3 consecutive days were considered to have disappeared. Disappearances were further categorized as either confirmed or presumed deaths or emigrations, based on skeletal remains, radio‐collar tracking, or age–sex class at disappearance.

Pre‐ and post‐release behavioral data were collected using focal sampling methods. Focal follows were 20 min in length and consisted of instantaneous point sampling every minute, as well as continuous sampling using a combination of all‐occurrence and one‐zero recording (Altmann 1974). Behaviors were defined using an ethogram developed by LWT (Supporting Information S1: Table 1). For every instantaneous behavior recorded, the focal animal's position in the forest strata was also recorded, using a combination of canopy position (Ground, Lower Canopy, Middle Canopy, Top Canopy) and distance above ground (< 5 m, 5–10 m, 10–15 m, > 15 m). Additional categories were specified for a “Natural Structure,” such as a tree stump or termite mound or a “Man‐made Structure” (e.g., the release enclosure). Scan samples were conducted to assess the proximity of troop members to the focal individual on the 0, 10, and 20‐min mark of the focal session. Each visible individual was placed into an approximate distance category (< 1 m, 1–5 m, 5–10 m, > 10 m) from the focal individual.

Data Analysis

Datasheets were archived at LWC and subsequently transcribed to Microsoft Excel (v2018) and imported into R version 3.6.1 and implemented in RStudio version 2023.6.1 (Posit Team 2023). Four age categories were created for analyses: adult (> 4 yrs), subadult (males only: 3–4 yrs), juvenile (males: 1–3 yrs; females: 1–3 yrs or first offspring), and infant (< 1 yr).

Social Rank Determination

Agonistic behaviors, including aggression, threats, and displacement, from the continuous sampling data set were used to determine social rank within the troop both pre‐ and post‐release. We developed a dyadic matrix for each release phase with all agonistic behaviors noting the winner and loser of each interaction using the tidyverse package in R (v2.0.0, Wickham et al. 2019). We included both males and females in a combined dyadic matrix to rank both sexes in a single troop hierarchy (Young et al. 2017). We generated standardized David's scores using the EloRating package in R (v0.46.28, Neumann and Kulik 2025) to determine dominance rank for each release phase. David's scores analyze the outcomes of the agonistic interactions, while also accounting for the relative competitive strength of opponents (Gammell et al. 2003). Lastly, we used triangle transitivity (t tri) to quantify hierarchy linearity for each release phase using the EloRating package in R. Triangle transitivity quantifies the proportion of transitive triads in a dominance matrix (Shizuka and McDonald 2012). Due to insufficient data, one juvenile (MY) was excluded from this analysis. Three individuals (TI, ZI, and BA) were never observed engaging in dominance interactions post‐release and therefore do not have assigned David's scores for that release phase.

Social Network Analysis

We quantified social cohesion using pairwise social proximity (< 5 m apart). To account for unequal observation effort, emigration, and mortality during the study, we constructed (1) a proximity matrix with the number of observed proximity events between each focal individual and associate for both release phases and (2) an effort matrix with the number of focal follows conducted while both individuals were present. We divided the proximity matrix by the effort matrix to generate pairwise rates of proximity per focal follow, standardized to the period of temporal overlap in survival. These weighted adjacency matrices were used to build undirected social networks using igraph (v1.2.6; Csárdi and Nepusz 2006), and eigenvector centrality scores were calculated for pre‐ and post‐release to identify the extent of social integration of each individual within the troop.

Survival Analysis

We assessed post‐release survival using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis (Jager et al. 2008) using survival package (v3.8.3; Therneau and Grambsch 2000a). We included only individuals recorded as troop members upon release. We measured survival length as the number of days from the first day of release to the last recorded focal or the last day of the study on December 15, 2016, for those who survived and remained in the troop. Individuals who left the troop were retained as “survivors” if confirmed alive or emigrated (based on VHF tracking), as well as those strongly presumed to have emigrated based on age and sex (i.e., dispersal‐age males between 3.5 and 5.5 years; Henzi et al. 2023; L'Allier et al. 2022; Turner et al. 2019). Although dispersal comes with inherent risks, such as predation and starvation (L'Allier et al. 2022), we considered emigrants as survivors for the purpose of this study. Individuals that disappeared and were unlikely to have emigrated (i.e., adult females; Cheney and Seyfarth 1983) or confirmed as deceased were retained as “dead.” Right‐censored data were used to account for those individuals deemed to have survived at the end of the observation period. Proportional hazards assumption (Cox 1972) was used for the model as a whole, as well as for each covariate, based on scaled Schoenfeld residuals (Therneau and Grambsch 2000b) to test for independence between residuals and time. Influential observations were assessed graphically using df beta (Therneau and Grambsch 2000b) and a survival curve was created for the troop. Since survival times are not normally distributed, the median was used to determine central tendency. A between‐group significance test was run using a log‐rank test to compare survival across sex and age categories. Cox proportional hazards regressions were run with post‐release social rank, individual mean hourly counts of pre‐ and post‐release antipredator behaviors (e.g., vigilance), and post‐release eigenvector centrality scores obtained from social network analyses. The individuals without post‐release David's scores (TI, ZI, MY, and BA) were excluded from rank‐based analyses. One multivariate Cox regression was run with covariates sex, age, and rank and a second with sex, age, rank, and eigenvector centrality scores. Mean counts of antipredator behavior post‐release were added as a potential survival covariate for a third analysis. Due to limited behavioral data, two juveniles (RE and SK) were removed from this third analysis.

Forest Strata Use

Density plots of forest strata use were developed using kernel density estimates to assess canopy use post‐release. Two wild adult males (CI and HO) immigrated into the troop shortly after release and were included in the density plot for comparison with released males. Forest strata use was modeled using ordinal logistic regression using the ordinal package (v2019.12‐10; Christensen 2015) with individual as a random effect and age and sex as predictors. Individuals with insufficient data (ZI, RE, and SK) and the two wild males (CI and HO) were removed from the forest strata modeling to focus on the released individuals, specifically. Forest strata use was not assessed for the pre‐release phase due to improper coding during data collection.

Activity Budgets

Recorded behaviors were grouped into six behavioral categories: social, locomote, predator, feeding, stress, and other (Supporting Information S1: Table 1). “Predator” included vigilance and predator avoidance behaviors (referred to here as antipredator behaviors), and “stress” included scratching, self‐grooming, yawning, self‐mutilation, and pacing behaviors. Using focal data, we calculated average hourly behavior rates for the six categories for each individual for both the pre‐ and post‐release phases. We then calculated average troop hourly behavioral rates across categories. Data were omitted for one adult male (ZI) with only one formal behavioral observation post‐release and two adult males (BA and BT) with no behavioral observations post‐release. Data were also omitted for the three juveniles (RE, SK, and MY) that were infants pre‐release and therefore had no behavioral data during that time. Mean hourly activity budgets were compared within individuals for the pre‐ and post‐release phases using paired Wilcoxon sign rank tests and Kruskal–Wallis tests. Post hoc Mann–Whitney U tests were employed to assess the difference in the troop's overall activity budget in the pre‐release phase compared to the post‐release phase.

Behavioral Diversity

According to Miller et al. (2016), behavioral diversity is defined as the number and frequency of species‐specific behaviors displayed by each individual. The Shannon–Weaver Index (H) (referred to here as “Shannon Diversity”), which considers both richness and evenness, was used to quantify the behavioral diversity displayed by each individual during the pre‐ and post‐release phases. Shannon Diversity measures diversity using the formula: H=−∑i=1Bpilnpi where p is the proportion of a particular behavior recorded divided by the total number of behaviors, B, recorded, ln is the natural log, and ∑ is the sum of all calculations across behaviors (Miller et al. 2016). The higher the Shannon Diversity, the lower certainty of prediction, and so the more diverse the behavioral repertoire. To assess behavioral diversity, each behavior per month was counted for each individual. Monthly Shannon Diversity scores were calculated using the vegan package (v2.5‐6; Oksanen et al. 2012) and averaged to create a score for each individual during both the pre‐ and post‐release phases (hereafter behavioral diversity scores). The differences in the mean behavioral diversity scores were assessed both pre‐ and post‐release for the troop, as well as differences between sexes, using a paired Wilcoxon test. Differences in mean behavioral diversity values were analyzed across age and social rank during both the pre‐ and post‐release phases using a Kruskal–Wallis test and linear regression between mean behavioral diversity scores and social rank.

Results

The pre‐release phase lasted a total of 262 days from June 1, 2015, until February 17, 2016, and the post‐release phase lasted a total of 272 days from March 18, 2016, until December 15, 2016. A total of 200 and 92 days of data collection occurred in the pre‐ and post‐release phases, respectively, resulting in 1785 and 766 individual focal sampling periods. Between 95–101 and 1–65 focal sampling periods were conducted for each individual during the pre‐ and post‐release phases, respectively. In total, nine individuals—seven of which were from the original troop (32%)—and two wild males who successfully immigrated into the troop survived the first 9 months post‐release.

If all disappearances were counted as “deaths,” the survival rate in the troop 9 months after release was 32%. If disappeared males of dispersing age were considered emigrations rather than deaths, the overall survival rate increased to 45%. The confirmed mortality rate was 18%. The presumed infant mortality rate for the troop was 100%, including all infants born both pre‐ and post‐release. The presumed juvenile mortality rate was 43%. All disappearances were abrupt and permanent for the remainder of the study period. No instances of individuals temporarily leaving the troop and returning at a later date were recorded.

We were able to materially confirm four deaths. The radio collar and/or skeletal remains of one adult male (JA: June 2016) and three adult females (TI: August 2016; BM: September 2016; AL: November 2016) were found during post‐release monitoring (Table 1). Two newborn infants (unnamed) disappeared at the same time as their mothers' (TI and BM) skeletal remains and radio collars were discovered, suggesting the infant disappearances were linked to these two confirmed predation events.

Three males disappeared post‐release. BA, a radio‐collared male, was a confirmed emigrant on the first day of the release. Using the signal from his radio collar, he was found 1.5 km away from the release site the following day. He soon moved outside of signal range and could no longer be tracked. ZI, an adult male, disappeared a few days after release, and MG, a subadult male, disappeared 6 months after release; they were both presumed to have emigrated (Table 1). By the time MG disappeared, three wild adult males (CI, HO, and PA) had immigrated into the troop, one of which fought frequently with MG. It is likely that these wild male immigrants influenced the emigration of MG. Altercations between wild and released vervet monkeys resulting in fatal injuries have been previously documented (Wimberger et al. 2010), so it is possible MG sustained life‐threatening injuries and died shortly after emigrating. The restricted signal range of the collar made it difficult to track emigrant males. One wild male (PA) eventually disappeared and was presumed to have emigrated as well.

Adult females that disappeared were presumed dead since wild female vervet monkeys typically remain in their natal groups (Cheney and Seyfarth 1983) and to the best of our knowledge, there are no documented instances of released adult females emigrating on their own. Missing juveniles or infants were also presumed dead as most would be unable to survive without their mothers and/or were too young to disperse. A total of 12 adult females and immatures disappeared and were presumed dead over the course of post‐release monitoring (Table 1). Two infants under 6 months of age (MT and BB), who were born toward the end of the pre‐release phase, were the troop's first recorded disappearances (excluding presumed emigrations) on April 19, 2016, and were presumed dead or predated. Between August and September (5–6 months post‐release), three adult females (TO, AU, and BO), three juveniles (NE, RE, SK), and three newborn infants disappeared and were presumed dead. Overall, all four infants born post‐release disappeared shortly after birth and were presumed dead (see Supporting Information S1: Table 4 for an overview of individual survival).

Social Rank Determination

The dominance hierarchy was highly linear both pre‐ (t tri = 0.887, p < 0.001) and post‐release (t tri = 0.850, p < 0.001). David's scores calculated using agonistic interactions show the hierarchy was also relatively stable across release phases but did shift slightly after the presumed emigrations of BA and ZI shortly after release; they did not have sufficient data prior to their emigrations to be assigned a rank position post‐release. Male PO and females BL, AM, and AL were assigned the highest‐ranking positions in the dominance hierarchy both pre‐ and post‐release. Individuals NE, BM, and MG shifted to a higher rank position post‐release and individuals AU and MA shifted to a lower rank position post‐release (Supporting Information S1: Table 2).

Social Network Analysis

The social proximity network revealed relatively strong troop cohesion pre‐release (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2A), with the majority of individuals showing high eigenvector centrality (Supporting Information S1: Table 3). Social proximity networks during the post‐release phase revealed higher variability in social connectedness (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2B), with AU, AM, and KO showing the highest eigenvector centrality and SK, JA, and ZI showing the lowest (Supporting Information S1: Table 3).

Survival Analysis

Considering both confirmed deaths and unexplained disappearances that we interpret as being likely deaths, there was a clear decrease in troop survival probability throughout the post‐release phase with the first death/disappearance (excluding infants under 6 months) on Day 86 (Figure 2). The probability of surviving the post‐release period to the end of our observations (~273 days) was 38%, whereas the probability of surviving the first half of the post‐release phase (~ 136 days) was 94%. The median survival time for all individuals was 251 days.

Kaplan–Meier survival curve with associated confidence intervals and risk table during post‐release phase for the entire troop. Time is in days. Horizontal dotted line shows 50% survival probability and vertical dotted line shows median survival. The first death/disappearance (excluding infants under 6 months) occurred on Day 86.

We found no significant difference in survival time between sexes (males—251 days, females—256 days; χ 2 = 0.5, df = 1, p = 0.5) or age categories (adults—225.5 days, juveniles—251 days; χ 2 = 0.8, df = 2, p = 0.7). Post‐release dominance rank (HR = 0.90, p = 0.79) and antipredator behavior both pre‐ (HR = 1.03, p = 0.9) and post‐release (HR = 1.65, p = 0.2) were not significant predictors of survival. Post‐release eigenvector centrality scores for proximity were significant predictors of survival (HR = 0.009, p < 0.05).

Using Cox multiple regression analysis, we did not find age (J: HR = 0.19, p = 0.10; SA: HR = 1.6 × 10−09, p = 0.9), sex (HR = 12.3, p = 0.06), or rank (HR = 0.32, p = 0.06) to be statistically significant factors in survival. For the Cox multiple regression analysis using the covariates sex, age, rank, and eigenvector centrality scores for proximity, the covariate sex did not pass the proportional hazards assumption due to a single outlier and appeared to be slightly influential using df beta to assess score residuals (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1). As a result, it was removed from the analysis. The analysis without sex showed that post‐release rank was not a significant factor in survival (HR = 0.2, p = 0.12) but higher eigenvector centrality scores (HR = 3.73 × 10−7, p < 0.05) and being juvenile (HR = 0.007, p < 0.05) were significantly associated with lower risk of death. This model met the proportional hazards assumption (χ 2 = 6.9, df = 4, p = 0.14) and Likelihood ratio test (p < 0.05) and Score (logrank) test (p < 0.05) were significant for this model, suggesting a good fit.

A final Cox multiple regression analysis incorporating post‐release antipredator behavior showed that, again, post‐release eigenvector centrality scores for social proximity were significantly associated with lower risk of death (HR = 8.7 × 10−7, p < 0.05) but no other covariates were implicated in risk of death. The Likelihood ratio test (p < 0.05) and the Score (logrank) test (p < 0.05) were significant for this model.

Forest Strata Use

Ordinal logistic regression involving released individuals only, showed no significant effect of sex (β = −0.018; p = 0.85) or age (SA: β = −0.036; p = 0.78; J: β = 0.114, p = 0.23) on forest strata use. The released vervet monkeys were frequently on the ground during the post‐release phase and were rarely found in the tree canopy or higher than 10 m above the ground (Figure 3A–C). When in the trees, individuals remained < 5 m above the ground. The highest‐ranking released male, PO, was positioned on the ground more than any other male, whereas CI, the immigrant wild male, was positioned between 0–5 m and 5–10 m above the ground in the tree canopies more than any other male (Figure 3A).

Activity Budgets

Overall, there was variation in the troop's activity budget pre‐ and post‐release with antipredator behavior expressed the most and stress‐related behavior expressed the least in both release phases (Figure 4). Kruskal–Wallis tests suggest significant differences in the troop's mean activity budget across all six behavior categories both pre‐release (χ 2 = 77.76, df = 5, p = < 0.001) and post‐release (χ 2 = 66.28, df = 5, p = < 0.001), while Mann–Whitney U tests indicate that the troop's average display of antipredator behavior (n = 15, x̄ = 9.02/h) was significantly higher than all other pre‐release behavior categories. The troop's average display of antipredator behavior and feeding behavior was significantly higher than all other behavior categories post‐release (n = 15, x̄ = 6.29/h and 5.96/h, respectively). The troop's average display of stress‐related behavior was significantly lower than all other behavior categories both pre‐ and post‐release (n = 15, x̄ = 1.53/h and 1.50/h, respectively).

Mean hourly percentage of troop behavior displays in each activity budget category pre‐ and post‐release (see Supporting Information S1: Table 1 for activity budget breakdown).

The troop's mean hourly display of antipredator behavior was significantly higher pre‐release than post‐release (V = 120, n = 15, p < 0.001; Figure 4). The troop moved significantly more post‐release (3.1 times/h) than pre‐release (1.9 times/h; V = 0, n = 15, p < 0.001, Figure 4). There were no significant differences in the troop's mean hourly displays of social (V = 70, n = 15, p = 0.60), feeding (V = 40, n = 15, p = 0.28), or stress‐related behavior (V = 63, n = 15, p = 0.89) between the two release phases.

Behavioral Diversity

The troop's mean behavioral diversity was significantly greater pre‐release than post‐release (V = 105, p < 0.05, n = 15). Females had significantly higher behavioral diversity than males during pre‐release (W = 64, p < 0.001, n = 16) and post‐release (W = 42, p < 0.05, n = 14) and the removal of nursing—the sole female‐specific behavior observed—did not change these results. There was no significant difference in mean behavioral diversity across age groups pre‐release (χ 2 = 2.34, df = 2, p = 0.31) or post‐release (χ 2 = 1.71, df = 2, p = 0.43), although juveniles generally had higher behavioral diversity than adults. There was no significant difference in mean behavioral diversity values across social rank order either pre‐release (χ 2 = 16, df = 16, p = 0.45) or post‐release (χ 2 = 13, df = 13, p = 0.45).

Discussion

We analyzed data from a vervet monkey troop released by LWT in 2016 to better understand the drivers of mortality and survival in rehabilitated primates. Using pre‐ and post‐release behavioral data, we considered troop dynamics, social hierarchy, social networks, forest strata use, antipredator behavior, activity budgets, and behavioral diversity as specific factors affecting survival rates and behavior of the troop. This adds to the limited scientific literature on primate R&R and may help improve future release success across primate species.

Overall, the troop had an estimated survival rate of 45%, which is similar to other published vervet monkey releases (Guy et al. 2011, 2012a; Guy 2013; Wimberger et al. 2010). There was a clear decrease in troop survival probability throughout the post‐release phase with the first death/disappearance on Day 86 (excluding infants under 6 months). The probability of surviving the post‐release phase to the end of the observations (~ 273 days) was 38%, whereas the probability of surviving halfway through the post‐release phase (~ 136 days) was 94%. This emphasizes the importance of sufficient long‐term monitoring to accurately capture the outcome of the release and better understand drivers of mortality.

We presume leopard (Panthera pardus) predation to be the cause of most deaths, as leopards are the apex predator in KNP (Davis, Yarnell, et al. 2021) and a common predator of vervet monkeys in the wild (Hayward et al. 2006). Although no direct observations of leopard predation were made in this study, a leopard was seen in the area during data collection and previous research has attributed vervet monkey remains found near tracks to leopard predation (Isbell et al. 2009). A wild vervet monkey troop in Kenya, living with similar mean annual rainfall as KNP, had an average adult female mortality rate of 15% (14/96) over 11 years, though rates increased to 45% (5/11) during a year with high leopard predation (Isbell et al. 2009). In comparison, our adult female mortality rate was 75% (6/8). Leopard predation was suspected in half of the adult female deaths for which skeletal remains were found, highlighting that predation risk remains an important threat to both wild and released vervet monkey survival. Similarly, all four infants born post‐release were presumed dead, aligning with wild infant mortality rates that range from 30% (36/118; Henzi et al. 2023) to 50% (28/58; Isbell et al. 2009) and can reach 100% (6/6; Isbell et al. 2009) during years with exceptionally high predation pressure.

Upon analysis, the dominance hierarchy in the troop, based on triangle transitivity and David's scores, appeared stable with only a few individuals shifting in rank between release phases. The formation of a stable dominance hierarchy in any troop is important since it reduces physical aggression between members and helps conserve energy (Struhsaker 1967c). Contrary to predictions, our survival analyses did not show a consistent effect of social rank, as we determined it, on the likelihood of survival. Although the three highest‐ranking individuals (PO, BL, AM) survived to the end of the study period, the three subsequently ranked individuals (AL, TO, JA) did not. Also, two juvenile offspring (RE, SK) of high‐ranking females (BL and AL, respectively) did not survive but two juveniles without mothers in the troop (ED, KO) did survive. Cheney et al. (1981) found nonrandom mortality in predation across free‐ranging vervet monkeys, where predation was greater among high‐ranking individuals. The precise cause of increased predation risk among these individuals remains unknown. These results along with our own show that the relationship between social rank and survival is complex and requires further investigation.

The troop split up twice during the post‐release study period. The first occurred on the first day after release from the in‐situ enclosure. The troop was found together again the next morning except for BA, who was the first male to emigrate. The second occurred one morning in April, approximately 30 days after release, due to a suspected predation of infants during the night. The troop was found back together later that day. Wimberger et al. (2010) also saw immediate troop fission on the day of a vervet monkey release. It is uncommon for wild vervet monkey troops to dissolve completely unless resource competition becomes too great (Hauser et al. 1986); therefore, fission events may be indicative of troop instability and lead to reduced survival (Wimberger et al. 2010). Unlike LWT's 2014 vervet monkey release troop, which split up many times (LWT, unpublished data), this vervet monkey troop remained together for the majority of the study period and came back together quickly after separating. The high social connectedness of the troop pre‐release may have been influenced by the confines of the enclosure, but the relatively high social connectedness post‐release suggests tolerance and close social bonds within the troop (Donaldson 2017; Lehmann et al. 2007; Seyfarth 1980).

We predicted that individuals with strong social bonds who remained in close proximity to others would benefit more from collective vigilance to mitigate predation risk. Close proximity to others, as measured by eigenvector centrality scores, did increase an individual's chance of survival. Eigenvector centrality not only counts the number of nodes connected to a focal node but also considers the weight of the node's centrality (i.e., the amount of time spent in proximity with those connected to it; Borgatti et al. 2018; Sueur et al. 2010). Many individuals with high post‐release centrality scores (PO, AM, BL, KO, and ED) survived until the end of the study, whereas individuals with low centrality scores (TI, RE, SK, and JA) were either presumed or confirmed dead. Highly connected individuals may benefit more from collective vigilance and by being physically located in the center of the troop rather than the periphery where predation risk is higher, although these data cannot explicitly confirm this. Although proximity rates were standardized by observation effort and temporal overlap in survival, eigenvector centrality was calculated across the entire post‐release phase, and individuals that survived longer inherently had greater opportunity to accumulate social connections. As a result, higher centrality may partially reflect increased social sampling rather than social integration prior to death exclusively. Since the exact timing of many deaths is unknown, it is difficult to determine individuals' social proximity to others immediately prior to disappearance or predation. Predation may have occurred when they had moved to the periphery of the troop or had fallen behind, even though they were frequently observed close to others during observations. Juveniles appeared to have a slightly reduced risk of mortality in our study. Previous research suggests juvenile vervet monkeys form social “bubbles” throughout their development composed of a mixture of strong and weak social ties (Vilette et al. 2022, 2023). These social ties may provide protection against predators and reduce competitive pressures from conspecifics, thereby potentially lowering mortality risk. However, given the small sample size and the inclusion of multicovariate models at the lower boundary of recommended events‐per‐variable ratios for Cox proportional hazards analyses (Peduzzi et al. 1996), these findings should be interpreted with caution and warrant further investigation.

Forest strata density plots show a high proportion of time spent on the ground among all released individuals (Figure 3A–C). Adult male, ZI, was positioned 5–10 m off the ground in the lower tree canopy more often than other males, but his post‐release data were limited due to his early emigration and may not reflect consistent behavioral patterns (Figure 3A). Wild males, HO and CI, were positioned 0–5 m and 0–10 m off the ground, respectively, in the top canopy, whereas most released individuals were frequently positioned 0–5 m off the ground in the lower and middle canopies; however, we found no significant effect of sex or age on forest strata use.

Ground foraging increases perceived predation risk by terrestrial predators (Makin et al. 2012), particularly for those in the troop's periphery that may be farther away from the safety of trees; however, these data show little variation in survival based on strata use. Due to their semi‐terrestrial lifestyle (Enstam and Isbell 2007), it is not uncommon for vervet monkeys to locomote on the ground, but wild vervet monkeys often shift to the canopy to escape large terrestrial predators, such as leopards, and run down out of tree canopies to avoid aerial predators, such as eagles (Seyfarth et al. 1980). This indicates that variation in vertical forest strata use is an important antipredator strategy (Makin et al. 2012; Willems and Hill 2009). Having spent his entire life exposed to the risks of natural predators, CI's higher concentration of time spent elevated off the ground, where it is easier to identify and avoid terrestrial predators, is possibly a learned antipredator strategy. Naive predator awareness and avoidance, resulting from time spent in an environment devoid of terrestrial carnivores, may also have contributed to high mortality rates for the released troop.

Forest strata use, especially among individuals assumed to have less prior foraging experience, such as former pets, may have been skewed toward terrestriality by the feeding routine during rehabilitation, wherein food was provisioned on the ground. The troop was observed successfully feeding on a variety of natural foods and vegetation once released, but forest strata use particularly when feeding was not analyzed in this study. The food provided by LWT during the first 8 weeks of release was also primarily provided on the ground, which may have reinforced the preference for ground foraging in the new habitat. Since appropriate variation in forest strata use is important for evading both terrestrial and aerial predators (Seyfarth 1980), the addition of 5 m+ high feeding platforms in the pre‐release enclosure may help promote greater vertical canopy use post‐release.

The troop did display appropriate antipredator behaviors for birds of prey by alarm calling and descending from trees quickly during the pre‐release phase—a behavior frequently seen in wild vervet monkey troops (Seyfarth et al. 1980). Even though antipredator behavior was not associated with survival, the troop's antipredator behavior for terrestrial predators, specifically, appears insufficient. The troop suffered multiple predation events that may have been caused by poor antipredator strategies and were observed returning to areas where multiple predation events had occurred. However, recent work on semi‐urban vervet monkeys suggests responses to terrestrial predators may be modulated by risk predictability, with a reduction in response intensity when risks are expected (Robira et al. 2025). This may suggest that the troop's post‐release responses reflect altered risk assessment rather than poor antipredator responses. Additional research is needed on the efficacy of different predator‐awareness training strategies during rehabilitation (Guy et al. 2012a; Tetzlaff et al. 2019). For group‐living species such as vervet monkeys, the presence of an experienced individual may help facilitate social transmission of appropriate antipredator behaviors to naive individuals via social learning (Griffin et al. 2000; Shier and Owings 2007). Future research should collect behavioral data on any wild individuals that join troops undergoing rehabilitation for comparison.

The ultimate goal of many release programs is to return displaced or injured animals back to their native habitat to help improve their overall health and welfare (Cowlishaw and Dunbar 2000; Guy and Curnoe 2013). The troop displayed significantly lower levels of stress‐related behaviors (e.g., self‐scratching) per hour than all other behavior categories both pre‐ and post‐release and these behaviors did not increase post‐release. As recommended by Baker (2002), human exposure and handling should be minimized during rehabilitation as it can cause unnecessary stress. Prolonged stress and anxiety in primates can compromise overall health, welfare, and cognitive function (Moberg and Mench 2000; Muehlenbein et al. 2012; Mendl 1999). These guidelines were followed by LWT and human presence near the enclosure was reduced as much as possible. Beyond stress reduction, minimizing human presence pre‐release may also be important for maintaining appropriate levels of neophobia toward humans and anthropogenic artifacts, as increased exposure to human‐modified environments has been shown to influence exploratory responses to novel objects in vervet monkeys (Ellington et al. 2024). In addition, the 2‐week acclimation period at the KNP pre‐release enclosure as part of the “soft‐release” protocol was implemented to help ease the transitionary period before the full release and reduce potential causes of stress, such as finding food in a novel environment. The troop's two fission events post‐release may indicate stress and troop instability, but they were quickly found back together again. Although stress‐related behaviors remained low over the course of data collection, a lack of baseline behavioral data prior to rehabilitation and physiological measures of stress makes it difficult to assess how R&R impacted the troop's overall stress.

Contrary to our predictions, the troop's behavioral diversity decreased post‐release. Shannon Diversity is used to quantify behavioral diversity and considers both behavioral evenness and richness. With only six behavioral categories, however, this metric has inherently limited resolution and is sensitive to shifts in a single behavior. Changes in one category will impact evenness and Shannon Diversity, even if the breadth of behaviors remains unchanged. Consistent with this limitation, activity budgets showed an increase in locomotion post‐release, likely reducing time spent engaging in other behaviors and thereby shifting the behavioral evenness and lowering behavioral diversity. Unlike the pre‐release phase, where the troop was provided food each day and could therefore allocate more time to other behaviors, such as grooming and resting, the post‐release phase required the troop to spend more time looking for food. Guy et al. (2012b) found their released troop regularly traveled up to 2 km away from the release site, so this finding is not unusual. Despite the decrease in behavioral diversity post‐release, time spent moving through their new habitat is also a desirable outcome as it suggests environmental engagement. The other behaviors may have diversified post‐release but was not apparent due to the confines of our descriptively coarse ethogram where behavioral richness was lost (see Cronin and Ross 2019). For example, when considering behavioral diversity, the ethogram included a single umbrella term like “foraging,” rather than a variety of foraging techniques.

Conclusion and Recommendations

Given the results of this study, we recommend that R&R programs for vervet monkeys, and nonhuman primates more generally, prioritize long‐term, post‐release monitoring to accurately capture the outcome of the release and better understand drivers of mortality. During the rehabilitation period, vervet monkeys may benefit from raised feeding platforms to help diversify forest strata use and reduce dependency on ground foraging in the wild, as this may increase their predation risk. We recommend more research on predator‐awareness training as a potential strategy to improve post‐release survival in rehabilitated vervet monkeys. Future studies should consider collecting behavioral data on wild conspecifics that join or remain near the troop during rehabilitation to allow for behavioral comparisons between wild and rescued vervet monkeys. If considering behavioral diversity, studies should use highly specific ethograms that differentiate between a diverse array of behaviors. This will help get a detailed representation of each individual's behavioral repertoire.

Rehabilitation centers should also consider releasing the troop earlier in the rainy season. This would potentially allow more time for the troop to strengthen their foraging skills when resources are plentiful and before predators rely heavily on shared, permanent water sources (Valeix et al. 2009). This was also recommended by Guy et al. (2011) and Wimberger et al. (2010), as food would be more abundant during the wet season and hopefully discourage congregation near supplementary feeding stations. Lastly, future research on rehabilitated troops should, if possible, include aspects of life history (e.g., captive born, orphaned), prior to rehabilitation in their records and as covariates in their analyses. A lack of information on vervet monkey life‐history prevents rehabilitation centers from adjusting their protocols to fit the unique needs of individuals raised under differing human impacts.

Overall, the LWT troop's mortality rate was comparable to that seen in other R&R studies, but unlike some previously monitored releases, the troop formed a stable dominance hierarchy, had relatively strong troop cohesion, engaged in appropriate species‐specific behaviors, and displayed few stress‐related behaviors. LWT's extensive pre‐ and post‐release monitoring is unique among vervet monkey release programs and provides vital insight into the troop's social dynamics, behavioral repertoires, and overall survival. Other rehabilitation centers should follow this strategy, since all newly monitored and reported releases will add valuable information to the development of more highly effective vervet monkey rehabilitation and release programs.
