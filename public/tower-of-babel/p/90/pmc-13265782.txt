Bio Polyhydroxybutyrate /Fiber castor Oil Hybrid Composite (CO-BPFHC) as adsorbent for simultaneous removal of Pb(II), Cd(II), and Cr(VI) from wastewater: mechanistic insights and thermodynamic evaluation
Authors: Eman Fayad, Mohammad Abu Shuheil, Dalal Nasser Binjawhar, Khalid M. Alsyaad, Mohammad Albayari, Ahmed Abu-Rayyan, H. S. Ali, A. Ebada, F. M. Mahmoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Simultaneous contamination of industrial effluent discharges with Pb, Cd, and Cr(VI) poses a critical threat to the environment due to their toxicological profile, nonbiodegradability, and potential for causing cancer. Herein, a bio polymer/fiber hybrid composite (CO-BPFHC) derived from castor oil and prepared via isophoronediisocyanate (IPDI)-mediated urethane polymerization on a scaffold of alkali treated sisal/jute bi fibers is introduced for the simultaneous adsorptive removal of Pb(II), Cd(II), and Cr(VI) from contaminated industrial wastewater. Extensive characterization of CO-BPFHC was carried out using FTIR, XRD, SEM–EDS, TEM, BET, TGA, DSC, zeta potential, particle size analysis, and pHpzc measurements to prove a hierarchical mesoporous architecture (SBET = 418.3 m2 g−1), high thermal stability (T5% = 251°C), and a high density multifunctional surface for adsorption. A key and innovative aspect of the work is the proof of the adsorption of Pb(II) and Cd(II) by electrostatic attraction and complexation under mildly acidic conditions (pH 5–6), whereas the adsorption of Cr(VI) occurs by a reduction adsorption mechanism involving the reduction of Cr(VI) to Cr(III) by the electron donating groups of the bio polymer matrix, followed by the complexation of the reduced Cr(III) species by the adsorption sites of the adsorbent. The combined spectroscopic, kinetic, and thermodynamic analyses suggest that adsorption occurs through a combination of electrostatic interactions, surface complexation, and redox assisted pathways, particularly for Cr(VI). The Langmuir adsorption capacities of the adsorbent reached 591.4, 432.7, and 387.6 mg g−1 for Pb(II), Cd(II), and Cr(VI), respectively, demonstrating competitive adsorption performance compared with recently reported bio based adsorbents. The adsorption kinetics of the metal ions by the adsorbent follows the pseudo second order kinetics (R2 > 0.999). Thermodynamic calculations confirm the spontaneous adsorption of the metal ions by the adsorbent under endothermic conditions for Pb(II) and Cd(II) ions, whereas the adsorption of Cr(VI) occurs by a spontaneous exothermic process. Introduction

One of the most ecologically hazardous pollution situations in the world is caused by the co-occurrence of lead (Pb(II)), cadmium (Cd(II)), and hexavalent chromium (Cr(VI)) in industrial wastes, primarily from electroplating, leather tanning, mining, pigment manufacturing, and battery industries1,2. Pb(II) and Cd(II) are considered Priority Hazardous Substances by the European Water Framework Directive and are also related to nephrotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, and irreversible neurological damage at concentrations higher than WHO permissible limits of 0.01 and 0.003 mg L−1, respectively3,4. Cr(VI), present as chromate anion (CrO42−) and dichromate anion (Cr2O72−), is classified as a human carcinogen, Group 1, and is associated with pulmonary carcinoma and DNA strand breaks. Its WHO guideline value for drinking water is 0.055,6. Simultaneous treatment of effluent containing heavy metal cations(Pb(II), Cd(II)) and heavy metal oxyanions (Cr(VI)) poses a special scientific and engineering challenge, as they demand different chemical treatment conditions7,8. For instance, conventional technologies such as chemical precipitation, ion exchange, membrane filtration, and electro coagulation have shown significant limitations for mixed metals, including selectivity, operational costs, and secondary wastes9–11. Adsorption using multifunctional adsorbents that integrate both cationand anionexchange functionalities, along with redox active sites, represents a highly promising and unified strategy for the efficient removal of diverse contaminants from aqueous systems12–14.

Bio-based composites derived from renewable feed stocks are particularly attractive adsorbents because they minimize the environmental footprint of the remediation process itself, aligning with circular economy and green chemistry principles15,16. Castor oil (CO), obtained from Ricinuscommunis seeds, is a naturally occurring hydroxylated triglyceride predominantly composed (~ 90%) of ricinoleic acid (C18:1–OH). This unique structure imparts bifunctional properties to CO: the pendant hydroxyl groups actively participate in urethane-forming crosslinking reactions, while the unsaturated aliphatic chains provide electron-rich sites that facilitate the reduction of hexavalent chromium Cr(VI) to less toxic forms12,17. Natural lignocellulosic fibers such as sisal and jute provide a large number of hydroxyl, carboxyl, and aromatic18,19.

Critically, the distinctive challenge for removal of Cr(VI) compared to other cationic metals is due to its anionic form, where, under acidic pH, the primary species of CrO42−/Cr2O72− dominate, and hence, positively charged or electron-rich surfaces of adsorbents are necessary for efficient removal. Several studies have shown that bio based adsorbents containing hydroxyl and phenolic groups have the ability to reduce Cr(VI) to its less toxic and more complexable form, Cr(III)20,21. However, in the literature, a detailed evaluation of a single bio-based composite that can exhibit efficient simultaneous removal of both cationic species (Pb(II), Cd(II))and anionic species (CrO42−/Cr2O72−) of heavy metals in a single process, along with mechanistic elucidation of the contrasting removal processes, has not been achieved.

Therefore, the present study focuses not merely on the development of another bio based adsorbent, but on the integration of multiple adsorption functionalities within a single hybrid material architecture. The novelty of CO-BPFHC arises from the synergistic combination of: (i) a bio-based polyurethane network derived from castor oil, (ii) alkali treated sisal/jute fibers providing abundant oxygen containing functional groups, (iii) ultrasonicationassisted dispersion generating hierarchical macro mesoporosity, and (iv) the coexistence of cation-binding and Cr(VI) reduction assisted adsorption pathways within the same adsorbent system. This integrated multifunctionality enables simultaneous treatment of chemically distinct heavy metal species using a single sustainable material platform.

In the current study, we have addressed this issue by testing a novel bio-based composite known as CO-BPFHC, which is composed of a castor oil-based bio-polymer/fiber hybrid composite prepared using IPDI-mediated urethane polymerization on a sisal/jute scaffold, for simultaneous removal of Pb(II), Cd(II), and Cr(VI) species from synthetic industrial wastewater. This study is differentiated by: (i) the integration of multifunctional adsorption and redox-active features within a single bio-based composite; (ii) simultaneous removal of both cationic and oxyanion heavy metals; (iii) ultrasonication-assisted generation of hierarchical porosity; and (iv) comprehensive comparative evaluation of adsorption, kinetic, isotherm, and thermodynamic behaviors for Pb(II), Cd(II), and Cr(VI).

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Castor oil (food grade, hydroxyl value = 163 mg KOH g−1, acid value = 1.2 mg KOH g−1), isophoronediisocyanate (IPDI, 98%), dibutyltindilaurate (DBTL, 95% catalyst), benzoyl peroxide (BPO, 97%), and metal salts Pb(NO3)2, Cd(NO3)2•4H2O, and K2Cr2O7 (all ≥ 99%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Sisal (Agave sisalana) and jute (Corchorusolitorius) fibers were obtained from a certified agricultural processing facility (Egypt). These were pre-treated by sequential alkali delignification (4 wt% NaOH, 80°C, 2h) and acid neutralization (0.5 M HCl, 60 min) to remove extractives and enhance reactivity. Dimethyl Sulfoxide (DMSO) 99.5%, HNO3, HCl, and NaOH of analytical grade from Fisher Scientific were the sources of the reagents. Ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ•cm, Milli-Q Advantage A10, Merck) was employed.

Cr(VI) stock solution of 1000 mg/L concentration was prepared from K2Cr2O7. Solutions of Cr(VI) were prepared by dilution. The concentration of Cr(VI) was analyzed by the 1,5-diphenylcarbazide (DPC) colorimetric method (λ = 540 nm, Shimadzu UV-1800) whereas the concentration of total Cr was analyzed by ICP-OES (Agilent 5110) after suitable digestion. Synthesis of CO-BPFHC

The synthesis of CO-BPFHC was accomplished in three sequential steps as depicted schematically in Fig. 1. Stage I: Castor oil (50.0 g, 0.054 mol) and IPDI (30.8 g, 0.138 mol; NCO/OH = 1.05) were mixed in a three-necked round-bottom flask under dry N2 atmosphere using DBTL catalyst (0.05 wt% based on total reactants) and reacted for 4 h at 70°C with mechanical stirring (200 rpm) to give hydroxyl-terminated castor oil-based prepolymer (CO-PP) as confirmed by FTIR (degradation of N = C = O absorption band at 2270 cm-1 for less than 50% conversion). Stage II: Pre treated sisal/jute fibers (1:1 mass basis; 30 wt% based on final composite) were uniformly dispersed in CO-PP dissolved in DMSO (30 wt/vol%) by probe ultrasonic treatment (Branson 750W, 30 min, 60% amplitude, and ice bath) for uniform impregnation of fibers. Stage III: BPO (1.0 wt%) was used as a radical initiator, and the mixture was cast into Teflon molds and cured for 6 h at 80°C in N2 for 3D crosslinking. The product was washed repeatedly (ethanol 3 times, and then ultrapure water 5 times) and dried in a vacuum oven for 24 h at 60°C; it was then ground and sieved to 100 mesh (149 μm) for use.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the three stage synthesis of CO-BPFHC: Stage I IPDI mediated urethane prepolymer formation from castor oil; Stage II ultrasonication-assisted sisal/jute fiber impregnation; Stage III BPO initiated free radical crosslinking and network curing. Characterization techniques

The FTIR spectra were recorded on a Bruker TENSOR 27 spectrometer (ATR-diamond, 400–4000 cm−1, 64 scans, and 4 cm−1 resolution). XRD patterns were recorded on a RigakuSmartLabdiffractometer (Cu Kα radiation, λ = 0.15406 nm; 2θ = 5–80°; step = 0.02°; 2s per step). SEM/EDS images and elemental maps were taken on a JEOL JSM-7610F (10 kV; Au-coated samples). TEM and HRTEM images were taken on a FEI Tecnai G2 F20 STEM (200 kV). N2 adsorption desorption measurements were performed at 77 K using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 surface area analyzer. Prior to analysis, samples were degassed under vacuum at 150°C for 12 h to remove physically adsorbed moisture and residual solvents. BET surface area calculations were conducted within the relative pressure range P/Po = 0.05–0.30, where the BET plot exhibited satisfactory linearity (R2 > 0.999). Pore size distribution was determined using the BJH method from the desorption branch of the isotherm. TGA/DTG and DSC were simultaneously carried out using a Netzsch STA 449F3 under a N2 atmosphere at a flow rate of 50 mL min−1 and a heating rate of 10°C min−1 over a range of 25 to 700°C. Zeta potential and hydrodynamic diameter were measured using DLS (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS; 25°C). pHpzc was measured using the pH drift method with a 0.01 M NaNO3, adsorbent dose 1.0 g L−1, 24 h equilibration.

Batch adsorption experiments

The batch experiments were performed at 250 mL polypropylene conical flasks using an orbital shaker (150 rpm, New Brunswick Innova 44) at a controlled temperature regime (± 0.5 K). For Pb(II) and Cd(II), the baseline conditions were fixed at: Co = 100 mg L−1, m = 0.5 g L−1, pH 5.5 (Pb) and pH 6.0 (Cd), T = 298 K, t = 120 min. For Cr (VI), the baseline conditions were fixed at: Co = 100 mg L−1, m = 0.5 g L−1, pH 2.5, T = 298 K, t = 90 min (equilibrium time reduced due to reductive mechanism). pH was adjusted with 0.1 M HNO3 or 0.1 M NaOH solutions for Pb(II) and Cd(II). Only HNO3 was used for Cr (VI) experiments to avoid chloride interference effects. Only one parameter was varied at a time. Pb(II) and Cd(II) were quantified by ICP-OES after filtration through 0.45 μm PVDF membranes. Cr(VI) was determined by DPC colorimetry while total chromium was determined by ICP-OES. All batch experiments were triplicated. Error bars represent ± SD (n = 3). Effect of pH

For Pb(II) and Cd(II), pH was adjusted from 2.0 to 10.0, and for Cr(VI), it was adjusted from 1.0 to 8.0, considering a broader pH range for the adsorption of oxyanions. PH > 6 for Cr (VI) was not considered in the model because of the predominance of Cr(III) precipitation due to reduction.

Effect of adsorbent dosage

Dosage was varied from 0.1 to 1.5 g L−1 at fixed Co = 100 mg L−1, optimum pH, T = 298 K, and t = 120 min (Pb/Cd) or 90 min (Cr).

Effect of contact time and kinetics

The samples were extracted after 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 45, 60, 90, 120, 180, and 240 min. Four kinetic models, namely pseudo-first-order kinetics (PFO), pseudo-second-order kinetics (PSO), intraparticle diffusion kinetics (IPD), and Elovich kinetics, were employed. Non-linear methods were implemented in OriginPro 2023 software (OriginLab Corp.) to minimize χ2 and maximize R2.

Effect of initial concentration and isotherm modeling

Co was varied from 10 to 500 mg L−1 at equilibrium conditions. Four isotherm models were fitted: Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, and Dubinin–Radushkevich (D–R).

Effect of temperature and thermodynamics

The temperatures used in this study were 298, 308, 318, and 328 K. ΔG°, ΔH°, and ΔS° values were obtained from the van’t Hoff plot. Note that for Cr(VI), the exothermic thermodynamic signature (ΔH° < 0) was carefully interpreted in the context of the coupled redox adsorption mechanism.

Effect of ionic strength and competing ions

For Pb(II) and Cd(II) studies, NaCl solutions of 0–0.5 M concentration were used for varying ionic strength. For Cr(VI) studies, Na2SO4 solutions of 0–50 mM concentration were used as a competitor, as sulfate directly competes for oxyanion binding sites, simulating a more environmentally relevant scenario. Competing cations such as Na+, K+, Ca2+, Mg2+ at 10 mM concentration were used for Pb(II) and Cd(II) studies, and competing anions such as (SO42−, NO3−, Cl−, PO43− at 10 mM) were tested for Cr(VI).

Regeneration studies

Pb(II)/Cd(II)-loaded CO-BPFHC was regenerated with 0.1 M HNO3 (25 mL, 60 min, 150 rpm). For Cr (VI)loaded CO-BPFHC, the eluent was 0.1 M NaOH + 0.05 M NaCl (to dissolve and release Cr(III) complexes and any unreduced Cr(VI)); then 0.1 M HNO3 was used to reprotonate the surface. Five complete cycles were evaluated.

FTIR spectroscopy

FTIR spectra of castor oil, raw sisal/jute fibers, pristine CO-BPFHC, and CO-BPFHC after adsorption of Pb(II), Cd(II), and Cr(VI) ions are presented and discussed together in Fig. 4 to illustrate and explain how specific functional groups contribute to the adsorption of pollutants. The absence of absorption at 2270 cm−1 indicates total reaction of isocyanate group of IPDI. The absorption at 1718 cm−1 indicates C = O stretching of urethane carbonyl group, while N–H bending of amide II at 1535 cm−1 and C-N stretching at 1241 cm−1 indicate formation of aliphatic Polyhydroxybutyrate8. After simultaneous adsorption of Pb(II), Cd(II), and Cr(VI) ions (post-CO-BPFHC spectrum, Fig. 4), the following spectral changes are diagnostic: (i) the O–H/N–H absorption band shifts from 3350 to 3218 cm−1 and becomes much broader, suggesting strong coordination of Pb(II)and Cd(II)ions with hydroxyl and urethane N–H groups; (ii) the urethane C = O stretching band shifts from 1718 to 1701 cm−1, consistent with coordination of metal ions with the carbonyl oxygen; (iii) a new weak absorption band at 880 cm−1 appears, which can be ascribed to the Cr–O11,21; (iv) the O–H band broadening is more pronounced on the low-wavenumber shoulder (~ 3100 cm−1), consistent with H-bonding between Cr(VI) oxyanions and protonated surface groups prior to reduction. These post adsorption spectral shifts suggest the involvement of hydroxyl, urethane, and carbonyl functional groups in the adsorption mechanism and support the proposed multi-mechanism interaction discussed in Sect. "Proposed adsorption mechanism".

To further verify the adsorption mechanism, a detailed comparison of the FTIR spectra for pure and metal-loaded CO-BPFHC samples is provided. Obvious shifts in the position and changes in the intensity of the main functional groups provide evidence supporting the interaction between the adsorbent functional groups and metal ions. A wide band of O–H/N–H stretching vibrations, initially located in the region of ~ 3350 cm−1, is shifted to a region of lower wave numbers (~ 3218 cm−1) and exhibits considerable changes in width after adsorption.

Furthermore, the urethane carbonyl (C = O) stretching vibration shows a distinct red shift from 1718 cm−1 to approximately 1701 cm−1. This indicates the involvement of the carbonyl oxygen atoms in the complexation reaction with the Pb(II)and Cd(II)ions. Moreover, the band due to the stretching vibrations of the C-N group (~ 1241 cm−1) shows slight shifts.

In the case of Cr(VI) adsorption, the spectrum shows the appearance of a new band at approximately ~ 880 cm−1 due to the presence of the Cr–O group of Cr(III) species. Furthermore, the increased broadening of the bands in the low wavenumber region (3000–3200 cm−1) is due to the increased hydrogen bonding.

These spectral changes indicate that the adsorption mechanism likely involves contributions from surface complexation, electrostatic interactions, and redox-assisted binding rather than purely physical adsorption alone.

BET surface area and pore structure

N2 adsorption/desorption isotherm (Fig. 6) and BJH pore size distribution (Fig. 7) of the CO-BPFHC sample give quantitative pore structure data. The isotherm shows the characteristics of a Type IV isotherm (IUPAC classification) with a well definedH2(b) type hysteresis loop at P/Po = 0.42–0.91, indicating the presence of complex pore structure of the mesoporous material3. The BET surface area of the CO-BPFHC sample was determined to be 418.3 m2 g−1. Although this value is relatively high for polyurethane/lignocellulosic composites, it is attributed to the formation of a hierarchical porous structure generated through alkali fiber treatment, ultrasonicationassisted dispersion, and interfacial void formation between the polymeric matrix and lignocellulosic fibers. The observed Type IV isotherm with H2(b) type hysteresis further supports the existence of interconnected mesoporous domains rather than a dense nonporous structure. Comparable increases in surface area have been reported for porous bio based polyurethane composites containing chemically treated lignocellulosic fibers and phase separated porous architectures, where fiber dispersion and interfacial pore generation significantly enhanced accessible surface area. Therefore, the high BET value observed for CO-BPFHC is interpreted as arising primarily from the interconnected macro mesoporous network and fiber polymer interfacial porosity rather than intrinsic microporosity of the polyurethane phase alone.

Water Penetration, Swelling Behavior, and Internal Diffusion Mechanism

In addition to the hierarchical pore structure, the transport of aqueous metal ions into the interior of the CO-BPFHC is also affected by the water-induced swelling of the polymer fiber composite. The presence of hydrophilic functional groups, i.e., hydroxyl groups from the cellulose fibers, carboxyl groups from the partially oxidized fibers, and urethane groups from the polymer matrix, is beneficial for the absorption of water by the composite. During the absorption of water from aqueous solution, the composite swells to a certain extent due to the partial opening of the polymer chain structure.

This swelling phenomenon is seen to be crucial in the improvement of mass transfer since it reduces the diffusional resistances and allows the hydrated metal ions to access areas beyond the external surface. This loosening of the polyurethane network facilitates the creation of temporary diffusional paths for the migration of Pb(II)and Cd(II)cations, and Cr(VI) oxyanions (HCrO4−/Cr2O72−) towards the cellulose fibers, which act as active adsorption sites.

The mechanism of diffusion can thus be depicted as a multi-step procedure: (i) external film diffusion of the solution to the composite’s surface; (ii) pore diffusion via the interconnected macro mesoporosity; and (iii) intra-matrix diffusion via the phenomenon of polymer swelling, thus gaining access to areas of the fibers-polymer interface that are rich in functional binding sites. This analysis is completely in accordance with the intraparticle diffusion model (Fig. 24), in which multi-linearism indicates that both boundary layer and intraparticle diffusion are governing the rate of adsorption.

More importantly, the swelling of CO-BPFHC is limited and does not affect its structural integrity, as confirmed by its high thermal stability (TGA) and high surface area retention of > 93% after regeneration. Thus, the composite has an optimum balance of structural rigidity and dynamic swelling. This facilitates efficient internal mass transfer while providing mechanical stability during repeated adsorption–desorption cycles.

Zeta potential and particle size analysis

The pH-dependent zeta potential profile (Fig. 8) indicates a change in surface charge from highly positive at low pH values (ζ = + 21.4 mV at pH 1.5) through zero at the point of zero charge (pH 4.6) to highly negative at higher pH values (ζ = −34.8 mV at pH 10). At higher pH, both the electrostatic attraction is weakened (surface becomes negative, repelling CrO42−) and the reduction driving force decreases. Optimum pH values of 5.5 (Pb), 6.0 (Cd), and 2.5 (Cr) were selected for subsequent experiments.

Effect of temperature

The results of temperature experiments conducted in the range of 298 K to 328 K showed sharply different trends in the adsorption of cationic metal ions compared to anionic metal ions, as depicted in Fig. 17. For Pb(II) and Cd(II), qe increases with increasing temperature (e.g., qe(Pb) increases from 187.3 to 219.6 mg g−1 in the range of 298 K to 328 K). This behavior is consistent with an endothermic adsorption contribution, as ΔH° > 0 (Table 3). This can be explained by the increased flexibility of the polymer chains at increasing temperatures, thus exposing buried coordination sites in the urethane soft segments, and by the increased dehydra-tion of metal ions, thus facilitating coordination at the surface. Role of Polyurethane Matrix & Isotherm Interpretation

The adsorption capacity of CO-BPFHC is largely affected by the multifunctional effect of the polyurethane matrix as part of the composite structure. Besides functioning as a physical binder to the lignocellulosic fibers, the polyurethane matrix is actively involved in the adsorption mechanism through several mechanisms. Firstly, the presence of the urethane group –NH–COO– creates additional coordination sites with the capacity to coordinate with metal ions through the use of the lone pairs of the nitrogen and oxygen atoms, thereby enhancing the affinity of the composite toward metal ion adsorption. Second, the PU phase improves the dispersion and accessibility of cellulose fibers, preventing agglomeration and ensuring uniform exposure of active sites.

In addition, the PU matrix offers a level of hydrophilicity and swelling characteristics because of the polar functional groups, which helps the absorption of water and the transportation of hydrated metal ions into the composite. The effective use of the adsorption sites, particularly the interface between the fibers and polymer, is a result of the swelling characteristics. In addition, the organic structure of the PU matrix also contributes electron density, which is responsible for the reduction of Cr(VI) to Cr(III), thereby supporting the adsorption-reduction mechanism.

The models of the isotherm curves are consistent with the hybrid adsorption mechanism. The good correlation achieved by the Langmuir isotherm model(R2 = 0.9978–0.9989) indicates that the adsorption mechanism is dominated by the adsorption of a monolayer on energetically homogeneous sites, and this may be explained by the relatively homogeneous composition of functional groups on the surface of the PU-coated composite. Nevertheless, the fair correlation achieved by the Freundlich model (R2 ≈ 0.98) indicates the existence of surface heterogeneities resulting from the coexistence of the polymeric and lignocellulosic domains with different binding affinities.

Additionally, the Temkin model also supports the concept of adsorbate-adsorbent interaction through the gradual decrease of the adsorption energy with the degree of coverage on the adsorbent surface. Furthermore, the Dubinin-Radushkevich model shows the average adsorption energy (E = 3.66–5.07 kJ mol−1) corresponding to physisorption-assisted chemisorption, as supported by the combined effect of electrostatic interaction, coordination interaction, and redox reaction. The analysis of the isotherm results confirms the active contribution of the PU matrix towards the structural integrity as well as the adsorption process.

The positive values of the enthalpy changes (ΔH° = + 39.84 kJ mol−1 for Pb(II) and + 33.72 kJ mol−1 for Cd(II)) clearly indicate that the adsorption mechanism is endothermic in nature, requiring energy input during the adsorption process. Importantly, the values of the enthalpy changes provide critical insight into the nature of the adsorption mechanism. Enthalpy changes between 20–80 kJ mol−1 are commonly associated with chemisorption processes, while values of less than 20 kJ mol−1 are associated with weak physisorption.

In the existing mechanism, the high values of ΔH° imply that adsorption of Pb(II) and Cd(II)occurs mainly through chemisorption, where inner-sphere complexes are formed between the metal ions and the surface functional groups of CO-BPFHC. This involves partial dehydrating the hydrated metal ions of the type [M(H2O)n]2+ and altering the surface of the adsorbent, both of which are endothermic processes.

This conclusion is strongly supported by independent evidence from various experiments, including changes in FTIR band positions (Sect. "FTIR spectroscopy" and good fits of the pseudo-second-order kinetic model (Sect. "Adsorption kinetics modeling"), all of which are indicative of chemisorption. Therefore, the thermodynamic data, particularly the magnitude of ΔH°, provide direct and quantitative support for a chemisorption-dominated adsorption mechanism.

Conclusions

A novel castor oil derived bio polymer/fiber hybrid composite (CO-BPFHC) was successfully synthesized, characterized, and assessed for the simultaneous adsorptive removal of Pb(II), Cd(II), and Cr(VI) ions from simulated industrial wastewater. The salient findings of the study are as follows:

A novel CO-BPFHC was synthesized through a scalable three stage IPDI mediated urethane polymerization reaction on a sisal/jute scaffold to produce a highly thermally stable composite with hierarchically mesoporous texture (SBET = 418.3 m2 g−1, Vp = 1.29 cm3 g−1) and rich –COOH, –OH, and –NHCOO– surface functionality as confirmed through FTIR.

The primary contribution of this work lies in the development of an integrated multifunctional bio-based adsorbent capable of simultaneously addressing chemically distinct heavy metal pollutants through complementary adsorption pathways. Rather than relying solely on high adsorption capacity, the significance of CO-BPFHC arises from the combination of hierarchical porosity, multifunctional surface chemistry, and reduction-assisted Cr(VI) removal within a single sustainable composite framework.

A noteworthy and novel observation is the switchability of the CO-BPFHC surface with pH: at pH 2.5 (< pHpzc), the positively charged CO-BPFHC surface selectively binds Cr(VI) oxyanions; at pH 5.5–6.0 (> pHpzc), the negatively charged CO-BPFHC surface selectively binds cationic Pb(II)and Cd(II)species.

Maximum adsorption capacities for Pb, Cd, and Cr, based on the Langmuir isotherm model, were 591.4, 432.7, and 387.6 mg/g, respectively, and are among the highest reported for bio-based adsorbents, exceeding the most recently reported adsorbents for the removal of Cr(VI).

Adsorption kinetics followed the pseudo-spinning order model, and adsorption processes for Pb, Cd, and Cr(VI) were spontaneous and endothermic, except for the process for Cr(VI), which was spontaneous and exothermic—a mechanistic difference of considerable scientific significance, related to the different energy contributions to the two types of complexation.

CO-BPFHC demonstrated efficiencies higher than 92% for Pb/Cd and higher than 88% for Cr(VI) after five cycles of regeneration by the use of specifically developed eluent systems, confirming practical reusability and economic viability.The findings herein demonstrate CO-BPFHC to be a versatile, high capacity, and mechanistically distinct green adsorbent for the treatment of industrial wastewater containing multiple metals. Future research avenues involve the modeling and experimental investigation of CO-BPFHC in continuous fixed bed columns, industrial effluent treatment, life cycle assessments, and the investigation of CO-BPFHC for the development of electrochemical-assisted adsorption processes to boost the reduction efficiency of Cr(VI).
