Using All of Us data to examine the mental health change during COVID-19 pandemic among people living with HIV: A longitudinal study protocol
Authors: Shan Qiao, Jiajia Zhang, Chen Liang, Xiaoming Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe COVID-19 pandemic has negatively affected people’s mental health around the globe. Such effects may be especially compounded among some vulnerable populations such as people living with HIV (PLWH). However, large-scale data on mental health outcomes among PLWH are limited. Few studies have also identified potential protective factors for mental health outcomes.Methods and analysisGuided by theories of resilience and socioecology, we will leverage multiple datasets from National Institutes of Health’s Cloud-based All of Us Programme including electronic health records (EHRs), a series of COVID-19 Participant Experience (COPE) Surveys conducted from May 2020 to February 2021, and other self-reported survey data to achieve two specific aims: (1) to examine the mental health outcome changes during COVID-19 pandemic among PLWH by employing both EHR data (2018–2022) and survey data (2020–2021) in the All of Us Programme; and (2) to identify potential protective factors based on COPE Survey data for mental health outcomes at multiple socioecological levels including individual level (eg, coping strategy), interpersonal level (eg, social support) and health institutional level (eg, health service accessibility), adjusting for pre-existing health conditions including psychiatric disorders and other relevant factors (eg, COVID-19 infection). Interaction terms will be included in the multivariable analysis to identify different socially disadvantaged or stigmatised subgroups.Ethics and disseminationThe study has been approved by the institutional review board at the University of South Carolina (Pro00124044). Study findings will be disseminated through presentations at academic conferences and publications in peer-reviewed journals.

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY

This study will take advantage of the rich data from an existing large cohort of people living with HIV (PLWH) in the All of Us Programme through employing both electronic health record (EHR) data and survey data at the individual level, which will contribute to conducting comprehensive analysis of mental health outcomes through different lenses, exploring factors at various socioecological levels and increasing the implications of our findings in multiple settings of public health practices (eg, clinical settings and community settings).

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has negatively affected people’s mental health around the globe.1 2 Such effects may be especially compounded among some vulnerable populations such as people living with HIV (PLWH).3 4 Of the 1.2 million PLWH in the USA, over half are 50+ years of age with a high prevalence of non-communicable comorbidities.5 6 Compromised immune systems, ageing and coexisting chronic diseases such as diabetes, hypertension and lung diseases put many PLWH at high risk of severe illness from COVID-19.7 Disruption of HIV-related service, fears of comorbidities and COVID-19, elevation of stigma, financial hardship and anxiety towards the COVID-19 vaccination all contribute to exacerbated mental health outcomes among PLWH.8 9

In addition, the pandemic has exacerbated the existing mental health disparities endured by this population due to their socioeconomically disadvantaged situations and stigmatised social identities (eg, racial/ethnic minorities, sexual and gender minorities). Extant literature suggests disparities in mental health outcomes among PLWH by income, education, race/ethnicity, and sexual and gender identity.10–12 The pandemic has further aggregated the pre-existing mental health disparities and relevant risk factors of mental health outcomes. Recent studies report increased alcohol consumption, binge drinking and recreational drug use during the pandemic among men who have sex with men, including those living with HIV.13 14 Compared with their white counterparts, African American PLWH have been disproportionately affected by COVID-19 and are more likely to develop health complications and experience homelessness, unemployment and poverty.9 15–17 Transgender women living with HIV are almost five times more likely to suffer food insecurity than the general population.18

Addressing mental health and related disparities among PLWH during the COVID-19 pandemic has been prioritised by the Office of AIDS Research and National Institute of Mental Health as a key component of the response to this public health emergency. To inform tailored mental health promotion interventions among PLWH and the subgroups, it is critical to identify protective factors and resources such as resilience that can mitigate the negative impacts of COVID-19 pandemic on psychological well-being. Resilience in the face of adversity or stress is a necessary capacity for human development.19 Over the past three decades, the definition of resilience has evolved from the view of an individual personality trait, to an ecological view that resilience is a dynamic, multidimensional construct that incorporates bidirectional interaction between individuals and their environment (eg, family, peers, community, society).20 For PLWH, resilience could be enhanced by various protective factors including a range of problem-solving skills, self-confidence and positive future orientation, close relationships with competent and caring partners or family, and supportive healthcare settings.21–23 Extant literature suggests that resilience/protective factors exist at different levels (individual, interpersonal and institutional) and mitigate the negative effects of challenges during public emergencies and adverse events.24–28

Guided by theories of resilience and socioecology, we will leverage multiple datasets from All of Us including electronic health records (EHRs), a series of COVID-19 Participant Experience (COPE) Surveys conducted from May 2020 to February 2021, and other self-reported survey data (eg, Basics, Overall Health). Resilience is the capacity to which individuals are able to respond to stress-induced challenges and burdens.19 From the perspective of socioecology, it is also a bidirectional interaction between individuals, communities and social environment.20 Resilience can be cultivated through a series of protective factors such as social support, self-efficacy, positive self-perception and an optimistic perspective of the future.21–23 The extant literature demonstrates the essential role of protective factors in promotion and maintenance of resilience within the individual, community and the institutional levels in times of crisis including public emergencies.25–27 29 The overarching goal is to apply an adapted resilience conceptual framework30 in the context of COVID-19 and generate preliminary data to inform the design and delivery of resilience-based mental health promotion interventions among PLWH for addressing mental health disparities and improving their well-being in response to public health emergency. Specifically, we will (1) examine the trends and patterns of mental health outcomes among PLWH before and after COVID-19 outbreak by employing both EHR data and survey data in the All of Us Programme and explore how socially disadvantaged status (low income and low education) and stigmatised identities (racial/ethnic minorities, sexual and gender minorities) further affect mental health outcomes among PLWH; and (2) identify potential protective factors based on COPE Survey data for mental health outcomes at multiple socioecological levels including individual level (eg, resilience), interpersonal level (eg, social support) and health institutional level (eg, health service accessibility), adjusting for pre-existing health conditions including psychiatric disorders and other relevant factors (eg, COVID-19 infection). We will also conduct analysis for different socially disadvantaged or stigmatised subgroups to identify the factors that may be unique to different groups.

Methods

Multilevel resilience conceptual framework in the context of COVID-19

Our study will be guided by a multicomponent, multilevel conceptual framework (figure 1) that depicts challenges and vulnerabilities of PLWH in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, role of resilience development in mitigating negative effects on mental health outcomes and the key protective factors for mental health outcomes. Employing the socioecological model of human development31 and theories of resilience,30 32 33 we adapted an existing framework30 to be responsive to the context of the COVID-19 pandemic and existing health disparities with several inter-related focuses: (1) it posits dual challenges of PLWH when the COVID-19 and HIV epidemics collide by including both COVID-19-related impacts (eg, COVID-19 infection and vaccine uptake) and HIV-related factors (eg, viral suppression and pre-existing health conditions); (2) it suggests heterogeneity within the PLWH population and emphasises the synergistic vulnerabilities due to socioeconomical status and stigmatised identities, which implies the importance of identifying the most vulnerable subgroups to address mental health disparities; (3) it includes additional factors beyond individual levels (eg, social determinants of health (SDOH), quality of healthcare service) underscoring the need to engage all three essential entities (PLWH, family and community, health institutes) in resilience development, especially addressing structural disparities and providing supportive social contexts and resources to foster the development of PLWH’s internal assets; and (4) it focuses on mental health outcomes measured by two complementary approaches: psychiatric disorder diagnoses which are important outcomes in clinical settings and psychological assessments which have wide public health implications in mental health prevention and intervention in community settings. The framework provides a ‘blueprint’ that guides data extraction and measurement selection, the analytical plan, and results interpretation to inform resilience promotion strategies and mental health interventions for various subgroups among PLWH.

Data sources

The All of Us Programme plans to enrol a diverse group of at least 1 million persons (18 years of age or older) in the USA.34 The programme has collected various types of data such as EHR, survey responses, physical measurements, wearables data and genomic data from biospecimens from over 340 recruitment sites since 2018. As of 15 June 2022, over 344 000 participants completed all initial steps of the programme including consenting, agreeing to share EHR and completing the first three surveys (ie, Basics, Overall Health and Lifestyle). More than 80% of these participants are from groups that have been historically under-represented in biomedical research. With appropriate approvals, registered researchers can access deidentified data through three tiers of data access including Public Tier (contains only aggregate data), Registered Tier (contains individual-level EHR, wearables and survey data) and Controlled Tier (contains genomic data in addition to Registered Tier). We will use data at the Registered Tier in the proposed study.

EHR data

All participants are invited to share EHR with the programme. All of Us removes personal identifiers from participants’ EHR before adding the data to the Researcher Workbench. For the Registered Tier, EHR data are normalised by the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) Common Data Model (CDM). Additional personal data collected from All of Us participants, including socioeconomic status, are indexed by the All of Us Data Dictionary. EHR data used in our study will be longitudinal data extracted from 2018 to 2022. The extracted EHR data are a key source of outcome variables (psychiatric disorders) and are also used to define our study cohort (via HIV status).

Survey data

All participants are invited to complete surveys on the secure All of Us Participant Portal. Initial surveys include Basics, Overall Health and Lifestyle. Basics survey (28 questions) asks information on basic demographics, household size, health insurance, disability status, employment and income, and housing condition. Overall Health survey (21 questions) focuses on health literacy, general health condition, women’s health issues (if applicable) and history of transplant. Lifestyle survey (26 questions) collects information on smoking, alcohol and recreational drug use. Additional surveys on specific subjects are added regularly such as personal medical history, family medical history, and health access and utilisation. To understand the lived experience during the COVID-19 pandemic and its impact on mental and physical health among the US population, a series of COPE Surveys were conducted from May 2020 to February 2021 among a total of 100 340 participants (~30 000 to ~50 000 per survey). The COPE Survey, composed of 191 questions, can be used as a psychological assessment of mental health symptoms (eg, anxiety, loneliness, stress) since the survey adopts standardised scales to assess these mental health symptoms. For example, anxiety will be assessed by Generalised Anxiety Disorder-7,35 depression measured by Patient Health Questionnaire-936 and stress measured by Perceived Stress Scale-10.37 It also includes measures of potential factors that may influence mental health outcomes among PLWH. Data of Basics, Overall Health and Lifestyle surveys will be used as baseline data. The COPE Survey data, however, will be used as longitudinal data and extracted from May 2020 to February 2021. Survey data are also normalised by OMOP CDM.

Data processing and management

Data retrieval

Because data at the Registered Tier of All of Us are normalised by OMOP CDM, electronic phenotyping methods will be used to identify cohorts (ie, PLWH) from EHR.38 Specifically, all the study groups of interests are annotated by the relevant OMOP CDM concept sets. An OMOP CDM concept is a unique identifier that is often cross-mapped to different medical codes (eg, ICD, CPT, RxNorm) with the same semantic meaning but may be presented in different forms (eg, HIV-related conditions as coded differently in ICD, Nebraska Lexicon and clinical notes) and in different EHR systems.

Missing data treatment

Missing values are common challenges in datasets. People with missing demographics could be either included as a missing category, imputed or excluded from the analysis, depending on the missing rate. In order to capture the longitudinal visits and reduce the impact of missing data, we will split the time scale into 6-month intervals. If there is any mental health visit in the specific time interval, it will be coded as 1, else it will be coded as 0.

We will also use multiple imputation, including but not limited to mean/median imputation and multivariable regression as appropriate.39

Study cohorts

The study cohort for aim 1 (HIV cohort) will be PLWH identified through phenotyping based on the EHR data from 2018 to 2021 and data of Personal and Family Health History (614 questions about a participant’s medical history). For EHR data, the concept sets of the phenotyping are mainly the ones we have previously published in a study based on the N3C dataset (see appendix of the paper).40 We will modify the concept sets given the All of Us Project contains survey data in addition to EHR data. The logical procedures for phenotyping the PLWH cohort are as follows: first, to develop an operational definition of a cohort/measure. For example, to define ‘HIV’, we will determine diagnostic codes of HIV to include clinical procedures relevant to the treatment of HIV, medication prescriptions for HIV, annotated clinical notes suggestive of HIV and COPE Survey items indicating self-reported HIV. Second, to identify a set of OMOP CDM concepts that are suggestive of the operational definition of measure. We will collect required concepts via the ATHENA vocabulary, a controlled vocabulary that underpins phenotyping based on All of Us data. Third, to validate the developed concept sets. We plan to extract EHR records of 20 identified PLWH and invite the domain experts (clinicians in HIV) to label those samples. Then, we will compare those labels with our definition to evaluate the consistency rate. If it is less than 90%, we will refine the definition and repeat the process. The HIV cohort is estimated to have 4000–6000 participants.

There are six waves of COPE Survey data available now in All of Us (May, June, July, November, December 2020 and February 2021). Participants of the All of Us Project were allowed to take multiple COPE Surveys. Our initial analysis estimates there are 1411 PLWH with at least one survey response and 889 PLWH with at least two survey responses. For aim 2, PLWH with at least two COPE Survey responses will be included in the COPE cohort.

Key measures

Outcome measures

The mental health outcomes include psychiatric disorders and psychological assessment. The psychiatric disorders will be derived from ICD-10 (the 10th revision of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems) codes across six broad domains: mood disorders, anxiety disorders, substance use disorders, stress-related disorders, schizophrenia and personality disorders.41 The specific ICD-10 codes are available in the appendix of the cited paper. The psychological assessment will be based on existing standardised scales used by the COPE Surveys regarding anxiety and mood, stress, loneliness, substance abuse and general psychosocial well-being (table 1). The psychiatric disorder status variable will be coded as ‘yes’ (if presence of any relevant ICD-10 codes or a positive response to relevant survey items) or ‘no’ (neither presence of a relevant ICD-10 code nor a positive response to survey items). The psychiatric disorder status will be investigated in a 6-month interval from 2018 to 2022 (~9 intervals).

Sociodemographic measures

Demographic and basic socioeconomic characteristics include race/ethnicity, sex assigned at birth, gender, age, household income, education attainment, marital status, sexual orientation and physical disability. These variables are available from EHR data and the Basics survey as All of Us participants completed it at the time of their enrolment.

Protective factors

Protective factors at various socioecological levels include: (1) individual level: resilience, physical activity and adaptive coping; (2) interpersonal level: social support and social capital; and (3) institutional level: health access and utilisation. The data of these variables come from COPE Survey.

Contextual factors

The key contextual factors at various socioecological levels will be treated as covariates in modelling. These variables include: (1) COVID-19 pandemic indicator: no (for time points before 10 March 2020) and yes (for time points after 11 March 2020); (2) COVID-19-related factors: self-reported COVID-19-related symptoms, COVID-19 infection status, COVID-19 vaccine uptake and COVID-19 impacts on life; (3) pre-existing health conditions: chronic disease burden measured by the number (0, 1, ≥2) of chronic diseases conditions including myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, chronic pulmonary disease, diabetes without chronic complication, diabetes with chronic complication, renal disease, any malignancy; (4) discrimination during the COVID-19 pandemic; and (5) SDOH factors. Data on SDOH, neighbourhood and social life come from the Social Determinants of Health Survey (80 questions) conducted in 2021.

Statistical analyses

Preliminary analysis

We will calculate the prevalence rate of psychiatric disorders among PLWH cohort before and after the COVID-19 outbreak based on EHR data from the All of Us Database. Specifically, we will calculate the prevalence rate of psychiatric disorders in every 6 months from 2018 to 2022. The HIV cohort defined using developed algorithm will serve as denominator throughout all periods. We will count all diagnoses instead of new diagnosis in calculating the prevalence rate. Participants identified with psychiatric disorders before 10 March 2020 will be categorised as pre-pandemic, while participants with psychiatric disorders after 11 March 2020 will be considered as peri-pandemic. We will conduct preliminary analyses and examine descriptive statistics for key variables including proportion of PLWH with psychiatric disorders in pre-pandemic and peri-pandemic, sociodemographics, pre-existing conditions and COVID-19 infection status. Associations between primary outcomes and these background variables will be assessed using appropriate procedures (eg, tests of proportions, Χ2 tests, analysis of variance).

Examine mental health outcomes

Mental health outcomes (defined as whether there is mental health visit in each time interval) during the COVID-19 pandemic will be modelled via generalised logistic mixed model. First, the random term will be included to account the repeated measure within each individual. COVID-19 infection status will address change of mental health outcomes (ie, psychiatric disorders) due to COVID-19 infection status. Second, to investigate whether disparities in mental health outcomes by key demographic factors and pre-existing conditions have widened during the pandemic, we will further adjust for sex, gender, age, marital status, COVID-19 infection and underlying health conditions such as hypertension and diabetes. Third, variables related to socioecological disadvantages (ie, income, education) and stigmatised identities (ie, race/ethnicity, sexual and gender minority) will be added to examine how these factors further affect mental health outcomes among PLWH. The final model will be compared based on Akaike information criterion (AIC) or Bayesian information criterion (BIC), which will penalise the dimension of the model. The smaller AIC or BIC will indicate better fit. When there are minimum differences between different models, we will combine domain expert on model selection which considers the easy interpretation in practice.

Identification of protective factors

Multilevel hierarchical regression will be used to identify protective factors of mental health outcomes (ie, depression, anxiety) measured by COPE Survey. We will build the full model by adjusting for pandemic indicator, demographics, other covariates/contextual factors and protective factors at multiple socioecological levels (eg, resilience, physical activities, social support, health access and utilisation). In the modelling procedure, the outliers, missingness, multicollinearity and non-linear will be assessed accordingly, and sensitivity analysis will be conducted comparing models with or without outliers or missing data. The variables for final full model will be selected via the stepwise selection procedure.

Interaction analysis

To explore the different impacts of protective factors on mental health outcomes across subgroups, we will include interaction variables in the multivariable analysis. These variables could be the interaction between the protective factors and category variables as qualifiers of vulnerable subgroups, for example, racial/ethnic minority groups, sexual and gender minority groups (ie, lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, intersex and asexual), low-income group (annual household income <US$35 000) and low-education group (less than a high school degree or some high school). The likelihood ratio test will be used to examine the interaction effects.

Ethics and dissemination

The study was approved by the institutional review board at the University of South Carolina (Pro00124044). This study meets the Not Human Subject criteria set forth by the Code of Federal Regulations (45 CFR 46) of: (a) the specimens and/or private information/data were not collected specifically for the currently proposed research project through an interaction/intervention with living individuals OR (b) the investigator(s) including collaborators on the proposed research cannot readily ascertain the identity of the individual(s) to whom the coded private information or specimens pertain. Study findings will be presented at relevant workshops (All of Us Project workshops), academic conferences and published in peer-reviewed journals.

Patient and public involvement

No patient involved.

Discussion

Extant literature has explored risk factors of exaggerated mental health problems among PLWH in the context of COVID-19, but few studies also have identified potential protective factors that can mitigate the negative impacts of COVID-19 on mental health outcomes. The identification of these protective factors is critical for developing tailored health promotion interventions to reduce mental health disparities among PLWH. Guided by the socioecological theories, we will go beyond individual-level resilience to explore protective factors at interpersonal, community and institutional levels. The multilevel approach emphasised in the proposed study will address the potential limitations of an ‘individual resilience only’ approach (eg, lack of cultural reflection on individualism, victim blaming) and comprehensively address mental health needs of PLWH through identifying key resources at all socioecological levels.

Our study has several limitations. First, COPE Survey was launched since the COVID-19 outbreak; therefore, we will not have survey data before the pandemic. Second, psychiatric disorders were defined through existing ICD-10 codes, which may underestimate the prevalence rate of psychiatric disorders. Third, some aggregated (ie, county-level) key SDOH factors were not able to be integrated into the dataset given the unavailable information on zip code in All of Us data. Fourth, participants in the All of Us Programme were required to complete three basic surveys (ie, Basics, Overall Health, Lifestyle) at their enrolment, but they might not finish other survey questionnaires. It might be the case the information extracted from the three basic surveys was not consistent with the EHR data. However, we use the three basic survey data as baseline. Finally, shifting dates in EHR data could be an issue. To protect participants’ confidentiality, all dates associated with a participant are shifted backwards by a random number between 1 and 365. The shift is constant for each participant so that the temporality of events is preserved. However, caution is needed in result interpretation.

Regardless of these potential limitations, our study will take advantage of the rich data from an existing large cohort of PLWH in the All of Us Programme through employing both EHR data and survey data at the individual level. The combination of multitype data will contribute to conducting comprehensive analysis for mental health outcomes through different lenses, exploring factors at various socioecological levels and increasing the implications of our findings in multiple settings of public health practices (eg, clinical settings and community settings).

Our study will investigate the trend of mental health outcomes among PLWH during the COVID-19 pandemic across various phases and may inform temporal pattern of mental health challenges during the public health emergency. The finding may be applied in public health resource allocation in COVID-19 and beyond. For example, to help the healthcare system to be better prepared for more patients seeking mental health treatment due to the stress caused by COVID-19 infection and/or social context of the pandemic. Our study also highlights heterogeneous experiences of PLWH during COVID-19 pandemic, and the research findings thus will promote precision medicine in HIV treatment and care. Given that populations historically underserved (eg, racial/ethnic minorities) disproportionately bear the HIV epidemic burden, it is not surprising to identify subgroups among PLWH who have suffered multiple health disparities (eg, gender, race, sexual orientation) during the pandemic due to existing low socioeconomic status, transportation barriers and limited infrastructure. The identification of socially disadvantaged or stigmatised subgroups will inform tailored health interventions to improve mental health outcomes and reduce disparities across various disadvantaged subgroups among PLWH.

In summary, we will comprehensively investigate protective factors at multiple socioecological levels and inform tailored health interventions to improve mental health outcomes among PLWH, especially those from various disadvantaged subgroups. Our goal is aligned with the public health research priorities in terms of social sciences studies and health disparities reduction. The study will leverage existing National Institutes of Health investment (on All of Us Programme), capitalise on a rapid understanding of mental health needs among PLWH, stimulate additional collaborations with the All of Us Programme and promote the translation of All of Us data to public health implications. The experience and preliminary data obtained from this supplement will position us for further efforts in using All of Us data to improve the mental and other health outcomes of PLWH in the USA.
