Integrated discovery and mechanistic characterization of xanthine oxidase inhibitory peptides from sheep Milk
Authors: Tai Zhang, Yisuo Liu, Yuan Shi, Yiya Wang, Ruibin Zhu, Chengye Liu, Hongzhao Sun, Huaxi Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHyperuricemia is rising worldwide, with xanthine oxidase (XOD) as a key therapeutic target. This study integrated peptidomics, machine learning, molecular dynamics simulations, and experimental validation to discover XOD-inhibitory peptides from sheep milk. A VHSE-CatBoost model screened milk peptides and identified four candidates: FAWP, GPGGAW, FGER, and YPF. All peptides inhibited XOD dose-dependently, with FAWP showing the strongest activity (IC₅₀ = 12.99 mM). Kinetic analysis showed mixed-type inhibition for FAWP and FGER, and competitive inhibition for GPGGAW and YPF. Thermodynamics demonstrated spontaneous FAWP-XOD binding (ΔG = −6.0 kcal/mol). UV and CD spectra indicated perturbation of aromatic microenvironments and reduced α-helix/β-sheet content in XOD. MD simulations showed that FAWP, GPGGAW, and FGER stably occupy the active site through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. Network pharmacology further suggested multi-target effects on inflammation and renal pathways. These sheep milk-derived peptides offer promising natural agents for hyperuricemia management.Keywords: Sheep milk, Bioactive peptides, Xanthine oxidase, Machine learning

Introduction

In recent years, the prevalence of hyperuricemia has increased rapidly worldwide, driven largely by dietary shifts toward high-purine foods such as seafood and beer. Hyperuricemia, often referred to as the “fourth high” following hyperglycemia, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia, has surpassed diabetes in prevalence, with a global annual increase rate of 8.7%. This trend is particularly pronounced in China, where prevalence has risen from 8.5% to 18.7% (She et al., 2022), and is increasingly observed in younger populations. Persistent uric acid supersaturation leads to monosodium urate crystal deposition in joints, kidneys, and blood vessels, causing gout, kidney stones, and obstructive nephropathy (Shu et al., 2022). Epidemiological studies further link hyperuricemia with fatty liver, metabolic syndrome, hypertension, insulin resistance, obesity, and type 2 diabetes (Zha et al., 2022).

Current hyperuricemia therapy mainly targets uric acid synthesis and excretion with XOD as a key therapeutic target. Clinically used XOD Inhibitors such as allopurinol, febuxostat, and topiroxostat effectively lower uric acid levels but are limited by adverse effects and variable patient responses, including severe skin reactions and hepatic or gastrointestinal toxicity (Schumacher Jr. et al., 2008). Uricosuric drugs such as probenecid and benzbromarone also present renal safety concerns and reduced efficacy in patients with impaired kidney function (Afinogenova et al., 2022). Consequently, natural uric acid-lowering compounds have attracted increasing attention (Liu et al., 2024). Bioactive peptides derived from food sources, such as dairy products, seafood, nuts, and grains, have been shown to inhibit XOD activity, regulate uric acid transporters, and exhibit anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties (Li et al., 2024; Li, Zhang, Abubaker, Shu and Liu, 2025). Compared to traditional pharmaceuticals, these natural peptides offer higher safety, lower toxicity, and better absorption, positioning them as promising candidates for the next generation of uric acid-lowering interventions. Sheep milk contains higher dry matter, protein, and fat than cow or goat milk, giving a macronutrient profile closer to human milk and providing an excellent source of bioactive peptides (Zhu et al., 2025). Recent studies have demonstrated that peptides derived from sheep milk exhibit significant antihypertensive and antidiabetic activities (Pipaliya et al., 2025). In addition, many bioactive peptides from sheep milk demonstrate antimicrobial and immunomodulatory activities, showing potential for the prevention and treatment of various diseases (Atanasova & Ivanova, 2010). Moreover, some peptides in fermented sheep milk have demonstrated ACE inhibitory activity, making them strong candidates for antihypertensive therapy (Dalabasmaz et al., 2023). To our knowledge, systematic studies on XOD-inhibitory peptides specifically derived from sheep milk are limited.

Due to the complex structure and mechanisms of XOD, traditional experimental methods are inefficient for screening potent inhibitory peptides. Computational approaches, including machine learning, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics, offer faster and more cost-effective alternatives (Chen et al., 2024). Machine learning efficiently predicts potential bioactive peptides from large datasets but depends on data quality and cannot reveal detailed interaction mechanisms (Bustillo et al., 2022). Molecular docking estimates binding affinity and identifies candidate peptides but cannot capture dynamic structural changes in physiological environments (Yang et al., 2025). The combining machine learning with molecular docking can overcome the limitations of both methods. Machine learning can predict potential bioactive peptides by learning from extensive peptide-target interaction data and generate an initial list of candidate peptides for molecular docking (Li et al., 2025). Incorporating molecular dynamics further analyzes dynamic interactions, validating and optimizing screening results for higher reliability (Jia et al., 2024).

In this study, the sheep milk-derived peptides as XOD inhibitors were systematic investigated using an integrated machine learning and molecular docking approach. The peptides generated from LC-MS profiling and in silico hydrolysis were screened using a VHSE-CatBoost model, followed by high-throughput molecular docking to prioritize candidates with strong binding potential. The inhibitory activity, binding mechanisms, and potential multi-target effects were further elucidated through enzyme kinetics, spectroscopic analysis, molecular dynamics simulations, and network pharmacology. Overall, this work presents a comprehensive strategy integrating peptidomics, machine learning, computational modeling, and experimental validation for the discovery and mechanistic characterization of bioactive XOD-inhibitory peptides from sheep milk.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Fresh sheep milk was collected from a local farm and stored at 4 °C prior to processing. LC-MS grade acetonitrile (ACN) and formic acid were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA). Allopurinol (>98%) was obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology (China). Dithiothreitol (DTT) and iodoacetamide (IAM) were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich (USA), and trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical (China). Deionized water was prepared using a Milli-Q system (USA), and all other chemicals were of analytical grade.

Identification of endogenous peptides in sheep milk by Nano LC-MS/MS

Endogenous peptides in fresh sheep milk were identified following a modified method (Ning et al., 2024). Briefly, 200 μL of milk was filtered through a 3 kDa ultrafiltration membrane, centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the peptide concentration was measured at 205 nm. Peptides were reduced with 2 μL of 1 M DTT (10 mM final) at 56 °C for 1 h, and alkylated with 4 μL of 1 M IAM (20 mM final) at room temperature for 40 min. Excess IAM was neutralized with DTT and acidified with TFA. Desalting was done using C18 Stage-Tips, and peptides were eluted with 50% ACN/0.1% TFA. Nano LC-MS/MS analysis was conducted on a C18 reversed-phase system with a gradient of 0–95% B (ACN with 0.1% formic acid) over 66 min at a flow rate of 600 nL/min. MS data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition mode, with full MS resolution of 70,000 and MS/MS resolution of 17,500. De novo peptide sequencing was performed using PEAKS software with specified parameters for modifications, peptide mass tolerance, and fragment mass tolerance.

Preparation of major sheep milk protein sequences and in silico multi-enzyme hydrolysis

Four caseins (κ-, αs1-, αs2-, β-casein), two whey proteins (α-lactalbumin, β-lactoglobulin), serum albumin (BSA), and lactoferrin were retrieved from UniProt (κ-casein: P02669; αs1-casein: P04653; αs2-casein: P04654; β-casein: P11839; α-lactalbumin: P09462; β-lactoglobulin: P67976; BSA: P14639; lactoferrin: R9QXS6). These proteins were subjected to in silico enzymatic hydrolysis using the BIOPEP-UWM database (Bai et al., 2025) with nine commercial proteases: animal-derived (chymotrypsin, trypsin, pepsin, elastase), microbial-derived (Proteinase K, subtilisin), and plant-derived (papain, bromelain, ficin). A total of 129 single-, double-, and triple-enzyme hydrolysis patterns were applied to simulate in vivo digestion, generating a diverse peptide set for subsequent XOD inhibitory screening.

Machine learning model development

Balanced dataset and descriptors for XOD peptides

A balanced binary classification dataset was constructed for developing machine learning models to predict XOD inhibitory peptides. The positive set comprised 202 experimentally validated XOD-inhibitory peptides collected from published literature (Table S1). An equal number of negative samples (202) were selected from three sources (Table S2), specifically non-inhibitory peptides extracted from the BIOPEP-UWM database, peptides reported in the literature as lacking XOD inhibitory activity, and randomly selected peptides with PeptideRanker scores below 0.1 (indicating low probability of bioactivity). This approach ensured the negative set represented peptides unlikely to exhibit the target activity while maintaining dataset balance (Chen et al., 2024). To characterize peptides, we used seven amino acid descriptors (ZScale, ISA-ECI, FASGAI, DPPS, PseAAC, VHSE, MS-WHIM) that capture physicochemical and spatial properties, converting each sequence into a multidimensional feature matrix for machine learning. The balanced dataset (404 samples) was used for model training, hyperparameter tuning, and performance evaluation via cross-validation, with stratified sampling to preserve the 1:1 class ratio throughout the process.

Machine learning model construction

Following previous reports (Ileri, 2025), four ensemble learning algorithms CatBoost, GBDT, LightGBM, and XGBoost were selected for peptide prediction. These algorithms were configured with optimized hyperparameters: CatBoost with a depth of 6, learning rate of 0.05, and 1000 iterations; GBDT with 200 estimators and early stopping; LightGBM with num_leaves = 31 and max_bin = 255; and XGBoost with max_depth = 5 and a learning rate of 0.01. All models were evaluated using 5-fold stratified cross-validation on the balanced dataset (n = 404). The dataset was randomly partitioned into five folds while preserving the 1:1 class ratio. In each fold, the model was trained on 80% of the data (n = 323) and validated on the remaining 20% (n = 81). Performance metrics-including accuracy, sensitivity (recall), precision, F1-score, and AUC-ROC, were calculated for each fold and averaged across all folds.

Virtual screening of XOD inhibitors from sheep milk

A peptide library derived from endogenous peptides identified by LC-MS and in silico hydrolysis of sheep milk proteins was first filtered to retain only sequences of 3–12 amino acids. The filtered peptides were encoded numerically using the VHSE descriptor, resulting in a uniform structural feature matrix that captured their physicochemical and spatial properties. The pre-trained CatBoost classifier predicted the probability score (ranging from 0 to 1) for each peptide, indicating its potential to inhibit XOD. Peptides with scores above 0.8 were selected as high-confidence candidates for subsequent molecular docking and in vitro validation.

Molecular docking screening of XOD inhibitory peptides

Candidate peptides selected from the VHSE-CatBoost model were further evaluated using molecular docking. Peptide 3D structures were generated from N- to C-terminus using BioPython and energy-minimized in PyMOL. The XOD crystal structure (PDB ID: 3NVW) was prepared by removing ligands and water molecules, adding polar hydrogens, and assigning Gasteiger charges in AutoDock Tools. Docking was performed with AutoDock Vina (exhaustiveness = 8, num_modes = 5) using a grid box centered at the active site. Peptides with docking affinities ≤ −8.0 kcal/mol were considered potential high-affinity candidates. The top docking poses were selected for subsequent molecular dynamics simulations.

Peptide synthesis and XOD inhibition assay

Selected peptides were synthesized by solid-phase peptide synthesis, and their purity (≥95%) was confirmed by HPLC and mass spectrometry. XOD inhibitory activity was determined using the XOD Activity Assay Kit with WST-8 (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, Catalog No. S0111S) based on WST-8 reduction by superoxide anions, with absorbance measured at 450 nm (Hille et al., 2014). Allopurinol served as a positive control. For inhibition kinetics, XOD (0.04 U/mL) was pre-incubated with YPF (0, 10, 20, 40 mM), GPGGAW, FAWP, or FGER (0, 10, 20, 30 mM) at 37 °C for 30 min. The reaction was started by adding xanthine (25–200 μM), and the increase in absorbance at 290 nm was monitored. Inhibition types were analyzed using Lineweaver-Burk plots.

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC)

The interaction between peptide FAWP and XOD was analyzed using a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC instrument at 25 °C. The concentration of FAWP in the syringe was 2.0 mg/mL, while XOD in the sample cell was adjusted to 0.10 mg/mL, while the reference cell contained buffer. Following the method described by Ma et al. (2025) with slight modification, an initial injection of 0.4 μL was discarded, followed by 18 injections of 2 μL each. The cell was stirred at 500 rpm throughout the experiment. Heat signals were integrated and corrected for dilution heat when necessary. Binding parameters, including the stoichiometry (n), dissociation constant (Kd), and thermodynamic parameters (ΔH, ΔS, ΔG), were obtained by fitting the data using the MicroCal PEAQ-ITC analysis software.

UV absorption spectroscopy

To investigate peptide-induced conformational changes in XOD, UV absorption spectra were recorded. XOD and each peptide (both at 0.5 mg/mL) were mixed at a 1:1 (v/v) ratio. An XOD solution in which the peptide was replaced with an equal volume of buffer served as the control. The samples were transferred into a quartz cuvette with a 1 cm path length, and UV spectra were recorded using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer UV1700 (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The scanning wavelength range was set from 220 to 300 nm with a step interval of 1 nm. The same buffer was used as the blank for baseline correction during measurements.

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy

Far-UV CD spectra (190–260 nm) of XOD and XOD-peptide complexes were recorded on a JASCO J-1500 spectropolarimeter using a 0.1-cm quartz cuvette (Hou et al., 2022). XOD (0.10 mg/mL) was mixed with peptide solution (2 mg/mL) at a 9:1 (v/v) ratio, resulting in a final peptide concentration of approximately 0.2 mg/mL in the mixture, and incubated for 1 h at room temperature before measurement. Spectra were collected with 1.0 nm bandwidth, 2.0 nm·s−1 scan rate, and 0.5 s response time. Each spectrum was the average of at least three independent scans. The buffer spectrum was subtracted as baseline, and the data were smoothed using the instrument's built-in software when necessary. Secondary structure contents (α-helix, β-sheet, β-turn, and random coil) were estimated using the CDSSTR algorithm on the DICHROWEB server.

Molecular dynamic (MD) simulations

To investigate the stability and dynamic interactions of the selected peptides with XOD, MD simulations were performed. The XOD-peptide complexes selected from the molecular docking results in Section 2.6. MD simulations of XOD-peptide complexes were performed using GROMACS v2022.03 with the amber99sb-ildn force field (Zhang et al., 2023). Complexes were solvated in TIP3P water in a dodecahedral box (15 Å from the complex), and Na+/Cl− ions were added to neutralize and reach 0.1 M saline. Energy minimization was performed for 50,000 steps, followed by 200 ps NVT and NPT equilibration at 300 K. Production runs were 200 ns with a 2 fs timestep. Trajectories were analyzed for RMSD, RMSF, Rg, hydrogen bonds, and MM-PBSA binding free energies using gmx-MMPBSA with adaptive Poisson-Boltzmann calculations.

Network pharmacology analysis

Potential hyperuricemia-related targets of the XOD-inhibitory peptides were predicted using the Swiss Target Prediction database (https://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/). Hyperuricemia-associated targets were retrieved from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/). The overlapping targets were subjected to protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/), and the resulting network was visualized and analyzed with Cytoscape 3.10.0. Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed on the overlapping targets using the R packages clusterProfiler, org.Hs.eg.db, and enrichplot (Homo sapiens). Targets with high connectivity (> 8) in the PPI network were prioritized for visualization and interpretation.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical differences were evaluated using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test in SPSS software, with significance defined as P < 0.05. Data visualization and figure preparation were conducted using GraphPad Prism.

Results and discussion

Physicochemical characteristics of endogenous sheep milk peptides

The total ion chromatogram of sheep milk–derived endogenous peptides (Fig. 1A) showed broad distribution across 0–66 min, with major peaks at 22.25, 22.38, and 27.07 min corresponding to hydrophobic constituents. De novo sequencing identified 9918 peptides, mainly from caseins and whey proteins cleaved by endogenous proteases, with sheep milk containing significantly more peptides than bovine milk, indicating higher bioactive potential (Ning et al., 2023).

Characterization of endogenous peptides from sheep milk. (A) Total ion chromatogram of sheep milk peptides. (B) Molecular weight distribution of peptides. (C) Peptide length distribution. (D) Amino acid frequency distribution. (E) Amino acid frequency by peptide length group.

The molecular weight distribution of the peptides (Fig. 1B) showed that 85% were below 1.5 kDa, with most under 1 kDa. This low molecular weight favors XOD inhibition, as smaller peptides can more effectively access the enzyme's narrow catalytic pocket, while larger peptides may face steric hindrance (Zhong et al., 2021). Peptide lengths ranged from 2 to ≥30 residues, peaking at 8–9 residues, with 77.7% within the 3–12 residue range (Fig. 1C). This size range is ideal for bioactivity screening, as these peptides maintain flexibility and solubility, allowing efficient engagement with the XOD active site (Wang et al., 2025). These results suggested that the endogenous low–molecular weight peptides in sheep milk form a promising pool of potential XOD inhibitors. The amino acid composition (Fig. 1D) showed that hydrophobic residues and aromatic residues were abundant, which are important for XOD inhibition due to the nonpolar nature of the catalytic pocket (Zhao et al., 2022). The combination of hydrophobic, aromatic, and charged residues provides a favorable chemical framework for potent XOD-inhibitory peptides. Peptide hydrophobicity analysis (Fig. 1E) revealed a subset with strong hydrophobic character, enhancing interactions with XOD's hydrophobic regions. Although smaller than the predominantly hydrophilic population, this fraction represents a key pool of candidates for strong inhibition (Bi et al., 2025).

Characteristics of peptides generated via in silico hydrolysis

In silico hydrolysis produced a library rich in short peptides, with over 69% being no longer than 5 residues; tripeptides were the most abundant group (Fig. 2A). The total number of peptides released from each major milk protein generally corresponded to its molecular weight. Lactoferrin and serum albumin yielded the highest counts, followed by caseins and whey proteins (Fig. 2B). Despite these differences in yield, proteins such as caseins (Gao et al., 2023), β-lactoglobulin and α-lactalbumin are known to release peptides with potent XOD-inhibitory activity, confirming that hydrolytic output is not a direct indicator of bioactive potential.

To identify high-yield hydrolysis schemes, the top ten enzyme combinations from 129 in silico patterns were selected for each sheep-milk protein, and enzyme occurrence frequencies are summarized in Fig. 2C. Proteinase K and ficin were the most prevalent, appearing across high-yield schemes for nearly all proteins. Proteinase K, a broad-specificity serine protease favoring cleavage near aromatic residues, frequently cleaved κ-casein, αs1-casein, and β-lactoglobulin, generating short hydrophobic/aromatic peptides associated with XOD inhibition (Paudyal et al., 2022). Ficin predominated for αs2-casein, lactoferrin, and serum albumin, producing Trp- and Phe-rich fragments suitable for the hydrophobic XOD catalytic pocke (Zhang, Jiang, Lei, Yan and Chang, 2023). Pancreatic elastase, stem bromelain, and chymotrypsin also contributed complementary cleavage, particularly for whey proteins, releasing aromatic-rich peptides commonly found in XOD inhibitors (Wang et al., 2025). Less frequent enzymes, such as papain and subtilisin, aided lactoferrin and serum albumin cleavage. Overall, proteinase K and ficin served as core enzymes, with others expanding peptide diversity for subsequent XOD-inhibitory screening.

To evaluate the XOD-inhibitory potential of in silico–generated peptides, a bubble plot (Fig. 2D) was constructed using peptide length (x-axis), mean hydrophobicity (y-axis), and aromatic residue proportion (bubble size) based on reported features of food-derived XOD inhibitors (Wang et al., 2025). Most peptides were short (3–8 residues), with broad hydrophobicity (−4.0 to +4.0) and variable aromatic content. Notably, short, hydrophobic peptides tended to be aromatic-rich, favoring accommodation and stabilization in XOD's largely nonpolar active site (Yang et al., 2025). These results indicate that the in silico peptide set provides a suitable focused library for XOD-inhibitor screening.

Machine learning optimization for XOD inhibitory peptide

To identify potential XOD inhibitory peptides from in silico and de novo derived libraries, four machine learning algorithms—LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost, and GBDT—were evaluated using seven amino acid descriptor sets. Model performance was assessed via Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1 score, and ROC AUC (Fig. 3A). AUC values above 0.85 are generally considered indicative of reliable discrimination between active and inactive peptides (Madsen et al., 2022). The use of FASGAI, ISA_ECI, PseAAC, MSWHIM, or Z-scale descriptors yielded higher predictive accuracy than DPPS descriptors (Fig. S1). The confusion matrices for all four models were presented in Fig. S2. The CatBoost model with VHSE descriptors achieved the highest performance (Fig. 3B), with superior Precision and F1 scores. VHSE descriptors, integrating hydrophobicity, steric volume, and electronic properties, effectively captured features relevant to XOD inhibition, including hydrophobic and aromatic interactions (Wang et al., 2025). Compared to LightGBM and XGBoost, CatBoost offered superior stability and interpretability, with its ordered boosting and efficient encoding reducing overfitting on smaller datasets (Hancock & Khoshgoftaar, 2020). Consequently, the CatBoost–VHSE model was selected to screen the in silico hydrolyzed library, enriching for peptides with hydrophobic cores and aromatic residues while minimizing false positives, thus providing a high-confidence candidate set for experimental validation. It should be noted that the number of experimentally validated XOD-inhibitory peptides currently reported in the literature remains limited. The positive dataset used in this study (n = 202) represents all such peptides we could identify through comprehensive literature searching. This data scarcity is a common challenge in the field and may affect model generalizability. Future accumulation of more experimentally validated data will be crucial for developing more robust predictive models.

Integrated screening and identification of XOD-inhibitory peptides

The XOD-inhibitory potential of peptides from in silico digestion and LC–MS/MS identification was evaluated using the VHSE–CatBoost model. Most peptides scored low (0.05–0.15), but 538 with probabilities >0.8 were selected for further analysis (Fig. 3C). High-affinity peptides were enriched in aromatic residues, especially Phe and Trp (1.71- and 2.20-fold, p < 0.01), promoting π–π stacking and hydrophobic interactions, while Thr and Gly likely contributed to hydrogen bonding and backbone flexibility (Fig. 3D) (Xu et al., 2024).

Binding energy analysis suggested a clear length-dependent trend (Fig. 3E), with short (3–5 residues; −9.7 to −8.0 kcal/mol), medium (6–10 residues; −7.9 to −5.0 kcal/mol), and long peptides (>10 residues; −6.5 to −4.1 kcal/mol) exhibiting strong, moderate, and weak affinity, respectively (Liu et al., 2024). A few longer peptides (11–12 residues) maintained moderate affinity (−6 kcal/mol), suggesting that favorable residue positioning can offset reduced flexibility. The VHSE–CatBoost model predicted the likelihood of peptides acting as XOD inhibitors using VHSE descriptors, which capture hydrophobic, steric, and electronic properties of amino acid (Mei et al., 2005). While effective at identifying global physicochemical patterns, such QSAR models lack structural context and cannot determine whether a peptide can access or stably bind the XOD active site (Zhao, He and Ashaolu, 2026). To improve reliability, molecular docking with AutoDock Vina was performed on the 538 peptides, integrating spatial and energetic information with machine-learning predictions. Docking revealed clear stratification of binding affinities, with the top ten peptides exhibiting strong interactions (−9.7 to −8.9 kcal/mol; Fig. 3F), including FAWP, FGER, TNAYF, YPF, and GPGGAW. All top peptides exceeded the −7 kcal/mol threshold typically considered indicative of strong XOD binding (Chen et al., 2024). Mechanistically, high-affinity peptides were enriched in aromatic and hydrophobic residues, which inserted into XOD's hydrophobic pocket and formed π–π interactions near the molybdopterin center (Hou et al., 2022). However, these docking results are computational predictions, experimental validation is required to confirm actual XOD inhibitory activity.

Experimental validation of candidate XOD-inhibitory peptides

The five peptides predicted by VHSE–CatBoost and AutoDock Vina were synthesized with >95% purity (Fig. S3) and tested for XOD inhibition. All peptides showed dose-dependent inhibition (Fig. 4A-E), with FAWP being the most potent (IC₅₀ = 12.99 ± 0.33 mM), followed by GPGGAW (15.51 ± 0.35 mM), FGER (16.90 ± 0.14 mM), and YPF (20.98 ± 0.72 mM; Fig. 4F). As a positive control, allopurinol demonstrated potent dose-dependent inhibition with an IC₅₀ of 21.10 ± 2.23 μM (Fig. S4). The hydrophobic XOD active site favored aromatic residues, which enhanced binding through π–π stacking and hydrophobic interactions, likely explaining the strong inhibition by FAWP and GPGGAW (Liu et al., 2024). FAWP's C-terminal Trp appeared to provide terminal aromatic stabilization (Zhao et al., 2023), whereas GPGGAW's longer glycine-rich backbone reduced penetration. Charged residues, as in FGER, further enhanced binding via electrostatic interactions (Meng et al., 2024). YPF showed moderate activity, likely due to its smaller interaction surface and lower flexibility. TNAYF inhibited less than 50% even at the highest concentration, suggesting that rigid docking did not fully capture dynamic binding, with backbone flexibility and XOD's solution-phase movements likely reducing binding stability (Vidal-Limon et al., 2022).

Although the IC₅₀ of FAWP against XOD was determined to be in the millimolar range, this potency was comparable to many food-derived XOD-inhibitory peptides. Wang et al. (2025), reported that IC₅₀ values for such peptides typically ranged from 3 mM to 25 mM. Within this context, FAWP and GPGGAW fell in the mid-to-upper range, outperforming peptides from tuna (He et al., 2019) and macadamia nuts (Zhao et al., 2022). For food and nutraceutical applications, millimolar activity could still be meaningful, as bioactive peptides could be used at relatively high concentrations in functional foods, and their natural origin, safety, and possible synergy with other components might contribute to in vivo hypouricemic effects. However, their potency remained modest compared to synthetic drugs like allopurinol. Further optimization would likely be needed to develop more effective nutraceutical ingredients.

Inhibition types of different peptides on XOD

Lineweaver-Burk plots were used to characterize the inhibition modes of four peptides on XOD. FAWP and FGER exhibited mixed-type inhibition (Fig. 4G and H). The double-reciprocal lines intersected in the second quadrant, indicating simultaneous increases in apparent Km and decreases in Vmax. This pattern suggested that both peptides could bind to the free enzyme as well as the enzyme-substrate complex. The presence of Trp in FAWP and Arg in FGER likely contributed to this mixed behavior by enabling additional hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions at both catalytic and allosteric sites, which resulted in a stronger non-competitive component, particularly evident for FGER at high substrate concentrations (Chen et al., 2023). GPGGAW and YPF displayed classical competitive inhibition (Fig. 4I and J). The lines intersected on the y-axis, showing unchanged Vmax but increased apparent Km with rising inhibitor concentrations. This kinetic profile implied that the peptides competed directly with xanthine for the active site. The presence of Pro together with aromatic residues (Phe in both, Tyr in YPF) might facilitate specific hydrophobic and π-stacking interactions within the substrate-binding pocket, consistent with competitive binding (Zhang et al., 2023).

Thermodynamic analysis of FAWP–XOD interaction

ITC is a powerful method for directly quantifying the thermodynamic parameters of molecular interactions. Among the five identified peptides, FAWP exhibited the strongest inhibitory activity and was thus selected as the representative for thermodynamic analysis. As shown in Fig. 5A, successive injections of FAWP into the XOD solution produced pronounced exothermic peaks that gradually decreased and approached saturation, indicating a specific, saturable binding rather than nonspecific dilution effects. The integrated binding isotherm (Fig. 5B) fitted well to a single-site binding model, yielding a binding stoichiometry n ≈ 1 and a dissociation constant Kd = 38.6 μM, confirming that FAWP interacts with XOD through one dominant and specific binding site with moderate affinity. The binding of FAWP to XOD was spontaneous, with a ΔG of −6.0 kcal/mol (Fig. 5C). Notably, the interaction was characterized by a positive enthalpy change (ΔH = 4.2 kcal/mol) and a large favorable entropy contribution (−TΔS = −10.2 kcal/mol), demonstrating that the binding process is entropy-driven (Wang et al., 2024). The positive ΔH and the accompanying large positive ΔS suggest that the interaction is likely characteristic of hydrophobic contributions to binding. When FAWP associates with XOD, hydrophobic residues within the peptide may insert into hydrophobic regions of the enzyme, potentially near or within the active pocket. This process could displace structured water molecules that are originally ordered around hydrophobic surfaces, releasing them into the bulk solvent and contributing to an entropy gain. Therefore, the binding of FAWP to XOD is likely primarily driven by hydrophobic interactions and van der Waals contacts rather than specific directional bonding.

Peptide induced structural changes of XOD

The UV–vis spectra of XOD in the presence of different peptides are shown in Fig. 5D. Native XOD exhibited a characteristic absorption peak around 280 nm, mainly attributed to aromatic residues such as tryptophan and tyrosine (Vijeesh et al., 2022). Incubation with FAWP, YPF, FGER, or GPGGAW slightly decreased the peak intensity at 280 nm without an obvious shift in wavelength. This change suggests that peptide binding may induce subtle alterations in the local environment of aromatic residues, possibly due to partial burial or altered exposure of hydrophobic regions.

Far-UV CD revealed that FAWP, FGER, GPGGAW, and YPF induced conformational changes in XOD (Fig. 5E). All peptides decreased the negative ellipticity at 222 nm and 208 nm while increasing the signal intensity at 200–205 nm, suggesting a reduction in α-helical content and an increase in random coil structure. Quantitative analysis showed that free XOD contained approximately 38.5% β-sheet and 33.6% random coil; upon peptide binding, the β-sheet content decreased and the random coil proportion increased, with more pronounced changes observed for FAWP and FGER (Fig. 5F). These observations are consistent with the binding events detected by ITC and the conformational modulation indicated by the mixed-type or competitive inhibition patterns observed in the enzyme kinetic studies (Du et al., 2025).

Molecular dynamics analysis of XOD-peptide complexes

To evaluate stability and interaction mechanisms, 200 ns MD simulations were performed for the four XOD–peptide complexes. RMSD stabilized within 10–30 ns (fluctuating between 0.15 and 0.27 nm, Fig. 6A, B), indicating that the complexes had reached equilibrium (X. Liu et al., 2024). Rg analysis revealed that peptide binding induced distinct conformational behaviors. FAWP and GPGGAW adopted more flexible conformations, while FGER and YPF maintained more compact and rigid structures (Fig. 6C, D). Overall Rg remained stable throughout the simulation, suggesting no significant global unfolding of XOD upon peptide binding (Yang et al., 2025).

(F) RMSF of XOD residues. (G) Hydrophobic SASA of peptides. (H) Hydrophobic SASA of XOD. (I) Binding energy analysis of different peptides with XOD.

Hydrogen bond analysis (Fig. 6E) revealed that FGER formed the most persistent bonds, enhancing local pocket stability, whereas FAWP and YPF formed fewer stable interactions. Residue-level RMSF analysis demonstrated that peptide binding induced differential local dynamic changes in XOD (Liu et al., 2025). FGER significantly reduced fluctuations near the active site, FAWP stabilized residues in the hydrophobic pocket while increasing flexibility in peripheral loops, and YPF induced only minimal changes (Fig. 6F. SASA analysis indicated modest solvent exposure increases (Yu et al., 2024), with FAWP causing the largest increase (Fig. 6G). Consistently, FAWP and GPGGAW exposed larger hydrophobic surfaces than FGER and YPF (Fig. 6H), which aligned with the secondary structure alterations observed by CD spectroscopy. MM-PBSA binding free energy decomposition (Fig. 6I) suggested that the interactions were primarily driven by van der Waals and electrostatic forces, partially offset by polar solvation energy. The calculated net ΔG_bind values ranked FAWP (−124.22 ± 14.97 kJ/mol) > GPGGAW (−116.77 ± 9.95 kJ/mol) > FGER (−89.71 ± 3.99 kJ/mol) > YPF (−51.97 ± 7.74 kJ/mol). However, the binding free energy of FAWP obtained from MD simulations (−124.22 ± 14.97 kJ/mol) was substantially more negative than the experimental value measured by ITC (−6 kcal/mol). This discrepancy mainly resulted from neglecting entropy loss and solvent effects in the simulations, as MD reflected the interaction energy of the most favorable binding state. Importantly, these computational binding energies should be interpreted as relative indicators of binding stability rather than absolute measures of biological activity. Despite the difference in magnitude, both methods indicated that peptide binding to XOD was spontaneous and followed a consistent trend in relative strength.

Analysis of peptide-XOD interaction mechanisms at the amino acid level

Residue-level analysis after 200 ns MD simulations showed that FAWP, FGER, GPGGAW, and YPF remained stably bound within the XOD catalytic pocket. To further elucidate the key amino acid residues involved in the peptide–XOD interactions, detailed interaction mapping was performed. FAWP, FGER, and GPGGAW exhibited more focused occupancy volumes within the pocket, whereas YPF displayed a broader yet confined distribution (Fig. 7A). In the FAWP–XOD complex (Fig. 7B), binding was stabilized by four persistent hydrogen bonds primarily involving the key residues Glu1261, Gln767, Gln1040, and Ser1082. This interaction was further reinforced by an extensive hydrophobic network centered on Phe914 and Phe798, including Ala1078, Ala1079, Thr1077, Met1038, Gly1260, Thr1083, and Ala1258. Aromatic stacking between the peptide's Phe1/Trp3 and Phe914 also contributed significantly to binding stability (Ma et al., 2024). For FGER–XOD (Fig. 7C), multiple hydrogen bonds were formed with the key residues Gln767, Ser1080, Glu802, Arg912, Asp1191, and Gln1040. These polar interactions were supported by hydrophobic contacts with Phe914, Leu873, and Phe798, along with π–π stacking between Phe1 and Phe914, collectively enhancing binding affinity (Chen et al., 2024). In GPGGAW–XOD (Fig. 7D), the peptide was anchored by multiple hydrogen bonds involving Gln112, Gln1194, Glu1261, Arg912, and Gly913, with Trp6 engaging surrounding hydrophobic residues to position the peptide deeply within the catalytic pocket. YPF–XOD (Fig. 7E) although fewer polar contacts were observed (Tyr1 with Phe798 and Phe3 with Gln1040), hydrophobic interactions with Phe914, Phe1005, and Ala1078 still provided substantial stabilization.

Network pharmacology analysis of XOD inhibitory peptides

Molecular docking, MD simulations, and in vitro assays confirmed that FAWP, FGER, YPF, and GPGGAW inhibited XOD through stable binding within the active pocket. Beyond direct enzyme inhibition, network pharmacology analysis suggested additional pathway-level regulatory effects. A total of 32 overlapping targets were identified between the predicted targets of the peptides and hyperuricemia-related genes (Fig. 8A). Similar target-overlap strategies have been widely used to elucidate multi-target characteristics of food-derived bioactive compounds (Bao et al., 2025). The protein-protein interaction network constructed based on the 32 common targets showed a highly interconnected topology (Fig. 8B). Several hub genes, including STAT3, PTGS2, ACE, REN, PPARG, CXCR4, and CTSB, exhibited high degree values, suggesting their central regulatory roles within the network. STAT3 and PTGS2 are key regulators of inflammatory signaling and oxidative stress, which have been reported to be closely associated with hyperuricemia-induced gout (Peng et al., 2024). ACE and REN are core components of the renin-angiotensin system, critical for renal urate handling and blood pressure regulation. KEGG pathway enrichment revealed significant enrichment in hyperuricemia-related pathways (Fig. 8C), including the renin-angiotensin system, TNF signaling, and NF-κB signaling. Activation of TNF and NF-κB signaling promotes inflammatory responses and renal dysfunction under hyperuricemic conditions (Riaz et al., 2023). GO biological process analysis indicated that the common targets were mainly involved in response to hypoxia, blood pressure regulation, and peptide hormone processing (Fig. 8D). Hypoxia responses and blood pressure regulation are closely linked to renal homeostasis and uric acid excretion (H. Zhang et al., 2023). Network pharmacology indicated these peptides as multifunctional agents for hyperuricemia by targeting inflammation and renal pathways.

Conclusion

This study established a comprehensive framework for discovering XOD inhibitory peptides from sheep milk, integrating peptidomics, machine learning-based screening, molecular dynamics simulations, and experimental validation. The VHSE–CatBoost model successfully identified FAWP as the most potent inhibitor (IC₅₀ = 12.99 ± 0.33 mM), exhibiting mixed-type inhibition. Thermodynamic, spectroscopic, and simulation analyses revealed spontaneous binding, conformational changes in XOD, and stable active-site occupancy through hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions. Network pharmacology further suggested multi-target effects on inflammation and renal pathways. These findings provided mechanistic insights into XOD inhibition by sheep milk-derived peptides and highlighted their potential as functional bioactive compounds. Future studies should aim to optimize peptide activity, evaluate in vivo efficacy, and assess their performance within complex food systems.
