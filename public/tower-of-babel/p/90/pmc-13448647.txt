Balanced electrochemical reaction kinetics and mass transfer for stable zinc negative electrode
Authors: Ao Chen, Shuo Yang, Funian Mo, Qing Li, Zhuoxi Wu, Zhaodong Huang, Ze Chen, Yiqiao Wang, Hu Hong, Zhiquan Wei, Hui Yang, Chunyi Zhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The formation of zinc dendrites is a significant obstacle to commercializing zinc-ion batteries. Although the discrepancy between the fast electrochemical reaction kinetics and the comparatively sluggish mass transfer leads to the formation and growth of dendrites, a profound understanding of how the relationship between the two factors influences dendrite formation is essential. Here, through investigating a series of urea derivatives for regulating Zn2+ plating, we find that achieving a better balance between electrochemical reaction kinetics and the mass transfer rate is crucial for effectively suppressing dendrite formation. A dimensionless constant, K, is proposed to quantify the balance between these two factors. As a result, the electrolyte with N, N-dimethylurea has the highest K value, enabling cumulative capacities of 11,000 mAh cm−2 for Zn | |Zn cells and 7,500 mAh cm−2 for Zn | |Cu cells achieved at a current density of 10 mA cm−2. Furthermore, the Zn | |Zn0.25V2O5·nH2O pouch cell with a mass loading of 60 mg cm−2 delivers a capacity of 6.95 Ah and demonstrates stable cycling performance using the modified electrolyte. This work provides theoretical insights into governing the formation and growth of zinc dendrites.

Introduction

Aqueous zinc-ion batteries (ZIBs), known for their high safety and cost-effectiveness, have emerged as potential candidates for large-scale energy storage1–4. Unlike commercial lithium-ion batteries, which can utilize graphite as the negative electrode material for storing Li+ ions, Zn metal is currently a commonly used negative electrode material for ZIBs. However, the formation of Zn dendrites and the resultant battery short circuits present a significant challenge to the practical application of ZIBs5–7. Recently, the introduction of various organic molecules into the electrolyte has been shown to be effective to some extent in achieving uniform Zn plating8–15. These studies either demonstrate the regulation of the Zn2+ solvation structure or the control of the electric double layer (EDL), thereby improving the cycling stability of the Zn negative electrode. However, the underlying mechanisms by which these additives improve Zn plating and mitigate dendrite formation have not been well elucidated.

The primary mechanism of dendrite formation and growth is that the electrochemical reaction kinetics of metal ions on the electrode surface exceed their diffusion rate in the electrolyte16–18. When metal ions on the electrode surface are rapidly consumed due to the fast electrochemical reaction, the sluggish ion transport leads to a significant concentration gradient of ions near the electrode. Consequently, the preferential plating of ions in the regions with higher ion concentrations facilitates the formation and rapid growth of dendrites. Considering that further improvement of the ion diffusion rate in aqueous electrolytes for static ZIBs is challenging, our previous work and some other studies have demonstrated that the electrochemical reaction kinetics can be moderated by utilizing the steric hindrance effect of the large-size solvation structure of Zn2+ to increase the electron transfer reorganization energy18–21. Benefiting from the moderation of the electrochemical reaction rate, the formation and growth of dendrites can be effectively suppressed. However, the steric hindrance effect of organic additives may also negatively affect the migration of Zn2+, thereby slowing down the mass transfer process for electrodeposition and resulting in non-uniform deposition22. Therefore, the profound insights on the balance between the electrochemical reaction and mass transfer are crucial for achieving dendrite-free plating.

Herein, we investigated a series of urea derivatives with the same C = O and N-H functional groups but different alkane chains, including urea, N, N-dimethylurea (DMU), trimethylurea (TMU), and tetramethylurea (TTMU), for regulating the electroplating of Zn. A dimensionless constant, K, defined as the ratio of activation energy for charge transfer (Ect) to activation energy for mass transfer (Emt) is proposed to quantify the balance between the electrochemical reaction kinetics and the mass transfer rate. The results demonstrate that the morphology of electroplated Zn is highly correlated with the K value of the electrolyte. Among these urea derivatives, the electrolyte with DMU has the highest K, indicating a better balance between electrochemical reaction and mass transfer. As a result, smoother and more compact plated Zn was obtained, leading to stable cycling over a cumulative capacity of 7500 mAh cm−2 at a high current density of 10 mA cm−2 and an areal capacity of 10 mAh cm−2 for the Zn | |Cu asymmetric cell. More importantly, the Zn | |Zn0.25V2O5⋅n H2O pouch cell with a high mass loading of 60 mg cm−2 could deliver a capacity of over 6.9 Ah and maintain stable cycling performance. Our investigation suggests that balancing the electrochemical reaction rate and mass transfer of Zn2+ to achieve a high K value may be a promising mechanism for achieving the long-term cycling life of the Zn negative electrode.

Results

Solvation structures of Zn2+

According to the electrostatic potential (ESP) on the van der Waals surfaces shown in Fig. 1a and Supplementary Data 1, it can be observed that the ESP values for the oxygen (O) atom in the C = O bond of various urea derivatives are more negative than the ESP value of the O atom in H2O, suggesting that all the urea derivatives may exhibit a stronger affinity towards Zn2+ ions and potentially occupy the primary Zn2+ solvation shell23. Furthermore, similar ESP values suggest that the influence of various urea derivatives on the size of the Zn2+ solvation structure is predominantly determined by the methyl chain. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was employed to determine the detailed Zn2+ solvation structure in the electrolyte with different urea derivatives (Fig. 1b-i). As shown in Fig. 1b and e, the representative snapshots demonstrate that Zn2+ is distributed homogeneously in the electrolytes (Supplementary Data 2). The radial distribution function and coordination number (CN) reveal the local environments around Zn2+ in different electrolytes. As shown in Fig. 1c, a distinct peak at a distance of 2.0 Å can be ascribed to the Zn-H2O bond, while the peak located at 3.1 Å corresponds to the Zn-SO42− coordination, demonstrating that the H2O molecules dominate the Zn2+ first solvation shell24. The dominant Zn2+ solvation structure is the [Zn(H2O)6]2+ complex in the ZnSO4 electrolyte (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Data 3). Upon the introduction of DMU into the ZnSO4 electrolyte (denoted as the DMU electrolyte), an additional peak appears at 2.01 Å, while the CN of the Zn-H2O coordination decreases from 5.7 to 5.0 (Fig. 1f), suggesting that DMU molecule replaces H2O in the first solvation shell due to its high ESP. The solvation structure of Zn2+ changes to [Zn(H2O)5.0(DMU)0.7]2+ (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Data 3)25. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S1, such solvation modifications are also observed when introducing other urea derivatives into the ZnSO4 electrolyte. Urea, TMU, and TTMU can also enter the first solvation shell and substitute H2O to form Zn2+(H2O)5(Urea)0.5, Zn2+(H2O)5.3(TMU)0.38 and Zn2+(H2O)5.2(TTMU)0.4, respectively. The alteration in solvation structure can be further verified by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy and Raman spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 1h, the peak at 1638 cm⁻¹ in the ZnSO4 electrolyte can be attributed to the O-H bending vibration. The blue shift of the O-H bending vibration indicates that urea derivatives have entered the solvation shell and interacted with H2O molecules. In the Raman spectra (Fig. 1i), the proportion of weak H-bonds increase significantly after adding various urea derivatives, which is related to the disruption of the H-bond network by urea derivatives. In addition, the blue shift observed in strong H-bond suggests the coordinated H2O molecules are replaced by urea derivatives13,26. Figure 1j presents a summary of the energy levels of the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) and highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) for H2O and different solutes, along with their respective frontier molecular orbital diagrams. Compared to H2O, the narrower band gap between the LUMO and HOMO energy levels of the urea derivative indicates a more favorable electron transfer capability, which is beneficial for the adsorption of these molecules onto the Zn electrode and the reconstruction of the Inner Helmholtz Layer27,28. This was verified by simulations of the EDL structures and the distribution of Zn2+ and H2O on the electrode surface in the 2 M ZnSO4 and DMU electrolytes (Supplementary Fig. S2). K values of electrolytes

Previous work demonstrates that the steric hindrance effect of large solvation structures can moderate electrochemical reaction kinetics. Accordingly, the solvation radii of different electrolytes were calculated by determining the distance between the two furthest atoms within the solvation structures and adding the respective van der Waals radii of the two atoms29. As shown in Fig. 2a and Supplementary Data 3, the solvation size of Zn2+ in ZnSO4 electrolyte is around 3.91 Å, while this value increases to 5.05, 5.34, 5.43, and 5.51 Å for the urea, DMU, TMU, and TTMU electrolytes, respectively. During the electrodeposition process, electron transfer occurs between the charge-delocalized electrode and the adsorbed Zn2+ ions, converting Zn2+ ions into Zn atoms30. The larger solvation radius results in an increase in R, resulting in higher electron transfer reorganization energy during electrodeposition, which consequently moderates the electrochemical reduction process. In order to accurately determine the reorganization energy in different electrolytes, we conducted transient voltammetry experiments using ultramicroelectrodes. Supplementary Fig. S3 illustrates the battery configuration, including a tungsten (W) ultramicroelectrode (R0 = 10 μm, representing the radius of the W microelectrode) as the working electrode and a Zn foil serving as the counter and reference electrodes. All the cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were operated at a scan rate of 50 V s⁻1 to eliminate the effect of mass transport and make sure the process was under reaction kinetic control32,33. As a result, the λ values obtained by fitting the Tafel curve using the Marcus-Hush model for ZnSO4, urea, DMU, TMU, and TTMU electrolytes are 0.233, 0.284, 0.386, 0.399, and 0.403 eV, respectively (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. S4). The charge transfer characteristics in different electrolytes can also be verified by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). As shown in Supplementary Fig. S5, the semicircle in the EIS curve represents the charge transfer resistance (Rct), which exhibits a similar increasing trend with the λ34. However, it is important to recognize that the formation of Zn dendrites can be attributed to the conflict between fast charge transfer and relatively slow mass transfer. Consequently, although introducing urea derivatives with a larger electron transfer reorganization energy to slow down the electrochemical process may be beneficial in suppressing dendrite formation, the impact of steric hindrance on mass transfer must also be carefully evaluated. According to previous research, organic compounds possessing higher steric hindrance have been observed to be related to the limited movement of solvated ions22,35. Supplementary Fig. S6 presents the plots of mean squared displacement (MSD) versus (vs.) time for various electrolytes, as obtained from the MD simulations. The electrolytes with urea derivatives have shown lower Zn2+ ion diffusion coefficients than the ZnSO4 electrolyte, as observed from the slope of the MSD curves. The steric hindrance effect, while reducing the electrochemical reaction rate, also negatively impacts the mass transfer of Zn2+ ions. Therefore, the balance between these two factors is crucial for optimal performance.

To better quantify the relationship between these two rates, we conducted a comparative analysis of the activation energy for charge transfer and mass transfer among different electrolytes. According to the Marcus theory, it is known that the activation energy for charge transfer (Ect) is approximately equal to λ/4 in an electrochemical process36,37. On the other hand, the activation energy for mass transfer (Emt) can be estimated by fitting the temperature-dependent ionic conductivity using the Arrhenius equation (Fig. 2c)38,39. Therefore, we proposed a dimensionless constant, K, defined as the ratio of activation energy for charge transfer (Ect) to activation energy for mass transfer (Emt), to quantify the balance between electrochemical reaction kinetics and mass transfer rate. Figure 2d summarizes the values obtained for Emt, Ect and K in various electrolytes. Although both Emt and Ect exhibit a monotonically increasing trend, the K value first rises and then sharply decreases. This indicates that the K value is the result of the synergistic effects of Emt and Ect, rather than being determined by a single trend alone. The DMU electrolyte shows the highest K value and is located at the inflection point, suggesting a closer match between the electrochemical reaction rate and the mass transfer rate. Although TMU and TTMU electrolytes have higher λ values, which lowers the electrochemical reaction kinetics, the transport of Zn2+ ions is worsened, resulting in a decrease in the K value. Consequently, the electrolyte incorporating DMU may facilitate a more uniform deposition of Zn during the electrochemical process, thereby potentially enabling enhanced electrochemical performance.

Morphologies of plated Zn in various electrolytes

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was first employed to investigate the plating morphology of Zn2+ in various electrolytes. As shown in Fig. 3a, the Zn negative electrode with ZnSO4 electrolyte exhibits a consistently rough surface during the entire plating process. High-magnification SEM images (inset of Fig. 3a) reveal the presence of hexagonal and mossy-like Zn dendrites on the surface. In contrast, the Zn negative electrode in the DMU electrolyte maintains a flat surface even after plating for extended periods (Fig. 3b). High-magnification SEM images exhibit a densely packed plating morphology with no observable dendrite formation. Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) inverse pole figure (IPF) mapping exhibits a large number of black noise signals, indicating that the plated Zn in the ZnSO4 electrolyte has a loose and irregular structure (Supplementary Fig. S7a). In addition, the grain size of Zn is smaller than 2 μm, with an average grain size of 0.92 μm (Supplementary Fig. S7b). Supplementary Fig. S7c shows that the plated Zn in the DMU electrolyte possesses dense and regular grains, with similar crystal orientations and a significant decrease in disordered structures, which corresponds well to the SEM results. The average grain size of Zn increases to 9.5 μm (Supplementary Fig. S7d). X-ray diffraction (XRD) results demonstrate that the Zn (100) plane is the primary growth orientation in DMU, while strong diffraction peaks corresponding to the (002), (100), and (101) crystal planes can be observed for the Zn negative electrode in ZnSO4 electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. S8). In electrolytes with other urea derivatives, the morphologies of plated Zn are relatively smooth during the initial plating stage (Supplementary Fig. S9). However, after continuous plating for 10 h, obvious dendrites can still be observed on the negative electrode surface. The results indicate the electrolyte incorporating DMU exhibits greater effectiveness in suppressing Zn dendrites compared to other electrolytes, which corresponds well to its highest K value. Besides the SEM observation, in-situ optical microscopy was conducted to distinguish the electrodeposition behaviors between the ZnSO4 and DMU electrolytes. As shown in Figs. 3c and 3d, the DMU electrolyte exhibits flat and compact Zn plating over 30 minutes, even under a high current density of 10 mA cm−2, while the Zn became rough with dendrite formation after 5 min of plating in the ZnSO4 electrolyte. The 3D optical profilometer was used to generate a 3D colormap of the negative electrode surface (Fig. 3e). Compared to the ZnSO4 electrolyte, the surface of the Zn negative electrode in the electrolyte with DMU exhibits a significantly low roughness after plating. The linear roughness along the horizontal direction (Supplementary Fig. S10) of the Zn negative electrode in different electrolytes was analyzed and shown in Fig. 3f40. The height profile of the Zn negative electrode using the ZnSO4 electrolyte shows significant unevenness with multiple sharp peaks in the curve, demonstrating the rough surface caused by Zn dendrites. The height profile of the Zn negative electrode in the electrolyte with DMU shows markedly reduced fluctuations, indicating the more uniform Zn plating. Therefore, the introduction of DMU can effectively regulate the charge transfer process while ensuring efficient mass transport, leading to a better balance between electrochemical reaction and mass transfer, thus effectively suppressing dendrite formation and promoting uniform Zn plating.

In addition, although the introduction of DMU has a limited effect on extending the electrochemical stability window (ESW) (by merely 100 mV) (Supplementary Fig. S11), it can effectively suppress the hydrogen evolution reaction. In-situ optical microscopy observations of the Zn electrode during plating reveal that, in the 2 M ZnSO4 electrolyte, small hydrogen bubbles formed on the Zn electrode in the ZnSO4 electrolyte within 10 min. As the plating time increased, the bubbles gradually grew, generating more bubbles (Supplementary Fig. S12a). In contrast, in the DMU electrolyte, small bubbles did not appear until 40 min (Supplementary Fig. S12a). The reduced hydrogen evolution reaction implies that the formation of by-products during cycling is effectively suppressed. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S13, the surface of the Zn negative electrode cycled in the ZnSO4 electrolyte exhibits clear signals of Zn4SO4(OH)6·4H2O and a small amount of Zn(OH)2, whereas no diffraction peaks corresponding to by-products are observed for the Zn negative electrode cycled in the DMU electrolyte.

Long-term cycling life of Zn electrode

We first employed the Zn | |Zn symmetrical cells to evaluate the cycling stability of the Zn electrode. Supplementary Fig. S14 exhibits the voltage curves of Zn | |Zn cells using ZnSO4 and DMU electrolytes at different current densities. The introduction of DMU only leads to a slight increase in the polarization voltage during Zn plating and stripping. As shown in Fig. 4a, the galvanostatic cycling of Zn | |Zn symmetric cells was initially measured at a current density of 2 mA cm−2 and an areal capacity of 1 mAh cm−2. In the ZnSO4 electrolyte, the Zn | |Zn cell exhibited rapid failure after 125 h, characterized by significant voltage fluctuations. In contrast, the Zn | |Zn cell with the DMU electrolyte demonstrated good stability, as the voltage profile remained consistently stable after cycling for over 3750 h. Moreover, the stability of the symmetric cells was further investigated by cycling at a higher current density of 10 mA cm−2 and an areal capacity of 5 mAh cm−2 (Fig. 4b). The Zn | |Zn cell utilizing DMU electrolyte exhibited a long cycling lifespan of over 1100 h, corresponding to a cumulative capacity of 11 Ah cm−2. In addition to the Zn | |Zn symmetric cell, the cycling performance and coulombic efficiency were also evaluated using a Zn | |Cu asymmetric cell. As shown in Fig. 4c, the Zn | |Cu cell with ZnSO4 electrolyte failed rapidly within 100 h at a current density of 2 mA cm−2 and an areal capacity of 1 mAh cm−2. In contrast, the Zn | |Cu cell with the DMU electrolyte demonstrated a significantly longer lifespan of approximately 3500 h, with an average coulombic efficiency of over 99.92%. Furthermore, it exhibited a stable voltage-time curve throughout the entire plating/stripping process, as depicted in the inset of Fig. 4c. Although the Zn | |Cu cell with the DMU electrolyte showed excellent performance at low current density and areal capacity, practical cells with asymmetric electrodes typically work at higher current densities and areal capacities. Therefore, to meet the practical requirements, the cycling performance of Zn | |Cu cells were investigated at a high current density of 10 mA cm⁻2 and an areal capacity of 10 mAh cm⁻2 (Fig. 4d)41. The Zn | |Cu cell with the DMU electrolyte demonstrates a lifespan of ~750 h, corresponding to a cumulative capacity of ~7500 mAh cm⁻2, and the average coulombic efficiency is over 99.6%, which significantly outperforms other electrolytes (Supplementary Fig. S18). The voltage curves at different cycles demonstrate that although the stripping voltage of the Zn | |Cu cell with ZnSO4 electrolyte remains unchanged, the significantly increased stripping capacity suggests the occurrence of soft short circuits after 44 cycles (Supplementary Fig. S19a). In contrast, the Zn | |Cu cell using the DMU electrolyte exhibits no soft short circuits (Supplementary Fig. S19b). Figure4e illustrates the relationship between the K value and the cycling lifespans of Zn negative electrodes in the five electrolytes. The results demonstrated a strong correlation between the cycling stability of the Zn electrode in the electrolyte and the K value. Significantly, since the DMU electrolyte has the highest K value, the Zn | |Zn and Zn | |Cu cells in this electrolyte exhibited improved cycling stability. In contrast, the K value of the TTMU electrolyte is lower and comparable to that of the ZnSO4 electrolyte due to the worse mass transfer, which leads to similarly poor cycling performance for both.

In addition, we compared the electrochemical performances with other works based on different electrolytes. As shown in Fig. 4f and Supplementary Table 1, our work not only achieved a high cumulative capacity of 11,000 Ah cm⁻2 for Zn | |Zn cell under a high areal capacity of 5 mAh cm⁻2 but also demonstrated an impressive cumulative capacity of 7500 Ah cm⁻2 for Zn | |Cu cell even under a higher current density of 10 mA cm⁻2 and an areal capacity of 10 mAh cm⁻2. It is worth noting that most of the previously reported Zn | |Cu cells were evaluated under relatively low current density and areal capacities, such as 1 mAh cm⁻², which may not meet the practical demands42. However, our study evaluated the performance under more practical conditions, which highlights a more significant advantage43–50.

Electrochemical performances of full cells

Given the good stability demonstrated by the Zn electrode in the DMU electrolyte, we systematically investigated the electrochemical performance of the full cell using Zn0.25V2O5·nH2O nanobelts as the positive electrode material. As shown in Fig. 5a, the Zn | | Zn0.25V2O5·nH2O coin cells exhibit similar cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves in ZnSO4 and DMU electrolytes. The slightly decreased current in the DMU electrolyte can be attributed to the moderated electrochemical reaction kinetics due to the steric hindrance effect. Figure 5b exhibits the cycling performance of the Zn | |Zn0.25V2O5·nH2O cells. Benefiting from the effective suppression of the dendrites in DMU electrolyte, the cell achieved a long cycling performance of over 4000 cycles at 2 A g⁻1. In contrast, the cell with ZnSO4 electrolyte experienced a short circuit within 500 cycles. Further, to meet practical application requirements, it is essential to investigate the electrochemical performance in large pouch cells that provide capacity on the ampere-hour scale. Consequently, a large-scale positive electrode with a size of 10 cm × 10 cm was fabricated using polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) as the polymer binder (Supplementary Fig. S21), achieving a high mass loading of ~60 mg cm⁻2. Figure 5c shows the optical image of the large pouch cell with a single layer of positive electrode. The pouch cell delivered an initial capacity of 1.27 Ah (corresponding to a high areal capacity of 12.7 mAh cm⁻2) and retained a capacity of 988 mAh after 350 cycles at a current of 2 A, with a capacity retention of 72% (Fig. 5d). Since practical pouch cells generally use multiple layers of electrodes, we also fabricated a larger cell with four stacks of positive electrodes (Fig. 5e). As a result, the Zn | |Zn0.25V2O5·nH2O pouch cell exhibited an initial discharge capacity of 6.95 Ah (Fig. 5f), which corresponds to a high areal capacity of 17.4 mAh cm⁻2. Additionally, the cell maintained stable charge and discharge curves over 50 cycles at a current of 4 A and achieved a capacity retention of 90% (Fig. 5g). The electrochemical performance of the large pouch cells demonstrates the promising potential of DMU electrolyte in promoting the practical application of ZIBs.

Discussion

In summary, we found that the balance between electrochemical reaction kinetics and the mass transfer rate is crucial for suppressing the formation of Zn dendrites by investigating electrolytes with a series of urea derivatives. In addition, to better quantify the balance, a dimensionless constant, K, is proposed. As a result, the DMU electrolyte has the highest K value since it achieves a better balance between electrochemical reaction kinetics and mass transfer, enabling uniform and dendrite-free Zn plating. High cumulative capacities of 11,000 mAh cm⁻2 for Zn | |Zn cells and 7,500 mAh cm⁻2 for Zn | |Cu cells were achieved in DMU electrolyte at high current densities. Pouch cells with capacities of 1.2 Ah and 6.95 Ah were fabricated using the modified electrolyte and exhibited stable cycling performance. This work provides profound insights into the importance of balancing electrochemical reaction kinetics and mass transfer rate to suppress dendrite formation, which offers fundamental support for advancing the practical application of ZIBs.

Methods

Chemicals and electrolyte solutions

Zinc sulfate heptahydrate (ZnSO4·7H₂O, AR), zinc acetate (Zn(Ac)2, 99.99% metals basis), urea (98%), N,N-dimethylurea (DMU, 98%), trimethylurea (TMU, 95%), tetramethylurea (TTMU, 99%) and vanadium(V) oxide (V2O5, 99.99 metals basis) were purchased from Aladdin reagent. All the chemicals were used as received without any additional purification steps. The deionized water (DI water) was purified using a Milli-Q CLX 7040 water purification system. The urea, DMU and TMU electrolytes were prepared by dissolving the corresponding urea derivatives into a 2 M ZnSO4 solution to achieve the desired concentration of 1 mol L⁻1. TTMU electrolyte was prepared by dissolving 2 M ZnSO4 in a 1 mol L⁻1 TTMU solution.

Positive electrode preparation

The synthesis of Zn0.25V2O5·nH2O nanobelts was carried out according to a previous study51. In this process, 2 mmol of V2O5 and 1.3 mmol of Zn(Ac)2 were dispersed in a 50 ml mixture of water and acetone in a 15:1 volume ratio. The resulting solution was then transferred to a sealed Teflon stainless steel autoclave and maintained at 200 °C for 72 h. After that, the product was washed with distilled water and ethanol and dried at 60 °C for 24 h.

The positive electrode was prepared by mixing 70% active materials powder, 20% Super p (Timcal), and 10% PTFE (60 wt% dispersion in H2O, Sigma-Aldrich). After the slurry was thoroughly ground in a mortar, it was rolled onto a Ti mesh (purity >99.5%, 80 mesh, thickness: 0.1 mm) using a doctor blade. Subsequently, the positive electrode was compressed under a pressure of 10 MPa and then dried at 60 °C for 24 h. The loading mass of Zn0.25V2O5·nH2O was 60 mg cm⁻² for the pouch cell and 5 mg cm⁻² for the coin cell.

Electrochemical characterizations

Half-cells and full cells were fabricated in CR2032 coin cells using bare Zn foil (12 cm diameter) as the negative electrode. Glass fiber (Whatman 1820-070 GF/A; thickness: 260 μm; nominal air flow rate: 4.3 s/100 mL/in²; average pore size: 1.6 μm) served as the separator, and 120 μL of electrolyte was applied. Prior to assembling the cell, the Zn foil (100 μm) was carefully polished to remove the surface oxide layer. For the Zn | |Cu cells, Cu foil with a thickness of 30 μm and a purity exceeding 99.9% was employed as the current collector.

For the construction of the Zn | |Zn0.25V2O5·nH2O pouch cell with a capacity of ~1 Ah, a bipolar structure was employed. This structure involved two Zn foils (100 µm) sharing one piece of Zn0.25V2O5·nH2O electrode, with the positive electrode and negative electrode separated by one piece of glass fiber separator. For the construction of the Zn | | Zn0.25V2O5·nH2O pouch cell with a capacity of over 6 Ah, the cell utilized four Zn0.25V2O5·nH2O electrodes. These electrodes were arranged in the cell using the same bipolar structure depicted in Fig. 5e, which was schematically prepared using Microsoft PowerPoint. Zn0.25V2O5·nH2O electrode was prepared with double-sided coating, with an electrode size of 10 cm × 10 cm. The electrolyte dosage was 150 µL cm⁻2 (based on the separator). After electrolyte injection, the cell was degassed under vacuum and then sealed. During cycling, an external pressure of approximately 19.6 kPa was applied across the entire cell.

Galvanostatic charge–discharge experiments at various current densities were performed using the Land battery testing system (Wuhan, China) and the Neware battery testing system (Neware, Shenzhen, China) within an environmental chamber maintained at 25 ± 1 °C. The Zn | | Zn0.25V2O5·nH2O cells were tested between the voltage ranges of 0.3–1.6 V. Cyclic voltammetry (CV), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) measurements were conducted on a CHI-760 electrochemical workstation. EIS measurements were conducted in potentiostatic mode by applying a 5 mV amplitude AC signal over a frequency range from 100 kHz to 0.01 Hz. A total of 68 data points were collected during each measurement. Prior to EIS testing, the cells were rested at open-circuit voltage for 2 s, and the measurements were performed around the open-circuit voltage.

Characterizations

Zn electrodeposition experiments were performed in Zn | |Zn coin cells at 25 ± 1 °C. After electrodeposition, the cells were disassembled, and the Zn-deposited electrodes were carefully collected. The electrodes were subsequently washed with deionized water to remove residual electrolyte and then subjected to further characterization. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed on a D2 PHASER XE-T X-ray diffractometer system equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm) to determine the crystal structure and phase composition. The data were collected in continuous scanning mode over a 2θ range of 10°–80°. Zn | |Zn coin cells with different electrolytes were electrodeposited at a current density of 1 mA cm⁻2 for 1, 2, 5, and 10 h at 25 ± 1 °C. After electrodeposition, the cells were disassembled, and the cycled electrodes were collected. The electrodes were subsequently washed with deionized water to remove residual electrolyte before surface morphology characterization. The micromorphology of Zn electrodes was investigated by scanning electron microscope (SEM, ESEM, FEI/Philips XL30). Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) was measured by Helios 5CX. The Zn electrodes for EBSD measurements were prepared by electrolytic polishing in a mixed solution of perchloric acid and alcohol (1:9) at a temperature of −20 °C for 60 s under 12 V. In-situ optical observations were performed using an Aosvi NX30T. Raman spectra were collected using a WITec alpha300 Raman system, and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy measurements were performed on a PerkinElmer FTIR spectrometer.

Computational details

ESP calculations were performed with the DMol3 package. Structure optimizations and calculations were performed using the B3LYP hybrid functional, while dispersion corrections were applied via the Grimme method (DFT-D). Relativistic effects were considered by using the DFT semicore pseudopotential core treatment, where the core electrons were represented by a single effective potential. The double numerical plus polarization basis set was used in these calculations. The energy convergence threshold was set to 10⁻6 Hartree, with 1 Hartree equal to 27.21 eV, and the maximum allowed force and displacement were 0.002 and 0.005 Hartree Å⁻1, respectively.

MD simulations were carried out with the Forcite module in Materials Studio to investigate the Zn2⁺ solvation structures in different electrolytes. The COMPASSIII forcefield was adopted for all subsequent simulations. All the simulations were performed with constant temperature (298.15 K) in a cubic box with periodic boundary conditions in all three Cartesian directions. The bulk electrolyte systems were first equilibrated for 4 ns under the NPT ensemble. During this equilibration stage, the Velocity-rescale thermostat and Berendsen barostat were employed to control the temperature and pressure, respectively. Electrostatic interactions were evaluated using Ewald summation, whereas van der Waals (vdW) interactions were treated with a cut-off scheme using a cut-off radius of 12.5 Å. A subsequent 6 ns production run was then performed under the NVT ensemble with a Nose–Hoover thermostat. Electrostatic interactions were treated using the Ewald sums, and Van der Waals (vdW) interactions were adopted using cut-off methods with a cut-off radius of 18.5 Å. Only the final 1 ns was sampled for radial distribution function (RDF) and coordination structure counting analyses.
