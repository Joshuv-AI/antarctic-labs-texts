Local spatial distribution of J‐aggregates in molecularly oriented bisazo‐dye thin films
Authors: Jian Yu, Tetsuya Aoyama, Atsuya Muranaka, Masamitsu Ishitobi, Hirohito Umezawa, Masanobu Uchiyama, Yutaka Yamagata, Toshihiko Tanaka, Shinya Matsumoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe spatial distribution of partially grown J‐aggregates in films of oriented linear bisazo‐dye molecules is analysed using polarised optical microscopy (POM), atomic force microscopy (AFM), and hyperspectral imaging. By depositing the bisazo dye onto a poly(tetrafluoroethylene) alignment layer, an oriented thin film containing highly oriented J‐aggregates with a dichroic ratio exceeding 12 is prepared. AFM and POM using monochromatic light in the same area of the thin film reveal that the J‐aggregates are locally formed and have a distribution different from that of the crystalline phase and grains. Furthermore, local spectral analysis of the film using a hyperspectral camera validates the local formation of J‐aggregates. These results provide new insights into the formation process and control of J‐aggregates, which are crucial for their potential applications in optoelectronics. LAY DESCRIPTION: Polarised optical microscopy (POM) combined with atomic force microscopy (AFM) enables visualisation of submicron‐sized grains and reveals that the J‐aggregate–containing phases (JCP) are mainly localised on the surface and sides of the grains.Keywords: bisazo dye, hyperspectral imaging, J‐aggregates, molecular orientation, polarised optical microscopy

INTRODUCTION

Molecularly oriented J‐aggregates in thin films are important owing to their scientific significance and potential for optoelectronic applications. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 J‐aggregates exhibit unique optical properties, such as a tremendous redshift in their optical peaks, as well as nonlinear optical properties, including ultrafast nonlinear optical responses. These aggregates have been used as sensitisers in silver halide photography for nearly half a century. Recently, J‐aggregates have been widely explored for near‐infrared (NIR) light sensing 6 , 7 and bioimaging 8 , 9 owing to their semiconducting properties, NIR absorption capabilities, and NIR emission characteristics.

In general, most molecules that form J‐aggregates are ionic, whereas zwitterionic, quadrupolar, and nonpolar dyes have also been reported. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Our research focuses on non‐ionic dyes that facilitate the preparation of thin films with good controllability via vacuum deposition. Previously, we examined the optical and semiconducting properties of vacuum‐deposited J‐aggregate films. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 In particular, we succeeded in macroscopically orienting ordered J‐aggregates of bisazo, bisazomethine, monoazo, and pyrazine dyes 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 using molecular orientation techniques with poly(tetrafluoroethylene) (PTFE) alignment layers. 25 , 26 Furthermore, we investigated the orientation mechanism of the J‐aggregates using molecular dynamics simulations. 19 , 21 , 27 The appropriate orientation of J‐aggregates can enhance their interaction with laser light because the molecular axis is aligned with the direction of light polarisation, significantly improving performance.

However, based on atomic force microscopy (AFM) analyses of thin films containing J‐aggregates, it has been suggested that J‐aggregates are formed locally near the crystalline grain boundaries. 17 In addition, the formation process and spatial distribution of J‐aggregates remain unclear. Direct observation of the region in which J‐aggregates form has not yet been achieved to confirm the proposed model. Therefore, these unknown fundamentals must be clarified to increase the amount of J‐aggregates and prepare J‐aggregate thin films with enhanced optoelectronic properties.

In this study, we determined that J‐aggregates have a different spatial distribution than grains and are formed locally in vacuum‐deposited films, as revealed by polarised optical microscopy (POM) and AFM analyses of the same region in J‐aggregate thin films of a molecularly oriented bisazo dye (BA; Figure 1A). These findings are reinforced by spatially resolved spectroscopic analysis using a hyperspectral camera. The crystal grains grow with molecular migration, and J‐aggregates are gradually formed in such a metastable state during grain growth. In addition, this study demonstrates that conventional POM combined with AFM enables visualisation of submicron‐sized grains and the distribution of J‐aggregates in thin films through analytical strategies, including the use of POM images acquired under monochromatic illumination.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Film preparation

First, 50 nm thick layers of PTFE were deposited on pre‐cleaned glass substrates via thermal evaporation. The PTFE layers were rubbed with a cloth at a pressure of 5 kg/cm2 in a given direction at a speed of 5 mm/s along the middle line of the substrates. 20 The bisazo‐dye BA, purchased from the Japanese Research Institute for Photosensitizing Dyes (Japan), was vacuum deposited on top of the PTFE alignment layers at a rate of 3 Å/s to obtain molecularly oriented dye films. The film thicknesses presented in this paper were determined based on the results of a profiler (P‐10, KLA‐Tencor), as detailed in the Supporting Information.

Characterisation

The absorption spectra of the thin films and CHCl3 solution were acquired using a UV–vis–NIR scanning absorption spectrophotometer (V‐670, JASCO). An integrating sphere (ISN‐923, JASCO) was used for the thin‐film measurements to evaluate the intrinsic absorption of the film. The linear polarisation of the light was controlled using an ultra‐broadband wire‐grid linear polariser and a quartz depolariser. The POM images were obtained using a microscope (Eclipse E600 POL Nikon) with 50× NA 0.80 objective lens. A one‐Nicol configuration was employed, and the transmission axis of the polariser was set parallel to the rubbing direction unless otherwise noted. The parallel configuration in the POM observation was confirmed, as detailed in Figures S1 and S2. To observe the crystalline phases and J‐aggregates, light was monochromatised by inserting 50 mm size square interference filters (bandpass width (FWHM): 10–15 nm) into the light path. The surface morphology of the films was investigated using AFM (NanoNavi IIs/NanoCute, Seiko Instruments) in dynamic force mode. Local reflection spectra were acquired using a hyperspectral camera (NH‐8, EBA JAPAN) mounted on a polarising microscope (Eclipse E600 POL Nikon). The reflection image was generated from the hyperspectral data by assigning 620, 550, and 440 nm bands to the red, green, and blue channels, respectively.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Molecularly oriented J‐aggregate films

Photographs of the sample shown in Figure 1B were captured using a polariser, with the transmission axis of the polariser parallel or perpendicular to the rubbing direction. The colour intensity in the rubbed area depended on the relationship between the rubbing direction and the direction of the polarising sheet. The film appeared nearly transparent in the perpendicular configuration, indicating a high degree of orientation of the bisazo dye on the rubbed PTFE film. Figure 1C shows the polarised absorption spectra of the oriented film along with the unpolarised absorption spectrum of BA in chloroform (CHCl3) solution. The polarised spectra were measured using an integrating sphere to evaluate the intrinsic absorption.

Under light polarisation parallel to the rubbing direction, the film exhibits a distinct absorption peak significantly redshifted from the absorption spectrum of the dye in CHCl3 solution, centred at ∼580 nm. This peak was shifted from the isolated molecules by over 80 nm (0.34 eV). Therefore, the peak must be caused by the J‐aggregates, although the peak was not particularly prominent owing to the local formation of the J‐aggregates, as explained later. Under light polarisation perpendicular to the rubbing direction, the absorbance is considerably low, and the dichroic ratio (D) at the peak wavelength is remarkably high, reaching 12 (Figure S3).

Local formation of J‐aggregates

Polarised optical microscopy and atomic force microscopy

The absorption peak at 580 nm is attributed to the J‐aggregates, whereas the peak at ∼520 nm, on the lower‐energy side of the absorption band of the solution, is associated with the crystalline phase. Therefore, the distributions of the crystalline phase and J‐aggregates could be analysed by POM employing monochromatic light. Figure 2 shows the POM images obtained using white light and monochromatic light, as well as an AFM image of the same area on the thin film. The AFM image (Figure 2A) reveals that the thin film consists of grains smaller than 1 µm. The AFM image and the green POM image (Figure 2B), which represents the crystalline phase, are similar, thereby confirming that the grains can be successfully observed using POM and are predominantly composed of the crystalline phase (see also Figure S4). Additionally, the red POM image (Figure 2C) was obtained using monochromatic light at 625 nm, at which the crystalline phase is negligible, allowing selective observation of the J‐aggregate distribution. The red POM image (Figure 2C) differs significantly from the AFM image, indicating that the J‐aggregate distribution is distinct from that of the crystalline phase and grains. For example, the large grain indicated by the blue arrow in Figure 2C is transparent to light at 625 nm, signalling the absence of J‐aggregates. Instead, J‐aggregates are present in the dark areas.

AFM (A) and POM images obtained using 520 nm (B), 625 nm (C) monochromatic light, and white light (D). The thin white lines represent the grain boundaries defined by the AFM image. The image size is 8 µm × 8 µm. The light polarisation in the POM images was parallel to the rubbing direction. The film thickness was 116 nm.

It is also possible to explain the spatial distribution of J‐aggregates in the POM image (Figure 2D) obtained using white light. The colours in the image are not uniform and include light red–purple and dark blue–purple areas. This is because J‐aggregates strongly suppress the transmission of red light, whereas the crystalline phase does not efficiently transmit light at wavelengths shorter than 580 nm. Therefore, these distinct absorption characteristics lead to the observed colour distribution. Judging from the distribution of the crystalline phase and the J‐aggregates, oligomers and other aggregates are likely to exist around the J‐aggregates. These J‐aggregate–containing phases (JCPs) appear dark blue–purple colour. Again, the coexistence of two phases with distinct absorption properties directly explains the colour distribution observed in the white‐light POM image (Figure 2D). As mentioned earlier, the large grain indicated by the blue arrow in Figure 2C is transparent to light at 625 nm, indicating that this grain does not contain J‐aggregates. Therefore, the crystalline phase appears light red–purple, as indicated by the blue arrow in Figure 2D. On the other hand, the dark region (yellow arrow in Figure 2C) results from strong absorption at 625 nm, indicating the presence of J‐aggregates. Consequently, the regions containing J‐aggregates appear dark blue–purple in the white‐light POM image (Figure 2D).

Local reflection spectra

To obtain definitive spectroscopic evidence for the localised formation of J‐aggregates, the surface of the thin film was analysed using a POM equipped with a hyperspectral camera. This technique allows for the acquisition of a reflection spectrum at each pixel of the image, providing not only the average reflection spectrum of the entire film area but also enabling the evaluation of reflectance in localised regions, potentially even within individual grains. Figure 3A shows a reflection image obtained using a hyperspectral camera mounted on the POM under episcopic illumination. In this image, a colour distribution is apparent across the entire field of view. Various regions with different colours (indicated by arrows B*–I*) and their corresponding localised reflection spectra are plotted in Figure 3B–I, respectively. Among them, the red regions (indicated by arrows B*–D*) consistently exhibit sharp and intense reflection peaks (Figure 3B–D). These sharp peaks are characteristic of J‐aggregate formation, arising from the narrow absorption band of J‐aggregates that drastically alters the film's optical constants (i.e., the refractive index (n) and the extinction coefficient (k)). 28

(B–I) Local reflection spectra corresponding to the regions indicated by arrows (B*–I*) in (a). Spectra (B–D) correspond to the red regions (indicated by arrows B*–D*), while spectra (E–I) correspond to the other coloured regions (indicated by arrows E*–I*). Arrow (J) indicates a void region. The film thickness was 112 nm.

This hyperspectral analysis with spatially resolved spectroscopy confirms the results of the monochromatic POM analysis (Figure 2). While the monochromatic images indicate that the JCP distribution differed from that of the crystalline grains, this hyperspectral analysis provides more direct spectroscopic evidence for this distinction. The J‐aggregates are not uniformly distributed within the crystalline grains but are instead confined to specific and localised domains, which appear as red regions in the reflection image. The identification between the results of local spectroscopy and POM greatly guarantees each other's results. In addition, it can be confirmed that the dark blue–purple region in the transmission POM image, which indicates JCP, appears red in the reflection image captured by the hyperspectral camera (Figure S5).

Our previous assumption 17 that J‐aggregates of nonionic bisazomethine dyes may exist near grain boundaries is corroborated by the results of this study. This work also clarifies that J‐aggregates of nonionic bisazo dyes are primarily located on grain surfaces and edges. Several research groups have studied the formation of J‐aggregates and have analysed the molecular orientation of the aggregates using polarised microscopy. 29 , 30 , 31 For instance, various assemblies involving J‐aggregates have been investigated. Examples include the large‐scale aligned J‐aggregates of porphyrin in water, 32 J‐aggregate polymorphs of a cyanine dye, 33 and core–shell‐type liquid crystals composed of hybrid J‐ and H‐type aggregates. 34 Previous studies have also focused on the distribution of J‐aggregates, 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 such as their formation within fibres 38 , 39 and their coexistence with other phases, as revealed by techniques such as scanning near‐field optical microscopy imaging 40 and fluorescence microscopy. 41 In this study, we demonstrate that the spatial distribution of JCP in vacuum‐deposited films can be analysed using general methods such as AFM and POM, and we successfully visualise the locations of locally formed J‐aggregates. Furthermore, the local reflection spectra of the regions where J‐aggregates exist could be confirmed using a hyperspectral camera.

Growth of grains and J‐aggregates

Figure 4 shows the thickness dependence of the AFM images of the vacuum‐deposited films, illustrating grain growth. The cross‐sectional data is confirmed in Figure S6. During deposition, numerous small grains were present in the initial stages, which grew gradually. Simultaneously, the voids between the grains expanded. Therefore, in addition to molecular deposition, molecular migration promotes grain growth.

AFM images of 14 (A), 25 (B), 35 (C), 53 (D), 80 (E), 105 (F) 147 (G), and 168 (H) nm thick vacuum‐deposited BA films. The image size is 5 µm × 5 µm.

POM was also performed to analyse the deposition process. During the early stages of deposition (Figure 5), the resolution of the POM images is insufficient for observing small grains, which are resolved in the AFM images. As the film thickness increases, dark blue–purple regions emerge, indicating the onset of J‐aggregate formation. With a further increase in the film thickness, the colour darkens and the voids expand, consistent with the AFM images. The local formation of J‐aggregates is confirmed even in the 216 nm thick film, as shown in Figure S7. Moreover, the POM images reveal that the J‐aggregates (dark blue–purple) begin to form in the early stages, when the film thickness is <50 nm.

POM images of 25 (A), 49 (B), 51 (C), 68 (D), 91 (E), 112 (F) 129 (G), and 216 (H) nm thick vacuum‐deposited BA films. The image size is 8 µm × 8 µm. The light polarisation was parallel to the rubbing direction.

Based on the results, the grain growth and J‐aggregate formation processes can be described using a model that does not contradict the model derived for bisazomethine dyes. 17 The deposition process, which involves molecular migration and grain growth, results in the formation of a temporarily unstable thin film. Consequently, in addition to the nuclei of the most stable crystalline phase, the nuclei of metastable states that can grow as J‐aggregates are also expected to form (Figure 6A, B, D, and E). As the grains grow, their crystalline nature generally becomes dominant, where the centre of the grains is likely dominated by the crystalline phase, whereas the grain surface likely contains metastable J‐aggregates (Figure 6C and F). This explains the spatial distribution of the crystalline phase and JCP, indicating that most of the JCP form on the sides and boundaries of the grains. 17 Therefore, regarding the J‐aggregates as a type of metastable crystalline polymorph can explain the observed spatial distribution of the crystalline phase and JCP, as shown in Figure 2 .

(C) Dominant crystal growth; JCP is formed on the sides of large grains. Drawings (A–C) show bottom views, and the corresponding cross‐sectional views are shown in (D–F).

CONCLUSION

We demonstrated the local formation of J‐aggregates in molecularly oriented bisazo‐dye films deposited on a PTFE alignment layer. The polarised absorption spectra of the films exhibited a large bathochromic shift, indicating the formation of J‐aggregates. The J‐aggregates are highly oriented, with a dichroic ratio of >12. By monochromatising the POM light source to each wavelength corresponding to the absorption of the crystalline phase or J‐aggregates, we observed them separately. Using AFM and POM to observe the same region of the molecularly oriented thin film, the JCP distribution was found to differ from that of the crystalline phase and grains. Furthermore, spectral analysis using a hyperspectral camera provides direct spectroscopic evidence for the local formation of J‐aggregates. The results clearly indicate that J‐aggregates and the crystalline phase coexist in the thin film with distinct spatial distributions.

Considering the increase in JCP‐related dark blue–purple regions with film thickness, as well as the grain growth driven by molecular migration and deposition, we believe that the J‐aggregates are mainly localised on the surface and sides of the grains. This distribution can be explained by the model in which the J‐aggregates are considered to be a type of metastable polymorph that emerges with the growth of the crystalline phase. Our findings provide significant insights into the formation process of J‐aggregates and represent an important step toward controlling their formation in high‐performance organic optoelectronic thin films.
