Impact of Autoimmune Hypothyroidism and Celiac Disease on Progression to Diabetes in Individuals Positive for GAD65 or IA‐2 Autoantibodies
Authors: Afif Nakhleh, Gil Beeri, Omer Sudri, Shay Rotkopf, Naim Shehadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo assess whether autoimmune hypothyroidism (AH) or celiac disease (CD) influences diabetes progression among individuals who tested positive on a pooled screening assay for glutamic acid decarboxylase 65 and insulinoma‐associated protein 2 autoantibodies (GADA/IA‐2A).Material and MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used data from Maccabi Healthcare Services, Israel (2010–2024), and included 363 GADA/IA‐2A‐positive individuals without diabetes. In a 316‐patient subcohort, individual GADA autoreactivity was assessed. The primary outcome was incident clinical diabetes. Cox regression estimated hazard ratios (HRs) for incident diabetes associated with AH, CD, and asthma (comparator), modelled as time‐dependent covariates, adjusting for age, sex, socioeconomic status and baseline fasting plasma glucose.ResultsOver a median 5.8‐year follow‐up, 75 individuals (20.7%) developed clinical diabetes. Progression occurred in 35.8% of dysglyceamic versus 9.9% of normoglycemic individuals (p < 0.001). The prevalence of AH or CD was higher among progressors (21.3% vs. 10.4%; p = 0.01). Adjusted HRs (95% CI) were 2.55 (1.40–4.64) for the composite of AH or CD, 2.85 (1.00–8.15) for AH, and 2.41 (1.21–4.81) for CD. The HR for the composite association was 3.08 (1.36–3.96) in individuals aged < 18 years and 2.10 (0.86–5.12) in adults (p for interaction = 0.56). In a secondary analysis of the subcohort with individual GADA assessment (n = 316), further adjustment for GADA status attenuated the composite HR to 1.97 (1.06–3.65).ConclusionsConcurrent AH or CD is associated with accelerated diabetes progression in autoantibody‐positive individuals. Although this partly reflects a shared polyautoimmune predisposition, both conditions may serve as accessible markers for risk stratification.

Introduction

The presymptomatic continuum of type 1 diabetes (T1D) is clinically staged by the presence of circulating islet autoantibodies and the subsequent onset of early dysglycaemia [1, 2, 3, 4], yet the rate of progression to clinical disease remains highly heterogeneous [5, 6]. Although autoimmune hypothyroidism (AH) and celiac disease (CD) frequently cluster with T1D [7, 8, 9] and increase the risk of incident disease in the general population [10], their specific contribution to progression risk among individuals already harbouring islet autoimmunity has not been defined. We therefore aimed to assess whether concurrent AH or CD independently influences the risk of progression to clinical diabetes in normoglycemic and dysglycaemic individuals who tested positive on a pooled screening assay for glutamic acid decarboxylase 65 (GAD65) and insulinoma‐associated protein 2 (IA‐2) autoantibodies (GADA/IA‐2A).

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This retrospective cohort study utilised anonymised data from Maccabi Healthcare Services (MHS), Israel's second‐largest health maintenance organization (HMO), which covers approximately 25% of the population. This representative sample provides comprehensive electronic health records, including demographics, diagnoses, healthcare utilisation, interventions, medications and laboratory results. We included individuals of all ages who had a positive pooled GADA/IA‐2A screen between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2024. The index date was defined as the date of the first positive pooled GADA/IA‐2A screen. To ensure that the cohort captured active progression towards disease rather than pre‐existing conditions, we excluded individuals already recorded in the MHS diabetes registry before or within 3 months after the index date. Follow‐up concluded at the time of clinical diabetes diagnosis (defined as inclusion in the MHS diabetes registry), disenrollment from MHS, death or 30 September 2025, whichever occurred first.

Definitions of Study Variables

Clinical and biochemical data were derived from the MHS central database. All laboratory samples were analysed at an MHS‐certified central laboratory.

Pooled islet autoantibody screening for anti‐GAD65 and anti‐IA‐2 was performed using two commercial antigen‐bridging enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs). From January 2010 to December 2020, screening used the AESKULISA IA2‐GAD65 ELISA (AESKU.DIAGNOSTICS, Wendelsheim, Germany), which captures bivalent autoantibodies using a recombinant fusion protein (positivity threshold: ≥ 30 IU/mL). In January 2021, the laboratory transitioned to the Anti‐GAD65/IA2 Pool ELISA (IgG) (EUROIMMUN AG, Lübeck, Germany), utilising distinct pooled GAD65 and IA2 antigens (positivity threshold: ≥ 4 IU/mL). Both assays employ divalent binding to prevent detection of low‐affinity antibodies. Given the pooled nature of these screens, a positive result indicates the presence of anti‐GAD65, anti‐IA2 or both.

For a subset of the cohort, specific GAD65 autoreactivity was further assessed at the index date using individual GAD65 autoantibody assays from the respective manufacturers for each time period: the AESKULISA GAD65 ELISA (positivity threshold: ≥ 30 IU/mL) for the earlier period, and the EUROIMMUN Anti‐GAD ELISA (positivity threshold: ≥ 10 IU/mL) for the later period.

Comorbidities were identified using standard International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐9‐CM) codes or through inclusion in dedicated MHS chronic disease registries for diabetes (criteria detailed in Supporting Information Table S1) [11, 12], hypertension [13], chronic kidney disease [14] and cardiovascular disease (comprising ischemic heart disease, peripheral or cerebrovascular disease, heart failure, and cardiac arrhythmias including atrial fibrillation and flutter) [15], and osteoporosis [16].

Baseline characteristics, measured within 6 months preceding or on the index date, included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) and socioeconomic status (SES). SES was based on residential socioeconomic data from the Israeli Central Bureau of Statistics, as previously described [17], using the national 1–10 residential SES scale. SES was categorised as low [1, 2, 3, 4], medium [5, 6, 7], or high [8, 9, 10]. Baseline dysglycaemia was defined as FPG ≥ 100 mg/dL.

The primary outcome was a clinical diagnosis of diabetes, defined as formal inclusion in the MHS diabetes registry [11, 12]. Primary autoimmune exposures were AH, defined by ICD‐9‐CM codes and positive thyroid peroxidase (TPO) antibodies, and CD, defined by ICD‐9‐CM codes alone (Supporting Information Table S1). Asthma was included as a non‐autoimmune comparator to assess the specificity of autoimmune associations. Status for AH, CD and asthma was evaluated at baseline (index date) and at the end of follow‐up (or at the time of diabetes diagnosis for those who progressed).

Ethics Statement

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of MHS (protocol number MHS‐0033‐25, dated 6 August 2025). Due to the retrospective nature of the analysis involving de‐identified data, a waiver of informed consent was granted.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages (n [%]). Differences in baseline characteristics between progressors and non‐progressors were evaluated using Student's t‐tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, chi‐square tests or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate.

To provide a life‐course perspective accounting for cumulative autoimmune exposure, person‐time was calculated from birth to the earliest of diabetes diagnosis or end of follow‐up, and crude incidence rates of diabetes per 1000 person‐years were derived accordingly for each comorbidity stratum, with exact Poisson 95% confidence intervals. Incidence rate ratios (IRRs) were derived as the ratio of incidence rates in individuals with versus without each comorbidity to provide unadjusted estimates of association.

To evaluate the independent association of AH, CD, and asthma with diabetes progression while accounting for potential confounders, Cox proportional hazards regression models were used, with time from the index date to diabetes diagnosis or end of follow‐up. AH, CD, and asthma were modelled as time‐dependent covariates, with their status updated at the date of each diagnosis during follow‐up. Models were adjusted for age at index, sex, SES and baseline FPG. Prespecified age‐stratified analyses (< 18 years vs. ≥ 18 years) were performed. A sensitivity analysis adjusting for baseline HbA1c instead of baseline FPG was conducted. The proportional hazards assumption was verified and satisfied for the primary comorbidity exposures (p > 0.05). Additionally, E‐values were calculated to quantify the robustness of the primary estimate to potential unmeasured confounding.

An additional analysis was performed on the subcohort with individual GADA autoreactivity assessment (n = 316) using a multivariable Cox regression model with time‐dependent comorbidity covariates adjusted for age at index, sex, SES, baseline FPG and GADA status (positive vs. negative).

To address potential confounding introduced by the transition between commercial screening assays, we performed prespecified, era‐stratified sensitivity analyses. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, with AH, CD and asthma modelled as time‐dependent covariates, were run independently for the ‘earlier assay era’ (index date ≤ 2020; AESKULISA) and the ‘later assay era’ (index date ≥ 2021; EUROIMMUN) in both the full cohort and subcohort with available individual GADA status.

Statistical significance was set at a two‐tailed p value of < 0.05. Data analysis was performed using SPSS software, version 29 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Of 3187 individuals who tested positive on the pooled GADA/IA‐2A screen during the study period, 363 met the eligibility criteria and comprised the final study cohort (Figure 1). The mean age was 27.1 ± 18.4 years (52.9% female). Over a median follow‐up of 5.8 years (IQR 2.2–9.4), 75 participants (20.7%) progressed to incident clinical diabetes. Among progressors, the median time to progression was 1.6 years (IQR 0.8–3.6). Baseline demographic and anthropometric parameters, including age, sex and BMI, did not differ significantly between progressors and non‐progressors (Table 1). Baseline median FPG was higher among progressors (107 mg/dL [IQR 95–116]) than non‐progressors (96 mg/dL [IQR 90–103]; p < 0.001). Baseline median HbA1c was also significantly higher in progressors than in non‐progressors (5.9% [IQR, 5.7–6.1] vs. 5.5% [IQR, 5.2–5.9]; p < 0.001) (Table 1). Progression occurred significantly more often among individuals with baseline dysglycemia (FPG ≥ 100 mg/dL) compared with those with normoglycemia (35.8% vs. 9.9%; p < 0.001).

At the index date, the prevalence of AH or CD was significantly higher among individuals who subsequently progressed to diabetes compared with non‐progressors (16.0% vs. 7.3%; p = 0.03) (Table 1). At the end of follow‐up (or at diabetes diagnosis for progressors), the prevalence of AH or CD remained higher among those who developed diabetes compared with those who did not (21.3% vs. 10.4%; p = 0.01). Evaluated individually, AH was present in 8.0% of progressors versus 3.1% of non‐progressors (p = 0.08), and CD in 14.7% versus 7.3% (p = 0.05) (Table 2). When stratified by age, the prevalence of AH or CD was significantly higher among progressors than non‐progressors in the paediatric cohort (< 18 years: 25.0% vs. 9.6%; p = 0.02), but not in adults (≥ 18 years: 17.1% vs. 11.1%; p = 0.20) (Table 2).

The incidence rate of diabetes was 12.2 (95% CI 7.2–19.3) among individuals with AH or CD versus 5.6 (95% CI 4.3–7.1) per 1000 person‐years in those without either condition (crude IRR 2.2; 95% CI 1.2–3.7) (Supporting Information Table S3). In age‐stratified analysis, the IRR for the composite of AH or CD was 2.68 (95% CI 1.25–5.30) in individuals < 18 years, compared to 1.67 (95% CI 0.63–3.75) in adults ≥ 18 years (Supporting Information Table S3).

In multivariable Cox proportional hazards models with AH, CD and asthma modelled as time‐dependent covariates and adjusted for age, sex, SES and baseline FPG, the hazard ratio (HR) for incident diabetes was 2.55 (95% CI 1.40–4.64) for individuals with AH or CD compared with those without either condition (Table 2). When analysed separately, the HR was 2.85 (95% CI 1.00–8.15) for AH and 2.41 (95% CI 1.21–4.81) for CD. The HR for asthma was 2.14 (95% CI 0.96–4.76) (Table 2).

In age‐stratified analyses, the association between AH or CD and diabetes progression yielded an HR of 3.08 (95% CI 1.36–3.96; n = 175) in individuals aged < 18 years and 2.10 (95% CI 0.86–5.12; n = 188) in adults, with no significant age‐by‐comorbidity interactions (Table 2).

A sensitivity analysis adjusting for baseline HbA1c (instead of FPG) yielded consistent results (Supporting Information Table S4).

In the subcohort of 316 individuals with available individual GADA status, multivariable Cox models with time‐dependent comorbidity covariates further adjusted for GADA status (positive vs. negative) showed adjusted HRs of 1.97 (95% CI 1.06–3.65) for AH or CD, 2.24 (95% CI 0.79–6.37) for AH, 1.86 (95% CI 0.91–3.81) for CD and 1.63 (95% CI 0.73–3.64) for asthma (Supporting Information Table S5). Within this evaluated subcohort, individual GADA positivity was significantly less prevalent among children compared to adults (34.6% vs. 72.6%; p < 0.001) (Supporting Information Table S2).

Compared with GADA‐negative individuals (n = 147), those who were GADA‐positive (n = 169) had a higher rate of progression to clinical diabetes (32.0% vs. 11.6%; p < 0.001; HR 4.35, 95% CI 2.49–7.57). The median time to diabetes onset did not differ significantly between the groups (1.7 [IQR 0.7–3.4] years for GADA‐positive vs. 1.5 [IQR 0.8–3.9] years for GADA‐negative; p = 0.84).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 363 GADA/IA‐2A‐positive individuals, concurrent AH or CD was associated with more than a doubling of the risk of progression to clinical diabetes.

A major strength of this study is its comprehensive, population‐based cohort drawn from a large health maintenance organisation, which ensures rigorous longitudinal tracking with standardized diagnostic criteria. Furthermore, the inclusion of asthma as a chronic non‐autoimmune comparator provides a negative control, suggesting that the accelerated risk is specific to autoimmune comorbidity rather than reflecting the general burden of chronic illness.

Our findings extend recent large‐scale observational data demonstrating the longitudinal impact of these autoimmune comorbidities. Edelman et al. reported that individuals with hypothyroidism or CD face a roughly 2.4‐ to 2.5‐fold increased risk of developing incident T1D compared with matched controls [9]. Similarly, Twig et al., evaluating a large cohort of Israeli adolescents, found that pre‐existing autoimmune thyroid disease (HR 3.99) and CD (HR 2.82) were associated with increased T1D risk in adulthood [10].

While these studies established this epidemiological association in broad populations, our findings uniquely localize this accelerated trajectory to the presymptomatic, autoantibody‐positive window. The underlying biology is coherent: AH, CD and T1D share HLA‐DQ2/DQ8‐mediated genetic susceptibility alongside common single nucleotide polymorphisms in immune‐regulatory genes such as CTLA4, PTPN22 and IL2RA [7, 8], and their clinical co‐occurrence reflects significant autoantibody cross‐positivity [7].

A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis reported that approximately 14.5% of individuals with T1D exhibit latent polyautoimmunity (defined by non‐islet autoantibodies) and 8.5% have overt polyautoimmunity (clinically manifest autoimmune disease), with autoimmune thyroid disease being the most common co‐occurrence [18]. Our data extend this framework by demonstrating that such polyautoimmunity is already identifiable and prognostically relevant at the presymptomatic stage.

In age‐stratified analyses, the hazard ratio for the association between AH or CD and diabetes progression was numerically higher in younger individuals (< 18 years) than in adults, though formal interaction testing by age was not statistically significant. The greater magnitude of association observed in the paediatric cohort aligns with established evidence that beta‐cell destruction progresses more rapidly in children [5] and that thyroid autoimmunity and CD strongly predict T1D in younger cohorts [10]. Furthermore, a Swedish registry study showed that paediatric autoimmune comorbidities (especially celiac and thyroid disease) are highly prevalent in young individuals with T1D, although this co‐occurrence may not worsen post‐diagnosis metabolic control or mortality [19]. Because interaction tests often require large sample sizes, the lack of significance may simply reflect limited statistical power in our cohort. Alternatively, a true biological divergence may exist, as Templeman et al. recently demonstrated, autoantibody‐positive adults exhibit distinct progression trajectories compared with children, emphasising the need for tailored, age‐specific monitoring [5]. Further studies should clarify if paediatric and adult populations require distinct risk‐stratification approaches.

Adjusting for individual GADA status attenuated the magnitude of the HR for AH or CD, although it remained statistically significant at 1.97 (95% CI 1.06–3.65). This association demonstrates that AH and CD confer an independent risk for diabetes progression. Notably, individual GADA positivity was significantly less prevalent in the paediatric cohort (34.6%) compared to adults (72.6%). Although children and adults exhibited similar overall progression rates and median time to onset, the HR for the AH/CD association was numerically higher in the paediatric group, where GADA prevalence was lowest. This suggests that AH and CD may capture prognostic information that is not fully explained by GADA alone, particularly in children, and may reflect shared genetic susceptibility through pathways not captured by a single autoantibody measurement. Unlike IA‐2A, which is more specific to rapid beta‐cell destruction, GADA often emerges early as a marker of systemic autoimmune activation and correlates with concurrent celiac and thyroid disease [20, 21, 22]. This shared polyautoimmune predisposition is likely driven by pleiotropic genetics, most notably the HLA‐DR3‐DQ2 haplotype [23]. Clinically, these observations support the prognostic utility of AH and CD. Because they are routinely documented in electronic health records, they serve as practical clinical markers that flag a high‐risk, polyautoimmune phenotype warranting closer glycemic surveillance and earlier specialist referral.

While existing staging models emphasize beta‐cell markers and glycemic deterioration [1, 24, 25, 26], our results introduce an extra‐pancreatic dimension to risk stratification. We demonstrate that concurrent AH or CD significantly amplifies early‐stage progression risk. Because these conditions are routinely identifiable in electronic health records, they serve as practical, low‐cost risk modifiers for screening programs. This holds particular urgency following the approval of teplizumab, which can delay clinical T1D onset in at‐risk individuals [27, 28]. By refining presymptomatic risk stratification, accessible markers like AH and CD could potentially supplement existing screening approaches and help identify high‐risk individuals for consideration of immunomodulatory intervention.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, the retrospective design precludes causal inference. Importantly, the clinical rationale for initiating autoantibody screening is not systematically recorded in the database. Tests may have been driven by clinical suspicion of dysglycemia, a family history of T1D, or surveillance of patients with known autoimmune disease. Consequently, individuals with pre‐existing AH or CD are likely over‐represented in our cohort due to clinician awareness of autoimmune clustering. This referral‐driven case mix limits generalizability to unselected populations. However, while this ascertainment bias may inflate the baseline prevalence of AH and CD, it is unlikely to fully explain the differential rate of progression. Once identified as autoantibody‐positive, all individuals required active endocrinological follow‐up. Therefore, surveillance bias alone would not generate a faster rate of progression, suggesting that the accelerated risk associated with AH and CD reflects a true prognostic signal. Furthermore, while AH, CD and asthma were modelled as time‐dependent covariates to account for differing observation periods, non‐progressors had a longer follow‐up (median of 5.8 vs. 1.6 years) and therefore greater opportunity to accumulate comorbidities. Despite this, the prevalence of AH and CD remained significantly higher among progressors.

Second, unmeasured confounders and missing data restrict deeper phenotypic and mechanistic characterisation. Data on family history of T1D, family history of other autoimmune diseases, and exposure to viral infections were unavailable. However, the calculated E‐value of 4.54 for the primary composite point estimate suggests that a substantial degree of unmeasured confounding would be required to fully explain away the observed primary association (Table 2). Furthermore, while individual GADA status was assessed in a subcohort, the lack of data on additional islet autoantibodies (including individual autoantibodies against IA‐2, insulin, or zinc transporter 8) precluded the confirmation of multiple autoantibody positivity. The pooled GADA/IA‐2A primary screening assay also cannot distinguish between single and dual islet‐antibody positivity, which carry different progression risks, as individuals with multiple autoantibodies represent a high‐risk group [5]. Additionally, while our models adjust for baseline FPG and HbA1c, the exact duration of pre‐existing dysglycemia (Stage 2 T1D) prior to the index date remains unknown. This may introduce bias, as varying durations of preceding Stage 2 disease can affect the timeline to clinical progression. Furthermore, our dataset lacks sufficient repeated measures of HbA1c or FPG during the follow‐up period, precluding our ability to model these metrics as time‐dependent variables to track longitudinal glycemic trajectories. The absence of HLA genotyping further limits mechanistic interpretation. Additionally, relying on ICD‐9 diagnostic codes without serological or biopsy confirmation introduces a risk of misclassification for comorbidities such as CD. The small number of patients with concurrent AH or CD limits statistical power. Consequently, the overall findings must be interpreted within the constraints of these missing covariates and sample size limitations.

Finally, a critical methodological consideration is the laboratory's transition between two screening assays with differing numerical thresholds. Era‐stratified sensitivity analyses (Supporting Information Tables S6 and S7) revealed instability: confidence intervals for the composite of AH or CD consistently crossed the null in the early‐assay era, whereas the late‐assay era produced highly inflated hazard ratios with extremely wide confidence intervals. This instability is likely driven by sparse‐data bias arising from the small number of events within the divided subcohorts. While the pooled primary model retains the full sample, the assay transition itself remains a potential source of heterogeneity, and the overall association should therefore still be interpreted with caution.

In conclusion, concurrent AH or CD is associated with a significantly higher risk of progression to clinical diabetes in individuals with islet autoimmunity. Incorporating AH and CD into screening frameworks could serve as an accessible, low‐cost strategy to identify high‐risk individuals warranting intensified glycemic surveillance and earlier specialist referral. Prospective validation in larger, multi‐ethnic cohorts incorporating comprehensive autoantibody characterisation and HLA genotyping is needed to confirm these findings and determine whether these markers can optimally augment existing risk prediction models.
