Diagnostic Value of Calcaneofibular Ligament Tortuosity on Oblique Coronal MRI in Chronic Ankle Instability
Authors: Jin Su Kim, Sang-Bum Kim, Ken Meng Tai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Lateral ankle sprains are common injuries that may progress to chronic ankle instability (CAI). Although stress radiographs are widely used to assess mechanical instability, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based parameters for evaluating calcaneofibular ligament (CFL) instability remain limited. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the diagnostic value of the CFL tortuosity angle (C angle) measured on oblique coronal MRI in patients with CAI and to examine its relationship with clinical and radiographic measures.Methods:A retrospective analysis of 120 patients (82 CAI, 38 controls) was performed. The C angle was measured on oblique coronal T2-weighted MRI. Talar tilt angle was assessed using stress radiography in the CAI group. Functional outcomes and ankle eversion strength were also evaluated.Results:A total of 120 patients were included, comprising 82 patients with CAI and 38 controls without a history of ankle sprain. The C angle was significantly greater in the CAI group compared with controls (76.5° ± 18.3° vs 31.9° ± 5.4°, P < .001). Receiver operating characteristic analysis demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance (area under the curve, 0.97), with an optimal cutoff of 40.9° (sensitivity, 97.6%; specificity, 100%). The C angle showed a moderate correlation with talar tilt angle (r = 0.327, P = .003) but limited association with functional scores and muscle strength.Conclusion:The CFL tortuosity angle on oblique coronal T2-weighted MRI demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy for CAI and may serve as a useful adjunctive parameter in evaluating ligament instability.Level of Evidence:Level IV, retrospective comparative study.Keywords: ankle, instability, chronic, calcaneofibular ligament, magnetic resonance imaging, tortuosity angle

Introduction

Lateral ankle sprain is one of the most common musculoskeletal injuries in both athletic and general populations and is a well-established precursor to chronic ankle instability (CAI).1,2 In the United States, approximately 30 000 ankle sprains occur daily, and a substantial proportion of patients seek emergency care. 1 Despite appropriate management, including immobilization and rehabilitation, 20% to 40% of patients with lateral ligament injury progress to persistent mechanical and functional instability consistent with CAI.1,3 Patients with CAI commonly report recurrent sprains, activity-related pain, and a subjective sense of giving way during daily and athletic activities.

The progression to CAI is multifactorial. Greater initial injury severity, recurrent sprains, elevated body mass index, and inadequate early management—particularly when the injury is underestimated and appropriate treatment is not pursued—have been associated with increased risk of chronic instability.3,4 Mechanical instability is primarily attributed to insufficiency of the lateral ligament complex, most commonly involving the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) and calcaneofibular ligament (CFL). Combined injuries of the ATFL and CFL are frequently observed and have been associated with inferior clinical outcomes compared with isolated ATFL injury.5,6

Manual anterior drawer and talar tilt examinations are commonly used to evaluate mechanical instability. Furthermore, stress radiography provides additional information, with the talar tilt angle widely used to quantify lateral ligament laxity and guide surgical decision making. 6 However, both physical examination and stress radiography may be influenced by examiner-dependent factors, and stress testing may not always be feasible in the acute setting or in patients with pain intolerance. 4

Recent studies have demonstrated that quantitative evaluation of CFL injury using oblique coronal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is feasible. Nekomoto et al 7 reported that MRI-based parameters, including the CFL cross angle and fibulocalcaneal angle (FCA), were associated with CFL injury requiring surgical repair in patients with chronic lateral ankle instability. However, their study focused on identifying CFL involvement within a surgically treated CAI population. In contrast, the ability of MRI-derived morphologic parameters to discriminate patients with CAI from individuals without ankle instability has not been established. Whether CFL tortuosity may provide additional diagnostic information in the evaluation of CAI remains to be determined.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the CFL tortuosity angle (C angle) measured on oblique coronal MRI in patients with CAI (Figure 1). Specifically, we aimed to assess its discriminative ability in relation to stress radiographic findings and to establish an optimal diagnostic threshold for identifying CAI. We hypothesized that the C angle would be significantly greater in patients with CAI, demonstrate high diagnostic accuracy, and show a positive correlation with measures of mechanical instability.

Materials and Methods

This retrospective case-control comparative study included patients who presented to our clinic with ankle symptoms between March 2020 and May 2022. Patients were divided into a CAI group and a control group. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from our institutional review board (SUIRB-HR-2026-002), and informed consent was secured from all participants.

The CAI group consisted of patients diagnosed with CAI by a single fellowship-trained foot and ankle specialist. Inclusion required both mechanical instability on physical examination, demonstrated by a positive anterior drawer test and/or talar tilt test, and functional instability defined as a Cumberland Ankle Instability Tool score 8 of less than 20 points.

The control group comprised patients diagnosed with acute Achilles tendon rupture who had no prior history of lateral ankle sprain.

Exclusion criteria included acute lateral ligament tears, peroneal tendon pathology, concomitant medial ankle instability, trauma related to traffic accidents, inflammatory arthropathy (including rheumatoid arthritis), and generalized ligamentous laxity.

The CAI group included 82 patients (47 males, 35 females), with a mean age of 25.7 years. Thirty-nine cases involved the right ankle and 43 involved the left ankle. The control group included 38 patients (31 males, 7 females), with a mean age of 34.0 years; 20 right ankles and 18 left ankles were affected (Table 1). As this was a retrospective study, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, and that all eligible patients during the study period were included in the analysis.

All patients underwent ankle MRI using a 3.0-tesla (T) system (Achieva; Philips Medical Systems). In addition to the standard ankle protocol, a 45° oblique coronal T2-weighted MRI sequence was obtained to align with the anatomical course of the CFL. This technique was selected to allow accurate visualization of CFL morphology (Figure 1). 9

The tortuosity angle of the CFL (C angle) was measured on the 45° oblique coronal MRI plane using 3 anatomical landmarks along the ligament: the fibular origin, the calcaneal origin, and the point of maximal ligament bending. Two-line segments were constructed by connecting the point of maximal ligament bending to the fibular and calcaneal origins, respectively. The angle formed by these 2-line segments was defined as the C angle (Figure 2). Measurements were performed using the institutional PACS system.

Measurement of the CFL tortuosity angle (C angle) on oblique coronal MRI. (A) Representative control MRI demonstrating a relatively straight CFL configuration and a small C angle. (B) Representative MRI from a patient with chronic ankle instability demonstrating increased CFL tortuosity and a larger C angle. The C angle was measured using 3 anatomical points along the CFL: the fibular origin (proximal point), the point of maximal ligament bending (middle point), and the calcaneal origin (distal point). The acute angle formed by the 2-line segments was defined as the C angle. CFL, calcaneofibular ligament; MRI, magnetic resonance imaging.

To assess interobserver reliability of the C angle measurement, 3 independent observers (2 orthopaedic surgeons and 1 physician assistant) performed measurements in a blinded fashion. Interobserver reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with a 2-way random effects model and absolute agreement. The 95% CIs for ICC values were calculated.

In the CAI group, anterior displacement and talar tilt angle were measured using a Telos device under a standardized load of 30 N. These values were compared with the C angle.

In the control group, stress radiography was not performed because of anticipated discomfort and lack of clinical indication.

Manual anterior drawer testing was graded as follows: grade 1 (<5 mm), grade 2 (5-10 mm), and grade 3 (>10 mm). Manual talar tilt testing was graded as follows: grade 1 (>5°), grade 2 (10°-15°), and grade 3 (>15°).

Preoperative manual instability findings were recorded. Isokinetic ankle eversion strength was measured using a Biodex dynamometer at an angular velocity of 30°/s, and peak torque values were analyzed. Evertor strength deficit (%) in the CAI group was calculated as 100 − [(affected side strength/contralateral side strength) × 100]. Isokinetic ankle eversion strength was measured only in patients able to complete the examination. Because of the retrospective study design, strength testing was not available for all patients, including those unable to undergo testing because of pain or logistical factors at the time of evaluation. Analyses involving muscle strength were performed using available data only (complete-case analysis).

Functional instability was assessed using the Cumberland Ankle Instability Tool (CAIT). 8

Although the oblique coronal MRI sequence was incorporated prospectively into the imaging protocol, data collection and analysis were conducted retrospectively using medical records and PACS imaging.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 14.0; SPSS Inc). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables were compared between groups using independent samples t tests for normally distributed data and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were analyzed using χ2 or Fisher exact tests as appropriate.

Correlation analyses were considered exploratory and were performed to investigate potential associations between the C angle and clinical measures of instability. Accordingly, P values from these analyses were interpreted descriptively and were not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of MRI Tortuosity Angle. The area under the curve with 95% CIs was calculated using the Hanley-McNeil method. The optimal cutoff value was determined by maximizing the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, positive likelihood ratio, and negative likelihood ratio were calculated at various cutoff points.

To adjust for potential confounding variables, multivariable linear regression analysis was conducted with the C angle as the dependent variable and group status (CAI vs control), age, sex, and body mass index (BMI) as independent variables. Standardized regression coefficients (β) were reported.

Effect size for between-group differences was calculated using Cohen d, with values of 0.2 considered small, 0.5 moderate, and ≥0.8 large. Statistical significance was defined as P <.05.

Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis demonstrated an area under the curve of 0.970 (95% CI, 0.943-0.998) for discrimination between CAI and control groups. The optimal cutoff value determined using the Youden index was 40.9°. At this threshold, sensitivity was 97.6% and specificity was 100%. The positive predictive value was 100%, the negative predictive value was 95.0%, and the overall diagnostic accuracy was 98.3% (Figure 3).

Pearson correlation analysis demonstrated weak to moderate correlations between the C angle and other measures of instability. The C angle demonstrated weak to moderate correlations with clinical measures of instability, including the manual talar tilt test (r = 0.373, P = .001; Figure 4) and manual anterior drawer test (r = 0.300, P = .007). The C angle showed weak to moderate correlations with anterior displacement (r = 0.259, P = .019) and talar tilt angle (r = 0.327, P = .003) in stress radiograph. The C angle showed a weak negative correlation with preoperative CAIT score (r = −0.201, P = .006) and no significant correlation with muscle strength deficit (r = 0.067, P = .639). Isokinetic strength data were available for 51 of the 82 patients (62.2%). The mean evertor strength deficit in the CAI group was 22.8% (Table 5).

Multivariable linear regression analysis adjusting for age, sex, and body mass index demonstrated that group status remained independently associated with C angle (β = 48.72, P < .001). The between-group difference corresponded to a large effect size (Cohen d = 2.88). Interobserver reliability for C angle measurement showed moderate to good reliability for single measurements, ICC(2, 1) = 0.72 (95% CI: 0.60-0.81), and good to excellent reliability for average measurements across 3 raters, ICC(2, k) = 0.88 (95% CI: 0.82-0.93).

Discussion

CAI is commonly associated with injury to the lateral ligament complex, particularly ATFL and CFL. In patients undergoing the Broström procedure for lateral ankle instability, isolated ATFL injury has been reported in approximately two-thirds of cases, whereas concomitant CFL injury occurs in roughly 20%. 3 However, MRI-based studies have reported a broader range of concomitant CFL injury in patients with lateral ankle sprains, with frequencies ranging from 10% to 64.2%.10,11,12 From a biomechanical perspective, combined injury of the ATFL and CFL results in greater mechanical instability compared with isolated ATFL injury. Samoto et al 6 reported that patients with combined ATFL and CFL injuries had consistently lower functional scores during follow-up compared with those with isolated ATFL injuries. These findings highlight the importance of evaluating CFL involvement when assessing patients with CAI.

During clinical evaluation, we encountered a patient who had previously undergone ankle MRI before sustaining a subsequent ankle injury. Comparison of the 2 images revealed that the normal defined contour of the CFL was lost following ligament rupture. This observation suggested that alterations in CFL morphology may reflect structural failure of the ligament and prompted us to investigate whether CFL tortuosity could serve as a measurable imaging parameter in patients with CAI (Figure 5). This observation suggested that changes in CFL tortuosity may reflect structural failure of the ligament, which led us to hypothesize that quantifying the tortuosity angle of the CFL on MRI could have diagnostic value in patients with CAI.

The principal finding of the present study was that the tortuosity angle of the CFL measured on oblique coronal MRI demonstrated excellent discriminatory ability between patients with CAI and individuals without a history of ankle sprain. The mean C angle was markedly higher in the CAI group compared with controls, and receiver operating characteristic analysis demonstrated a high diagnostic performance with an area under the curve of 0.97 and an optimal diagnostic threshold of approximately 40°. These results suggest that structural alteration of the CFL may represent an imaging marker of chronic ligament laxity in patients with CAI.

Previous MRI studies of the CFL have mainly focused on morphologic parameters such as ligament thickness or cross-sectional area. Park et al 13 demonstrated that the cross-sectional area of the CFL was significantly increased in patients with CAI and suggested that chronic mechanical stress may lead to structural remodeling of the ligament. Interestingly, they also reported that larger cross-sectional areas were associated with increased ligament tortuosity. The findings of the present study extend these observations by demonstrating that ligament configuration itself, quantified as the tortuosity angle, may provide a measurable imaging parameter that reflects chronic structural changes of the CFL.

Accurate visualization of the CFL on conventional MRI can be challenging because the ligament runs obliquely between the fibula and calcaneus and is not consistently aligned with standard imaging planes. Previous studies have demonstrated that the oblique coronal plane improves visualization of the CFL by aligning the imaging plane parallel to its anatomical course. 9 This approach allows more reliable assessment of CFL morphology and enables consistent evaluation of ligament tortuosity in patients with CAI. Nekomoto et al 7 demonstrated that quantitative evaluation of CFL injury using oblique coronal MRI is feasible in patients with chronic lateral ankle instability. They reported that a CFL cross angle ≤3.8° and an FCA ≤121.2° were associated with CFL injury requiring surgical repair. Unlike the present study, their analysis was designed to identify CFL involvement within a surgically treated CAI population based on intraoperative CFL repair findings. Although the FCA and the C angle used in our study both reflect CFL morphology, FCA evaluates the overall ligament orientation angle whereas the present study quantified the acute angle created by maximal ligament bending. Despite these methodologic differences, both studies support the clinical value of MRI-based morphologic assessment of the CFL in patients with CAI.

Recent biomechanical studies have suggested an additional functional role of the CFL beyond joint stabilization. Yoshizuka et al 14 demonstrated that the CFL may act as a “tensioner” for the peroneal tendons. When the CFL is tensioned, the peroneus longus and peroneus brevis tendons are elevated, which may facilitate efficient transmission of peroneal muscle contraction and contribute to dynamic stabilization of the ankle joint. Conversely, injury or elongation of the CFL may lead to loss of this tensioner function, potentially delaying peroneal muscle response and impairing dynamic ankle stability.

In the present study, isokinetic testing demonstrated a tendency toward reduced evertor strength in the CAI group. This finding may be interpreted in the context of the tensioner function of the CFL. Loss of ligament tension due to CFL injury may reduce the efficiency of force transmission from the peroneal muscles, potentially resulting in decreased eversion strength. However, the correlation between the C angle and evertor strength deficit was weak in our analysis. Therefore, although the tensioner mechanism provides a possible explanation for reduced evertor strength in CAI, the relationship between CFL morphology and peroneal muscle function remains unclear. This finding suggests that the structural ligamentous changes reflected by tortuosity angle and the functional muscle performance operate through independent pathophysiological pathways. Additionally, compensatory neuromuscular adaptation in CAI patients may maintain or even enhance peroneal muscle strength despite structural abnormalities. The limited sample size may have also contributed to insufficient statistical power to detect weak associations. Further studies investigating the interaction between CFL structural changes, neuromuscular control, and peroneal muscle performance are required.

Interestingly, despite the strong discriminatory performance of the C angle between groups, its correlation with conventional measures of instability was relatively weak. Weak to moderate associations were observed between the C angle and conventional measures of instability, whereas correlations with functional variables were limited. These findings suggest that the C angle may function primarily as a structural marker rather than a functional marker of CAI. Notably, some patients demonstrated increased C angles despite minimal mechanical instability. Mechanical instability assessed by stress radiography and functional instability assessed by patient-reported outcome measures represent different dimensions of ankle instability. Therefore, morphologic imaging parameters such as the C angle may provide complementary structural information that is not directly captured by conventional clinical or radiographic assessments.

Stress radiography and manual instability testing remain widely used methods for evaluating mechanical ankle instability and continue to play a key role in clinical decision making. However, stress radiography can be influenced by several factors, including patient discomfort, examiner technique, and ankle positioning, which may reduce measurement reproducibility in clinical practice. In this context, MRI-based evaluation of CFL tortuosity may provide additional structural information when assessing ligament insufficiency. Rather than replacing stress radiography, this imaging parameter may serve as a complementary marker that assists clinicians in the comprehensive evaluation of CAI.

The CAIT score in the CAI group averaged 10.5, which is well below the commonly used threshold of 20 points indicating functional instability. However, CAIT scores did not demonstrate a clear relationship with increasing degrees of mechanical instability. This finding may reflect the relatively homogeneous clinical presentation of patients presenting to specialized clinics with symptomatic CAI, in whom functional scores are already markedly reduced. Further studies may be required to refine clinical scoring systems or develop stratification methods that better reflect the spectrum of mechanical and functional instability.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the control group consisted of patients with Achilles tendon rupture rather than completely healthy volunteers, which may introduce selection bias. Healthy volunteers were not included because MRI examination was not clinically indicated in asymptomatic individuals. Second, anthropometric differences existed between the 2 groups, including age and sex distribution. Third, stress radiography and lower limb strength testing could not be performed in the control group because of pain and clinical considerations related to Achilles tendon rupture. Fourth, although visualization of the CFL may vary depending on ligament morphology and MRI plane orientation, a measurable tortuosity angle was obtained in all included cases. To account for potential measurement variability, interobserver reliability analysis was performed and demonstrated good agreement among observers. However, because the correlation analyses were exploratory in nature, the results should be interpreted with caution. Finally, the relatively limited sample size and retrospective design may limit the generalizability of the findings. Prospective studies with larger cohorts will be required to further validate the clinical utility of MRI-based CFL tortuosity measurement.

Taken together, the findings of this study suggest that increased tortuosity of the CFL observed on MRI may represent a structural imaging marker of chronic ligament laxity in patients with CAI and may provide complementary information to conventional stress radiographic evaluation. Future studies incorporating contralateral ankle comparison may further clarify the clinical significance of CFL tortuosity and account for individual anatomical variation.

Conclusion

Measurement of the CFL tortuosity angle on oblique coronal MRI demonstrated clear differences between patients with CAI and individuals without a history of ankle sprain. The control group showed a mean angle of approximately 31°, whereas patients with CAI typically demonstrated angles greater than 40°, providing high diagnostic performance. Although the association between the C angle and functional or radiographic measures of instability was limited, increased tortuosity of the CFL may reflect structural ligament laxity. Therefore, MRI-based assessment of CFL tortuosity may provide useful structural information in the evaluation of CAI.
