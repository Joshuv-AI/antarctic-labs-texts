The landscape of clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential in long-term breast cancer survivors

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) can confound blood-based genomics and may be shaped by cytotoxic therapy; clarifying its persistence after breast-cancer chemotherapy is relevant for long-term survivorship follow-up.Patients and methodsBuffy-coat whole-exome sequencing was performed in 189 stage I–III breast-cancer survivors with blood collected a median 136.5 months after diagnosis. CHIP was assessed using a prespecified 100-gene hematopoietic-driver compendium and exome-wide interrogation. Introduction

Age-related clonal hematopoiesis (CH) is characterized by the expansion of hematopoietic stem/progenitor cell clones harboring leukemia-associated somatic mutations in individuals without overt hematologic malignancy (commonly termed clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential, CHIP) [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. CHIP prevalence rises with age and involves a recurrent set of driver genes, including DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, PPM1D, TP53, SRSF2, and JAK2, whose genetic architecture has been refined by large-scale population analyses [[1], [2], [3]]. In cancer survivors, exposure to cytotoxic therapy is associated with distinct patterns of clonal selection, particularly enrichment of mutations affecting DNA damage response and apoptotic pathways (e.g., PPM1D, TP53), and with adverse clinical outcomes [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Landmark studies demonstrate that prior chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy shape the CHIP fitness landscape and modify subsequent risk of therapy-related myeloid neoplasms, while the presence, dynamics, and gene-specific effects of CHIP vary with treatment class and cumulative dose [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]].

Concurrently, adjuvant systemic therapy in early breast cancer (BC) has been progressively individualized using multigene expression assays, allowing many patients to safely omit chemotherapy without compromising outcomes [14,15]. Nevertheless, anthracycline- and alkylator-containing regimens remain standard for biologically or clinically high-risk disease, bringing long-term toxicities into focus, including a small but measurable increase in AML/MDS after certain regimens and doses [16]. Therefore, the clinical significance of CHIP in BC is an area of active investigation. Recent prospective and retrospective studies have begun to define CHIP prevalence at diagnosis, its evolution after therapy, and its prognostic associations for cardiovascular and oncologic outcomes, including in older adults receiving chemotherapy and in young women treated for BC [8,9,17]. Moreover, specific CHIP drivers linked to prior genotoxic exposures may serve as molecular footprints of treatment selection pressures and potential biomarkers of late effects [6,7,10,11].

In this project, leveraging long-term clinical follow-up, we aim to identify and characterize chemotherapy-associated CHIP in BC survivors and evaluate its association with clinical variables. Our objectives are to delineate therapy-imprinted mutational profiles, quantify clone size and spectrum, and test multivariable associations, thereby informing risk-adapted survivorship surveillance and CHIP-aware interpretation of peripheral blood genomics.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

We performed a retrospective cohort study including women with a prior diagnosis of stage I–III invasive BC, long-term follow-up, and no intervening chemotherapy between completion of curative therapy and the blood draw for this study. Patients were stratified into two cohorts: A) prior (neo)adjuvant chemotherapy and B) no chemotherapy at any time (Table 1). Eligible cases were diagnosed between 1999 and 2014 at the University Hospitals Regional, Virgen de la Victoria, and Costa del Sol (Málaga, Spain). The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Data were de-identified and handled in accordance with institutional data protection and confidentiality standards.

Clinical characteristics of the study cohort.

Sample collection and DNA extraction

Between 2022 and 2023, 20 mL of peripheral blood was collected in EDTA tubes. Whole blood was centrifuged to isolate the buffy coat. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted and purified from buffy coat using a Qiagen QIAamp DNA Blood kit, following the manufacturer's instructions.

Whole-exome library preparation and sequencing

Whole exome sequencing (WES) libraries were prepared from buffy-coat gDNA using the SureSelect Human All Exon V6 capture panel (Agilent). Sequencing was performed by BGI Genomics (Shenzhen, China) on a DNBSEQ platform with paired-end 150-bp (PE150) reads, targeting ∼100× mean on-target coverage.

Whole-exome data processing and variant calling

Quality control of WES data was assessed using fastQC (v0.11.9) followed by paired-end reads adapters trimming and quality filtering performed by Trim Galore (v0.6.7). Pre-processed reads were mapped to the GRCh38 reference genome using BWA-mem (v0.7.17) and data correction for technical biases was performed following GATK's best practices. Mapped reads files were sorted by coordinates and formatted with Samtools (v1.9). Duplicated reads were marked using GATK (v4.2.2.0) and grouped using Picard (v2.26.10). Base quality score recalibration was performed using GATK and quality control of mapped data by Qualimap (v2.3). All samples met predefined sequencing quality-control criteria (Supplementary Table 1).

Somatic SNVs/indels were called in a tumor-only setting using Mutect2 (GATK) restricted to predefined callable regions (high-mappability BED), using a gnomAD-derived population germline resource and a GATK panel of normals to suppress recurrent artifacts. Contamination and read-orientation bias were modeled and incorporated. Variants were annotated with ANNOVAR and filtered to retain coding/splice variants within capture targets with depth ≥30, ≥3 alternate reads and VAF ≥2%, and gnomAD AF <1%. To minimize false positives, putative germline variants with VAF ≥30% were excluded, except for DNMT3A R882, SRSF2 P95, SF3B1 K666/K700 and JAK2 V617. Variants with VAF ≥30% were excluded to reduce the likelihood of retaining variants compatible with germline heterozygosity in blood-derived DNA, in the absence of an orthogonal non-hematopoietic comparator. This conservative VAF-based strategy was selected to prioritize specificity for likely somatic hematopoietic variants and is consistent with previous CH/cancer sequencing studies using a similar VAF threshold to classify likely germline events [18].

Additional filters removed variants in homopolymeric regions, recurrent at read ends (end-repair artifacts), or showing significant strand/orientation bias; a final manual post-filtering step removed residual artifacts. Analyses were performed using two predefined variant sets: (i) variants located in a prespecified 100-gene CHIP panel (Supplementary Table 2) and (ii) variants from the 100-gene panel plus additional WES-derived candidates supported by recurrent hematopoietic/lymphoid COSMIC evidence. For the latter, variants outside the 100-gene panel were retained only if they were reported more than once in hematopoietic or lymphoid tissue in COSMIC. Variants outside the 100-gene panel were not considered canonical CHIP events and were analyzed separately as hematopoietic/lymphoid-COSMIC-supported WES candidates.

COSMIC SBS mutational signature analysis

COSMIC single-base substitution (SBS) mutational signature analysis was performed using WES-derived blood somatic SNVs. Variants were restricted to PASS SNVs with read depth ≥30, ≥3 alternate reads, VAF ≥1% and VAF <30%. SBS96 matrices were generated using SigProfilerMatrixGenerator with GRCh38 as reference genome. COSMIC SBS signatures were assigned using SigProfilerAssignment and the COSMIC v3.4 reference catalogue.

Signature relative contributions were calculated at patient level and compared descriptively between chemotherapy-exposed and non-exposed patients. For presence-based analyses, a signature was considered present in a given patient when its relative contribution was ≥1%. Signatures were considered evaluable for group comparison if present in at least 10 patients overall.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages and compared using Fisher's exact test or χ2 test as appropriate. Clinical associations with retained variant positivity were assessed using univariable tests (Fisher's exact or χ2), and multivariable logistic regression models evaluated independent associations with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, age, smoking status and obesity. Per-gene mutation frequencies by chemotherapy exposure were compared using Fisher's exact test. Age-focused analyses compared age distribution according to variant status using the Mann–Whitney U test and assessed variant positivity across predefined age strata using χ2 tests. Sensitivity analyses related to adjuvant radiotherapy included models restricted to radiotherapy-exposed patients and focused comparison of (DNA damage response) DDR/p53-related retained variants according to radiotherapy exposure. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For COSMIC SBS signature analysis, each evaluable signature was compared between chemotherapy-exposed and non-exposed patients using Fisher's exact test after defining signature presence as relative contribution ≥1%. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were estimated, and p values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate method.

Results

Prevalence and spectrum using a 100-gene panel CHIP compendium

Before assessing retained blood somatic variants, we examined the age distribution of the cohort at blood draw, given the well-established association between clonal hematopoiesis and age. The cohort showed a relatively concentrated age distribution, with most patients clustered around the sixth and seventh decades of life (Supplementary Fig. S1). This feature was considered when interpreting age-stratified analyses and multivariable models adjusted for age.

From whole-exome data, we interrogated a prespecified 100-gene CHIP panel. Variants in these genes were identified in 54 of 189 patients (28.6%) (Fig. 1). Detailed mutation-level annotation of retained variants is provided in Supplementary Table 3. Alterations were predominantly missense SNVs, with fewer truncating events (nonsense, frameshift ins/del), and multi-hit cases were uncommon. The most frequently mutated genes were DNMT3A (39%), TET2 (9%) and SETBP1 (6%); additional genes occurred in 4% of mutated cases (BCOR, BRCC3, CREBBP, NF1, NOTCH2, PHIP, TP53, ZNF318) or 2% (ATM, ATRX, BRAF, CBL, CUX1, EP300, ETNK1 EZH2, GNB1, KIT, MPL, MYC, PPM1D, PTEN, RUNX1, SH2B3, U2AF2, ZRSR2). In addition, we compared per-gene mutation frequencies between chemotherapy-exposed and never-exposed women. We observed no differentially mutated genes by chemotherapy exposure within the prespecified 100-gene panel (Supplementary Fig. S2A).

Next, we fitted multivariable logistic regression models for the presence of ≥1 variant in the 100-gene panel, including chemotherapy, age, radiotherapy, obesity, and smoking status as covariates (Fig. 1B). Chemotherapy showed no association with 100-gene panel-defined variant positivity (OR 0.77; 95% CI 0.40–1.48; p = 0.432). Age >60 years versus ≤60 was likewise non-significant (OR 1.32; 95% CI 0.66-2.65; p = 0.431). Adjuvant radiotherapy (OR 1.65, 95% CI 0.52-5.23, p = 0.396), obesity (OR 0.85; 95% CI 0.39-1.88; p = 0.690) and smoking status (OR 1.46; 95% CI 0.76–2.79; p = 0.257) were not significantly associated with variant positivity.

Consistent with the multivariable analysis, age-focused descriptive analyses did not support a significant association between age and 100-gene panel-defined variant positivity. Age distribution was not significantly different between variant-negative and variant-positive patients (Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.168; Supplementary Fig. S2B), and variant positivity did not differ significantly across predefined age strata (<50, 50–59, 60–69 and ≥ 70 years; global age-strata test, p = 0.407; Supplementary Fig. S2C). Overall, these analyses provide no robust evidence that chemotherapy, or any other clinical covariate examined affects panel-defined CHIP prevalence in this cohort.

Exome-wide analysis of CHIP mutations

We next complemented the 100-gene panel analysis with additional WES-derived candidate variants supported by recurrent hematopoietic/lymphoid COSMIC evidence. Using this 100-gene panel plus hematopoietic/lymphoid-COSMIC-supported WES approach, 67 of 189 patients (35.4%) harbored ≥1 retained variant (Fig. 2A). Detailed mutation-level annotation of retained variants is provided in Supplementary Table 3. Alterations were predominantly missense SNVs, with fewer truncating events, and multi-hit cases were uncommon. The most frequent genes included DNMT3A (31%), TET2 (7%), and SETBP1 (4%). Among the additional WES-derived candidates, a recurrent MYEOV variant was observed in seven patients; however, given the recurrence of the identical variant with comparable VAFs, this finding was considered an exploratory recurrent VUS or possible artefact. Other recurrently altered genes included BCOR, BRCC3, CREBBP, HNRNPCL1, NF1, NOTCH2, PHIP, TP53, and ZNF318, each detected in approximately 3% of variant-positive cases, while other genes occurred at lower frequency. As performed for the 100-gene panel, we compared per-gene variant frequencies between chemotherapy-exposed and non-exposed women. No gene reached nominal statistical significance by chemotherapy exposure using this expanded WES-supported approach (Supplementary Fig. S3A).

Pathway-level annotation of the retained variant landscape integrated genes captured by the 100-gene panel and by the hematopoietic/lymphoid-COSMIC-supported WES analysis (Supplementary Fig. S4). Genes detected in more than two patients mapped to DNA methylation/epigenetic regulation (DNMT3A and TET2), myeloid malignancy-associated biology (SETBP1), and oncogenic transcriptional/proliferative signaling (MYEOV). Additional mutations were detected in genes involved in chromatin modification, DNA damage response/p53 signaling, RNA splicing, transcriptional regulation, RAS/MAPK signaling, JAK-STAT/cytokine signaling and other broader functional categories, while poorly characterized genes were explicitly annotated as such.

We also fitted multivariable logistic regression models for the presence of ≥1 variant using the 100-gene panel plus hematopoietic/lymphoid-COSMIC-supported WES approach, including chemotherapy, age, radiotherapy, obesity, and smoking status as covariates (Fig. 2B). Chemotherapy showed no association (OR 0.89; 95% CI 0.48-1.67; p = 0.721). Age >60 years versus ≤60 was likewise non-significant (OR 0.99; 95% CI 0.52-1.88, p = 0.966). Adjuvant radiotherapy yielded OR 1.05; 95% CI 0.39-2.81; p = 0.786; obesity OR 0.72 95% CI 0.34-1.53; p = 0.40; and smoking status OR 1.77; 95% CI 0.96-3.27; p = 0.069. Therefore, no covariate provided evidence of association with variant positivity under the 100-gene panel plus hematopoietic/lymphoid-COSMIC-supported WES approach.

Consistent with the multivariable analysis, age-focused descriptive analyses did not support an association between age and variant positivity in the 100-gene panel plus hematopoietic/lymphoid-COSMIC-supported WES analysis. Age distribution was comparable between variant-negative and variant-positive patients (Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.621; Supplementary Fig. S3B), and variant positivity did not differ significantly across predefined age strata (<50, 50–59, 60–69 and ≥ 70 years; global age-strata test, p = 0.223; Supplementary Fig. S3C).

Because adjuvant radiotherapy may contribute to therapy-related clonal hematopoiesis, particularly involving DDR/p53-related genes, we performed additional sensitivity analyses. In patients who received adjuvant radiotherapy, chemotherapy was not associated with retained variant positivity using the 100-gene panel plus WES-supported definition (OR 0.94, 95% CI 0.49–1.81; p = 0.858; Supplementary Table 3). DDR/p53-related retained variants were infrequent and showed no significant enrichment according to radiotherapy exposure, although all detected events occurred among radiotherapy-exposed patients (6/168 vs 0/21; Fisher's exact p = 1.000; Supplementary Table 4).

Given prior exposure to cytotoxic therapy, we performed a COSMIC single-base substitution (SBS) mutational signature analysis using WES-derived blood somatic SNVs. We first examined selected signatures with potential relevance to ageing or treatment-related mutational processes, including clock-like signatures (SBS1 and SBS5), DNA repair-associated signatures and therapy-associated signatures. The dominant relative contributions were mainly attributable to SBS5 and SBS1. In presence-based comparisons using a ≥1% per patient contribution threshold, no evaluable SBS signature was significantly enriched in chemotherapy-exposed patients (Supplementary Fig. S5).

High-VAF clone–restricted analyses

Given the median ∼11-year interval between diagnosis and blood collection, we considered that chemotherapy-driven CHIP clones capable of long-term persistence would be expected to have undergone substantial expansion and thus to present at relatively high VAF. Following previous work that used a VAF threshold of 10% to capture high-burden clones with greater clinical impact, we therefore repeated our analyses using the presence of ≥1 CHIP mutation with VAF ≥10% at the patient level as the outcome [19]. Descriptively, high-VAF CHIP was detected in 3/32 (9.4%) chemotherapy-exposed versus 9/35 (25.7%) never-exposed survivors in the whole-exome data, and in 3/28 (10.7%) versus 7/26 (26.9%) women in the 100-gene panel (Supplementary Fig. S6).

In the 100-gene hematopoietic panel, chemotherapy-exposed survivors again showed numerically higher odds of harboring at least one high-VAF variant than never-exposed women, but neither chemotherapy nor any other clinical variable was associated with high-VAF CHIP (Supplementary Fig. S6). Exome-wide clone-restricted models yielded very similar results. Prior chemotherapy again showed a modest positive association with the presence of ≥1 variant with VAF ≥10%, but this was not statistically significant. Older age and the other clinical covariates did not show associations, and ever-smoking again showed a suggestive but non-significant trend towards higher odds of high-VAF clones (Supplementary Fig. S6). Taken together, these clone-size–restricted multivariable analyses do not identify any clinical factor that is robustly associated with the presence of large CHIP clones in this cohort, reinforcing the previous results.

Discussion

We profiled exome-wide somatic variation in buffy-coat DNA from long-term BC survivors to characterize CH beyond predefined driver panels. Two consistent observations emerged. First, the retained variant landscape was dominated by canonical hematopoietic drivers, particularly DNMT3A and TET2, consistent with the mutational spectrum described in population-based clonal hematopoiesis studies and in breast cancer CHIP cohorts such as Morganti et al. and Mayerhofer et al. [2,8,17]. However, these prior breast cancer studies were performed in substantially different clinical and temporal settings, assessing patients around diagnosis or during treatment, whereas our cohort consisted of long-term survivors sampled a median of more than 11 years after diagnosis. Therefore, minor differences in low-frequency genes, such as ASXL1, are not unexpected and should be interpreted in light of differences in cohort structure, sampling time, sequencing strategy, VAF thresholds and/or variant filtering. Second, in multivariable models including prior chemotherapy, adjuvant radiotherapy, age, smoking and obesity, no variable independently predicted positivity for retained variants under the 100-gene panel plus hematopoietic/lymphoid-COSMIC-supported WES approach. Together, these data are consistent with therapy-selection models in which DDR clones may expand after cytotoxic exposure, but cohort-level detectability depends on regimen, dose, latency and baseline VAF, as observed in BC cohorts [6,7,9,17].

Age is a major determinant of CHIP, but its apparent effect depends strongly on cohort structure, sampling time point and sequencing strategy. Although population-based studies established the age-related increase in CHIP across broad age ranges [1,2], and BC-specific studies such as Morganti et al. also reported age-associated CHIP patterns [17], our cohort consisted of long-term BC survivors sampled a median of more than 11 years after diagnosis, with age at blood draw concentrated mainly around the sixth and seventh decades. This restricted age structure may explain why age was not significantly associated with variant positivity in our analyses. In addition, as in previous BC-specific CHIP studies, which generally relied on internal comparisons among patients rather than matched non-cancer controls [8,9,17], the absence of an age-matched non-cancer control cohort limits our ability to determine whether the observed prevalence exceeds that expected in the general population. Therefore, prevalence estimates should be interpreted within this selected survivorship context.

Adjuvant radiotherapy should also be considered when interpreting treatment-associated CHIP, as it may select DNA damage response- or p53-related clones. In our study, radiotherapy was included as a covariate in the main multivariable models, and chemotherapy remained non-significantly associated with retained variant positivity after adjustment for radiotherapy and other clinical covariates. Moreover, in a sensitivity analysis restricted to radiotherapy-exposed patients, chemotherapy was not associated with variant positivity. DDR/p53-related variants were infrequent and did not show statistically significant enrichment according to radiotherapy exposure, although all detected events occurred among irradiated patients. Therefore, radiotherapy cannot be excluded as a contributor to individual DDR/p53-related events, but our data do not support it as a major confounder of the chemotherapy analysis. This conclusion is limited by the small number of non-irradiated patients.

On the other hand, chemotherapy regimen composition may influence therapy-associated CHIP. In our cohort, chemotherapy exposure was relatively homogeneous and largely anthracycline-based, with approximately half of chemotherapy-exposed patients also receiving taxanes and no platinum-containing regimens recorded. This is relevant because, in the prospective BC cohort reported by Morganti et al., platinum-containing regimens were significantly associated with the emergence of new CHIP, whereas anthracycline exposure and radiotherapy did not differ between patients with and without new CHIP(17). Given the absence of platinum-treated patients in our cohort and the overlapping nature of anthracycline- and taxane-containing regimens, agent-specific association analyses were not performed.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to interrogate CHIP mutations in BC survivors using buffy-coat whole-exome sequencing and the first to do so in long-term post-therapy survivors with an exome-wide perspective. Prior BC cohorts have predominantly relied on targeted panels and relatively short-interval sampling, collecting blood during or shortly after adjuvant chemotherapy and reporting heterogeneous, gene- and VAF-specific therapy effects with only modest cohort-level shifts [8,9,17]. By extending beyond predefined driver sets, our WES approach delineates both the canonical CHIP backbone and the accompanying exome-wide spectrum in survivors, providing a complementary, unbiased reference for future gene- and exposure-resolved analyses. Importantly, the ∼11-year median interval from diagnosis to sampling means that our estimates capture a late post-therapy equilibrium rather than early post-exposure dynamics, thereby attenuating acute signals of therapy selection but strengthening inference about the durable CHIP landscape in long-term survivorship. This long latency may also explain the exceedingly low frequency of DDR-associated CHIP mutations observed in our cohort. Treatment-selected DDR-mutant clones, particularly PPM1D-mutant clones, may expand during or shortly after chemotherapy but subsequently contract once the selective pressure of cytotoxic exposure is removed [20]. Therefore, blood sampling more than a decade after BC diagnosis may underestimate the early burden of transient therapy-selected clones.

This study has several limitations. First, although WES enabled broader interrogation of blood somatic variation beyond predefined CHIP drivers, the sequencing depth achieved with our WES approach limits sensitivity for detecting very small clones. Dedicated targeted CHIP panels can, in principle, achieve substantially higher sequencing depth and therefore greater sensitivity for low-VAF variants. However, the present study was primarily designed to characterize the durable blood somatic variant landscape in long-term BC survivors, rather than to capture very low-VAF clones of uncertain persistence in this setting. In addition, because matched non-hematopoietic germline DNA were not available, we applied a conservative VAF >30% exclusion to reduce germline misclassification; this may have excluded true large CHIP clones and therefore may have led to conservative prevalence estimates. Second, our cohort is intrinsically selected for favorable BC prognosis and long-term survival, which likely under-represents women who developed therapy-related myeloid neoplasms or aggressive therapy-driven clonal dynamics and may bias the observed CHIP landscape towards clinically indolent clones. Third, the cross-sectional design with a single late time point precludes direct assessment of clonal trajectories from diagnosis through survivorship. Finally, hematological parameters at the time of blood collection were not systematically available, preventing formal evaluation of cytopenias, clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance (CCUS), or the clonal hematopoiesis risk score (CHRS). However, no secondary hematological malignancies were documented during the available clinical follow-up. Together, these factors argue for cautious interpretation of absolute CHIP prevalence and therapy effects, and highlight the need for larger, longitudinal studies with deeper sequencing to fully characterize the spectrum and dynamics of therapy-related clonal hematopoiesis.

Conclusion

Across panel-based and exome-wide analyses, prior chemotherapy was not associated with CHIP in long-term BC survivors. High-VAF sensitivity analyses further suggest that, a decade after treatment, any therapy-selected CHIP clones either fail to expand uniformly or remain confined to a susceptible minority.
