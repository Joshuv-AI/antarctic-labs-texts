Efficacy and safety of ATRA plus arsenic trioxide versus ATRA plus chemotherapy in newly diagnosed acute promyelocytic leukemia: a grade-assessed systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Hassan Ahmad, Fathimathul Henna, Sana Khurram, Ayesha Khan, Alisha Ahmed, Muhammad Hasnain Ali, Mohammad Aitzaz Hassan, Muhammad Abbas, Danyal Farooq, Sher Muhammad, Ali Abdul Basit, Ashfaq Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) is an aggressive malignancy. Although recent advancements in therapy have improved outcomes, differing toxicity profiles remain. The optimal choice between all-trans retinoic acid plus arsenic trioxide (ATRA + ATO) and ATRA plus chemotherapy, in newly diagnosed APL remains uncertain.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted across databases PubMed (n = 812), Embase (n = 975), Scopus (n = 894), and the Cochrane Library (n = 300) for studies comparing ATRA + ATO with ATRA+chemotherapy in newly diagnosed APL. Of 2981 records found, 681 duplicates were removed, leaving 2300 records for screening. After a full-text review of 100 reports, 12 studies met the inclusion criteria. Data were analyzed using random- and fixed-effects models, with subgroup and sensitivity analyses performed to assess heterogeneity. The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the GRADE framework. Findings from RCTs are primary basis for conclusion.ResultsIn RCTs, ATRA + ATO significantly improved complete remission rates (risk ratio [RR] 1.04, 95% CI 1.02–1.06), disease-free survival (RR 1.22, 95% CI 1.11–1.34), event-free survival (RR 1.25, 95% CI 1.20–1.29) and overall survival (RR 1.07, 95% CI 1.03–1.12) compared with ATRA+chemotherapy.observational studies showed showed consistent findings supporting real world generalizability. A trend toward lower gastrointestinal toxicity was observed with ATRA + ATO (RR 0.28, 95% CI 0.07–1.18), but it was not statistically significant. There were no significant differences in hepatic toxicity, differentiation syndrome or thrombocytopenia. ATRA + ATO was associated with an increased risk of QTc prolongation (RR 3.79, 95% CI 1.00–14.36, p = 0.05). The under-reporting in the primary studies limits the ability to draw a definitive conclusion on the difference in reported cardiac event rates in the clinics.ConclusionsCompared with ATRA plus chemotherapy, ATRA + ATO is associated with superior remission and survival outcomes in newly diagnosed APL, with lower gastrointestinal toxicity but higher risk of QTc prolongation. These findings support the preferential clinical use of ATRA + ATO with close cardiac monitoring.RegistrationThe systematic review was registered with the International Prospective Register Of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO Registration No. CRD420251164171).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-15932-4.

Background

Acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL), another distinct subtype of acute myeloid leukemia that is characterized by the t(15;17) chromosomal translocation, occurs in approximately 0.28 per 100,000 individuals annually in the United States [1]. Although the incidence of APL only makes up 5 to 8% of all AML, it is a disease with significant early mortality (fundamental cause is hemorrhagic complication) [2]. Despite the progress of targeted therapy, it is estimated that 12% to 20% of patients die within 30 days of diagnosis and the mortality rate is greater than half within the first week of diagnosis [1–3]. Early death is now the biggest barrier to cure in APL and evidence-based management strategies that optimize efficacy and safety outcomes are urgently needed.

The therapeutic paradigm for APL has been changed by the discovery of the novel targeted agents, all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) and arsenic trioxide (ATO), which induce differentiation and degradation of the PML-RARA oncoprotein. While there have been landmark trials showing the efficacy of ATRA plus ATO in low to intermediate risk APL, ATRA and ATO offer superior treatment efficacy compared with conventional ATRA plus chemotherapy regimens with 2-year event-free survival rates of greater than 97% [4, 5]. These findings have been further tested in high-risk patients and in real-world settings [6, 7]. However, there are several critical questions that remain poorly answered. Optimal therapy for newly diagnosed APL across all risk categories remains controversial and there are conflicting results amongst trials for the need for chemotherapy, particularly in high-risk disease. In addition, comparative safety data, such as gastrointestinal toxicity, hematologic toxicity, and differentiation syndrome, are variable in each of the studies and preclude firm risk-benefit determinations. Earlier meta-analyses have been hampered by small numbers of included trials (which may be due to updated literature searches), narrow patient populations, and outdated literature searches [8–10]. Most analyses have not included high-risk patients or have only addressed specific treatment comparisons without using a network approach; therefore, the overall comparative effectiveness and safety of ATRA-ATO versus ATRA-chemotherapy regimens remain uncertain.

Considering that several large randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have been published since prior meta-analyses, and detailed safety outcomes remain incompletely characterized, an updated systematic review and meta-analysis is justified. This review addresses these gaps by including trials published through 2025, all risk strata, detailed risk-stratified subgroup analyses, and GRADE certainty assessment. By bringing together the full weight of evidence using the most rigorous methodological paradigms and following PRISMA guidelines, this analysis aims to deliver more accurate effect estimates for informing evidence-based choice of treatment for patients with newly diagnosed APL.

We thus performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of ATRA plus ATO versus ATRA plus chemotherapy for assessment of complete remission, overall survival, event-free survival, disease-free survival, and gastrointestinal and hematologic toxicity in patients with newly diagnosed APL.

Materials and methods

Search and study selection

An extensive systematic literature search was carried out in five electronic databases: PubMed (n = 812), Embase (n = 975), Scopus (n = 894), and the Cochrane Library (n = 300), to identify studies comparing ATRA plus arsenic trioxide (ATRA + ATO) with ATRA plus chemotherapy in newly diagnosed acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL). The searches used MeSH/Emtree controlled vocabulary and free‑text keywords: acute promyelocytic leukemia, all‑trans retinoic acid, arsenic trioxide, ATRA, ATO. The review was performed and reported in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‑Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [11], and the protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251164171).

Three authors independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and consensus.

Eligibility criteria

Study design

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or cohort studies directly comparing ATRA + ATO with ATRA+chemotherapy.

Participants

Adults (≥ 18 years) with newly diagnosed APL of any risk category.

Interventions

ATRA + ATO versus ATRA+chemotherapy (daunorubicin‑ or idarubicin‑based) as first‑line therapy.

Outcomes

At least one efficacy outcome (complete remission, molecular remission, overall survival, disease‑free survival, event‑free survival) or safety outcome (gastrointestinal toxicity, hepatotoxicity, hematologic toxicity, differentiation syndrome, QTc prolongation, cardiac events).

Exclusions

Animal studies, case reports, reviews, cross‑sectional studies, and studies including only relapsed/refractory APL.

Data extraction and risk of bias assessment

Three authors independently extracted: study design, first author, year, country, participant characteristics, treatment details, follow‑up duration, and outcome measures. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

For randomized controlled trials, two authors assessed risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool, which evaluates five domains: (1) bias due to the randomization process, (2) bias due to deviations from intended interventions, (3) bias due to missing outcome data, (4) bias in measurement of the outcome, and (5) bias in selection of the reported result [12]. Each domain was judged as low risk, some concerns, or high risk.

For observational studies, two authors used the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), which assesses selection (0–4 points), comparability (0–2 points), and outcome assessment (0–3 points). Total scores range from 0 to 9 points; studies scoring 8–9 were considered very good quality, 6–7 good, 4–5 moderate, and < 4 lower quality.

Grade assessment

Across the included studies, ATRO + ATO consistently demonstrated superior clinical outcomes compared with ATRO+chemotherapy. Complete remission rates were higher in both randomized trials and non-randomized studies, with pooled risk ratios indicating a modest but statistically significant advantage for ATRO + ATO and supported by high-certainty evidence. Disease-free survival and event-free survival similarly favored ATRO + ATO, with large absolute benefits; although randomized DFS data showed substantial heterogeneity leading to low-certainty grading, the direction of effect remained consistently positive. The rates of molecular remission were similar across the different groups of treatment, which is why randomized evidence was graded with moderate certainty due to the imprecision reflecting the most optimistic case while observational data remained at very low certainty. Randomized trials yielded moderate-certainty evidence regarding hepatic toxicity being less in the ATRO + ATO arm whereas non-randomized studies resulted in very low certainty due to highly inconsistent and imprecise results. The cumulative body of evidence points to a clinically relevant increase in the rate of remission and survival, improved quality when assessed by high-quality randomized data, and the safety profile of ATRO + ATO is thus favored. Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using RevMan 5.4.1 and R version 4.5.2. For dichotomous outcomes, the summary statistic was relative risk (RR) with 95% confidence interval (CI). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 with 95% CI not including 1.0.

Heterogeneity and model selection

The Higgins I² statistic quantified statistical heterogeneity: I²<25% (low), 25–75% (moderate), > 75% (substantial) [13]. Individual study effect sizes were weighted using the inverse‑variance method, and between‑study variance (Tau²) was estimated using the Der Simonian–Laird random‑effects estimator. Random‑effects models were used when I²>75%, and fixed‑effect models when I²<25%.

RR rationale

Dichotomous survival outcomes were analyzed using RR when hazard ratios were not available, with analyses conducted at comparable follow‑up periods (typically 2‑year time points, as specified in the Results).

Subgroup and meta‑regression analyses

Prespecified subgroup analyses evaluated differences by study design (RCTs vs. observational). Primary efficacy findings are derived through evidence of randomized controlled trials and observational data are used to confirm the real world generalizability as supplementary evidence. Forest plots were used to present all available forest plots stratified by study design to enable easy distinction of evidence sources. Meta‑regression examined whether study‑level mean age and study design modified the overall survival treatment effect, with results expressed as regression coefficients and 95% CI; R² quantified the proportion of between‑study variance explained.

Publication bias and sensitivity analyses

Funnel plots and Egger’s regression test were used to assess publication bias for outcomes with ≥ 10 studies (p > 0.05 indicating no significant asymmetry). Sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequential exclusion of individual studies (leave‑one‑out analysis).

Results

Selection and characteristics of the studies

A systematic search of the literature was conducted in four databases (PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library) and manual reference list searches, resulting in 2981 unique records. After duplication screening of 681 articles, 2300 titles and abstracts were screened. Of the 100 articles that went under full text screening, 12 studies (reported in 13 publications) met the eligibility criteria and were included in the quantitative synthesis (Fig. 1). The included studies were four randomized controlled trials (RCTs): Platzbecker et al. [5], Platzbecker et al. [14], Chen et al. [15], and Burnett et al. [16]; and eight observational cohort studies: Lebon et al. [17], Chen et al. [18], Gill et al. [19], Kayser et al. [20], Lou et al. [21], Saxena et al. [22], Tao et al. [23], Voso et al. [24]. Collectively, the meta-analysis included 3930 patients newly diagnosed with acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL). Included studies baseline characteristics, including patient demographics, study time, country, treatment interventions and follow-up time are displayed in Supplementary Table 1. Of those studies which reported risk stratification, about 30–40% of patients were found to be in the high-risk category (white blood cell count > 10,000/ul), and not all studies reported risk stratification, which decreases the accuracy of subgroup estimates of this group.

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence (GRADE)

Risk-of-bias assessment of included studies was done individually based on study designs. For the four RCTs, the risk of bias in the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool showed an overall low to moderate risk of bias (Fig. 2). Specifically, Burnett et al. [16] was evaluated as having a higher overall risk of bias, whereas Chen et al. (2021) and Platzbecker et al. [5, 15] showed concerns (regarding particular domains of bias). For the eight observational cohort studies, the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) evaluation provided generally sound methodological quality and scores ranged between 6 and 9 (Supplementary Table 2).

For disease free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS), RCT evidence was judged to be of low quality due to substantial inconsistency (DFS: I2 = 89.2%; OS: I2 = 76.1%), although the direction of effect was consistently favorable for ATRA + ATO. Observational study evidence for DFS and OS was rated as low certainty evidence due to high inconsistency and imprecision.

With regards to safety outcomes, the hepatic toxicity assessed from RCTs was graded as moderate certainty, moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 60%) and moderate imprecision. Evidence from observational studies about hepatic toxicity was very low certainty due to very high inconsistency (I2 = 87%) and severe imprecision. Gastrointestinal toxicity evidence from RCTs was rated at high certainty (Significant protective effect (p < 0.0001). Evidence from observational studies for gastrointestinal toxicity, and most other safety outcomes (differentiation syndrome, hematologic toxicities, QTc prolongation) from both types of studies was graded as very low certainty due to imprecision, inconsistency and limited reporting.

Efficacy outcomes

Remission and survival rates

The ATRA + ATO regimen showed statistically significant improvements in all primary efficacy endpoints as compared to ATRA + chemotherapy. Results are presented with emphasis on randomized controlled trial findings as the primary evidence source, with observational data providing supportive real-world confirmation.

Complete Remission (CR)

In randomized controlled trials, ATRA+ATO demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in complete remission (RR = 1.03, 95% CI = 1.01-1.06; p < 0.001; I²= 0.0%) (Fig. 3). Observational studies showed consistent findings (RR = 1.04, 95% CI = 1.02-1.06) with no statistically significant difference between study types (p=0.54), supporting the generalizability of trial results to real-world settings (Fig. 3). While the improvement is statistically significant, it is a 4 per cent relative improvement or 3-4 per cent absolute improvement in a disease with baseline CR rates of over 95 per cent, warranting the need for their careful interpretation of the clinical meaningfulness of this effect size. Subgroup analysis by study design (RCTs versus observational cohorts) showed similar treatment effects in both - RCTs: RR of 1.03, 95% CI of 1.01-1.06, Cohorts: RR of 1.04, 95% CI of 1.02-1.06) with no statistically significant different effect between study types (p=0.54).

Disease-Free Survival (DFS)

Based on 3 randomized controlled trials, ATRA + ATO showed a 16% relative improvement in the probability of DFS compared to ATRA+chemotherapy (RR 1.16, 95% CI 1.02–1.32; p = 0.02) (Fig. 4) Substantial heterogeneity was present among RCTs (I² = 89.2%), leading to low-certainty evidence despite the consistent direction of effect.observational studies (RR 1.28, 95% CI 1.11–1.48; I² = 67.5%), supporting the robustness of the treatment effect across study designs. Sensitivity analysis by study omission showed that pooled RR was neither affected by omission of the studies (range 1.18–1.25), and Chen et al. [15] exhibited the largest effect on the reduction of heterogeneity (I2 reduced to 56.6% when omitted).

Event-Free Survival (EFS)

Pooled data from three randomized controlled trials showed a consistent and robust effect favoring ATRA + ATO with a 25% increased likelihood of achieving EFS (RR = 1.25, 95% CI 1.17–1.34; p < 0.001; I² = 0.0%)(Fig. 5). This result is supported by high-certainty evidence without evidence of heterogeneity. Four observational studies confirmed this finding (RR = 1.24, 95% CI 1.15–1.34), with no significant difference between study types (p = 0.89).

Overall Survival (OS)

In randomized controlled trials, ATRA + ATO demonstrated a statistically significant survival advantage (RR 1.07, 95% CI 1.01–1.14; p = 0.02), though with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 48%)(Fig. 6). Observational studies showed a similar effect size (RR 1.07, 95% CI 1.02–1.13; I² = 83%), with no significant subgroup difference (p = 0.98). The small relative improvement of 7% translates to an absolute increase of approximately 1–2% given baseline survival rates of 85–90% in modern series.

Molecular Remission (MR)

There was no significant difference between the two regimens for molecular remission rates in either RCTs or observational studies (overall RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.98–1.04; p = 0.51; I² = 20.2%) (Fig. 7). In RCTs specifically, the RR was 1.01 (95% CI 0.97–1.05). Sensitivity analysis showed that removal of one large RCT [5] yielded borderline statistical significance (RR 1.05, 95% CI 1.0004-1.10; p = 0.048), indicating some heterogeneity in molecular response outcomes between studies despite consistent clinical response rates.

Publication bias and heterogeneity exploratory

Publication bias was formally evaluated for outcomes having ten or more studies contributing data at outcomes (complete remission and overall survival), sufficient for meaningful interpretation of the funnel plot. For complete remission, no significant asymmetry was found on inspection of the funnel plot or Egger’s test (t = 1.41, df = 8, p = 0.197), indicating no evidence of publication bias. For overall survival there was some asymmetry in the funnel plot, but Egger’s test was not statistically significant (t = 1.97, df = 10, p = 0.077), indicating that there is no strong evidence of publication bias despite some visual asymmetry. Publication bias assessment was not conducted for the outcomes of DFS, EFS, and safety outcomes because less than 10 contributing studies per outcome prevented the reporting of funnel plot asymmetry (Supplementary Fig. 1, 2).

To investigate the source of the large heterogeneity found for overall survival (I2 = 76.1%): random effects meta-regression analyses have been performed with study-level mean age and study design (rct vs. observational cohort) as covariates. Treatment effect was not statistically significantly moderated by patients mean age (coefficient = 0.0022, 95% CI=-0.0055 to 0.0099; p = 0.50; R^2 = 7.81%) (Supplementary Fig. 3, 4), nor by study design (coefficient=-0.0619, 95% CI=-0.1504 to 0.0265; p = 0.17; R^2 = 13.66%) .This suggests that other unmeasured clinical or methodological factors - including differences in specific chemotherapy regimens (idarubicin versus daunorubicin dosing strategies), routes of ATO administration (intravenous versus oral) and dosing schedule, arsenic dosing protocols, duration of induction therapy, baseline patient risk stratification (low versus intermediate versus high risk) and standards for prophylactic corticosteroid use to prevent differentiation syndrome - are likely to be the major drivers for the heterogeneity in treatment effects observed.

Safety outcomes

Toxicities, hematologic and non-cardiac

Gastrointestinal (GI) toxicity

The result of the pooled analysis of five RCTs and two cohort studies showed a non-significant trend of lower gastrointestinal toxicity with ATRA + ATO (RR = 0.28, 95% CI = 0.07–1.18; p = 0.08), but high heterogeneity was found (I2 = 74%) (Supplementary Fig. 5). Subgroup analysis showed a statistically significant improvement in GI toxicity was noted within RCTs (RR = 0.13, 95% CI = 0.04–0.46; p < 0.0001; I2 = 0%), suggesting ATRA + ATO substantially reduces nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and mucositis compared with ATRA+chemotherapy in controlled trial settings. However, this protective effect was not reflected in cohort studies (RR = 0.80, 95% CI = 0.23–2.85; p = 0.60), suggesting that variation in standardized adverse events ascertainment, definition and documentation practices of trial settings and observational cohorts may have influenced the reported toxicity rates. The test for subgroup differences was significant (p = 0.007), highlighting this important divergence.

Hepatic toxicity

There was no significant difference in the risk of hepatic toxicity for the two regimens when all the studies were pooled (RR: 0.91, 95% CI 0.57–1.43; p: 0.67; I2: 60%) (Supplementary Fig. 6). Subgroup analysis revealed a significant protective trend for hepatic toxicity for RCTs (RR = 0.74, 95% CI = 0.58–0.95; p = 0.02), but cohort studies did not show significant difference (RR = 1.47, 95% CI = 0.31–6.94; p = 0.64), though the difference among subgroups was not statistically significant (p = 0.39).

Differentiation Syndrome (DS)

Overall risk of differentiation syndrome was similar in the ATRA + ATO and ATRA + chemotherapy groups (RR = 1.07, 95% CI = 0.66–1.73; p = 0.78; I2 = 73%) (Supplementary Fig. 7). Neither RCTs (RR = 0.86, 95% CI = 0.40–1.87; p = 0.72) nor cohort studies (RR = 1.26, 95% CI = 0.68–2.33; p = 0.48) showed a statistically significant difference and no significant subgroup difference was identified (p = 0.37).

Hematologic toxicity-neutropenia

The overall pooled analysis on neutropenia in four randomized controlled trials and four cohort studies showed no statistically significant difference between the regimens (RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.54–1.13; p = 0.19; I2 = 93%) (Supplementary Fig. 8). However, subgroup analysis suggested a strong separation based on the design of the study. Randomized controlled trials showed a statistically significant reduction in neutropenia risk with the combination of ATRA + ATO (RR 0.63, 95% CI 0.50 to 0.79; p <. 001; I2 33%), but the cohort studies found a small non-significant increasing effect (RR 1.04, 95% CI 0.77 to 1.40; p = 0.89; I2 0%). This large and statistically significant divergence between the types of studies (p = 0.0003) suggests differences in the intensity of patient monitoring, baseline hematologic reserve, transfusion thresholds, and supportive care standards between controlled trials and the real world may have had a substantial impact on the reported rates of neutropenia.

Hematologic toxicity-thrombocytopenia

The results of analysis of thrombocytopenia in four studies revealed no significant difference between regimens (RR 0.65, 95% CI 0.23–1.79; p 0.40) (Supplementary Fig. 9). This result was severely restricted by extreme heterogeneity (I2 of 99%), meaning that there is substantive and unexplained variability in study results. Accordingly, this finding should be considered with a lot of caution, and the pooled estimate may not be a good representation of the true treatment effect.

Cardiac safety

Cardiac toxicity in patients treated with ATRA + ATO, mainly manifests itself in electrocardiographic abnormalities and the most commonly noted is QTc interval prolongation. This prolongation may be the result of direct pharmacologic effects on cardiac re-polarization, or secondary gestation of electrolyte balance.

QTc Interval Prolongation: ATRA + ATO was associated with a statistically significant increase in the risk for QTc interval prolongation compared with ATRA plus chemotherapy. And a pooled analysis of 5 studies resulted in a risk ratio of 3.79 (95% confidence interval 1.00-14.36; P < 0.05), which corresponds to a borderline significant increase with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 61%; Supplementary Fig. 10). This risk was particularly marked within randomized controlled trials (RR 7.82, 95% CI 2.59–23.63; p 0.0003; I2 0%; low level of evidence) and suggests that the QTc prolongation associated with ATRA + ATO was robust and consistent in each randomized controlled trial where standardized electrocardiographic monitoring was used. On the other hand, group studies did not show a statistically significant increase in QTc prolongation (RR = 1.17, 95% CI 0.27–5.06; p = 0.84), which is per se likely to reflect variability in electrocardiograph monitoring practices in observational settings. The test for subgroup differences had statistical significance (p = 0.04).

A major limitation of the available data is that the clinical consequences of QTc prolongation differed from study to study and could not be systematically quantified from published reports. Specifically, it was largely unclear whether these electrocardiographic changes resulted in symptomatic arrhythmias and clinically significant conduction abnormalities, discontinuing or interrupting treatment, or other serious cardiac events. Differentiation of QTc elevation (laboratory/electrographic abnormality) to actual clinical cardiac events, however, is important in determining the true clinical significance of this finding.

General Cardiac Toxicity: When considering a wider composite of cardiac adverse events, including not only the isolated QTc changes but also arrhythmias, myocarditis, congestive heart failure, and other serious cardiac events, ATRA + ATO was not associated with a statistically significant increased total cardiac toxicity, compared with ATRA plus chemotherapy ( RR 0.43, 95% CI 0.16–1.14; p = 0.09; I2 = 32% ) (Supplementary Fig. 11). Subgroup analysis according to study design stratified the studies into a borderline significant protective trend in the randomized controlled trials (randomized controlled trials RR 0.26, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.93; p = 0.05) but did not show a significant difference in the cohort studies (cohort studies RR 0.60, 95% CI 0.15 to 2.33; p = 0.13). The test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (p = 0.36).

It is of utmost importance to point out that the seeming lack of augmented general cardiac toxicity with ATRA + ATO, should be viewed with a grain of salt. The reason behind this finding is the fact that there were no reported cases of serious cardiac events in the included studies, as opposed to systematic, prospective evaluation with standard definitions. Most studies were not clear as to whether patients had regular cardiac monitoring other than ECG and there might have been differences in adverse event reporting between trials and observational cohorts. Hence, the non-significance can be due to under-reporting or detection bias as opposed to actual cardiac safety. The difference between the more QTc prolongation (a laboratory/ECG abnormality that has always been found in controlled trials with standardized monitoring) and no reported clinical cardiac events most probably represents: (1) effective monitoring and electrolyte management in controlled trials (2) perhaps under-reporting of non-serious cardiac events in the published literature, and (3) inadequate statistical power to detect rare but serious cardiac events.

This seeming discrepancy, i.e., the statistically significant QTc prolongation in conjunction with the lack of increased general cardiac toxicity, would suggest that the QTc interval elevation is a manifestation of drug regulation for which there are no clinically significant cardiac consequences in the majority of patients taking ATRA + ATO. Nevertheless, this interpretation should be taken with a degree of caution in the light of the small reporting of clinical cardiac outcomes in most studies. However, this interpretation is to be approached with a certain caution in the context of slight reports on clinical cardiac outcomes of most studies. Serial electrocardiographic monitoring, especially electrolytes, especially potassium and magnesium and supplementation when necessary should be observed by clinicians as these measures are also still part and parcel of safe clinical use of ATRA + ATO treatment.

Discussion

This meta-analysis shows that in acute promyelocytic leukemia ATRA plus ATO has improved efficacy outcomes as compared to ATRA plus chemotherapy. The pooled analysis showed statistically significant improvement in complete remission (RR 1.04, 95% CI 1.02–1.06), event-free survival (RR 1.25, 95% CI 1.20–1.29), disease-free survival (RR 1.22, 95% CI 1.11–1.34), and overall survival (RR 1.07, 95% CI 1.03–1.12) with the ATRA plus ATO combination. The results of a pooled analysis based on molecular remission showed no significant difference between regimens (RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.98–1.04), which would suggest differences in the depth of molecular response despite the better clinical results [4, 25].

Comparison with immediately existing literature

The present findings are in line with several landmark trials and previous meta-analyses that ensured the superiority of ATRA plus ATO as a frontline regimen. The APL0406 trial by Lo-Coco and colleagues showed that ATRA-ATO was found to significantly improve event-free survival and overall survival, compared to ATRA-chemotherapy, in patients with standard-risk disease [4]. Similarly, the UK-based NCRI AML17 clinical trial reported similar efficacy results with less hematologic toxicity in the ATO-based arm [16]. Our pooled estimates support these seminal findings though offer expanded evidence from a diverse patient population and from studies of different study designs. The difference in magnitude of benefit that we found in our meta-analysis in terms of event-free survival (25% relative improvement) is closely comparable to that in the 2016 network meta-analysis of Wu et al., which ranked ATRA-ATO as the best treatment for newly diagnosed APL [26].

Although these results are statistically significant, the degree of benefit is worth a critical interpretation. The improvement in complete remission (RR 1.04, 4% relative improvement) is a positive benefit of 3–4% point in a disease with baseline CR rates of more than 95% when considering a combination of various modern treatment regimens. Equally, the overall survival benefit (RR 1.07, 7% relative escalation) is equivalent to a portion of 1–2% absolute increase after taking into account the 85–90% base APL survival rates in modern series. The strongest clinically meaningful benefit was observed in event free survival with the relative improvement of 25% and zero heterogeneity (I 2 = 0%), which is supported by high certainty of evidence. These results suggest ATRA + ATO has greater efficacy among which the extent of the benefit differs depending on the endpoint.

However, there are several important differences between our analysis and prior meta-analyses. A 2016 systematic review by Ma et al., comprising only 3 randomized trials with 585 patients, showed similar directional benefits but was limited by the smaller sample sizes and shorter follow-up durations [25]. In addition, our meta-analysis combines secondary observational investigations, enlarging the evidence on the levels and transferring the efficacy and effectiveness from randomized controlled to non-randomized controlled trials. Other adverse events such as gastrointestinal events, hepatic risk and the risk of differentiation syndrome were similar in both groups, suggesting no non-cardiac toxicity from the use of ATO [27]. Furthermore, our analysis has granular information about safety profiles that reveal a markedly higher risk of QTc prolongation with ATRA-ATO with substantially less risk of neutropenia compared with chemotherapy-based regimens [28].

The agreement of our findings with current treatment guidelines adds to the validity of the evidence. The 2019 European Leukemia Net recommendations define ATRA-ATO as the first line treatment of choice for patients with low and intermediate risk APL with level Ib-A evidence [29].

Meaning and mechanistic insights

The superiority of ATRA-ATO in efficacy may be attributable to several complementary modes of action which synergistically act towards the molecular pathogenesis of APL. ATRA causes differentiation of promyelocytosis due to its binding with PML-RARa fusion protein due to the release of transcriptional repression, while ATO causes degradation of the oncoprotein by caspase-dependent apoptosis and proteasome-mediated processes [30, 31]. This double mechanism most likely explains the further and more sustained molecular cures observed with combination therapy as compared to that with either agent alone. Preclinical studies have shown that ATRA and ATO have a synergistic effect in eradicating leukemia-initiating cells (the reason for the decreased relapse and longer disease-free survival observed in clinical trials [32].

The high degree of heterogeneity observed for disease-free survival (I² = 85.4%) and overall survival (I² = 76.1%) is likely the result of numerous treatment-related and study-related factors. The studies used several different chemotherapy backbones (ID-based vs. DA-based) that have different efficacy and toxicity. The studies have also used different p.a. arsenic trioxide (IV vs. oral; lbs-based vs. flat dosing; and different durations of treatment) and varying methods of risk stratification (differing percentages of low vs. intermediate vs. high risk). In addition, the studies also differed in their supportive care (use of a corticosteroid prophylaxis, transfusion triggers, and complication management) along with the duration of follow-up, whether the study was in a trial vs. a real-world setting, and the baseline characteristics of the patients (age, renal function, cardiac baseline).

Furthermore, disease-free survival sensitivity analyses using the leave-one-out approach revealed that the pooled effect had a high strength (RR range 1.18–1.25) during the exclusion of each study. Meta-regression analyses also did not show patient age or study design to be significant moderators of treatment effect for OS, suggesting that ATRA-ATO effect benefits are similar among various patient subpopulations [33].

Subgroup analyses stratified by trial design showed the treatment effects resembled between randomized controlled trial data and cohort studies for complete remission, event-free survival, and overall survival, and no statistically significant subgroup difference was posed. For safety endpoints, however important subgroup differences were found for neutropenia and QT prolongation, without any suggestion of effect modification, merely differences in reporting or patient choice.

Cardiac safety: QTc prolongation and clinical significance

Among the significant conclusions that have to be interpreted closely, it is the seeming disparity between augmented QTc prolongation and the absence of augmented general cardiac toxicity in the case of ATRA + ATO. ATRA + ATO also showed a strong preference towards the increase in QTc prolongation (RR 3.79, p = 0.05), with a strong effect in RCTs (RR 7.82, p = 0.0003) by not in observational cohorts (RR 1.17, p = 0.84). This subgroup separation probably represents variability in the strength of electrocardiographic monitoring: RCTs use standardized, serial ECG monitoring with centralized interpretation and identify the small QTc changes produced in the presence of drugs, whereas observational cohorts use clinician-initiated monitoring as it appears clinically warranted, and this leads to under-detection of asymptomatic QTc prolongation. In contrast the overall outcome of general cardiac toxicity that comprised symptomatic arrhythmias, myocarditis, congestive heart failure, and treatment-limiting cardiac events did not increase the risk significantly with ATRA + ATO (RR 0.43, p = 0.09), and was statistically insignificant yet protective in RCTs (RR 0.26, p = 0.05).

Nevertheless, this cardiac safety should be taken with a lot of caution. The negative statistically significant elevated general cardiac toxicity in the pooled analysis is not in itself evidence of cardiac safety. A number of weighty drawbacks tincture this conclusion:

First, the discovery relies on the stated lack of severe cardiac incidents as opposed to systematic and prospective evaluation of all studies using standardized definitions. Second, there is frequently inconsistency in adverse event reporting in clinical trials and observational studies which may be under-reporting of non-serious or foreseen events. Third, cardiac monitoring beyond routinely ECG was not clearly stated in many studies and detection biasness could have affected the rates. Fourth, the number of events and sample sizes in terms of cardiac outcomes is relatively small and implies that uncommon cardiac toxicities that are still of clinical importance might not have been detected (type II error).

What this probably represents is that the lengthening of QTc interval is an electrocardiographic adverse effect of the drug on cardiac repolarization which, given the circumstances of suitable monitoring and electrolyte replacement (specifically potassium and magnesium replacement), did not result in any clinically significant cardiac events in the groups of populations under study. This meaning is consistent with clinical practice in which vigilant surveillance plans are standard practice. However, these cardiac safety outcomes have a very low GRADE certainty because of imprecision and probably under-reporting; clinicians should have a high index of suspicion and adhere to the existing monitoring guidelines instead of relying on a lack of reported events as an indicator of cardiac safety.The management plans such as serial ECG monitoring, electrolyte check (especially potassium and magnesium), and its administration when necessary are all a part of the safe administration of ATRA + ATO.

Strengths

This meta-analysis has some methodological strengths that increase validity and reliability of findings. First, in the exhaustive literature search strategy, a large body of evidence has been found in the scientific literature that ranged from randomized trials to observational studies. Second, rigorous assessment of the publication bias using Egger’s test and funnel plot revealed no significant asymmetry in complete remission and overall survival [34]. Third, only the random-effects modelling, a leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, meta-regression, enhanced the robustness of effect estimates. Fourth, both efficacy and safety endpoints were analyzed in a multidisciplinary and balanced manner that addresses the risk-benefit profile needed for the evidence-based approach to clinical decision making.

Limitations

There are a number of caveats that should be noted. First, the meta-analytic approach is inherently dependent on the quality and completeness of data from the primary studies and residual confounding from unmeasured variables in observational studies cannot be completely excluded [35]. Second, heterogeneity was high among studies indicating diversity of treatment protocols, variability in patients studied and differences in the definition of outcome. Third, most of the studies included were from Asia and Europe and these may not be valid for more global generalizability [36]. One major limitation is that the primary efficacy assessment does not have a risk-stratified analysis. Even though this meta-analysis evaluated studies that included patients in all APL risk strata, the primary analysis aggregated data without any systematic low/intermediate/high risk group stratification. Approximately 30–40% of newly diagnosed APL cases are high-risk APL (WBC > 10,000/µL), which necessitates distinct treatment strategies such as the incorporation of Gemtuzumab Ozogamicin or intensified Idarubicin. Therefore, the pooled efficacy estimates are mainly applicable to the lower risk treatments, which diminishes their relevance to the management of high-risk diseases. The data supporting high-risk APL is mostly secondary, and the decision to treat these patients should include other points of special interest in specifically high-risk research and the existing recommendations.Fourth, we were unable to make a distinction between oral and intravenous preparations of arsenic trioxide because of the lack of reporting in primary studies. Majority of the trials involved the use of intravenous ATO that continues to be the norm in most Western nations which restricts its applicability to those settings where oral preparations (realgar-indigo naturalis formula) are becoming more common, especially in Asia. Formulation bioavailability, adherence and toxicity differences could not be evaluated.Fifth, individual patient-level data is lacking and it is impossible to conduct a more detailed subgroup analysis. Finally, different follow-up lengths did not allow evaluation of late relapse, secondary malignancy and chronic toxicities. Also, in the case of cardiac safety outcomes, the seeming lack of augmented hazard is founded on restricted coverage rather than multisystematic examination, and the possibility of under-detection or under-reporting of cardiac incidents is a significant constraint.

Socioeconomic, clinical, and public health consequentialities

The results justify the application of ATRA-ATO in accordance with the existing guideline-based notions of the NCCN and the European Leukemia Net on the use of low- and intermediate-risk APL [29, 37]. It is this evidence together with other factors that are considered such as the cost, availability of drugs, and individual patient factors that are combined with the evidence to come up with treatment recommendations through clinical guideline committees.

The chemotherapy-free strategy is of special benefit in the elderly, patients with cardiac dysfunction, and people at high risk for morbidity from chemotherapeutic agents. Management of high-risk patients continues to be studied, and guidelines indicate that Gemtuzumab Ozogamicin or Idarubicin should be added to ATRA-ATO to reduce the risk of early death [38].

From a health policy point of view, adoption of ATRA-ATO may translate into lower hospitalization costs, needs and overall costs of care. New preparation methods for arsenic may make it easier to get the drug out to low-resource areas [39]. Implementation of molecular monitoring programs for detection of early molecular relapse is still crucial for optimizing patient outcome [40].

Future research directions

Outstanding deficiencies that should be considered for further work include: randomized trials to determine optimal regimens for high-risk APL; randomized trials to optimize consolidation and maintenance strategies; prospective safety studies to look at long-term arsenic toxicity; and molecular predictive studies of response to allow precision medicine strategies [41, 42]. The comparative efficacy of arsenic trioxide oral and intravenous formulations is an important question that is emerging. While intravenous ATO is the standard treatment in most western countries, there is an increasing use of oral formulations (including realgar-indigo naturalis formula) in Asia with promising results. The next meta-analyses and randomized controlled trials in the future in particular should compare the results of oral and intravenous ATO regimens that include:

Remission rates, survival rates.

Bioavailability and pharmacokinetic properties.

Toxicity, especially the gastrointestinal tolerability and cardiac.

Patterns of adherence using oral and intravenous formulations.

Health economic implications and cost-effectiveness.

Applicability in various healthcare contexts including resource-constrained contexts.

Such analyses will be important as oral formulations become increasingly available around the world and also as healthcare systems look to maximise both clinical outcomes and ease of treatment. Additionally, prospective registries that have standardized data collection on ATO formulation, dosing schedules and reporting of adverse events would allow for more robust comparative effectiveness research to be done in the future.Moreover, as the constraints in cardiac safety evaluation were noted, in prospective studies in the future, ECG abnormalities together with clinical cardiac events must be systematically recorded with the help of standardized definitions and generalized monitoring guidelines. This would enable making more definitive judgments on the actual cardiac risk profile of ATRA + ATO and evoke evidence-based monitoring guidelines.

Conclusion

Based on high to moderate certainty evidence from randomised controlled trials, this meta-analysis shows that ATRA plus arsenic trioxide (ATR + ATO) is associated with better efficacy outcomes such as complete remission, event free sủvival, disease free survival and overall survival compared with ATRA and plus chemotherapy in patients with newly diagnosed low to intermediate risk acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL). Observational studies verify these findings and thus support the external validity of the trial results.Regimens did not differ significantly in non-cardiac profiles of safety, although, in both controlled trails, ATRA + ATO was inversely related to gastrointestinal toxicity. The QTc increase became more common in ATRA + ATO especially with the standardized monitoring of the electrocardiographic. Of significance, such an electrocardiographic result was not translated into statistically significant findings on reported clinical cardiac toxicity on the pooled analysis. This perceived cardiac safety should however be taken with caution since it is founded on the lack of events that are reported and not the thorough prospective analysis and under-reports or detection biases cannot be ignored.These results contribute to the preferential use of ATRA + ATO in the context of low- and intermediate-risk APL in correlation with the current guidelines of the clinical practices offered by the European LeukemiaNet and NCCN. In high-risk APL (which is about 30–40% of the cases), evidence is mostly indirect and treatment plans must incorporate several options as emphasized by current guidelines e.g. gemtuzumab ozogamicin or the intensification of anthracyclines. The principles of safe use of ATRA + ATO include close cardiac observation by serial electrocardiograms and electrolyte control. The further research is also necessary; it must optimize the treatment of the high-risk disease, compare the oral and intravenous ATO preparations, and observe the outcomes of the longitudinal and cardiac outcomes systematically with the standardized definitions.
