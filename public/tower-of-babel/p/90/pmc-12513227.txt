Introduction

Cecal volvulus represents 1–3 % of intestinal obstructions and 10–30 % of colonic volvulus cases [1], occurring when the cecum torsionates around its mesentery, classically presenting with acute abdominal distension, pain, vomiting, and constipation [2].

besides its rarity it is most often seen in patients over the age of 60 or younger patients with comorbid anatomic or hypomotility disorders [3].

Major risk factors include intestinal malrotation, Hirschsprung disease, megacolon, pregnancy, abdominal adhesions, and notably higher incidence in neuropsychiatric inpatients [2,4].

The diagnosis of cecal volvulus in children presents significant challenges due to both its extreme rarity and nonspecific clinical presentation, typically manifesting as abdominal pain, distension, and vomiting. These diagnostic difficulties are further compounded in pediatric patients with communication barriers, particularly children with neurodevelopmental disorders. Although abdominal radiographs may demonstrate nonspecific bowel dilation, they frequently fail to provide definitive diagnosis. Computed tomography (CT) imaging remains the gold standard for confirmation, with the pathognomonic “whirl sign” representing the twisted mesentery and providing conclusive evidence of volvulus [[5], [6], [7]].

Emergency surgical intervention is mandatory, with options ranging from detorsion with cecopexy/cecostomy to definitive resection-anastomosis [4]; the latter remains gold-standard for recurrence prevention despite lack of consensus guidelines due to condition rarity [1,4].

We present a case of cecal volvulus with intestinal malrotation in a 12-year-old boy successfully managed by emergent detorsion and right hemicolectomy, highlighting the importance of early surgical intervention in pediatric bowel obstruction. We detail the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of managing this complex presentation while adhering to the SCARE guidelines for surgical case reporting [8].

Case presentation

History & initial symptoms

A previously healthy 12-year-old boy presented with sudden-onset, severe abdominal pain localized to the right iliac fossa, accompanied by repeated vomiting and absolute constipation (no stool passage). He had no significant medical, surgical, or family history. Physical examination revealed marked tenderness in the right iliac fossa with tympanitic percussion notes, raising suspicion for bowel obstruction.

Initial laboratory tests revealed mild SGPT elevation (46 U/L) with otherwise normal CBC and biochemistry. Abdominal ultrasound, while ruling out appendicitis, demonstrated dilated and edematous bowel loops, raising suspicion of obstruction. Subsequent abdominal X-ray provided critical confirmation, showing: (1) marked cecal/ascending colon dilation extending toward the left upper quadrant, (2) collapsed distal colon, and (3) small bowel air-fluid levels - collectively diagnostic of cecal volvulus Fig. 1.

Management & outcome

The volvulus was manually reduced, but due to significant colonic dilation and necrotic patches, an extended right hemicolectomy (including terminal ileum) with primary ileocolonic anastomosis was performed. The abdomen was irrigated, and two drains (Morrison's pouch and pelvis) were placed.

In our patient, resection was prioritized over detorsion + fixation due to irreversible ischemia and the high recurrence risk associated with cecopexy in pediatric malrotation-associated volvulus.

Postoperatively, Fig. 2 the patient recovered well—the drains were removed on days 3 and 5, respectively, and he was discharged in good condition without complications.

Discussion

Cecal volvulus occurs due to incomplete retroperitoneal fixation of the cecum and ascending colon, an anatomical configuration similar to that seen in intestinal malrotation; however, despite malrotation being present in approximately 1 % of the general population, cecal volvulus remains exceptionally rare in pediatric patients, suggesting that additional factors beyond simple anatomical predisposition are required for clinical manifestation of volvulus in children [9].

Patients with colonic volvulus typically present with acute intestinal obstruction symptoms - severe abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and obstipation - often with striking abdominal distension exceeding other obstruction causes. The condition carries high risk of ischemic complications from either bowel wall tension or mesenteric torsion, with ominous signs including refractory pain, rebound tenderness, and tachycardia indicating potential necrosis [10].

Our patient exhibited classic cecal volvulus symptoms (acute abdominal pain, vomiting, obstipation) but lacked typical marked distension or hemodynamic instability, while uniquely demonstrating malrotation anatomy with left-sided small bowel - highlighting how pediatric presentations may diverge from adult patterns.

Diagnostic workup for colonic volvulus includes labs (CBC, metabolic panel, lactate) and imaging - abdominal X-rays may show classic cecal volvulus features (RLQ-to-LUQ bowel dilation, upward appendix displacement) or sigmoid volvulus' “bent inner tube” sign, while CT scans provide definitive diagnosis in 90 % of cecal cases, with 10 % diagnosed intraoperatively [11].

Our streamlined diagnostic approach—combining ultrasound's red flags with X-ray's “coffee bean” sign—achieved what CT typically provides, proving resourcefulness rivals technology when clinical expertise leads the way.

Surgical findings matched our imaging suspicions perfectly, validating that in skilled hands, even basic tools can unlock complex diagnoses.

Cecal volvulus requires surgical intervention, as non-operative approaches (colonoscopy/barium enema) carry high perforation risks and miss necrosis in 20–25 % of cases. The surgical strategy depends on intraoperative viability assessment, ranging from detorsion to resection [12].

We performed an emergency right hemicolectomy with primary anastomosis after confirming cecal volvulus with malrotation intraoperatively, following our clinical diagnosis supported by ultrasound findings. This definitive surgical management aligns with standard treatment principles for complicated cecal volvulus, achieving complete resolution without complications.

Conclusion

This case highlights the successful management of a rare pediatric cecal volvulus with intestinal malrotation through prompt surgical intervention. Despite atypical presentation without marked abdominal distension, the diagnosis was achieved using a combination of clinical suspicion, ultrasound, and abdominal X-ray findings. Emergency right hemicolectomy with primary anastomosis proved effective, emphasizing the importance of early surgical decision-making in preventing complications. The patient's full recovery underscores the value of timely intervention in pediatric bowel obstruction cases.
