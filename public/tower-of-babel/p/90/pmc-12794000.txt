Incremental Steps Toward the Holy Grail of Contemporary PCI
Authors: Martin W Bergmann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: bending angle, bioresorbable scaffold, coronary biomechanics

The treatment of coronary artery disease (CAD) by percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) has revolutionized cardiology. Outcomes of both acute myocardial infarction and chronic CAD have improved over the years dramatically when PCI is combined with optimal medical treatment. The rate of repeat revascularization has dropped markedly both by improved stenting techniques, including intravascular imaging, and by plaque regression through intense lipid-lowering therapies. PCI has emerged as the standard technique for revascularization in patients with relevant CAD, whereas coronary artery bypass grafting is reserved for patients with complex 3-vessel disease. However, long-term data follow-up still finds target vessel revascularization (TVR) to be necessary in >10% of patients at 5 years. TVR risk factors include an extensive stent area or 2-stent techniques.1 Conversely, PCI showed symptom relief and reduced emergency revascularization but had no mortality benefit in patients with chronic coronary syndromes who received guideline-directed optimal medical therapy in recent trials.1 In summary, many experts regard the concept of “leave nothing behind” as the next frontier for improving outcomes of PCI and symptomatic relief from angina and dyspnea.

Restoring physiological coronary vessel lumen without extensive metallic prosthesis in the vessel wall can be achieved by either drug-coated balloon (DCB) or bioresorbable scaffold (BRS) treatment of ischemic CAD. The concept of a sirolimus-coated balloon with provisional drug-eluting stent (DES) implantation is currently being tested in the randomized, multicenter, noninferiority SELUTION DeNovo trial (A Prospective Randomized, Multi-center, International, Single-blind, Clinical Trial Compared the Selution DEB Strategy Versus DES Strategy) aiming to recruit 3,326 PCI patients with de novo lesions.2 In brief, the study is testing the hypothesis that the SELUTION DCB (M.A. Med Alliance) combined with provisional stenting in areas of flow-limiting dissection is noninferior to prima vista DES (XIENCE, Abbott) treatment regarding target vessel failure (TVF) at 1 and 5 years. If noninferiority is met, the study is also powered to test for superiority.

Another approach for restoring physiological coronary vessel lumen centers on the development of coronary scaffolds. Several devices are currently under investigation or development and are in part available in Europe and/or Asia (Table 1). Ongoing studies in this field include a new, large-scale study aiming for global approvement of the FREESOLVE third-generation magnesium scaffold (Biotronik). The BIOMAG-II (Safety and Clinical Performance of the DREAMS 3G Resorbable Magnesium Scaffold System) randomized clinical trial recruited 1,859 all-comer patients indicated for PCI randomized to scaffold vs DES (XIENCE) treatment. Initial enthusiasm for the concept of BRSs replacing contemporary DESs was dampened by increased rates of clinical events driven by scaffold thrombosis compared with DES despite dual antiplatelet therapy (Table 1). It is in this context that the study by Fezzi et al,12 published in this issue of JACC: Asia, adds an important aspect. Aside from plaque characteristics and vessel size, dynamic changes of 3-dimensional vessel bending could affect BRS performance with respect to TVR and clinical event rates. The study also describes the following long-term findings: after BRS resorption (≈48 months), the BA increased and returned to pre-PCI baseline values. ΔcBA also recovered, indicating restoration of vessel biomechanics. In summary, the study by Fezzi et al12 finds BRS to reduce coronary bending flexibility in the short term after implantation; greater biomechanical restriction after implantation is linked to higher long-term vessel failure, with an HR of 1.65 with every 10° increase in BA. Once the scaffold is resorbed, natural biomechanics are restored.12

What can we learn from these data for contemporary BRS? BA assessment may help identify vessels more suitable for BRS implantation. This may be important for either BRS implantation or DCB combined with provisional DES stenting if “leave nothing behind” is the aim for the individual patient. In addition, a shorter duration of scaffold resorption may have an advantage in patients with lesions with an increased BA because these vessels would be expected to restore physiologic biomechanics earlier. These considerations could be important regarding the choice of BRS material. As depicted in Table 1, resorption time differs dramatically among different types of BRS, with the magnesium alloy and desaminotyrosine-derived polymer resorbed over approximately 12 months, whereas poly-L-lactic acid (PLLA)–based BRS resorbs only over a period of 2 to 4 years. BRS resorption may also differ depending on initial plaque components and vessel size, as well as other individual patient characteristics such as diabetes, hypertension, or cholesterol levels. Conversely, a shorter resorption time may lead to increased TVR rates secondary to instability of the vessel if restoration of physiologic biomechanics may not be completed.

The first BRS for PCI was the Igaki-Tamai PLLA stent studied between 1998 and 2000. The Absorb BRS gained the CE mark in 2011 and Food and Drug Administration approval by 2016 before data of increased clinical event rates forced Abbott to stop the program. Critically, so far, no study has been able to prove noninferiority, not to mention superiority, with either the BRS or DCB combined with provisional DES stenting compared with thin-strut fourth-generation DESs in PCI. At least 2 ongoing studies (BIOMAG-II, SELUTION DeNovo) are designed to fill that gap. In parallel, clinical event rates following PCI decrease as pharmacologic control of atherosclerosis risk factors such as low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and lipoprotein(a), as well as diabetes and hypertension, improve. Antiplatelet therapy or anticoagulation finds a better balance between bleeding and prevention of ischemic clinical events. Both invasive imaging and noninvasive imaging improve surveillance, planning, and short- and long-term diagnosis and treatment results in patients with acute or chronic coronary heart disease.1 Nevertheless, our tools to prevent ischemic cardiomyopathy as well as morbidity and mortality in these patients still need to be improved. A better understanding and further development of PCI using BRS will certainly be part of our armamentarium in the years to come, including the well-performed study by Fezzi et al.12
