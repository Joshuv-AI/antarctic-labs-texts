The Relationship between Vesicoureteral Reflux and Urinary Stones in Children Presenting with Urinary Tract Infections
Authors: Maryam Sadat Mohsenzadeh, Mitra Naseri, Niayesh Tafazoli, Nooshin Tafazoli, Sayed Ali Alamdaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) can contribute to the formation of urinary stones. This study aims to assess the relationship between VUR and nephrolithiasis, as well as the correlation between the presence and grade of VUR with the formation and size of urinary stones.Methods:A retrospective and cross-sectional study was conducted from September 2003 to December 2016. Children diagnosed with urinary tract infections (UTIs) were enrolled if they underwent both kidney-bladder ultrasound and voiding cystourethrography. The study examined the presence of stones in kidney-ureter units (KUUs) with and without VUR and assessed the relationship between the grades of VUR and both stone formation and size. Patients with anatomical urinary tract obstructions were excluded from the study.Results:A total of 898 patients were enrolled. The frequencies of VUR, nephrolithiasis, and both VUR and nephrolithiasis were found to be 46.65%, 13.8%, and 5.7%, respectively. Three cases were excluded due to anatomical urinary tract obstructions. Of the 48 enrolled cases, 62.5% were female. Unilateral VUR was reported in 43.75% of the patients. Nephrolithiasis was identified in 68% refluxing KUUs and 57.2% of nonrefluxing units (P = 0.354). No significant differences were observed in the frequency of nephrolithiasis, the size of the largest stones, or the number of stones among patients with high-grade versus mild to moderate VUR (P < 0.05 for both).Conclusion:The study indicates that neither the presence of VUR nor it grading significantly influences stone formation in patients with UTIs. Other factors beyond urinary stasis due to VUR may be responsible for stone formation.

INTRODUCTION

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the most common bacterial infections in children, accounting for 10% of all febrile illnesses.[1] Simultaneously, kidney stones affect 2%–20% of the global population.[2] The interplay between UTIs and kidney stones raises important health concerns, as both can exacerbate each other. The key contributing factors to kidney stone formation include anatomical abnormalities and metabolic disorders.[3456]

In the United States, kidney stones result in approximately one out of every 1000–7500 pediatric hospital admissions,[78] and their prevalence has risen alarmingly in recent decades.[5] Certain urease-producing organisms can lead to UTIs that contribute to infection stones, while existing kidney stones may cause urinary obstruction, increasing UTI risk.[2] Additionally, the prevalence of vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) among patients with renal stones is 8%–18%, significantly higher than in the general population,[91011121314] further studies confirmed the relationship between these conditions.[1516]

Despite this connection, research on VUR and nephrolithiasis in children remains limited. This study aims to fill that gap by examining the relationship between VUR and nephrolithiasis in children with UTIs, focusing on the following questions:

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design, selection, and description of participants

An observational study was conducted at the Nephrology Clinic of a tertiary academic children’s hospital from September 2003 to December 2016. The sample size was determined using a census method. Eligible participants included children aged 18 years or younger with a confirmed diagnosis of a UTI.

For urine sampling, the midstream method was employed for toilet-trained children, while urine collection bags were used for nontoilet-trained children. A culture was defined as positive if a single microorganism was present with colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL) ≥105. In samples collected using urine bags, the presence of white blood cells ≥5 per high power field, coupled with CFUs/mL growth ≥105, was required for a UTI diagnosis.

Urolithiasis referred to the presence of calculi at any location along the urinary tract. On ultrasound, renal stones appear hyperechoic and exhibit posterior acoustic shadowing, which can vary depending on their size and the frequency of the transducer.[17] The diagnosis of urolithiasis (kidney stones) was confirmed using kidney and bladder ultrasound at the initial presentation. Patients underwent a voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) after completing a 2-week course of treatment for UTIs. Some outpatient cases were referred for follow-up several months (3–6 months) after the initial diagnosis of a UTI. In these instances, VCUG was performed within a maximum of 6 months.

Kidney-bladder ultrasonography was performed using Adra, Siemens (Berlin, Germany), Esoate Class C, Esoate Company, Genoa, Itally, or Samsung H60 (Korea) equipment, Samsung Company, Seoul, South Korea. Kidney ultrasounds utilized 5, 7.5, and 10 MHz probes. According to the international grading system,[18] VUR was categorized into five Grades: I-V. Grades I-III were identified as mild to moderate, while Grades IV and V were classified as high-grade VUR. A checklist was developed for data collection, which was conducted using patients’ medical records.

Of the 1245 eligible patients, 898 cases (72.1%) who underwent both kidney-bladder ultrasound and VCUG were assessed to determine the frequency of VUR associated with nephrolithiasis (the first part of the study). Then, patients with VUR whose initial kidney ultrasounds showed nephrolithiasis were enrolled in the second part of the study. Confounders contributing to stone formation, including ureteropelvic junction obstruction (UPJO), renal cysts, and neurogenic bladder, were excluded.

Kidney-ureter units (KUUs) were divided into refluxing and nonrefluxing units. Refluxing KUUs were classified into units with high-grade and mild-to-moderate VUR. The incidence of stones in refluxing versus nonrefluxing units was determined and compared. In addition, the relationship between the sizes of the largest stones and the grades of VUR (high-grade vs. mild to moderate) was assessed. The criteria for selecting eligible cases for enrollment in the study

The Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences approved the study (IR.MUMS.MEDICAL.REC.1397.153) (approval date, June 27, 2018) and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Statistical analyses

The collected data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 16, SPSS Institute, Inc., Chicago, IL,USA). The normality of the data was assessed with either the one-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test or the Shapiro–Wilk test, depending on the sample size. For the comparisons of quantitative data, both the t-test and Mann–Whitney test were utilized. The Chi-squared and Fisher’s exact tests were employed to compare the categorical data. P <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Population with vesicoureteral reflux and urinary stones

Among the 419 cases with VUR, 51 patients (12.2%) had urinary stones, while 41.1% of the children with urinary stones (51 out of 124 patients) presented with VUR. In addition, three cases were noted to have UPJO or posterior urethral valves and were therefore excluded from the study.

The enrolled cases (n = 48) included 30 girls (62.5%) and 18 boys (37.5%). The median age at the first UTI was 13.5 months, with an interquartile range (IQR) of 7.5–60 months. At the initial presentation, 24 patients (50%) experienced febrile UTIs, while the remaining patients presented with cystitis.

VUR was classified as unilateral in 21 patients (43.75%) and bilateral in 27 patients (56.25%). This indicates that out of 96 kidney units analyzed (48 patients), 75 units (78.1%) were refluxing, and 21 units (21.9%) were nonrefluxing. Out of 27 cases with bilateral VUR, one patient (3.7%) had bilateral high-grade (severe) VUR. Five cases (18.5%) exhibited high-grade VUR in one kidney and mild-to-moderate VUR in the other. The remaining 21 cases (77.8%) had bilateral mild-to-moderate VUR.

Urinary stones in refluxing and nonrefluxing kidney-ureter units

Urinary stones were present in 51 out of 75 refluxing KUUs, representing a significant 68%, compared to 12 out of 21 nonrefluxing KUUs, which accounts for 57.2% (P = 0.354). This data clearly indicates that urinary stones are prevalent in both refluxing and nonrefluxing KUUs. Notably, high-grade VUR is identified in seven cases (14.6%), with two being bilateral (28.6%). The high-grade VUR being observed in 9.4% of the total KUUs (9 out of 96 KUUs) and in 12% of the refluxing KUUs (9 out of 75 KUUs). Among the 75 refluxing KUUs, VUR manifests in 40 (53.3%) on the left side and in 35 (46.7%) on the right side. Out of the 63 KUUs with urinary stones, a substantial 36 (57.1%) are located on the left, while 27 (42.9%) are on the right. Both urinary stones and VUR were more common in the left KUUs compared to the right.

Hypercalciuria and hyperuricosuria in patients

Hypercalciuria and hyperuricosuria were defined by urinary calcium-to-urinary creatinine (U Ca/U Cr mg/mg) and urinary uric acid to urinary creatinine (U Uric acid/U Cr mg/mg) ratios that exceed the expected values for age, as established in the literature[19] [Table 2]. The reported U Ca/U Cr ratio was 0.28 (ranging from 0.13 to 0.44) mg/mg, and the U Uric acid/U Cr ratio measures 0.8 (ranging from 0.58 to 1.38) mg/mg. Specifically, hypercalciuria was observed in 4 out of 30 girls (13.3%) and 2 out of 16 boys (12.5%). Hyperuricosuria was present in 1 out of 26 girls (3.8%) and 2 out of 15 boys (13.3%) (P = 1 and P = 0.543, respectively). It is important to note that all cases of hyperuricosuria (n = 3) and hypercalciuria (n = 6) were associated with mild to moderate VUR (Grades I-III).

Examining the relationship between urinary stone sizes and vesicoureteral reflux grades

A thorough analysis revealed that the occurrence of urinary stones and the dimensions of the largest stones in KUUs do not differ significantly between high-grade and mild-to-moderate VUR. Interestingly, we found that 77.8% of KUUs with high-grade VUR experienced urinary stones, while this rate stood at 65.15% for those with mild-to-moderate VUR, demonstrating no statistically significant correlation (P = 0.709; Fisher’s exact test).

Moreover, when we compare the sizes of the largest stones, the results remained consistent: KUUs with high-grade VUR presented stones averaging 2.46 ± 1.37 mm, while those with mild to moderate VUR had stones averaging 3.05 ± 1.6 mm. DISCUSSION

The results clearly indicate that the frequency of nephrolithiasis does not differ significantly between refluxing and nonrefluxing KUUs. Furthermore, the grade of VUR – whether high or mild to moderate – has no significant impact on the frequency of nephrolithiasis, the size of the largest stones, or the overall number of stones present. This also applies to urine calcium to creatinine and uric acid-to-creatinine ratios (P > 0.05 for all).

There is a pronounced variation in the prevalence of nephrolithiasis across different countries. The incidence in Asia is alarmingly high at 7%, contrasting sharply with just 0.1% in the United States.[5] Nephrolithiasis represents a significant complication of VUR, primarily due to urinary stasis and infection. Numerous studies have established VUR as a definitive risk factor for nephrolithiasis.[14152021]

The formation of nephrolithiasis in patients with VUR is directly linked to UTIs and urinary stasis induced by reflux, which leads to the creation of urinary crystals. Additionally, various anatomical abnormalities indisputably contribute to an increased risk of stone formation. They include UPJO, horseshoe kidney, vesical diverticula, medullary sponge kidney, and caliceal cysts.[22] Such anatomical anomalies cause urinary stasis and crystallization, which are critical precursors to stone formation.[23]

Akhavan Sepahi et al.[24] conducted an evaluation of 199 children with nephrolithiasis and established a prevalence rate of 16.1% for VUR. In our study, we determined that the frequency of VUR among children with nephrolithiasis is significantly higher at 41.1% (51 out of 124 patients), approximately 2.5 times greater than the figure reported by Akhavan Sepahi et al. The notable differences in methodology account for this discrepancy. Their research exclusively targeted children diagnosed with nephrolithiasis, while our study included a pediatric population with UTIs as well. Furthermore, they only considered patients with nephrolithiasis measuring ≥2 mm, whereas our investigation encompassed the cases with nephrolithiasis <2 mm.

The genetic factors are crucial in the development of kidney stone diseases. A family history of nephrolithiasis is prevalent, a report indicating that 25% of infants ≤2 months with nephrolithiasis have such a history.[25] In our study, we analyzed 48 cases of patients who had both VUR and urinary stones. We found that a significant 58.3% (28 cases) had a positive family history of kidney stone disease. This was further divided into 11 cases (22.9%) with affected first-degree relatives and 16 cases (33.3%) with affected second-degree relatives. In addition, one patient (2.1%) reported a family history of stones in both first and second-degree relatives. Notably, 27.1% (13 cases) had no known family history of stones, while in 14.6% (seven cases), the parents were uncertain about the family history.

The current study found a higher frequency of family history of kidney stones (58.3%) compared to the study by Naseri, which reported a frequency of 25%.[25] The difference can be attributed to variations in methodology and the ages of the enrolled cases. Naseri[25] focused on pediatric patients aged 2 months or younger who were diagnosed with kidney stones. In contrast, our study evaluated children aged 18 years or younger who had a confirmed diagnosis of UTI, along with both VUR and kidney stones, and who did not have any anatomical obstruction in the urinary tract.

A positive family history of stone has been reported in 62.7% of pediatric stone formers, a frequency higher that we noted in current study (58.3%).[4] Again it seems that different methodology is attributed for different results. Their participants included children with a diagnosis of kidney stone, whereas we enrolled cases with a history of UTI who had both VUR and kidney stone, also no anatomical urinary tract obstruction. An extensive study of adult stone formers found a positive family history of stones in 18% of the cases.[26]

Numerous studies have established a significant link between VUR and conditions such as hypercalciuria and hyperuricosuria.[141527] A study conducted by García-Nieto et al.[14] brought attention to this issue by examining 46 pediatric patients diagnosed with VUR, whose average age was 3.9 ± 2.6 years, spanning from 0.16 to 9.8 years. Their findings were concerning: 19.5% of patients exhibited renal stones on kidney ultrasound, while a staggering 58.6% were found to have hypercalciuria.

In contrast, our research revealed a lower prevalence of nephrolithiasis in the VUR population at 12.2% (51 out of 419 cases). Notably, we assessed hypercalciuria only in patients presenting with both VUR and nephrolithiasis, resulting in a discovery rate of approximately 13%. This figure highlights a crucial difference – our findings reflect roughly one-fourth of the hypercalciuria prevalence reported by García-Nieto et al.[14]

The variation between these studies can largely be attributed to different methodological approaches. While García-Nieto et al.[14] employed the criteria established by Stapleton et al.,[28] which defined hypercalciuria based on urine calcium to urine creatinine (U Ca/U Cr) ratios (nonfasting single spot samples > 0.5 mg/mg for those under 2 years and > 0.2 mg/mg for older children).

Utilizing the Stapleton et al. criteria,[28] our study found hypercalciuria in 7 out of 29 children (24.1%) under 2 years, 6 out of 17 (35.3%) over 2 years, and 13 out of 46 patients overall (28.3%). These results starkly contrast with those of García-Nieto et al.[14] who reported hypercalciuria prevalence at an alarming 58.6%, nearly double what we observed.

These findings call for a deeper understanding of VUR and its complications, as well as a reconsideration of the criteria used to evaluate hypercalciuria. Only through diligent research and consistent evaluation can we hope to improve patient outcomes and advance our knowledge in this critical area of pediatric health. Madani et al.[15] reported that hypercalciuria affects 21.3% of children with VUR, compared to only 3.6% of healthy children. Their findings show that the association of nephrolithiasis with VUR occurs in 29.6% of cases, which is significantly higher – 2.4 times more – than the 12.2% we documented in our total VUR population (51 out of 419 cases). Additionally, our analysis reveals that 13% of patients with both VUR and nephrolithiasis exhibit hypercalciuria. They defined hypercalciuria using a urine calcium to creatinine ratio (mg/mg) of > 0.8 for children under 6 months, >0.6 for those aged 6–12 months, and > 0.21 for patients older than 2 years. Their criterion for hyperuricosuria was a urine uric acid-to-creatinine ratio (mg/mg) of >1 mg/mg. Using these definitions, we calculated the prevalence rates of hypercalciuria in children: 11.1% (one out of nine) for those aged 0–6 months, 25% (three out of twelve) for those aged 6–12 months, and 48% (12 out of 25) for those older than 2 years. Overall, hypercalciuria was prevalent in 34.8% (16 out of 46 patients) of our cases, indicating a clear trend toward higher rates compared to the prior study.

Furthermore, a case-control study involving 130 children under 10 years old with a confirmed diagnosis of UTI was conducted to precisely define the frequency of VUR in sub-groups with and without nephrolithiasis.[29] The patients were divided into two groups: Group 1 included those with UTI and stones, while Group 2 comprised those with UTI but no stones. All children underwent VCUG to assess for the presence of VUR. They found that VUR was present in 25.38% of total cases. Specifically, the rates were significantly higher in Group 1 (32.3% with stones) compared to Group 2 (18.46% without stones), confirming a significant relationship between nephrolithiasis and VUR (P = 0.011).

In the first part of our study, we enrolled 898 cases to determine the frequency of VUR associated with kidney stones. The participants’ ages ranged from 1 month to 13 years and 11 months. Out of the total, 124 cases (13.8%) had urinary stones, while 774 cases (86.2%) did not. We found that 419 patients (46.6%) had VUR, and 479 patients (53.4%) did not have VUR [Flowchart 1]. In the group with stones, 51 out of 124 patients (41.1%) were recorded as having VUR, compared to 368 out of 774 patients (47.5%) in the nonstone group. This difference was statistically significant, with a P = 0.046, indicating that VUR was significantly more prevalent in the group without stones.

While hypercalciuria has been reported to affect only 2.9%–3.8% of normal children,[2130] we documented a remarkable prevalence of 13.05% in our cohort – 3.5–4.5 times higher than the normal population. The majority of our cases (86.7%) exhibited normal urinary calcium excretion, underscoring that factors other than hypercalciuria are likely responsible for stone formation. Although hypercalciuria has been suggested as a potential explanation for the association between VUR and nephrolithiasis,[152830] Ristoska-Bojkovska et al.[31] found no significant difference in hypercalciuria rates between patients with and without VUR.

A major strength of our study is the evaluation of patients with unilateral VUR, allowing us to explore the association between VUR and stone development accurately. By comparing the occurrence of stones between refluxing and nonrefluxing kidney units within the same individual, we effectively controlled for other influencing factors. Our analysis definitively determined stone occurrence in both refluxing and nonrefluxing units, while also correlating the sizes and numbers of stones with the grades of VUR. A comprehensive review of the literature revealed no similar study, validating the uniqueness of our research.

Urolithiasis can arise from various factors, including genetic predispositions, environmental influences, medical conditions, metabolic disorders, and lifestyle choices. Conditions such as high urinary supersaturation, low urinary volume, or low urinary pH can promote the formation of stones.[32] Anatomical anomalies, such as UPJO and VUR, can lead to urinary stasis, which creates a favorable environment for stone formation.[33]

One of the primary applications of kidney ultrasound in children is for those presenting with UTIs to detect anatomical abnormalities, primarily VUR, that may require further evaluation. It also assesses the renal parenchyma and provides an evaluation of renal size. There is considerable variability in the timing of the recommended kidney ultrasound, as different guidelines exist for UTIs in children. These guidelines focus on diagnosis, treatment, and imaging studies for pediatric patients with UTIs. The American Academy of Pediatrics and the Canadian Pediatric Society recommend performing kidney ultrasonography in all children aged 2–24 months who have experienced their first febrile UTI. However, guidelines in other regions may differ. Some recommend performing kidney ultrasonography after the first febrile UTI even beyond 24 months, such as up to 36 months or at any age. The UK’s National Institute for Health and Care Excellence recommends kidney ultrasonography for children younger than 6 months and only for those older than 6 months to 16 years if the UTI is recurrent or atypical.[34]

Congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract are among the most commonly diagnosed antenatal conditions. Prenatal ultrasound can lead to the early diagnosis of these anomalies before they result in UTI. In our country, prenatal kidney ultrasounds are not routinely performed for all pregnant mothers, and many children presenting with UTIs have not undergone kidney ultrasounds before their UTIs. Therefore, we recommend kidney ultrasound in all cases of pediatric UTIs, taking into account the age of the child at presentation.

The current study aimed to determine whether urinary stasis caused by VUR is responsible for the formation of kidney stones. If we found that kidney stones were more prevalent in refluxing than in nonrefluxing kidneys, it would support the hypothesis that urinary stasis due to VUR promotes the development of kidney stones. However, we noted that kidney stones were equally common in both refluxing and nonrefluxing kidneys (68% vs. 57.2%, respectively; P = 0.354). It is important to note that the small sample size of our study is a limitation, and we recommend conducting future studies with a larger sample of VUR patients with kidney stones.

The retrospective design of our study does pose limitations, potentially increasing the risk of bias and constraining the generalizability of our findings. Nonetheless, the implications of our research are clear and significant in the understanding of the relationship between VUR and nephrolithiasis.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, our findings revealed that the occurrence of kidney stones is equally frequent in both refluxing and nonrefluxing KUUs. Furthermore, it appears that the frequency of nephrolithiasis, as well as the sizes of the largest stones, is not influenced by the severity of VUR. Therefore, we concluded that neither the presence of VUR nor its grading significantly contributes to stone formation.

Regarding the revised definitions for hypercalciuria and hyperuricosuria, we observed that these metabolic abnormalities are rare in the pediatric population with UTIs who also have VUR and nephrolithiasis. Finally, further investigations are needed to determine whether urinary stasis, metabolic abnormalities, or a combination of these factors plays a role in the development of nephrolithiasis in patients with VUR.
