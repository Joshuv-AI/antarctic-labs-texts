Determinants, risk score, and prognostic implications of left ventricular thrombus in ischemic cardiomyopathy assessed by cardiac magnetic resonance: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Chayathorn Aramcharoen, Pongsakorn Buraphat, Thidanan Wanitanantakun, Sasima Tongsai, Yodying Kaolawanich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeft ventricular thrombus (LVT) is a significant complication in ischemic cardiomyopathy (ICM). However, factors associated with LVT have mainly been reported in patients with recent myocardial infarction (MI), while data in chronic ICM remain limited. This study aimed to identify factors associated with CMR-detected LVT in patients with chronic ICM (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] < 50% of ischemic etiology, >40 days post-MI), develop a CMR-based risk score, and evaluate the prognostic implications of LVT on major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE).MethodsThis study included 790 patients with ICM who underwent CMR at an academic hospital in Thailand between 2016 and 2023. Factors associated with LVT were identified using logistic regression analyses. A CMR-based risk score was developed and validated, with discriminative performance assessed by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC). MACE, defined as a composite of cardiovascular death, ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack, systemic embolism, nonfatal MI, or heart failure hospitalization, was also evaluated. Factors associated with MACE were assessed using Cox regression analysis.ResultsThe mean age was 66.9 ± 11.3 years, and 75.3% were male. LVT was detected in 116 patients. Independent factors associated with LVT included apical aneurysm, apical late gadolinium enhancement (LGE), and the number of LGE segments. The CMR-LVT Score, incorporating these variables, demonstrated an AUC of 0.73 (95%CI, 0.68–0.77;p < 0.001) in the derivation cohort and 0.79 (95%CI, 0.69–0.90;p < 0.001) in the validation cohort. During a median follow-up of 3.2 years (IQR, 1.6–5.5), 150 MACE occurred. Baseline LVT was independently associated with MACE (adjusted HR 2.38, 95%CI 1.31–4.30;p = 0.004).ConclusionsIn patients with ICM undergoing CMR, apical aneurysm, apical LGE, and LGE extent were independently associated with LVT. The CMR-LVT Score demonstrated acceptable discriminative performance for identifying higher-risk patients who may benefit from more intensive follow-up. LVT was also independently associated with MACE.

Introduction

Ischemic cardiomyopathy (ICM), characterized by impaired left ventricular (LV) systolic function due to prior myocardial infarction (MI) or obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD) [1], is a leading cause of heart failure and is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality despite advances in therapeutic options [2].

Left ventricular thrombus (LVT) is a significant complication that can occur after MI, particularly in patients with a low left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) [3]. There is a variation in the reported incidence of LVT, possibly due to differences in the imaging modalities used for diagnosis, as well as the timing and frequency of screening [4]. The presence of an LVT is linked to a 5.5-fold increased risk of stroke and systemic embolism (SE). However, the risk of embolization can be mitigated with systemic anticoagulation therapy [5,6].

Imaging for diagnosing LVT is essential for guiding treatment and improving outcomes. Transthoracic echocardiography is the conventional imaging modality for LVT detection; however, its diagnostic performance may be limited by poor image quality and advanced LV remodeling, particularly for mural thrombi [7,8]. Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging on cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) has been validated as a method for detecting LVT [9]. LGE-CMR is more accurate for detecting LVT, with a sensitivity of 88% and specificity of 99%, compared with transthoracic echocardiography, which has a sensitivity of 24%–33% and specificity of 94%–95% [3,10–13]. The superiority of LGE-CMR in detecting LVT is attributed to its higher resolution and the tissue characterization of LVT, which lack of LGE and can be clearly distinguished from the surrounding myocardium [8,9].

Previous studies have found that LVT is a frequent complication, occurring in approximately 26% of patients after anterior wall MI with LVEF < 45% [3]. Other factors associated with LVT include increased LV end-diastolic volume (LVEDV), high myocardial scar burden, apical dyskinesia or akinesia, and apical aneurysm [9,14–18]. However, most studies included only patients with acute or recent MI and did not comprehensively evaluate patients with chronic ICM.

The primary aim of the study was to identify factors associated with LVT in patients with chronic ICM who underwent CMR. The secondary aims were to develop and validate a CMR-based risk score for detecting LVT and to evaluate the prognostic implications regarding major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE).

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at the Division of Cardiology, Department of Medicine, Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand. We screened patients aged ≥18 years with known or suspected ICM who underwent CMR between June 2016 and January 2023. Exclusion criteria included severe primary left-sided valvular disease, cardiomyopathy other than ICM, acute coronary syndrome within 40 days before CMR, congenital heart disease, and a prior diagnosis of LVT on CMR.

Diagnosis of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack (TIA), MI, and heart failure was based on current guidelines [22–27].

Data from eligible patients was obtained through a review of electronic medical records in the hospital information system. The last follow-up date was October 30, 2024. Collected data included baseline characteristics such as age, sex, blood pressure, heart rate, past medical history, electrocardiography (ECG), current medications, echocardiographic parameters, CMR parameters, and clinical outcomes. Echocardiographic parameters were obtained from studies performed within 12 months of CMR. Patients were followed up for MACE, defined as a composite of cardiovascular death, ischemic stroke/TIA/systemic embolism (SE), nonfatal MI, or hospitalization for heart failure. Cardiovascular death and other endpoints were defined according to the 2017 Cardiovascular and Stroke Endpoint Definitions for Clinical Trials [28]. Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis, and no data imputation was performed.

Echocardiography

LVEDV, LV end-systolic volume (LVESV), and LVEF were assessed using the biplane Simpson’s method from standard apical 4-chamber and 2-chamber echocardiographic views, in accordance with current echocardiographic recommendations [29]. Volumetric parameters were indexed to body surface area. LV apical aneurysm was defined as a discrete, thin-walled, dyskinetic or akinetic segment in the most distal portion of the LV chamber.

CMR protocol and image analysis

CMR studies were performed using a 1.5 or 3.0 Tesla Philips Achieva XR scanner (Philips Medical Systems, Best, The Netherlands) to evaluate cardiac function and LGE. Images for the cardiac functional study were acquired using the steady-state free precession (SSFP) technique in multiple short-axis slices, as well as in 2-chamber, 3-chamber, and 4-chamber views. The imaging parameters for the cardiac functional study at 1.5 Tesla were as follows: echo time (TE) of 1.8 milliseconds (ms), repetition time (TR) of 3.7 ms, number of excitations of 2, field of view (FOV) of 390 × 312 mm, matrix size of 256 × 240, reconstruction pixel size of 1.52 × 1.21 mm, slice thickness of 8 mm, and a flip angle of 70 degrees [30]. LGE images were acquired 10 min after administering gadolinium at a dose of 0.1 mmol/kg and a rate of 4 mL/s, using the 3D segmented gradient-echo inversion-recovery sequence. These images were obtained in multiple short-axis slices, as well as in long-axis, 2-chamber, and 4-chamber views, similar to the functional images. Image parameters at 1.5 Tesla included a TE of 1.25 ms, TR of 4.1 ms, a flip angle of 15 degrees, a FOV of 303 × 384 mm, matrix size of 240 × 256, in-plane resolution of 1.26 × 1.5 mm, slice thickness of 8 mm, and a sensitivity encoding factor of 1.5 [30]. In patients for whom vasodilatory stress perfusion tests were performed before LGE imaging, the CMR protocol followed standard procedures described elsewhere [31,32]. Quantitative measurements of LV volume, and LVEF were obtained from the stack of short-axis SSFP cine images. LGE images were evaluated using visual assessment and were deemed present only when confirmed in both the short-axis view and at least one other orthogonal plane [30,33].

LVT detected by CMR on cine sequences were defined as filling defects within the LV cavity, typically adherent to regions of abnormal wall motion then confirmed by LGE technique [34]. In LGE images, thrombus was diagnosed as an LV mass with post-contrast inversion-recovery characteristics consistent with avascular tissue. Moreover, LGE-CMR can be further assessed for thrombus identification by increasing the inversion time to selectively null avascular tissue such as thrombus. This ‘long inversion time’ approach provides an image that renders black thrombus and surrounding bright myocardium.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. The prevalence of LVT was reported as numbers and percentages. Comparisons between patients with and without LVT were performed using the chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test, when appropriate) for categorical variables and the Student’s t-test (or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, when appropriate) for continuous variables. The normality of variable distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, median (interquartile range [IQR]), or number (%), as appropriate. Factors associated with LVT were assessed using univariable logistic regression analysis. Variables with a p value <0.05 in the univariable analysis were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model using backward selection to identify independent factors associated with LVT. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Clinical and imaging variables found to be independently associated with LVT were used to develop a risk score for predicting LVT. Risk score was generated by summing assigned points, defined as the exponential of the estimated coefficients and rounded to the nearest integer. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the scores and to determine optimal cutoff values for predicting LVT. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated. A validation cohort was used to assess the performance of the developed risk scores. For clinical outcomes, time-to-event analyses for MACE were performed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and compared with the log-rank test. Univariable Cox regression analysis was initially performed, and variables with a p value <0.05 were entered into the multivariable Cox regression model using backward selection to identify independent factors associated with MACE. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using log-minus-log survival plots. Multicollinearity in the final model was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF) testing, with a VIF >5 considered indicative of significant multicollinearity. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value <0.05.

Results

Study population

A total of 863 patients with ICM referred for CMR were screened. Of these, 3 had severe primary valvular disease, 57 had specific non-ischemic cardiomyopathy, 6 had acute coronary syndrome within 40 days before CMR, 2 had congenital heart disease, 3 had a prior diagnosis of LVT on CMR, and 2 had incomplete CMR parameters; these patients were therefore excluded. A total of 790 participants were included in the analysis of factors associated with LVT. Indications for CMR included known CAD with suspected myocardial ischemia (48.5%), suspected CAD in patients without a prior diagnosis (16.5%), heart failure evaluation or worsening LV systolic function (16.5%), viability assessment before revascularization (13.2%), preoperative evaluation for non-cardiac surgery (3.8%), and other indications (1.5%). Follow-up information was unavailable for 168 participants; consequently, 622 participants were included in the analysis of clinical outcomes. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of patients with ICM with and without LVT on baseline CMR. The mean age was 66.9 ± 11.3 years, and 595 patients (75.3%) were men. LVT was detected in 116 patients (14.7%). Patients with LVT had lower systolic blood pressure (SBP) (129.2 ± 19.7 vs. 134.2 ± 23.0 mmHg; p = 0.01), a higher prevalence of Q waves on ECG (80.0% vs. 67.2%; p = 0.01), higher use of oral anticoagulants (25.0% vs. 13.2%; p = 0.001), and lower echocardiographic LVEF (30.3 ± 10.5% vs. 33.6 ± 12.0%; p = 0.03). For patients with a history of MI prior to CMR, the median duration from the most recent MI to CMR was 15.6 months (IQR, 4.1–75.3). Patients with LVT appeared to have a shorter duration from MI to CMR than those without LVT, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (with LVT: 6.4 months [IQR, 4.6–87.4] vs without LVT: 18.8 months [IQR, 4.7–77.3], p = 0.06).

Binary logistic regression analysis for factors associated with LVT

Univariable analysis showed that SBP (OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.98–0.99; p = 0.02), Q waves on ECG (OR 1.95, 95% CI 1.18–3.24; p = 0.009), echocardiographic LVEF (OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.95–0.99; p = 0.03), echocardiographic LV apical aneurysm (OR 2.42, 95% CI 1.37–4.26; p = 0.002), CMR LVESV index (OR 1.005, 95% CI 1.00–1.009; p = 0.03), CMR LVEF (OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.95–0.99; p = 0.003), CMR LV apical aneurysm (OR 3.86, 95% CI 2.55–5.86; p < 0.001), apical LGE (OR 7.66, 95% CI 4.04–14.51; p < 0.001), and number of LGE segments (OR 1.15, 95% CI 1.09–1.21; p < 0.001) were associated with LVT. Multivariable analysis showed that echocardiographic LV apical aneurysm (OR 2.01, 95% CI 1.06–3.79; p = 0.03), CMR LV apical aneurysm (OR 2.30, 95% CI 1.22–4.30; p = 0.009), LV apical LGE (OR 2.88, 95% CI 1.14–7.26; p = 0.02), and number of LGE segments (OR 1.10, 95% CI 1.01–1.20; p = 0.02) were independently associated with LVT.

As the number of LGE segments was significantly higher in the LVT group, ROC analysis was performed to determine the optimal number of LGE segments for predicting LVT. An optimal cutoff value of >7 LGE segments showed a sensitivity of 0.71 and a specificity of 0.56 for the detection of LVT, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.65.

Development and validation of a risk score to predict LVT

The CMR-based risk score, termed the CMR-LVT Score, was developed and validated. Point values were derived from the multivariable logistic regression model, with weighted points assigned based on the exponential of the estimated β coefficients and rounded to the nearest integer, resulting in 3 points for CMR LV apical aneurysm, 3 points for LV apical LGE, and 2 points for LGE involving >7 segments, as shown in Supplementary Table 1. The score was calculated as follows: CMR−LVT Score = (3 × apical aneurysm on CMR) + (3 × apical LGE) + (2 × LGE >7 segments)

A cutoff value of ≥5 points was used to predict the presence of LVT on CMR, with an AUC of 0.73 (95% CI, 0.68–0.77; p < 0.001). The sensitivity was 0.87 (95% CI, 0.80–0.92), specificity 0.52 (95% CI, 0.48–0.56), PPV 0.24 (95% CI, 0.20–0.28), and NPV 0.96 (95% CI, 0.93–0.98).

In the validation cohort (n = 95), the CMR-LVT score demonstrated AUCs of 0.79 (95% CI, 0.69–0.90; p < 0.001). The sensitivity was 0.90 (95% CI, 0.69–0.97), specificity 0.49 (95% CI, 0.38–0.60), PPV 0.30 (95% CI, 0.19–0.43), and NPV 0.95 (95% CI, 0.83–0.99). Patient outcomes

During a median follow-up of 3.2 years (IQR, 1.6–5.5), a total of 150 MACE occurred: 29 events (36.7%) in the baseline LVT group and 121 events (22.3%) in the group without LVT. The proportional hazards assumption was adequately satisfied based on visual inspection of the log-minus-log survival plots. Patients with LVT at baseline had a higher rate of MACE compared with those without LVT (univariable analysis: HR 1.81, 95% CI 1.20–2.71; p = 0.004). Table 3 demonstrates the results of the univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses of factors associated with MACE. Multivariable Cox regression analysis demonstrated that factors independently associated with MACE included age (adjusted HR 1.02, 95% CI 1.002–1.04; p = 0.03), congestive heart failure (adjusted HR 2.13, 95% CI 1.31–3.46; p = 0.002), CMR LVEF (adjusted HR 0.97, 95% CI 0.95–0.99; p = 0.01), and presence of LVT at baseline CMR (adjusted HR 2.38, 95% CI 1.31–4.30; p = 0.004). Discussion

In this study of patients with chronic ICM undergoing CMR, we observed three main findings. First, independent factors associated with LVT included LV apical aneurysm, apical LGE, and greater LGE extent. Second, the CMR-LVT Score, incorporating these parameters, demonstrated acceptable discriminative performance for predicting LVT and was validated. Third, the presence of baseline LVT was independently associated with MACE. These findings underscore the potential clinical value of CMR for risk stratification and prognostic assessment in patients with chronic ICM, particularly for the detection of LVT.

Previous studies have predominantly focused on patients in the early phase following acute MI, in whom the prevalence of LVT varies widely depending on infarct size, location, and imaging modality [35–37]. The prevalence of LVT observed in our study among patients with chronic ICM was 14.7%, which is comparable to that reported in acute MI populations, highlighting that LVT remains a clinically important complication even beyond the acute MI setting [4,36]. Our findings suggest that LVT remains relatively common even in stable or chronic disease states, likely reflecting persistent adverse ventricular remodeling, regional wall motion abnormalities, and myocardial scar formation.

In our study, patients with LVT generally had more advanced structural and functional LV abnormalities, including lower LVEF and higher LVESV index, compared with those without LVT. Higher oral anticoagulant use was observed in patients with LVT at the time of CMR. This finding may reflect underlying comorbidities requiring anticoagulation, such as atrial fibrillation/flutter or prior SE. In addition, some patients may have undergone echocardiography prior to CMR that suggested possible LVT, leading to initiation of anticoagulation before CMR confirmation. Therefore, this association should be interpreted cautiously and does not necessarily imply a causal relationship.

Our findings are consistent with prior studies indicating that apical aneurysm, apical LGE, and extensive myocardial scarring are independently associated with LVT and are important determinants of LVT [9,14–18]. LV apical aneurysm was strongly associated with LVT, likely reflecting severe regional wall motion abnormalities and blood stasis within akinetic or dyskinetic apical segments that predispose to thrombus formation [37]. In addition, apical LGE showed the strongest association with LVT and likely reflects extensive apical myocardial scar and endocardial injury, which may promote local inflammation, impaired contractility, and thrombogenicity [37]. These findings are consistent with Virchow’s triad and support the importance of myocardial scar characterization by CMR in identifying patients at higher risk for LVT.

Clinical parameters such as low SBP and the presence of Q waves on ECG were associated with LVT in the univariable analysis but not in the multivariable analysis. Several studies have linked the hemodynamic compromise associated with low SBP to LVT formation [38]. However, after analysis including CMR parameters that directly assess myocardial function and tissue characterization, these clinical variables were no longer independently associated with LVT. In particular, Q waves on ECG may reflect underlying myocardial scar burden and therefore may show collinearity with CMR scar-related variables such as LGE extent and apical LGE.

Transthoracic echocardiography is a fundamental imaging modality for the detection of LVT. However, it has several limitations, including suboptimal image quality, particularly in cases of small or mural LVT. Contrast echocardiography improves the detection of LVT but may be associated with an increased risk of kidney injury and is unavailable in many settings, including Thailand. LGE-CMR has been shown to be the most accurate imaging modality for the detection of LVT [3,10–13].

We developed and validated the CMR-LVT Score, a CMR-based risk score comprising LV apical aneurysm, apical LGE, and extensive LGE burden (>7 segments). The score demonstrated acceptable discriminative performance for predicting LVT in both the derivation and validation cohorts. Because the score incorporates CMR parameters directly related to myocardial scar burden and adverse ventricular remodeling, it may help identify patients at higher risk for LVT who may benefit from closer follow-up and enhanced surveillance for thrombus development. Notably, the CMR-LVT Score demonstrated an excellent negative predictive value, suggesting potential utility for identifying patients at low risk for LVT. Further studies are needed to evaluate the clinical utility of this score and its role in guiding follow-up strategies and anticoagulation management.

Patients with LVT on baseline CMR experienced a higher rate of MACE compared with those without LVT. This increased risk may be related to more severe LV dysfunction, greater scar burden, or a higher incidence of stroke or systemic embolism attributable to LVT itself [39–41]. We further performed multivariable analysis and found that LVT was independently associated with MACE, confirming its prognostic significance. These findings are consistent with previous studies [42,43].

This study has several limitations. First, the study population included patients referred for CMR for various clinical indications, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to all patients with ICM. Second, although the overall cohort was relatively large, the number of events remained limited for some analyses, particularly subgroup analyses, and the proportion of female patients was relatively low. Third, the follow-up duration may have been insufficient to capture additional long-term MACE. Fourth, the multivariable Cox model treated baseline LVT as a time-fixed variable; however, LVT may develop or resolve during follow-up, and changes in anticoagulation therapy during follow-up were not systematically assessed, which may have affected the estimated hazard ratios. In addition, 168 patients were excluded from the outcome analysis because of missing follow-up data, which may have introduced attrition bias.

Conclusions

In patients with chronic ICM undergoing CMR, LV apical aneurysm, apical LGE, and greater LGE extent were independently associated with LVT. The CMR-LVT Score demonstrated acceptable discriminative performance for identifying higher-risk patients who may benefit from more intensive follow-up and showed a high negative predictive value. In addition, baseline LVT was independently associated with MACE. These findings highlight the potential role of CMR tissue characterization and ventricular remodeling assessment for risk stratification and prognostic evaluation in patients with chronic ICM.
