Dexmedetomidine–remifentanil-assisted awake videolaryngoscopic intubation in a patient with Madelung disease and compensated liver cirrhosis undergoing prone lumbar spine surgery: a case report

Background

Madelung disease, also referred to as multiple symmetric lipomatosis or Launois-Bensáude syndrome, is a rare disorder of adipose tissue metabolism characterized by the accumulation of benign, non-encapsulated lipomatous tissue in the cervical, nuchal, and parapharyngeal regions [1]. The disease predominantly affects middle-aged men and has been associated with chronic alcohol consumption, mitochondrial dysfunction, and dysregulation of adrenergic-mediated lipolysis [2]. Although the deposited tissue is histologically benign, its progressive accumulation in the upper neck and pharynx can critically compromise the airway by distorting laryngopharyngeal anatomy, restricting cervical mobility, and reducing the functional cross-sectional area of the laryngeal inlet [1].

Patients with Madelung disease may present with dysphagia, obstructive sleep apnea, restricted neck extension, and difficult mask ventilation, all of which substantially increase the risk of a cannot-intubate-cannot-oxygenate scenario if general anesthesia is induced before the airway is secured [3]. Awake tracheal intubation (ATI) is therefore recommended when difficult airway features are anticipated, as it preserves spontaneous ventilation and allows continuous monitoring of the airway throughout the intubation process [4].

ATI via fiberoptic bronchoscopy (FOB) has traditionally been regarded as the technique of choice in patients with anatomically distorted airways, and most published cases of airway management in Madelung disease have employed this approach [5, 6]. However, FOB requires considerable operator expertise and dedicated equipment, and its utility may be limited by secretions, mucosal bleeding, or poor patient cooperation [7, 8]. In contrast, videolaryngoscopy (VL) provides direct visualization of the supraglottic structures with the additional mechanical advantage of displacing redundant perilaryngeal soft tissue via the rigid blade, which may be particularly beneficial when airway crowding is caused by lipomatous infiltration rather than an intrinsic glottic lesion [7, 8]. Recent meta-analyses and international guidelines have recognized VL as an evidence-based alternative to FOB for ATI in selected patients [7–10].

We report a case of successful dexmedetomidine–remifentanil-assisted awake videolaryngoscopic intubation in a patient with Madelung disease and compensated cirrhosis undergoing prone lumbar spine surgery. To our knowledge, this is one of the first cases to describe awake VL combined with pharmacologic sedation in this rare condition, and we discuss the rationale for technique selection, sedation strategy, and practical considerations relevant to this setting.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old man (height 165 cm, weight 78 kg, body mass index 28.6 kg/m²) with an established diagnosis of Madelung disease presented for posterior lumbar decompression and fusion at the L4–5 level for progressive right lower extremity weakness of five months’ duration. His medical history included compensated liver cirrhosis (Child-Pugh class A, Model for End-stage Liver Disease [MELD] score 8), hypertension, and benign prostatic hyperplasia. No family history of lipomatous disorders or neuromuscular disease was identified. The clinical timeline of this episode of care is summarized in Table 1. Preoperative laboratory investigations confirmed preserved synthetic hepatic function (international normalized ratio [INR] 1.03, activated partial thromboplastin time [aPTT] 26.2 s, serum albumin 3.8 g/dL, total bilirubin 1.1 mg/dL).

The patient had been under regular otolaryngological surveillance for Madelung disease. Cervical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed six months previously demonstrated extensive non-encapsulated lipomatous infiltration of the posterior cervical, submental, and bilateral parapharyngeal regions, with circumferential reduction of the hypopharyngeal lumen and posterior displacement of the larynx (Fig. 1). Although the MRI had been performed six months prior to surgery, no clinical signs of disease progression were observed during this interval and current airway status was confirmed by preoperative flexible nasopharyngolaryngoscopy performed immediately before the procedure. On physical examination, prominent symmetric adipose tissue deposition was evident in the submental and posterior cervical regions, with markedly restricted neck extension. The patient reported snoring but denied witnessed apnea, excessive daytime sleepiness, exertional dyspnea, or stridor at rest. Standard bedside airway assessment revealed adequate mouth opening (inter-incisor distance 4.5 cm) and a Mallampati class I oropharyngeal view. The STOP-BANG score was 5 (S1, T0, O0, P1, B0, A1, N1, G1), indicating high risk for obstructive sleep apnea [11]. However, preoperative flexible nasopharyngolaryngoscopy performed by the otolaryngologist confirmed significant supraglottic crowding caused by lipomatous infiltration of the aryepiglottic folds and lateral pharyngeal walls, with partial obscuration of the epiglottic inlet on quiet breathing and near-complete obstruction on forced inspiration (Fig. 2). No intrinsic vocal cord pathology, subglottic stenosis, or tracheal deviation was identified. Based on these findings, a predicted difficult laryngoscopy with a high risk of airway loss after induction was formally documented.

In view of the distorted supraglottic anatomy and the concurrent hepatic dysfunction, an ATI strategy was planned. Therefore, in the presence of anticipated difficult airway anatomy, pre-induction airway securing was considered the safest strategy [4]. FOB was designated as the rescue technique and a surgical airway kit was available throughout the procedure.

On the day of surgery, standard monitoring was established including electrocardiography, non-invasive blood pressure measurement, pulse oximetry, and continuous cortical activity monitoring using the SedLine® Patient State Index (PSI; Masimo, Irvine, CA, USA). After five minutes of preoxygenation to an end-tidal oxygen fraction exceeding 0.80, high-flow nasal cannula oxygen at 15 L/min was maintained throughout the awake phase to provide apnoeic oxygenation [12].

Multimodal topical airway anesthesia was performed using 10% lidocaine spray (total dose approximately 40 mg, well within the maximum recommended dose of 4 mg/kg) applied to the oropharynx, tongue base, and supraglottic structures [4]. Sedation was initiated with a dexmedetomidine loading infusion of 1.0 µg/kg over 10 min, followed by a maintenance infusion of 0.5 µg/kg/h [13], combined with remifentanil at 0.05 µg/kg/min, titrated to effect. Both agents were titrated over approximately 10 min to achieve cooperative sedation, defined as PSI values of 70–85 with maintained verbal responsiveness and stable spontaneous ventilation confirmed by continuous waveform capnography (Fig. 3). No episodes of apnea, oxygen desaturation below 95%, or airway obstruction occurred during the titration phase. Minor transient bradycardia (lowest heart rate 52 beats/min) was noted during the dexmedetomidine loading phase but resolved spontaneously without requiring intervention, and no hypotension was observed.

SedLine® brain function monitor display obtained during awake sedation prior to tracheal intubation, showing a Patient State Index (PSI) of 77, within the target range for cooperative sedation (70–85). Electromyographic activity (EMG) is 0%

After confirming cooperative sedation and adequate topical anesthesia, the McGrath™ MAC videolaryngoscope (size 3 blade; Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was introduced orally. Advancement of the blade to the vallecula mechanically displaced the base of tongue and the redundant perilaryngeal lipomatous tissue anteriorly, revealing a Cormack-Lehane grade I glottic view. A 7.0-mm internal diameter reinforced endotracheal tube (ETT) was advanced over a stylet on the first attempt without coughing, gagging, or laryngospasm. Correct tracheal placement was confirmed by capnography waveform and bilateral auscultation (Fig. 4). The tube was secured at 23 cm at the lips.

Videolaryngoscopic view obtained using a McGrath™ MAC videolaryngoscope (size 3 blade) during awake orotracheal intubation, demonstrating a Cormack-Lehane grade I glottic view. The endotracheal tube is seen passing through the glottic inlet

Following successful airway securing, general anesthesia was induced with propofol 80 mg (approximately 1.0 mg/kg, dose-reduced in view of hepatic impairment) and rocuronium 50 mg (0.64 mg/kg). Rocuronium was selected for its predictable neuromuscular blockade and reversibility with sugammadex, the efficacy of which is independent of hepatic metabolism [14]. Although atracurium undergoes Hofmann elimination and ester hydrolysis independently of hepatic or renal function and might theoretically be preferred in this setting, it was not available at our institution at the time of surgery; rocuronium with sugammadex reversal was therefore the only viable option for organ-independent neuromuscular management with confirmed complete reversal before extubation [14]. Anesthesia was maintained with desflurane and a continuous remifentanil infusion. A right radial arterial catheter was placed following successful intubation for continuous blood pressure monitoring. The patient was then repositioned to the prone position, and correct ETT position was reconfirmed by auscultation after repositioning.

The intraoperative course was uneventful. Quantitative train-of-four (TOF) monitoring was initiated immediately after rocuronium administration and was maintained continuously throughout surgery. At the conclusion of surgery (operative duration 4.5 h), TOF monitoring demonstrated two twitches, prompting the administration of sugammadex 200 mg (2.56 mg/kg). Because the TOF ratio failed to reach 0.9 within three minutes of initial reversal, an additional 100 mg (1.28 mg/kg) of sugammadex was administered, after which complete neuromuscular recovery was confirmed. The delayed neuromuscular recovery was primarily attributed to mild intraoperative hypothermia (tympanic temperature 35.6 °C) and the prolonged operative duration (4.5 h), both of which are well-recognized causes of impaired rocuronium clearance [14, 15]. The contribution of hepatic cirrhosis per se is likely to have been minimal, given that rocuronium pharmacokinetics are not substantially altered in Child-Pugh class A disease [14].

Following confirmation of a TOF ratio ≥ 0.9 and adequate clinical recovery, tracheal extubation was performed with the patient fully awake, defined as spontaneous eye opening with the ability to follow verbal commands. Equipment for immediate reintubation, including the McGrath™ MAC videolaryngoscope and FOB, was available at the bedside throughout, in accordance with Difficult Airway Society (DAS) extubation guidelines [16]. Post-extubation vital signs were stable (blood pressure 133/72 mmHg, heart rate 92 beats/min, SpO₂ 100% on 2 L/min supplemental oxygen). The patient was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU), stepped down to the general ward on postoperative day 1, and discharged home on postoperative day 16 without airway-related sequelae.

Discussion

This case illustrates a structured approach to awake airway management in a patient presenting two concurrent challenges: distorted supraglottic anatomy due to Madelung disease and hepatic dysfunction requiring pharmacologic modification of sedation and neuromuscular management. The successful outcome depended on three interdependent elements: systematic preoperative airway characterization, rational selection of VL as the primary intubation technique, and pharmacologically appropriate sedation that preserved spontaneous ventilation throughout.

The key decision in this case was selecting VL over FOB. This was based on the preoperative finding that the predominant mechanism of airway compromise was extrinsic compression of the supraglottic structures by perilaryngeal lipomatous tissue, without intrinsic glottic or subglottic pathology. As demonstrated in the present case (Fig. 4) and consistent with prior reports of awake VL in Madelung disease [17, 18], advancement of the rigid blade to the vallecula resulted in anterior displacement of the redundant perilaryngeal lipomatous tissue, thereby exposing a Cormack-Lehane grade I glottic view. This mechanical advantage is not replicated by a flexible fiberoptic scope, which lacks the rigid structure necessary to displace compressing soft-tissue masses and may be susceptible to deviation around, rather than through, the obstructing tissue [7]. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis [8] demonstrated that VL for awake intubation achieves equivalent first-attempt success rates compared with FOB, while significantly reducing intubation time [8]. Current guidelines from both the DAS and the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) recognize VL as an acceptable primary technique for ATI when performed by a trained clinician with fiberoptic backup available [9, 10].

The selection of dexmedetomidine and remifentanil was driven by specific pharmacokinetic requirements in this patient. While either agent alone might theoretically have provided satisfactory sedation, the combination was selected for complementary reasons: dexmedetomidine alone does not reliably suppress laryngeal and cough reflexes sufficiently for comfortable laryngoscopy in patients with significant supraglottic crowding, whereas remifentanil at a deliberately low dose (0.05 µg/kg/min) provided targeted attenuation of airway reflexes without inducing respiratory depression, a threshold well below that typically associated with apnea (≥ 0.1 µg/kg/min) [19]. Dexmedetomidine undergoes extensive hepatic glucuronidation; however, pharmacokinetic data indicate that clearance is meaningfully reduced only in moderate-to-severe hepatic impairment [20]. In Child-Pugh class A, the degree of hepatic dysfunction is sufficiently limited that the pharmacokinetic impact on dexmedetomidine clearance is expected to be clinically negligible, and standard doses with careful titration to clinical effect were therefore considered appropriate [20]. Dexmedetomidine provides cooperative sedation with preserved airway reflexes and spontaneous ventilation, making it a well-suited agent for ATI sedation [13]. Remifentanil was selected as the opioid adjunct because it is metabolized by non-specific plasma and tissue esterases independently of hepatic function, rendering its pharmacokinetics unaffected by liver disease [19]. The pharmacokinetic profile of remifentanil in patients with severe hepatic dysfunction is equivalent to that in healthy subjects, supporting its use as the preferred opioid in this setting [19]. In view of the concurrent hepatic dysfunction and the requirement to preserve spontaneous ventilation throughout the awake intubation phase, remifentanil was intentionally administered at a reduced infusion rate of 0.05 µg/kg/min to minimize the risk of dose-dependent respiratory depression while still providing adequate attenuation of airway reflexes [19]. In this patient, the combination of these two agents provided stable cooperative sedation without any episode of hypoventilation or airway obstruction throughout the awake intubation phase.

The requirement for supplemental sugammadex to achieve complete neuromuscular recovery is most likely attributable to the prolonged operative duration (4.5 h) and mild intraoperative hypothermia (35.6 °C), both of which are well-recognized causes of delayed rocuronium clearance and prolonged neuromuscular blockade [14, 15]. The contribution of hepatic cirrhosis per se was likely minimal in this case; although rocuronium undergoes partial biliary excretion and may exhibit an altered volume of distribution in cirrhotic patients, these pharmacokinetic changes are not clinically significant in Child-Pugh class A disease [14]. This observation underscores the importance of initiating quantitative neuromuscular monitoring immediately after neuromuscular blocking agent administration and maintaining it continuously throughout surgery in patients with hepatic dysfunction, to guide reversal dosing and confirm complete recovery (TOF ratio ≥ 0.9) before extubation [14, 16].

Taken together, these pharmacologic adaptations reflect the compounded complexity of this case. Several limitations of this report merit acknowledgement. First, as a single case report, the findings cannot be generalized, and the optimal sedation regimen for awake VL in Madelung disease remains to be established through larger observational studies. Second, a direct comparison between VL and FOB was not performed in this patient; the superiority of VL over FOB in this specific anatomical context therefore remains inferential rather than demonstrated. Third, the degree of hepatic dysfunction in this patient was mild (Child-Pugh class A), and the pharmacokinetic considerations described here may not apply to patients with more advanced cirrhosis. Finally, the prolonged hospital stay (postoperative day 16) was attributable to the underlying spinal pathology and not to airway-related complications; however, detailed postoperative neurological data were not systematically collected and are not reported.

Conclusions

Awake VL combined with dexmedetomidine–remifentanil sedation and topical anesthesia is a feasible and safe approach for airway management in Madelung disease when supraglottic soft-tissue crowding is the predominant mechanism of airway compromise. The rigid VL blade provides a mechanical advantage in displacing compressing perilaryngeal lipomatous tissue that is not achievable with a flexible fiberoptic scope. FOB should remain immediately available as a rescue technique. Remifentanil is the preferred opioid adjunct in patients with hepatic dysfunction owing to its hepatic-independent elimination. Quantitative neuromuscular monitoring with confirmation of complete reversal of the neuromuscular blocking agent is essential before awake extubation in patients with anticipated difficult reintubation.
