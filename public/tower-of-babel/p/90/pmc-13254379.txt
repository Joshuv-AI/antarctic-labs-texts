The functional role of opioidergic system within the ventral tegmental area and the dentate gyrus of hippocampus in modulation of the persistent inflammatory pain in the rats
Authors: Mohammad Saghafi, Ronak Azizbeigi, Abbas Haghparast
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ventral tegmental area (VTA) and hippocampus are implicated in various brain functions, including pain modulation. However, the precise involvement of their opioidergic system in this process has not yet been fully elucidated. The current study investigated the role of opioid receptors in the VTA and dentate gyrus (DG) region of the hippocampus on inflammatory pain-related behaviors. One hundred fifty-three adult albino Wistar rats (200–250 g) underwent stereotaxic surgery for unilateral cannula implantation into either the VTA or DG. Following recovery, the formalin test was used to evaluate inflammatory pain after drug or vehicle microinjections into the VTA or DG. In the first set of experiments, animals received intra-VTA or intra-DG morphine, a μ-opioid receptor (MOR) agonist, at different doses, and pain responses were measured. In the second set, various doses of naloxone, a non-selective opioid receptor antagonist, were administered prior to the effective dose of morphine identified in the first set. Results indicated that morphine reduced nociceptive responses in both phases of the formalin test, with a lower effective dose required in the VTA (10 mmol in the VTA; 50 mmol in the DG). In contrast, naloxone pretreatment attenuated morphine-induced analgesia, with lower doses observed in the DG. In summary, these findings underscore region-specific differences in MOR-mediated analgesia between the VTA and DG, suggesting that distinct cellular and neural circuit mechanisms may contribute to opioid-induced modulation of inflammatory pain.

Introduction

Pain is described as an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience linked to actual or possible tissue damage (Raja et al., 2020) and serves as a protective mechanism against potentially harmful stimuli (Cook et al., 2018). Inflammatory pain, which arises in response to infection or tissue injury, is mediated by a cascade of multiple pro-inflammatory mediators, including prostaglandins, chemokines, cytokines, and neuropeptides. These mediators sensitize peripheral nociceptive neurons by lowering their excitability threshold and contribute to the development of pathological pain states such as allodynia and hyperalgesia (Muley et al., 2016). Beyond peripheral mechanisms, several supraspinal structures integrate, process, and mediate inflammatory pain signals, including the hypothalamus (Zakeri et al., 2020), amygdala (Zussy et al., 2018), periaqueductal gray (PAG) (Blanton et al., 2022), ventral tegmental area (VTA) (Li et al., 2025), nucleus accumbens (NAc) (Abolghasemi et al., 2025), and hippocampus (Baghani et al., 2024).

The VTA is a crucial region in the midbrain, traditionally recognized for its role in reward processing and addiction (Bouarab et al., 2019, Polter and Kauer, 2014). However, evidence indicates it also contributes to pain modulation (Saghafi et al., 2025, Mazaheri et al., 2023). Direct stimulation of the VTA produced antinociceptive responses in a model of inflammatory pain (Li et al., 2016), and its neurons are responsive to noxious stimuli (Brischoux et al., 2009). The VTA consists primarily of 60–65% dopaminergic neurons, 30–35% GABAergic neurons, and 2–3% glutamatergic neurons (Nair-Roberts et al., 2008) and receive afferent inputs from multiple brain regions, including the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), NAc, rostromedial tegmental nucleus (RMTg), pedunculopontine tegmentum, as well as local neurons (Morales and Margolis, 2017). Notably, opioid receptors, particularly μ-opioid receptors (MORs), are expressed in VTA neurons (Devine et al., 1993). Opioids promote dopamine release from the VTA to target regions, such as the NAc, by inhibiting GABAergic interneurons after receptor binding (Johnson and North, 1992). A microdialysis study showed that morphine injection into the VTA elevates dopamine and its metabolites in the NAc by 50–150% (Leone et al., 1991). However, inflammatory pain has been shown to impair the functional activity of MORs, leading to dose-dependent modifications in dopamine release in the NAc and an increased intake of high doses of heroin in rats, suggesting MOR desensitization within the VTA (Hipólito et al., 2015).

The hippocampus is a critical brain structure primarily involved in learning, memory, anxiety, and depression (Barkus et al., 2010). It receives dopaminergic projections from the VTA, as evidence indicates that it contributes to addictive behaviors by storing drug-cue associations (Kutlu and Gould, 2016). Beyond its role in cognition and addiction, the hippocampus is also involved in pain modulation. For instance, according to a functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) study, subcutaneous formalin injection into the hind paw of animals increased blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) signals across cortical and subcortical brain structures, including the hippocampus (Shih et al., 2008). Moreover, multiple studies highlight that hippocampal subregions, including the dentate gyrus (DG), CA1, and CA3, are also implicated in pain processing (Baghani et al., 2024, Danesh et al., 2024, Zhu et al., 2017). The DG, the primary gateway of the hippocampal trisynaptic circuit, receives afferent projections from the entorhinal cortex, and sends excitatory projections to CA3 (Borzello et al., 2023). It is particularly sensitive to modulation by stress and aversive stimuli (Campeau et al., 2010, Fa et al., 2014).

The hippocampus contains a heterogeneous distribution of opioid receptor subtypes, whose activity is regulated by the endogenous peptides such as enkephalin and dynorphin. This suggests that both opiates and opioid peptides influence hippocampal function in the context of addiction and pain control (Simmons and Chavkin, 1996). Morphine administration increases neuronal activity within the DG, both in vitro and in vivo (Linseman, 1982), while blockade of MORs with the selective antagonist funaltrexamine decreases long-term potentiation (LTP). Consistently, MOR knockout animals exhibited an inability to form LTP in the DG (Matthies et al., 2000). Moreover, persistent nociception has been reported to enhance LTP triggered by theta burst stimulation (TBS) within the DG and CA1 regions of rat hippocampal slices (Zhaon et al., 2009). Despite these insights, the role of DG MORs in inflammatory pain remains insufficiently understood.

The VTA and DG of the hippocampus are functionally and circuit-level distinct brain regions involved in pain modulation. The VTA, as part of the mesolimbic dopamine system, contributes to the processing of both aversive and rewarding components of pain. Dopamine release induced by nociceptive stimuli acts as a salience cue that shapes context-dependent motivational responses (Taylor et al., 2016). On the other hand, the hippocampus, including the DG, plays an essential role in the consolidation of pain-related memories, and its activation has been associated with the recall of painful memories (Berger et al., 2018). These differences may shape region-specific MOR–mediated analgesic responses.

Nevertheless, the precise role of MORs in the VTA and DG during inflammatory pain remains poorly understood. Therefore, the present study aims to evaluate the contribution of MORs within the VTA and DG to modulation of the formalin-induced inflammatory pain.

Materials and methods

Animals

One hundred fifty-three adult male albino Wistar rats, weighing 200–250 g, were purchased from the Pasteur Institute (Tehran, Iran) and housed three per cage under standard laboratory conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, 23 ± 2 °C, and 37–42% relative humidity) with ad libitum access to food and water. All experimental procedures were conducted during the light phase (7:00 AM to 7:00 PM). To minimize stress, animals were handled daily and habituated to the laboratory environment for one week before initiating the experiments. The protocol of this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (IR.SBMU.PHNS.REC.1403.060), Tehran, Iran. Additionally, all experiments were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication; 8th edition, revised 2011).

Cannula implementation

Animals were deeply anesthetized with an intraperitoneal mixture of ketamine hydrochloride (100 mg/kg) and xylazine hydrochloride (10 mg/kg). After hair trichotomy, they were positioned in a stereotaxic frame (Stoelting, USA). To reduce bleeding, 0.2 ML of Lidocaine with epinephrine was administered around the surgical site. Subsequently, using a scalpel, a midline incision (1.5–2 cm) was made, and the muscles and connective tissues were carefully retracted. The skull surface was then disinfected with 10% iodopovidone and dried to reveal the bregma and lambda. Stereotaxic coordinates for the VTA and DG were identified using the Paxinos and Watson atlas (The Rat Brain in Stereotaxic Coordinates). A 23-gauge stainless-steel guide cannula was then unilaterally implanted 1 mm above either the VTA (anteroposterior (AP) = 4.8 mm from bregma, mediolateral (ML) = 0.8 mm from the midline, and dorsoventral (DV) = 8.3 mm from the skull surface) or DG ((AP) = − 3.7 ± 0.15 mm, ML = ±1.7 mm, and (DV) = − 3.6 ± 0.2 mm) (Paxinos and Watson, 2007). The cannula was anchored to the skull using a stainless-steel screw and dental acrylic cement. During non-experimental periods, a dummy cannula was inserted into the guide cannula to prevent obstruction, which was removed on injection days. Following surgery, rats were transferred to the animal room, housed separately, and allowed to recover for 5–7 days before behavioral testing.

Drugs and microinjection

In this study, these pharmacological agents were used: Morphine sulfate, a potent MOR agonist (Temad, Tehran, Iran), and naloxone hydrochloride, a non-selective opioid receptor antagonist (Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, UK). Both drugs were freshly dissolved in 0.9% saline (pH 6.8) on the day of the experiment. Additionally, a 2.5% formalin solution was prepared from a 37% stock formaldehyde solution (Merck, Germany) to induce inflammatory pain.

Microinjections into the VTA or DG were performed using a 1 μl Hamilton syringe connected via a polyethylene tube (PE-20) to a 30-gauge injection cannula, which extended 1 mm beyond the guide cannula. Drugs or the vehicle were administered manually at a slow rate over 1 min, and the injection cannula was kept in place for an additional 1 min to facilitate drug diffusion and prevent backflow. Injection volumes of 0.3 μl for VTA and 0.5 μl for DG were selected based on previous studies (Baghani et al., 2024, Saghafi et al., 2025) and anatomical considerations. A smaller volume was used in the VTA to minimize diffusion into adjacent structures, whereas 0.5 μl was used in the DG to ensure adequate drug distribution. The length of the polyethylene tubing allowed free movement of animals within their boxes during injections. Five minutes after drug or vehicle administration, formalin was injected subcutaneously into the dorsal surface of the hind paw to induce inflammatory pain.

Pain assessment

Five minutes after microinjection of drugs (morphine or naloxone) or vehicle (saline) into the VTA or DG, the formalin test was performed to assess nocifensive behaviors. Briefly, rats were gently restrained with a towel, and 50 μl of 2.5% formalin solution was injected subcutaneously into the dorsal surface of the hind paw using a 27-gauge needle. Subsequently, animals were placed individually in a transparent cubic plexiglass chamber (35 cm height × 35 cm width × 35 cm depth) equipped with a mirror positioned at a 45° angle beneath the floor to allow unobstructed observation during the test. Pain assessment was conducted at 5-minute intervals over 60 min and was analyzed into two distinct phases: The early phase (0–5 min) and the late phase (20–50 min). Pain scores were classified as follows: 0: No noticeable change in animal behavior in the injected paw. 1: The animal places only a small portion of its weight on the injected paw. 2: The animal elevates the paw and is unable to place it on the surface. 3: The injected paw is shaken, beaten, or continuously licked. Nociceptive score for each 5-minute interval was calculated based on the following formula:Nociceptive score = (t0 × 0) + (t1 × 1) + (t2 × 2) + (t3 × 3) / t0 + t1 + t2 + t3where t0 to t3 represent the time (in seconds) spent in each behavioral category, multiplied by its corresponding weight. The denominator (t0 + t1 + t2 + t3) equals 300 s for each 5-minute interval. To prevent potential bias, the observer was trained and remained blinded to the experimental treatments throughout the study.

Experimental design

The present study was designed to investigate the effects of morphine on inflammatory pain, determine its effective dose, and evaluate the impact of opioid receptor blockade on morphine-induced analgesia in two brain regions: The VTA and the DG. A pilot study was carried out to establish the optimal dose range of morphine and naloxone for administration in these regions. The impact of intra-VTA or -DG injection of morphine on the inflammatory pain

In the first set of experiments, to determine the effective dose of morphine within the VTA and DG, animals received different doses of morphine into VTA (2.5, 5, 10, or 25 mmol/0.3 μl saline) or DG (5, 10, 25, or 50 mmol/0.5 μl saline) and control groups received equivalent volumes of saline into these regions. Nociceptive behavior was subsequently assessed using the formalin test (Fig. 1A).

The impact of the VTA or DG pretreatment with naloxone on morphine-induced analgesia

In the second set of experiments, to assess the role of opioid receptor blockade in morphine-induced antinociception, separate groups of animals were pretreated with naloxone into VTA (0.5, 1.5, 5, or 15 mmol) or DG (0.5, 1.5, 5, or 15 mmol) prior to the effective dose of morphine, with a total injection volume of 0.3 μl in the VTA and 0.5 μl in the DG. Control groups received saline, followed by the effective dose of morphine. Formalin-induced nociception was then evaluated for 60 min.

Locomotor activity

To assess locomotor activity between control and treatment groups and its potential impact on formalin test outcomes, seven separate groups of animals (intact, sham, vehicle, morphine (25 and 50 mmol), and naloxone (15 mmol)) were tested in an open-field apparatus (60 × 60 × 60 cm³). Animals were individually placed in an open-field arena and allowed to move freely. The cumulative distance traveled by each animal (cm) was recorded over 60 min as an index of locomotor activity using a ceiling-mounted 3CCD camera (Panasonic Inc., Japan) and analyzed with EthoVision video tracking software (Noldus Information Technology, The Netherlands; version 17.5).

Cannula placements verification

For histological verification, following completion of behavioral testing, rats were euthanized by an overdose of CO2 inhalation. Brains were carefully extracted and post-fixed in 10% formalin solution for at least five days. Subsequently, coronal sections (50-μm thickness) were prepared using a rotatory microtome, and cannula tip locations were examined under a stereomicroscope and with reference to the rat brain atlas by Paxinos and Watson (Paxinos and Watson, 2007). Only animals with precise cannula placement were included in the final statistical analysis. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and graph plotting were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 5.0; GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Results were reported as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and in all cases, a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data distribution and variance homogeneity were tested with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. To evaluate the effects of time and treatment on nociceptive scores in saline- and formalin-control groups under formalin-induced inflammatory pain, a repeated-measures two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test was applied. Furthermore, the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated separately for the early phase (0–5 min) and the late phase (20–50 min). Comparisons of AUC values between groups were performed using a one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparison test and the Newman-Keuls post-hoc test.

Impacts of morphine microinjection into the DG on inflammatory pain behavior

Various doses of morphine (5, 10, 25, and 50 mmol/0.5 μl saline) were infused into the DG, and five minutes later, formalin was injected into the hind paws to induce inflammatory pain. One-way ANOVA was calculated to evaluate the AUC values between the morphine-treated groups and the saline control group during both early [F (4,34) = 10.62, P < 0.0001] and late [F (4,34) = 11.43, P < 0.0001] phases of the formalin test. In the early phase, morphine at doses of 25 and 50 mmol significantly reduced AUC values compared to control. In the late phase, significant reductions were observed at 10, 25, and 50 mmol doses (Figs. 4C and 4D). These results suggest that morphine exerts antinociceptive effects through the DG in modulating inflammatory pain behaviors.

Following identification of the effective dose of morphine in the DG, naloxone at doses of 0.5, 1.5, 5, and 15 mmol was microinjected prior to morphine administration (50 mmol), with a total injection volume of 0.5 μl. After formalin injection into the hind paws, inflammatory pain behaviors were assessed for 60 min. As observed in Figs. 5C and 5D, acquired AUC values of one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test revealed that naloxone considerably attenuated the analgesic effects of morphine during formalin test, as evidenced by significant differences in the AUC values for pain scores between treatment groups and the control (0.25 μl saline +50 mmol/0.25 μl morphine) group during both the early [F (4,32) = 10.50, P < 0.0001] and late [F (4,32) = 7.966, P = 0.0001] phases of the formalin test. In both phases, naloxone at two high doses (5 and 15 mmol) showed notably increased AUC values of pain scores compared to the control group. These results underscore a pivotal role for opioid receptors in this region in modulating inflammatory pain.

Discussion

The main findings of this study can be summarized as follows: (Raja et al., 2020) subcutaneous formalin injection produced significant biphasic nociceptive responses; (Cook et al., 2018) intra-VTA or -DG administration of morphine effectively attenuated formalin-induced pain, with a slightly stronger effect in the early phase for VTA and in the late phase for DG; (Muley et al., 2016) The effective dose of morphine producing analgesic responses was lower in the VTA than in the DG; (Zakeri et al., 2020) naloxone pretreatment reduced these effects in both regions, confirming MOR involvement. The effect was slightly stronger in the early phase for VTA and equal for DG; (Zussy et al., 2018) neither cannula implantation nor vehicle/drug administration into targeted regions had any significant effect on locomotor activity.

Various experimental models of inflammatory pain in rodents have been established using different inducers, including formalin, carrageenan, and complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA) (Aoki et al., 2014). Among these, the formalin test is a widely validated paradigm in both mice and rats for evaluating analgesic responses and the interactions between different analgesic agents (Bornhof et al., 2011). This test is typically conducted over a 60-minute period and is divided into two distinct phases. The early phase is characterized by transient pain, primarily caused by direct chemical stimulation of nociceptors. As the initial irritation subsides, pain intensity decreases. In the late phase, pain re-emerges due to the release of inflammatory mediators (Chichorro et al., 2004, Tjølsen et al., 1992). In the present study, formalin injection into the hind paw induced biphasic nociceptive responses compared to the saline-control group, in agreement with previous reports in our laboratory (Abolghasemi et al., 2025, Nazari-Serenjeh et al., 2024).

Opioid receptors are broadly classified into three main subtypes: mu (MOR), kappa (KOR), and delta (DOR), and their activation results in unique pharmacological effects (Dhawan et al., 1996). MOR agonists, such as morphine, are widely used to treat moderate to chronic pain, mainly acting through G-protein–coupled receptor (GPCR) pathways (Bao et al., 2015). Morphine reduces nociceptive transmission by engaging descending inhibitory pathways in the CNS and suppressing nociceptive inputs at the level of peripheral afferent neurons (Gopalakrishnan et al., 2022). Within the CNS, opioid receptors contribute to antinociceptive signaling through key descending modulatory pathways, such as the locus coeruleus, medulla, and PAG (Zöllner and Stein, 2007). Robust analgesia was observed following bilateral morphine injections into the posterior hypothalamic area (PHA) and PAG, with naloxone altogether abolishing this effect in the PHA and partially in the PAG (Manning and Franklin, 1998). Beyond the classical pain pathways, multiple brain regions participate in pain processing, including the VTA. A preclinical study demonstrated that the spinal–parabrachial–mesencephalic circuit transmits nociceptive information from the spinal cord to the VTA dopaminergic neurons (Yang et al., 2021). An animal imaging study revealed increased activity in the VTA, NAc, thalamus, and amygdala in a model of inflammatory pain using formalin (Arimura et al., 2019). VTA stimulation notably suppressed dorsal horn neuronal activity elicited by paw pressure and pinch stimuli, suggesting that its analgesic effects may be mediated via descending modulatory pathways and projections to brainstem and cortical regions (Li et al., 2016). Furthermore, the VTA sends dense dopaminergic projections to the NAc, a pathway that also contributes to pain modulation. In chronic neuropathic pain, the spontaneous firing rate of VTA-NAc neurons increased (Zhang et al., 2017). MOR activation in the VTA enhances dopaminergic neuronal firing. In support, a microdialysis study in conscious rats demonstrated that morphine administration markedly increased extracellular dopamine in a dose-dependent manner, an effect abolished entirely by systemic injection of naloxone (Klitenick et al., 1992). Consistently, in an inflammatory pain model, intra-VTA microinjection of morphine or substance P analog DiMe-C7 induced analgesic responses, whereas injection of dopamine receptor antagonists in the NAc prevented or reduced these effects (Altier and Stewart, 1998). Furthermore, lesions in the VTA neurons with 6-hydroxydopamine eliminated the analgesic effects of morphine in the formalin test (Morgan and Franklin, 1990). Consistent with these findings, the present study demonstrates that morphine administration into the VTA elicited analgesic effects, diminished by naloxone pretreatment.

Moreover, the present findings demonstrated that morphine microinjection into the DG significantly attenuated nociceptive behaviors in the formalin test, an effect that was effectively antagonized by prior naloxone administration. The hippocampal formation receives direct and indirect nociceptive signals and can modulate spinal nociceptive processing via descending pathways (Liu and Chen, 2009). These results are consistent with previous evidence showing that morphine administration into the hippocampus induced analgesic effects in an orofacial formalin pain model, attenuated by naloxone pretreatment. In contrast, naloxone alone caused a non-significant increase in pain intensity. Similarly, histaminergic receptor blockade reduced morphine-induced analgesia (Tamaddonfard et al., 2011). Reisi et al. also reported that intra-CA1 morphine administration elicited analgesia that was reversed by naloxone. They further demonstrated that pretreatment with naloxone before intra-CA1 administration of SKF-38393 and quinpirole (D1- and D2-like receptor agonists, respectively) attenuated inflammatory pain (Reisi et al., 2014). Consistent with these findings, intra-hippocampal administration of morphine, bicuculline (a GABAergic antagonist), and pilocarpine (a cholinergic agonist) produced antinociceptive effects in guinea pigs exposed to electric shock–induced pain, whereas naloxone pretreatment diminished the antinociceptive effect of carbachol (Favaroni Mendes and Menescal-de-Oliveira, 2008). Collectively, these studies suggest that while the opioidergic system within the hippocampus, particularly the DG, plays a crucial role in pain modulation, it likely interacts with other neurotransmitter systems in regulating pain responses.

At the cellular level, both the DG and the VTA exhibit opioid-induced disinhibition as a common mechanism, albeit with different downstream cellular targets. In the DG, this results in enhanced excitability of granule cells (Akaishi et al., 2000), whereas in the VTA it leads to disinhibition of dopaminergic neurons through activation of Gi/o-coupled MORs on GABAergic interneurons. In addition to the shared mechanism, opioid signaling in the VTA involves more complex synaptic modulation, including facilitation of presynaptic glutamate release (Chen et al., 2015) and postsynaptic MOR expression in a subset of dopaminergic neurons, where opioid peptides can induce both excitatory and inhibitory responses (Margolis et al., 2014).

One of the observations from the present study was that the effective dose of morphine required to produce analgesia within the VTA was lower than that in the DG. Additionally, lower doses of naloxone reduced morphine-induced analgesia in the DG, indicating region-specific opioid receptor sensitivity in pain modulation. Consistent with these findings, a previous study has reported that morphine produces analgesic effects at lower doses in the NAc compared to the CA1 region of the hippocampus in a model of inflammatory pain (Dinparast et al., 2026) which may suggest differential opioid sensitivity across mesolimbic and hippocampal circuits. In addition, in vitro autoradiography revealed moderate MOR binding in the VTA, whereas in the DG, both the granular and molecular cell layers showed dense to very dense mu binding, but only in the ventral region. In the dorsal DG, MOR binding was virtually absent (Mansour et al., 1987). Together, these findings indicate that differences in cellular mechanisms, local circuitry, receptor density, and receptor subtype distribution likely contribute to the differential sensitivity to opioid-induced analgesia between the VTA and DG. Nevertheless, to elucidate the underlying mechanisms, additional studies employing cellular, molecular, and electrophysiological approaches are needed. The current study revealed no significant differences in locomotor activity between drug-treated and control groups. This aligns with prior work in our lab showing that intra-NAc microinjections of morphine or naloxone did not alter locomotor activity relative to controls (Shahani et al., 2025). In contrast, an animal study demonstrated that intra-VTA administration of morphine enhances locomotor activity through a dopamine-dependent mechanism, with repeated exposure leading to progressive sensitization (Vezina et al., 1987). Methodological differences, including the morphine dose and the behavioral paradigms, may explain this apparent discrepancy.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, the investigation was restricted to two brain regions (VTA and DG of the hippocampus), one opioid receptor agonist and antagonist, and a single inflammatory pain model, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, although the injection volumes were adjusted based on anatomical differences, potential diffusion of the drug beyond the VTA or DG could have influenced the observed outcomes, thereby complicating the interpretation of the findings. In addition, complementary molecular analyses could have provided stronger mechanistic support for the findings of the present behavioral research.

In summary, the current behavioral study underscores the critical role of opioid receptors within the VTA and DG in modulating inflammatory pain. Furthermore, blocking MORs with naloxone attenuates the analgesic effects triggered by morphine, confirming the contribution of MORs to pain management. Future studies could explore the role of opioid receptors in the VTA and DG across different pain models, such as acute and neuropathic pain. Additionally, the interaction between opioid receptors with other neurotransmitter systems involved in pain modulation, such as dopamine, orexin, and glutamate in the VTA and DG, warrants investigation. Such studies depend on an understanding of the precise neurobiological mechanisms underlying pain control. They may pave the way for the development of novel and more effective pharmacological analgesic strategies for pain management.
