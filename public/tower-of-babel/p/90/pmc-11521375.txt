Clinical utility of an artificial intelligence radiomics-based tool for risk stratification of pulmonary nodules
Authors: Roger Y Kim, Clarisa Yee, Sana Zeb, Jennifer Steltz, Andrew J Vickers, Katharine A Rendle, Nandita Mitra, Lyndsey C Pickup, David M DiBardino, Anil Vachani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical utility data on pulmonary nodule (PN) risk stratification biomarkers are lacking. We aimed to determine the incremental predictive value and clinical utility of using an artificial intelligence (AI) radiomics-based computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) tool in addition to routine clinical information to risk stratify PNs among real-world patients.MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort study of patients with PNs who underwent lung biopsy. We collected clinical data and used a commercially available AI radiomics-based CAD tool to calculate a Lung Cancer Prediction (LCP) score. We developed logistic regression models to evaluate a well-validated clinical risk prediction model (the Mayo Clinic model) with and without the LCP score (Mayo vs Mayo + LCP) using area under the curve (AUC), risk stratification table, and standardized net benefit analyses.ResultsAmong the 134 patients undergoing PN biopsy, cancer prevalence was 61%. Addition of the radiomics-based LCP score to the Mayo model was associated with increased predictive accuracy (likelihood ratio test, P = .012). The AUCs for the Mayo and Mayo + LCP models were 0.58 (95% CI = 0.48 to 0.69) and 0.65 (95% CI = 0.56 to 0.75), respectively. At the 65% risk threshold, the Mayo + LCP model was associated with increased sensitivity (56% vs 38%; P = .019), similar false positive rate (33% vs 35%; P = .8), and increased standardized net benefit (18% vs -3.3%) compared with the Mayo model.ConclusionsUse of a commercially available AI radiomics-based CAD tool as a supplement to clinical information improved PN cancer risk prediction and may result in clinically meaningful changes in risk stratification.

Pulmonary nodules (PNs) are frequently detected by chest computed tomography (CT) (1), but their diagnostic evaluation requires complex and challenging medical decision-making (2). The goal of early detection of lung cancer must be carefully balanced with the risks associated with lung biopsy, which is required to confirm a cancer diagnosis (3,4). The current American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) PN management guidelines recommend using specific malignancy risk thresholds to stratify PNs into very low (<5%), intermediate (5%-65%), and high-risk (>65%) categories, which directly inform management decisions (5). Very low-risk PNs should be managed with noninvasive imaging surveillance. Among patients with intermediate-risk PNs, a nonsurgical lung biopsy is often suggested, while high-risk PNs can be considered for surgical biopsy and resection. However, quantifying PN risk is difficult. Clinical risk prediction models designed to quantify PN malignancy risk using routinely available clinical variables have existed since the 1990s but are infrequently used in practice (6,7). Among these models, the Mayo Clinic model is the most extensively validated and most commonly used in clinical research (8-11). Recent significant advances in innovative technologies for PN risk stratification have sparked optimism (12), but uptake of these novel biomarkers has been limited, due, in part, to a lack of robust clinical utility data supporting their use (13).

Artificial intelligence (AI) radiomics-based computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) tools use advanced mathematical analysis of CT imaging data and automation of image review to provide supplemental information on cancer risk for clinician-detected PNs (14,15). The purported additional advantages of such tools include their noninvasiveness, rapid turnaround time, and the ability to utilize existing imaging data to extract information not otherwise accessible to the human eye (eg, PN shape, wavelet transformations, textures, spatial complexity) (16). We have previously demonstrated that use of an AI radiomics-based CAD tool was associated with increased clinician diagnostic performance, interobserver agreement, and appropriate PN management decisions (17,18). The objective of this study was to determine the diagnostic performance and clinical utility of using a commercially available AI radiomics-based CAD tool in addition to routine clinical information in real-world patients evaluated for PNs.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of consecutive adult patients without a history of active lung cancer who were evaluated by a single interventional pulmonology service and underwent bronchoscopic lung biopsy for PN diagnostic evaluation from June 2020 to April 2022 at the University of Pennsylvania Health System. We identified eligible individuals via electronic health record (EHR) review and selected those who had solid or part-solid PNs 8 to 30 mm in maximal diameter. We excluded patients with chest CT imaging incompatible with use of the AI radiomics-based CAD software and those missing information to determine PN final diagnosis (Figure 1). PN final diagnosis was categorized as either malignant or benign based on histologic evaluation. For cases without a definitive histologic diagnosis (ie, those with a nondiagnostic biopsy), PNs were determined to be malignant if they were empirically treated due to clinical suspicion or benign if follow-up imaging confirmed complete resolution or 12-month PN diameter stability. This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines and was approved by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Review Board (Protocol #824356).

Malignancy prediction models

Quantitative clinical assessment of PN malignancy risk was not consistently documented in the EHR in this retrospective study. Therefore, we used the Mayo Clinic model to represent a surrogate measurement of routine clinical risk assessment (9), as this well-validated clinical risk prediction model has previously been demonstrated to perform comparably to clinicians (19). The AI radiomics-based CAD tool we assessed was the commercially available Optellum Virtual Nodule Clinic Lung Cancer Prediction (LCP) CAD tool, which provides a quantitative risk assessment designed to supplement clinical risk assessment. Using solely CT imaging data, the LCP-CAD tool employs an AI convolutional neural network framework to generate a LCP score ranging from 1 to 10, which represents PN malignancy risk on a decile scale with increasing values corresponding to higher predicted risks of malignancy (17,18,20,21). In a population with 30% malignancy prevalence, LCP scores correspond to the following probability estimates of malignancy: 1: 0.2%, 2: 0.4%, 3: 0.8%, 4: 2.0%, 5: 5.6%, 6: 15.0%, 7: 34.0%, 8: 64.0%, 9: 84.0%, and 10: 93.0% (22). Per the device’s Food and Drug Administration (FDA) clearance, the intended use population excludes individuals under 35 years, those with a history of cancer within 5 years, and those with lung cancer screening-detected PNs (23). Notably, we evaluated the theoretical off-label use of the Optellum LCP-CAD tool in this retrospective study, as we did not exclude adult patients based on age, non-lung cancer history, or type of CT imaging (ie, we included low-dose CT imaging).

We compared the performance of the following multivariable logistic regression malignancy risk prediction models: 1) the Mayo Clinic model (Mayo) (9) and 2) the Mayo model plus the LCP score (Mayo + LCP). The Mayo model included age (in years), smoking status (current or former, never), history of cancer (yes, no), PN diameter (in mm), spiculation (yes, no), and upper lobe location (yes, no), while the Mayo + LCP model comprised variables from the Mayo model and the LCP score (5-10).

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive statistics to evaluate patient demographics and clinical, PN, and procedural characteristics. Categorical variables were described with frequencies and percentages and continuous variables with medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Differences in variables between individuals with malignant and benign PNs were summarized using the Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables.

We assessed the incremental predictive accuracy of adding the LCP score to the Mayo model by using nested logistic regression models with robust standard errors, performing the likelihood ratio test (24,25), and employing 10-fold cross-validation to correct for model overfitting (26). All subsequent analyses used predicted values from the Mayo and the Mayo + LCP models after cross-validation. We evaluated model diagnostic performance by estimating discriminative accuracy via area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), which summarizes the ability of a model to categorize PNs as malignant or benign (27). We additionally performed exploratory subgroup analyses and report model AUCs stratified by patient age, smoking status, PN diameter, and PN margins. We used calibration belt plots (28) to describe model calibration and the likelihood ratio test to evaluate overall calibration quality (29). We compared the clinical utility of the two models with a risk stratification table to cross-tabulate clinically relevant risks categories based on the ACCP guidelines (ie, intermediate vs high risk) (5,30). For each model, we assessed classification accuracy by calculating the true positive rate (sensitivity) and the false positive rate (1-specificity) of a high-risk (>65%) prediction (31). To quantify relative clinical utility, we compared the standardized net benefit between the Mayo and Mayo + LCP models at the 65% risk threshold by calculating the difference between the proportion of true positives and false positives weighted by the relative harm of a false positive and a false negative result (net benefit) divided by the observed cancer prevalence (32,33). Notably, as standardized net benefit is derived from decision curve analysis and classical decision theory, no formal hypothesis testing for statistical significance was performed (34). We then used a risk stratification table and scatter plot depicting Mayo vs Mayo + LCP predicted malignancy estimates to perform a reclassification analysis assessing how the addition of the LCP score to the Mayo model altered risk classification. We performed 2 sensitivity analyses: one excluding individuals with part-solid PNs, which may represent more indolent disease (35), and another using alternative risk thresholds of 10% and 70% suggested by the British Thoracic Society guidelines (36). We defined statistical significance with a 2-sided P value less than .05. Data analyses were conducted between October 2023 and April 2024 using Stata/MP 17.0 and adhered to published statistical analysis and reporting guidelines (37).

Results

Of the 134 patients who underwent bronchoscopic lung biopsy for indeterminate PN evaluation, 81 (60%) were pathologically diagnosed and 1 (1%) clinically diagnosed with cancer within 12 months, yielding an overall cancer prevalence of 61%. After initial bronchoscopic biopsy, 9 (6.7%) and 11 (8.2%) required subsequent transthoracic needle biopsy and surgical lung biopsy, respectively (Table 1). Compared to individuals with a benign PN, those with a malignant PN were more likely to have a history of cancer (51% vs 25%; P = .003), a family history of lung cancer (28% vs 12%; P = .018), and a higher predicted malignancy probability calculated by either the Mayo Clinic model (median [IQR] = 0.54 [0.33-0.70] vs 0.31 [0.15-0.64]; P = .010) or the LCP-CAD tool (median [IQR] = 0.84 [0.84-0.93] vs 0.84 [0.64-0.88]; P = .004). There were no significant differences in patient age, smoking status, emphysema history, or PN diameter, density, spiculation, or location. Among those with a malignant PN (n = 82), 38 (46%) were diagnosed with adenocarcinoma, 21 (26%) with squamous cell carcinoma, 2 (2.4%) with small cell carcinoma, and 6 (7.3%) with carcinoid tumors. Of the patients with a benign PN (n = 52), 32 (62%) did not have a specific histologic diagnosis available at 12-month follow-up, 12 (23%) were diagnosed with granulomatous disease, 5 (9.6%) with infection, and 2 (3.8%) with hamartomas.

The risk stratification table compares the Mayo and Mayo + LCP models’ classification of PN malignancy risk into intermediate- and high-risk categories (Table 2). Overall, 31 of 82 malignant PNs were classified as high-risk (>65%) by the Mayo model (sensitivity: 38%), compared to 46 of 82 by the Mayo + LCP model (sensitivity: 56%; P = .019). Among benign PNs, 18 of 52 were classified as high-risk by the Mayo model (false positive rate: 35%), compared to 17 of 52 by the Mayo + LCP model (false positive rate: 33%; P = .8). Cancer prevalence was 63% (31 of 49) among PNs classified as high-risk by the Mayo model, compared with 73% (46 of 63) among those classified as high-risk by the Mayo + LCP model. At the 65% risk threshold, the standardized net benefit of the Mayo model was -3.3%, compared with 18% for the Mayo + LCP model. The reclassification analysis demonstrated that 37% (19 of 51) of malignant PNs classified as intermediate-risk by the Mayo model were reclassified as high-risk by the Mayo + LCP model while 13% (4 of 31) classified as high-risk by the Mayo model were reclassified as intermediate-risk by the Mayo + LCP model (Figures 3-4). On the other hand, 44% (8 of 18) of benign PNs classified as high-risk by the Mayo model were reclassified as intermediate-risk by the Mayo + LCP model, and 21% (7 of 34) classified as intermediate-risk by the Mayo model were reclassified as high-risk by the Mayo + LCP model.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of real-world patients who underwent bronchoscopic lung biopsy of indeterminate PNs, we evaluated the theoretical impact of using a commercially available AI radiomics-based CAD tool along with routinely available clinical information to predict PN malignancy risk. We found that the addition of supplemental radiomics-based data to clinical risk factors improved cancer risk prediction and resulted in clinically significant reclassification of malignancy risk. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate the additive value of a commercially available AI radiomics-based CAD PN risk stratification tool to clinical data in a real-world patient population.

As recommended by the American Thoracic Society, a biomarker for early detection of lung cancer “should be used in clinical practice only if it reliably adds to a clinician’s judgment, resulting in a more favorable clinical outcome for the target population” (13). Despite the multitude of such biomarkers being developed and analytically validated, few are currently commercially available, and widespread uptake and adoption has not yet occurred (12). While many published studies have described external validation of novel radiomics-based algorithms, only a minority have included real-world populations reflective of those routinely seen in PN clinics, and even fewer have reported clinical utility data or how data generated by such tools could be integrated into routine clinical practice (15). For the first time, we have demonstrated that the supplemental information provided by a commercially available AI radiomics-based CAD PN malignancy prediction tool enhances malignancy risk prediction, which could ultimately improve medical decision-making.

Existing clinical PN malignancy risk prediction models (eg, the Mayo Clinic model) do not outperform routine clinical assessment when directly compared (11,19). While such models seek to quantify cancer risk by using readily available clinical information, they do not provide any additional information on top of what any given clinician would already have access to. For example, as part of routine clinical practice, a clinician evaluating a patient with an indeterminate PN will carefully review available CT imaging and obtain a history from the patient to gather all the data included within the Mayo Clinic model. Thus, it is not necessarily unexpected that such clinical risk prediction models do not outperform routine clinical assessment. We chose to use the Mayo model as a surrogate measure of routine clinical risk stratification because it is the longest standing clinical PN malignancy risk prediction model, the most extensively validated in patient cohorts with varying malignancy prevalences, and freely accessible on the internet (7,10,38). The intended use of radiomics-based models such as the LCP-CAD tool is to provide supplemental information not otherwise available to the clinician to aid in PN risk assessment. For this reason, we evaluated the additive value of the LCP score to the Mayo Clinic model and did not directly compare the diagnostic performance of the LCP-CAD tool alone to the Mayo Clinic model. This is an important distinction to make when evaluating novel biomarkers for PN risk stratification (13,15,30).

The results of our clinical utility analysis suggested that the addition of the LCP score to routine clinical risk stratification was associated with a clinically meaningful reclassification in predicted PN malignancy risk. A greater proportion of malignant PNs were classified as high-risk (>65%) with incorporation of the LCP score, which theoretically would trigger consideration of upfront surgical resection rather than bronchoscopic biopsy according to the 2013 ACCP clinical guidelines (5). Importantly, this increase in sensitivity at the 65% threshold did not come at the expense of a relative increase in the proportion of benign PNs classified as high-risk (similar false positive rate). Thus, the addition of the LCP score to the Mayo model was associated with a net increase in the proportion of malignant PNs classified as high-risk. Our standardized net benefit analysis at the 65% risk threshold demonstrated that the Mayo + LCP achieved nearly 20% of the maximum possible clinical utility. Taken together, among individuals being considered for lung biopsy, supplementing routine clinical risk assessment with data from the radiomics-based LCP-CAD tool provided additional predictive value and resulted in improved malignancy risk stratification, which may facilitate guideline-concordant medical decision-making for PN evaluation.

A major strength of our study is its use of real-world, detailed clinical data and broad inclusion criteria, which allowed us to assess the additive effect of the LCP-CAD tool within the context of routine clinical care. Additionally, our analyses focused on clinical utility as opposed to analytical or clinical validation, making our results more relevant to clinicians evaluating PNs in practice (13). Our study has limitations. First, due to its retrospective design, we were only able to evaluate the theoretical effect of the LCP-CAD tool on PN evaluation and were unable to assess other potential practical advantages of using this AI technology. On the other hand, because of its retrospective nature, we were able to evaluate the performance of the LCP-CAD tool in an expanded population of individuals with PNs outside of the intended use population per the device’s FDA clearance, making our results potentially more generalizable (23). In our ongoing pragmatic randomized controlled trial (Assessment of a Radiomics-based Computer-Aided Diagnosis tool for pulmonary nodulES [ARCADES]; NCT05968898), we will prospectively assess its impact on PN medical decision-making and other patient-centered outcomes, including timeliness, procedure-related harms, and health-care costs (39). Second, because we limited our study cohort to individuals who underwent lung biopsy, our cohort had a relatively high malignancy prevalence (61%) so our findings should not be assumed to apply to distinct populations with varying cancer prevalences. Third, this study was performed at a single high-volume, resource-rich institution with a comprehensive cancer center and robust interventional pulmonology service line so our results may not be generalizable to other health-care settings. Notably, our exploratory subgroup analyses found reduced additive predictive value of the LCP-CAD tool among individuals who never smoked and those with PNs less than 15 mm in diameter. Future studies will need to evaluate the LCP-CAD tool in diverse health-care systems and among clinicians with varying levels of experience. Finally, while the model we tested in this study incorporated the LCP score into an existing clinical risk model, we were unable to directly evaluate how clinicians might practically integrate the LCP score into clinical practice. Our ongoing clinical trial and other future mixed methods studies aimed at determining clinician perspectives will be required to evaluate the facilitators and barriers for clinical implementation (15,39).

In conclusion, our study found that use of a commercially available AI radiomics-based tool as a supplement to clinical information improved PN cancer risk prediction and accurate risk stratification, which could impact downstream medical decision-making. Our clinical utility analysis highlights the importance of contextualizing data provided by PN risk stratification biomarkers and demonstrates the promise of radiomics-based tools for enhancing the PN diagnostic process. Future rigorous prospective studies are necessary to assess the clinical effectiveness and effect on clinical decision-making of such biomarkers.
