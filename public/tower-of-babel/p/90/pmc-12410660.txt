Effect of health promotion interventions in supermarkets on dietary variety among Japanese older adults: a protocol for a cluster non-randomised parallel-group comparative trial
Authors: Tatsunosuke Gomi, Keiko Motokawa, Maki Shirobe, Masanori Iwasaki, Misato Hayakawa, Ayako Edahiro, Hirohiko Hirano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWith an ageing population, prevention of frailty among older adults has become a critical public health issue. Dietary habits are one of the essential components in frailty prevention, which involves promoting changes in dietary behaviours, such as including dietary variety. However, community-level health promotion interventions face significant challenges, including limited spatial access to food, which is important as it is not easy to change the behaviour of older adults. The dissemination of community-level health promotion interventions targeting dietary behavioural changes among older adults remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of supermarket-based health promotion interventions on dietary variety among older Japanese adults.Methods and analysisThis study is a cluster non-randomised parallel-group comparative trial involving 15 supermarkets in the Yamanashi and Nagano Prefectures, Japan. Seven supermarkets will be assigned to the intervention group and eight to the control group. The intervention group will receive a health promotion programme consisting of three components to enhance dietary diversity: information, education and support delivery. The intervention design incorporates social marketing strategies, and programme evaluation will be conducted concurrently. Data, including the Dietary Variety Score (DVS) for the primary outcome, will be collected through postal and electronic surveys at baseline and at 1, 2 and 3 years, with the 3-year follow-up serving as the primary endpoint for effectiveness evaluation. Statistical analyses will use a generalised linear mixed model, focusing on changes in the DVS as the primary outcome. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses will be performed to assess the generalisability of the findings.Ethics and disseminationThe Tokyo Metropolitan Institute for Geriatrics and Gerontology Research Ethics Committee has approved the research protocol (approval number: R23-116). The results will be disseminated through conference presentations and publication in peer-reviewed international journals.Trial registration numberUMIN000056023.Keywords: Implementation Science, Public health, Behavior, Nutrition, Frailty

Introduction

With the rapid global increase in the ageing population,1 the prevention of frailty among older adults has become a public health priority.2 Frailty is defined as a state of increased vulnerability to poor resolution of homoeostasis after a stressor event.2 Increasing dietary variety in daily life is an essential component of preventing frailty in older adults.3 4 High dietary variety has been positively associated with various health outcomes, including lower risk of depression,5 reduced mortality risk6 and extended healthy life expectancy.7

However, ensuring sufficient dietary diversity is challenging due to factors such as limited physical access,8 insufficient nutritional knowledge9 and socioeconomic constraints.10 Although face-to-face interventions have been shown to improve dietary diversity,11 health promotion strategies aimed at achieving community-level behavioural changes remain difficult to implement.12 A community-based comprehensive intervention in Unnan City, Japan, successfully improved physical activity rates at the community level by implementing a strategically designed approach incorporating three key components—information, education and support delivery—combined with social marketing strategies.13 One crucial aspect of social marketing content is identifying ‘where’ to implement the intervention.14 This study focuses on supermarkets, which have been reported as an important food environment resource associated with dietary variety.8 A previous randomised controlled trial reported that individuals who received both in-store dietary counselling by a registered dietitian and support using web-based tools at supermarkets showed significantly greater improvements in dietary quality, as assessed by adherence to the heart-healthy the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet, compared with the control group.15 Supermarkets are frequented by a broad spectrum of individuals, including those who may not participate in conventional health programmes, making them important locations for health promotion interventions.

This study aims to evaluate the effect of a health promotion intervention based on social marketing principles comprising information, education and support delivery implemented in supermarkets on the dietary variety of older adults, and to assess the intervention’s effect on changes in awareness, knowledge, beliefs and intentions, which are precursors to behavioural change.

Methods and analysis

Study design, setting and procedures

This study presents the protocol of a cluster non-randomised parallel-group comparative trial, classified as a phase III trial, scheduled to be conducted in 15 supermarkets of ‘Ichiyama Mart’ located in Japan. The intervention period is planned to span 3 years. Each store is considered a cluster, with supermarkets allocated to either the intervention group (seven stores) or the control group (eight stores). This protocol is designed according to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials statement (online supplemental material 1).

Ichiyama Mart operates 13 stores in Yamanashi Prefecture and 2 in Nagano Prefecture. Health promotion interventions will be implemented in stores allocated to the intervention groups. The stores in the control group will conduct business as usual. The evaluation will be conducted by recruiting members of Ichiyama Mart’s reward card programme and conducting mail-based and web-based surveys. Recruitment for the baseline survey was carried out via the messaging app LINE (operated by LINE Yahoo Corporation) from September to October 2024, using the official Ichiyama Mart account and in-store notices. This LINE-based recruitment involved sending a broadcast message to individuals already registered with the official Ichiyama Mart LINE account. Surveys are planned to be conducted at baseline and at 1, 2 and 3 years after the intervention, with the 3-year follow-up serving as the primary outcome evaluation (figure 1).

Overview of the study design.

The assessments at 1 and 2 years will serve as interim evaluations, focusing on the effects of the intervention and potential harm (including adverse health impacts on residents or unacceptable business losses). Adjustments to the timing of the primary outcome evaluation may be considered, depending on the results of the interim evaluations.

Participants

The inclusion criteria for this study are as follows: (a) Membership in the Ichiyama Mart rewards card programme, (b) individuals aged 60–84 years and (c) those with the ability to complete the survey questionnaire independently.

The exclusion criteria are as follows: (a) Residence in nursing care facilities and (b) individuals unable to visit the supermarket for daily shopping. Thus, all those who were able to visit the store for daily shopping by any means, including being accompanied or transported by family members, were included in the study participants.

Recruitment was initially conducted between September and October 2024 to confirm the eligibility criteria and gather responses from individuals willing to participate in the survey. Along with the baseline survey, detailed study information (including electronic materials) was provided, outlining the purpose, procedures, confidentiality, potential benefits and disadvantages, anticipated risks and measures to address these risks. Only individuals who provided written informed consent and completed the questionnaire were eligible to participate. The baseline survey was conducted between October and November 2024 for those who responded to the recruitment using either postal mail (paper questionnaires) or email (web-based survey forms). A total of 277 participants were solicited for mail surveys and 634 for web-based surveys (via email). During the preparation of this manuscript, the collection of baseline survey responses has been completed, and data entry is underway.

Allocation

All stores were allocated to one of two groups: seven in the intervention group and eight in the control group. From the perspective of supermarket operations management, random allocation of stores was not feasible. Representatives of Ichiyama Mart allocated intervention stores based on the following criteria: (a) Ensuring that the intervention stores were not concentrated in a specific geographic area. (b) A relatively high likelihood of obtaining understanding and support from store staff for implementing the intervention. (c) Securing agreement from store managers.

Of the 15 stores, the closest store is 2.2 km away from the nearest store by road. In order to minimise contamination of the intervention, the subjects were asked in the baseline survey which stores they shop at most frequently, and they were assigned to a store (group) according to their answers.

Intervention

The intervention consists of three components designed to promote dietary variety: information, education and support delivery.13 The specific content of each component was determined using social marketing (table 1). Social marketing is an extension of conventional marketing theory and is applied to public health programmes and behaviours. It is a strategic approach aimed at designing interventions that encourage community members to ‘purchase’ (adopt) the offered product (behaviour) as if it were a marketable product.16 Given that the objective of the social marketing approach is to achieve behavioural changes, each step of the intervention design is informed by theories related to behavioural decision-making, such as the Health Belief Model and the Theory of Planned Behaviour.16 Since the primary aim of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of a population-level approach, all interventions will be implemented at the store level. This means that individual-level interventions that identify only the evaluation participants will not be implemented.

Information

A core message emphasising the importance of dietary variety is developed through hearings, with target segments identified via the social marketing process. This message is promoted through various channels, including posters, leaflets, in-store announcements and digital media. Registered dietitians will publish articles on healthy eating habits in the form of columns provided across digital media approximately twice per month. Participants in the intervention group will receive notifications for the publication of these articles via the LINE messaging application. The content of these articles will vary to maintain reader engagement, but will be consistently centred around the importance of consuming a diverse range of foods.

Education

Regular health-promotion events will be held to convey the importance of various diets. These events are scheduled to be held approximately four times per year and will focus on the key topics necessary for promoting health among older adults, such as dietary and nutritional approaches to prevent frailty and sarcopenia. Furthermore, simple health assessment sessions will be conducted to help participants understand their health status, as well as to provide a starting point for behavioural changes aimed at improving dietary variety. These sessions are scheduled to be held approximately two times per year and will include measurements of grip strength and walking speed, with immediate feedback provided to the participants.

Support

The in-store environment will be designed using the nudge theory from behavioural economics17 to encourage the purchase of diverse food groups.18 For example, recommended foods will be strategically placed at eye level for the target segment on supermarket shelves. Point-of-purchase displays will indicate the number of food groups included in prepared meals or boxed lunches, making it easier for customers to select various food groups. These approaches aim to enable healthier choices without pressuring customers. Moreover, an electronic platform using applications, including LINE, will be introduced to facilitate communication not only between customers and the supermarket but also among customers themselves. The platform supports shared experiences and promotes engagement in healthier food choices.

Monitoring of intervention adherence

To monitor adherence to the intervention protocol at participating stores, we will conduct an email-based survey of store managers. Any cases where the intervention is not implemented during certain periods will be documented. Additionally, information regarding intervention adherence will be collected through monthly meetings with representatives of the supermarket chain.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome is the change in the Dietary Variety Score (DVS)19 from baseline to 3 years. Secondary outcomes are changes in the logic model (awareness, knowledge, beliefs and intentions) leading to behavioural changes (figure 2), changes in nutritional status and changes in frailty rates. All the endpoints will be assessed using a questionnaire. In addition, the economic impact of the intervention will be evaluated as the difference in the change in sales and operating income between the intervention and control groups.

The logic model for health promotion to improve dietary variety.

Dietary Variety Score

DVS is calculated based on the frequency of intake of ten food groups: meat, fish and shellfish, eggs, soybean products, milk, green and yellow vegetables, seaweed, fruits, potatoes, and oils and fats.19 Each food group consumed daily is scored 1 point, whereas all others are scored 0 points, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 10. A higher DVS has been associated with the maintenance of muscle strength and walking speed20 as well as the prevention of frailty.10

Logic model for behaviour change

The effects of the intervention will be evaluated by assessing changes in each stage of the logic model for behavioural change12 (figure 2). For ‘awareness’, participants will be asked whether they are aware of the intervention, with responses recorded as ‘Yes’ or ‘No’. Regarding ‘knowledge’, participants will be asked if they know that consuming a variety of foods can help prevent physical decline, and responses will similarly be recorded as ‘Yes’ or ‘No’. To assess ‘beliefs’, participants will be asked whether consuming a variety of foods contributes to preventing physical decline. Their responses will be measured on four options: ‘strongly agree’, ‘somewhat agree’, ‘somewhat disagree’ and ‘strongly disagree’. For ‘intentions’, two questions will be used. The first, focusing on behavioural intention, will ask whether participants intend to consume a variety of foods within the next 6 months, with responses recorded as ‘Yes’ or ‘No’. The second, addressing implementation intention, will inquire whether participants plan to incorporate a variety of foods into their diet if they feel signs of physical decline, again with responses recorded as ‘Yes’ or ‘No’. As the reproducibility of these items has not yet been verified, caution is warranted when interpreting the relevant results.

Nutritional status

Nutritional status will be assessed using the Japanese version of the Mini Nutritional Assessment-Short Form (MNA-SF).21 The MNA-SF consists of six items: reduction in food intake, weight loss, mobility, the presence of psychological stress or acute illness, the presence of neuropsychological problems and body mass index. The responses to these items are totalled to a maximum score of 14 points, with scores categorised as follows: 12–14 points indicate ‘normal nutritional status’, 8–11 points indicate ‘at risk of malnutrition’ and 7 points or less indicate ‘malnourished’.22

Frailty

Frailty is assessed using the Questionnaire for Medical Checkup of Old‐Old (QMCOO, ‘Koki‐Koreisha no shitsumon‐hyou’ in Japanese), which was developed by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, and is officially used in health examinations of older Japanese adults in the later stages of life. The QMCOO assesses the overall health status of older adults and consists of 15 questions in 10 domains: health status, mental health, eating behaviour, oral function, weight loss, physical function and falls, cognitive function, smoking, social participation and social support. All items in the QMCOO have been described elsewhere,23 and the QMCOO has been reported to be valid for determining frailty.24 In the present study, we follow validity studies and judge three applicable items as prefrailty and four or more items as frailty.24 Changes in physical function will be assessed based on changes in the binary response (‘yes’ or ‘no’) to the QMCOO item ‘Do you feel that you walk more slowly than before?’.

Economic impact evaluation

The economic impact of this study will be assessed by analysing sales data for each intervention and control group. The changes in total sales and sales per customer passing through the cash register before and after the intervention will be compared. The analysis of seasonal trend variations will be controlled by calculating and comparing year-over-year ratios for total sales and sales per customer passing through the cash register for each store during the same month of the previous year.

Adverse effects

Feedback sessions with supermarkets will be held monthly to share information and gather opinions regarding whether the intervention has negatively affected customer shopping experiences, such as making shopping more inconvenient. Additionally, a significant decline in supermarket sales in the intervention group will be monitored to evaluate adverse effects.

Baseline information

Data on the participants’ basic attributes were collected using a questionnaire, including information on sex, age, educational background, household composition, household income, driving licence status, smoking habits, alcohol consumption habits, number of comorbidities, medication use, nutritional status, oral function, shared meal practices, subjective difficulty accessing food, shopping methods, use of shopping services, shopping frequency, physical activity habits and accessibility to supermarkets (calculated based on residential address). As part of the store-level information, we aim to calculate variables for the population density and the proportion of older adults in the area where each store is located, as well as the average monthly number of customers throughout the year.

Assessment of intervention contamination

To evaluate the extent of intervention contamination, participants will be asked to report the store they most frequently visited over the past year, as well as the second most frequently visited store, and their corresponding visit frequencies.

Sample size calculation

Based on previous studies,11 the difference in changes in the primary outcome, DVS, between groups was assumed to be +1.2 points, with an SD of 2.6 points for the overall postintervention DVS. The sample size was subsequently calculated under the conditions of an alpha error of 0.05 and power of 0.90. The calculations indicated that the required number of participants per cluster ranged from 19 to 25. The sample size was set to 25 participants per cluster (the intervention group consists of 7 clusters with 175 participants, and the control group consists of 8 clusters with 200 participants). Sample size calculations were performed using the Sample Size Calculators provided by the National Institutes of Health.26

Data management

Data quality management involves two key components: (a) Designing the questionnaire to facilitate ease of response and (b) employing a double-entry method for data input. Manual checks included verifying adherence to the eligibility criteria, identifying missing responses and reviewing values to ensure that they fall within the expected range. Once all data have been entered, analysts will perform logical checks and data coding using a combination of programming and manual review to address any data quality issues. The data management will follow strict protocols to ensure confidentiality and data integrity, including the use of encrypted databases and secure data storage.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables at baseline will be presented as the mean (SD) or median (IQR), while categorical variables will be expressed as n (%).

Main analysis

To test the effectiveness of the programme, the difference in changes in the DVS between the groups will be analysed using a generalised linear mixed-effects model, adjusting for baseline values and accounting for clustering (difference-in-differences approach). The endpoint for the primary analysis will be set at the 3-year follow-up. The covariates included in the model will be sex, age, educational background, household composition, household income, driving licence status, smoking and alcohol consumption habits, number of comorbidities, medication use, and shopping frequency, subjective difficulty accessing food, accessibility to supermarkets, population density and the proportion of older adults in the area, and the average monthly number of customers at baseline. Statistical significance will be set at a p<0.05.

Additional analyses

For sensitivity analysis, changes in the proportion of individuals with a decrease in the DVS will be analysed based on the cut-off values identified in previous studies. Two cut-off patterns will be analysed: 3/420 and 2/3.27 Sensitivity analysis will also be implemented to evaluate the heterogeneity caused by missing follow-up data (missing data bias). Missing data will thus be handled using multiple imputations by chained equations, generating 20–50 imputed datasets based on the baseline results, allocation and group variables. Missing data will be handled using multiple imputation by chained equations, resulting in the generation of 20–50 imputed datasets based on baseline outcomes, allocation and group variables. The proportion of missing values will be calculated for the overall dataset as well as for each individual variable. To assess the generalisability of the findings, subgroup analyses will be conducted based on participants’ demographic characteristics, such as sex (male and female) and age (<75 years and ≥75 years). For public health evaluation, the intervention dose will be identified using programme evaluation results from the RE-AIM model metrics, while the dose-response relationships will be analysed to evaluate the relationship between the intervention dose and outcomes. E-values will be used to confirm the extent to which potential confounding factors not measured in this study could potentially overturn the interpretation of the main results.28 Given the nature of the intervention, it is difficult to prevent contamination between groups. Consequently, we plan to assess the extent of contamination through questionnaires and conduct sensitivity analyses accounting for such contamination. This approach is aligned with the principles of the per-protocol analysis.

For secondary outcomes, including changes at each stage of the logic model (awareness, knowledge, beliefs and intentions), changes in nutritional status and changes in frailty prevalence, the differences in the changes in proportions between groups will be analysed using mixed-effects models adjusted for baseline values and accounting for clustering (difference-in-differences analysis). Economic impact analysis will be conducted to compare the changes in total sales and sales per customer passing through the cash register between the intervention group (n=7 stores) and the control group (n=8 stores) using the Mann-Whitney U test and two-way repeated measures analysis of variance.

All analyses will be carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (V.29.0 or higher; IBM).

Patient and public involvement

The public will not participate in the design, reporting or dissemination plans of this study. However, they will be partially involved in the implementation phase. Due to the nature of the social marketing approach adopted in this study, insights from the target segment are gathered through a series of hearings. These insights can be used to develop precise messages aimed at promoting behavioural change.16 Additionally, materials such as messages and posters will be refined to be more comprehensible and user-focused through repeated hearings with the target segment.13

Discussion

Few health promotion interventions targeting older adults’ dietary habits have employed systematic strategies at the community level. This study is thus expected to provide valuable insights into the effectiveness of a structured programme designed to improve dietary diversity following a social marketing process. A key strength of this study is its ability to provide actionable insights for broader implementation through a programme evaluation of public health impacts. Additionally, using supermarkets as a setting for intervention may provide useful insights for future health promotion, as interventions can be delivered to individuals who may not participate in traditional programmes, such as classroom-based initiatives.

The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of Japan launched the third term of Health Japan 21 in 2024,29 in which it focuses on promoting the ‘Strategic Initiative for a Healthy and Sustainable Food Environment’. In Japan, a certification programme recognising establishments that provide healthy meals and food environments has been implemented, and 548 establishments have been certified as of August 2024.30 31 However, there are currently no health promotion initiatives at the environmental level specifically aimed at preventing frailty among older adults. The insights gained from this study may contribute to the development of strategies to create food environments that support frailty prevention. This study was designed based on the concept of creating shared value (CSV),32 which underscores the importance of pursuing both economic performance and corporate social contribution. Consequently, when generalising the study process to other supermarket chains, it is essential for each company to define the significance of the initiative from a long-term perspective, considering both the financial costs involved and the potential benefits.

This study will have some limitations. First, the allocation of groups is not randomised but is rather determined by supermarket representatives. This introduces a potential selection bias that may lead to an overestimation of the intervention effects. Second, the study participants will be selected from supermarket members who voluntarily opted to participate, thus limiting the representativeness of the sample relative to the broader population. This may result in an overestimation of the effects of the intervention. Although resource constraints will prevent us from implementing broader recruitment strategies in this study, integrating large-scale web-based surveys and comprehensive household-distribution surveys in the vicinity of the store should facilitate evaluation across a more diverse target population. Third, as the intervention effects are demonstrated within the customer base of a specific supermarket chain, the extent to which the findings can be generalised remains uncertain. Further, we will describe the programme evaluation in detail to provide a foundation for assessing the generalisability of the findings to different participant populations. Fourth, there is currently no evidence to demonstrate the reproducibility of the DVS, which is the primary outcome of this study. Consequently, the sensitivity for detecting the intervention effect cannot be clearly determined at this stage. To address this limitation, we have calculated a sample size with the highest possible precision based on the effect sizes reported in previous studies. Nevertheless, it remains necessary to generate evidence on the reproducibility of the DVS over multiple years among community-dwelling older adults.
