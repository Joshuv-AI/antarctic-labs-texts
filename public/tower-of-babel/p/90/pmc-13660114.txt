Efficacy of TACE combined with targeted immunotherapy in the treatment of advanced liver cancer
Authors: Peng Xie, Fuqiang Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the efficacy of transcatheter arterial chemoembolization (TACE) combined with targeted immunotherapy in the treatment of advanced liver cancer.MethodA total of 115 patients with intermediate-advanced liver cancer treated in our hospital from June 2021 to June 2024 were retrospectively selected and divided into two groups according to the actual treatment regimen received. The grouping was a non-randomized retrospective design. The conventional group (n = 57) received TACE alone, while the combination group (n = 58) received TACE combined with targeted immunotherapy. Clinical efficacy, tumor markers [alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125), carbohydrate antigen 199 (CA199), protein induced by vitamin K absence-II (PIVKA-II)], immune function [immunoglobulin A (IgA), immunoglobulin G (IgG), immunoglobulin M (IgM)], and liver function indicators [serum albumin (ALB), glutamic-pyruvic transaminase (GPT), total bilirubin (TBIL)] were compared between the two groups. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and the Cox proportional hazards regression model were used to evaluate survival rate, the incidence of adverse events was also compared. To control for potential confounding factors, 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) was performed as a sensitivity analysis, subgroup analyses of efficacy and safety for different targeted drugs within the combination group were also carried out. Immunohistochemistry was used to detect the expression of Ki-67, CD8 ⁺ T, and PD-L1 in two groups before treatment.ResultCompared with the conventional group, the proportion of objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR) patients in the combined group was significantly higher (P < 0.05). After treatment, the levels of tumor markers in both groups were significantly reduced, which were lower in the combined group than the conventional group (P < 0.001). After treatment, the immune function in both groups were improved, with the combined group showing superior efficacy compared to the conventional group (P < 0.001). After treatment, the liver function in both groups were improved. The ALB level in the combined group was significantly higher than that in the conventional group, while the GPT and TBIL levels were significantly lower than those in the conventional group (P < 0.001). The one-year cumulative survival rate of patients in the combined group was higher than that of the conventional group (P < 0.05). The overall incidence of grade ≥ 3 adverse events did not differ significantly between the two groups (P > 0.05). After PSM, the DCR and 1-year cumulative survival rate in the combination group remained significantly superior to those in the conventional group (both P < 0.05). Combination therapy was an independent protective factor for survival (HR = 0.520, 95% CI 0.310–0.874, P = 0.012), while CNLC stage IIIa was an independent risk factor (HR = 2.062, 95% CI 1.210–3.514, P = 0.008). The Ki-67 positivity rate and PD-L1 expression were decreased, CD8⁺ T cell infiltration was increased in the combination group after treatment, while the above indicators did not show significant changes in the conventional group.ConclusionIn this retrospective study, compared with TACE alone, TACE combined with targeted immunotherapy significantly reduced tumor marker levels, enhanced immune function, improved liver function, and prolonged survival time in patients with intermediate-advanced liver cancer, demonstrating better clinical efficacy. However, given the heterogeneity of the targeted drug regimens in the combination group, the generalizability of this conclusion requires further validation through prospective randomized controlled studies with standardized protocols.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12938-026-01604-8.

Introduction

Liver cancer is currently the most common type of malignant tumor in the digestive system, which can invade the entire liver tissue of patients and seriously endanger their life safety [1, 2]. Early-stage liver cancer can be treated with anti-cancer therapy or surgical resection, and the prognosis is generally favorable. However, the condition is no longer suitable for surgical removal upon diagnosis for most patients with advanced-stage liver cancer [3].

Transcatheter arterial chemoembolization (TACE) refers to the process of injecting embolic agents and high concentration of chemotherapy drugs through a catheter into the target artery that supplies blood to the tumor, causing the target arteries to become blocked and resulting in ischemic necrosis of the tumor tissue [4]. Clinical practice has revealed that the indications for TACE are extensive, and the postoperative adverse reactions are relatively few. However, some patients may experience serious complications such as liver dysfunction and liver failure after embolization [5]. Therefore, it is necessary to provide corresponding adjuvant therapy on the basis of TACE to further improve the treatment effect of advanced liver cancer. Small molecule targeted immunotherapy drugs such as lenvatinib mesylate and bevacizumab, which are first-line anti-angiogenic drugs, can inhibit tumor angiogenesis and suppress tumor growth [6]. Although studies have shown that TACE combined with targeted immunotherapy had a synergistic anti-tumor effect, there are differences in the design between different studies, such as inclusion criteria, treatment regimens, follow-up time, etc., which may lead to deviation. For example, the differences in staging directly affect tumor burden, degree of vascular invasion, and prognosis, leading to biases in the assessment of disease progression rate and treatment response. Some studies included patients with stage B2 Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) [7, 8], while others might include cases with stage C or higher [9]. In addition, some studies did not exclude patients with severe liver dysfunction or portal vein thrombosis, which might confuse the efficacy analysis [10]. The existing studies on TACE combined with targeted immunotherapy are mostly reports of single-target drug combination regimens [7–9]. However, patients often receive different types of targeted drugs in real clinical practice due to factors such as drug accessibility, medical insurance policies, economic conditions, and individualized treatment needs. Single-drug studies cannot comprehensively reflect the treatment effects in the real-world, and there is a lack of comprehensive evaluation of the overall efficacy and safety of different targeted immunotherapy regimens combined with TACE.

In view of this, this research focused on the treatment mode of TACE combined with various commonly used clinical targeted and immunotherapeutic drugs in the real-world based on previous studies. By comparing the overall treatment effects of simple TACE therapy and TACE combined with different types of targeted and immunotherapeutic drugs, the aim was to further explore the efficacy of this combined treatment strategy for advanced liver cancer, as well as its impact on tumor markers, immune function and survival time. This study was based on real-world data and includes patients who have received different targeted and immunotherapy regimens such as lenvatinib, anlotinib, apatinib, and bevacizumab. in order to provide more practical evidence-based support for the clinical application of combined treatment strategies.

Comparison of survival time

The overall survival (OS) was used as the analysis indicator, with a follow-up period of 1 year, 49 patients (84.48%) in the combined group survived, while 35 patients (61.40%) in the conventional group survived. The Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that the 1-year cumulative OS rate of the combined group was higher than that of the conventional group, and the difference was statistically significant (log-rank x2 value = 6.619, P = 0.028, Fig. 1). Immunohistochemical characteristics of tumor tissues before and after treatment

Before treatment, both groups showed high Ki-67 positive rates (approximately 65% in the conventional group and about 60% in the combined group), with sparse CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and positive PD-L1 expression. Discussion

Liver cancer poses a serious threat to patients' lives and health; the surgical resection rate for patients in the advanced stage is low. The selection and optimization of treatment plans for such patients have always been a focus of clinical research. TACE is an important treatment method for advanced liver cancer. It can block the blood supply arteries to the tumor, causing the tumor to become ischemic and necrotic. It has the advantages of quick postoperative recovery and minimally invasive nature, and is widely used in clinical practice [16]. Targeted immunotherapy achieves remarkable results in the treatment of advanced liver cancer by inhibiting the key components of cancer cells [17, 18]. This study focused on patients with advanced liver cancer at stage IIb or IIIa. Unlike previous studies that focused on a single standardized targeted-immunotherapy regimen, this research compared the overall therapeutic effect of TACE combined with multiple commonly used clinical targeted-immunotherapy drugs with the efficacy of TACE alone. The aim was to provide more practical evidence-based support for the application of this combined treatment strategy in real clinical scenarios.

This study found that the proportion of patients achieving DCR and ORR in the combined group was significantly higher than that in the conventional group, indicating that TACE combined with targeted immunotherapy can improve the therapeutic effect of patients with advanced liver cancer. The results are consistent with the research findings of Liu et al. [19] and Yao et al. [20] on the combination of TACE and lenvatinib in the treatment of BCLC stage B2 liver cancer, both of which confirmed that the short-term efficacy of the combination regimen was superior to that of TACE alone. The key difference is that the above studies mostly focused on single-target drug combination regimens. However, in this study, even when multiple immunotherapy and targeted therapy regimens were included in the combination group, a consistent trend of improved short-term efficacy was still observed. This suggests that in real clinical settings, the efficacy advantage of combination therapy may have robustness across different regimens. In terms of tumor markers, this study found that the levels of AFP, CEA, CA125, CA199, and PIVKA-II in the combined group were significantly lower than those in the conventional group, indicating that TACE combined with targeted immunotherapy can effectively reduce the tumor marker levels in patients with advanced liver cancer and delay the progression of the disease. The study by Chen et al. [21] on the application of drug loaded microspheres TACE combined with systemic chemotherapy and targeted therapy in patients with colorectal cancer liver metastasis also found that combination therapy can more effectively reduce tumor marker levels and inhibit tumor progression. On this basis, this study expanded the observation scope from a single or a few biomarkers to five indicators including AFP, CEA, CA125, CA199 and PIVKA-II. This approach comprehensively reflected the combined regulatory effect of the combined treatment on liver cancer-related tumor markers, while previous studies mostly focused on a single or two indicators for comparison. Zhang et al. [22] also conducted a comparative study between hepatic artery infusion chemotherapy and TACE in high-risk liver cancer patients. They indicated that a reasonable combined treatment plan was conducive to better protection of liver function reserves and creates conditions for subsequent treatment. This is consistent with the trend of the protective effect of combined treatment on liver function observed in this study.

In immune function, this study found that the levels of IgA, IgG, and IgM in the combined group were significantly higher than those in the conventional group, indicating that TACE combined with targeted immunotherapy can improve the immune function of patients with advanced liver cancer. Cui et al. [23] also observed the efficacy of apatinib combined with chemotherapy in advanced non-small cell lung cancer. They found that the targeted drug combination therapy could improve the immune function indicators of patients to a certain extent. This suggested that targeted drugs have potential value in regulating anti-tumor immune responses. Liu et al. [24] also confirmed that lenvatinib combined with TACE transformation therapy can induce a strong anti-tumor immune response. However, all these studies focused on a single targeted drug combination scheme and have not yet demonstrated the consistency of this immune regulatory effect across different targeted immunotherapy schemes. In contrast, this study further provided synchronous changes in IgA, IgG, and IgM immunoglobulins in a heterogeneous cohort of commonly used clinical target immune drugs, including lenvatinib, anlotinib, apatinib, and bevacizumab. This study was the first to confirm that TACE combined with different types of target immune drugs can produce consistent humoral immune enhancement effects, providing evidence-based support for the immunomodulatory effects of combination therapy from a broader clinical practice perspective. In terms of liver function, the ALB level in the combined group was significantly higher than that in the conventional group, while the GPT and TBIL levels were significantly lower than those in the conventional group, indicating that TACE combined with targeted immunotherapy to some extent alleviated the liver function damage in patients with advanced liver cancer. Li et al. [25] only reported the protective effect of TACE combined with apatinib monotherapy on liver function. This study further verified that this liver function protection effect holds true in both TKIs and anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody drugs with different mechanisms of action, supporting that TACE combined with multiple targeted-therapy regimens has good liver safety. In survival benefits, the 1-year cumulative survival rate of patients in the combined treatment group was higher than that of the control group (84.48% vs 61.40%, log-rank P = 0.028), indicating that TACE combined with targeted immunotherapy can prolong the survival time of patients with advanced liver cancer. Cai et al. [26] also found that combined with targeted immunotherapy on the basis of TACE could improve the survival rate of patients with intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. Different from previous studies that mainly focused on a single targeted immunotherapy regimen, the survival benefit observed in this study was in a heterogeneous regimen containing multiple targeted drugs, and potential confounding factors were controlled through PSM and Cox multivariate regression analysis, thereby enhancing the credibility of this finding.

Regarding the synergistic effect mechanism of TACE combined with targeted immunotherapy, TACE achieves this by perfusing chemotherapy drugs into the arteries and occluding the blood supply arteries of the tumor, causing ischemic necrosis of the tumor tissue and releasing tumor antigens, thereby triggering the body's anti-tumor immune response [27]. This creates favorable conditions for subsequent targeted immunotherapy. Secondly, among the targeted drugs, lenvatinib can inhibit the proliferation of vascular endothelial cells and the activity of tyrosine kinases, blocking tumor angiogenesis and signal transduction [28]. Anlotinib, as a multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitor, can comprehensively inhibit multiple angiogenesis related targets such as VEGFR-2/3, FGFR, PDGFR α/β, and inhibit the formation of blood vessels around the tumor [29]. Apatinib can compete for the ATP binding site of VEGFR-2, inhibit downstream signal transduction, and alleviate damage to normal liver tissue [30]. Thirdly, the immunotherapy drug bevacizumab can bind to the PD-1 receptors on the surface of immune cells, blocking the release of the PD-L1 signaling molecules by tumor cells, thereby enabling immune cells to regain their normal function of killing cancer cells [31]. The tumor hypoxic microenvironment caused by TACE may enhance the sensitivity of target immune drugs to tumor cells, while target immune therapy can inhibit angiogenesis in residual tumors after TACE and activate T lymphocyte subsets, forming a complementary synergistic effect and jointly enhancing anti-tumor effects [32]. However, it is necessary to point out that the above mechanism speculation is mainly based on existing literature and indirect evidence. This study cannot provide direct verification data for the mechanism at present, and it needs to be confirmed through molecular biology experiments in the future.

As a retrospective clinical observation, this study did not design in vitro or in vivo experiments regarding the potential mechanism of TACE combined with targeted immunotherapy to improve efficacy; therefore, direct mechanism evidence cannot be provided. Based on the existing research results, it is speculated that the synergistic effect of combined therapy may involve the following processes: after TACE treatment, the tumor tissue experiences ischemia and hypoxia, which may upregulate the expression of PD-L1 on the surface of tumor cells and promote the release of tumor antigens, creating a favorable tumor microenvironment for subsequent immunotherapy [33]. The multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as lenvatinib can inhibit the vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) and fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) pathways, thereby reducing tumor angiogenesis, lowering tumor stromal pressure, improving immune cell infiltration, and enhancing the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors [34]. Bevacizumab can normalize tumor blood vessels by neutralizing VEGF, facilitating the delivery of immune effect cells and chemotherapy drugs [35]. The above mechanisms are all inferred from literature and require further verification through prospective studies combined with analysis of the immune microenvironment of tumor tissues before and after treatment (such as multiplex immunofluorescence and transcriptome sequencing).

This study aimed to assess the potential impact of heterogeneity of targeted drugs within the combination group on the results. Therefore, the combination group was further divided into TKIs subgroups and anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody subgroups for exploratory analysis. The results showed that there were no significant differences in ORR, DCR and 1-year survival rate between the two subgroups, and there was also no significant difference in the incidence of grade 3 and above adverse events. This finding initially indicates that when different types of targeted drugs are combined with TACE, their short-term efficacy and safety may be consistent. This supports the rationality of evaluating "TACE combined with targeted immunotherapy" as an overall strategy in real-world settings. Given that this study was a non-randomized retrospective design, to reduce the bias caused by the baseline imbalance between groups, this study employed 1:1 PSM and Cox multivariate regression analysis to conduct a sensitivity analysis of the results. After PSM matching, the DCR and 1-year cumulative survival rate of the combined group were still significantly better than those of the conventional group, and Cox regression further showed that receiving combined treatment was an independent protective factor for improving patient survival. The above analysis, after controlling for known prognostic factors such as age, gender, tumor number, tumor diameter, and CNLC staging, showed that the survival benefits of combination therapy remained robust. This statistically enhanced the internal validity of the conclusions of this study and was consistent with previous reports by Cai et al. [26] on the improvement of survival through combination therapy. However, PSM can only balance the distribution of measured variables but cannot eliminate the influence of unmeasured confounding factors. The conclusion still needs to be confirmed by a prospective randomized trial. Furthermore, the IHC analysis in this study further confirmed the biological effects of the combined treatment at the tissue level: the Ki-67 positive rate in the combined treatment group after treatment was significantly decreased, indicating that the tumor proliferation activity was effectively inhibited, and the infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells was increased, suggesting that the anti-tumor immune response was activated, and the expression of PD-L1 was decreased, indicating that the immunosuppressive microenvironment was to some extent reversed. The above changes were not significant in the conventional group, providing direct protein level evidence for the efficacy of TACE combined with target immunotherapy.

Although this study observed that TACE combined with targeted immunotherapy demonstrated advantages over TACE alone in terms of short-term efficacy, tumor markers, immune function, and short-term survival, the results should be critically evaluated within the context of existing literature. Firstly, Chen et al. [7]'s multi-center study reported that the ORR of TACE combined with lenvatinib and tislelizumab in treating intermediate-stage liver cancer was 58.3%, which was similar to that of our study (55.17%). However, their study excluded patients with portal vein invasion (CNLC stage IIIa), while the proportion of stage IIIa patients included in our study was approximately 30%, suggesting that the combined treatment can still achieve similar efficacy in more advanced populations. Secondly, the PSM study by Cai et al. [8] showed that the DCR of TACE combined with atezolizumab + bevacizumab was 82.4%, which was slightly lower than 87.93% in our study. This might be related to the fact that some patients in our study received TKIs monotherapy combined with other drugs instead of PD-1 inhibitors, suggesting that the efficacy of different targeted-antibody combinations may vary. On the other hand, Liu et al. [9] reported that the ORR of TACE combined with apatinib in treating BCLC C stage liver cancer was only 27.8%, which was significantly lower than 52.5% in the TKIs subgroup of this study. This difference might be related to the different baseline liver function (in this study, only about 23% were Child–Pugh B grade), tumor burden and previous treatment history of the enrolled population. The above comparison indicated that although the efficacy data of this study were consistent with those in most previous literature, the results cannot be directly generalized to other treatment regimens or populations due to differences in inclusion criteria, combination of targeted and immunotherapeutic drugs, and staging systems. Furthermore, this study included multiple targeted drugs in the combined group. Although subgroup analysis confirmed that there was no significant difference in efficacy among different types of drugs, it still cannot rule out the potential influence of drug selection bias (such as economic conditions, medical insurance policies, etc.) on the results, which is an inherent limitation of retrospective studies.

In summary, in the comparison of this retrospective study, TACE combined with targeted immunotherapy showed better clinical efficacy compared to TACE alone. It could significantly reduce the tumor marker levels in patients with advanced liver cancer, enhance the immune function of patients, improve liver function, and help prolong the survival time of patients. Moreover, the clinical efficacy was high, and it has clinical promotion value.

This study has the following limitations. Firstly, it is a single-center retrospective design: the groups were not randomly assigned. Although PSM and Cox regression were used to control for measured confounding factors such as age, gender, tumor burden, and CNLC stage, it was impossible to eliminate the selection bias caused by unmeasured confounding factors (such as socioeconomic status, treatment compliance, drug dosage adjustment and intensity, and differences in supportive treatment, etc.), which limited the strength of causal inference. Secondly, the heterogeneity of the combined treatment regimen: the combined group included four different targeted immunotherapy drugs (TKI class and anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody class). Although the subgroup analysis showed no significant difference in efficacy and safety between the TKI subgroup and the anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody subgroup, the sample size of this subgroup analysis was small (40 cases for TKIs and 18 cases for anti-VEGF monoclonal antibodies), resulting in insufficient statistical power and inability to rule out potential differences among different TKI drugs. In addition, the specific reasons for patients receiving targeted drugs (such as medical insurance reimbursement, doctor preferences, patient economic status) were not recorded in detail in this study, and the bias introduced by treatment plan heterogeneity was difficult to completely eliminate, which reduces the reproducibility and extrapolation of the results. Thirdly, the follow-up period was relatively short: the median follow-up was only 12 months, and the longest was 21 months. This resulted in insufficient assessment of long-term survival benefits (such as 2-year and 3-year survival rates) and long-term adverse events (such as delayed hypertension, proteinuria, immune-related endocrine toxicity, etc.). In addition, the lack of immune microenvironment analysis of tumor tissue specimens before and after treatment (such as CD8⁺ T cell infiltration density, PD-L1 expression, Treg cell ratio, etc.) makes it impossible to verify whether combination therapy indeed enhances anti-tumor immune response at the mechanistic level, which is also the core limitation of this study that fails to provide direct biological evidence. Fourthly, this study was a retrospective design and failed to systematically collect and organize typical imaging images (enhanced CT/MRI) before and after treatment that were comparable. It was unable to visually display tumor volume changes, necrosis, and efficacy evaluation process in the form of imaging images, which to some extent weakened the visual evidence of efficacy conclusions. The research team will systematically collect standardized imaging data before and after the treatment in the subsequent prospective studies to support the conclusion of tumor regression or effective treatment.

Future research should be conducted in the following aspects in depth. (1) Conduct multi-center prospective randomized controlled trials, using a standardized TACE combined with targeted immunotherapy regimen (such as TACE + lenvatinib + PD-1 inhibitor), and set up TACE alone and targeted immunotherapy alone as controls to clearly determine the net benefit of the combined treatment and the optimal applicable population. (2) Exploring biomarkers for predicting therapeutic efficacy: based on pre-treatment tumor biopsies or liquid biopsies (ctDNA, circulating tumor cells), analyze genomic characteristics (such as TP53 mutation status, activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway) and the relationship between tumor mutation burden and therapeutic efficacy to achieve precise stratification and identify patients most likely to benefit from combined treatment. (3) Perform immune micro-environment analysis of paired tumor tissues before and after treatment: through multiple immunofluorescence or single-cell sequencing, evaluate changes in indicators such as CD8⁺ T cells, Tregs, tumor-associated macrophages, and PD-L1 expression, directly verifying whether TACE combined with targeted immunity truly reverses the immunosuppressive state, and supporting the efficacy observation from the mechanism level. (4) Extend follow-up time, focus on long-term survival (3 years, 5 years) and delayed adverse events, and conduct pharmacoeconomic evaluation to assess the cost-effectiveness ratio of combination therapy, providing a basis for clinical decision-making and medical insurance policies.

Materials and methods

General materials

A retrospective selection was made of 115 patients with advanced liver cancer who were treated in our hospital. The selection period was from June 2021 to February 2024. The inclusion process is shown in Fig. 3. According to the actual treatment plan received by the patients, those who received TACE treatment were included in the conventional group (57 cases), and those who received TACE combined with target immunotherapy were included in the combination group (58 cases). The grouping was not randomized and was determined by clinical doctors based on the patient's condition, treatment guidelines, and patient wishes. It was a non-randomized controlled retrospective design. The conventional group consisted of 30 males and 27 females, aged 43–79 years, with an average age of (53.79 ± 4.91) years and a body mass index (BMI) of (20.79 ± 1.52) kg/m2. The conventional group were made up of 32 cases of single tumors and 25 cases of multiple tumors, with tumor diameters ranging from 3-15 cm, an average diameter of (7.16 ± 2.50) cm, and the median diameter of the largest tumor of 6.8 cm. The combined group consisted of 32 males and 26 females, aged 45 to 80 years, with an average age of (52.88 ± 5.12) years and a BMI of (21.33 ± 1.48) kg/m2. There were 34 cases of single tumors and 24 cases of multiple tumors in combined group, with tumor diameters ranging from 3 to 16 cm, an average diameter of (7.25 ± 2.45) cm, and the median diameter of the largest tumor of 7.0 cm. Both groups of patients were all newly diagnosed cases and had not received TACE, targeted therapy, immunotherapy or liver resection before. Patients who had relapsed after undergoing radical surgical resection, or those who had undergone local ablation or radiotherapy were excluded. In terms of the Child–Pugh classification of liver function, there were 45 cases of grade A and 13 cases of grade B in the combined group, while in the conventional group, there were 43 cases of grade A and 14 cases of grade B. There was no significant difference between the two groups (χ2 = 0.074, P = 0.794). There was no significant difference in gender, age, tumor number, tumor diameter, and BMI between the two groups (P > 0.05). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital.Fig. 3Patient inclusion process diagram

Patient inclusion process diagram

To assess the potential impact of different targeted drugs on the results, the combined group was divided into two subgroups based on the mechanism and category of the targeted drugs: lenvatinib, anlotinib, and apatinib are all small molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and were classified into the TKIs subgroup (n = 40). Bevacizumab is a monoclonal antibody against vascular endothelial growth factor. Its mechanism of action is different from that of TKIs, and it was classified separately as an anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody subgroup (n = 18). The objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR) and 1-year survival rate were compared between the TKIs subgroup and the anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody subgroup. The treatment-related adverse events for both subgroups were reported separately.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) patients met the diagnostic criteria for liver cancer [11] and had been confirmed as liver cancer through imaging; (2) the patients were stage IIb or IIIa based on the China liver cancer (CNLC) staging system [12]; CNLC stage IIb was defined as the number of intrahepatic tumors ≥ 4 without vascular invasion or extrahepatic metastasis, and CNLC stage IIIa was defined as the presence of vascular invasion without extrahepatic metastasis; (3) following the modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST) [13], at least one measurable lesion was included; (4) the patient's clinical data were complete; and (5) the patient had a good mental condition and cognitive function. Exclusion criteria: (1) patients who were allergic to the drugs used in this study; (2) patients with severe primary diseases of the brain, heart, and kidneys; (3) patients with infectious diseases; (4) patients with other malignant tumors or metastatic tumors; (5) patients with secondary liver cancer; (6) patients had previously received any systemic treatment (including targeted therapy, immunotherapy or chemotherapy) or local treatment (including TACE, hepatic artery infusion chemotherapy, ablation, and radiotherapy) for hepatocellular carcinoma; and (7) patients with liver function classified as Child–Pugh C grade.

Methods

The conventional group received TACE treatment. Local anesthesia was administered in the patient’s femoral artery, and the groin area was thoroughly disinfected. The femoral artery was punctured by a puncture needle, with a sheath tube setting. Under X-ray fluoroscopy, the catheter was guided to the hepatic artery and contrast agent was injected through the catheter. The location, size, and quantity of the lesion were observed through imaging equipment, and the course of the hepatic artery and its branches were clearly identified. Subsequently, super emulsified ethyl iodide oil, gelatin sponge, microspheres, etc., were injected to block the nutrient vessels of the lesion, causing the tumor cells to die due to lack of nutrition. TACE treatment should be performed by attending physicians or above with at least 5 years of operating experience.

The combined group received targeted immunotherapy on the basis of TACE. Based on the actual clinical medication situation, the patients were, respectively, administered one of the following treatment regimens: (1) Lenvatinib Mesylate Capsules (produced by Xiansheng Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Standard No. H20213599, Specification: 4 mg) was taken orally. The dosage was calculated based on body weight. For patients with a body weight of less than 60 kg should receive a dose of 8 mg/d, for patients with a body weight of ≥ 60 kg should receive a dose of 12 mg/d, once a day; (2) Anlotinib Hydrochloride Capsules (produced by Zhengda Tianqing Pharmaceutical Group Co., Ltd., Standard No.: H20180004, Specification: 12 mg) was taken orally, 12 mg/time, once a day; (3) Apatinib Mesylate Tablets (Jiangsu Hengrui Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., National Standard No. H20140103, Specification: 0.25 g) was taken orally, 0.5 g/time, once a day; and (4) Bevacizumab (produced by Xinda Biopharmaceutical (Suzhou) Co., Ltd., National Pharmaceutical Standard: S20200013, Specification: 4 ml) was taken by intravenous infusion, 3w IU/once.

Outcome measures

Clinical efficacy: the mRECIST score [14] was used to compare the clinical efficacy of the two groups of patients before and after treatment. The evaluation criteria were listed as follows. Progressive disease (PD): the total diameter of the lesion increased by more than 20% or new lesions appeared. Stable disease (SD): the total diameter of the lesion had decreased by less than 30% or increased by less than 20%. Partial response (PR): the diameter of the lesion decreased by more than 30%. Complete response (CR): the lesion disappeared. Objective response rate (ORR) = PR + CR, disease control rate (DCR) = SD + PR + CR.

Tumor markers: the levels of tumor markers between two groups were compared on the first day after admission and the day following the last treatment. 5 ml of fasting blood was extracted from the patient at 7:00 a.m. and was centrifuged using a high-speed refrigerated centrifuge (model: 5810R, produced by Eppendorf, Germany) at 3500 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was collected and stored in a -20℃ freezer for testing the levels of alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125), and carbohydrate antigen 199 (CA199). Abnormal prothrombin (PIVKA-II) was detected using Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Wuhan Merck Biotechnology Co., Ltd), following the manufacturer's instructions strictly.

Immune function: immune function of two groups was compared on the first day after admission and the day after the last treatment. 5 ml of fasting blood was extracted from the patient at 7:00 a.m. and was centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was collected and stored in a −20 ℃ freezer for testing immunoglobulin A (IgA), immunoglobulin G (IgG) and immunoglobulin M (IgM).

Liver function indicators: the liver function indicators of two groups were compared on the first day after admission and the day after the last treatment. 5 ml of fasting blood was extracted from the patient at 7:00 a.m. and was centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was collected and stored in a − 20℃ freezer for testing serum albumin (ALB), alanine aminotransferase (GPT), total bilirubin (TBIL) using ELISA method.

Survival time: the prognosis of the two groups of patients was compared. The overall survival period (OS) is defined as the period from the date of the first TACE treatment to death due to any cause or the date of the last follow-up. The follow-up methods included outpatient visits, medical record inquiries, and telephone follow-ups. The follow-up deadline was February 28, 2025. The median follow-up period was 12 months (ranging from 3 to 21 months). The survival curve was plotted using Kaplan–Meier analysis, and the log-rank test was used for inter-group comparison.

The adverse events that occurred during and after the treatment of the two groups of patients were recorded. The adverse events were classified according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0 of the National Cancer Institute of the United States [15]. This standard divided the severity of adverse events into 1 to 5 levels: Level 1 was mild (asymptomatic or mild symptoms, no intervention required), Level 2 was moderate (requiring minor, local or non-invasive intervention), Level 3 was severe (medically significant but not immediately life-threatening, requiring hospitalization or prolonged hospital stay), Level 4 was life-threatening (requiring emergency intervention), and Level 5 was adverse event-related death. The included events were not limited to: post-embolization syndrome (fever, pain, nausea, vomiting), liver function impairment (increased transaminase, elevated bilirubin), hypertension, proteinuria, hand-foot skin reaction, diarrhea, fatigue, bone marrow suppression, etc. The incidence rates of adverse events of grade 3 and above were compared in the two groups.

Tumor tissue immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis: tumor tissue samples were obtained from patients in the conventional group and the combination group before the first TACE treatment (biopsy) and within one week after the last treatment (surgery or biopsy). After being fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin, the specimen was continuously sliced into 4 μ m thick sections. After dewaxing with xylene and gradient ethanol hydration, the slices were subjected to microwave antigen repair using sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and blocked with 10% goat serum at room temperature for 30 min. The following primary antibodies were separately added: rabbit anti-human Ki-67 monoclonal antibody (dilution ratio 1:200, Abcam, ab16667), rabbit anti-human CD8 monoclonal antibody (dilution ratio 1:100, Abcam, ab101500), rabbit anti-human PD-L1 monoclonal antibody (dilution ratio 1:200, Abcam, ab213524), and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The next day, HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (dilution ratio 1:500, Abcam, ab205718) was added and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. It was stained by DAB and counterstained by hematoxylin, dehydration and transparency treatment followed by neutral gum mounting. For each slice, 5 high-power fields (× 200) were randomly selected under the microscope, and were independently evaluated by two pathologists. The Ki-67 positive rate was expressed as the percentage of positive cell nuclei. CD8-positive T cells were indicated by the density of positive cells in the stroma and tumor nests (number of cells per high-power field). PD-L1 positivity was defined as the presence of brownish-yellow granules on the tumor cell membrane or cytoplasm, and a positive cell proportion of ≥ 1% was considered positive expression.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 statistical software was applied to analyze the data. Measurement data that conformed to normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. T-tests were conducted between the two groups, and count data were expressed as n/percentage. Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test were performed between the two groups. The survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan–Meier method, and the log-rank test was used for comparisons between groups. Cox proportional hazards regression model was used for multivariate survival analysis, including variables such as age, gender, number of tumors, tumor diameter, CNLC stage, treatment plan, and Child–Pugh liver function grading, to control for the influence of confounding factors on survival outcomes. To reduce the selection bias in retrospective studies, gender, age, CNLC stage, number of tumors, and tumor diameter were used as covariates. A 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM, with a cutoff value of 0.02) was employed for a sensitivity analysis to compare the efficacy and survival differences between the two matched groups. All tests were two-tailed. P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.
