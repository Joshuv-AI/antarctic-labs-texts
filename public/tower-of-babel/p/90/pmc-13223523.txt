Antimicrobial resistance and genetic characterization of Clostridium perfringens isolated from companion animals in South Korea, 2018–2024
Authors: Ji-Hyun Choi, Md Sekendar Ali, Yeon-Hee Lee, Hee-Seung Kang, Seung-Chun Park, Bo-Youn Moon, Suk-Kyung Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClostridium perfringens is an emerging pathogenic bacterium associated with various gastrointestinal diseases in humans and animals. We aimed to investigate the prevalence, antimicrobial resistance, and molecular characteristics of C. perfringens isolated from companion animals. A total of 743C. perfringens isolates were recovered from diarrheal feces of dogs (n = 516) and cats (n = 227) in South Korea between 2018 and 2024. The majority of isolates were resistant to tetracycline (58.1%) and clindamycin (11.3%). Nonetheless, ampicillin, cefotetan, cefoxitin, chloramphenicol, metronidazole, penicillin, and piperacillin resistance were low (0.1–6.2%). Toxinotyping showed that 97.4% of C. perfringens belong to type A, comprising the toxin gene cpa, and 1.7% to type F, encompassing cpa and cpe. Multi-locus sequence typing of 116C. perfringens isolates resistant to metronidazole, penicillin, or clindamycin revealed that ST102 (11.1%) was the most consistently prevalent among 82 STs, clustering in seven clonal complexes. Notably, we identified the optrA-encoding plasmid in companion animals for the first time. The plasmids harbor various antimicrobial resistance genes, including those for phenicols, macrolides, tetracyclines, and lincomycins. Transposable elements IS1216E and ISEfa5 were detected in the plasmids, facilitating the dissemination of resistance. Taken together, C. perfringens carrying resistance and toxin genes could pose human health hazards.Keywords: Clostridium perfringens, Companion animals, Toxinotype, Sequence type, Clonal complex, Resistance gene

Introduction

Clostridium perfringens is a Gram-positive spore-forming anaerobic bacterium that resides in the gut flora of humans and other animals [1]. It is responsible for several life-threatening diseases, including necrotic enteritis, enterotoxemia, and hemorrhagic gastroenteritis [2]. The pathogenicity of C. perfringens is reinforced by the presence of one or more toxins, including α (CPA), β (CPB), ε (ETX), and ι (IAP) [3]. Due to the high prevalence of virulent strains, human infection with C. perfringens has become more severe in recent years [4].

Companion animals, such as dogs and cats, infected by C. perfringens can experience mild diarrhea to deadly necrotic enteritis [5]. The prevalence of C. perfringens and its toxin profiles in humans and companion animals has been described in different countries [2], [6], [7], [8], [9]. Moreover, it was revealed that C. perfringens isolated from humans and companion animals shares common genetic traits [10].

Several antimicrobials, including metronidazole, tetracycline, macrolides, and β-lactams, are used to treat diarrhea in companion animals caused by C. perfringens [11]. However, overuse and/or misuse of the antimicrobials trigger the development of antimicrobial resistance. Furthermore, these resistances can be spread to humans through clonal and horizontal transmission, making treatment challenging and increasing morbidity and mortality [12], [13].

The occurrence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) C. perfringens in companion animals is frequently reported globally, including in Japan [14], Spain [15], and the USA [16]. Additionally, resistance mechanisms in C. perfringens that harbor corresponding resistance genes conferring resistance to tetracyclines, macrolides, lincosamides, streptogramins, chloramphenicol, and bacitracin have been described [17], [18].

Moreover, resistance to oxazolidinones in C. perfringens has been found to result from a mutation in the chromosomal rplD gene, which encodes ribosomal protein L4 [19]. OptrA is an ATP-binding cassette (ABC)-F protein that mediates oxazolidinone resistance through ribosomal protection, often conferring co-resistance to phenicols. This gene is frequently found on mobile genetic elements such as plasmids or transposons, enabling its horizontal transfer between bacteria, particularly enterococci [20]. Previous research has reported the presence of optrA in C. perfringens and other facultative pathogenic bacteria, from human clinical and food-animal sources [21], [22], [23], [24]. Given that optrA is frequently located on mobile genetic elements, its existence in companion animal C. perfringens raises the possibility of clonal and horizontal gene transmission within the gut microbiota and across bacterial species in humans and other animals. Because companion animals live in close contact with humans, they may serve as reservoirs and vectors for the dissemination of this transferable oxazolidinone resistance gene, posing potential public health risks.

In South Korea, a few investigations have described the prevalence of MDR C. perfringens in dogs and cats [2], [25]. Furthermore, these studies investigated the comparatively limited number of isolates collected from a few particular areas over a short period. Additionally, the detailed genetic characterization of these isolates is still scarce. A comprehensive investigation into the prevalence of C. perfringens in companion animals, and the assessment of its antimicrobial resistance through phenotypic and genotypic examination, can elucidate the role of resistance transfer. Thus, we aimed to determine the antimicrobial resistance profiles and molecular characterization by identifying key molecular markers associated with pathogenicity, virulence, and resistance in C. perfringens isolated from dogs and cats nationwide in South Korea from 2018 to 2024.

Materials and methods

Isolation and identification of C. perfringens

We isolated and identified C. perfringens strains following the protocols described in the previous study [2]. The diarrheal fecal samples were collected by gently rotating the sterile cotton swab in the rectum of dogs and cats. The sample was inoculated in tryptose sulfite cycloserine (TSC; Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) medium and then incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. The subculture was streaked onto the TSC agar plate, followed by incubation overnight at 37 °C in an anaerobic condition to collect characteristic black colonies. The selected colonies were further confirmed as C. perfringens using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time-of-flight mass spectrometry (Bruker Corporation, MA, USA). A total of 743C. perfringens isolates (dogs, n = 516; cats, n = 227) were obtained from 175 veterinary hospitals participating in the Korean Veterinary Antimicrobial Monitoring System in 7 metropolitan cities from 2018 to 2024 (Fig. 1).

Geographic locations of Clostridium perfringens isolate collection hospitals in various cities of South Korea.

Antimicrobial susceptibility test

The antimicrobial susceptibility of C. perfringens against amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, ampicillin/sulbactam, cefotetan, cefoxitin, chloramphenicol, clindamycin, imipenem, meropenem, metronidazole, mezlocillin, penicillin, piperacillin, piperacillin/tazobactam, and tetracycline was performed by the broth microdilution method using ANO2B plates (Sensititre; Trek Diagnostics, Cleveland, USA). The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined according to the guidelines established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2023 [26]. C. difficile ATCC 700057 served as the control strain. MDR was defined as the isolates demonstrating resistance to three or more classes of antimicrobials [27]. Isolates resistant to metronidazole, penicillin, or clindamycin were selected for further characterization. Among the 743C. perfringens isolates analyzed, a total of 116 isolates that were identified as resistant to at least one of the clinically important antimicrobials, namely metronidazole, penicillin, or clindamycin, were selected for multi-locus sequence typing (MLST).

Detection of toxin gene

The extracted genomic DNA was subjected to polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification to detect toxin genes in C. perfringens using the previously described method [28]. A 20 μL PCR reaction mixture containing PCR premix (iNtRON Biotechnology, South Korea), 50 ng of template DNA, and 500 nM of each primer was used for amplification. The PCR conditions and primer sequences used to amplify the target toxin genes, such as α (cpa), β (cpb), ε (etx), ι (iap), entero (cpe), β2 (cpb2), and netB in C. perfringens, are shown in Supplementary Table S1. Eight microliters of amplified PCR products were loaded onto a 1.5% agarose gel, and the bands were visualized under UV light after staining with ethidium bromide.

Multi-locus sequence typing

To determine the clonal relationship of C. perfringens, MLST was carried out using the previously described methodology [29]. The following housekeeping genes were amplified by PCR and sequenced in this classical scheme: colA, groEL, sodA, plc, gyrB, sigK, pgk, and nadA. PCR was conducted on 20 ng of extracted genomic DNA as the template in a 30 μL reaction mixture with DNA polymerase (Bionner, Daejeon, South Korea): activation of Taq polymerase at 95 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles at 95 °C for 30 s, 52 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. After incubating the mixture at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by cooling on ice, the sequence was analyzed using an ABI Prism 3730 analyzer (SolGent, Daejeon, South Korea). We used the official website (https://pubMLST.org/cperfringens) to compare the allelic profiles and sequence types (STs) of C. perfringens based on sequencing data. The novel ST was submitted to the PubMLST database and assigned a new ST number. The STs of the C. perfringens strains were grouped using PHYLOViz software (version 2.0) based on the allelic variations among various STs, where STs differing at only one allele (single-locus variants) from the predicted founder ST were grouped in the same clonal complex (CC) [30].

Next-generation sequencing (NGS)

Genomic DNA was extracted from C. perfringens ST494 using the bacterial DNA isolation kit (SolGent, Daejeon, South Korea) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The NGS was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina Inc., CA, USA) and Oxford Nanopore sequencing platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies Ltd., Oxford, UK). Library preparation and DNA sequencing were carried out by Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, South Korea). We conducted quality control on the Illumina and Nanopore sequencing data and subsequently performed de novo assembly using Unicycler (version 0.4.8). Assembled genomes in FASTA format were uploaded to Proksee (https://proksee.ca/) and annotated using embedded annotation tools, including Prokka and Bakta. The annotated genomes were further analyzed and visualized using BRIG (BLAST Ring Image Generator) and Easyfig to compare genomic structures and sequence similarities among strains. We deposited the hybrid assembly of the isolate in the GenBank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) under the NCBI accession number SAMN56693759. Moreover, the reference genome sequences of C. perfringens strains, including QHY-2 (GeneBank accession no. CP118266.1), 21-D-5b (GeneBank accession no. CP119189.1), 2C45 (GeneBank accession no. NZ_JAAQTM010000004.1), and 19TSBNCP (GeneBank accession no. CP073071.1), were retrieved from the public database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) to compare the genetic relatedness with our isolate.

Statistical analysis

We used Rex Software (version 3.0.3, RexSoft Inc., Seoul, South Korea) for statistical analysis. The chi-square test was used to compare the antimicrobial resistance rates among groups. The p-values below 0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

Results

Antimicrobial resistance

Table 1 displays the resistance of C. perfringens to the tested antimicrobial agents. The highest resistance among the C. perfringens isolates was found for tetracycline (58.1%) and clindamycin (11.3%). However, resistance to ampicillin, cefotetan, cefoxitin, chloramphenicol, metronidazole, penicillin, and piperacillin was very low (0.1–6.2%). All C. perfringens isolates demonstrated sensitivity to amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, ampicillin/sulbactam, imipenem, meropenem, mezlocillin, piperacillin, and piperacillin/tazobactam. Among the animal species, dog isolates demonstrated significantly greater resistance to clindamycin (13% vs. 7.5%), metronidazole (2.3% vs. 0%), and tetracycline (61.8% vs. 49.8%) than cats (p < 0.05). Nevertheless, the ampicillin resistance rate (7.9% vs 3.1%) was higher in cat isolates compared to those from dogs.

Multidrug resistance and resistance patterns

It was observed that 65.3% of the isolates obtained from dogs and 53.3% from cats exhibited resistance to one or more antimicrobial agents (Table 4). Specifically, MDR was detected in 4.3% of the dog isolates and 6.2% of the cat isolates (Table 1). In terms of the resistance pattern, tetracycline resistance was often detected in both dog (48.6%) and cat (38.3%) isolates. Additionally, tetracycline resistance in combination with clindamycin was identified in both dog (9.1%) and cat (2.6%) isolates. Interestingly, one dog isolate was resistant to chloramphenicol, clindamycin, and tetracycline.

Toxin gene profile

The toxin genes identified in C. perfringens are presented in Table 5. Among the seven different toxins, three toxins were observed. Most of the C. perfringens isolates (97.4%) were type A in both dogs and cats. Furthermore, one and two isolates were toxin E and toxin F, respectively. Nonetheless, toxins B, C, D, and G were not detected.

Multi-locus sequence typing analysis

A comprehensive multilocus sequence typing (MLST) analysis was performed on a total of 116 C. perfringens isolates, which resolved into 82 distinct STs (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table S2). The isolates demonstrated notable differences in diversity across host species. Specifically, 88 isolates obtained from dogs were distributed across a highly diverse pool of 71 STs, whereas the 28 isolates from cats belonged to only 15 STs.

Analysis of the ST distribution showed a high level of strain variability. ST102 was the most prevalent genotype, accounting for 13 isolates (11.2%) of the total collection. An additional twelve STs collectively comprised 28.4% of the isolates: ST502 (n = 5), ST1079 (n = 4), ST952 (n = 3), ST1088 (n = 4), ST62 (n = 3), ST72 (n = 2), ST182 (n = 2), ST333 (n = 2), ST420 (n = 2), ST461 (n = 2), ST511 (n = 2), and ST629 (n = 2). The remaining 68 STs were each represented by a single isolate, underscoring the high genotypic diversity observed. Furthermore, the analysis identified 32 novel STs with no prior record in public MLST databases.

The trends analysis showed that the 82 identified STs appeared sporadically across the study years (Supplementary Fig. S3). Notably, ST102, the most frequently distributed type, has been consistently detected in dogs since its emergence in 2020 through 2023. In cats, ST102 first appeared in 2021 and persisted through 2023. Other sequence types, such as ST1088 and ST62 in dogs, and ST502 and ST1079 in cats, occurred more sporadically.

The genetic relatedness among the dog isolates was visualized using an eBURST analysis based on the 71 unique STs identified in the 88 dog isolates. The eBURST grouped the isolates into seven clonal complexes (CC1–CC7), but importantly, no single CC was identified as predominant.

The majority of the STs displayed minimal clonal structure: 67 of the 82 STs (81.7%) were confirmed as singletons, meaning they did not belong to any defined CC. Only 15 STs (18.4%) were part of a clonal complex. CC1 was the largest complex, comprising three STs (ST502, ST1079, ST1088), while the other six CCs each encompassed two STs. Examination of the host distribution revealed that while both dogs and cats maintained their own unique STs, four STs (ST502, ST102, ST511, and ST333) were notably shared between the dog and cat populations.

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) of MDR C. perfringens

We performed NGS of the MDR ST494 strain isolated from a diarrheic dog in 2023 (Fig. 3). The isolate was resistant to chloramphenicol (MIC, 32 μg/mL), clindamycin (MIC, >8 μg/mL), and tetracycline (MIC, >8 μg/mL) (Supplementary Table S3).

Circular plasmid mapping of the ST494 Clostridium perfringens.

We identified five plasmids (Supplementary Table S4). Among them, plasmid 1 (pCP51, GeneBank accession no. SAMN56693759), approximately 66 kbp in size, carried the optrA gene. In addition, this plasmid carries the resistance genes fexA, ermA, and ANT(9)-la, as well as transfer elements, including insertion sequence (IS) factors, and conjugation-related genes trbC and virB4. Plasmid 2 was found to contain the resistance genes tetB(P) and tetA(P), as well as the toxin gene cpb2. This plasmid also carries the conjugation-related genes tcpH, tcpG, tcpF, tcpE, and tcpD. No antimicrobial resistance, toxin, or mobile genes in plasmids 3, 4, 5, and 6. The chromosomal DNA was found to contain the toxin A gene, lincosamide resistance gene lnu(P), and the antimicrobial peptide gene mprF, which is specific to C. perfringens.

A BLAST analysis of the full sequence of pCP51 showed the highest similarity to an optrA-harboring C. perfringens plasmid isolated from cattle in China (19TSBNCP), with a query cover of 99% and an identity of 99.9% (Fig. 4). Furthermore, a BLAST analysis of the surrounding region of the optrA gene on pCP51 showed high identity with sequences from isolates QHY-2 (97% query cover, 100% identity), 21-D-5b (97% query cover, 99.8% identity), and 19TSBNCP (96% query cover, 100% identity). The presence of the resistance genes fexA and emrA, which are co-located with optrA and flanked by IS elements (IS1216E and ISEfa5). The optrA environment was similar in size and coding sequence, with numerous plasmids, including pQHY-2, p21-D-5b, p2C45, and 19TSBNCP, obtained from sheep, goat, chicken, and cattle, respectively (Fig. 4).

Discussion

In this investigation, we found a significant proportion of C. perfringens obtained from diarrheic dogs and cats demonstrated resistance to at least one antimicrobial. Moreover, a majority of C. perfringens contains CPA, and the specific clone ST102 was predominantly detected in multidrug-resistant C. perfringens. In note, resistance gene optrA, mobile genetic elements IS1216E and ISEfa5-harboring plasmid found in a dog for the first time.

In this study, C. perfringens isolates recovered from dogs (61.8%) and cats (49.8%) showed a high tetracycline resistance rate (58.1%). Kather et al. [16] revealed that 62% of C. perfringens strains isolated from companion animals showed resistance to tetracycline, which is consistent with our findings. Moreover, it was higher than the report conducted by Marks and Kather [31] in the USA (21%). In addition, 11.3% of C. perfringens strains recovered from companion animals showed resistance to clindamycin, concurring with a previously published study in South Korea (21.2%) [2]. In contrast, a much higher clindamycin resistance in C. perfringens was detected in dogs and cats in Japan (50%) [32]. Resistance rates for ampicillin, cefotetan, cefoxitin, chloramphenicol, metronidazole, and penicillin were very low, ranging from 0.1% to 6.2%. Resistance to these antimicrobials may also present a substantial health risk to humans and other animals [33]. Among them, metronidazole is the preferred antimicrobial for treating C. perfringens infections in humans and companion animals [34]. However, an increase in the prevalence of metronidazole-resistant C. perfringens has been noted in both humans and animals [34], [35]. Moreover, penicillin resistance in C. perfringens was also detected in dogs and cats globally [11], including in Korea [2], which concurs with our findings. Thus, these antimicrobials should be used judiciously in treating bacterial infections in humans and animals.

The incidence of antimicrobial-resistant C. perfringens in dogs and cats by age was evaluated to understand the impact of antimicrobial resistance on host age. In this study, we found that dogs of older ages (>6 years) exhibited higher resistance to ampicillin, cefotetan, and cefoxitin. Similarly, in cat isolates, resistance to ampicillin and penicillin was detected to be higher in older ages than in younger ages. This might be due to prolonged exposure to the antimicrobial during their lifespan [36]. In our previous investigation, we observed that older companion animals showed a higher incidence of antimicrobial resistance [37]. Nonetheless, antimicrobial resistance in isolates recovered from companion animals, such as dogs and cats, exhibited inconsistent resistance dynamics in relation to age [38].

A significant proportion of C. perfringens isolates exhibited resistance to one or more antimicrobials, possessing various resistance patterns. Moreover, MDR C. perfringens exhibited resistance to three distinct classes of antimicrobials, with the occurrence of MDR in both dogs and cats. A previous study conducted in South Korea showed the prevalence of MDR C. perfringens isolates from dogs, which is consistent with our findings [2]. In addition, numerous investigations have described the occurrence of MDR C. perfringens worldwide in humans and other animals, including dogs and cats [39], [40], [41], [42]. Our investigation revealed that resistance patterns often include tetracycline and clindamycin, which are frequently used in humans, concordant with previous findings [2]. Thus, identifying MDR C. perfringens and its resistance to these antimicrobials emphasizes the importance of ongoing antimicrobial resistance monitoring in isolates recovered from dogs and cats.

In this investigation, the majority of C. perfringens strains obtained from dogs and cats were classified as toxin type A, while only a few isolates were identified as type F and type E, concurring with previously published reports [3], [13]. Similarly, most of the toxin genes were found to be cpa and cpe as well as their combination. It was shown that cpa toxin is linked to canine diarrhea [43]. In accordance with this study, the cpa gene was highly detected in C. perfringens isolated from companion animals in Korea (100%) [2], Spain (97.2%) [15], and Germany (59%) [8]. The prevalence of cpe in this study was consistent with previous reports in India (2.7%) [3]. Nevertheless, it was below those described in Brazil (10.8%) [44], Spain (17%) [15], the USA (16.3%) [14], and Canada (21%) [45]. Investigations have found a strong correlation between C. perfringens positive for cpe and the incidence of diarrhea in dogs and cats [11]. Moreover, the prevalence of toxin type F (cpa and cpe gene) detected from companion animals in India (2.7%) [3] concurs with our current study. It was revealed that cpa, in combination with cpe toxin, plays a role in fatal canine diarrhea [46].

Our findings showed that ST102 and ST502 were predominantly detected among STs, with consistent prevalence of ST102 in dogs and cats up to 2023. The scarcity of C. perfringens ST prevalence data in companion animals makes direct comparisons challenging. However, STs found in this study were reported in various sources. Only ST102 was reported in the dog, while other STs were reported in livestock and humans, but not in companion animals. A previous study identified C. perfringens ST102 in a dog from the Queensland region of Australia [47], whereas the ST502 isolate was reported from a human clinical case in China [48]. Furthermore, C. perfringens ST502 was found in food-producing animals, specifically pigs from South Korea [49]. Moreover, several other STs identified in our study appear to have originated from a common ancestor, with ST494, ST179, and ST274 being considered predominant core STs. ST494 has been isolated from chicken organs in different provinces of China [50]. ST179 has been reported in clinical isolates from hospitalized patients with diarrhea [51]. In addition, ST274 has been described in human patient isolates from South Africa [52]. Nonetheless, STs reported in humans need to be carefully monitored and managed. Thus, these STs could have spread to humans, posing a significant health hazard and contributing to the local and global epidemiology of C. perfringens in humans and other animals.

We observed that among the seven CCs, CC1 was the largest, containing three STs, whereas the other six CCs each comprised two STs. Furthermore, 18.3% of STs were part of CC. The diversity in sequence types suggests that the antimicrobial-resistant strains did not originate from a single dominant clone, but rather from multiple genetically distinct strains that independently acquired resistance or were introduced from various sources. However, CC1 is distributed in different metropolitan cities. This result suggests that C. perfringens spreads between dogs and cats from different cities.

We conducted NGS analysis of the MDR ST494 isolate, revealing the presence of antimicrobial resistance genes and transposable elements on the chromosome and plasmids. Various antimicrobial resistance genes were detected on the chromosome and in two plasmids. In particular, the optrA gene encoding the plasmid, as well as the fexA and ermA genes, was detected. The ermA gene encodes a methyltransferase that modifies the bacterial ribosome, specifically the 23S rRNA, leading to resistance to macrolides, lincosamides, and streptogramins in C. perfringens [53]. Similarly, the fexA gene encodes the fexA protein, an efflux pump that actively transports phenicols, such as chloramphenicol, out of the bacterial cell, thereby reducing their intracellular concentration and rendering the bacteria resistant [54].

Other resistance genes, emrQ, tetP, and lnuP, and toxin genes cpa and cpb2 were detected on chromosomes and plasmid 2. emrQ in C. perfringens refers to a gene that encodes a multidrug efflux transporter, usually classified within the major family facilitator superfamily, which is a part of the organism's intrinsic or plasmid-associated antimicrobial resistance repertoire [55]. The tetP gene on plasmids encodes a tetracycline efflux pump that exports tetracycline from bacterial cells, lowering intracellular drug concentration and conferring tetracycline resistance in C. perfringens [56]. Likewise, InuP encodes a lincosamide nucleotidyltransferase inactivating lincosamide antimicrobials, including clindamycin and lincomycin [57]. The presence of the alpha-toxin gene cpa in C. perfringens, which contributes to intestinal damage, usually synergizes with the other toxin gene cpb2 [58]. Additionally, cpb2, a spore-forming exotoxin produced by C. perfringens, is strongly associated with diarrhea [59].

The optrA protein, produced by the optrA gene, confers resistance to oxazolidinones, which are used for the treatment of severe MDR Gram-positive bacterial infections in humans [60]. The prevalence of optrA may complicate the treatment of C. perfringens infections and facilitate the co-selection of other antimicrobial resistance genes, thereby posing a significant hazard to public health. The optrA gene in C. perfringens exhibited a gene environment similar to those in other bacteria, including Enterococcus and Staphylococcus, suggesting its dissemination among these organisms [61].

In addition, this plasmid carried the insertion sequences ISEfa5 and IS1216E, which play a significant role in the mobilization and dispersion of resistance genes. Previous studies described the presence of these transposable elements in the plasmid of C. perfringens isolated from humans and companion animals [61], [62]. Furthermore, these ISs are linked to oxazolidinone resistance in Enterococcus carrying the oprtA gene. Egan et al. [63] showed that the ISEfa5 transposon harboring the optrA gene in E. faecium implicates the dissemination of resistance. Moreover, it was found that the optrA gene in E. faecalis is flanked by codirectional ISEfa5 and IS1216E transposons, which effectively caused the transformation of the optrA gene in Enterococcus species [64]. The structure of our plasmid (pCP51) closely resembles that of other plasmids, including pQHY-2, p21-D-5b, p2C45, and 19TSBNCP, which were isolated from sheep, goat, chicken, and cattle, respectively. Additionally, all of these plasmids contain similar resistance genes, including optrA and insertion sequences, indicating their possible transmission among different animals [23], [62]. From a One Health perspective, animal-to-human transmission represents a major risk pathway for the dissemination of the optrA gene [65]. Humans may acquire optrA-carrying bacteria through direct contact with companion animals, occupational exposure among farmers and veterinarians, or indirectly via the food chain through handling or consumption of contaminated meat products, presenting a significant health concern.

Conclusion

Our investigation revealed that C. perfringens isolates recovered from diarrheic dogs and cats demonstrated resistance to various antimicrobials, including those essential for humans. Moreover, the occurrence of C. perfringens type A containing cpa and Type F possessing cpa and cpe genes in diarrheal feces of dogs and cats suggests that companion animals can serve as important reservoirs for these toxin genes. In addition, specific clones, such as C. perfringens ST102, were predominantly detected among many other STs. We found optrA carrying plasmid in MDR C. perfringens from dogs, which is the first report of optrA harboring plasmid in companion animals. Furthermore, the presence of mobile genetic elements IS1216E and ISEfa5 potentially promotes horizontal dissemination of resistance. These findings suggest that the companion animals may be a hotspot for the transmission of the oxazolidinone resistance gene optrA, increasing the risk of human infection as C. perfringens evolves and spreads. Thus, continuous surveillance and control of indiscriminate use of antimicrobials in companion animals is essential to mitigate potential risks associated with C. perfringens in humans and other animals.
