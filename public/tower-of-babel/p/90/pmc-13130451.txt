Erythrodermic psoriasis complicated by immune complex-mediated crescentic glomerulonephritis and atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome: a case report
Authors: Xin Chen, Junxia Huang, Ziyan Shen, Qifan Zhu, Ji Yang, Xialian Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundErythrodermic psoriasis (EP) is a severe, systemic inflammatory dermatosis. While kidney involvement is rare, the massive cytokine release associated with EP can induce systemic complications. Background

Severe psoriasis can manifest as erythrodermic psoriasis (EP), a life-threatening inflammatory state associated with systemic complications and multiorgan involvement. Renal involvement in erythrodermic psoriasis has been increasingly recognized, and case-based evidence suggests that severe psoriatic inflammation may coincide with glomerular disease, most commonly IgA nephropathy [1]. Although psoriasis-associated renal disease is most often reported as isolated IgA nephropathy, rare reports have also described membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN) occurring in patients with psoriasis, supporting the plausibility that psoriatic systemic inflammation and immune activation may contribute to immune complex–mediated glomerular injury [2]. In parallel, atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) is a complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy characterized by endothelial injury driven by dysregulated complement activation; importantly, clinical onset is frequently precipitated by inflammatory or autoimmune “second hits,” including infections, autoimmune disorders, and other systemic inflammatory stresses [3]. The simultaneous presentation of severe EP, MPGN, and aHUS is exceptionally rare. We report a case that emphasises the severe systemic nature of EP and the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of managing this complement-driven overlap syndrome.

Case presentation

A 52-year-old man was admitted with progressive, diffuse erythema and desquamation involving over 90% of his body surface area over three months (Fig. 1). This dermatosis was complicated by a one-month history of generalised oedema and progressive dyspnoea. He reported ingesting herbal health supplements containing Ziziphus jujuba, Lycium barbarum, and Morus alba prior to the onset of skin symptoms, taken for insomnia. The patient had a history of hypertension for approximately two months prior to admission, with a maximum recorded blood pressure of 180/90 mmHg. Blood pressure was well controlled with doxazosin, metoprolol, and amlodipine. He had no documented prior history of diabetes mellitus, psoriasis or autoimmune disease. Upon physical examination, the patient was erythematous and universally desquamative, with significant hyperkeratosis and fissuring of the palms and soles. He presented with bilateral, severe pitting oedema. His blood pressure was recorded at 130/80 mmHg. Baseline renal function prior to this episode was preserved, with a serum creatinine level of 103 µmol/L documented at the initial dermatology visit.

Investigations

Initial laboratory findings indicated acute kidney injury (AKI), proteinuria, and a microangiopathic process. Renal function tests showed elevated serum creatinine (416 µmol/L; reference < 104 µmol/L) and urea nitrogen (24.5 mmol/L). Urinalysis confirmed nephrotic-range proteinuria (6.1 g/24 h) and microscopic haematuria (35–40 RBCs/HPF). Haematological assessment revealed microangiopathic haemolysis, characterised by anaemia (haemoglobin 84 g/L), thrombocytopaenia (41 × 10^9/L), elevated LDH (519 U/L), and the presence of schistocytes on the peripheral blood smear. Notably, the complement pathway was suggested to be involved by a significantly decreased complement C3 level (0.48 g/L; reference range 0.90–1.80 g/L), while C4 remained normal. Autoimmune workup was negative for ANA, ANCA, and anti-dsDNA. Direct and indirect Coombs tests were negative, supporting a microangiopathic rather than immune-mediated haemolysis. Laboratory investigations revealed marked hypoalbuminemia, with a serum albumin level of 20 g/L. C-reactive protein was 2.5 mg/L. Testing for anti–factor H antibodies was negative. Direct immunofluorescence of the skin was not performed. The diagnosis of erythrodermic psoriasis was established based on typical clinical presentation and characteristic histopathological findings, including psoriasiform epidermal hyperplasia with parakeratosis, neutrophilic microabscesses in the granular layer, elongated rete ridges, and superficial perivascular inflammatory cell infiltration. The renal biopsy revealed diffuse endocapillary proliferation with crescent formation in 20% of glomeruli (Fig. 2). Immunofluorescence microscopy demonstrated granular IgG and IgA deposition in the mesangial areas, with negative staining for C3, C4, and C1q. Immunohistochemical staining for C5b-9 was positive, whereas staining was negative in a control specimen. Electron microscopy demonstrated electron-dense deposits in the mesangial and subendothelial regions, along with features of endothelial injury, including endothelial swelling and double-contour formation of the glomerular basement membrane.

Renal biopsy findings by light microscopy, immunofluorescence, electron microscopy, and immunohistochemistry. Light microscopy (A, PAS stain; B, silver methenamine stain) revealed diffuse endocapillary hypercellularity with mesangial expansion, mesangiolysis, and double-contour formation of the glomerular capillary walls, consistent with a membranoproliferative pattern. Cellular crescents were observed in approximately 20% of glomeruli. Immunofluorescence microscopy (C) demonstrated granular deposition of immunoglobulins and complement components along the mesangium and capillary walls, supporting immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritis. Electron microscopy (D) revealed electron-dense deposits in the mesangial and subendothelial regions, further confirming immune complex deposition. Immunohistochemical staining for C5b-9 (E) showed positive staining within glomerular capillary walls, indicating activation of the terminal complement pathway, whereas staining was negative in a control specimen (F)

Differential diagnosis

The clinical and laboratory triad of AKI, haemolytic anaemia, and thrombocytopaenia immediately suggested a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Given the low C3 level and absence of evidence for Shiga toxin–producing infection, aHUS was strongly suspected. Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP) was considered but subsequently ruled out by normal ADAMTS13 activity and antibody levels. Other systemic autoimmune causes, such as Lupus Nephritis or Vasculitis, were excluded by the negative ANA and ANCA results. Infection-related GN/Post-infectious GN was deemed less likely given negative cultures and serology. The possibility of a secondary TMA related to the ingested herbal supplements was also noted, although no specific nephrotoxin was identified. Ultimately, the renal biopsy findings, demonstrating the simultaneous presence of IC-GN and TMA features, established an overlap syndrome best classified as IC-mediated crescentic GN with superimposed complement-mediated TMA.

Treatment

The initial therapeutic intervention aimed to control the underlying severe inflammation. The patient was treated with intravenous methylprednisolone (40 mg/day), followed by oral prednisone (30 mg/day), intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg), and supportive care for oedema. While the skin erythema and systemic oedema partially improved, the AKI and TMA (thrombocytopaenia, LDH elevation) persisted, raising suspicion of uncontrolled complement activation. Given the refractoriness of the TMA and persistently low C3 levels, indicating severe, persistent complement dysregulation, the decision was made to commence targeted terminal complement blockade. Plasma exchange was considered during the initial assessment of thrombotic microangiopathy. However, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) was deemed unlikely based on the overall clinical presentation and laboratory findings. In addition, evidence of complement-mediated endothelial injury, including elevated C5b-9 levels, supported a diagnosis of atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome. Given the limited efficacy of plasma exchange in complement-mediated TMA and increasing evidence favoring early complement inhibition, plasma exchange was not initiated. Instead, targeted therapy with eculizumab was selected as the primary treatment strategy. Eculizumab (a monoclonal antibody targeting C5) therapy was initiated (900 mg weekly for 4 weeks, followed by 1200 mg every 2 weeks for maintenance) after appropriate meningococcal vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis. An angiotensin receptor blocker was initiated after hemodynamic stabilization for antiproteinuric management.

Outcome and follow-up

Following initiation of eculizumab, the patient exhibited rapid and dramatic clinical improvement. The haematological parameters quickly normalised, with platelet count recovering to > 150 × 10^9/L and LDH returning to the normal range within three weeks. Renal function showed gradual but significant recovery over the following months, with serum creatinine decreasing from its peak to 179 µmol/L (2.0 mg/dL) and proteinuria declining to 4.8 g/24 h. The patient remains stable on low-dose oral prednisone and maintenance eculizumab therapy, with no relapse of TMA or severe EP, confirming the efficacy of complement inhibition in this specific overlap syndrome.

Discussion

This case demonstrates a rare and complex overlap syndrome in which severe erythrodermic psoriasis (EP) coexisted with immune complex–mediated crescentic glomerulonephritis (IC-GN) and complement-mediated atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS). It highlights several important clinical and mechanistic considerations: (1) EP can be associated with profound systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction; (2) complement activation represents a shared pathogenic pathway linking inflammatory skin disease and thrombotic microangiopathy; and (3) timely complement inhibition may be lifesaving when complement-mediated TMA complicates severe inflammatory conditions.

Psoriasis is associated with dysregulated innate and adaptive immunity, particularly the IL-23/Th17 axis and downstream cytokines such as IL-17, IL-22, IL-6, and TNF-α [4]. These mediators not only sustain epidermal hyperplasia but also exert systemic vascular effects, promoting endothelial activation and dysfunction. Experimental and clinical studies have demonstrated complement activation in both lesional skin and circulation in patients with psoriasis, suggesting that psoriasis may function as a systemic complement-amplifying inflammatory state rather than a purely cutaneous disease [5–7]. Endothelial dysfunction, now recognized as a frequent comorbidity of psoriasis, further increases susceptibility to complement-mediated microvascular injury [8]. Importantly, psoriasis is not typically considered a primary driver of classical immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritis, although systemic inflammation may modulate immune and complement activation. In this patient, immunofluorescence microscopy demonstrated granular, coarse, clumped deposition of IgG and IgA within mesangial areas, while C3, C4, and C1q were negative. This pattern suggests an immune-complex–mediated glomerular process unlikely to be directly driven by psoriasis alone. Instead, it is more plausible that an independent or triggered immune-complex mechanism contributed to IC-GN, whereas EP served as a systemic inflammatory amplifier.

Previous renal involvement reported in association with psoriasis has been predominantly limited to isolated glomerular diseases, most commonly IgA nephropathy. Recent case reports have described psoriasis-associated IgA nephropathy without evidence of thrombotic microangiopathy or complement-mediated hemolysis [1, 9]. In contrast to these previously reported cases, our patient demonstrated a far more complex renal and systemic phenotype characterized by immune complex–mediated crescentic membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis, accompanied by features of thrombotic microangiopathy and atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome.

Immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritis is characterized by activation of the classical and alternative complement pathways, leading to glomerular inflammation and, in severe cases, crescent formation. In this case, immunofluorescence microscopy did not demonstrate glomerular C3 deposition; however, immunohistochemical staining revealed terminal complement complex C5b-9 within glomerular structures. The absence of detectable tissue C3 does not exclude functional complement activation, as complement activity may be transient, localized, or predominantly cell-bound and therefore not captured by routine immunofluorescence. In contrast, C5b-9 deposition provides supportive evidence of terminal complement pathway activation and ongoing endothelial injury.

Renal pathologic features of TMA, including endothelial swelling and double-contour formation, may occur even in the absence of significant glomerular complement deposition, reflecting functional complement dysregulation [10]. Histologically, the combination of diffuse endocapillary hypercellularity and double-contour formation supports an MPGN-like pattern of injury, while the presence of superimposed crescents reflects severe inflammatory activity. Overlap between immune complex–mediated glomerular diseases and complement-driven TMA has increasingly been recognized as part of a spectrum of complement-mediated renal disorders rather than as entirely distinct entities [11–13]. Such overlap syndromes suggest that different initiating insults—immune complex formation or systemic inflammation—may converge on shared complement-dependent pathways, resulting in combined glomerular and microvascular injury.

Atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome is now understood as a disease of alternative complement pathway dysregulation that frequently requires an environmental or inflammatory trigger to become clinically manifest [14]. Severe systemic inflammatory states, referred to as “complement-amplifying conditions,” can precipitate aHUS in individuals with underlying or acquired susceptibility to complement dysregulation [14]. In this context, EP, characterized by massive cytokine release and endothelial activation, likely lowered the threshold for uncontrolled complement activation in this patient, allowing immune complex–mediated glomerular injury and systemic complement dysregulation to coexist. The central role of complement overactivation in aHUS is well established. Terminal complement inhibition with eculizumab effectively blocks C5 activation and has dramatically improved outcomes in patients with aHUS [15]. In this case, the rapid normalization of platelet count and lactate dehydrogenase levels, together with sustained renal recovery following eculizumab initiation, supports complement-mediated endothelial injury as the dominant driver of the TMA component. Importantly, recent evidence indicates that complement inhibition may be beneficial even in secondary or overlap TMAs when clinical, laboratory, and histologic features support complement-driven disease [16, 17].

Conclusion

This case illustrates a rare overlap of erythrodermic psoriasis, immune complex–mediated crescentic glomerulonephritis, and complement-mediated atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome. It highlights that severe EP may act as a systemic inflammatory and complement-amplifying condition, facilitating the coexistence of immune complex–mediated glomerular injury and complement-driven thrombotic microangiopathy. Recognition of this overlap is critical, as early renal biopsy and timely initiation of terminal complement inhibition may be decisive in preventing irreversible kidney injury and improving clinical outcomes.
