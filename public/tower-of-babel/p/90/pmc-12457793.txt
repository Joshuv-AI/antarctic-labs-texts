Comparative analysis of surgical outcomes of laparoscopic appendectomy performed by six surgeons with different subspecialties: a retrospective cohort study using risk-adjusted cumulative summation
Authors: Ji Hyeong Song, Inyoung Na, Song-Yi Kim, Youn Ju Lee, Sun Jong Han, Sang Il Youn, Sa-Hong Min, Jin Soo Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeLaparoscopic appendectomy, a routine surgical procedure for acute appendicitis, is commonly performed in general hospitals. However, postoperative outcomes based on surgical subspecialty have not been well established. This study aimed to compare surgical outcomes of laparoscopic appendectomy across different surgical subspecialties using risk-adjusted cumulative summation (RA-CUSUM) analysis.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 631 patients undergoing laparoscopic appendectomy between July 2020 and December 2022. Surgeons were categorized into gastrointestinal (GI) or non-GI groups and senior or young groups. Surgical failure was defined as operation time exceeding the mean plus 2 standard deviations, presence of postoperative complications, or readmission within 30 days after surgery. RA-CUSUM analysis was employed to evaluate surgical failure within each group.ResultsThe GI group performed more partial cecectomies (P = 0.017) and had longer operation times (P = 0.019) than the non-GI group. The senior group exhibited shorter operation time (P < 0.001), reduced length of stay (P = 0.011), and a higher complication rate (P < 0.001) than the young group. RA-CUSUM analysis indicated a decrease in surgical failure as accumulation progressed in both senior and young groups, as well as in the GI and non-GI groups.ConclusionOutcomes of laparoscopic appendectomy varied by subspecialty and length of experience. However, each surgeon showed trends of decreasing surgical failure over time. These results suggest that the surgeon’s subspecialty minimally affects the outcomes of laparoscopic appendectomy, reflecting its fundamental nature within general surgery.Keywords: Appendectomy, Laparoscopy, Risk adjustment, Surgical specialties

INTRODUCTION

Acute appendicitis is one of the most common surgical emergencies, necessitating prompt intervention to prevent complications such as perforation and abscess formation [1]. Over the years, laparoscopic appendectomy has been widely accepted as an effective alternative to open appendectomy [2]. The minimally invasive nature, reduced postoperative pain, faster recovery, shorter hospital stay, and improved cosmetic results have contributed to the increasing popularity of laparoscopic appendectomy. As this minimally invasive technique has gained popularity, it has been adopted by surgeons from various subspecialties.

Within the field of general surgery, different subspecialties may exist, including hepatobiliary-pancreatic, colorectal, pediatric, gastrointestinal (GI), breast, vascular, or endocrine surgery. In Korea, individuals complete a one-year internship and 3 years of residency to qualify as a surgeon. During their residency, they learn about laparoscopic appendectomies. Afterward, those who complete a prescribed surgical subspecialty training program at a designated training hospital and pass the examination are granted a subspecialist certificate by the Korean Surgical Society. Each subspecialty may have specific expertise, experience, and nuances in performing emergency surgery. These subspecialty variations could potentially influence surgical outcomes, such as complication rates, operation time, and length of hospital stay. Robson et al. [3] reported a significant reduction in patient mortality when perforated peptic ulcers were operated on by an upper GI surgeon. Implementing a specialist-led approach to urgent cholecystectomy in an acute care hospital with a dedicated upper GI team demonstrated reduced open conversion rates, shorter length of stay, fewer unexpected readmissions, satisfactory operation times, and lower complication rates [4]. Other studies have reported that the management of colorectal emergencies by colorectal surgeons was associated with lower overall and operative mortality, while safely achieving high primary anastomosis rates [5,6].

On the other hand, when comparing the outcomes of pediatric surgeons and general surgeons for laparoscopic appendectomy in children, several studies have shown that pediatric surgeons have slightly better outcomes [7,8].

However, to our best knowledge, no studies have compared the outcomes of laparoscopic appendectomy by subspecialty other than pediatric surgery, such as upper GI, colorectal, hepatobiliary-pancreatic, vascular, or breast surgery. Therefore, this study aimed to compare surgical outcomes according to the subspecialty of surgeons in laparoscopic appendectomy using risk-adjusted assessment to identify differences between subspecialties and provide insights into the performance of each subspecialty.

METHODS

Patients

We performed a retrospective study of patients who underwent emergency laparoscopic appendectomy or partial cecectomy between July 2020 and December 2022. Patients who underwent incidental appendectomy during unrelated surgery, or were diagnosed with diseases other than appendicitis after surgery, were excluded from the study. Data on patient characteristics and operative outcomes were collected from the electronic medical records.

Approval for this retrospective study was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Chungnam National University Sejong Hospital (No. 2023-06-006), and the need for informed consent was waived.

Surgeon’s subspecialty and seniority

The present study involved 6 surgeons: 2 upper GI surgeons, and 1 each from colorectal, hepatobiliary-pancreatic, vascular, and breast surgeons. All surgeons had little experience performing laparoscopic appendectomy during the period when they were specializing in subspecialties. Surgeons specializing in upper GI tract and colorectal surgeries were classified as the GI group, while those specializing in hepatobiliary-pancreatic, vascular, and breast surgeries were classified as the non-GI group. Additionally, surgeons who had been certified in a surgical subspecialty for more than 10 years were classified as a senior group; otherwise as young. Patients’ characteristics and operative outcomes were compared according to the surgeon’s seniority and subspecialty.

Operative outcomes and surgical failure

Based on surgical findings, perforated appendicitis or periappendiceal abscess formation was categorized as complicated appendicitis. We defined operation time as the duration between the first skin incision and the completion of skin closure. Postoperative complications included wound infection, ileus, and intra-abdominal abscess. Surgical failure was defined as operation time exceeding the mean plus 2 standard deviations, presence of postoperative complications, or readmission within 30 days after surgery.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the R software (ver. 4.1.0, The R Foundation). The Student t-test and Mann-Whitney U-test were performed for continuous variables, and the chisquare and Fisher exact test were performed for categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at a P-value of less than 0.05. Multivariate analysis was performed on variables with a P-value of less than 0.1 in the univariate analysis. Risk factors for surgical failure were identified through logistic regression analysis, and the predicted probability of surgical failure was obtained using significant variables from the logistic regression analysis. Based on this predicted probability, risk-adjusted cumulative summation (RA-CUSUM) was calculated and displayed in a graph to assess surgical failure. The horizontal axis of the graph indicates the number of surgical cases, and the vertical axis indicates an upward slope when the surgery fails and a downward slope when the surgery is successful.

RESULTS

Patients’ characteristics

A total of 631 patients were included in the present study. All patients’ characteristics and operative outcomes are presented in Table 1. There were 371 males (58.8%) and 260 females (41.2%), with 105 pediatric patients (16.6%) under the age of 18 years, and 17 elderly patients (2.7%) aged 75 years or older. Complicated appendicitis was observed in 115 patients (18.2%), and partial cecectomy was performed in 67 (10.6%). The total complication rate was 13.6% (86 of 631), and the rate of surgical failure was 15.8% (100 of 631).

Operative outcomes

Table 3 presents operative outcomes according to the seniority or subspecialty of the surgeons. The GI group tended to perform more partial cecectomies (P = 0.017) than the nonGI group. The operation time was longer in the GI group (P = 0.019) than in the non-GI group. The senior group showed shorter mean operation time (P < 0.001) and length of stay (P = 0.011) compared to the young group. However, the rate of complications (P < 0.001), readmission (P = 0.003), and surgical failure (P = 0.001) was higher in the senior group compared to the young group. In terms of complications, wound infection (P = 0.004) and intra-abdominal abscess (P = 0.001) were more common in the senior group than in the young group. There were no significant differences in complications between the GI and non-GI groups (Table 4). No serious complications such as sepsis, intensive care unit treatment, reoperation, or death occurred in this study.

Risk factors affecting surgical failure

Univariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated that complicated appendicitis (P < 0.001) and senior surgeons (P = 0.001) were significant variables associated with surgical failure. In multivariate analysis, complicated appendicitis (P < 0.001) and senior surgeons (P = 0.002) were found to be independent risk factors for surgical failure (Table 5).

DISCUSSION

Laparoscopic appendectomy is one of the most basic surgical procedures in general surgery and has therefore been primarily used for resident training [9,10]. The recent trend in Korea has been to specialize in each subspecialty rather than general surgery. Subspecialists focus on their own field, while residents mainly perform laparoscopic appendectomy during their training [11,12]. Chungnam National University Sejong Hospital, which opened in July 2020, has had no residents since then. Laparoscopic appendectomies were performed by subspecialists in upper GI, colorectal, hepatobiliary-pancreatic, vascular, or breast surgery. All surgeons had little experience performing laparoscopic appendectomy during the period they were specializing in subspecialties. Thus, we designed this study with a practical interest in comparing surgical outcomes by surgeon subspecialty in laparoscopic appendectomy.

In our results, a few differences in operative outcomes were noted between GI and non-GI subspecialties. The GI group tended to perform more partial cecectomies and had longer operation times compared to the non-GI group. The non-GI group was more reluctant to perform cecectomy than the GI group because they were not familiar with the anatomy. Despite these differences, the overall complication rates between the GI and non-GI groups were comparable, indicating that while partial cecectomy may be more challenging, they are not necessarily associated with higher rates of adverse events if performed by GI surgeons.

In addition to differences by subspecialty, we observed some differences in operative outcomes between senior and young surgeons. The senior group exhibited shorter operation time and length of stay; however, they had a higher rate of complications and surgical failure compared to the young group. Meanwhile, logistic regression analysis identified complicated appendicitis and senior surgeons as risk factors for surgical failure. Given their extensive experience in their subspecialty, it can be assumed that the senior group encountered more trial and error in the early stages of resuming laparoscopic appendectomies compared to the younger group, as they had not performed the procedure for an extended period.

The CUSUM method is widely used to analyze the learning curve of laparoscopic appendectomy [12,13,14]. We used the RA-CUSUM analysis to assess surgical failure for each subspecialist. In the present study, the rate of surgical failure (15.8%) was higher than in the previous study [12]. This is likely due to the inclusion of prolonged operation time in the definition of surgical failure. Interestingly, our RA-CUSUM analysis revealed consistent decreasing trends in surgical failure rates across all surgeons as surgical experience accumulated. Although the learning curve for laparoscopic appendectomy of subspecialists has been achieved, the gradual decrease in surgical risk may be a feature of our institution, which is a brand-new hospital. The RA-CUSUM chart is thought to be downward because the operating room staff, instruments, and equipment settings stabilize over time compared to the initial opening of the hospital. However, it is important to note that the rate of improvement may vary between different groups. The GI group showed a slightly faster reduction in surgical risk compared to non-GI, but the overall reduction was similar. Although the senior group experienced more surgical failure than the young group, the slope of the RA-CUSUM chart for the senior group was steeper, indicating a rapid decrease as the number of surgeries increased. This may be due to the fact that there was a period when subspecialists did not perform laparoscopic appendectomies themselves, and in the absence of residents, they faced initial challenges but eventually overcame them.

Despite the strengths of our study, including a comprehensive analysis of surgical outcomes, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a retrospective study, our findings are subject to selection biases and confounding factors. Second, the study was conducted at a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of our results to other settings. Third, while we adjusted for various risk factors in our analysis, there may be unmeasured variables that could influence surgical outcomes. Finally, we were unable to separately analyze the outcomes of laparoscopic appendectomies in pediatric patients because there were no pediatric surgeons at our institution.

In conclusion, our findings provide valuable insights into the influence of surgeon subspecialty and seniority on surgical outcomes, specifically in the context of laparoscopic appendectomy. While differences were observed between senior and young surgeons, as well as between GI and non-GI subspecialties, overall, our results suggest that surgical outcomes improve with increasing experience, and the surgeon’s subspecialty has a minimal impact on the outcomes of laparoscopic appendectomy. Further research is warranted to validate these findings and explore additional factors that may influence surgical outcomes in different subspecialties.
