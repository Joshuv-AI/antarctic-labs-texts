Low dose radiotherapy-induced tumor-preferential antigens enhance antibody-drug conjugate efficacy in solid tumors
Authors: Yun Xing, Yuxuan Yang, Yimin Liu, Teng Yang, Letao Ma, Jiahui Chen, Yang Yang, Yanzhi Xu, Ye Lu, Peng Guo, Peijing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) efficacy in solid tumors is often limited by low or heterogeneous antigen expression. Here, we investigated low-dose radiotherapy (LDRT) as a noninvasive sensitization strategy to enhance tumor surface antigen expression and improve ADC activity. Transcriptomic profiling and flow cytometry identified ICAM1 as the most strongly radiation-inducible antigen after 2 Gy irradiation, showing an approximately 4-fold increase and exceeding several established ADC targets. Mechanistic studies revealed that radiation-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative stress signaling upregulated ICAM1, enhanced antibody internalization, and improved ICAM1-targeted ADC delivery. In xenograft models, LDRT combined with ICAM1-targeted ADCs achieved >70% tumor growth inhibition with minimal toxicity. Transcriptomic and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining analyses further showed that LDRT induced recruitment of immunosuppressive CCL8high M2-like tumor-associated macrophages, whereas ADC-mediated bystander killing attenuated this adaptive feedback and promoted a more immunoactive tumor microenvironment. Together, these findings support LDRT as a clinically translatable strategy to increase ICAM1 expression, overcome antigen-low barriers, and enhance ADC efficacy in refractory solid tumors.

Introduction

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) represent a groundbreaking therapeutic approach for solid tumors, leveraging the specificity of monoclonal antibodies to selectively deliver potent cytotoxic agents to cancer cells [1]. Despite this promise, the overall response rate for ADCs remains limited, with only a subset of patients benefiting from currently approved therapies. For example, trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd), an ADC targeting HER2, achieved an impressive overall resonse rate (ORR) of 82.7% in patients with HER2-positive metastatic breast cancer in the DESTINY-Breast03 trial [2]. However, despite the success of HER2-targeted therapy in HER2-positive disease, its benefit in HER2-low tumors is often attenuated and remains largely absent in HER2-negative tumors, leaving these patients with substantially poorer therapeutic prospects. Similarly, in the DESTINY-Lung01 trial, T-DXd demonstrated an ORR of 54.9% in HER2-mutant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) but was ineffective in patients without HER2 mutations [3]. More importantly, ADC efficacy intrinsically correlates with their target antigen expression levels, restricting applicability to patients whose tumors express no or low antigen levels. For instance, mirvetuximab soravtansine (IMGN853), an ADC targeting folate receptor alpha (FRα), has shown meaningful clinical activity predominantly in patients with high FRα expression. In a Phase III FORWARD I trial, patients with an immunohistochemistry (IHC) score of 3+ for FRα levels demonstrated better progression free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in comparison with those with lower FRα expression (IHC score of 1 or 2). This underscores a major clinical challenge: a significant proportion of patients with low or absent antigen expression remain unresponsive to ADC therapy. This disparity underscores a critical unmet medical need: how can we convert antigen-negative or low tumors into antigen-positive ones to expand the pool of patients who can benefit from ADC therapy?

Several strategies have been explored to enhance tumor antigen expression, aiming to expand the patient population that can benefit from ADC therapy[4]. One approach involves histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors, which modulate chromatin structure and gene expression to upregulate tumor antigens [5]. For example, the HDAC inhibitor vorinostat has been shown to increase HER2 expression in breast cancer cell lines, potentially sensitizing HER2-low tumors to HER2-targeted therapies[6]. However, the lack of tumor specificity and dose-limiting toxicities due to off-target effects limit the clinical utility of HDAC inhibitors. Another strategy leverages epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors to enhance HER3 antigen expression[7]. Studies indicate that afatinib, an EGFR inhibitor, upregulates HER3 expression by approximately threefold in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells, increasing susceptibility to HER3-targeted ADCs such as U3-1402[8]. While promising, resistance development and associated toxicities pose significant challenges. A third approach employs BRAF or MEK inhibitors to modulate downstream signaling pathways and augment antigen expression. For instance, the BRAF inhibitor vemurafenib has been reported to increase MHC class I expression by 2.5-fold in melanoma, enhancing immune recognition[9]. However, the efficacy of these inhibitors is often restricted to specific mutations and may lead to systemic toxicities. Despite these advancements, a clinically safe, broadly applicable method to reliably upregulate tumor antigen expression across diverse tumor types remains an unmet need.

Low-dose radiotherapy (LDRT) presents a clinically viable and effective strategy for enhancing tumor-associated antigen expression, potentially converting antigen-low tumors into antigen-positive ones. Conventional radiotherapy (RT), widely utilized in oncology, not only damages tumor cells but also induces the expression of antigens or neoantigens on the tumor surface, thereby increasing susceptibility to ADC targeting [10]. For instance, in lung cancer patients, RT has been shown to upregulate PD-L1 expression on tumor cells, with levels rising more than twofold post-irradiation [11], thereby enhancing responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Similarly, in colorectal cancer, RT increased MHC class I molecule expression by threefold, improving antigen presentation and immune recognition [12]. Additionally, RT-induced upregulation of ICAM1 has been observed in multiple tumor types [13,14]. Despite these promising findings, the precise mechanisms governing LDRT-induced antigen expression, the types of antigens involved, and the optimal strategies for integrating LDRT with ADC therapy remain incompletely understood.

In this study, we hypothesized that LDRT could create a tumor-preferential therapeutic window by inducing and sustaining the expression of actionable cell-surface antigens in antigen-low tumors, thereby improving target engagement and responsiveness to ADC therapy without comparably increasing antigen expression in normal cells. To test this hypothesis, we performed unbiased transcriptomic and protein-level screening to identify radiation-responsive surface antigens and characterized their dose-dependent, temporal, and tumor-versus-normal expression patterns. We further investigated the molecular processes associated with radiation-induced antigen upregulation and examined whether this modulation enhanced antibody internalization and tumor accumulation. Finally, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy and safety of combining LDRT with an ADC directed against the identified antigen in preclinical tumor models and explored the associated remodeling of the tumor microenvironment.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human embryonic kidney HEK293T cells was purchased from American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). Human esophageal squamous-cell carcinoma KYSE30 cells was purchased from Kefan-bio (Guangzhou, China). Human breast cancer SUM190 cells was purchased from Baihua-bio (Shanghai, China). Human colon cancer HCT116 cells was purchased from American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). HCT116, KYSE30 were cultured in RPMI-1640, SUM190 in MEM, and 293T cells in DMEM, respectively. All cell culture medium were supplied with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/strepto-mycin (P/S). All cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Quantification of cell membrane protein expression

To assess membrane surface protein expression, HCT116 colon cancer cells and normal 293T cells were analyzed by flow cytometry. A total of 1 × 10⁶ cells were harvested and washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by blocking with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 30 min on ice to prevent non-specific binding. After the blocking step, cells were incubated with 1 μg of PE-conjugated antibodies specific to the target membrane proteins for 1 h at room temperature. Post-incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS and resuspended in PBS for flow cytometric analysis. The geometric mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of 10,000 cells per sample was measured using the Thermo Attune NxT flow cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Additionally, exposure to ionizing radiation was conducted to study radiation-induced changes in protein expression. Both 293T and HCT116 cells were exposed to graded X-ray doses ranging from 1 to 5 Gy using the RS2000 Super X-ray irradiator. SUM190 cells were subjected to fractionated radiation treatment, receiving 2 Gy per fraction daily, totaling 6 Gy. KYSE30 cells were similarly irradiated. After a 48-hour incubation post-irradiation, cells were collected and prepared for flow cytometry following the same protocol as described above. The MFI of 10,000 cells per sample was again assessed to evaluate radiation-induced alterations in surface protein expression across the different cell lines.

Quantitative analysis of antibody internalization efficiency

To evaluate the internalization efficiency of mouse anti-human ICAM1 antibodies in HCT116 and KYSE30 cells, flow cytometry analysis was employed. A total of 1 × 10⁶ cells, both treated with 2 Gy radiation (RS2000 Super) for 48 h and untreated control cells, were harvested and washed once with PBS. Following this step, cells were blocked with 1% BSA in PBS for 30 min on ice to minimize non-specific antibody binding. Subsequently, cells were incubated with 1 μg of purified mouse anti-human ICAM1 antibodies in an ice bath for 1 h. After antibody incubation, cells were washed once in PBS, resuspended in fresh PBS, and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere for varying time points: 0, 30, 60, 120, and 240 min. Following incubation, 0.5 μg of PE-conjugated rat anti-mouse IgG1 antibodies were added to each sample, and cells were incubated on ice for 30 min. Samples were then washed twice with PBS, resuspended, and analyzed for geometric MFI using the Thermo Attune NxT flow cytometer (Thermo Fisher). Quantitative analysis of cellular antigen expression was performed using Quantum™ Simply Cellular® microspheres (Bangs Laboratories) following the manufacturer's protocol. The ratio of antibody internalization was calculated using the following formula:%internalized={(ABCofanti−mouseIgG)0min−(ABCofanti−mouseIgG)tmin}/(ABCofanti−mouseIgG)0min×100.

Fluorescence confocal microscopy for anti-ICAM1 antibody endocytosis

To further visualize anti-ICAM1 antibody endocytosis, confocal fluorescence microscopy was utilized. A total of 1 × 10⁶ HCT116 cells, treated with 2 Gy radiation for 48 h, and an equal number of untreated control cells were seeded into 35 mm glass-bottom dishes containing 1 mL of cell culture medium and incubated overnight at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. After overnight incubation, the culture medium was removed, and cells were incubated with 1 μg of PE-conjugated anti-human ICAM1 antibodies for 1 h on ice. Following antibody incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS and then cultured at 37 °C for 0, 30, 60, 120, and 240 min. Hoechst 33342 was used to stain the cell nuclei for 10 min. After staining, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and analyzed using a Stellaris 8 (STED) confocal microscope (Leica) to visualize the internalization and endocytosis of the anti-ICAM1 antibody over time.

Measurement of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation

Intracellular ROS generation was assessed using the peroxide-sensitive fluorescent probe 2′,7′-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). Cells treated with 2 Gy radiation (RS2000 Super) for 48 h, along with untreated control cells (1 × 10⁶ each), were incubated in culture medium containing 5 μM DCFH-DA for 1 h at 37 °C. Following the incubation, intracellular ROS levels were quantitatively analyzed via flow cytometry. Additionally, confocal microscopy was employed for qualitative visualization of ROS production.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis

HCT116 cells, both irradiated with 2 Gy for 48 h and untreated control cells, were harvested by trypsin digestion and centrifuged at 1200 rpm. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellets were immediately fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde prepared in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.2–7.4) for 2 h at 4 °C. After fixation, the cells were rinsed 3–4 times with 0.1 M phosphate buffer solution. Subsequently, the cells were post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.2–7.4) for 2 h at 4 °C and rinsed again 3–4 times with the same buffer. The samples were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series and embedded in Epon resin. Ultra-thin sections (50–70 nm) were cut and stained with 2% uranyl acetate followed by lead citrate. The cellular ultrastructure was examined using a TEM (JEM-2100plus) to observe potential radiation-induced morphological changes.

Preparation and characterization of ICAM1-DXd

ICAM1-DXd was custom manufactured by MabPlex Biosciences using the anti-human ICAM1 monoclonal antibody (clone number: R6.5c, human–mouse chimeric, GenScript) and the cleavable linker–payload MC-GGFG-DXd (Catalog number: HHY-13631E). Briefly, antibody interchain disulfide bonds were partially reduced with tris (2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP) at an antibody-to-TCEP molar ratio of 1:4 for 45 min at 37 °C. MC-GGFG-DXd was then added at an antibody-to-linker–payload molar ratio of 1:10, and the reaction was incubated overnight at 37 °C. The resulting conjugate was purified by dialysis (molecular weight cut off 14 KDa) for 24 h to remove unconjugated linker–payload and other low-molecular-weight components. Conjugation was confirmed by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry. The molecular mass increased from approximately 145 kDa for the unconjugated antibody to approximately 149 kDa for ICAM1-DXd. The average drug-to-antibody ratio was calculated from the molecular mass difference and was approximately 8. The corresponding ICAM1-DXd chemical structure and mass spectra are shown in Fig. S1.

In vivo biodistribution and treatment studies

Animal studies were conducted in accordance with the protocols approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Hangzhou Institute of Medicine, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The mice used in the mouse studies were sourced from gempharmatech. In the experiments, male and female mice were used according to the type of tumor, and the age of the mice was 4-6 weeks. The biodistribution study was conducted using a xenograft nude mouse model of human colon cancer (HCT116). Subcutaneous implantation of 5 × 10⁶ HCT116 cells, mixed 1:1 with Matrigel, was performed into the right flank of 4- to 6-week-old nude mice. Once tumors reached 100–200 mm³, mice were randomly allocated to three groups (n = 5 per group) by randomization, in which all animals were placed in a common cage and sequentially selected at random for group assignment: [1] PBS (control), [2] ICAM1–ATTO 647 (5 mg/kg, intravenous injection), and [3] radiation (2 Gy per fraction) plus ICAM1–ATTO 647 (5 mg/kg, intravenous injection). Intravenous injection was administered 24 h after radiation. In vivo near-infrared (NIR) fluorescence imaging was performed 24 h post-injection using the IVIS Lumina III system (PerkinElmer). After imaging, the mice were euthanized with CO2, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and brain) were collected. Fluorescence intensities of excised tumors and organs were quantified using the IVIS Lumina III system. Fluorescence images were analyzed using Living Image software. Fluorescence intensity was expressed as mean radiant efficiency, with identical imaging parameters applied to all samples. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and no data points were excluded as outliers.

For antitumor efficacy experiments, xenograft models of human colon cancer (HCT116) and human breast cancer (SUM190) were used male and female nude mice respectively. When tumors reached approximately 100–200 mm³, tumor-bearing mice were randomly assigned to four groups by simple randomization, with all animals placed in a common cage and selected sequentially at random for group allocation: [1] PBS (control), [2] radiation alone (2 Gy per fraction, once daily for 5 consecutive days), [3] ICAM1–DXd alone (5 mg/kg, intravenous injection), and [4] radiation plus ICAM1–DXd (Combo). Each group included 10–12 mice. Power analysis (α = 0.05, 80% power) indicated that at least eight mice per group were required; additional animals were included to account for potential attrition. Tumor volume was measured every other day using a caliper and calculated according to the formula V = L × W² × π/6. The end point was reached 2 weeks after the start of treatment, at which point the mice were euthanized with CO2. Subcutaneous tumors were excised for tumor mass measurement. For additional analysis, five mice from both the control and radiation groups were randomly selected to assess ICAM1 expression on the surface of tumor tissues via flow cytometry. Furthermore, histological and hematological analyses were performed on 3–5 mice from each group to evaluate treatment-related tissue responses.

RNA-seq and bioinformatics analysis

RNA extraction, cDNA library preparation, RNA sequencing, quality control, and transcriptome profiling of end point in HCT116 cells and tumor tissues were performed by LC-Bio Technologies (Hangzhou, China). Differential genes were analyzed using DESeq2 in R programming language.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise specified. Group comparisons were carried out using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni post hoc tests, as appropriate. Before applying parametric tests, data were assessed for normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Brown–Forsythe test). A P value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Safety assessment

Safety analyses were conducted using blood and tissue samples collected from the HCT116 xenograft efficacy study. Six mice per group were evaluated, including the PBS (control), LDRT, ICAM1–DXd, and combination (Combo) groups. Animals received the same treatment regimens as in the efficacy study: LDRT at 2 Gy per fraction once daily for five consecutive days and ICAM1–DXd at 5 mg/kg by intravenous injection. At the study endpoint, 14 days after treatment initiation, blood was collected for hematological and serum biochemical analyses. The mice were then euthanized, and the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with H&E. Histopathological evaluation was performed by a pathologist blinded to treatment allocation. Normal physiological ranges were defined using reference intervals provided by the animal vendor and facility and were cross-checked against published values for age- and strain-matched nude mice [15,16]. Statistical differences were interpreted in relation to both the control group and these reference ranges. The anti-ICAM1 antibody used in ICAM1–DXd recognizes human ICAM1 but does not cross-react with murine ICAM1.

Results

Screening LDRT-induced cell surface antigens as ADC target

To explore whether LDRT modulates the expression of cell surface antigens amenable to ADC targeting, we conducted transcriptome RNA sequencing on the human colorectal cancer cell line HCT116 following exposure to 2 Gy of ionizing radiation or no treatment (Fig. 1A). Radiation exposure led to the upregulation of 481 genes encoding membrane-associated proteins (Fig. 1B). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that these upregulated membrane proteins were significantly enriched in immune-related pathways, including immune responses and leukocyte and lymphocyte activation (Fig. 1C). Notably, intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM1) exhibited marked upregulation post-radiation relative to other surface antigens (Fig. 1D). ICAM1 has previously been recognized as a promising ADC target across various refractory solid tumors, such as triple-negative breast cancer, pancreatic cancer, and cholangiocarcinoma [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]].We further validated this finding at the protein level by quantitatively comparing ICAM1 expression with 15 other well-established ADC targets including HER2, TROP2, ROR1, and EGFR using an unbiased flow cytometric assay (Fig. 1E). This analysis demonstrated that LDRT induced a markedly greater upregulation of ICAM1 compared to other surface antigens, consistent with their respective mRNA expression levels (Fig. 1F). To assess the clinical relevance of ICAM1 in the context of radiotherapy, we conducted immunohistochemical (IHC) staining on 13 human esophageal cancer tissue samples collected before and after radiotherapy. The IHC analysis demonstrated a marked increase in ICAM1 expression in tumor tissues following radiotherapy. Quantitative analysis showed that the percentage of ICAM1-positive cells increased from 10% in pre-radiotherapy tissues to 37.2% in post-radiotherapy tissues (Fig. 1G). These data suggest that ICAM1 could serve as a promising target for ADC-based therapies, particularly in antigen-low tumors that have undergone radiotherapy.

Preferential upregulation of ICAM1 by LDRT in different tumors

As shown in Fig. 2A, flow cytometric analysis revealed that ICAM1 surface expression increased significantly as early as 12 h post-2 Gy irradiation and reached a plateau at approximately 48 h across three human tumor cell lines: HCT116 (colorectal cancer), KYSE30 (esophageal cancer), and SUM190 (triple-negative breast cancer) as well as normal human epithelial 293T cells. To further assess the relationship between radiation dose and ICAM1 expression, we irradiated HCT116, KYSE30, and SUM190 tumor cells, along with 293T cells, at doses ranging from 0 to 5 Gy and measured ICAM1 expression 48 h later (Fig. 2B and Fig. S2). In HCT116 and KYSE30 cells, ICAM1 expression increased in a dose-dependent manner, rising from 25,825 to 82,216 molecules per cell. In contrast, SUM190 cells exhibited no appreciable increase in ICAM1 expression with escalating single-dose radiation. However, when subjected to a fractionated regimen of three 2 Gy doses, SUM190 cells showed a robust, three-fold increase in ICAM1 expression, indicating that fractionated LDRT more effectively induces ICAM1 in certain tumor contexts.

We also compared the persistence of ICAM1 induction between tumor cells (HCT116) and normal epithelial cells (293T) following LDRT. As shown in Fig. 2C, tumor cells maintained elevated ICAM1 expression for over one week, whereas levels in normal cells returned to baseline more rapidly. This prolonged induction in tumor cells may be attributed to impaired reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis, which enhances radiation-induced stress signaling and surface protein expression.

To assess whether the upregulated ICAM1 on the cell surface post-radiation could facilitate antibody internalization via antigen-mediated endocytosis, we employed phycoerythrin (PE)-labeled anti-ICAM1 antibodies to track the dynamic procedure of ICAM1 antigen-mediated antibody internalization in HCT116 cells through confocal fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 2F). Quantitative analysis through flow cytometry (Fig. 2D) revealed that by 240 min, the internalization efficiency of anti-ICAM1 antibodies in irradiated KYSE30 cells reached approximately 60%, compared to ∼40% in non-irradiated cells. Similarly, irradiated HCT116 cells exhibited significantly higher antibody internalization efficiency than untreated cells. Immunofluorescent staining showed that anti-ICAM1 antibodies initially bound to the cell surface, and over time, the irradiated cells exhibited a markedly increased rate of internalization compared to non-irradiated cells within the first 60 min (Fig. 2E and F). Moreover, pre-incubating HCT116 cells with unconjugated anti-ICAM1 antibody reduced the binding of PE-labeled anti-ICAM1 antibody, decreasing ICAM1-dependent binding by approximately 40% (Fig. S3A). Consistently, ICAM1 blockade increased the IC₅₀ of ICAM1-DXd in HCT116 cells from 29.09 to 65.57 μg/mL (Fig. S3B). These findings support the ICAM1-dependent binding and cytotoxic activity of ICAM1-DXd. These results demonstrate that LDRT enhances ICAM1 antigen-mediated antibody internalization, supporting the potential of ICAM1 as a viable target for ADC-based therapies in tumors treated with radiotherapy.

LDRT enhances in vivo tumor targeting by anti-ICAM1 antibodies

To investigate the effect of LDRT on the tumor specificity and anti-ICAM1 antibody biodistribution, we utilized an ICAM1-targeting antibody conjugated with the near-infrared fluorescent dye ATTO 647 (ICAM1-ATTO647) in a human colon cancer (HCT116) xenograft model. The fluorescently labeled anti-ICAM1 antibody was administered via tail vein injection (Fig. 3A). The experimental group received 2 Gy radiation treatment 24 h prior to antibody injection, while the control group remained untreated. Tumor and organ biodistribution were analyzed 24 h post-injection using near-infrared fluorescence imaging (Fig. 3B and C). Furthermore, quantified biodistribution of ICAM1-ATTO647 in normal tissues, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and brain, revealed no significant differences between the irradiated and untreated groups (Fig. 3D). Compared to the control group, the fluorescence signal intensity in tumors from radiated mice was approximately two-fold greater than that observed in non-irradiated tumors (Fig. 3E). ROS is associated with LDRT-induced ICAM1 expression

To explore the mechanisms driving LDRT-induced upregulation of ICAM1 antigens, we first measured ROS levels in HCT116, SUM190 and normal 293T cells exposed to various doses of radiation, using DCFH-DA and flow cytometry (Fig. 4A). Correlation analysis showed a positive association between ROS and ICAM1 expression across the three cell lines (Fig.4B). The results revealed a dose-dependent increase in intracellular ROS levels following radiation exposure. To verify the dominant role of ROS on LDRT-induced ICAM1 expression, we next conducted a rescue experiment using the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC) to assess the relationship between ROS production and ICAM1 expression. Synergistic antitumor effects of LDRT and ICAM1-DXd in vivo

We evaluated the efficacy of combining LDRT with ICAM1-DXd, a house-made ADC with DNA topoisomerase inhibitor payload and potent bystander activity, in two xenograft tumor models: HCT116 (colorectal cancer) and SUM190 (breast cancer), both of which are ICAM1 low expression cancer cell lines as illustrated in Fig. 2A. The experimental workflow is shown in Fig. 5A and F. In the HCT116 colorectal cancer model, the combination of LDRT and ICAM1-DXd resulted in a significant 72.1% reduction in tumor volume compared to control group at the end point (Fig. 5B). In contrast, the ICAM1-DXd alone and LDRT-only groups showed only 12.7% and 47.5% reductions, respectively. Consistent with the longitudinal tumor-volume measurements, representative images of the excised HCT116 tumors showed greater tumor inhibition in the LDRT plus ICAM1–DXd group than in either monotherapy group (Fig. 5D). Endpoint tumor weights were not available for this experiment because they were not recorded during terminal tissue collection. Importantly, no significant body weight changes were observed in any group, indicating that the combination therapy did not induce systemic toxicity (Fig. 5E). Similarly, in the SUM190 breast cancer model, the combination treatment resulted in a 96.0% reduction in tumor volume compared to control group at the endpoint (Fig. 5G-H). The excised SUM190 tumors photographed at the endpoint visually mirrored the quantitative findings (Fig. 5I). Single-agent ICAM1-DXd and radiation treatments led to reductions of only 80.2% and 78.1%, respectively. Tumor mass at the endpoint was reduced by 97.4% in the combination group, compared to just 80.3-85.6% reductions in the single-agent groups (Fig. 5J). No significant changes in body weight were observed in any treatment group, suggesting the combination therapy was well-tolerated (Fig. 5K). These results demonstrate that combining ICAM1-DXd with LDRT leads to significantly enhanced tumor growth inhibition in both colorectal and breast cancer models, outperforming single-agent therapies. ICAM1-DXd counters radiotherapy-induced immunosuppressive TAM infiltration

To investigate the underlying biological mechanisms of LDRT and ADC combination therapy, we performed transcriptomic RNA sequencing on HCT116 tumor tissues to identify key genes and signaling pathways altered by the combination treatment (Fig. 6A). Consistent with in vitro findings, ICAM1 expression in tumor tissues from the treatment groups was elevated compared to the control group (Fig. 6B). To evaluate the in vivo effect of LDRT on ICAM1 antigen expression, IHC staining was conducted on tumor samples from HCT116 xenograft mice treated with PBS or LDRT. Microscopic analysis revealed a pronounced increase in ICAM1 expression following radiotherapy, which was corroborated by quantitative assessment. Specifically, the LDRT group exhibited a 26-fold increase in ICAM1 expression compared to the PBS group (Fig. 6C), further supporting the radiation-induced upregulation of ICAM1 and its potential to enhance ICAM1-targeted ADC therapy. Recent studies have demonstrated that hypofractionated radiotherapy promotes the recruitment of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), particularly the immunosuppressive M2-like CCL8high subpopulation, causing an immunosuppressive microenvironment [23]. To investigate this phenomenon in our model, we performed IHC staining on tumor tissues from the four treatment groups to evaluate macrophage infiltration using F4/80 (pan-macrophage marker), CD206 (M2 macrophage marker), and CCL8 (a marker of highly immunosuppressive M2 TAMs). In Fig. 6D, the results indicated that LDRT alone led to a marked increase in M2 macrophages and elevated CCL8 expression. To further validate the clinical relevance of CCL8high TAMs, we analyzed data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) revealed that in both breast cancer (BRCA) and colorectal adenocarcinoma (COAD), patients with high CCL8-expressing TAMs had poorer overall survival compared to those with low CCL8 expression (Fig. 6E). In parallel, Gene Ontology Biological Process (GOBP) enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes under each treatment condition revealed that the LDRT and ICAM1-DXd combination therapy activated inflammation-related pathways such as the positive regulation of inflammatory response, more robustly than LDRT alone (Fig. S4).

We further examined tumor microenvironment remodeling by performing tumor purity analysis and calculating stromal and immune scores across treatment groups (Fig. 6F). The combination group exhibited significantly lower tumor purity than the control, consistent with enhanced infiltration of non-tumor components, particularly immune cells. Collectively, these findings indicate that LDRT promotes an immunosuppressive environment through CCL8high M2 TAM recruitment, while ICAM1-DXd effectively reverses this effect via bystander depletion of these CCL8⁺ M2 macrophages, contributing to a more immunostimulatory tumor milieu.

Safety profile of LDRT/ICAM1-DXd combination therapy in preclinical models

To evaluate the potential for off-target toxicity, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney. No significant histopathological changes or damage were observed in any of the organs examined (Fig. 7A). Discussion

To expand the patient cohort benefiting from current ADC therapy, converting antigen-low tumors into antigen-positive ones remains a critical challenge due to the lack of effective strategies for upregulating ADC target antigens. In this study, we demonstrated that LDRT significantly upregulates ICAM1, a cell membrane protein and promising ADC target antigen, in colorectal, esophageal, and breast cancer cells. This upregulation is mediated by radiation-induced ROS and is closely associated with oxidative stress pathways. Importantly, the increased expression of ICAM1 enhances antibody internalization, significantly boosting the efficacy of ICAM1-targeted ADCs in targeting tumor cells post-radiotherapy. Notably, the bystander effect of ICAM1-targeted ADCs with cleavable linkers effectively eliminates CCL8high M2-like macrophages recruited by radiotherapy, thereby alleviating the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. In xenograft models, the combination of radiotherapy and ICAM1-targeted ADCs resulted in a remarkable tumor volume reduction exceeding 70%, with minimal off-target toxicity. These findings provide a novel approach for enhancing ADC efficacy by converting antigen-low tumors into antigen-positive ones, thus broadening the clinical utility of ADCs.

A key finding of our study is the pivotal role that ROS plays in mediating ICAM1 upregulation following LDRT. By inducing oxidative stress, ROS can activate the transcription factor NFκB, which binds to the promoter of ICAM1, leading to increased ICAM1 expression on the surface of tumor cells [25]. This renders the tumor cells more susceptible to ICAM1-targeted ADC treatment. Our use of the antioxidant NAC to suppress ROS production resulted in a marked reduction in ICAM1 expression. These findings suggest that radiation-induced oxidative stress is associated with increased ICAM1 expression. However, because NAC is a broad antioxidant and may affect multiple cellular pathways, further studies will be required to determine detailed signaling pathway is necessary for LDRT-induced ICAM1 upregulation. Additionally, we screened for changes in other cancer-associated antigens following radiotherapy and found that several clinically-approved ADC targets, such as HER2 and TROP2, did not show a significant increase in expression compared to ICAM1. This suggests that their expression is not responsive to intracellular oxidative stress. However, other radiotherapy-induced cell membrane proteins, such as PD-L1 and MHC class I molecules, have been reported in previous studies [26,27], further supporting the notion that radiotherapy can modify the tumor microenvironment and enhance antigen presentation. This underscores radiotherapy’s broader potential to increase antigen expression across multiple tumor types, enhancing the efficacy of targeted therapies like ADCs.

The relationship between antigen expression and ADC efficacy has been well established in previous clinical studies [28], where higher antigen expression levels often correlate with better clinical outcomes, as reflected by improved ORR and PFS. For example, in the DESTINY-Breast04 trial [29], patients with HER2-low (IHC 1+ or 2+/ISH-low) breast cancer treated with T-DXd demonstrated an ORR of 52.6% and a median PFS of 9.9 months. In contrast, in the DESTINY-Breast01 trial, HER2 3+ patients showed a significantly higher ORR of 61% and a median PFS of 16.4 months. This stark difference highlights the strong correlation between HER2 expression levels and clinical outcomes, where patients with higher HER2 expression derive greater benefit from ADC therapies. Similarly, IMGN853, targeting folate receptor-alpha (FRα), demonstrated an ORR of 31.6% in patients with high FRα expression (IHC 3+), compared to minimal responses in patients with lower FRα expression (IHC 1 or 2) [30]. Likewise, sacituzumab govitecan (SG), targeting TROP2, showed a significant increase in ORR in patients with high TROP2 expression (IHC 3+) compared to those with low expression, as observed in the ASCENT trial for metastatic triple-negative breast cancer [31]. In line with these clinical observations, our study demonstrated that ICAM1 upregulation following radiotherapy significantly increased ADC binding and internalization, leading to enhanced tumor cell killing. The efficacy of ADCs is clearly correlated with target antigen expression levels, and our results align with these findings. Moreover, in our animal models, the combination of radiotherapy and ICAM1-targeted ADCs resulted in a tumor volume reduction of over 70%, further highlighting the therapeutic potential of enhancing antigen expression.

Compared to other methods for enhancing antigen expression, such as HDAC and EGFR inhibitors(5, 7), radiotherapy offers distinct advantages in terms of specificity and clinical applicability. While HDAC and EGFR inhibitors can increase antigen expression, they are often limited to specific genetic contexts and are associated with systemic toxicities. In contrast, radiotherapy can broadly upregulate antigens across multiple tumor types and is already an established treatment modality in clinical cancer management [32]. This makes radiotherapy a more practical and safer option for combination with ADCs across a wider range of tumor types. The combination of radiotherapy-induced antigen upregulation with ADC therapy represents a promising strategy for improving treatment outcomes, particularly in tumors with low or absent antigen expression. By converting antigen-low tumors into antigen-positive ones, radiotherapy expands the pool of patients who can benefit from ADC therapy. Specifically, the upregulation of ICAM1 provides novel therapeutic targets for tumors that have traditionally been resistant to ADC treatment.

More importantly, we observed a novel synergistic benefit when combining radiotherapy and ADCs with bystander killing capabilities. A significant drawback of radiotherapy is its promotion of M2-like macrophage infiltration [23], which reprograms the tumor microenvironment into an immunosuppressive state. However, by leveraging the potent bystander killing activity of ICAM1-DXd, which employs a cleavable GGFG linker, we found that ICAM1-DXd not only eliminates ICAM1-high tumor epithelial cells but also effectively ablates the M2-like macrophages recruited by radiotherapy through the release of DXd payloads in the tumor microenvironment. The reduced macrophage infiltration observed after combination treatment may reflect treatment-associated remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. One possible explanation is that Fcγ receptor-mediated ADC uptake and processing by TAMs, together with the membrane permeability of the released DXd payload, may expose macrophages or neighboring cells to bystander cytotoxicity. Similar interactions between ADCs and macrophages have been reported previously [24]. However, our data do not directly establish DXd-mediated macrophage killing, and alternative explanations, including reduced tumor burden and altered macrophage-recruiting signals, cannot be excluded. This mechanism should therefore warrant further experimental validation.

The combination of radiotherapy-induced antigen upregulation with ADC therapy represents a promising new strategy for improving treatment outcomes, particularly in tumors with low or absent antigen expression. By converting antigen-low tumors into antigen-positive ones, radiotherapy expands the pool of patients who can benefit from ADCs. In particular, the upregulation of ICAM1 offers a novel therapeutic target for tumors that are traditionally resistant to ADCs. This approach could be especially valuable in the treatment of refractory tumors, where current therapeutic options are limited.

Despite the promising results, our study has several limitations. First, the generalizability of these findings to other tumor antigens beyond ICAM1, particularly antigens with clinically approved targeted therapeutics such as EGFR and HER2, needs further investigation. Additionally, the interactions between radiotherapy-induced antigen upregulation and the tumor immune microenvironment remain unexplored. Understanding these interactions could provide valuable insights for combining this strategy with immunotherapies. Future studies should focus on validating the upregulation of ICAM1 and other antigens in additional tumor models and in clinical trials. Furthermore, optimizing the radiotherapy dosing and fractionation schedules will be essential to achieving the maximal therapeutic benefit when combined with ADCs. Although our data support LDRT-enhanced, tumor-preferential anti-ICAM1 antibody accumulation in HCT116 xenografts, ICAM1 is not tumor-specific and may also be expressed in endothelial cells, immune cells, and inflamed normal tissues. Moreover, 293T cells may not fully represent normal tissues exposed to radiotherapy. Future studies should evaluate ICAM1 induction and ICAM1-targeted antibody/ADC uptake in clinically relevant normal cells and irradiated adjacent tissues.

In conclusion, this study provides a compelling rationale for the use of radiotherapy as a means of enhancing tumor-preferential antigen expression in tumors, thereby expanding the applicability of ADC therapy. By identifying ICAM1 as a radiation-induced ADC target, we have conducted the proof-of-concept experiments of combining radiotherapy with ICAM1-targeted ADCs to achieve significant tumor growth inhibition. Further research is needed to optimize this approach, address potential challenges, and fully realize its therapeutic potential in cancer treatment.
