Acute Hepatic Failure in Crimean‐Congo Hemorrhagic Fever: A Case Report
Authors: Ahmed Abdulhussain Shahatta, Sajjad Ghanim Al‐Badri, Khadija Naeem, Abdulrahman Khaldoon Hamid, Nabeel Al‐Fatlawi, Ali Saad Al‐Shammari, Sabry Babiker H Sayed, Abbas Hamza Abbas, Ali Azeez Shlaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcute hepatic failure is a rare but critical complication of Crimean‐Congo hemorrhagic fever (CCHF). This case presents acute hepatic failure with liver enzyme elevation and thrombocytopenia. The patient responded well to treatment that included ribavirin, lactulose, rifaximin, and supportive therapies. This case highlights the importance of early management in CCHF cases, and recovery even in dire clinical scenarios.Keywords: acute hepatic failure, Crimean‐Congo hemorrhagic fever, encephalopathy, ribavirin, thrombocytopenia

Introduction

Crimean‐Congo hemorrhagic fever (CCHF) is a viral infection transmitted primarily through tick bites direct contact with infected animals or body fluids from infected individuals. While endemic in parts of Africa, the Middle East, Asia, and Europe, it is associated with significant morbidity and mortality [1]. The disease's manifestation can be severe, ranging from mild flu‐like symptoms to life‐threatening complications such as multi‐organ failure, and acute hepatic failure and hemorrhagic manifestations like petechiae, ecchymoses, and mucosal bleeding. Mortality rates vary significantly, ranging from 10% to 40%, depending on the healthcare response and timely intervention [2].

CCHF transmission primarily occurs through tick bites, direct contact with the blood or tissues of infected animals, and human‐to‐human transmission via exposure to bodily fluids [1, 3]. The disease often presents in two phases: an early febrile phase with nonspecific symptoms and a later hemorrhagic phase associated with severe complications, including thrombocytopenia and disseminated intravascular coagulation. Rare complications, including hepatic failure, underscore the severe systemic impact of the virus, with the liver frequently being a target of the infection, as evidenced by elevated liver enzymes in the majority of cases [2].

Hepatic failure represents a critical and severe complication of CCHF, impacting the overall prognosis and management of the disease, often leading to significant deterioration in the patient's condition. The occurrence of hepatic failure in CCHF patients significantly increases the risk of mortality, making it imperative for healthcare providers to understand and quickly identify this complication. This underscores the need for heightened vigilance and rapid intervention in cases of suspected or confirmed CCHF, particularly in patients presenting with symptoms indicative of liver impairment.

In this case, a 48‐year‐old male presented with acute hepatic failure associated with CCHF, characterized by high‐grade fever, hemorrhagic manifestations, severe thrombocytopenia, and encephalopathy. The patient required prompt management with ribavirin and supportive care, highlighting the complexity of such presentations.

Case History/Examination

A 48‐year‐old male from Iraq with no significant past medical history presented to the Emergency Room with a high‐grade fever, severe headache, abdominal pain, and repeated vomiting. These symptoms had started 3 days prior to presentation, were progressively worsening, and did not respond to outpatient medications. Approximately 24 h prior to presentation, the patient observed multiple hemorrhagic spots on his skin, particularly at the cannula insertion sites. He also reported passing black tarry stools. A review of other systems was unremarkable. The patient had a temperature of 40.2°C, a pulse rate of 141 bpm, and a blood pressure of 110/80 mmHg. Physical findings revealed diffuse ecchymosis, a tinge of jaundice, and marked tenderness in the right upper quadrant of the abdomen. There was no neck stiffness, and his Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was 15/15.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

Initial laboratory investigations showed (Table 1) a platelet count of 25,000/μL (normal: 150,000–450,000/μL), white blood cell count of 3.81 × 103/μL (normal: 4.0–11.0 × 103/μL), hemoglobin of 12 g/dL (normal: 13.5–17.5 g/dL in males), MCV of 83 μm3 (normal: 80–100 μm3), and MCHC of 29% Hb/cell (normal: 31%–36% Hb/cell). C‐reactive protein (CRP) of 73 mg/L (normal: < 10 mg/L), and blood urea levels were borderline at 40 mg/dL (normal: 16.6–48.5 mg/dL) with normal serum creatinine. Liver enzymes were markedly elevated, with aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 1669 U/L (normal: 10–50 U/L) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 839 U/L (normal: 10–50 U/L). The coagulation profile was deranged, with a prothrombin time (PT) of 19.6 s (normal: 11–13.5 s), an international normalized ratio (INR) of 1.66 (normal: 0.8–1.2), partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 47.6 s (normal: 24–38 s) and Crimean‐Congo hemorrhagic fever polymerase chain reaction (CCHF‐PCR) test was positive.

Shortly after admission, the patient's clinical condition worsened, with a drop in GCS to 3/15, development of deep jaundice, and marked tachypnea. Repeat investigations revealed a significant drop in hemoglobin to 8 g/dL (normal: 13.5–17.5 g/dL in males) and persistent severe thrombocytopenia with platelets at 18,000/μL (normal: 150,000–450,000/μL). Asterixis was elicited in the lower limbs, indicative of encephalopathy.

The patient was managed with ribavirin that was administered as a loading dose of 30 mg/kg, followed by 16 mg/kg every 6 h for the first 4 days, then 8 mg/kg every 8 h for the next 6 days (total 10‐day course). Ribavirin was chosen due to its established efficacy in inhibiting the replication of the CCHF virus, thus reducing viral load. Lactulose was administered through an NG tube with an initial dose of 30 g twice daily to manage hepatic encephalopathy by lowering blood ammonia levels and improving mental status. Rifaximin was given at a dose of 400 mg twice daily through an NG tube for 10 days to reduce gut flora that produces ammonia, complementing the treatment for encephalopathy. The patient received platelet transfusion and fresh frozen plasma (FFP) to address severe thrombocytopenia and support the coagulation system. Additionally, supportive care was provided, including intravenous fluids, electrolyte management, and respiratory support as needed to stabilize the patient's vital signs.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Throughout treatment, the patient's liver enzyme levels slightly decreased, and his GCS improved, returning to 15/15. The patient was discharged after 10 days of hospitalization. Follow‐up 2 weeks and 1 month post‐discharge showed continued improvement in liver function tests, with normalization of liver enzymes and platelet count. No signs of persistent hepatic dysfunction or other complications were noted during the follow‐up period. The patient has since returned to normal activity levels with no reported long‐term effects from the disease.

Discussion

CCHF is a severe tick‐borne viral infection that leads to significant morbidity and mortality. The infection, caused by the CCHF virus, is primarily transmitted through tick bites or contact with infected animal blood and tissues [4, 5]. In our case, the 48‐year‐old male patient from Iraq presented with rapid progression to acute hepatic failure, a rare but critical complication of CCHF, which highlights the importance of early diagnosis and intervention. As reported by Fillâtre et al., CCHF often begins with nonspecific symptoms such as fever, headache, and myalgia, followed by severe hemorrhagic manifestations [6]. The patient's presentation, including high‐grade fever, severe thrombocytopenia, and hepatic dysfunction, was consistent with typical CCHF progression, though the acute hepatic failure observed in this case occurred more rapidly than in most reported cases. In agreement with Mazzola and Kelly‐Cirino, who emphasize the importance of early detection for managing severe forms of CCHF, the patient's outcome underscores the need for prompt and aggressive treatment [5]. In the literature, the management of CCHF, especially in cases with hepatic involvement, typically relies on supportive care, as there are no definitive antiviral treatments approved for widespread use [1, 7]. Ribavirin, an antiviral agent, remains the cornerstone of treatment in CCHF. However, its effectiveness, especially in severe cases with multi‐organ involvement, remains inconclusive. While some studies report a reduction in viral replication with ribavirin use, Fabara et al. noted that its role as a definitive treatment for CCHF is still debated [1]. In this case, ribavirin was administered alongside supportive therapies, which included lactulose and rifaximin for hepatic encephalopathy management, consistent with the treatment strategies recommended by Arslan and Comoglu [8].

A significant feature of CCHF is the endothelial dysfunction observed during infection. The virus infects endothelial cells, leading to increased vascular permeability and hemorrhage, which exacerbates the severity of the disease [6, 9]. In this case, the development of ecchymosis and significant liver enzyme elevation points to extensive endothelial damage, a hallmark of severe CCHF infection [4]. This finding aligns with previous studies, including Ergönül, who highlighted the critical role of endothelial infection in the progression of CCHF [3].

The role of early ribavirin administration is crucial, especially as rural and farming communities are at higher risk, as demonstrated by the study conducted in Iraq, which reported a high incidence of CCHF cases related to occupational exposure [4]. While some studies suggest that ribavirin's effect may be more pronounced when given early (within 48 h of symptom onset), the evidence remains mixed, with some findings indicating no significant improvement in patient outcomes [7]. This case supports the use of ribavirin for post‐exposure prophylaxis and early treatment, given the patient's rapid deterioration and the availability of limited treatment options.

In conclusion, this case highlights the importance of early intervention and multidisciplinary care for patients with severe forms of CCHF. While ribavirin and supportive care provide some benefits, more research is needed to identify definitive treatment protocols and assess the efficacy of antiviral agents in severe cases. Additionally, with the increasing global incidence of CCHF, especially in endemic regions like Iraq, heightened awareness and improved surveillance are necessary to manage this potentially deadly disease effectively.
