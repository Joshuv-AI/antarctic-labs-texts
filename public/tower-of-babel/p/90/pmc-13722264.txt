Oral health status and treatment needs of pregnant women attending primary and tertiary healthcare centres of Telangana, India: A population-based cross-sectional study
Authors: V Pranitha, Anurag Negi, Frijo Xavier, Vijay Yadav, Nabnita Patnaik, G N Kusneniwar, Badam Vijomai, Kavita Chandrasekaran, Shruthi Nayak, M R Rakhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTContext:Maternal oral health plays an important role in pregnancy outcomes but is often overlooked in antenatal care in India.Aims:This study aimed to assess and compare oral health status and treatment needs among pregnant women attending primary and tertiary healthcare centres in Telangana.Settings and Design:A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted among 362 primigravida women recruited from eight primary health centres (PHCs) and a tertiary care hospital.Methods and Materials:Oral health status and treatment needs were assessed using WHO Oral Health Assessment Form (2013) with Decayed, Missing, and Filled Teeth Index; Community Periodontal Index; and Simplified Oral Hygiene Index (OHI-S).Statistical Analysis Used:Data were analysed using Chi-square and independent t-tests with P < 0.05 considered significant.Results:PHC participants showed significantly lower socioeconomic status, poorer oral hygiene practices, and fewer dental visits during pregnancy (P = 0.001). The mean OHI-S scores for each participant were significantly higher in the PHC group (2.83 ± 1.21) than in tertiary participants (2.42 ± 1.15; P = 0.001), reflecting comparatively poorer oral hygiene in the PHC group. Both groups showed high levels of dental caries and periodontal disease. However, women attending the PHC had substantially higher treatment needs. The requirement for root canal treatment was significantly higher in the PHC group (P = 0.040). Overall dental treatment needs were greater among PHC participants (P = 0.003).Conclusion:Significant disparities exist in oral health status and access to dental care between primary and tertiary healthcare settings, with a higher burden of untreated disease in primary care. Integrating oral health screening and preventive services into routine antenatal care is essential to improve both maternal and foetal health outcomes.

Introduction

Oral health during pregnancy is increasingly regarded as a significant public health concern due to the consistently high burden of dental diseases observed among pregnant women across diverse populations. Globally, dental caries has been reported to affect approximately 20% to 90% of pregnant women, depending on regional and socioeconomic factors, while the prevalence of periodontal disease ranges from 30% to nearly 100% in this population.[12]

Physiological and hormonal changes during pregnancy, particularly elevated oestrogen and progesterone levels, increase susceptibility to gingival inflammation and periodontal disease.[34] Longitudinal studies demonstrate that gingival inflammation commonly intensifies through the second trimester and tends to subside after delivery.[5] Beyond local manifestations, maternal periodontitis has been associated with adverse obstetric outcomes, including preterm birth, low birth weight, and intrauterine growth restriction, reinforcing its relevance to maternal and foetal health.[678]

Despite the recognised burden, utilisation of dental care during pregnancy remains low due to socioeconomic constraints, limited awareness, misconceptions about the safety of dental treatment, and inadequate availability of oral health services.[9] Indian studies similarly report high levels of untreated dental caries and periodontal disease among antenatal women, indicating persistent gaps in integrating oral healthcare into routine maternal health services.[1011] National antenatal guidelines in India do not mandate routine oral health screening, creating missed opportunities for prevention and early management.

In this broader national context, region-specific assessments are necessary to develop targeted interventions. Telangana, like several Indian states, shows disparities in healthcare access between primary health centres (PHCs) and tertiary hospitals. Tertiary hospitals often provide dental departments, trained personnel, and preventive counselling, whereas PHCs frequently lack dedicated dental facilities or regular dental staff.[12] Such differences may contribute to variations in oral health status, unmet treatment needs, and patterns of service utilisation among pregnant women attending these centres.

From a clinical perspective, early identification of oral disease during pregnancy enables timely preventive and therapeutic interventions, reducing disease progression and improving maternal outcomes. For healthcare providers, especially in primary care settings, understanding the burden of oral disease can facilitate structured referrals and integration of oral screening into antenatal services. For patients, improved awareness and access to dental care can enhance overall health and pregnancy outcomes. Thus, this study provides clinically relevant evidence to support integration of oral health into maternal healthcare programmes.

Subjects and Methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted to evaluate and compare the oral health status and treatment needs of primigravid women attending PHCs and a tertiary healthcare facility in Telangana, India. The study was carried out in the Yadadri Bhuvanagiri district, a predominantly rural region characterised by limited access to oral healthcare services. Eight PHCs – Bhongir, Bibinagar, Bollepally, Yadagirigutta, Kondamadugu, Valigonda, Motakondur, and Bommalaramaram – were purposively selected. AIIMS Bibinagar, which provides comprehensive antenatal and dental services, was included as the tertiary care centre to enable comparison between different levels of healthcare delivery.

The study population comprised primigravid women aged 18–30 years attending routine antenatal clinics at the selected PHCs and the tertiary centre. Eligibility required confirmation of primigravidity by an obstetrician or a medical officer at the respective facility. Only women attending antenatal care services at one of the selected centres and willing to provide written informed consent after receiving an explanation of the study in their preferred language were enrolled.

Women were excluded if they had systemic illnesses known to affect oral health, including uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, hypertension, renal disease, cardiac disorders, or epilepsy. They were also excluded if they reported long-term use of medications capable of altering oral status, such as corticosteroids or anticonvulsants. Additional exclusion criteria included multiple gestations, a history of stillbirth or recurrent miscarriages, and pregnancies classified as high-risk under national maternal health guidelines, such as those complicated by severe anaemia, pregnancy-induced hypertension, gestational diabetes, or placenta previa. Participants who reported deleterious oral habits, including tobacco chewing, smoking, betel nut or areca nut use, or habitual alcohol consumption, were also excluded to reduce confounding influences on oral health outcomes.

A two-stage sampling approach was followed. In the first stage, eight PHCs were selected purposively to represent rural service areas within the district. In the second stage, eligible primigravid women attending antenatal clinics at the selected PHCs and the tertiary centre were recruited through consecutive sampling during the study period.

The sample size was estimated using the formula n = (Z2 × p × q)/m2, for a single population proportion, with a Z value of 1.96 for a 95% confidence level, an expected prevalence (p) of periodontal disease of 0.95 based on a previous study conducted among pregnant women in Raichur, Karnataka,[10] a complementary value (q) of 0.05, and a desired precision (m) of 0.05. The minimum required sample size was calculated to be 362 participants. To allow meaningful comparison across healthcare levels, the final sample was divided equally, with 181 women assigned to the PHC group and 181 to the tertiary care group. Proportionate allocation was applied to distribute the PHC sample across the eight centres according to their antenatal caseloads.

The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of AIIMS Bibinagar (Approval No.: AIIMS/BBN/IEC/Apr/2025/664). Administrative permission to conduct data collection at the selected PHCs was obtained from the District Health Officer of Yadadri Bhuvanagiri district. Data collection was carried out over a 6-month period from May to October 2025.

Clinical examinations were conducted by a single trained examiner to minimise inter-examiner variability. The examiner was trained and calibrated prior to data collection to ensure consistency in clinical measurements. Examinations were performed within the antenatal units of the participating centres using a mouth mirror, CPITN probe, and adequate illumination in accordance with WHO infection control guidelines. Oral health status and treatment needs were assessed using the WHO Oral Health Assessment Form (2013).[13] Dental caries experience was recorded using the Decayed, Missing, and Filled Teeth (DMFT) index, while periodontal status was evaluated using the Community Periodontal Index (CPI) and measurements of clinical attachment loss. Oral mucosal lesions were systematically screened and documented, and oral hygiene status was measured using the Simplified Oral Hygiene Index (OHI-S) described by Greene and Vermillion.[14] Treatment needs were categorised according to WHO diagnostic criteria.

Statistical analysis was carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All continuous variables, including age, DMFT scores, OHI-S scores, and periodontal parameters, were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables demonstrating normal distribution were summarised as means with standard deviations, whereas non-normally distributed variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges. Categorical variables, such as oral hygiene practices, CPI scores, presence of oral mucosal lesions, and treatment needs, were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Comparative analyses between the PHC and tertiary care groups were performed using the independent samples t-test for continuous variables that met normality assumptions, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for variables that were non-normally distributed. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

A total of 362 primigravid women aged 18–30 years were included in the analysis, with equal numbers recruited from the eight selected PHCs and the tertiary facility. The mean age of participants was 23.1 ± 2.6 years, and the distribution of women across trimesters showed no significant intergroup differences (P = 0.967). Women attending PHCs were substantially more likely to belong to lower socioeconomic strata than those at the tertiary centre (62.4% vs. 32.6%, P < 0.001). Most participants in both groups were homemakers from rural backgrounds [Table 1].

Oral hygiene practices demonstrated significant variation across settings. The use of a toothbrush and toothpaste was more frequent among women visiting the tertiary centre (84.8%) than those attending PHCs (72.4%, P = 0.007). Traditional cleaning methods such as charcoal, herbal sticks, or finger cleaning were used more often by women at primary centres (27.6% vs. 15.2%, P = 0.007). Dental service utilisation during pregnancy remained low across both settings, although significantly more women at the tertiary facility reported having consulted a dentist during pregnancy compared with those at PHCs (35.4% vs. 19.3%, P = 0.001) [Table 1].

Dental caries was prevalent in both groups, affecting 69.6% of primary-care attendees and 61.9% of tertiary-care participants, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.121). The mean DMFT score per participant was comparable between groups (2.63 ± 2.74 at primary centres vs. 2.19 ± 2.49 at the tertiary centre; P = 0.111), and the individual components of the index showed no significant intergroup differences. Oral hygiene assessment using the OHI-S indicated that poor hygiene status was more frequent among women at primary centres than among those at the tertiary facility (46.4% vs. 36.9%), although the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.088) [Table 2]. The mean OHI-S score, however, was significantly higher among primary-care participants (2.83 ± 1.21) compared with women at the tertiary centre (2.42 ± 1.15; P = 0.001), indicating poorer oral cleanliness among rural antenatal attendees [Table 2].

Periodontal examination revealed that bleeding on probing occurred with a higher frequency among women at primary centres and the tertiary facility (8.8% vs. 7.2%, P = 0.699). Calculus deposition was significantly more common among tertiary-care participants (59.1%) than among those at the primary centres (48.1%; P = 0.045). Shallow periodontal pockets (4–5 mm) were observed in 29.3% of women at primary centres and 21.5% at the tertiary facility (P = 0.117), whereas deep periodontal pockets (≥6 mm) were present in 9.4% and 5.0% of the respective groups (P = 0.154). Although these differences were not statistically significant, the overall periodontal disease prevalence of 94.2% underscored widespread pregnancy-associated gingival and periodontal inflammation across both settings [Table 3].

Oral mucosal findings were largely unremarkable, with 93.4% of participants exhibiting clinically healthy mucosa. Intergroup differences were minimal and nonsignificant (P = 0.139). Pregnancy epulis was observed in small proportions of both groups, with a higher frequency among women attending primary centres (3.3% vs. 1.1%; P = 0.283). Oral submucous fibrosis occurred predominantly in primary-care attendees (2.8% vs. 0.6%, P = 0.217), reflecting higher areca-nut use in rural communities. Rare lesions such as leukoplakia and ulceration were identified only among primary-care participants; however, their low frequency precluded meaningful statistical comparison [Table 3].

Treatment needs were substantial across the study population, with 90.9% of women requiring at least one dental intervention. The need for one-surface restorations was higher among women attending primary centres (37.6%) than among those at the tertiary facility (28.7%), although this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.094). Two-surface restorations followed a similar pattern (29.8% vs. 22.7%, P = 0.152). The requirement for RCT was significantly greater among primary-care participants (19.3%) compared with women at the tertiary centre (11.0%; P = 0.040). Tooth extraction needs were also more frequent among primary-care attendees (15.5% vs. 9.9%), although this difference did not reach significance (P = 0.156). Prosthetic needs were minimal and comparable between groups. Preventive care requirements were higher among women attending primary centres (28.2%) than among those at the tertiary facility (19.9%), indicating a trend toward greater unmet preventive needs (P = 0.085). Overall, the proportion of women requiring any form of dental treatment was significantly higher in the primary-care group (95.6%) compared with the tertiary-care group (86.2%; P = 0.003) [Table 4 and Figure 1].

Discussion

Pregnancy involves complex endocrine, vascular, and immunological alterations that markedly influence oral tissues. Increased levels of oestrogen and progesterone enhance gingival vascularity and modify immune responses, heightening susceptibility to plaque-induced gingival inflammation and periodontal changes. While these physiological shifts are largely reversible, inadequate oral hygiene can exacerbate existing periodontal disease or accelerate the progression of untreated conditions.[151617]

The mean age reported in this study is consistent with the young maternal demographic typical of rural and semi-urban regions in India, and restricting the sample to primigravidae minimised confounding arising from cumulative oral-disease exposure across multiple pregnancies. The significantly higher proportion of lower-socioeconomic-status women attending PHCs reflects the well-established socioeconomic gradient in oral health across Indian settings such as Bhubaneswar[18] and similarly observed in international studies from Australia[19] and Tanzania.[20]

Oral hygiene behaviours varied substantially between the two groups, with PHC attendees more frequently relying on traditional cleaning methods such as charcoal or herbal sticks, a pattern consistent with reports from rural Indian communities.[21] In contrast, cohorts in Brazil demonstrate near-universal toothbrush use, highlighting structural and cultural differences in oral-health behaviours.[22] The significantly poorer OHI-S scores among PHC participants are consistent with evidence indicating that nausea, vomiting, and fatigue during pregnancy, when combined with limited professional guidance, reduce the effectiveness of daily oral hygiene.[23]

Dental-service utilisation remained low overall, particularly in the PHC group. This finding is consistent with previous Indian and international studies reporting low utilisation of dental services during pregnancy due to lack of awareness, perceived safety concerns, and limited access to care.[101824] International evidence further highlights that misconceptions regarding the safety of dental treatment and lack of structured referral pathways contribute significantly to poor service utilisation.[2526]

Periodontal disease was highly prevalent, affecting more than 94% of participants, which aligns with global estimates reporting pregnancy-associated periodontal inflammation in 60–90% of women.[2728] The predominance of shallow pockets and low frequency of attachment loss are consistent with longitudinal evidence from Ethiopia showing that pregnancy-associated gingival inflammation seldom causes irreversible periodontal destruction within a single gestational period.[29] Nevertheless, the established association between maternal periodontitis and adverse obstetric outcomes, including preterm birth and low birth weight,[3031] underscores the importance of incorporating periodontal screening into routine antenatal care.

The caries burden observed in this study is consistent with findings from Indian cohorts[1032] and exceeds those reported in several developing countries where dietary patterns include lower refined-sugar intake.[3334] The dominance of untreated decay and the minimal presence of restored teeth suggest significant unmet treatment needs, particularly among PHC attendees.

Oral mucosal findings were infrequent, consistent with earlier studies.[35] Pregnancy epulis occurred at rates comparable to existing literature,[36] and oral submucous fibrosis was more prevalent among PHC participants, reflecting higher areca-nut use in rural communities.

Treatment needs were substantial, with more than 90% of participants requiring intervention. The significantly higher requirement for RCT among PHC women suggests delayed presentation and disease progression, a pattern similarly reported in underserved Indian populations.[1037]

These findings indicate inadequate integration of oral health into primary antenatal care, with system-level barriers such as limited dental personnel, insufficient counselling, and weak referral pathways contributing to the higher burden of untreated disease. System-level barriers, including the absence of trained dental personnel in PHCs, limited oral-health counselling, and inadequate referral pathways, likely contribute to the higher burden of untreated disease observed in PHC attendees. Strengthening antenatal oral-health integration will require routine oral-health screening, structured referral mechanisms linking PHCs with dental facilities, and targeted training for frontline healthcare workers, including ASHAs and auxiliary nurse midwives. Community-oriented strategies focusing on oral hygiene, diet, and reduction of tobacco and areca-nut use have the potential to improve maternal oral health substantially.

Methodologically, the study benefits from its focus on primigravidae, use of standardised WHO indices, and single-examiner approach. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference. The study was conducted in a single district using purposive sampling, which may affect generalisability. Examiner calibration was not quantitatively assessed, and behavioural data were self-reported, introducing potential bias.

Future research should include multicentric longitudinal studies to assess changes in oral health across pregnancy and postpartum periods. Interventional studies evaluating the integration of oral health screening into antenatal care, particularly at PHC, are warranted. Additionally, community-based oral health education programmes should be explored to improve awareness and service utilisation. This study is among the first from Telangana to provide a comparative evaluation of oral health status and treatment needs between primary and tertiary antenatal care settings. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on single-centre populations, this study highlights system-level disparities in oral healthcare access and utilisation, providing important insights for strengthening primary healthcare services.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that pregnant women in Telangana, particularly those attending primary health centres, experience a substantial burden of untreated oral disease, characterised by a high prevalence of dental caries, periodontal inflammation, and unmet treatment needs. Poorer oral health outcomes in the primary healthcare group reflect the influence of socioeconomic disadvantage, limited oral health awareness, and insufficient integration of dental services into routine antenatal care.

Strengthening oral health within primary maternal healthcare through routine screening, patient education, timely referrals, and training of frontline healthcare providers may help reduce preventable oral disease during pregnancy. Despite limitations related to study design and setting, these findings provide important baseline evidence supporting the integration of oral health into antenatal care programmes to improve maternal health outcomes.
