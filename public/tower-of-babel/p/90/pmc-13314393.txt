Effects of Citrus unshiu Intake on Serum Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor Levels and Mood States: A Pilot Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Kensuke Nakajima, Takahiro Muro, Masatsugu Ohgami, Shigeru Oiso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) is a critical mood regulator; increasing BDNF levels may help improve mood states. We conducted a pilot randomized controlled trial to investigate the effects of Citrus unshiu intake on serum BDNF levels and mood states. Healthy participants were assigned to an intake group [n=13; two C. unshiu fruits daily (106.9±37.2 g each)] or a non-intake group (n=13). Serum BDNF levels and mood states were assessed at Weeks 0, 4, and 8. In the primary analysis, a linear mixed-effects model revealed no significant group-by-time interaction contrast for serum BDNF levels, and no significant between-group differences were observed at any time point. Serum BDNF levels declined in the non-intake group over time, whereas an increase occurred from Week 4 to Week 8 in the intake group. In exploratory and unadjusted analyses, we examined within-group changes in serum BDNF levels. In the intake group, the change from Week 4 to Week 8 (median: +0.636 ng/mL) was significantly greater than that from Week 0 to Week 4 (median: −2.453 ng/mL). Based on the Profile of Mood States 2nd Edition results, between-group comparisons of change scores showed significant improvements in Depression-Dejection and Vigor-Activity scores in the intake group compared with the non-intake group. Within-group analyses demonstrated decreases in Depression-Dejection and Total Mood Disturbance and an increase in Vigor-Activity following C. unshiu intake. The findings of our preliminary study suggest that C. unshiu intake may be associated with attenuating the decline in serum BDNF levels and inducing modest favorable changes in certain mood domains.

INTRODUCTION

Mood disturbance is an important psychological outcome that can substantially affect daily functioning and overall mental health. In modern society, psychological stress has been steadily increasing due to rapid social changes, economic instability, and excessive exposure to information (Daly and Macchia, 2023). Although mood disturbances and chronic stress are conceptually distinct, chronic stress may contribute to the development of mood disturbances, including subclinical emotional dysregulation, which may progress to psychiatric disorders such as depression and anxiety disorders (Al Bazzal et al., 2025). These mood disturbances, even in mild or subclinical forms, can substantially impair daily functioning and impose considerable social and economic burdens. Notably, depressive and anxiety disorders consistently rank among the leading contributors to years lived with disability worldwide, underscoring their profound public health impact (GBD 2021 Diseases and Injuries Collaborators, 2024).

Although pharmacological treatments for depression and anxiety are available, established treatment options for mild or subclinical mood disturbances remain scarce. Because these mood disturbances can meaningfully reduce overall well-being, there is a growing need to develop simple, safe, and accessible approaches to prevent and alleviate mood disturbances－including those that do not meet diagnostic thresholds for psychiatric disorders. Among these approaches, dietary interventions have emerged as a promising strategy for modulating psychological well-being as part of daily lifestyle behaviors (Marx et al., 2021). In particular, dietary components such as polyphenols, omega-3 fatty acids, fermented foods, and flavonoid-rich citrus fruits have been increasingly investigated as functional foods with potential benefits for emotional health (Osher and Belmaker, 2009; Aslam et al., 2020; Park et al., 2020; Gamage et al., 2023).

As a biological marker associated with mood states, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) has been widely investigated (Hashimoto, 2010; Castrén and Monteggia, 2021). BDNF is also implicated in mood disturbances, including depression and anxiety disorders. BDNF is a neurotrophin that plays a significant role in neurogenesis, axonal and dendritic growth, neuronal survival, and synaptic plasticity (Zhao et al., 2017; Barde, 2025). A reduction in BDNF levels in the brain has been associated with mood disorders in human clinical and in vivo animal studies (Duman and Monteggia, 2006; Yeh et al., 2015; Gliwińska et al., 2023). Furthermore, reduced BDNF levels have been reported in the postmortem brains of patients with depression and other mood disorders (Thompson Ray et al., 2011; Guilloux et al., 2012). In addition, even in healthy individuals, exposure to psychological stress can reduce circulating BDNF levels, suggesting that BDNF is involved in mood regulation beyond clinical populations (Mitoma et al., 2008; Okuno et al., 2011).

In humans, BDNF is synthesized in the brain as well as in peripheral tissues, including the kidneys, lungs, heart, and skeletal muscles (Matthews et al., 2009; Barrios and Ai, 2018; Endlich et al., 2018; Rana et al., 2025). In rodents, BDNF migrates to the brain by crossing the blood-brain barrier (Pan et al., 1998; Lima Giacobbo et al., 2019). Given this background, we hypothesized that compounds capable of promoting BDNF production in peripheral tissues may exert beneficial effects on mood disturbances, including depressive and anxiety disorders, by increasing brain BDNF levels, even if the compounds themselves lack blood-brain barrier permeability.

To explore such compounds, we established a screening system for BDNF upregulators using the human kidney adenocarcinoma cell line ACHN (Nakajima et al., 2020). Furthermore, we demonstrated that the flesh of Citrus unshiu, commonly known as Satsuma mandarin, significantly upregulated BDNF mRNA and protein levels in ACHN cells (Nakajima et al., 2024). C. unshiu is a widely consumed citrus fruit in various countries such as China, South Korea, Japan, Spain, and Turkey, known for its seedlessness, ease of peeling, and sweetness (Liu et al., 2025). Foxtail millet and the traditional Japanese Kampo medicine Hochuekkito were identified as BDNF-enhancing candidates in our ACHN cell-based screening assay and were subsequently shown in vivo in rodents to respectively increase BDNF levels and attenuate age-related declines in BDNF (Nakajima et al., 2023, 2025). Accordingly, C. unshiu may also positively influence serum BDNF levels and mood states in vivo.

We hypothesized that dietary C. unshiu intake would increase serum BDNF concentrations and improve mood states. To test this hypothesis, we conducted a pilot randomized controlled trial in healthy participants.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was conducted from November 13, 2024, to January 8, 2025 (8 weeks), at the Faculty of Pharmaceutical Sciences, Nagasaki International University, Nagasaki, Japan. This study was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Pharmaceutical Sciences at Nagasaki International University (Approval No. 69). All procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines, regulations, and the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Participants

Participants were recruited from students at Nagasaki International University. The age range of the participants was from 22 to 31 years, and 26 participants were recruited (11 males, 15 females). The exclusion criteria were: (1) current treatment for any disease, (2) current use of medications, (3) current intake of functional foods or supplements related to mental health or sleep disorders, (4) past or present diagnosis of psychiatric disorders, (5) habitual intake of citrus fruits, (6) allergies to citrus and alcohol, and (7) current smokers. Although pregnancy was not specified as an exclusion criterion, no participants were pregnant. The participants received both oral and written explanations regarding the study’s objectives, significance, schedule, potential risks, and compensation. Participants received a modest compensation after completing the study, in accordance with institutional guidelines.

Study design

A total of 26 healthy participants were randomly assigned to the C. unshiu intake group (CU; n=13, 5 males and 8 females) and the C. unshiu non-intake group (NCU; n=13, 6 males and 7 females). The CU group consumed two C. unshiu per day (average weight: 106.9±37.2 g per fruit) for 8 weeks, whereas the NCU group received neither a placebo nor any alternative to C. unshiu. Participants in the CU group were instructed to consume only the two C. unshiu fruits provided by the investigators and to refrain from consuming any other citrus. The NCU group was instructed to refrain from consuming any citrus, including C. unshiu. The C. unshiu variety used in this trial was Saikai No. 1, cultivated in Saikai City, Nagasaki, Japan (Fig. 1).

The schedule of C. unshiu consumption, blood collection, and mood states assessment is shown in Fig. 2. At the start of the study (Week 0), Week 4, and Week 8, all participants completed the Profile of Mood States 2nd Edition (POMS2), a widely used questionnaire for assessing mood states, followed immediately by blood collection. POMS2 testing and blood collection were conducted between 13:30 and 16:00 on November 13, 2024 (Week 0), December 11, 2024 (Week 4), and January 8, 2025 (Week 8).

Measurement of serum BDNF concentrations

The blood samples from healthy participants were collected in BD MicrotainerⓇ blood collection tubes (Cat. No.: 365967; Becton, Dickinson and Company) following the measurement of mood states using the POMS2. After a 2-h incubation at room temperature, serum was obtained by centrifugation at 15,000 g for 90 s at 20°C, according to the protocol recommended for the BD MicrotainerⓇ blood collection tubes, and stored at −80°C until analysis. Serum BDNF concentrations were measured using the Mature BDNF ELISA kit Wako, High Sensitive (Cat. No.: 290-85801, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation).

Primary outcome: between-group analyses of serum BDNF concentrations

To evaluate the effects of the intervention in this pilot randomized controlled trial, we conducted between-group analyses of serum BDNF levels using (i) a linear mixed-effects model with fixed effects for group (CU vs. NCU), time (Weeks 0, 4, and 8; treated as a categorical factor), group-by-time interactions, and a random intercept for participant ID; and (ii) an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model for Week 8 BDNF levels including group and baseline BDNF levels (Week 0) as covariates. Model-based estimates are presented with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Exploratory analyses: within-group analyses of serum BDNF concentrations

In addition to the primary between-group analyses, exploratory within-group analyses were conducted to examine changes in serum BDNF concentrations over time. Within-group changes between Week 0 and Week 4, and between Week 4 and Week 8 were evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Effect sizes and corresponding P-values were presented for these exploratory analyses.

Mood states survey using the POMS2

Mood states, including subclinical emotional disturbances, are often evaluated using validated psychological scales such as the POMS2. The POMS2 assesses Total Mood Disturbance (TMD) along with five negative and two positive mood subscales. Because the effects of food intake can vary across specific mood domains, it is important to evaluate the POMS2 subscales in addition to the TMD score, as in previous studies investigating the mood-related effects of dietary components and functional foods (Higashikawa et al., 2020; Watanabe et al., 2022; Tanihiro et al., 2024).

We administered the adult version of the POMS2 online (Kaneko Shobo Co., Ltd.) to the participants at Weeks 0, 4, and 8 to assess their mood states over the previous 7 days. The full Japanese translation of the adult POMS2 consists of 65 questions rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “not at all” to “extremely” (Yokoyama and Watanabe, 2015).

The five negative subscales include Anger-Hostility (AH), Confusion-Bewilderment (CB), Depression-Dejection (DD), Fatigue-Inertia (FI), and Tension-Anxiety (TA). Higher scores on these subscales indicate stronger negative mood states. The two positive mood subscales comprise Vigor-Activity (VA) and Friendliness (F), with higher scores reflecting more positive mood states. The TMD score is calculated by summing the scores of five negative mood subscales and subtracting the score for VA, but F score is not included (Yokoyama and Watanabe, 2015). A higher TMD score indicates more severe overall mood disturbance. Each mood cluster and TMD score is expressed as T-scores, standardized by age and sex, with a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10.

The POMS2 is a well-validated and widely used instrument that provides a comprehensive assessment of multidimensional mood states (Yokoyama and Watanabe, 2015). Accordingly, we used the POMS2 to evaluate participants’ mood states from multiple perspectives. To evaluate the internal consistency of the POMS2 in our sample, we calculated Cronbach’s α coefficients. Cronbach’s α values indicated acceptable to good internal consistency across all POMS2 subscales and TMD: 0.85 for AH, 0.84 for CB, 0.91 for DD, 0.78 for FI, 0.89 for TA, 0.91 for VA, and 0.68 for F, while the α coefficient for TMD was 0.90.

Statistical analysis

Values are presented as mean±standard deviation or median. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and JMP software (version 17, SAS Institute Inc.). Baseline characteristics were compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney U-test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for sex.

For the primary between-group analyses of serum BDNF concentrations, a linear mixed-effects model was fitted with group, time, and group-by-time interaction as fixed effects and participant ID as a random intercept. Time was treated as a categorical factor with three levels: Week 0, Week 4, and Week 8. In the model-based estimates, group differences were defined as CU minus NCU. The group-by-time interaction contrasts were parameterized as [(CU−NCU at Week 0)−(CU−NCU at Week 8)] and [(CU−NCU at Week 4)−(CU−NCU at Week 8)]. An ANCOVA model was also used to compare Week 8 serum BDNF levels between groups, with group as the explanatory factor and baseline BDNF level at Week 0 as a covariate.

Exploratory within-group changes in serum BDNF levels were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Cronbach’s α coefficients were calculated to evaluate the internal consistency of the POMS2 subscales and Total Mood Disturbance. Between-group comparisons of changes in POMS2 scores from Week 0 to Week 4, from Week 4 to Week 8, and from Week 0 to Week 8 were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Within-group comparisons of POMS2 scores were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Associations between serum BDNF levels and POMS2 scores were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as P<0.05. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed. The within-group analyses, POMS2 analyses, and correlation analyses were exploratory and unadjusted.

RESULTS

Effects of C. unshiu intake on serum BDNF concentrations

No significant differences were observed between the two groups in age, sex, and baseline serum BDNF levels (Table 1). Serum BDNF concentrations in the CU group were 15.58±6.64 ng/mL at Week 0, 12.34±5.63 ng/mL at Week 4, and 13.75±9.21 ng/mL at Week 8, whereas those in the NCU group were 17.39±11.36, 14.65±12.78, and 12.43±5.70 ng/mL at the corresponding time points (Fig. 3). The mean serum BDNF concentration tended to increase from Week 4 to Week 8, showing an approximate rise of 1.41 ng/mL (11.4%).

Between-group analyses: The group-by-time interaction contrast for Week 0 vs. Week 8 (CU−NCU) was −3.130 (95% CI −11.730 to 5.470; P=0.468), and the contrast for Week 4 vs. Week 8 (CU−NCU) was −3.632 (95% CI −12.232 to 4.967; P=0.400) (Table 2). Because time contrasts were defined as Week 0－Week 8 and Week 4－Week 8, a negative interaction contrast indicates that the between-group difference (CU−NCU) was larger at Week 8 than at Week 0 or Week 4 under this parameterization (i.e., the difference-in-differences). The main effect of group corresponds to the between-group difference at the reference time point (Week 8) and was not significant (estimate +1.320, 95% CI −5.730 to 8.370; P=0.710) (Table 2). In addition, no significant between-group differences were observed at any time point.

We also performed an ANCOVA adjusting for baseline BDNF levels (Week 0). After adjusting for baseline BDNF (Week 0), the estimated between-group difference in Week 8 BDNF (CU−NCU) was +0.860 (95% CI −2.213 to 3.933; P=0.568) (Table 2). Overall, across all analyses, we found no evidence of between-group differences in serum BDNF levels or in their changes over time.

Within-group analyses: As shown in Fig. 3, both groups exhibited changes in serum BDNF levels across the study period. We conducted exploratory and unadjusted within-group analyses examining changes from Week 0 to Week 4 and from Week 4 to Week 8 using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. These analyses were exploratory and were not part of the primary analysis. In the CU group, the median change from Week 0 to Week 4 was −2.453 ng/mL, whereas the median change from Week 4 to Week 8 was +0.636 ng/mL. The difference between these periods was statistically significant (P=0.027). However, no such trends were observed in the NCU group (Table 3).

Effects of C. unshiu intake on mood states

We investigated the influence of C. unshiu intake on mood dynamics in healthy participants using the POMS2 assessment. Evaluations of mood states were exploratory and descriptive, and all P-values were unadjusted for multiple comparisons. We first conducted between-group comparisons at Weeks 0, 4, and 8, but no significant differences were observed at any measurement point for any of the mood variables. However, the between-group difference in the change in DD scores from Week 0 to Week 8 was statistically significant (P=0.0499), with a greater decrease in the CU group than in the NCU group. In addition, the CU group showed significantly greater increases in VA than the NCU group from Week 0 to Week 4 (P=0.0126) and from Week 0 to Week 8 (P=0.0200). Significant between-group differences were not observed for any of the other mood variables (Table 4).

Exploratory within-group analyses were then performed to describe temporal changes within each group (Table 5). In the CU group, significant decreases in scores were observed in the following: DD (Week 4 vs. Week 8; Week 0 vs. Week 8), TA (Week 4 vs. Week 8), and TMD (Week 0 vs. Week 8). Furthermore, VA (Week 0 vs. Week 8) showed a significant increase. In the NCU group, significant decreases in FI score (Week 4 vs. Week 8) and significant increases in TA score (Week 0 vs. Week 4) were observed.

We evaluated the associations between serum BDNF concentrations and POMS2 scores using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. Analysis of data from all subjects in this clinical trial revealed a negative correlation between serum BDNF concentrations and CB (P=0.001), DD (P=0.003), FI (P=0.021), TA (P=0.034), and TMD (P=0.021) (Table 6).

DISCUSSION

This pilot randomized controlled trial aimed to investigate whether daily intake of C. unshiu influences serum BDNF concentrations and mood states assessed using the POMS2. In this study, no significant differences were observed in serum BDNF concentrations between the CU and NCU groups. However, within-group analyses indicated an upward trend in BDNF levels from Week 4 to Week 8 only in the CU group. Regarding mood states, the CU group showed greater improvements in DD and VA scores than the NCU group, based on the between-group comparison of POMS2 change scores. Furthermore, within-group analyses revealed a significant decrease in DD and TMD and a significant increase in VA in the CU group. Notably, these favorable within-group changes were not observed in the NCU group. In this study, all analyses other than the primary analysis were exploratory and descriptive, and all P-values were unadjusted for multiple comparisons.

Although this pilot randomized controlled trial did not detect significant between-group differences in serum BDNF levels, exploratory within-group analyses suggested an upward shift in serum BDNF levels in the CU group after Week 4. Specifically, the median change in serum BDNF levels from Week 4 to Week 8 was significantly greater than that from Week 0 to Week 4. In contrast, serum BDNF levels in the NCU group showed a downward trend throughout the study period. These contrasting trends－an upward trend in the CU group and a consistent decline in the NCU group－suggest that C. unshiu may help attenuate the decline in serum BDNF levels.

Karege et al. (2005) reported that serum BDNF levels in patients with depression were 10.07 ng/mL, compared to 12.20 ng/mL in healthy controls. Similarly, Yoshida et al. (2012) demonstrated that serum BDNF levels in patients with major depressive disorder (21.09 ng/mL) were significantly lower than those in healthy controls (23.11 ng/mL). In addition, studies in healthy individuals have shown that serum and plasma BDNF concentrations are lower under severe stress－a risk factor for worsening of mood states－than under mild stress (Mitoma et al., 2008; Okuno et al., 2011). Given that serum BDNF levels are reduced in individuals with depression and those under high stress, C. unshiu－which, in our pilot study, showed evidence of attenuating serum BDNF declines－may represent a promising candidate for further investigation. However, because the within-group analyses were exploratory and no significant between-group differences were observed, this interpretation should be regarded as tentative. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to determine whether C. unshiu exerts a clinically meaningful effect on BDNF.

Evidence linking specific foods to BDNF modulation remains limited; therefore, the few available dietary intervention studies provide important context for interpreting our findings. Among these, a randomized controlled crossover trial reported that a 3-month intake of mold-fermented cheese in older women increased serum BDNF levels compared with the control condition (Suzuki et al., 2019). Although within-group longitudinal changes were not statistically analyzed, serum BDNF levels shifted from 26.6 to 27.8 ng/mL following mold-fermented cheese intake, whereas levels shifted from 26.6 to 25.4 ng/mL under the control condition. Similarly, a single intake of rye bread elevated plasma BDNF levels by 27% compared with white wheat flour-based bread (Sandberg et al., 2018). These studies demonstrate the extent to which BDNF levels can change in response to specific foods, providing a useful comparison for interpreting our findings. In our study, although the observed increase in serum BDNF concentrations from Week 4 to Week 8 did not reach statistical significance, the mean value showed a tendency to increase by approximately 1.41 ng/mL (11.4%) in the CU group. Because no significant between-group differences were observed, direct comparison should be interpreted with caution; however, the magnitude of this increase is comparable to that reported for mold-fermented cheese intake and lower than that observed for rye bread. Although these previous studies were not designed to examine mood-related outcomes, they help contextualize the potential dietary influence on BDNF.

A study by Park et al. (2020) examining the relationship between citrus fruit intake and BDNF levels indicated that an 8-week intake of flavonoid-low orange cordial showed no significant change in serum BDNF levels, whereas flavonoid-rich orange juice significantly increased serum BDNF concentrations. These findings suggest that the flavonoids present in citrus fruits may be involved in the elevation in serum BDNF levels. Our previous study demonstrated that nobiletin, a polymethoxyflavonoid, increased BDNF levels in vitro (Nakajima et al., 2024). Therefore, the flavonoids present in C. unshiu may have contributed to the observed increase in BDNF levels. However, the specific active compounds and underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated.

The duration in our study was comparable to that in the previous study involving an 8-week intervention with flavonoid-rich orange juice. Consistent with our findings, that study reported no significant between-group differences in the changes in serum BDNF levels. However, the flavonoid-rich orange juice group exhibited a significant approximately 1.26-fold increase from baseline after 8 weeks, whereas the control group, as in our study, showed no significant changes in serum BDNF levels.

The citrus varieties used differed between studies. Our study employed C. unshiu, whereas Park et al. (2020) used Florida orange (Citrus sinensis, sweet orange). Although we did not measure flavonoid content, Nogata et al. (2006) reported that C. unshiu and C. sinensis contain broadly similar types and amounts of flavonoids. This compositional similarity may partially account for the positive effects on serum BDNF observed in both studies. Although the units differ (mL of juice vs. g of whole fruit), the daily intake of 380 mL of orange juice in the study by Park et al. (2020) likely represents a greater amount of citrus-derived components than the two C. unshiu consumed in our study (average weight: 106.9±37.2 g per fruit). This greater intake may have contributed to the more prominent enhancement in serum BDNF observed in that study.

Furthermore, although the mean ages of participants were similar－23.50 years in our study and 21.83 years in the study by Park et al. (2020)－that study specifically recruited individuals with depressive symptoms. In contrast, we did not examine such participants in this study. Future research should investigate whether C. unshiu intake increases serum BDNF levels in individuals who already exhibit poor baseline mood states. The effects observed in our study resemble the patterns of change in serum BDNF levels reported by Park et al. (2020) following intake of flavonoid-rich orange juice, which supports the plausibility of our findings.

Our study was conducted over 8 weeks, corresponding to the seasonal transition from autumn to winter in Japan. Serum BDNF levels in the NCU group showed a decreasing trend from Week 0 to Week 8, which is consistent with previous findings indicating seasonal variation, with BDNF levels decreasing during the autumn-to-winter period (Molendijk et al., 2012). During winter, worsening of mood states is often observed, as exemplified by seasonal affective disorder (Galima et al., 2020). Furthermore, numerous studies have reported an association between reduced BDNF levels and mood disorders (Duman and Monteggia, 2006; Yeh et al., 2015; Gliwińska et al., 2023). Therefore, a seasonal decline in serum BDNF concentrations may contribute to the worsening of mood states in winter. Thus, long-term intake of C. unshiu, commonly available throughout the autumn and winter months, may help mitigate the seasonal decline in BDNF and could be associated with more stable mood states during winter.

In our investigation, depressive mood and vigor significantly improved in the CU group compared with the NCU group, as indicated by between-group differences in the changes in their respective POMS2 scores. Furthermore, exploratory within-group analyses indicated favorable changes in DD, TA, VA, and TMD among participants who consumed C. unshiu. These within-group findings should be interpreted cautiously, as such changes do not provide evidence of a causal effect. Nevertheless, the combination of the significant between-group difference in mood-related outcomes and the within-group changes observed in the CU group suggests that C. unshiu may help maintain mood states. Further studies with adequate sample sizes and appropriately powered between-group analyses are needed to determine whether the observed changes truly reflect an effect of C. unshiu intake.

Spearman’s rank correlation analysis showed that serum BDNF levels were negatively correlated with CB, DD, FI, TA, and TMD of the POMS2. Among these, DD, TA, and TMD showed significant decreases, while CB and FI exhibited a decreasing trend in the CU group. Furthermore, serum BDNF levels tended to increase from Week 4 to Week 8 in the CU group. Taken together, the CU group exhibited changes in serum BDNF levels along with improvements in selected mood-related scores. However, the current data are insufficient to draw conclusions about any underlying mechanistic relationship. Additional studies are warranted to investigate this potential association more rigorously.

In recent years, nutritional psychiatry, a novel field that investigates the impact of diet and nutrition on brain function and mental health, has attracted increasing attention (Marx et al., 2021). Several dietary components, such as polyphenols, omega-3 fatty acids, and fermented foods, have been associated with improved depressive symptoms and emotional well-being (Osher and Belmaker, 2009; Aslam et al., 2020; Gamage et al., 2023). Although numerous animal studies have demonstrated antidepressant-like effects of polyphenols, evidence from human trials remains limited and inconsistent, with some studies reporting no clear benefit (Gamage et al., 2023). Clinical trials of omega-3 fatty acids also show mixed findings, with high doses sometimes proving ineffective while lower doses appear beneficial (Peet and Horrobin, 2002). Given that outcomes of human intervention studies can vary depending on participant characteristics, study duration, and dosage, differences in study design may partly explain the inconsistent findings reported across previous trials. These variations highlight the need for trials conducted under a variety of conditions. Our pilot study provides preliminary evidence that daily intake of C. unshiu may improve mood states in healthy young adults, suggesting a potential contribution to mental well-being.

Furthermore, while many previous studies have focused on dietary patterns－such as adherence to the Mediterranean diet or high intake of fruits and vegetables－being associated with better mood states (Jacka et al., 2017; Lassale et al., 2019), relatively few have examined the effects of a single, specific food item. Focusing on a single food item can strengthen causal inference, facilitate mechanistic insights, enhance reproducibility, and enable more practical implementation of single-food dietary interventions in real-world settings. Therefore, this research, which examines the mental health effects of C. unshiu as a single dietary component, may contribute to the field of nutritional psychiatry.

This pilot trial has some limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small (n=26), and all participants were recruited from a single university, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Second, the intervention period may have been too short to fully capture potential changes in serum BDNF levels. In this pilot trial, serum BDNF levels in the intake group began to show an upward trend between Week 4 and Week 8. It is therefore conceivable that a longer intervention period might allow this increase to become more pronounced. Third, the study population consisted exclusively of healthy young adults; therefore, the effects of C. unshiu intake on middle-aged and older people remain unclear. Moreover, because the participants were all healthy, the findings may not be generalizable to individuals with underlying medical or mental health conditions. Further research in more diverse populations will be necessary to determine whether the observed effects of C. unshiu intake extend to groups with different health backgrounds. Fourth, because the aim of this study was to examine the effects of consuming C. unshiu fruit, it was not feasible to prepare a visually and sensorially identical placebo, making blinding difficult. As a result, the NCU group did not receive a placebo, which may have introduced an expectation bias that influenced subjective mood ratings, potentially leading participants to report greater improvements than would occur under blinded conditions. In addition, the open-label design may have contributed to reporting bias, particularly for self-reported outcomes such as POMS2 scores. These factors should be considered when interpreting the mood-related findings of this pilot trial. Future studies using C. unshiu-derived extracts, beverages, or encapsulated components to enable placebo-controlled, double-blind designs are needed. Finally, although the participants were instructed to avoid other citrus, other dietary habits and lifestyle factors were not strictly controlled, which may have influenced their mood and serum BDNF levels. In the future, clinical trials involving patients with depression or mood disorders are required to determine whether similar effects are observed in such cases.

In conclusion, although this randomized controlled trial of C. unshiu intake was conducted as a pilot study with limited power and did not detect significant between-group differences, exploratory within-group analyses suggested a possible upward trend in serum BDNF levels. Regarding mood states, significant improvements in DD and VA scores were observed in the CU group compared with the NCU group. Furthermore, within-group comparisons showed significant decreases in DD and TMD, as well as a significant increase in VA compared with baseline. While these findings are preliminary and should be interpreted with caution, they provide a basis for further investigation. Building on the data obtained in this pilot trial, future studies with larger sample sizes and longer intervention periods are warranted to more rigorously evaluate the potential effects of C. unshiu intake on serum BDNF levels and mental health.
