Hemocompatibility of Mechanical Circulatory Support Devices: An Integrated Review of Platelet Function Assessment in In-Vitro Blood Experiments
Authors: Xiangyu He, Michael B Fischer, Daniel Zimpfer, Alice Assinger, Marcus Granegger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeDespite persistent bleeding and thrombotic adverse events in patients supported with blood pumps, standardized preclinical assessment of hemocompatibility remains largely restricted to hemolysis testing. To advance the field, robust and comparable in vitro platelet function testing within mock-loop mechanical circulatory support (MCS) studies is needed. This review systematically summarizes current methodologies for platelet function assessment in MCS devices and critically examines their advantages and limitations to inform the development of a unified, evidence-based testing framework.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Scopus using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Articles were screened according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.ResultsThirty-nine studies met the eligibility criteria. Study designs varied substantially with respect to experiment duration, sampling strategies, mock-circulation loop configurations, and pump types. Human whole blood was the predominant test medium (n = 27). Anticoagulation strategies differed widely, with citrate-based formulations (n = 22) and heparin (n = 15) most frequently employed. Flow cytometry was the predominant analytical method (n = 25), primarily assessing platelet function through changes in surface receptor expression or shedding.ConclusionFlow cytometry offers a highly informative and direct approach for assessing platelet state in MCS research compared to other methods. However, substantial methodological heterogeneity continues to hinder data interpretation and cross-study comparability. Beyond standardizing experimental setups and protocols, future investigations should incorporate multiple complementary biomarkers and clarify their relationships to clinically relevant outcomes to more comprehensively characterize shear-induced platelet responses.Keywords: Mechanical circulatory support device, Platelet function, in vitro experiments, Shear stress, Blood experiment

Introduction

Mechanical circulatory support (MCS), particularly left ventricular assist devices (LVADs), has become an established therapeutic option for patients with advanced heart failure [1], enabling restoration of cardiac output and end-organ perfusion in cases where conventional therapies are insufficient. Contemporary MCS devices predominantly rely on continuous-flow rotary pumps, which operate using rapidly spinning impellers to propel blood and narrow flow pathways. While these designs have improved durability and clinical outcomes, they inherently expose blood components to non-physiological flow conditions, characterized by supraphysiological shear stresses and complex flow patterns such as recirculation zones and regions of flow stagnation [2, 3].

These mechanical forces, in combination with the time blood particles are exposed to them (residence time), play a central role in blood damage [3]. Shear-mediated alterations affect multiple blood components, including hemolysis of erythrocytes, degradation of von Willebrand factor (vWF), alteration of platelets function, and leukocyte activation [1]. For example, supraphysiological shear can unfold vWF multimers, rendering them susceptible to cleavage by ADAMTS13 and contributing to acquired von Willebrand syndrome, a condition strongly associated with bleeding complications in LVAD-supported patients [4]. At the same time, shear-induced platelet alterations may contribute to both thromboembolic and bleeding manifestations, reflecting a complex and not yet fully understood bidirectional dysfunction of platelet activity.

Clinically, these blood-device interactions are closely linked to hemocompatibility-related adverse events (HRAEs), including bleeding and thromboembolism, which remain a major limitation of current MCS therapy despite significant technological advances [5, 6]. For example, the MOMENTUM 3 trial demonstrated improved outcomes with centrifugal-flow pumps compared to axial-flow devices, including reduced bleeding rates (45.8% vs. 56.8%) and a markedly lower incidence of suspected pump thrombosis (2.1% vs. 17.6%) [6], yet these complications were not eliminated. These observations highlight further improvements in device hemocompatibility require a deeper mechanistic understanding of shear-induced blood damage.

Comprehensive preclinical hemocompatibility assessment is therefore essential for both device development and regulatory approval. Currently, standardized evaluation is largely limited to hemolysis testing, for which established protocols exist (e.g., ASTM guidelines) [7]. In contrast, no standardized framework is available for assessing platelet function in vitro, despite its central role in both thromboembolic and bleeding complications. Existing studies employ a wide range of experimental approaches, with differences in anticoagulant choice, mock circulations, sampling procedures, hemostatic and thrombotic parameters employed and evaluation methods. These variations may significantly influence observed platelet responses [8], underscoring the need for more consistent experimental design and reporting.

As a first step towards standardization, the aim of this review is to compare existing protocols for assessing platelet function in vitro under MCS device-induced shear conditions and to identify approaches most suitable for its assessment. Ultimately, establishing an evidence-based and standardized testing framework will be essential for improving the predictive value of preclinical studies and advancing the hemocompatibility of next-generation MCS devices.

Method

Data Collection

This integrated review is intended to give an overview of in vitro platelet function assessment with MCS devices. This analysis was conducted in accordance with systematic review and meta-analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [9]. A systematic review of data were performed from inception to September of 2025 through the PubMed database, Embase, Web of Science and Scopus.

Search Strategy

The following search keywords were used: ("Platelet" OR "Platelet activation" OR "Platelet aggregation" OR "Platelet function" OR "Hemocompatibility") AND ("Mechanical circulatory support" OR "Mechanical circulatory assist devices" OR "MCS" OR "Ventricular assist devices" OR "VAD" OR "Total artificial heart" OR "Blood pump" OR "Centrifugal pump" OR "Axial flow pump" OR "LVAD" OR "Left ventricular assist device" OR "Pulsatile flow device" OR "ECMO" OR "Extracorporeal circulation") AND ("Shear" OR "Shear stress" OR "Shear rate" OR "Hemodynamic stress" OR "Flow-induced"). The search was limited to original research papers. After the initial search, a screening and selection of titles and abstracts was conducted after the detection and removal of duplicates. Full articles were obtained for eligibility assessment when a study was deemed relevant. Subsequently, citation and secondary source were also checked for eligibility. Studies were selected by two independent reviewers.

Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria

Research articles were evaluated to meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) study focus is platelet function under MCS devices in mock circulating loops, (2) samples are collected from in vitro blood experiments, and (3) methods to evaluate the platelet function are described and results are presented. Articles were excluded if an English translation of the research was unavailable. A flow diagram summarizing the stepwise process of the literature search is presented in Fig. 1.

Results

Our search strategy generated a total of 1281 results from all the different databases listed above. 760 duplicates were found and excluded. We exported these articles to Endnote to screen the studies by title and abstract. 125 articles were selected for full-text eligibility, of which 30 articles were found to meet inclusion criteria, with further 9 articles being included based on citation and secondary source searches. A total of 39 articles met the requirements to be included in this review (Fig. 1). All of these studies are summarized in Table 1.

Blood Sources

Whole blood was the primary experimental material in 35 studies, with human samples most frequently used (n = 27), followed by porcine (n = 4) [15, 23, 25, 45] and bovine (n = 4) [16, 20, 40, 41]. In contrast, four studies employed gel-filtered platelets (GFPs), of which three used human GFPs [14, 26, 48] and one used bovine GFPs [27]. The number of donors varied substantially across studies, ranging from 1 to 17, with the majority falling between 5 and 10. Notably, two studies did not report their number of donors [31, 35].

Experiment Duration and Sampling Strategy

Among the 39 studies, most experimental durations ranged between 3 and 6 hours (n = 26). Eleven studies reported shorter exposure times of 2 hours [27, 33, 37, 39, 43], 1.5 hours [47], 1 hour [14, 25], and 30 minutes [26, 42, 48], respectively, while two studies extended to 24 hours [44] and 72 hours [36], respectively. Sampling frequency varied across studies: thirteen collected samples every hour [10–13, 17, 18, 21, 22, 34, 35, 38, 41, 46], five every two hours [20, 30, 40, 43, 45], and one every three hours [23]. Roka-Moiia et al. conducted frequent sampling, collecting every 30 minutes [15]. In two studies, samples were collected every 10 minutes [26, 48]. In the 24-hour study, samples were collected at multiple time points (0, 0.5, 1, 3, 12, and 24 hours) [44]. The remaining studies followed uncommon sampling intervals.

Anticoagulant

Citrate was the most commonly employed anticoagulant to prevent clot formation after whole blood collection, reported in 22 studies. The formulations included 3.8% sodium citrate (n = 4) [10, 12, 18, 21], 3.2% sodium citrate (n = 4) [11, 13, 22, 25], unspecified sodium citrate (n = 3) [17, 26, 30], acid citrate dextrose solution A (ACD-A, n = 1) [14], and citrate phosphate dextrose adenine−1 (CPDA-1, n = 10) [15, 16, 19, 20, 27, 29, 32, 33, 40, 41]. Some studies, however, deviated from this standard practice. For example, Oota-Ishigaki et al. anticoagulated blood with 3.2% sodium citrate but subsequently added 20 mL of 2% calcium chloride to reverse citrate’s effects prior to experimentation, while also supplementing the blood with 4 mL of heparin to further prevent clotting [25]. In another study, blood was anticoagulated with sodium citrate, and the experimental circuits were coated with either 400 IU of heparin or 20 μM IMB-F12 [30]. Roka-Moiia et al. reported pre-anticoagulation of porcine with systemic heparin (200 U/kg) prior to vessel catheterization, followed by blood collection into bags containing CPDA-1 [15]. Furthermore, in three studies, blood was anticoagulated with CPDA-1, however, samples designated for platelet function measurements were subsequently transferred into tubes containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), citrate, or hirudin [19, 32, 33].

Heparin was utilized as the anticoagulant in 15 studies. In 11 of these, the reported concentration ranged from 0.56 U/mL to 5 U/mL; notably, four studies also collected samples into tubes containing citrate, EDTA, ACD, or heparin [23, 39, 42, 44]. In four additional studies, the use of heparin was reported without specification of concentration [24, 34, 35, 46]. One of these studies further compared different anticoagulants, including tirofiban, apyrase, and EDTA, alongside heparin [24].

In addition to conventional anticoagulants, one study used aspirin to prevent clot formation [48], while another did not disclose the anticoagulant employed [31].

MCS Devices and Mock Loops

Across 39 studies, blood or GFPs were exposed to MCS devices within mock-circulation loops. Among these, centrifugal pumps were the most commonly employed (n = 28), followed by axial-flow pumps (n = 9) and roller pumps (n = 10) as shown in Table 1. Additionally, one study utilized a toroidal-flow pump (TORVAD) [29], and another employed a volume-displacement pump (Berlin Heart Excor) [20]. The configuration of mock loops in all studies included in this review is summarized in Table 2. Loop volumes ranged widely from 60 mL [28] to 850 mL [20], reflecting differences in experimental objectives and device scales. Reservoir designs also showed considerable variability in shape and size, with three studies explicitly reporting the absence of a reservoir [37, 43, 48]. Similarly, tubing materials and dimensions differed across studies, with PVC and silicone tubing most commonly used, typically featuring internal diameters of approximately 3/8 inch, but occasionally ranging from 1/4 to 1/2 inch. In addition, seven studies reported the details of oxygenators used in their experimental setups [13, 27, 28, 39, 44, 45, 47], and two studies incorporated mesh filters to remove clots generated within the loop [15, 25].

Sample Fixation

A total of eighteen studies reported the use of chemical reagents for platelet fixation during sample processing. The most commonly used fixative was paraformaldehyde (PFA, n = 11), followed by Streck Cell Preservative solution (n = 3) [16, 30, 40], BD Stabilizing Fixative solution (n = 3) [19, 20, 41], and BD Cell Fix (n = 1) [38]. Among the PFA-based protocols, five studies employed 1% PFA for 30 minutes at 4 °C in the dark [11, 13, 17, 18, 21], two used 1% PFA for 1 hour at room temperature [15, 47], and one utilized 3.5% PFA for 20 minutes [14]. Three studies mentioned PFA without more detailed information [28, 43, 44]. For Streck Cell Preservative solution, one study fixed platelets by gently inverting samples ten times after adding 100 µL of the solution in 100 µL blood, followed by overnight storage at 4 °C [16], while two studies used a 1:1 volume ratio of fixative and sample, with measurements conducted immediately thereafter [30, 40]. Regarding BD Stabilizing Fixative solution, two studies employed 200 µL in total without specifying further details on processing conditions [19, 20], and one stored fixed samples at 4 °C overnight before measurement [41]. BD Cell Fix was applied in one study prior to fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis [38].

Lysis Buffer

The use of lysis buffer during sample preparation was reported only in 5 studies. Four studies used 2 mL of BD FACS Lysing Solution to remove red blood cells [19, 20, 30, 41], one study used BD lyse wash assistant instrument to lyse red blood cells and remove unbound antibodies [23].

ELISA

Among the nine studies utilizing ELISA, two measured soluble CD62P (P-selectin). Roka-Moiia et al. found undetectable levels in porcine plasma after four hours of circulation [15], whereas human plasma showed significant increases after two hours [18]. Five studies assessed β-Thromboglobulin (β-TG), of which four reported time-dependent increases [34, 35, 44, 46], while one found no change in porcine samples after circulation [15]. Three studies observed elevated platelet factor 4 (PF4) levels during circulation [35, 36, 46]. Additional findings included decreased intracellular serotonin and increased plasma glycocalicin (the glycosylation of CD42b) with reduced CD42b expression [44]. Bartoli et al. reported a significant increase of plasma CD41/61 in HM2 but no change in TORVAD [29] and Linneweber et al. noted platelet adhesion (activation) reaching above 95% in mock loops after circulation [37].

Discussion

This integrated review provides a comprehensive summary of in vitro studies investigating platelet function in blood-contacting MCS devices. Across the 39 studies we included, substantial heterogeneity was observed in experimental materials, anticoagulation strategies, device types, and platelet function evaluation methods. While such diversity reflects the broad methodological nature of this field, it simultaneously underscores the lack of standardized testing protocols, making cross-study comparison challenging and limiting the interpretability of platelet responses to mechanical shear force.

Variability in Blood Sources

Human whole blood was the predominant sample type, reflecting its physiological relevance and translational value. Nevertheless, animal blood, particularly porcine and bovine, was also frequently used due to its accessibility and ethical considerations. Although these models enable large blood volume collection and controlled experimentation, species-specific differences in platelet size, reactivity, and coagulation properties may influence shear-induced responses and partly explain inconsistencies in activation markers across studies. The comparative work by Chan et al. [49], exemplifies the value of multispecies analyses, however, such systematic evaluations remain the exception. In addition, several studies employed GFPs, which provide valuable mechanistic insights on platelet behavior but limited physiological comparability due to reduced viscosity and absence of other blood components [50–53]. Together, these findings highlight the need to balance experimental feasibility with physiological relevance when choosing blood sources and sample preparation.

Anticoagulation Strategies as a Major Preclinical Variable

Citrate-based anticoagulants (sodium citrate, ACD-A, CPDA-1) were most commonly used across studies. While citrate effectively prevents clotting by chelating extracellular calcium, differences in citrate formulations, including calcium-binding capacity, buffering properties, and glucose content, may alter platelet activation thresholds [54, 55]. Importantly, calcium chelation can blunt shear-induced activation, leading to possible underestimation of platelet responsiveness. Efforts to counteract this effect through recalcification or supplemental heparin introduce further variables that complicate interpretation [14, 25–27, 48]. Heparin was also used at different concentrations ranging from 0.56 to 5 U/mL. Despite its clinical familiarity, the in vitro effects of heparin on platelet function remain poorly characterized. As reported, heparin can interfere with platelet function assays [56] and, in some cases, enhance expression of platelet activation markers [57]. However, the extent to which the choice of anticoagulant influences biomarker expression remains unclear, thereby limiting the interpretability of comparative analyses. Overall, anticoagulant choice represents a critical yet often underappreciated pre-analytical variable in vitro MCS research.

Platelet Function Assessment Methods

A wide range of analytical techniques has been employed to investigate and evaluate platelet function in in vitro studies involving MCS devices, however, weak concordance between methods underscores the complexity of capturing shear-dependent platelet responses.

Flow cytometry was the most frequently used method, due to its sensitivity and ability to distinguish specific activation pathways with low platelet counts [58, 59]. Recent evidence suggests that supraphysiological shear stress paradoxically reduces CD41/61, CD62P and GPIb expression [60], likely reflecting activation followed with subsequent receptor shedding or receptor shedding alone. This aligns with a few studies reporting decreased fluorescence intensity for CD42b and GPVI in sheared samples [10, 13, 17, 18, 24]. In contrast, findings for CD41/61 were inconsistent, stemming from differences in marker selection, fixation procedures, and sampling protocols [61–63]. Notably, Roka-Moiia et al. reported that continuous shear stress did not induce CD41/61 activation or CD62P exposure, whereas biochemical agonists readily triggered both responses [14]. These findings indicate that supraphysiological shear stress induces a distinct platelet activation pattern compared with biochemical stimulation, underscoring the need to further investigate the mechanistic differences between these pathways.

A recent review indicates that shear-induced platelet activation in microenvironments is primarily mediated through the PI3K/Akt, downstream of mechanosensitive signaling pathway, such as vWF-GPIb-IX-V receptor complex or Piezo1, with involvement of receptors such as CD62P, GPIb–IX, GPIIb/IIIa, GPVI, and αIIβ1 integrin [64]. However, the shear conditions encountered by platelets in microenvironments may not reflect those in mock-loop studies. Therefore, shear-induced platelet responses observed in microenvironmental warrant validation under controlled mock-loop conditions in future studies. These observations also raise a further important question: to what extent does shear exposure alter platelet’s subsequent responsiveness to biochemical agonists? Clarifying this will be essential for advancing our understanding of shear-mediated platelet dysfunction in MCS environments.

Additionally, a precise and standardized characterization of platelet biomarkers in flow cytometry analyses is essential to ensure comparability across studies. In this context, the most commonly reported parameters were mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) and the percentage of positive platelets. MFI reflects relative receptor density and may indicate receptor shedding under high shear conditions, whereas the percentage of positive platelets represents the proportion of platelets expressing a given marker. However, reliance solely on the percentage of positive platelets is problematic for constitutively expressed receptors, such as GPVI or GPIb, where baseline expression is present on nearly all platelets; therefore, concurrent reporting of both MFI and percentage positivity is recommended to provide a more comprehensive and accurate assessment of platelet activation and phenotype.

Ten studies examined platelet aggregation using a range of techniques, including laser-light scattering assays [22, 42], impedance aggregometry [23, 30, 32, 33], light transmission aggregometry [22, 28] among others. However, substantial methodological variation, particularly in protocols and agonist selection limits comparability across studies. Moreover, aggregometry provides information only on a platelet’s capacity to respond to exogenous agonists and does not directly reflect the underlying activation state induced by shear exposure.

Platelet adhesion was quantified in three studies using diverse approaches, including fluorescent microscopy [13, 28] and subsequent image analysis software (ImageJ) [25]. Although these methods enable visualization and quantification of platelet-surface interactions, the lack of standardized substrates and flow conditions limits direct comparability across investigations.

Electron microscopy techniques were used in a subset of studies to assess platelet morphology and thrombus formation under shear conditions. Scanning electron microscopy (n = 4) [23, 25, 37, 39] and transmission electron microscopy (n = 1) [28] provided detailed visualization of platelet adhesion, aggregation, and ultrastructural deformation. While these techniques offer qualitative insights into platelet-surface interactions and clot architecture, they are inherently limited by their low throughput and potential sample preparation artifacts.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs), used in nine studies, were primarily employed to quantify soluble platelet activation markers such as β-TG, PF4, and soluble CD62P. While several studies reported time-dependent increases in β-TG and PF4, others observed minimal or no changes, particularly in non-human samples [15]. These discrepancies may arise from interspecies differences, variation in shear duration, or the influence of anticoagulants [49]. Additional soluble biomarkers, including serotonin, glycocalicin, and released CD41/61, provided complementary insights on platelet degranulation and secretion, but lack the cellular specificity of flow cytometry-based assays. Additionally, ELISA results are highly sensitive to plasma preparation methods, processing delays and storage conditions, all of which can introduce variability across studies. One study employing Luminex multiplex immunoassays demonstrated the potential for simultaneous quantification of multiple soluble activation markers, offering a higher-throughput and more integrative assessment of platelet activation pathways [30]. Similarly, one study utilized Western blotting to analyze platelet-derived proteins in plasma, providing insight into molecular alterations secondary to mechanical stress [39].

Four studies determined the procoagulant activity of platelets via the platelet activity state (PAS) assay [14, 26, 27, 48], which quantifies thrombin generation by GFPs and acetylated prothrombin (Ac-FIIa), a thrombin substrate in capable of inducing clot formation or secondary platelet activation. By providing near real-time measurement of thrombin production, the PAS assay effectively links mechanical shear exposure to procoagulant properties. However, the use of GFPs within a mock-loop limits its physiological relevance and comparability to in-vivo conditions.

Device Diversity and Mock-Loop Configuration

Most studies focused on centrifugal pumps, followed by axial and roller pumps, with several investigating unconventional designs, such as toroidal-flow or volume-displacement pumps. Device geometry, flow characteristics, and operational settings directly affect shear exposure and even small divergences can produce divergent platelet responses. Notably, the currently available in vitro data on platelet responses are almost exclusively derived from continuous-flow LVAD platforms.

Emerging devices pose additional challenges for standardization. For example, total artificial hearts such as the BiVACOR, which incorporate two interconnected pumps [65], will require dedicated experimental setups and testing strategies, potentially limiting comparability across studies. Additionally, novel systems like the BiVACOR [65] and the CorWave LVAD [66] - featuring a biomimetic undulating polymer membrane - operate under pulsatile conditions in clinical use. The impact of such pulsatile flow on hemocompatibility remains an active area of research [67, 68] and is not adequately captured by the static mock circuits currently proposed in ASTM standards [69]. Moreover, variability in mock-loop configurations, including blood volume, tubing or device materials and circuit design, adds another layer of complexity. It has been reported that circulatory loop design and its components can introduce artifacts that influence the in vitro assessment of VAD thrombogenicity [70].

Accordingly, future preclinical assessments of hemocompatibility, particularly platelet function, should incorporate dynamic testing conditions that better replicate physiological hemodynamics. Despite the inherent heterogeneity of devices and mock-loop configurations, standardized reporting and harmonized experimental approaches will be essential to enable cross-study comparisons and to bridge the gap between preclinical findings and clinical outcomes.

Clinical Relevance

To improve the clinical relevance of preclinical in vitro studies, we propose that future investigations adopt comparative study designs in which two or more devices are evaluated under identical experimental conditions and their relative effects are related to clinical outcomes. Direct comparison of absolute in vitro measurements with in vivo data are currently not feasible due to the limited availability of standardized datasets and the absence of validated thresholds linking biomarker levels to clinical risk.

In this review, only one in vitro study [12] directly compared platelet biomarkers between the HeartMate II (HMII) and HeartMate 3 (HM3), reporting higher CD62P expression in HMII alongside an approximately twofold reduction in CD42b and GPVI expression. These relative differences may align with clinical findings from the MOMENTUM 3 trial, where HMII was associated with higher rates of bleeding, stroke, and thrombotic events. Two additional in vitro studies [10, 21] reported comparable CD41/61 expression between HMII and HVAD pumps, which may reflect the broadly similar incidence of bleeding and thrombotic events observed in the ENDURANCE trial. However, the significantly higher stroke rate in the HVAD group (29.7% vs. 12.1%, P < 0.001) underscores that current biomarkers may not fully capture clinically relevant differences in device-related risk.

Further complexity is highlighted by clinical observations. For example, Smadja et al. [71] reported no significant changes in soluble CD40L, P-selectin, PF4, or NAP2 following pump implantation, with no differences between aspirin and heparin treatment groups. These findings suggest that commonly used platelet activation markers may not adequately reflect shear-induced platelet activation in vivo, potentially due to rapid clearance mechanisms or systemic regulatory processes. Alternatively, current mock-loop assays may overestimate platelet activation in devices with optimized shear profiles.

Overall, establishing meaningful links between in vitro findings and clinical outcomes requires a shift toward standardized, comparative preclinical study designs, combined with harmonized biomarker definitions and reporting. Such approaches may enable more robust cross-device comparisons and provide a more reliable bridge between preclinical testing and clinical performance. Nevertheless, in vitro models will likely remain inherently limited in their ability to predict clinical outcomes, as they cannot fully replicate complex physiological processes such as clearance mechanisms and systemic biological responses.

Conclusion and Future Directions

Across the 39 studies reviewed, flow cytometry is the most utilized and promising method to evaluate the in vitro platelet function. Methodological heterogeneity emerged as the principal barrier to interpreting in vitro platelet testing in MCS research. Variations in sample type, anticoagulant use, fixation methods, sampling strategies, mock-loop configurations, and marker selection make cross-study comparison difficult and hinder the development of a coherent model of shear-mediated platelet activation. This variability also highlights a deeper gap: the mechanisms by which supraphysiologic shear alters platelet phenotype remain poorly defined. Although many observed responses, ranging from receptor shedding, phosphatidylserine exposure, impaired aggregation, and altered granule release, are well characterized in basic platelet biology, they are rarely thoroughly analyzed in MCS research in a standardized fashion.

To advance beyond descriptive findings, closer collaboration between engineering and platelet biology are required to determine whether supra-physiological shear induces true activation leading to aggregation or adhesion, apoptosis-like changes, functional exhaustion, or distinct mechano-stress-specific responses. A critical next step is establishing rigorous links between in vitro biomarkers and clinical events such as bleeding, thrombosis, acquired von Willebrand syndrome, or pump thrombosis. Pairing standardized in vitro tests with clinical datasets across specific devices and operating conditions will be key for translating bench findings into clinically meaningful predictors of hemocompatibility.

To advance the field, consensus guidelines such as ASTM should establish standardized mock-loop geometries, harmonized anticoagulation strategies, defined marker panels, validated sample processing protocols, consistent sampling time points, and comparative study designs. Such standardization would improve reproducibility, enable comparisons across studies and support regulatory acceptance of platelet-based assessments alongside conventional hemolysis testing.
