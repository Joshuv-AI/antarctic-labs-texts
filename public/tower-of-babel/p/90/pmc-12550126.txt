Sociodemographic, lifestyle, and medical factors associated with calculated free testosterone concentrations in men: individual participant data meta-analyses
Authors: Nick Narinx, Ross J Marriott, Kevin Murray, Robert J Adams, Christie M Ballantyne, Douglas C Bauer, Shalender Bhasin, Mary L Biggs, Peggy M Cawthon, David J Couper, Adrian S Dobs, Leon Flicker, Graeme J Hankey, Anke Hannemann, Robin Wilkening, Sean A Martin, Alvin M Matsumoto, Claes Ohlsson, Terence W O’Neill, Eric S Orwoll, Molly M Shores, Antje Steveling, Thomas G Travison, Gary A Wittert, Frederick CW Wu, Leen Antonio, Dirk Vanderschueren, Bu B Yeap
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:Sociodemographic, lifestyle, and medical variables influence total testosterone (T) and sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) concentrations. The relationship between these factors and “free” T remains unclear. We examined 21 sociodemographic, lifestyle, and medical predictors influencing calculated free T (cFT) in community-dwelling men across ages.Design:This is a cross-sectional analysis in 20 631 participants in the Androgens in Men Study.Methods:Individual participant data (IPD) were provided by 9 cohorts. Total T was determined using mass spectrometry, SHBG using immunoassays, and cFT using the Vermeulen formula. Associations were analyzed using 2-stage random effects IPD meta-analyses.Results:Cohort median ages ranged from 40 to 76 years and median cFT concentrations from 174.3 to 422.8 pmol/L. In men aged 17-99 years, there was a linear inverse association of cFT with age (−57.2 pmol/L [95% confidence interval, −69.4, −44.9] per 1 SD increase in age). Calculated free T increased with increasing baseline body mass index (BMI) among men with BMI < 23.6 kg/m2, but decreased among men with BMI > 23.6 kg/m2 (−24.7 pmol/L [−29.1, −20.3] per 1 SD increase in the 25.4-29.6 kg/m2 BMI range). Calculated free T was lower in younger men, who were married or in a de facto relationship (−18.4 pmol/L [−27.6, −9.3]) and in men who formerly smoked (−5.7 pmol/L [−8.9, −2.6]), were in poor general health (−14.0 pmol/L [−20.1, −7.8]), and had diabetes (−19.6 pmol/L [−23.0, −16.3]), cardiovascular disease (−5.8 pmol/L [−8.3, −3.2]), or cancer (−19.2 pmol/L [−24.4, −14.1]).Conclusions:Calculated free T was most prominently associated with age and BMI. The linear, inverse association with age, nonlinear association with BMI, and presence of diabetes, cancer, and sociodemographic factors should be considered when interpreting cFT values.Keywords: calculated free testosterone, Vermeulen formula, male, age, body mass index

Introduction

Testosterone (T), a pivotal hormone in male physiology, regulates a wide spectrum of vital functions ranging from sexual and reproductive function to body composition and mood. During the male life span, circulating levels of T are affected by various sociodemographic, lifestyle, and medical factors. Moreover, circulating T is bound by sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) and other binding proteins such as albumin and cortisol-binding globulin, thereby affecting its bioavailability.1,2 In men across ages, T is closely correlated with SHBG.3 Individual cohort studies have consistently shown that circulating concentrations of total T decline with increasing age4–6 and the presence of obesity and diabetes7–10 and are also influenced by other lifestyle factors such as smoking and physical activity.9,11 Sex hormone-binding globulin concentrations however increase with age, but decrease with higher body mass index (BMI) and the presence of diabetes.3,12

Current recommendations for the diagnosis of male hypogonadism uniformly emphasize the importance of clinical assessment for symptoms and signs of androgen deficiency.13,14 However, there is debate over how best to confirm the diagnosis with biochemical testing. As a result of T-binding protein interaction, it is postulated that only a small fraction of T dissociates from carrier proteins to cross semipermeable (or cellular) membranes.15,16 Measurement of this unbound or “free” T requires equilibrium dialysis which is technically demanding and not routinely performed.16 A calculation-based approach for free T (calculated free T, cFT), by integrating total T, SHBG, and albumin levels, is an alternate strategy. The European Male Ageing Study (EMAS) demonstrated a significant association between cFT and sexual function in men aged 40-79 years,17 while hypogonadal symptoms in men were better correlated with low cFT rather than low total T.18 These findings are supported by Mendelian randomization (MR) studies where genetic polymorphisms of cFT were correlated with androgen-sensitive endpoints such as bone mineral density, reduced body fat, and prostate-specific antigen.19 Thus, cFT, as a proxy for unbound or dissociable T, has been advocated to provide an additional biomarker to complement total T in the assessment of androgen status in men. Recent clinical practice guidelines from the Endocrine Society of the United States14 and the European Academy of Andrology together with the European Society of Endocrinology20 have recommended cFT determination in the assessment of hypogonadism in conditions in which SHBG levels are altered or total T is borderline low. Nevertheless, the free hormone hypothesis and the validity of cFT, as a derived rather than directly measured entity, have continued to be questioned.13,21

Earlier epidemiologic studies have generally not focused on cFT as their primary research focus. Furthermore, these studies have limitations such as small sample size of individual cohorts,4,9,11,22–25 or measurement of total T by immunoassay.4,9,11,22–25 We previously conducted an individual patient data meta-analysis (IPDMA) examining factors associated with total T (measured using mass spectrometry) and SHBG.12 This new analysis specifically examines the relationship of cFT concentrations with sociodemographic, lifestyle, and medical factors in men. Using IPDMA on multiple cohorts from diverse regions across the world that used mass spectrometry for the assessment of total T, we aim to provide some new insights into the factors associated with variations in cFT among men. This work emphasizes the need to interpret cFT in light of factors that may influence it differently from total T and SHBG, especially when it is being used as proxy of circulating unbound T.

Methods

This study models relationships of cFT with demographic variables and risk factors for disease in men. Analyses follow the protocol for analysis 1 of the Androgens in Men Study, with the exception that relationships of cFT are modeled, instead of total T (PROSPERO Registration CRD42019139668).26 Analyses are descriptive and focus on summarizing trends, effects, and heterogeneity across studies from the literature, rather than on testing any new or specific hypothesis. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data sources and searches

A systematic review was conducted to identify suitable studies from which to request IPD.27

Study selection

Studies were selected if they were prospective cohort studies of adult community-dwelling men who had baseline total T concentrations measured using mass spectrometry, with at least 5 years of follow-up for incident cardiovascular disease (CVD), mortality, cancers, cognitive decline, or dementia.26 Eleven studies were selected.26 Nine provided individual participant-level data (IPD), and two declined (Figure S1). Further details on the systematic review, including the full electronic search strategy in MEDLINE, have been published.12,27

Data extraction, variables, and quality assessment

Variables were requested from selected studies, and once approved, these data were securely sent, stored, and checked.12 Concentrations of total T and SHBG in blood samples were determined using mass spectrometry and immunoassay, respectively. Concentrations of cFT were calculated from measurements of total T and SHBG using the Vermeulen formula, assuming fixed albumin concentrations of 42 g/L.28 Although time of blood collection data was not available from all studies, most of the blood samples were taken from fasting participants in the morning.12 Additionally, 4 sociodemographic variables (participant age [years], BMI [kg/m2], married or de facto status [no, yes], and higher education attained [university degree or equivalent]), 3 lifestyle variables (levels of alcohol consumption [<19.2 g/day, ≥19.2 g/day], physical activity [vigorous intensity: >75 min/week, ≤75 min/week], and smoking status [never, previous, and current]), and 14 health status/medication usage variables were harmonized (Table S2).12 The cohort recruitment criteria for each study, diagnosis codes, definitions used for harmonizations, and quality assessments have been reported elsewhere.12,27

Data synthesis and analysis

A prespecified series of 2-stage random effects IPDMAs were done to summarize associations of cFT (dependent variable) with each of 21 sociodemographic, lifestyle, and prevalent health/medication usage predictors of interest. Multivariable IPDMA models were fitted to estimate the association, or marginal effect, with each predictor of interest. Associations with (i) sociodemographic predictors were estimated using model 1, which controlled for all other sociodemographic predictors; (ii) lifestyle predictors were estimated using model 2, controlling for all other lifestyle and all sociodemographic predictors; and (iii) prevalent health/medication usage predictors were estimated using models 3-16, which controlled for all sociodemographic and lifestyle predictors (Table S3).12 If a study did not provide data on the predictor of interest, it was excluded from that analysis. Continuous predictors were modeled using restricted cubic splines with prespecified knot points and values for centering, as calculated from the entire distribution of IPD (Table S2). Participants with missing total T or SHBG measurements (used to calculate cFT) were excluded, and all other missing values were imputed using substantive model compatible fully conditional specification.29 Substantive model compatible fully conditional specification imputations were congenial with each respective IPDMA model, and in cases where the predictor of interest was missing from a study, that study was excluded (please refer to Supplementary material for further details).12

Two-stage IPDMAs were conducted in R, version 4.3.1 (R Foundation). In stage 1, the same model was fitted to 40 imputed data sets from each individual study, with coefficient and variance estimates extracted and pooled using functions from the “mitools’ package.30 In stage 2, the study-specific estimates were pooled using a meta-analysis model. For continuous predictors, spline model coefficients and covariance matrices were pooled using the “mixmeta” and “dlnm” packages, with prediction intervals calculated using the “mvmeta” package.31,32 For categorical predictors, estimates of coefficients and standard errors were pooled using the metagen function of the package “meta.”33 The I2 statistic was assessed to gauge the relative amount of heterogeneity, and an appreciable level was interpreted in cases where the 95% confidence interval (CI) of I2 exceeded 50%.12,34 Meta-regressions and subgroup analyses were performed for results with appreciable heterogeneity (Supplementary material). For continuous predictors, summary curves are presented and summary estimates calculated from the summary curve as the mean difference (MD) for a 1 SD increase around the reference value. For categorical predictors, forest plots are presented and summary estimates, with CI and prediction interval, calculated for the corresponding model coefficient (ie, the MD from the reference level; Table S2).

These analyses were done for 9 of the 11 studies identified in the systematic literature search, for which IPD-level data sets were provided (main analysis). Individual patient data meta-analyses for models 1, 2, 7, and 10 were then repeated to evaluate the sensitivity to adjustments for additional model predictors. Individual patient data meta-analyses were repeated for the association with participant age for subgroups of “disease-free” men, to evaluate the sensitivity to an anticipated increased prevalence of comorbidities in older men (Supplementary material). The prospect for publication bias was evaluated by inspecting contour-enhanced funnel plots.

Results

Analysis population

Systematic literature searches of published and grey literature to July 2019 identified 11 studies as suitable to approach for IPD (Figure S1).27 Individual participant data were provided by 9 of these studies, and after excluding men with prior orchidectomy (n = 64), using androgens/antiandrogens (n = 287) or without total T or SHBG measurements (n = 6944), there were IPD-level data for n = 20 631 participants (ARIC, BHS, CHS, EMAS, FHS, HIMS, MAILES, MrOS USA, and SHIP; Table 1).35–46 Summary attributes are shown separately for the 2 FHS cohorts where participants of different ages were recruited during different periods (Table 2).43,46 Cohort median ages ranged from 40 (FHS Generation 3) to 76 (CHS, HIMS) years and median cFT concentrations from 174.3 (CHS) to 422.8 (FHS Generation 3) pmol/L, from blood samples taken between 1994 and 2012 (Table 1).

Sociodemographic factors

Summary curves were estimated for associations with the continuous predictors of age and BMI (Figure 1A and B). Although age was modeled using a restricted cubic spline, its summary curve for predicting cFT, after controlling for other sociodemographic factors (BMI, marital status, and education), was approximately linear and inverse and estimated a change of −58.3 pmol/L (CI [−67.7, −48.8]) in cFT for a 1 SD increase in age, from 62.2 to 77.8 years (Figure 1A; Figure S2; Table 3). Appreciable heterogeneity was estimated, but meta-regression and subgroup analyses failed to identify a moderator explaining the variability in effects among studies (Table 3; Tables S4 and S5). However, despite appreciable heterogeneity, estimates for individual studies were consistent in direction, describing lower cFT concentrations for older than younger men (Figure S3A and Table S6). Although the overall average summary effect of a 1 SD increase in BMI (25.4 to 29.6 kg/m2) predicted a change of −24.7 pmol/L (CI [−29.1, −20.3]) in cFT, this association was nonlinear, and an increasing cFT with BMI is estimated for men with BMI < 23.6 kg/m2 (Figure 1B and Table 3). Although the CI of I2 was relatively wide and covered 50% indicating some variability among studies, the shapes of marginal effect curves for individual studies were generally consistent with that of the summary curve, and all studies estimated lower cFT concentrations in men with higher BMI, for men with a BMI > 27.5 kg/m2.

Appreciable heterogeneity was estimated for the association with married/de facto status, with median age and proportion of frequent drinkers identified as significant moderators (Table 3; Table S4). Meta-regressions identified larger effects of married/de facto status (ie, a lower cFT) in studies of younger men and in studies with higher proportions of frequent drinkers (Figure S3B and C). Subgroup analyses of studies grouped by the median age being younger or older than 60 years gave more imprecise but lower estimates of heterogeneity than were estimated for the main analysis (Figure 1C; Table S5). In studies of younger men (median age < 60 years), those who were married or in a de facto relationship had an average −18.4 pmol/L (CI [−27.6, −9.3]) lower cFT than those who were not, after controlling for other sociodemographic predictors. However, in studies of older men (median age > 60 years), there was no association with married/de facto status. There was no overall association with higher education found, with negligible but imprecise heterogeneity estimated (Table 3).

Sensitivity analyses showed that summary estimates for sociodemographic predictors were not substantively different after having controlled for the additional terms in models 2, 7, and 10 (Table S7).

Modifiable lifestyle factors

Summary estimates demonstrated no overall association of cFT with alcohol consumption; a −5.3 pmol/L (CI [−10.3, −0.3]) lower cFT for men who undertook ≤ 75 min/week vigorous physical activity, as compared with those who had done more; and a −5.7 pmol/L (CI [−8.9, −2.6]) lower cFT for former smokers, as compared with men who had never smoked (Table 3 and Figure 1E–H). However, there was no departure in the summary MD of cFT for current smokers, as compared with men who had never smoked. Estimates of heterogeneity were generally imprecise, with wide CIs of I2 covering 50% (Table 3). Sensitivity analyses showed that controlling for additional terms did not substantively alter findings, except for the association with physical activity (Table S7). In that case, the model that additionally controlled for prevalent CVD (model 7) estimated a CI that overlapped 0, identifying an additional source of uncertainty in the estimated summary effect for physical activity.

Prevalent health conditions and medication usage

Summary curves showed lower estimated concentrations of cFT for men with lower values of LDL cholesterol (<3.2 mmol/L), HDL cholesterol (<1.3 mmol/L), and creatinine (<90 μmol/L), but no difference in cFT among men with higher values for these covariates (Figure S4). Calculated free T was directly associated with total HDL cholesterol ratio when the ratio was lower than 2.9 but inversely associated when the ratio was above 4.3 (Figure S4). There was no overall association of cFT with systolic or diastolic blood pressure but appreciable heterogeneity for diastolic blood pressure (model 4). Meta-regressions identified a moderating effect of the proportion of participants who had never smoked (Table S4). The 3 studies that demonstrated a significant decrease in cFT for a 1 SD increase in diastolic blood pressure (from 74.1 to 86.0 mmHg; ARIC, CHS, and SHIP) also had a low proportion of participants who had never smoked (<0.33; Table S6; Figure S3D). Average cFT was lower in men who did not report good or excellent health (MD, −14.0 pmol/L, CI [−20.1, −7.8]); had hypertension (MD, −6.0 pmol/L, CI [−10.7, −1.3]), diabetes (MD, −19.6 pmol/L, CI [−23.0, −16.3]), CVD (MD, −5.8 pmol/L, CI [−8.3, −3.2]), and cancer (MD, −19.2 pmol/L, CI [−24.4, −14.1]); or were using lipid-lowering medications (MD, −6.3 pmol/L, CI [−12.1, −0.5]) or psychotropic drugs (MD, −10.8 pmol/L, CI [−20.2, −1.4]; Figure 2A–H and Table 3).

Other analyses

Unplanned analyses investigating the association of cFT with age for subgroups of “disease-free” men showed consistent trends to that of the main analysis, with summary curves indicating near-linear inverse associations (Figure S5). Although the size of the estimated summary effects from subgroup analyses were slightly smaller, CIs were overlapping with the summary effect estimate obtained from the main analysis, indicating no substantive difference in results (Table S8). Contour-enhanced funnel plots were suggestive of possible publication bias in the estimated association with physical activity and significant asymmetry for married/de facto status, which was likely confounded by appreciable relative heterogeneity (Supplementary Material; Figure S6).

Discussion

This is the first IPDMA examining factors associated with cFT, including cohorts from different regions across the world which all used mass spectrometry for the assessment of total T. By comparing our previously reported results for total T and SHBG,12 we have a unique opportunity to contrast and compare how factors may be differentially associated with cFT versus total T and SHBG. Our findings reveal striking differences in the association between cFT with age and BMI, in comparison to their previously reported associations with total T and SHBG.12 In contrast to total T, cFT showed a significant linear, inverse relationship with age, across the age span, highlighting the major impact of aging on cFT. Additionally, although nonlinear, we also observed lower cFT concentrations among individuals with higher BMI, which was similar to the decrease of total T in these individuals. Apart from these major differences in age and BMI, we observed comparable associations between cFT and total T for other factors.

This IPDMA underscores a robust and linear, inverse association between age and cFT levels that is strikingly different from the association of total T with age.12 In our study, increasing age is consistently associated with a decrement in cFT levels, with a linear decrease of −57.2 pmol/L (CI [−69.4, −44.9]) for a 1 SD increase in age, highlighting the progressive nature of age-related hormonal changes. A similar trend of decreasing cFT with increasing age was observed in some individual cohort studies.3,4,9 Importantly, the observed age-related decline in cFT is independent of disease status. Although less steep, in healthy men free of hypertension, diabetes, CVD, cancer, COPD, lipid-lowering medication use, and chronic kidney disease, a similar linear and inverse association between cFT and age remained (supplementary analysis in Figure S5). Interestingly, our IPDMA reveals an age-related decline in cFT that is apparent at a younger age than would be anticipated. Our previous research showed that mean total T concentrations, in contrast with cFT, did not significantly differ with age until the age of 70 years after which a decline of 1.6 nmol/L per 15.6 years was observed.12 Simultaneously, and also starting at the age of 70, while total T decreased, LH increased, suggesting that Leydig cell impairment may occur in men beyond this age.12 Thus, both total T and cFT are inversely associated with age in older men. However, in younger men, we observed a strong linear decline in cFT with an onset at an age as young as 17 years. While we observed a decrease in cFT across the full male age span, SHBG increased with age across the same age span.12 Comparing the age-related trends of cFT and SHBG, our study suggests that the observed early decrease in cFT is primarily driven by the increase in SHBG, impacting on the calculation using the Vermeulen formula. The underlying mechanism of increasing SHBG starting at young age is currently not known. Notably, for cFT in younger men in the interval of 20-50 years old, the 95% CI was quite broad, resulting from fewer available data for and less representation of these age groups among the different cohorts.

We observed a nonlinear association of cFT with BMI. Up to a turning point of ~23.6 kg/m2, cFT is higher with increasing BMI. Above the turning point, cFT is, similar to total T, inversely related to BMI and lower with increasing BMI. The overall relationship of BMI with cFT is similar to earlier observations for the association between total T and BMI, despite of the well-established SHBG-lowering effect associated with increasing weight.47 The association between total T and BMI shows progressively lower total T with increasing BMI, over the full range of BMI values.12 Data of UK Biobank participants show a similar nonlinear trend of cFT, although with a slightly higher turning point (~25 kg/m2).3

Our analysis reveals appreciable heterogeneity in the association between cFT levels and marital or de facto status. While the overall trend was lower cFT concentrations among individuals in a married or de facto relationship, the magnitude of this effect varies across the included studies. Age of men within studies, and in the case of FHS within its two component cohorts, was identified as a significant moderator, indicating that age-related factors may influence the observed association. In particular, study cohorts with a median age below 60 years (BHS [Busselton Health Study], EMAS, FHS-Gen3 [Framingham Heart Study—Generation 3], and MAILES [Men Androgen Inflammation Lifestyle Environment and Stress and SHIP [Study of Health in Pomerania]) showed a stronger association between relationship status and lower cFT. These findings highlight the impact of contextual factors, such as marital/relationship status, on cFT.

Modifiable lifestyle factors also demonstrate associations with cFT in our analysis, in a similar way as compared to total T.12 Specifically, our findings reveal no overall association with alcohol consumption, indicating that alcohol intake does not significantly impact cFT levels. However, we observe lower cFT levels among individuals who undertake ≤75 min/week of vigorous physical activity compared to those who are more active, but the association is only marginally significant. Additionally, former smokers exhibit lower cFT concentrations compared to individuals who have never smoked. Curiously, compared to nonsmokers, cFT concentrations in individuals that report current smoking are not decreased. These findings highlight the importance of considering lifestyle factors in interpreting variations in cFT levels among individuals.

Prevalent health conditions and medication usage were also found to have significant associations with cFT levels in our analysis, consistent with our previous observations on total T.12 The most prominent finding is the association of lower cFT with both cancer and diabetes. Interestingly, when considering the adjustments made in the specific model on diabetes (model 10), corrected for sociodemographic and lifestyle factors including BMI, diabetes appeared to be associated with an independent negative influence on cFT in men. Furthermore, this effect seems to be relatively homogenous between study cohorts. Calculated free T levels were also lower among men with other health conditions, including hypertension and CVD. Additionally, men who self-reported poorer health had lower mean cFT levels and individuals using lipid-lowering medications or psychotropic drugs had lower cFT concentrations compared to nonusers.

The comprehensive scope of our IPDMA analysis, encompassing substantial population sizes and using nonlinear, multivariable models, has facilitated a more precise assessment of the associations of pertinent sociodemographic, lifestyle, and medical factors with cFT concentrations among community-dwelling men. Our study benefits from several strengths that enhanced the robustness of our findings. Firstly, the use of individual participant-level data from multiple cohorts across the world, as identified from systematic review, provided a diverse sample representative of current research that allowed for increased generality in the scope of our inferences.35–46 Secondly, leveraging IPDMA enhanced our methodology, by enabling a consistent statistical modeling framework, incorporating nonlinear and multivariable techniques, to be applied across these data sets, resulting in summary estimates that provided a more detailed understanding of how each of the investigated factors affected cFT. Thirdly, limiting the analyses to only those studies with measurements of total T made using mass spectrometry improved the confidence in our calculated values of cFT for representing unbound circulating T, or “free” T, in men.2 This approach stands in contrast to conventional meta-analyses, where disparate modeling strategies employed across studies can introduce additional uncertainty and inflate estimates of heterogeneity. Additionally, by putting the results of our present analysis in perspective to our previously published work on total T and SHBG,12 we are able to contrast associations of sociodemographic and lifestyle factors and prevalent health conditions with cFT, with their corresponding associations with total T and SHBG. In the analyses shown in Marriott et al.,12 there were 21 074 participants, and this cFT analysis had 20 631 participants. The 443 men (2.1%) not included in the current analysis had missing SHBG values, and thus, cFT could not be calculated. However, this is a relatively small number of men, and we propose that their omission would not have had a major impact on the results.

Despite these strengths, our study is not without limitations. Firstly, the cross-sectional nature of our study design precludes establishing causality, and the associations observed should be interpreted cautiously. Secondly, while efforts were made to harmonize variables across the nine study cohorts that provided IPD, differences in participant recruitment and data collection methods may inflate estimated heterogeneity and introduce bias. Additionally, our analysis relies on self-reported data for lifestyle factors, which may be subject to recall bias. Furthermore, the generalizability of our findings may be limited to adult community-dwelling men, with data sets containing data predominantly on white ethnicity, and may not extend to other populations. The fraction of T that dissociates from binding proteins and crosses a semipermeable membrane can be measured using equilibrium dialysis,16 but this is technically demanding and none of the cohorts studied had such data. The validity of cFT continues to be debated, since it is not a measured analyte and thus lacks suitable quality controls.21 Lastly, while the Vermeulen formula is not the only methodology available for estimation of cFT,28,48–51 it is the most commonly used and considered a standard method for estimating cFT from measurements of total T and SHBG, even though it is important to acknowledge several inherent limitations and potential sources of variability associated with its application. Estimation of free T by calculation is intricately reliant on the accuracy and calibration of total T and SHBG assays,52,53 which is partially countered in this study design by the use of mass spectrometry for all total T assessments. Even when accounting for these issues, the Vermeulen formula consistently presents with a positive bias of 20%-30%.54 Development of the Vermeulen formula was based on the assumption of fixed albumin concentrations and equilibrium binding of T to SHBG and albumin in the circulation. However, variations in T and binding protein levels, competing steroids, binding affinities, and rare genetic variants may introduce uncertainties in the accuracy of cFT estimations, which could shift steady-state protein binding characteristics that is assumed for each individual.2

In conclusion, our comprehensive IPDMA demonstrates a linear, inverse summary association of cFT with age. For men with a BMI below 23.6 kg/m2, cFT was positively associated with BMI, whereas for men with a BMI above 23.6 kg/m2, cFT was inversely associated with BMI. These findings differ from previously reported associations of total T with age and BMI. Associations of cFT with other sociodemographic, lifestyle, and medical factors, such as diabetes and cancer, were largely consistent with previously reported findings for total T. These differences and similarities between cFT and total T should be considered when assessing androgen status and their correlates in men.

Significance

Our study leveraged the use of mass spectrometry for total testosterone assessment in a large community-based multicohort study, to examine the influence of sociodemographic, lifestyle, and medical factors on calculated free testosterone (cFT) values in men. We found a robust, linear, and inverse relationship between age and cFT. Values for cFT increased and then decreased with body mass index (BMI), with a turning point at a BMI of 23.6 kg/m2. Men with a history of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, or cancer had lower cFT. These findings underscore the importance of considering nongonadal factors when interpreting cFT values.
