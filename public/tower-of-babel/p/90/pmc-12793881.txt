Many patients selected for left ventricular assist device (LVAD) have a cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) device at the time of implantation. However, studies have not demonstrated significant differences in survival, hospitalizations, or ventricular arrhythmias between LVAD patients with CRT versus implantable cardioverter-defibrillator alone.1,2 Guidelines recognize the prevalence of CRT in LVAD but highlight uncertainty surrounding postimplant programming, calling for individualized decision-making.3 Left bundle branch–optimized (LOT) CRT has emerged as an alternative to achieve cardiac synchrony with coronary sinus (CS) lead pacing and left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP), but it is not described in LVAD. LOT-CRT can provide superior electrical resynchronization (greater QRS narrowing and reduced LV activation time) and greater improvement in both LV ejection fraction and LV volumes compared with LBBAP or biventricular pacing alone.4 Pressure-volume (PV) loop analysis offers a powerful tool for assessing hemodynamics and guiding device optimization. We report here the optimization of LOT-CRT via biventricular PV loops in an LVAD patient.

Case Summary

A 60-year-old man presented with cardiogenic shock and underwent destination therapy (HeartMate 3, Abbott) for NYHA functional class IV nonischemic cardiomyopathy during his index hospitalization. Nine months later, despite LVAD support, transthoracic echocardiogram revealed an LV ejection fraction of 16%, severely enlarged LV (end-diastolic dimension: 82 mm), moderate to severe mitral regurgitation, and reduced right ventricular (RV) function. He had ongoing NYHA functional class II to III symptoms and chronic left bundle branch (LBB) block, with a QRS of 174 ms (Figure 1A). After discussing risks and benefits, a decision was made to pursue LOT-CRT without a defibrillator.

LOT-CRT implantation

Left axillary vein access was obtained with ultrasound guidance. After CS cannulation, venogram highlighted a lateral LV venous branch suitable for lead placement. Using standard techniques, a quadripolar lead (Medtronic #4598) was delivered. Satisfactory sensing and capture thresholds were obtained. A lumenless lead (Medtronic #3830), was delivered via C315 sheath to the basal RV septum. It was affixed, with evidence of LBBAP with short LV activation time. Lastly, an atrial lead (Medtronic #4076) was placed in the right atrial appendage. The QRS shortened to 160 ms after CRT (Figure 1B).

Device optimization

Three months later, the patient returned for right/left heart catheterization. Under an institutional review board–approved protocol, PV loop conductance catheters were used (Sigma-M, CD Leycom). Right radial access was obtained for placement of a 4-F catheter into the LV. A 7-F catheter was positioned into the RV via the right internal jugular vein. PV loops were selected from the ConductNT software (CD Leycom) based on appropriate shape/counterclockwise formation. Data were acquired for 10 to 15 seconds during end-expiration.

Depicting the 4 phases of the cardiac cycle, PV loop analysis allows for comprehensive real-time beat-to-beat assessment of ventricular loading conditions (preload and afterload), contractility, and indices of myocardial oxygen consumption.5 Stroke volume can be obtained from the PV loop width, and stroke work obtained from the associated area. From a contractility perspective, the end-systolic pressure-volume relationship (ESPVR) is derived as an intercept from the end-systolic volume and a slope of end-systolic elastance (Ees). Ees is reflective of peak chamber elastance and mechanistic properties from maximal actin-myosin bonds.5 Peak positive dP/dt may also serve as a surrogate for contractility as the maximal rate of pressure rise during isovolumetric contraction. Also derived from PV loops, arterial elastance (Ea) measures the net arterial load inflicted on the ventricle as the ratio of end-systolic pressure to stroke volume.6 Similarly, ventriculoarterial coupling is derived from the ratio of Ea to Ees and describes matching of ventricular and vascular properties for optimal metabolic efficiency.5 In contrast to a typical rectangular shape, PV loops in patients with LVAD support are more triangular given the continuous flow of the device and lack of true isovolumetric phases. Higher pump speeds are generally associated with a leftward shift of the LV PV loop and smaller loop area, reflective of mechanical unloading and reduction in native LV stroke work.7 Hemodynamics may be affected by the small apical core removed during placement of the LVAD inflow cannula, however the underlying principles and assumptions of PV loops are unchanged, as in someone with an apical infarct. The hemodynamic perturbation induced by the artificial pulse in the HeartMate 3 seems to cause a mild deviation in the aortic pressure tracing but little in terms of hemodynamic changes.

The goal of biventricular PV loops was to optimize the RV ESPVR and stroke volume while maintaining adequate LV unloading and minimizing the LV end-diastolic volume. Baseline LVAD settings included a speed of 5,600 rpm, flow 3.9 L/min, power 4.1 W, and pulsatility index 6.4. CRT was first turned off to assess hemodynamics during intrinsic sinus rhythm with LBB block. Preferential lead testing was then undertaken to assess the response to isolated CS and LBBAP, adaptive biventricular (CS→LBB), and CS-LBB/LBB-CS sequential pacing with various offsets (20, 40, 60, 80 ms). Finally, a temporary RV lead was inserted via the left femoral vein. Paced atrioventricular delay remained unchanged at 100 ms. Data were processed after appropriate user guidelines, with exportation of 5 to 10 cardiac cycles per configuration. Volume calibration was performed using biplane transthoracic echocardiogram. Data are described as mean ± standard deviation and are compared with mixed-effects model and Gessier-Greenhouse correction. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.1, GraphPad Software).

Biventricular functional parameters with preferential lead testing are summarized in Figures 2A to 2F. LV stroke volume was similar to baseline (43 ± 4 mL) with isolated pacing of RV, CS, and LBBAP, and was increased in adaptive biventricular (69 ± 6 mL). In the RV, stroke volume increased progressively from baseline (47 ± 7 mL), with pacing of RV-only (54 ± 22 mL), adaptive biventricular (59 ± 7 mL), CS (74 ± 3 mL), and LBBAP (76 ± 6 mL). Similar trends were seen for ejection fraction and cardiac output. CS→LBB offsets demonstrated a decline in LV function, whereas LBB→CS with increasing offset to 60 ms showed improvement. Figure 3 outlines average biventricular PV loops, and Figure 4 shows volume shifts. Although there was an improvement in LV stroke volume from intrinsic rhythm, adaptive biventricular pacing resulted in increased end-diastolic volume from baseline. The remaining configurations shifted LV PV loops to the left, indicating improved offloading. Similarly, in the RV, little change was noted in adaptive biventricular pacing end-diastolic volume, and the remainder resulted in leftward shifts.

LV dP/dt trends did not correlate with those seen in stroke volume, ejection fraction, or cardiac output, with lower dP/dt in many configurations associated with improved LV function. ESPVR was lower in adaptive biventricular (0.32 ± 0.03 mm Hg/mL) compared with baseline (0.35 ± 0.02 mm Hg/mL) but increased in the CS (0.40 ± 0.01 mm Hg/mL), LBBAP (0.38 ± 0.02 mm Hg/mL), and offset variations. In the RV, an increase in ESPVR was also noted in the CS (0.228 ± 0.004 mm Hg/mL) and LBBAP (0.191 ± 0.004 mm Hg/mL) compared with baseline (0.166 ± 0.005 mm Hg/mL). With sequential pacing, there was significant variation depending on offset, correlating with markers of RV function (Figure 5). An inverse relationship between both Ea and ventriculoarterial coupling to LV function was noted, with a reduction seen in adaptive biventricular and LBB-CS-60 (LBB-CS with offset to 60 ms) compared with baseline, and an increase with CS-LBB pacing. For the RV, both Ea and ventriculoarterial coupling were lower in CS and LBBAP compared with baseline, and RV–pulmonary artery coupling revealed similar trends (Figures 6 and 7). Why Beyond the Guidelines

Current guidelines suggest that both leaving CRT on (remodeling benefit) or turning it off (improved battery life) may be appropriate in LVAD patients, highlighting a gap in consensus and the need for individualized management.3 PV loops may serve as a powerful tool for guiding individualized device optimization.

Case Outcome and Follow-Up

Adaptive biventricular pacing resulted in the greatest improvement in LV stroke volume but also the greatest end-diastolic volume, therefore increasing potential for higher wall stress/myocardial oxygen uptake with inadequate unloading and limiting reverse remodeling. Peak positive dP/dt did not correlate with changes in ventricular function, however ESPVR did show consistency across pacing configurations, particularly for the RV. Changes in both Ea and ventriculoarterial coupling were inversely proportional to function in both ventricles. These results highlight the complexity of biventricular interaction in CRT, particularly with an LVAD, and demonstrate the value of invasive hemodynamics for individualized optimization. In this patient, based on our assessment, it was determined that CS-only pacing provided the greatest benefit in terms of biventricular contractility and volume reduction, and his device was reprogrammed accordingly. Follow-up electrocardiogram demonstrated a QRS of 154 ms (Figure 8). Given a lack of association between QRS duration and relevant endpoints in prior studies,8 we propose that resynchronization may not be critical in LVAD-supported patients, as electrical decoupling of LV and RV systolic contraction may provide a hemodynamic mechanism to avoid interventricular septal interaction in systole.

Discussion

Although 35% of LVAD patients have a pre-existing CRT device, no consensus remains regarding postoperative management.3,9 Benefits for continued CRT in LVAD patients include the potential for improved LV function and myocardial recovery, which has been demonstrated in case reports.10,11 However, prospective studies including LVAD patients with CRT have shown no acute hemodynamic benefit of biventricular over RV pacing, improved outcomes with RV pacing over biventricular, and no benefit of ventricular pacing over intrinsic rhythm.12,13 Another multicenter effort revealed no difference in outcomes with CRT on or off after LVAD, although with slightly increased LV ejection fraction in CRT patients.2 Based on the available evidence, many patients have their CS lead decommissioned after LVAD implantation to improve battery life, which the present case highlights may be counterproductive in select patients, as determined by hemodynamic testing.

Regardless of LVAD support, CRT has been found ineffective in approximately 30% of patients with heart failure.14 Various strategies for CRT optimization have been previously considered. An LV-RV offset to account for the margin of slow wave propagation can result in increased contribution to LV activation, although existing evidence is conflicting.14, 15, 16 Invasive parameters including dP/dt have been assessed in CRT optimization, but with poor reproducibility.17 The present case also did not demonstrate any correlation between improved ventricular function and dP/dt, which may relate to load dependence.18 ESPVR as a relatively load-independent contractility marker showed more consistent trends and thus may be more reliable for optimization purposes. The inverse relationship between ventricular function and both Ea and ventriculoarterial coupling also demonstrated consistency. Increased LVAD speed results in sinusoidal aortic waveforms with reduced pulse pressure, which has been suggested to preclude use of Ea for pulsatile afterload assessment.19 Ea is dependent on systemic vascular resistance, which itself may not be a useful marker of vascular tone in patients with advanced heart failure, given derivation constraints of low stroke volume. In addition, systolic loading sequence as an important component of ventriculoarterial coupling is not accounted for in Ea.19 However, the trends appear to correlate, and biventricular PV loops add unique insight to further our understanding of the ventriculoarterial and interventricular interactions in LVAD patients, in addition to assistance with pacing optimization. PV loops do require more resources than standard right heart catheterization, which may limit generalizability of use. However, this technology may be leveraged diagnostically for challenging cases.

Conclusions

The present case demonstrates vast changes in both ventricles in response to preferential lead testing and highlights a need for individualized management to optimize native LV and RV function in the context of continuous flow device–based support. The use of invasive hemodynamics, through biventricular PV loops, serves as a compelling strategy to bridge this knowledge gap.
