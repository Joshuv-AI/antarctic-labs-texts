AAV-mediated delivery of a proliferation inducing ligand (APRIL) to cortical neurons limits inflammation and demyelination in the corpus callosum of the cuprizone mouse model

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA Proliferation-Inducing Ligand (APRIL) is a tumour necrosis factor superfamily member with multiple effector roles in the peripheral and central nervous system (CNS). In the CNS, APRIL helps maintain immune homeostasis and supports neuronal survival, yet whether it can therapeutically modulate acute neuro-inflammatory and neurodegenerative processes is unclear. To investigate this in a severe neuro-inflammatory context, we tested whether local APRIL delivery can influence microglial/astrocytic responses and demyelination in the cuprizone (CPZ) mouse model of neuro-inflammation and demyelination.MethodsWe applied adeno-associated virus (AAV)–mediated gene transfer to drive APRIL expression by cortical neurons positioned directly above the splenium of the corpus callosum. Following CPZ intoxication, we evaluated microglial and astrocytic activation and myelin content by in vivo T2 and diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and validated them by post-mortem quantitative immunohistochemistry (IHC) and bulk transcriptomic and proteomic profiling.ResultsT2 and diffusion MRI, corroborated by quantitative IHC, demonstrated that secretion of APRIL by cortical neurons attenuated CPZ-induced microglial recruitment and demyelination in the splenium, but not in the cortex. Integrated transcriptome-proteome analysis of splenium and cortex, further supported by IHC, highlighted astroglial lipid-metabolic circuitry - marked by the astrocytic fatty-acid-binding protein 7 (Fabp7) - as a candidate axis underlying APRIL’s effects to reduce inflammatory cell recruitment and myelin preservation.ConclusionsAAV-mediated expression of APRIL by cortical neuron counteracted microglial recruitment and demyelination in the splenium of CPZ-treated mice. Our multi-omics integration analysis nominated several pathways, including Fabp7-linked astrocytic lipid metabolism, to elucidate APRIL’s mechanism of action. Together, these findings provide a framework for the mechanistic dissection of APRIL’s therapeutic potential and further translational evaluation in CNS pathologies characterised by acute neuro-inflammatory responses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-025-03569-2.

Introduction

A Proliferation Inducing Ligand (APRIL) is a member of the tumour necrosis factor (TNF) superfamily and has originally been described to play a vital role in several biological processes within the broad context of peripheral immune cell functioning [1]. Within the peripheral immune system, APRIL can be produced by myeloid cells (including monocytes, macrophages and dendritic cells), granulocytes (including neutrophils and eosinophils), as well as epithelial and stromal cells (especially at mucosal sites) [2]. With B cell maturation antigen (BCMA) and Transmembrane Activator and CAML Interactor (TACI) being the main receptors for APRIL, this signalling axis is well established as a critical factor for both T-cell independent and T-cell dependent B-cell survival and proliferation, as well as IgA Class switch recombination for B-cells in mucosal tissues [3]. Given these important regulatory functions, elevated APRIL expression has also been implicated in cancer biology, whereby it supports the survival of malignant plasma cells and B cells, particularly in multiple myeloma and some B-cell lymphomas [4]. Likewise, excess of APRIL has been shown to sustain autoreactive plasma cells that produce pathogenic autoantibodies (for example, in systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, and multiple sclerosis (MS) [5–7]. The latter observations brought forward the concept that APRIL - by the aid of drugs blocking BAFF(B cell-activating factor)/APRIL signalling - might be a useful therapeutic target in auto-immune diseases. However, a clinical trial of dual BAFF/APRIL blockade in MS patients was halted due to worsening of MS symptoms, especially in terms of increase in relapses [8]. The latter has also been recapitulated in the experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) mouse model, whereby the absence of APRIL exacerbates disease outcome [9]. In contrast, both intraspinal and intravenous delivery of APRIL protein has been demonstrated to positively influence disease progression in the EAE mouse model during the acute inflammatory phase [9]. Altogether, this new evidence points out a potential immune-modulating and/or neuro-protective role for APRIL during acute inflammatory phases in central nervous system (CNS) disease.

With our hypothesis that APRIL may have a beneficial influence on the development and/or progression of strong neuro-inflammatory responses, in this study, we performed a comprehensive analysis of the therapeutic effect of APRIL on the disease outcome in the cuprizone (CPZ) mouse model of CNS inflammation and demyelination [14–17]. The CPZ mouse model is a commonly used inflammatory and demyelinating disease model, whereby highly reproducible and localised inflammation and demyelination can be induced at the level of the corpus callosum, but also in grey matter (GM) regions, such as the cortex, hippocampus, and thalamus [14, 15]. Especially the highly specific localisation – and to a further extent the defined timing – of CPZ-induced inflammatory and demyelinating lesions makes this model highly suitable for follow-up using a battery of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques [16–18], as well as targeted immune gene therapeutic interventions [19]. Concerning the latter, and in contrast to preceding studies by us and others whereby viral vector-mediated therapeutic protein delivery, including (but not limited to) ciliary neurotrophic factor (CNTF) [20], brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) [21], interleukin (IL)−4, IL-10, IL-11, IL-13 [10, 11, 22, 23] and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) [20], was targeted to the splenium, in this study we opted for adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated gene transfer to the cortical area of the retrosplenial cortex. Given that APRIL is a secreted protein, and that cortical neurons - at least in short term - would survive CPZ intoxication (in contrast to oligodendrocytes in the splenium), we hypothesised that this route of delivery allows for sustained therapeutic protein availability in the cortex, as well as in the adjacent splenium which becomes subject to increased permeability and extracellular diffusion due to CPZ-induced inflammation and demyelination [16].

As such, our study aimed to investigate whether cortical delivery of APRIL, via adeno AAV-mediated gene transfer, could affect both areas of interest. Hereto, we applied our well-established non-invasive MRI, combining standard T2 mapping with diffusion MRI (dMRI) [12, 24], and quantitative immunohistochemical (IHC) toolbox [11] to document the therapeutic potential of APRIL on microglia-recruitment and demyelination in the acute phase of CPZ intoxication. Additionally, we performed an advanced transcriptome-proteome integration analysis [25] to validate the impact of APRIL at the molecular, cellular and functional level in the CPZ mouse model, with a specific focus on inflammatory and regenerative processes.

Materials and methods

Generation of the HA-Fc-MmAPRIL(88–252) AAV transfer plasmid

The HA-Fc-MmAPRIL(88–252) pAAV transfer plasmid (TF) was generated by designing an HA-Fc-MmAPRIL(88–252) ORF cDNA cassette (combining a haemagglutinin tag, a human Fc-tag, and the coding sequence for Aa 88–252 of Mus musculus APRIL protein [26] to replace the eGFP ORF in the CMVenhsyn-intron-eGFP pAAV TF plasmid [27], previously designed by the Leuven Viral Vector Core (LVVC, KULeuven, Belgium). The GeneBlock was ordered from Genscript, and cloning steps were verified by Sanger sequencing, resulting in the CMVenhsyn-intron-HA-Fc-MmAPRIL(88–252) pAAV TF plasmid.

Serum-free Recombinant (r)AAV production and downstream purification

The CMVenhsyn-intron-HA-Fc-MmAPRIL(88–252) and the CMVenhsyn-intron-eGFP rAAVs used in this study were produced by the LVVC as reported earlier [27, 28]. Final titers of the rAAV stocks were determined by real-time PCR analysis for genomic copy determination (GC/mL; 1.9 × 1012 GC/mL for AAV2/7_CMVenhsyn-intron-eGFP and 1.4 × 1012 GC/mL for AAV2/7_CMVenhsyn-intron-HA-Fc-MmAPRIL(88–252)) and Ruby-stained sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) analysis for vector purity. Throughout this study, the used rAAVs were renamed as AAV-eGFP and AAV-APRIL.

Generation of miPSC-derived NSPHs

Bi-partite NSPHs containing neurons and astrocytes were generated starting from an in house established and validated C57BL/6 CX3CR1eGFP+/− CCR2RFP+/− murine iPSC line, as previously described [25]. NSPHs were used for downstream experiments at the age of 4.5 weeks and at this stage individually cultured in 96-well format in 200 µL neural differentiation (ND) medium, composed of DMEM/F12/Neurobasal-A medium (at a 1:1 ratio) supplemented with 0.5% N2 supplement (Gibco), 1% B27 supplement (Gibco) and 100 U/mL–100 µg/mL penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco).

rAAV transduction of NSPHs

Cultured NSPH were transduced with AAV-eGFP and AAV-APRIL by adding a rAAV volume corresponding to 1.4 × 108 genome copies (GCs) directly to the culture medium of individual NSPHs for 24 h, after which culture medium was refreshed every other day. For the AAV-eGFP, live NSPH eGFP fluorescence imaging was applied at day 9 post-transduction using a Zeiss Observer Z1 microscope to validate eGFP-expression. For the AAV-APRIL, immunocytochemical analysis was applied at day 3 post-transduction to validate APRIL expression.

Preparation of NSPHs for immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis

Control and AAV-APRIL transduced NSPHs were prepared for downstream IHC analysis as previously described [25, 29]. In brief, NSPHs were individually fixed in 1.5 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde solution in PBS in 2 mL microtubes at RT for 30 min. Following two wash steps with 1.5 mL of PBS, spheroids were stored at 4 °C in PBS containing sodium azide, until further processing for cryosectioning and immunostaining.

Biological validation of APRIL functionality in NSPHs

Non-transduced and AAV-APRIL transduced NSPHs were stimulated for three days with 10 ng/mL interleukin 1 beta (IL1β; Biotechne), 10 ng/mL tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNFα; Abcam) and 50 ng/mL interferon-γ (IFNγ; ImmunoTools), as previously optimised [25]. Next, unstimulated control, cytokine-stimulated and AAV-APRIL transduced + cytokine- stimulated NSPHs were individually collected and washed once with PBS, followed by snap-freezing in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further processing for proteomics analysis.

Animals

A total of 120 wild-type C57BL/6J male mice (RRID: IMSR_JAX:000664, Charles River, Germany), aged 7 weeks upon arrival, were used throughout this study. Mice were acclimatized for one week before any experimental procedure was applied. Mice were group-housed (3–5 per cage) and maintained under a standard 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle with free access to food and water ad libitum. Cages were enriched with nesting material and red-plastic tubes. Mice were monitored daily for health status, weight, and signs of distress. Humane endpoints included weight loss > 20%, severe lethargy, or signs of persistent distress. All procedures were performed in compliance with the European guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals (Directive 2010/63/EU) and were approved by the Committee on Animal Care and Use at the University of Antwerp, Belgium (Approval No: 2021-09). The study design and reporting follow the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines.

Cuprizone mouse model

At eight weeks of age, mice were fed with standard rodent chow supplemented with 0.2% w/w cuprizone (Sigma-Aldrich) for 4 weeks to induce neuro-inflammation and demyelination, as described in previous studies [30–33].

Experimental design of the in vivo studies

Of the 120 mice used in this study, 60 mice underwent longitudinal MRI scanning and were used for immunocytochemical analysis, and 60 mice were used for transcriptomic and proteomic analyses (Fig. 1). Especially for the MRI analyses, sample size was determined using SPSS power analysis tool, based on data from a previous study [11]. For new test groups lacking prior in vivo data, expected mean values and standard deviations were approximated using two scenarios: (i) an optimal expected outcome and (ii) a non-significantly different outcome. This analysis returned a range of 8–11 mice per group; we selected a target of 10 mice/group as a compromise between statistical power and feasibility. Parameters for transcriptomic and proteomic analyses will be discussed further below. For both experimental pipelines (Fig. 1), the experimental design and conditions were kept the same. A six-group experiment was performed whereby control, AAV-eGFP injected and AAV-APRIL injected mice were randomly assigned to groups that received either a 4-week regular rodent diet (CTRL, CTRL AAV-eGFP, and CTRL AAV-APRIL; n = 10 each) or groups that received a 4-week CPZ-supplemented diet (CPZ, CPZ AAV-eGFP, and CPZ AAV-APRIL; n = 10 each). Because of practical limitations due to the scanning time and surgery with respect to the high number of animals, mice were divided into two cohorts of 30 mice each (n = 5 per group) and two consecutive longitudinal MRI experiments were performed. The two control groups (CTRL and CPZ, n = 10 + 10) did not receive any injection while, for the other groups (n = 40), the rAAV injections were performed at 8 weeks of age, and the CPZ-fed mice started the CPZ diet on that specific day until 12 weeks of age. Stereotaxic injection of rAAVs

All surgical interventions were performed according to institutional guidelines. In brief, mice were anesthetized using 5% isoflurane (IsoFlo Abbott, Illinois, USA) in 100% O2. Before the fixation, to prevent pain, mice received an s.c. injection (26G needle) of the analgesic buprenorphine (Temgesic, 0.05 mg/kg). Mice were then positioned in a stereotactic head frame to achieve a flat skull position (Stoelting) under 2% isoflurane for maintenance of anaesthesia. After disinfecting the mouse head, a midline scalp incision was made to expose the skull, and a hole was drilled in the skull using a dental drill burr (Stoelting). After 5 min of pressure equilibration, the AAV-APRIL or AAV-eGFP injection (coordinates: −1.6 mm AP, 0.4 mm ML, −0.7 mm DV from Bregma) was performed using a nano-injector (Nanoject II, Drummond Scientific) with a glass pipette attached to the stereotactic device at a speed of 13.8 nL/30 sec for a total injected volume of 200 nL. Before final needle retraction, a waiting period of 5 min allowed for pressure equilibration and prevented backflow. Next, the skin was sutured (Vicryl, Ethicon) and buprenorphine (Temgesic, 0.05 mg/kg) was given subcutaneously while mice were placed under a heating lamp to recover. Mice were monitored daily until recovered fully.

MRI acquisition

In vivo imaging was conducted on a 9.4T Bruker Biospec scanner (Biospec 94/20 USR, Bruker Biospin, Germany) interfaced with Paravision 360 v3.1 operating system and equipped with a cryogenic coil for mice brain (CryoProbe, Bruker). Each mouse was assigned a unique ID number to avoid experimental bias, and the acquisition order was kept random. Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane (IsoFlo, Abbott Laboratories Ltd., USA) in a gaseous mixture of 70% N2 and 30% O2 delivered to the animal at a flow rate of 600 mL/min (4% isoflurane for induction and 1–2% for maintenance). After anaesthesia induction, animals were positioned on a small animal bed suitable for the cryogenic probe of the 9.4T MRI scanner. Animals’ head was fixed using ear bars and tooth bars. Throughout the scanning procedure, the animal’s breathing rate and body temperature were continuously monitored (SA Instruments, INC., USA). The breathing rate was kept between 90 and 120 breaths per minute by adjusting the isoflurane concentration (between 1 and 2%), while the body temperature was maintained at (37.0 ± 0.1) °C using a feedback-controlled warm air system (SA instruments, INC., USA). Coronal MRI images for T2 mapping were acquired with a Multi-Slice Multi-Echo sequence using the following parameters: number of echoes = 12; first echo time (TE) = 7.5 ms; echo spacing = 7.5 ms; repetition time (TR) = 2200 ms; nr of slices = 3; slice thickness = 0.9 mm; matrix = [192 × 192]; Field of View (FOV) = (20 × 20) mm2; in-plane resolution = (100 × 100) µm2; scan time = 10 min. Subsequently, coronal diffusion-weighted images were acquired using a diffusion-weighted two-shot Spin Echo Echo-Planar-Imaging (DW SE-EPI) sequence with TR/TE = 8000/27 ms; nr of slices = 22; slice thickness = 0.3 mm; interslice space = 0.1 mm; matrix = [92 × 92], FOV = (13.8 × 13.8) mm2; in-plane resolution = (150 × 150) µm2. Diffusion weighting was achieved with a diffusion gradient duration (δ) of 3.5 ms and a separation (Δ) of 10 ms, b-values = 800 s/mm2, 1200 s/mm2 and 2800 s/mm2 with respectively 45, 60 and 90 diffusion directions per shell, and three additional b = 0 s/mm2 scans per shell. Additionally, two extra sets of three b = 0 s/mm2 images with opposite phase encoding directions were obtained for top-up correction. The total scan time for diffusion weighted imaging was 60 min.

MRI processing

T2 maps were reconstructed through an in-house written code in MATLAB R2021a (Mathworks, USA). Voxel-based T2 values were estimated from the signals S(TE) at different TEs using a mono-exponential fit function with an additional noise term:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:S\left(TE\right)\:=M{\:}_{0}\text{exp}\left(\frac{-TE}{T2}\right)\:+\:noise$$\end{document}

DW SE-EPI data was corrected for Gaussian noise biases, Gibbs ringing artefacts, topup/distortion, motion and outlier detection and replacement in Mrtrix3 and FSL6 [34–39]. The diffusion data was fitted with the diffusion kurtosis model in MRtrix3 [40] as previously described [24]. For each subject/timepoint following voxel-based maps of the diffusion metrics were extracted: fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), radial diffusivity (RD), and axial diffusivity (AD). For both the T2 and Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) analysis, study-based T2 and FA templates (population template) were generated using Advances Normalization Tools (ANTs) based on respectively the control T2 and FA maps of 30 baseline scans. For the T2 map and FA map of each subject at each time point, we estimated the spatial normalization warp to the respective T2 and FA template (ANTs). Two regions of interest (ROIs) were manually drawn on the respective population template: the splenium of the corpus callosum (CC) and the right retrosplenial cortex (injection site). The template ROIs were back-transformed to subject space using the inverse subject-to-template-warps and manually adjusted, if necessary. Lastly, the individual mean T2-relaxation time, FA, MD, RD and AD values were extracted from each ROI for each time point, for statistical comparison. To avoid bias, both processing and analysis of the MRI data were kept blind to the operator, and all the individual steps were performed automatically on all subjects at once.

Preparation of mouse brain tissue for IHC analysis

At week 12 weeks of age, all mice were transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution (Thermo Scientific Chemicals, US) as described previously [13]. Post-fixation was performed with 4% PFA for 1 h, followed by dehydration through a sucrose gradient of 5, 10, and 20%. Afterwards, the brain samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen until further processing for cryosectioning and immunostaining.

IHC stainings

IHC stainings were performed according to previously established protocols for NSPHs and CZP-treated brain tissue [11, 25]. Briefly, for both NSPHs and brain tissue, 10 μm thick cryosections were made using a microm HM500 cryostat (Thermo Scientific, US), collected on glass slides coated with poly-L-lysine (Sigma), air-dried and stored at −20 °C before further processing. For immunofluorescence labelling, sections were re-hydrated with PBS for 20 min, permeabilized with 0,1% (v/v) Triton X-100 (Sigma) in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) for 30 min and blocked with 20% serum (of the appropriate secondary antibody host species) or 1% BSA in TBS for 1 h. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing with TBS and a subsequent 1-hour incubation with the secondary antibodies in the dark, slides were washed again and counterstained with DAPI (1 µg/ml, Sigma) for 10 min at 4 °C. After a final washing step with distilled water, sections were mounted in ProLong R Gold antifade reagent (Thermo Fisher). The following antibody combinations were used in this study: a chicken anti-MBP primary antibody (Millipore, AB9348; 1:200 dilution) combined with a red-labelled donkey anti-chicken secondary antibody (Jackson, 703-166-155, 1:1000 dilution); a rabbit anti-GFAP primary antibody (Abcam, ab7260, 1:1000 dilution) combined with a Texas red-labelled goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Abcam, ab6719, 1:400) or with a green-labelled goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Jackson, 111-096-045, 1:200); a rabbit anti-Iba1 primary antibody (Wako, 019–19741, 1:500) combined with an Alexafluor 555 red-labelled donkey anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Invitrogen, A31572, 1:1000); a mouse anti-CS56 primary antibody (IgM, Sigma, 1:200) combined with a phycoerythrin-labelled goat anti-mouse IgM secondary antibody (Southern Biotechnology, 1:1000); a rabbit anti-Fabp7 primary antibody (IgG, Thermo Fisher, PA5-24949, 1:100) combined with an Alexafluor 555 red-labelled donkey anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Invitrogen, A31572, 1:1000). To stain for the Fc-tag on the APRIL protein, an Alexafluor 488 green-labelled goat anti-human secondary antibody (Invitrogen, A11013, 1:400) was used.

Microscopy and histological quantification

Immunofluorescence images of NSPHs and brain slides were acquired using a BX51 fluorescence microscope equipped with an Olympus DP71 digital camera and using a 10× (NA 0,30), 20× (NA 0,50) or 40× (NA 0,75) dry objective. All stainings were performed at least three times on 4–6 NSPHs and brain slices. Representative images were selected and presented for the manuscript figures. For histological quantification of all stainings performed on brain slices, a ROI-based analysis of the Mean Fluorescence Intensity (MFI) of both CC and cortex was performed. Briefly, Fiji image analysis freeware was used for image processing and analysis [41] (http://fiji.sc). The background signal was filtered using the ‘background removal filtering’ tool of Fiji, then, for each region, a circular, size-normalized ROI was manually drawn and consistently applied across all images to ensure comparability between samples. Subsequently, the MFI of the region was measured and used for further analysis.

Statistical analyses of MRI and IHC data

Graphs representing MRI metrics and IHC quantitative analyses were prepared using GraphPad Prism v.9.4.1 software. Statistical analysis of MRI metrics and IHC quantitative analyses were performed in JMP® Pro 17 using a linear mixed-effects model. For the MRI metrics, the linear mixed-effect model took into account the different experimental groups (CTRL, CTRL AAV-eGFP, CTRL AAV-APRIL, CPZ, CPZ AAV-eGFP, CPZ AAV-APRIL), the time point of analysis (week 0, week 4) and the variation over two independent experiments (n = 5 mice per experimental group per experiment). During analysis, one mouse from the CPZ AAV-APRIL group was excluded due to failed injection, and one mouse from the CPZ AAV-eGFP group was excluded after being identified as an outlier using the ROUT method (Q = 1%). For the IHC quantitative analyses, the linear mixed-effect model took into account the different experimental groups (CTRL, CTRL AAV-eGFP, CTRL AAV-APRIL, CPZ, CPZ AAV-eGFP, CPZ AAV-APRIL) and the variation over two independent experiments (n = 3 mice per experimental group per experiment, two slides stained per mouse per staining). Post-hoc analyses in the linear mixed-effects models were carried out with FDR correction for multiple comparisons (Benjamini-Hochberg) [42]. Statistical significance was set at a threshold of p ≤ 0.05, with median values and interquartile ranges reported to represent central tendency and variability across all datasets presented.

Brain dissection for transcriptomic and proteomic analysis

Mice were perfused with ice-cold PBS, directly followed by removal of the brain and dissection of the splenium of the corpus collosum as well as the area of the cortical injection. The extracted tissue sections were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and immediately stored at −80 °C until further processing for transcriptomic and proteomic analysis.

RNA sequencing

For each of the six experimental groups, transcriptomic analysis was performed separately for the dissected splenium and cortex. Each group contained five biological replicates that were sequenced once (total of 60 sequencing datasets). Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy® Mini kit (Qiagen, Benelux, Belgium), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, tissue was disrupted and lysate homogenized with lysis buffer containing b-mercaptoethanol, followed by RNA extraction and purification using RNeasy Mini spin columns. The concentration of the RNA samples was measured on the Qubit fluorometer 4.0, using the Qubit high sense RNA assay (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, USA). The integrity and purity of the RNA samples was evaluated by the Agilent 4150 TapeStation System using the recommended RNA ScreenTape assay (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Waldbronn, Germany). For all samples, the RNA integrity number (RIN) values were above 7, indicating the superior quality of the isolated RNA. The mRNA library preparation was performed using the QuantSeq 3’ mRNA-Seq V2 Library Prep Kit FWD with UDI 12 nt Set B1, combined with the QuantSeq-Flex First Strand Synthesis Module V2 to create longer fragments (Lexogen, Vienna, Austria). Before the library amplification PCR, the optimal PCR cycles were evaluated with the PCR Add-on and Reamplification Kit V2 (Lexogen, Vienna, Austria). The finalized cDNA libraries concentrations were measured using the Qubit high sensitivity dsDNA kit (Qubit 4.0, Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, USA). The fragment lengths, distribution and the quality of the libraries were evaluated using the D1000 ScreenTape assay (TapeStation, Agilent Technologies, Inc., Waldbronn, Germany). All libraries were equimolar pooled and send to VIB nucleomics core (Leuven, Belgium) for single read sequencing with a minimum of 10 M raw reads per sample (1 × 150 cycli) on the AVITI sequencer (Element Biosciences, San diego, USA). First quality control of the raw sequencing data was performed by VIB nucleomics core.

Processing of RNA sequencing data

Raw sequencing data were first quality controlled using the Trimmomatic software with following steps: (1) The 10 leading bases were removed from each read due per base sequence content bias; (2) The quality of the read was evaluated using a sliding window of 5 based and the read was clipped once the average quality within the window dropped below a Phred score of 25; and (3) Reads with a length shorter than 40 base pairs were removed to avoid mapping problems. Next, trimmed reads were mapped to the murine genome (mm10) using HISAT2 in standard settings and resulting SAM files were converted in position-sorted BAM files using samtools. Reads overlapping with exons in the murine genome were counted using the summariseOverlaps function of the BioC-package GenomicAlignments, counting all reads with a non-empty intersection. Next, genes with at least 10 counts in 8% of the samples, allowing low counts in 1 replicate per condition, were filtered in for further analysis resulting in 11.204 unique genes. Differential expression analysis was performed using the BioC-package DESeq2 and negative binomial models were set up by incorporating factors related to the mouse strain (CTRL vs. CPZ), the sampled brain region (splenium vs. cortex), and the treatment condition (CTRL vs. AAV-eGFP vs. AAV-APRIL). FDR-adjusted P-values inferior to 10% were considered significant. For visualization purposes, count data were further normalized using the variance stabilizing normalization algorithm (DESeq2-package) and heatmaps were generated using the BioC-package pheatmap with clustering parameters set to Manhattan distance and Ward linkage. The raw reads (i.e. FASTQ files) and the aligned reads (i.e. BAM files) have been deposited to ArrayExpress (BioStudies accession number: E-MTAB-14516).

Label-free proteomics

For label-free shotgun proteomics analysis, samples were treated according to the FASP (Filter Aided Sample Preparation) protocol. For the NSPH analysis, nine NSPHs were pooled for each experimental condition (CTRL, STIM and STIM + AAV-APRIL) to achieve respectively a protein concentration of 50 µg. For the brain tissue analysis (splenium and cortex), five splenia or cortices were pooled for each experimental condition (Control, AAV-eGFP and AAV-APRIL for regular diet and for cuprizone diet) with a final protein concentration about 100 µg. Next, NSPH and brain tissue samples were lysed by mechanical homogenization by using a turrax mixer (1-minute, highest speed) and ultrasonication (80% amplitude, Sonicator U200S control, IKA Labortechnik, Staufen, Germany) on ice in lysis buffer (urea 6 M in 100 mM TBS/HCl, p H = 7,5), followed by centrifugation (1300 rpm) to remove tissue debris. As reported in our previous work [25], samples were digested with trypsin (0.5 µg/µl Trypsin, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MI, USA). For protein label-free identification and quantification, 2 µl of tryptic peptides (corresponding to 0.05 µg/ml) from each condition were analysed in triplicate, with an EASY-spray Acclaim™ PepMap™ C18 (75 μm ID, 15 cm L, 2 μm PS, Thermo Fisher Scientific) nanoscale chromatographic column and a total run time of 65 min using a chromatographic gradient from 5 to 90% of acetonitrile (ACN). Proteomics data were acquired according to Di Stefano et al. [25]. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited in the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE [43] partner repository with the accession identifiers PXD056011.

Processing of label-free proteomics data

Proteomics raw data were processed using Thermo Proteome Discoverer (PD) version 2.4.0.305 (Thermo Fischer Scientific) to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) between the following experimental conditions for NSPHs (STIM vs. CTRL and STIM + AAV-APRIL vs. CTRL) using the “Protein Abundance Ratio” value and the “p-value” for each quantified protein, as described by Di Stefano et al. [25]. Proteomics raw data from brain tissue samples were processed using Thermo Proteome Discoverer (PD) version 3.1.0.638 (Thermo Fischer Scientific) to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) between the following experimental conditions: CPZ, CPZ AAV-eGFP and CPZ AAV-APRIL vs. CTRL; CTRL AAV-eGFP and CTRL AAV-APRIL vs. CTRL. The processing step was performed with minor changes compared to the previous: protein ratios were calculated as “Protein Abundance Based” and the statistics hypothesis by ANOVA was based on the abundances of individual proteins or peptides. To perform a robust quantification, we again set the parameters “High identification confidence” for each protein in at least two out of three analytical replicates and the number of “Unique peptides” greater than 1.

Ingenuity pathway analysis

For the proteomic analysis on NSPHs, the obtained DEP lists (STIM vs. CTRL and STIM + AAV-APRIL vs. CTRL), containing the protein ID, the expression ratio and significance level (uncorrected p-values), were uploaded to the Ingenuity Pathway Analysis tool (IPA, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) to perform a gene ontology and functional enrichment analysis based on all quantified proteins. For the transcriptome-proteome integration analysis on brain tissue, obtained DEG and DEP lists (CPZ, CPZ AAV-eGFP and CPZ AAV-APRIL vs. CTRL; AAV-eGFP and AAV-APRIL with regular diet vs. CTRL), containing the gene/protein ID, the expression ratio and significance level (uncorrected p-values), were first combined and uploaded to the IPA tool to perform a gene ontology and functional enrichment analysis by using a unique global matrix that contains all quantified proteins and genes. As such, IPA predicts the activation (z-scores ≥ 2.0) or inhibition (z-scores ≤ − 2.0) of ‘canonical pathways’ and ‘diseases and bio-functions’ (downstream pathways) for the loaded datasets. Next, the identified ‘canonical pathways’ and ‘diseases and biofunctions’ were manually curated based on NSPH or CPZ model-relevance and a significant activation z-score (≥ 2.0 or ≤ −2.0). For visual representation of the proteome analysis on NSPHs, a graded colour-scale was generated taking into account the z-score. For visual representation of the transcriptome-proteome integration analysis on brain tissue, bubble plots were generated providing z-score, ratio (for canonical pathways) or counts (for diseases and biofunctions), and -log10(p-value).

In vitro and in vivo validation of rAAV-vectors expressing eGFP and APRIL under control of the CMVenhsyn promotor

For this study, we generated two new rAAV vectors to promote eGFP (AAV-eGFP) or APRIL (AAV-APRIL) expression under the control of the CMVenhsyn promotor, as described in the Materials and Methods section. To evaluate the transduction capacity of the generated rAAVs, we applied a previously established in vitro miPSC-derived NSPH model having both neurons and astrocytes [25]. At first, we demonstrate that 5-week-old NSPHs can easily be transduced by AAV-eGFP as measured by direct live cell eGFP-fluorescence imaging at day 9 post-transduction (Fig. 2A). Likewise, cryosections obtained from AAV-APRIL transduced NSPHs demonstrated successful expression of the APRIL protein following staining for the human Fc-tag linked to the APRIL protein at day 3 post transduction (Fig. 2B). In our preceding work, we suggested that an untargeted proteomic analysis of NSPHs under steady-state, pro-inflammatory and/or treatment conditions is an elegant method to rapidly evaluate the therapeutic potential of novel CNS targeting interventions within a 3D neural-like environment [25]. Here, proteomic profiling of (i) control unstimulated NSPHs (CTRL), (ii) NSPHs stimulated with TNFa + IL1b + IFNg (STIM), and (iii) AAV-APRIL transduced NSPHs stimulated with TNFa + IL1b + IFNg (STIM + AAV-APRIL), and subsequent IPA-based ’diseases and biofunctions’ (DFs) pathway analysis upon STIM and STIM + AAV-APRIL comparison versus CTRL, it is clear that – even though the AAV-eGFP control was not included in this experiment – neuronal expression of APRIL in NSPHs is able to counteract upregulation of DFs pathways associated with DNA damage, apoptosis, necrosis and neurodegeneration, as well as to counteract the downregulation of the DFs pathway associated with cell survival (Fig. 2C). Based on these data, we concluded to initiate further in vivo studies to evaluate the therapeutic potential of AAV-APRIL administration in the CPZ mouse model. Hereto, we also first confirmed that upon cortical injection of AAV-eGFP neuronal eGFP expression could be achieved in the injected hemisphere (Fig. 2D, left side, AAV-eGFP). Cortical injection of AAV-APRIL limits CPZ-induced alterations in MRI metrics in the splenium

For the CPZ mouse model, it is well established that the process of inflammation (microgliosis + astrogliosis) and demyelination, as well as the influence of therapeutic gene delivery thereon, can be monitored using T2- and diffusion-weighted MRI [11, 12]. As outlined in Fig. 1, and described in detail in the Materials and Methods section, a 6-group mouse experiment - according to our previously established standards [11, 21] - was performed to investigate the influence of AAV-based delivery of APRIL to cortical neurons on inflammatory and demyelinating events in the CPZ mouse model. At first, no statistically significant differences in T2 or FA values were observed in the splenium between the 6 experimental groups for the week 0 baseline scan. Following four weeks of CPZ diet, as expected [12], for the splenium in non-injected and AAV-eGFP injected CPZ-treated mice a significant increase in T2 was noted (Fig. 3A and C, for both p ≤ 0.001), as well as a significant decrease in FA (Fig. 3B and D, for both p = 0.002), indicative of inflammation and demyelination. However, 4-weeks CPZ-treated mice that received the AAV-APRIL injection did not display such a significant deviation in T2 or FA in the splenium, indicative of, according to preceding studies [10, 12], reduced levels of CPZ-induced inflammation and/or demyelination in this condition. Regarding the experimental groups that received the normal rodent diet, a significant decrease in T2, but not FA, was noted for the non-injected mice (p < 0.05), the AAV-eGFP injected mice (p < 0.01) and the AAV-APRIL injected mice (p < 0.01). Noteworthy, and discussed further below, CPZ-treated mice that received the AAV-APRIL injection did not show this age-related decrease in T2, suggesting that - despite the major influence APRIL may have on MRI metrics in the splenium - there may still be cellular or structural processes different from control mice. Cortical injection of AAV-APRIL limits CPZ-induced microglial recruitment and myelin disruption, but not astrogliosis, in the splenium

Given our MRI-based observation that cortical AAV-APRIL-mediated delivery displays a beneficial effect on MRI metrics in the splenium, we next aimed to confirm these observations following IHC analysis. Hereto, IHC stainings were performed for Iba1 (Fig. 4A-B), MBP (Fig. 4C-D) and GFAP (Fig. 4E-F) to determine, respectively, microglial recruitment, myelin content and astrogliosis. Consistent with MRI metrics in the splenium (Fig. 3A-D), significantly increased microgliosis and significantly decreased myelin content were observed for CPZ-treated control, CPZ-treated AAV-eGFP injected and CPZ-treated AAV-APRIL injected mice (for all p < 0.0001). Cortical injection of AAV-APRIL does not limit CPZ-induced neuropathology in the cortex

We additionally investigated whether cortical AAV-APRIL injection could influence CPZ-induced alterations in the cortex. At first, and as expected, the T2 mapping and diffusion metrics used in this study were unfortunately unable to detect alterations and thus could not be used to investigate the therapeutic effects of APRIL (Fig. 3E-F, control-cuprizone diet groups). Interestingly, our MRI analysis could, however, demonstrate rAAV injection-related decreased T2 (p < 0.05) and RD (p < 0.05) values for mice on regular diet and decreased RD (p < 0.01), but not T2, values for CPZ-treated mice (Fig. 3E-F, rAAV injected groups). Subsequent IHC analyses were able to demonstrate significant CPZ-induced microgliosis (Iba1), demyelination (MBP) and astrogliosis (GFAP) in the cortical area, but - in contrast to the splenium - no beneficial effect was observed on the three parameters investigated (Supplementary Fig. 1A-F). Even more, and in line with our MRI observations, we noted an overall increased GFAP+ astrogliosis for AAV-eGFP and AAV-APRIL injected control and CPZ-treated groups (Supplementary Fig. 1E-F) (for all p < 0.05).

Our analyses determined a substantial number of genes and proteins being significantly (p < 0.05) upregulated or downregulated at a log2-fold change of, respectively, 0.5 and − 0.5 in the splenium (Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary File 1) and the cortex (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary File 2) over the different experimental groups as compared to control mice. In this study, we opted to mainly focus on CPs and DFs related to inflammatory and (neuro)regenerative processes.

Modulation of astrocyte activation May play a role in APRIL-induced protection against neuro-inflammation and demyelination

While from the data described above it is clear that cortical secretion of APRIL has a major inhibitory effect on microglia recruitment/activation and demyelination in the splenium, in the final part of this study we decided to investigate whether APRIL could also influence astrocyte behaviour in the CPZ mouse model, even though no major effect was observed on GFAP expression in the splenium and the cortex (where it was also increased due to rAAV injection). Hereto, we investigated the expression of (i) chondroitin sulphate proteoglycans (CSPGs) [44, 45] which are secreted by reactive astrocytes and form an important inhibitor of tissue regeneration in the CNS [46], and (ii) the fatty-acid–binding protein 7 (Fabp7) which protects astrocytes from ROS damage by scavenging free/oxidized fatty acids from damaged membranes/myelin [47, 48]. At first, and in contrast to our expectations, cortical AAV-eGFP or AAV-APRIL injection already is a strong trigger for increased CSPG expression in the cortex of CPZ-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. 1G-H, for both p < 0.001), with AAV-APRIL, but not AAV-eGFP, displaying the same effect in cortex of healthy mouse brain (Supplementary Fig. 1G-H, p < 0.001). Within the splenium (Fig. 4G-H) cortical rAAV injection did not increase the, presumably maximum, level of CSPG expression in CPZ-treated mice, however, it was noted that cortical injection of AAV-APRIL also increased CSPG expression in the splenium of healthy control mice (p < 0.001). This observation may indicate that – even under beneficial therapeutic conditions - astrocytes remain in an activated stage. In contrast, Fabp7 was significantly increased in the AAV-APRIL cortical injection site in both healthy control and CPZ-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. 1I-J, for both p < 0.05). Similarly, within the splenium, cortical AAV-APRIL injection induced increased expression of Fabp7 in both healthy control and CPZ-treated mice (Fig. 4I-J, for both p < 0.01). With Fabp7 being a unique difference between AAV-eGFP and AAV-APRIL injected CPZ-treated mice, we speculate that the APRIL-induced upregulation of Fabp7 might play a major role in detoxifying the CPZ-induced neuroinflammatory/demyelinating environment, and as such, reducing microglia recruitment/activation and demyelination.

Discussion

In this study, we integrated an in vitro cell-based screening model with subsequent pre-clinical in vivo validation, including quantitative MRI, histological and multi-omics analyses, to evaluate AAV-mediated delivery of APRIL as a new neuroimmune modulating therapy for inflammation-induced neurodegenerative diseases. Across both model systems, APRIL attenuated inflammation-linked neurodegeneration: in murine iPSC-derived NSPHs, unbiased proteomics and pathway analysis revealed that AAV-mediated delivery of APRIL was able to suppress activated cell-death programs under pro-inflammatory challenge; in the CPZ mouse model, cortical delivery of AAV-APRIL reduced microglial recruitment and demyelination in the splenium, with histological analyses corroborating the MRI-based observations.

Of specific interest to start this study, and before we performed the six-group pre-clinical in vivo experiment, we applied our recently established miPSC-derived neuron–astrocyte NSPH model [25] to evaluate APRIL’s effects on inflammatory injury. Building on our prior characterisation of this NSPH model under steady-state and pro-inflammatory conditions [25], we now show - via unbiased proteomics with pathway analysis - that therapy-responsive disease pathways can be resolved in bi-partite NSPHs (Fig. 2C). Although we do acknowledge that the applied NSPH model does not fully recapitulate brain tissue, we do believe that state-of-the-art in vitro NSPH models can provide meaningful mechanistic insights during first-stage evaluation of novel therapeutic interventions, and as such, de-risk subsequent animal studies [49]. Accordingly, our NSPH platform offers an important confirmatory step before time-consuming and expensive preclinical animal studies are performed, as implemented here.

Regarding the latter, and specifically related to the CPZ mouse model, our MRI-based preclinical imaging modalities have been confirmed, once again, to be a reliable non-invasive tool to either study the CPZ model itself, as well as to evaluate therapeutic intervention. In comparison to our previous works using this model, a significant technological advance in this study was the cryoprobe-enhanced MRI acquisition, which combined the more clinically familiar T2 relaxometry with microstructure-sensitive diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), for which we also used an improved processing pipeline compared to our previous CPZ studies [12, 24]. This approach increased sensitivity sufficiently to reliably (i) detect age-related white-matter changes in the splenium of healthy mice (Fig. 3C), (ii) capture CPZ-induced tissue injury in the splenium (Fig. 3C-D), and (iii) register treatment benefit in the splenium following cortical AAV-APRIL injection (Fig. 3C-D).

Of note, and subject to further investigation, our new MRI pipeline also flagged a safety/confounding signal: RD in the cortex decreased across all surgically treated groups, consistent with rAAV injection-related cortical inflammation and scarring (Fig. 3F and supplementary Fig. 1A-B-E-F). Although not described in the results section of this manuscript, we also performed a transcriptome-proteome analysis on the splenium and cortex from healthy control mice with and without rAAV injection (Supplementary Fig. 4). In line with the imaging data, our findings indicate a region-specific response to rAAV exposure. In the splenium, AAV-APRIL elicited only limited activation of DFs pathways implicated in demyelination and neurodegeneration, consistent with the absence of deviations in T2 metrics (Supplementary Fig. 4 A). In contrast, the cortex showed a broader innate and neuroinflammatory signature: both AAV-eGFP and AAV-APRIL activated CPs and DFs pathways associated with macrophage activation and CNS disorders, with responses more pronounced after AAV-APRIL (Supplementary Fig. 4B). These molecular changes align with cortical MRI abnormalities (increased T2 and RD under regular diet; Fig. 3E–F) and histopathology demonstrating rAAV-induced GFAP+ astrogliosis (Supplementary Fig. 1E–F) and a further increase in Iba1+ microgliosis specifically following AAV-APRIL (Supplementary Fig. 1A–B). Together, the data support a cortical vulnerability to rAAV-driven inflammatory programs while suggesting relative resilience of the splenium under the same conditions.

Further elaborating on the transcriptome-proteome analysis performed in this study, and more specifically related to the observed molecular alterations in the splenium, we acknowledge a primary focus on inflammatory processes and associated neuro-degenerative/regenerative processes. Herewith, our analyses identified two features that distinguished AAV-APRIL-injected mice from healthy control mice: (1) multi-omics showed a shift from classically pro-inflammatory macrophage programs (e.g., iNOS/IL-15-linked) toward alternative, repair-oriented activation in AAV-APRIL–treated mice [50, 51]; and (2) APRIL highly induced upregulation of astrocytic Fabp7 expression at the injection site and in the splenium, in both healthy and CPZ conditions. Given Fabp7’s role in lipid handling and ROS detoxification during myelin injury [47, 48], its high upregulation provides a plausible route by which APRIL could detoxify the microenvironment, reduce microglial recruitment and limit demyelination. Together with APRIL’s known capacity to bind to chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans (CSPGs) [45], and thereby inhibit their engagement with key inhibitory receptors of the receptor protein tyrosine phosphatase (RPTP) and Nogo receptor (NgR) families [45], APRIL may promote neuronal growth and support oligodendrocyte precursor cell (OPC) maturation and myelination [44, 45]. Our findings, together with those described in the literature, outline a dual mode of action for APRIL, being able to (re)direct both microglia and astrocyte inflammatory stages, and as such influence demyelinating processes in the CPZ mouse model.

Finally, this work adds to the expanding repertoire of gene-based interventions evaluated in the CPZ mouse model of neuroinflammation and demyelination. In addition to previously studied candidates, including (but not limited to) CNTF, BDNF, IL-4, IL-10, IL-11, IL-13 and HGF [10, 11, 20–23], our data position APRIL as a novel therapeutic protein capable of modulating neuro-inflammatory and degenerative cascades in the CNS. Building on our long-standing experience with cell- and gene-based strategies in the CPZ mouse model [10, 11, 21], we propose that the next experimental priority is a rational, temporally staged combination approach. Specifically, multiple factors may be deployed in alignment with their dominant mechanisms at defined phases of CPZ pathology (e.g., peak inflammation, acute demyelination, early remyelination, or chronic demyelination), with attention to dosing, delivery route, and cell-type targeting. Such mechanism-guided combinations are likely to yield synergistic effects across the modulation of inflammatory, degenerative and regenerative processes. Furthermore, since therapeutic interventions optimised in the CPZ mouse model have already been successfully transferred to more clinically relevant models (e.g., stroke and spinal cord injury) [52–54], systematically testing time-resolved delivery of combinations of multiple therapeutic proteins at different stages of disease may accelerate and de-risk translation toward clinical application.

Conclusion

In this study, by applying a multimodal pipeline that integrates quantitative MRI, immunohistochemistry, as well as a transcriptome-proteome integration analysis, we demonstrated that the cortical rAAV transduction driving neuronal APRIL expression and secretion exerts potent immunomodulatory and neuroprotective effects in the splenium of the CPZ mouse model. More specifically, APRIL attenuated inflammatory programs and molecular pathways linked to neurodegeneration, with downstream protection evident in vulnerable white-matter regions, thereby establishing APRIL as a therapeutic candidate for inflammatory and demyelinating neurodegenerative conditions of the brain. Exploratory transcriptome-proteome analyses, additionally, highlighted astroglial lipid–metabolic circuitry - marked by the astrocytic fatty acid–binding protein Fabp7 - as a potentially relevant axis of APRIL’s action. Although its contribution was confirmed by IHC, for the moment, the protective role of Fabp7 remains correlative and hypothesis-generating. Hereto, in future research, the contribution of Fabp7 has yet to be causally established (e.g. in loss-/gain-of-function models). Nevertheless, Fabp7 is presented here as a provisional mechanistic node of APRIL’s mode of action in the CPZ mouse model and invites further research in other neuroinflammation-driven degeneration models (e.g., spinal cord injury, stroke), as well as translation to human iPSC-derived organoid model systems modelling neurodegeneration.
