Introduction

Brachytherapy is a therapeutic option for prostate cancer, particularly for patients with low‐ to intermediate‐risk localized disease, and has demonstrated favorable long‐term oncological outcomes [1]. However, radiation therapy, including brachytherapy, carries risks of adverse events, including secondary malignancies [2]. Therefore, careful long‐term follow‐up is required.

This case report describes the management of prostate cancer, subsequent surveillance, and treatment strategies for secondary malignancies.

Case Report

An 83‐year‐old man who had undergone brachytherapy for prostate cancer 20 years earlier presented with dysuria. Cystoscopy revealed a space‐occupying lesion in the prostatic urethra (Figure 1a,b). Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a mass measuring up to 3 cm extending from the prostatic urethra to the external urethral sphincter (Figure 2a). Computed tomography revealed no evidence of distant metastases.

Cystoscopy findings. (a) A stricture is visible in the prostatic urethra. (b) Retroflex view of the bladder neck from within the bladder. (c) Recurrent stricture at the site of previous transurethral resection of the prostate.

Magnetic resonance images. (a) A tumor measuring up to 3 cm in diameter extending from the prostatic urethra to the external urethral sphincter; (b) Tumor shrinkage after neoadjuvant chemotherapy (gemcitabine plus cisplatin) therapy.

Prostate‐specific antigen (PSA) levels were below the limit of detection, and neuron‐specific enolase (13 ng/mL), pro‐gastrin‐releasing peptide (75.5 pg/mL), and carbohydrate antigen 19‐9 (7.2 U/mL) levels were within the normal range. The carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level (5.5 ng/mL) was slightly elevated.

Transurethral resection of the prostate (TUR‐P) was performed for histopathological diagnosis. During the procedure, several brachytherapy seeds were identified and removed. One month later, dysuria recurred, and cystoscopy revealed a recurrent lesion at the same site (Figure 1c), prompting a second TUR‐P.

Histopathology from both TUR‐P specimens was similar, showing an adenocarcinoma‐like component and a solid nested component with continuity between them. The adenocarcinoma‐like areas were partially CK7‐positive, or CK20‐ and CDX2‐positive, and p63‐ and GATA3‐negative, consistent with adenocarcinoma. The solid component was CK7‐, CK20‐, and CDX2‐negative and p63 and GATA3‐positive, suggesting urothelial carcinoma. Based on the immunohistochemical profile, urothelial adenocarcinoma was diagnosed and staged as T2N0M0.

Given the suspected urothelial origin, we administered three courses of gemcitabine plus cisplatin (GC) as neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC). After confirming tumor shrinkage (Figure 2b), the decrease in serum CEA level to 4.8 ng/mL, robot‐assisted radical cystectomy (RARC) with bilateral lymph node dissection and urethrectomy was performed. Total operative time was 8 h 23 min, with 5 h 37 min console time. The external urethral sphincter was partially resected and the urethra was completely removed. Urinary diversion was performed extracorporeally.

The surgical specimen was positive for CDX2, CK7, CK20, and p53, and negative for NKX3.1 and PSA. Nuclear β‐catenin staining was also negative. Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining demonstrated that extracellular mucin comprised less than 50% of the tumor. Viable tumor cells remained; however, the surgical margins were negative. Focal squamous metaplasia was observed. The tumor was diagnosed as enteric‐type urethral adenocarcinoma (Figure 3). Eighteen months postoperatively, the patient remained recurrence‐free, with a serum CEA level of 5.2 ng/mL.

Discussion

Prostate brachytherapy has been widely adopted as definitive treatment for localized prostate cancer. For patients with low‐ to intermediate‐risk disease, oncological outcomes, including overall survival, are comparable to those of other treatments such as radical prostatectomy [3]. However, long‐term complications, including secondary malignancies, remain an important clinical consideration [1].

Urethral cancer is rare, and chronic inflammation and prior radiation exposure are recognized etiological factors [4]. Radiation‐associated secondary malignancies (RASMs) arise in a different tissue from the primary tumor within the previously irradiated field, typically more than 5 years after exposure [5]. Brachytherapy carries a lower risk of secondary malignancy than external beam radiation therapy. RASMs exhibit a latency period of over 10 years, with risk increasing steadily thereafter [6]. A key clinical question is whether this tumor represents a secondary malignancy or recurrence/metastasis of a preexisting cancer.

No colonoscopy was performed in this case; however, the imaging findings did not indicate malignancy in other organs, including colorectal cancer. The primary lesion was centered on the prostatic urethra, and histopathological findings differed from those of prostatic adenocarcinoma, showing positivity for CK7, CK20, and CDX2 and negativity for PSA, suggesting urethral origin. Similar immunohistochemical staining patterns have also been reported in previous cases of urethral adenocarcinoma [7]. Furthermore, nuclear β‐catenin staining was negative, a finding consistent with urethral adenocarcinoma, whereas colorectal carcinoma typically shows nuclear β‐catenin positivity [8, 9]. As this case met the definition of RASMs, it likely represented a brachytherapy‐induced enteric‐type urethral adenocarcinoma. To our knowledge, although cases of urethral mucinous adenocarcinoma following brachytherapy have been reported, enteric‐type urethral adenocarcinoma has not [9].

Urethral cancer is an aggressive malignancy, with a reported 5‐year survival rate of approximately 40%–60%. Previous studies have identified proximal tumor location, advanced stage, lymph node positivity, and Black race as adverse prognostic factors. Taken together, these findings underscore the importance of early diagnosis and appropriate treatment [10]. One report described 13 cases of secondary urethral malignancy following brachytherapy; among the survivors, two underwent radical cystectomy [2]. However, RARC following NAC has not previously been reported. In the present case, NAC for GC as the treatment strategy for urethral carcinoma resulted in tumor shrinkage, enabling subsequent RARC and complete remission for 18 months after surgery. As the preoperative pathological diagnosis indicated a tumor of urothelial origin, we selected GC for NAC. Although GC is generally ineffective in colorectal adenocarcinoma, the response observed here suggests that secondary urethral adenocarcinoma may be sensitive to GC therapy [11, 12, 13]. Tumor shrinkage may facilitate intraoperative manipulation during RARC and reduce the risk of positive surgical margins. This case illustrates a potential treatment strategy for secondary urethral adenocarcinoma after brachytherapy. In the event of recurrence, GC chemotherapy would likely be considered again as a first‐line option, given the tumor shrinkage observed with neoadjuvant GC therapy. However, since viable tumor cells were still present in the surgical specimen after three cycles of GC chemotherapy, there is a possibility that recurrent disease may not be fully controlled by GC‐based treatment alone.

Secondary urethral carcinoma following brachytherapy may be overlooked because symptoms such as urethral stricture and hematuria resemble posttreatment adverse events and PSA elevation is typically absent. As in the present case, some patients develop radiation‐associated urethral carcinoma after completing routine follow‐up, delaying recognition until symptoms arise [2]. When selecting prostate cancer treatment, particularly radiation‐based therapies, patients should be fully informed about the potential risk of secondary malignancies and engaged in shared decision‐making. In patients with adverse events such as voiding dysfunction, RASMs should be included in the differential diagnosis, and further evaluation with cystoscopy and imaging studies such as MRI should be considered. Long‐term surveillance after brachytherapy is warranted to monitor adverse events, including RASMs.

Conclusions

This case highlights a potential treatment strategy for radiation‐associated enteric‐type urethral adenocarcinoma after brachytherapy and underscores the importance of considering secondary malignancies in the differential diagnosis.
