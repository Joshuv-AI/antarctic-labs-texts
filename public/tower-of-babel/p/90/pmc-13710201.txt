Using enantioselective biosensors to evolve asymmetric biocatalysts
Authors: Simon d’Oelsnitz, Wantae Kim, Nicole N Zhao, Haley Hardtke, Svetlana P Ikonomova, Nina Alperovich, Olga Vasilyeva, Michael J James, Eric S Zigon, Michael B Cory, Charlie D Johnson, Andrew D Ellington, Quincey A Justman, Michael Springer, Y Jessie Zhang, Pamela A Silver, David Ross
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiocatalysts are prized for their enantioselectivity, but slow chromatographic separations required to measure enantiomeric excess bottleneck their development. To overcome this limitation, we evolve enantioselective transcription factors (eTFs) that convert enzyme-catalyzed enantiomer concentrations into programmable gene expression outputs, focusing on imine reductases. Here, using a massively parallel reporter assay, we measure dose–response curves for over 300,000 transcription factor variants in response to an imine precursor and chiral amine products. We quantify the sensitivity, selectivity and dynamic range across variants generated by random, site-saturation and shuffling mutagenesis, isolating variants with exceptional specificity. High-resolution structures of evolved eTFs elucidate how steric effects enforce enantioselectivity, while charge interactions distinguish the imine from the amines. Using two eTFs, we create an ultrahigh-throughput chiral screen to evolve an imine reductase with inverted enantioselectivity. To support generalizability and speed, we design a genetic circuit that enables TF generation within weeks. Our methods enable rapid measurement of asymmetric reactions, supporting innovation in chemical manufacturing. Subject terms: X-ray crystallography, High-throughput screening, Synthetic biology, Biocatalysis

Main

Chirality has enormous biological consequences for pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals and fragrances, yet conventional approaches to asymmetric synthesis remain challenging1. Over the past decade, there has been rapid growth in the use of engineered enzymes due to their exquisite enantioselectivity2. Notable successes include the use of a transaminase for the production of the antidiabetic drug sitagliptin and a biosynthetic pathway engineered to produce the valuable fragrance ambrein3,4. A key challenge limiting the broad use of enantioselective enzymes is the substantial time and effort required to engineer them into fit-for-purpose catalysts, which is often bottlenecked by the speed of chiral screening5. Standard workflows require screening thousands of enzymes via slow chromatographic separations, typically across several iterative engineering rounds6. While recent innovations in high-throughput mass spectrometry have reduced screening time for some applications, these improvements cannot be used to tailor enantioselectivity because enantiomers cannot be distinguished by mass alone7.

One promising solution to the chiral screening bottleneck is the adoption of enantioselective ligand-inducible transcription factors (TFs), which convert specific intracellular chemical concentrations into programmable gene expression outputs. When coupled to a fluorescent protein, millions of biocatalysts can be screened within minutes using instruments such as fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). Many TFs are highly enantioselective by nature, such as the d-ribose-responsive regulator RbsR and the l-lactate-responsive regulator LldR8,9, and while natural TFs collectively recognize a limited chemical landscape, directed evolution methods can mold new ligand specificities for therapeutic molecules and their intermediates10,11. Encouragingly, an early study of the arabinose-responsive regulator AraC suggests that TF enantioselectivity can be tailored via protein engineering12. Recent developments in large-scale data generation are also beginning to accelerate TF customization. Coupling growth-based selections with massively parallel sequencing has enabled measurement of full dose–response functions for over 60,000 LacI mutants to IPTG13. Applying these powerful engineering methods to highly evolvable TF scaffolds, such as multidrug regulators, has the potential to rapidly modify TF selectivity for enzyme-screening applications. Despite this promise, the TF enantioselectivity has never been leveraged to evolve asymmetric biocatalysts, such as transaminases, ketone reductases or imine reductases (IREDs) prized by industry for installing stereocenters14,15.

Here we measure the dose–response curves for over 300,000 TF mutants to enantiomeric intermediates and an achiral precursor of the pharmaceutical solifenacin. Using this expansive dataset, we quantitatively compare three diverse library generation methods and isolate highly selective variants in a single round of evolution. High-resolution crystal structures reveal how sterics discriminate enantioselectivity, while charge–charge interactions are used to differentiate an imine precursor from amine reaction products. To demonstrate the practical utility of enantioselective TFs for enzyme engineering, we develop an ultrahigh-throughput screen to invert the enantioselectivity of an IRED. Finally, to improve the speed and generalizability of our approach, we create a second genetic circuit designed for rapid evolution and use it to generate enantioselective TFs for a chiral precursor of the neuromuscular blocking agent cisatracurium within weeks.

Results

Genotype–phenotype landscape of biosensor enantioselectivity

To explore the capacity of TFs to evolve enantioselectivity for valuable synthetic compounds, we targeted intermediates of the licensed pharmaceutical solifenacin—(1S)-1-phenyl-1,2,3,4-tetrahydroisoquinoline (S-THIQ), (1R)-1-phenyl-1,2,3,4-tetrahydroisoquinoline (R-THIQ) and 1-phenyl-dihydroisoquinoline (DHIQ; Fig. 1a). These molecules were chosen because (1) only the S-THIQ enantiomer is used in the production of solifenacin, (2) IREDs have been reported to convert DHIQ into either S-THIQ or R-THIQ and (3) the molecules resemble effectors of the RamR (3VVX_A) TF, which has proven to be highly evolvable10,16. A reporter plasmid expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) from a RamR-controlled promoter and a separate plasmid that constitutively expresses RamR were transformed into Escherichia coli cells, and fluorescence was measured with DHIQ, R-THIQ and S-THIQ, revealing that RamR responded similarly to all three molecules (Fig. 1a). To thoroughly explore RamR fitness landscape, three independent library design strategies were applied to diversify the RamR coding sequence on the RamR expression plasmid—random mutagenesis, site-saturation mutagenesis and gene shuffling (Fig. 1b). Random mutagenesis targeted three to four substitutions in the coding sequence on average. Site-saturation libraries targeted six sets of three proximal residues facing the ligand-binding pocket for combinatorial mutagenesis with degenerate NNS codons, resulting in 48,000 possible variants; this design has successfully altered selectivity in prior work10,16 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Shuffled libraries randomly combined wild-type RamR with three homologs by shearing each gene into ~150 bp fragments followed by reassembly using PCR. Homologs each had approximately 70%, 80% or 90% sequence identity to wild-type RamR and were selected because they showed some response to S-THIQ. (Supplementary Fig. 1).

To enable high-throughput measurement of TF enantioselective landscapes, we implemented a deep mutational scanning approach to measure the dose response of all RamR variants to S-THIQ, R-THIQ and DHIQ. Notably, measurement of full dose–response curves for all three ligands was necessary to quantify selectivity among library variants. This method relies on the following two core components: (1) a selection circuit that links TF response to cellular growth and (2) a barcode whose enrichment can be resolved using massively parallel sequencing, and whose identity can be mapped to a protein variant’s coding sequence via long-read sequencing. For phenotype–growth coupling, we used a previously developed selection circuit that couples transcriptional repression to growth in the presence of zeocin10. Repression by RamR prevents λcI (WP_000104863.1) expression, enabling the expression of the ShBle (ACY24905.1) zeocin resistance marker; conversely, RamR response to ligand blocks ShBle expression, resulting in zeocin sensitivity (Fig. 1c). The RamR expression plasmid encoding three libraries of barcoded RamR variants was transformed into E. coli cells bearing the selection plasmid. Deep sequencing analysis indicated that the final library contained a total of 309,461 unique RamR protein variants across the random mutagenesis (110,276), site-saturation mutagenesis (40,044) and shuffling mutagenesis (159,141) libraries (Supplementary Data 1).

The resulting population of cells was grown in 24 different chemical environments, which included varying concentrations of S-THIQ, R-THIQ and DHIQ with or without zeocin (Supplementary Fig. 2). Short-read sequencing of DNA barcodes was used to quantify the relative abundance of each RamR variant at four different time points to accurately resolve enrichment values (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 3). Finally, for each RamR variant, we used the fitness difference (with versus without zeocin) across all concentrations of each ligand to determine the dose–response curve using Bayesian inference. Ultimately, we generated dose–response curves for 309,461 unique RamR variants across three library design methods for S-THIQ, R-THIQ and DHIQ.

Comparison of three biosensor library design methods

For practical utility, biosensor-based screening requires efficient library design strategies to rapidly develop biosensors with target specificities and thus match the fast timescales of enzyme engineering campaigns. To help guide efficient library design, we quantitatively compared the phenotypes of RamR variants generated using three common library design strategies—random mutagenesis, site-saturation mutagenesis and gene shuffling (Fig. 2a).

For b and d, dotted lines indicate the corresponding metric (fold induction, sensitivity) of the RamR template protein; percentages indicate the fraction of variants that had higher (fold induction) or lower (sensitivity) values compared with WT. e, Number of variants in each library that surpass thresholds set for fold induction, selectivity and EC50 for each ligand. For b–e, metrics for DHIQ are in blue, R-THIQ in orange and S-THIQ in green.

First, we compared fold induction across each library, defined as the output at maximum ligand induction divided by the output with no ligand induction. Only 1.3–2.1% of variants from the site-saturation libraries yield fold induction greater than the template (wild-type RamR), whereas this fraction was slightly higher for the random mutagenesis library (4.8–5.1%) and significantly greater for the shuffled library (20.2–26.1%; Fig. 2b). The higher fold induction among shuffled variants is likely due to the lower background signal produced by homologs used during shuffling (Supplementary Fig. 4). The random and site-saturation libraries contained a similar number of mutations on average (random = 2.79, site-saturation = 2.99), therefore, the lower fold induction in the site-saturation library may be attributed to less conservative mutations accessible via the NNS codon compared with single-nucleotide substitutions (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Next, we determined the landscape of ligand selectivity and sensitivity explored by each library design method. We defined selectivity as the difference between the base-10 logarithm of the signal produced at 32 µmol l−1 of a target ligand and the average of the logarithmic signal for the two nontarget chemicals at 32 µmol l−1. Sensitivity was defined as the ligand concentration that produces a half-maximal response (EC50), by fitting to a Hill function. Across all library generation methods, the vast majority of variants lost specificity compared with the wild-type protein (Fig. 2c). Variants from the site-saturation library covered broader range of selectivities than those from the random mutagenesis library were biased towards selectivity for DHIQ and S-THIQ, which follows the selectivity of the template (Fig. 1a). Notably, the shuffled library produced variants with a wide range of selectivities covering all three ligands. In contrast, the range of sensitivities explored by each design method was comparable across ligands (Fig. 2d). Across all libraries, a greater number of variants became more sensitive to R-THIQ than to DHIQ or S-THIQ, consistent with the cross-library trend towards lower selectivity (Fig. 2c).

Finally, we compared the frequency of mutants that surpassed thresholds for fold induction (>50-fold), selectivity (>30-fold) and sensitivity (EC50 < 200 µmol l−1) for each of the three target ligands; thresholds were set to balance stringency and abundance. Interestingly, among mutants that surpassed these thresholds, all three libraries produced a larger fraction of S-THIQ-specific sensors than DHIQ-specific sensors, despite the fact that the template protein produced a similar response to both ligands (Figs. 1a and 2e). Among the three library generation methods, the shuffle library yielded a substantially higher ratio of performant sensors for each ligand compared to the random and site-saturation libraries, even when normalized by the number of variants in each library (Fig. 2e).

Characterization of highly selective biosensor variants

Effective screening of asymmetric reactions relies on the development of biosensors with high enantioselectivity and high dynamic range. We filtered our dataset on the performance characteristics of 309,461 sensor variants to identify the sensors with the highest selectivity and dynamic range for each of the three target ligands (Methods). The resulting variants were synthesized and experimentally characterized by flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. 6 and Supplementary Data 2). The top two variants for each target ligand, designated D2.1, D2.3, S2.3, S2.4, R2.1 and R2.2, were found to have very high selectivities, producing an EC50 for their respective ligands up to 56-fold lower than both noncognate ligands (Fig. 3a–c and Supplementary Fig. 6). All six sensors had very high dynamic ranges for their cognate ligands (>140-fold), which are more than sufficient for high-throughput screens. Furthermore, all three sensors had moderate to high sensitivities, with EC50 values ranging from 196 µmol l−1 (R2.1) to 2 µmol l−1 (S2.3).

To better understand the molecular adaptations underlying evolved enantioselectivity, we obtained structures for all six high-selectivity variants—four in complex with their cognate ligand and two in their apo form—S2.3 with S-THIQ (2.1 Å), S2.4 with S-THIQ (1.8 Å), D2.3 with DHIQ (2.4 Å), R2.1 with R-THIQ (2.5 Å), D2.1 apo (1.9 Å) and R2.2 apo (4.0 Å; Supplementary Table 1). The dimerization interfaces of the evolved variants were identical to those of wild-type RamR (Supplementary Fig. 7). For structures in complex with ligands, a strong positive electron density was consistently detected at the binding sites of evolved sensors, which fit with the target isoquinoline chemical structures (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Crystal structures of high-selectivity RamR variants show how charge–charge interactions have evolved to distinguish deprotonated imines from protonated amines. Under physiological pH, the amine groups of S-THIQ and R-THIQ are mostly protonated, whereas the imine group of DHIQ is predominantly deprotonated (pKa = 8.9 for S-THIQ/R-THIQ and pKa = 5.3 for DHIQ). Within the ligand-bound structures of S-THIQ–specific variants (S2.3 and S2.4), a negatively charged glutamate introduced by the C135E mutation strongly interacts with the protonated amine of S-THIQ by salt-bridge interactions (2.7 Å and 2.5 Å in distance, respectively; Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 9c). Similarly, the R2.1 variant uses a negatively charged Asp153 to specifically recognize the positively charged amine group of R-THIQ (3.7 Å in distance; Fig. 3f). Docking R-THIQ into the apo structure of R2.2 also yields a binding pose consistent with interactions between the protonated amine and Asp153 (2.8 Å in distance; Supplementary Fig. 10c). Interestingly, installation of negatively charged residues (C135E for S-THIQ–specific variants and C135D for R-THIQ–specific variants) is frequently observed among the enantiomer-specific members of the random and site-saturation libraries (Supplementary Fig. 11).

In stark contrast, the DHIQ-selective D2.3 variant contains a C135R mutation that selectively interacts with the neutral imine group of DHIQ at a distance of 3.5 Å (Fig. 3h). This same C135R substitution is highly enriched among DHIQ-specific variants in the random and site-saturation libraries (Supplementary Fig. 11). S-THIQ/R-THIQ interactions are not favored by this mutation, as amine groups are protonated at physiological pH, resulting in electrostatic repulsions with C135R (Fig. 3i). A plausible binding pose of DHIQ docked into the apo D2.1 variant also shows that positively charged Lys64 selectively interacts with DHIQ (3.4 Å in distance), whereas protonated S-THIQ and R-THIQ are not favored (Supplementary Fig. 12c,d).

While both S-THIQ–specific and R-THIQ–specific sensors use negatively charged residues to accommodate protonated ligands, their enantioselectivity is enforced by the steric hindrance introduced by the shape of the ligand-binding pocket. Both S2.3 and S2.4 variants strongly favor placement of S-THIQ over R-THIQ due to steric restraints introduced by Met185 and Phe156 (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 9d). Likewise, placement of S-THIQ is greatly restricted in the ligand-binding pocket of the R2.1 variant due to steric clashes with Met114 and Val139 (Fig. 3g). According to the docked structure of the R2.2 variant, the ligand-binding cavity prefers placement of R-THIQ, whereas modeling of S-THIQ suggests a steric clash with Cys135 (Supplementary Fig. 10d).

High-throughput screening of IRED enantioselectivity

To demonstrate the practical utility of enantioselective biosensors for rapid screening of asymmetric biocatalysts, we focused on assaying IREDs that reduce DHIQ to either R-THIQ or S-THIQ (Fig. 4a). Two screening plasmids were constructed to express either GFP or red fluorescent protein (RFP) using the RamR-S1.3 or RamR-R2.1 variants, respectively, which were selected based on their high selectivity and comparable responses to their cognate ligands (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 13). After tuning the expression of RamR-R2.1 to reduce background signal, the finalized pScreen-S1.3 and pScreen-R2.1 plasmids produced >24-fold responses with high enantioselectivity and low DHIQ interference after culturing in target enzyme reaction conditions (20 h, 37 °C; Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15). To validate the ability of these plasmids to monitor IRED enantioselectivity, a second plasmid expressing a previously characterized S-specific IRED (IR02) or R-specific IRED (IR16) was transformed with pScreen-S1.3 and pScreen-R2.1, respectively17. Culturing the resulting strains in the presence of DHIQ generated strong enantiomer-specific fluorescent signals (Supplementary Fig. 16).

To comprehensively compare our biosensor-based screening method with a state-of-the-art chromatographic method, we measured the activity and enantioselectivity of 46 diverse IREDs using biosensors and chiral liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) in triplicate. A bioinformatic approach was used to select 46 sequence-diverse IREDs based on homology to enzymes known to reduce DHIQ (Supplementary Fig. 17 and Supplementary Table 2). Following culturing with the DHIQ substrate, supernatants were used for LC–MS measurements and cell pellets were used for fluorescence measurements (Fig. 4c). Initial results with all IREDs revealed low correlation for poorly active IREDs due to the higher sensitivity of the LC–MS method (Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19). Therefore, we repeated the measurements using a subset of 15 high-activity IREDs that produce signals within the detectable range of the biosensors. This comparison revealed a logarithmic correlation between extracellular product measured by LC–MS and intracellular product measured by the biosensors (S-THIQ, r2 = 0.94; R-THIQ, r2 = 0.93), possibly due to efflux pump activity18 (Fig. 4d,e). Encouragingly, the enantiomeric excess reported by each method showed an excellent linear fit (r2 = 0.94), supporting the use of biosensors to accurately measure enzyme enantioselectivity (Fig. 4f).

We next aimed to showcase the utility of our biosensors for asymmetric enzyme engineering by screening a large library of IREDs to invert enantioselectivity. An S-specific IRED (IR35: WP_073484372.1) was selected as a template because it contains atypical ‘selectivity residues’ at standard positions 139, 187 and 194, which are thought to govern IRED enantioselectivity19. We combinatorially randomized all three selectivity residues using degenerate NNS codons to generate a library with 32,000 possible coding sequences. The library was transformed into cells carrying the pScreen-R2.1 plasmid, cultured with DHIQ, and the top 0.016% of fluorescent cells were isolated via FACS (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 20). A secondary plate-based screen was performed on clonal variants from the sorted population in the presence and absence of DHIQ. The top variants from this screen were subcloned into a fresh IRED expression plasmid, transformed with pScreen-S1.3 and pScreen-R2.1, and cultured in the presence of DHIQ to measure enantioselectivity via fluorescence (Supplementary Fig. 20).

Ultimately, we identified a mutant of IR35, referred to as IR35_C4, bearing the substitutions P130A, N177E and M184W, with inverted enantioselectivity, as determined by both the biosensor-based screen and chiral LC–MS, and a high enantiomeric excess of 86% (Fig. 5b–d). IR35_C4 was purified with a high expression yield of 10.1 mg l−1, and thermal shift assays indicated a melting temperature (Tm) of 51.86 °C. Kinetic assays revealed a turnover number (kcat) of 0.864 min−1, being modest compared to natural IREDs (~1.6 to 290 min−1), and a Michaelis constant (Km) of 75.4 µmol l−1, which is comparable to natural IREDs (~30 to 1,800 µmol l−1)17,20,21 (Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. 21). Analysis of the predicted structure of IR35_C4 docked with R-THIQ and the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP) cofactor suggested that the N177E substitution hydrogen bonds with the ligand amine, while the M184W residue π–π stacks with the ligand phenyl group to promote the formation of the R-enantiomer (Fig. 5e).

Rapid evolution of biosensors for additional chiral amines

For practical utility in enzyme engineering workflows, enantioselective transcription factors (eTFs) must be rapidly generated for a broad range of chemical structures. To improve both the generalizability and speed of our eTF-based screening approach, we report a streamlined version of our evolution circuit, termed seamless enrichment of ligand-inducible sensors (SELIS), to generate eTFs for another set of enantiomers in weeks rather than the months required to implement the massively parallel reporter assay (Fig. 1). Briefly, SELIS involves a growth-based selection to remove biosensor variants incapable of repressing transcription from their cognate promoter, followed by a fluorescence-based screen to isolate variants highly responsive to the target analyte10. The previous system split the genetic components of SELIS across two plasmids, which required cloning a separate reporter plasmid before implementation for enzyme screening, creating custom electrocompetent cells and justified lengthy subcloning between evolution rounds. To address these shortcomings, we describe a single-plasmid evolution circuit termed SELIS1 (Fig. 6a). Each round of evolution can be completed in a single week (Fig. 6b). Implementing a SELIS1 circuit for RamR required tuning the translation level of the λcI gene using modular bicistronic elements and tuning the strength of the promoter driving RamR22 (Supplementary Fig. 22).

To showcase rapid eTF generation using SELIS1, we sought to engineer biosensors selective for both enantiomers of tetrahydropapaverine (THP), because only the R-enantiomer of THP (R-THP) is used in the production of the neuromuscular blocking agent cisatracurium (Fig. 6c). RamR gene libraries were constructed using site-saturation mutagenesis, as described in this work. After two rounds of evolution, we identified two biosensors that were highly selective for S-THP (STHP2) or R-THP (RTHP2), showing a 51-fold or 16-fold difference in EC50 between the enantiomers, respectively (Fig. 6d,e and Supplementary Figs. 23 and 24). The resulting biosensors were also highly sensitive to their cognate ligands, with EC50 values ranging from 6.5 µmol l−1 (RTHP2) to 0.9 µmol l−1 (STHP2). Each sensor variant contained four mutations, and analysis of their predicted structures docked with their cognate enantiomers suggested mechanisms for evolved selectivity. For RTHP2, the K63W mutation may π–π stack with the benzyl moiety of R-THP to enforce selectivity, while the nearby L66A and M70L mutations may fine-tune pocket sterics. For STHP2, docking suggests the C134E mutation hydrogen bonds with the ligand amine while S137N bends back, in agreement with similar mutations observed in our S2.3 cocrystal structure (Fig. 3d).

Discussion

Using eTFs, we demonstrate a strategy to evolve asymmetric biocatalysts orders of magnitude faster than standard chromatographic methods. By converting enantiomer-specific reaction products into fluorescent signals, eTFs enabled screening of a library of ~8,000 IREDs expressed in cells in 30 min using FACS, which would otherwise have taken 11 days and ~16 l of solvent using a fast two-minute chiral LC–MS method. This general approach has the transformative potential to vastly accelerate both the engineering and characterization of enzymes, as most molecules in chemistry and biology are chiral23. To fully realize this potential, several current limitations of eTFs must be addressed.

First, eTFs must be sensitive enough to detect reaction products. LC–MS methods are typically more sensitive and have a wider operational range than biosensors. Indeed, our eTFs, with a ~5 µmol l−1 limit of detection, could not reliably measure the activity of poorly active IREDs (Supplementary Fig. 19). This constraint can be partially addressed through protein engineering, as TFs have been reported to detect single-digit nanomolar concentrations24,25. Furthermore, while the operational range of TFs can be narrow due to cooperativity, it can be expanded through engineering, and multiple sensors with different sensitivities can be used in parallel to broaden this range26.

Second, eTFs must be able to generalize across diverse molecular structures. Data from our group and others support the ability of multidrug regulators such as RamR and TtgR to recognize and evolve affinity for diverse chemical motifs, including synthetic heterocycles, steroids, alkaloids and terpenes10,27–29. Parallel efforts using multidrug regulators as artificial metalloenzymes further support their ability to recognize a range of unsaturated aldehydes, α-hydroxythioesters and hydroxyketones30–32. To access a broader range of chemistries, multidrug regulators can be mined from bacterial genomes using recently described computational tools33. While encouraging, profiling large chemical libraries will be needed to accurately define the boundaries of chemical space accessible to TFs and, therefore, the scope of enzymatic activities that eTFs can be used to screen.

Third, the chemical selectivity of a TF must be rapidly tailored to the target application. Here we show that extremely short evolutionary trajectories from multidrug regulators are capable of yielding eTFs suitable for enzyme screening, as only two mutations (C135E and S138E) were needed to create a RamR variant with >50-fold enantioselectivity and an EC50 under 2 μmol l−1 for S-THIQ (Fig. 3). Additionally, only four mutations were needed to ‘enantiodiverge’ RamR for binding either R-THP or S-THP (Fig. 6). These mutants can be accessed in just weeks using a single plasmid encoding the SELIS1 circuit. However, further gains in engineering speed will require moving beyond iterative evolution towards data-driven design strategies that efficiently navigate mutational landscapes.

To inform TF library design, we report a quantitative genotype–phenotype dataset for 309,461 TF variants. Our dataset uniquely quantifies selectivity for multiple ligands using full dose–response curves, which was necessary to determine enantioselectivity across all variants13,28,34. From this data, we found that the RamR-S2.3 variant approached the limit of measurable enantioselectivity (>50-fold), constrained by the purity of our R-THIQ compound (98%), highlighting the importance of disclosing inducer molecule purity when reporting large phenotype datasets. Deep TF fitness landscape datasets, such as the one provided here, should help guide the predictive design of biosensors with target phenotypes, mirroring data-driven efforts to engineer zinc fingers, Cas endonucleases and antibodies35–37.

Our comprehensive library analysis revealed important considerations for effective TF design. First, the capacity to evolve selectivity cannot be judged based on the ligand response of the template biosensor alone, similar to what has been observed for enzymes38,39. Across three library designs, many more RamR variants evolved selectivity for S-THIQ than DHIQ, despite the similar response of the template to both ligands (Fig. 2e). Second, homolog shuffling is a powerful TF library design method that may access a ligand scope wider than random or site-saturation mutagenesis. Structural analysis indicated that some shuffled mutants had enlarged binding pockets that permitted new ligand-binding poses important for R-THIQ selectivity (Supplementary Fig. 25). Similar observations have been made with generalist enzymes, where shuffled glutathione transferases exhibited unique specificity profiles40. Third, while site-saturation mutagenesis is effective in altering selectivity, its success depends heavily on the residues selected for mutagenesis. Of the six sets of three residues targeted for randomization, 90% of selective variants arose from only two sets (Supplementary Fig. 26). Intuition-based library design is complicated by the fact that TFs may bind ligands in very different poses and pocket regions41,42. Emerging opportunities to pair machine learning models trained on large phenotype datasets with oligo-pool-generated custom gene libraries may prove capable of effectively navigating complex protein–ligand fitness landscapes43,44.

In the future, we envision eTFs being used to generate large datasets that can, in turn, train models capable of predicting how enzyme sequence encodes enantioselectivity. Similar to the massively parallel reporter assay used herein to measure TF response, linking enzyme activity or selectivity to growth via eTFs would enable the large-scale quantitative measurement of enzyme enantioselectivity. Such datasets could synergize with ongoing advances in machine learning to predict enzyme selectivity and inform intelligent enzyme design45,46. In addition, these in vivo measurements would complement recently described techniques for high-throughput in vitro measurements of enzyme kinetics47,48. Overall, eTFs are powerful measurement tools with strong potential to support innovation in chemical manufacturing.

Molecular docking with AutoDock Vina

The initial 3D coordinates of the ligands were obtained by using SMILES strings for R-THIQ (C1CN[C@@H](C2 = CC = CC = C21)C3 = CC = CC = C3) and DHIQ (C1CN = C(C2 = CC = CC = C21)C3 = CC = CC = C3) as inputs to Phenix Elbow49. These ligand coordinates were docked into the binding pockets of the RamR variants R2.2 and D2.1. Docking with AutoDock Vina50 was repeated several times using different seeds and all docked poses were manually screened based on known binding modes from the R2.1 and D2.3 variants. The pose that best satisfied the mechanistic insight and the docking score was selected to generate publication-quality figures. The calculated binding affinities were −6.7 kcal mol−1 for R-THIQ in R2.2 and −7.6 kcal mol−1 for DHIQ in D2.1.

Curating IREDs

An illustration of the workflow is presented in Supplementary Fig. 17. This workflow was designed to identify sequence-diverse IREDs that reduce DHIQ. Twenty IREDs were sourced from the literature that have been reported to reduce DHIQ into either S-THIQ or R-THIQ17,21,51. Homologs of each IRED, with a minimum identity of 50% and a maximum identity of 70%, were collected using BLASTP, resulting in 1,216 total IRED homologs. IRED homologs longer than 320 amino acids were removed using the Galaxy platform52. The resulting homologs were aligned to one IRED (UniProt ID: Q1EQE0) to identify standard positions and homologs were binned into three groups according to their residues at standard positions 139, 187 and 194—homologs with P139, Y187 and F194 were considered to be S-specific; homologs with I139 or V139, and M194 or L194 were considered to be R-specific, and remaining homologs were categorized into a third group. Homologs in each group were clustered separately using CD-HIT, and percent identity cutoffs were selected to yield about 15 IREDs in each group53. Literature-verified IREDs were selected from each cluster where possible. The resulting IREDs were then pooled and IR02 was added back to yield a total of 46 IREDs.

IRED characterization with biosensor-linked fluorescence assays

The following protocol was used to generate the data shown in Figs. 4 and 5 and Supplementary Figs. 16 and 18–20. A standard curve was generated to convert compound concentration to fluorescence signals. The pScreen-S1.3 and pScreen-R2.1 biosensor plasmids were transformed into chemically competent E. coli DH10B cells and plated on LB agar plates containing kanamycin. Three colonies from the resulting plates were picked for each and grown overnight in 1 ml of M9-glycerol medium containing kanamycin in a 2 ml 96-deep-well plate (Corning, P-DW-20-C-S). The following day, 20 μl of each culture was used to inoculate separate wells in a 2 ml 96-deep-well plate sealed with AeraSeal film (Excel Scientific) containing 1 ml of M9-glycerol medium with appropriate antibiotics and 0, 2.5, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100 or 250 µmol l−1 of either R-THIQ or S-THIQ. Cultures were grown at 37 °C, shaking at 1000 rpm, for 20 h and subsequently centrifuged (3500g, 4 °C, 10 min). Cell pellets were resuspended in 1 ml PBS (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, 1.8 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.4). One hundred microliters of each cell resuspension for each condition was transferred to a 96-well microtiter plate (Corning, 3904), from which the fluorescence (GFP—excitation = 475 nm; emission = 509 nm or RFP—excitation = 569 nm; emission = 600 nm) and absorbance (600 nm) were measured using a plate reader (BioTek Synergy H1). For the data shown in Fig. 4, additional controls were included to improve measurement quality. Responses to a concentration gradient of the opposite enantiomer (for example, R-THIQ in the pScreen-S1.3-containing strain) were measured for fluorescence compensation. Additionally, the background signal produced by the DHIQ substrate was subtracted, as both S1.3 and R2.1 sensors respond slightly to 100 µmol l−1 DHIQ (Supplementary Fig. 16). These measurements were performed using both a plate reader (BioTek Synergy H1) and flow cytometry (CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer).

For IRED screening, biosensor (pScreen-S1.3 or pScreen-R2.1) and IRED plasmids were transformed into chemically competent E. coli DH10B cells and plated on LB agar plates containing kanamycin and ampicillin. Three colonies from each unique genotype were picked and grown overnight in 1 ml of M9-glycerol medium containing kanamycin and ampicillin in a 2 ml 96-deep-well plate (Corning, P-DW-20-C-S). The following day, 20 μl of each culture was used to inoculate separate wells in a 2 ml 96-deep-well plate sealed with AeraSeal film (Excel Scientific) containing 1 ml of M9-glycerol medium with the appropriate antibiotics and 100 µmol l−1 DHIQ. After 20 h of growth at 37 °C with shaking at 1000 rpm, cultures were centrifuged (3500g, 4 °C, 10 min). The supernatant was collected for HPLC analysis, and cell pellets were resuspended in 1 ml PBS (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, 1.8 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.4). One hundred microliters of the cell resuspension for each condition was transferred to a 96-well microtiter plate (Corning, 3904), from which the fluorescence (GFP—excitation = 475 nm and emission = 509 nm or RFP—excitation = 569 nm and emission = 600 nm) and absorbance (600 nm) were measured using a plate reader (BioTek Synergy H1).

IRED characterization with LC–MS

Culture supernatant from the IRED screen was filtered using a 0.22-μm nylon syringe filter (VWR, 76479-058) before analysis. S-THIQ and R-THIQ were detected by LC–UV–MS using an Agilent 1200–6130 system. Enantiomers were separated on a Phenomenex Lux Amylose-2 chiral column (250 × 4.6 mm, 3 μm) isocratically at 80% acetonitrile and 1 ml min−1 with detection at 220 nm and selected-ion monitoring of m/z 210 in electrospray positive mode over the course of a 10-min method. Unknown concentrations were calculated using standard curves analyzed concurrently with the samples. Standard curves for S-THIQ and R-THIQ were prepared using supernatants from assays performed as described in the ‘IRED characterization with biosensor-linked fluorescence assays’.

IRED library construction and enrichment with FACS

Residues at standard positions 139, 187 and 194 were targeted for combinatorial site-saturation mutagenesis in the IR35 template. Primers with degenerate NNS codons targeting these residues were used to PCR amplify the IRED expression plasmid. The resulting linear fragments were assembled using Gibson assembly (16 h, 50 °C), purified using a DNA Clean and Concentrator kit (Zymo, D4004), and transformed into cells bearing pScreen-S1.3 (for IR03 and IR16) or pScreen-R2.1 (for IR02 and IR35) via electroporation. The efficiency of all transformations exceeded 106. Transformed cells were grown overnight in LB medium at 37 °C with carbenicillin and kanamycin. The next day, the resulting populations were cultured for 20 h in the presence of 100 µmol l−1 DHIQ, and a highly fluorescent subpopulation was isolated by FACS as follows.

All sorting was performed on a Sony SH800 using IsoFlow Sheath Fluid (Beckman, 8546859). Settings were determined by adjusting voltages on the unstained control. A log scale was used and the threshold was reduced to visualize the cell population of interest on the forward scatter versus side scatter plots. The gating hierarchy was determined using negative controls by selecting single RFP-positive cells. RFP was excited using the 561 nm laser and collected in the FL3 channel, which has a 585/30 bandpass filter. The mean fluorescence intensity in the RFP channel for sorted cells was 55,609, whereas the mean fluorescence intensity of cells in the unstained control was 57.6. The Sony SH800 was calibrated according to Sony guidelines and all positive cells were bulk sorted into M9-glycerol medium for postprocessing. Sorting was performed using a 100-μm chip to reduce shear stress on the cells.

This same evolution workflow was applied to another S-specific IRED (IR02) and two R-specific IREDs (IR16 and IR03); however, variants with inverted enantioselectivity were not recovered (Supplementary Fig. 31). The high false-positive rate is likely due to cross-induction from one strain to another due to a prolonged culturing time (20 h). Specifically, the product (S-THIQ or R-THIQ) produced by a highly active IRED variant in one strain may leave the host cell, enter a neighboring cell with a poorly active IRED variant, and trigger expression of the fluorescent protein from that cell.

Enzyme thermostability determination

Protein thermostability was determined using nano differential scanning fluorimetry (nanoDSF) on a Prometheus NT.Plex (NanoTemper). The protein sample was exchanged into the reaction buffer using a 10DG desalting gravity column (Bio-Rad, 7322010). The sample was then filtered through a 0.2 µm PES filter and centrifuged at 16,000g and 4 °C for 20 min to remove any aggregates and particulates. The sample was brought to a concentration of 0.3 mg ml−1 and 20 µl of the sample was loaded onto standard capillary chips (NanoTemper, PR-AC002). Data collection was performed using the NanoTemper Thermal Denaturation Software (PR.ThermControl, v2.3.1) with a lamp intensity of 30% and a temperature ramp rate of 1.5 °C min−1 from 25 °C to 95 °C, with a refolding ramp back down to 25 °C. All data were collected in technical duplicate. Data analysis was performed using PR.Stability Analysis software (v1.1) with a two-state unfolding model, with temperature bounds defined between 40 and 90 °C.

Determining enzyme kinetic constants

Kinetic parameters were determined using purified enzyme by monitoring the decrease of NADPH at 340 nm using a multimodal plate reader (BioTek Synergy H1). Reactions were carried out using UV-transparent 96-well plates (Corning, CLS3635). The reaction volume (100 µl) contained potassium phosphate buffer (100 mM, pH 8.0), 4 µM enzyme, 0.4 mM NADPH, 5% (vol/vol) dimethyl sulfoxide and DHIQ substrate. The reaction was started by adding the enzyme to the mixture. The linear portion of the reaction, consisting of the first 30 min, was used to determine the initial velocity at each substrate concentration. A standard curve of 0.1–800 µM NADPH (Supplementary Fig. 21c) was used to convert absorbance values at 340 nm to µM product formed on the assumption that product formation is 1:1 with NADPH oxidation, and that the rate of NADPH oxidation substantially exceeds that of the IRED enzyme. Initial velocities were corrected for background oxidation of the NADPH and plotted against the substrate concentration. The data were then fit to a steady-state kinetic model (equation (1)) to determine Vmax and Km values. Vmax was then converted to a kcat value by dividing by the enzyme concentration in the reaction (4 µM). Data plotting and fitting were performed in GraphPad Prism (v11.0.0).

RamR evolution using pSELIS1

RamR libraries were generated by site-saturation mutagenesis (described in the ‘RamR library construction’) using the pSELIS1-RamR plasmid template. A 20-μl culture containing the sensor library was added to 5 ml of fresh lysogeny broth (LB) with antibiotics, zeocin and the nontarget THP enantiomer, and incubated at 37 °C for 7 h. After dilution, the culture was plated on LB agar containing antibiotics and the target THP enantiomer. Plates were incubated overnight, and the brightest colonies were selected and grown overnight in LB with antibiotics in a 96-deep-well plate. A parental RamR variant culture was also grown in parallel for comparison. The next day, 20 μl of each overnight culture was used to inoculate two wells in a fresh 96-well plate, one in the top half of the plate and the other in the bottom half. After 2 h of growth at 37 °C, the top half of the plate was induced with DMSO only, and the bottom half with the target enantiomer (typically at the same concentration used in screening). Cultures were grown for 4 h, centrifuged at 3,500g for 10 min, and resuspended in 1 ml of PBS. Fluorescence and absorbance were measured using a plate reader, and the clones showing the highest signal-to-noise ratios (typically the top 5%) were sequenced. This workflow was repeated a second time with a higher concentration of the nontarget THP enantiomer during growth selection and a lower concentration of the target enantiomer during the agar plate screen to select for more sensitive and enantioselective variants.

Structural modeling

For IRED modeling (Fig. 5e), AlphaFold3 was used to generate a structure of IR35 (MCP2166807.1) in complex with NADP, which was then docked with R-THIQ using SwissDock54. Alignment to the solved crystal structure of another IRED (Protein Data Bank ID: 5FWN) in complex with an analogous tetrahydroisoquinoline ligand ((R)-methyltetrahydroisoquinoline) in the closed configuration was used to guide the placement of R-THIQ. The close alignment of these two structures suggests that our IRED model is also in the closed conformation. In addition, we selected a ligand-binding configuration that adopted a catalytically active pose, which placed the proton-donating C3 position of NADP near the nitrogen-adjacent tertiary carbon of R-THIQ. For RamR modeling (Fig. 6d,e), AlphaFold was used to generate structures of the evolved RamR variants STHP2 and RTHP2, which were docked with S-THP and R-THP using SwissDock, respectively. The structural models were aligned with the S2.3 structure (Fig. 3d) to guide a plausible binding pose for the shared tetrahydroisoquinoline moiety of the ligands.
