Tailoring TB services for improved treatment adherence in Ghana – A mixed-methods study
Authors: Jacob Solomon Idan, Austin Arinze Obeifum, Aliyu Mohammed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis remains a significant health challenge in Ghana. While differentiated service delivery models have shown benefits in HIV care, their application to TB care for key populations other than persons with HIV is limited. This study aimed to assess the effect of the current untailored, facility-based TB care model on treatment adherence and explore preferences for Differentiated Service Delivery (DSD) among key populations in Ghana.MethodsA convergent parallel mixed-methods study was conducted in Ghana from October to December 2023. Participants included children, informal miners, persons with HIV, prisoners, rural poor, and smokers. Quantitative data were collected from 439 participants using REDCap-based structured questionnaires. Qualitative data were gathered through indepth and focus group interviews and analysed with Stata v.16 and Atlast.Ti respectively. Poisson generalized linear regression analysis was used to assess the association between lack of tailored services and medication adherence.ResultsQualitative findings revealed that the current untailored model presents rigid facility schedules, and a lack of nutritional support, which discourages adherence. Quantitative analysis confirmed that a lack of tailored services was significantly associated with lower treatment adherence scores in adjusted (aRR: 0.82, 95% CI: 0.68, 0.98) analyses. Prisoners had significantly lower adherence compared to persons with HIV (aRR: 0.52, 95% CI:0.28,0.88). Participants expressed strong preferences for DSD models, specifically community-based care with peer educators or community health workers (n=382, 87.0%) and mobile clinics (n=365, 83.1%).ConclusionThe current untailored, facility-based approach is associated with suboptimal treatment adherence. Key populations prefer DSD models that are community-based, augmented with mobile services, integrated with existing HIV and child health platforms, and supported by peer educators.Keywords: tuberculosis, differentiated service delivery, key populations, Ghana, mixed-methods

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant global health challenge, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. In 2021. 1 Sub-Saharan Africa accounted for a quarter of global TB cases, despite having only 14% of the world’s population. 1 In Ghana, there were an estimated 44,000 new TB cases in 2021, 2 with a regional treatment non-adherence of 18.4%. 3 Tailoring TB service to the needs of persons with TB has emerged in recent times as an approach to improve care and treatement adherence. 4 This approach referered as Differentiated Service Delivery (DSD) involves adjusting the what, who, where and when of health service delivery to improve quality, access and efficiency of healthcare. 5 In other contexts, such as the Tamil Nadu Kasanoi Erappila Thittam (TN-KET) and KHPT in India, DSD care models have significantly improved TB mortality and morbidity rates. 6 Similary in Ugandan, DSD model achieved 84.9% treatment success as against 72.9% pre-DSD. 7

In Ghana, DSD models for antiretroviral therapy (ART) have been implemented since 2017, including multi-month dispensing, community ART distribution, and fast-track refills. 8 For people with HIV (PWH) who are co-infected with TB, integrated TB/HIV services are provided, with TB treatment integrated into existing DSD models for ART delivery. 9 While DSD approaches have shown benefits such as treatment adherence and improved health status for PWH, including those co-infected with TB, there is a need to expand differentiated care to other key populations with TB. The TB programme report in Ghana other than PWH have identified priority TB key populations, including informal miners, prisoners, children, and rural poor communities. 2 However, among these other identified key population, DSD implemetation and scale up have been held back due to resource constraints and lack political willingness from leadership and governance. 10

Currently, TB services for key populations in Ghana, with the exception of PWH, largely follow a standardized facility-based approach.2,8 This services are referred as untailored which means the TB services are standardized, facility-based approach where the location, frequency, and provider of treatment remain static, requiring patients to adapt to the health system rather than the system adapting to them. It is a one-size-fits-all service model and does not adequately address the unique needs and treatement adherence challenges faced by diverse TB key populations.

TB adherence among key populations in Ghana has relied on quantitative measures 11 or qaulitative measures 12 only to report non-adherence rates. They often fail to capture the context-specific drivers of these behaviors for each of the key populatiions. The quantitative approaches also identify who is non-adherent but often miss why specific delivery models fail distinct groups. Therefore, this study employs a mixed-methods design to bridge this knowledge gap. This study aimed to assess the effect of the current untailored, facility-based TB care model on treatment adherence and explore preferences for DSD among key populations in Ghana. The findings are intended to inform the development of patient-centered DSD modules that bridge the gap between the health system and the individual.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a convergent parallel mixed-methods design, 13 combining quantitative and qualitative approaches. The quantitative component involved structured interviewer-administered questionnaires, while the qualitative component used in-depth interviews (IDIs) and focus group discussions (FGDs) guided by a descriptive phenomenological approach. The study was conducted across five health districts in Ghana: Kumasi Metropolitan, Obuasi, Sekyere Afram Plains, Ahafo Ano North, and Atwima Nkwabiagya North. These districts were strategically selected due to their high TB prevalence, as reported by the National TB programme. 2 Data collection took place over a three-month period (October 2023 to December 2023), allowing for a comprehensive assessment of TB key populations in these high-burden areas. The reporting of this study followed the STROBE guidline. 14

Research paradigm

This study is grounded in the pragmatism research paradigm. 15 Pragmatism prioritizes the research question and the practical application of findings to solve real-world problems using both quantitative and qualitative methods. By adopting a pragmatic stance, this study integrates the objective measurement of treatment adherence scores with the subjective lived experiences and service preferences of key populations. This approach allowed for an understanding of what is occurring (low adherence in untailored settings) and why it is occurring (structural and social barriers), facilitating the development of actionable DSD models.

Conceptual framework

The study utilized a Socio-Ecological Model framework (Figure 1) to conceptualize the factors influencing TB treatment adherence. 16 This framework posits that health behaviors are not solely determined by individual choices but are shaped by the dynamic interaction between the individual and their environment across multiple levels. These levels include:

• Individual Level: Socio-demographic characteristics (age, gender, occupation) and personal barriers (hunger/nutrition, financial constraints).

• Individual Level: Socio-demographic characteristics (age, gender, occupation) and personal barriers (hunger/nutrition, financial constraints).

• Interpersonal/Community Level: Social support, stigma from community members, and the role of peer educators.

• Interpersonal/Community Level: Social support, stigma from community members, and the role of peer educators.

• Institutional/Health System Level: The current Untailored facility-based service delivery model

• Policy Level: Broader structural factors such as prison regulations or mining laws.

Conceptual framework.

In this framework, the untailored service delivery model is conceptualized as a structural barrier that misaligns with the needs of key populations, directly contributing to the outcome variable of low treatment adherence. The qualitative exploration of preferences serves to identify the specific DSD interventions required to bridge the gap between the health system and the individual.

Sample size

For the quantitative component, the sample size was calculated using the cochrane sample size formula 17 with an estimated proportion of 0.5 of persons with TB among Key populations in Ghana (PLHIV). With a margin of error of 5%, 95% confidence level, and accounting for a 10% non-response rate, the minimum sample size estimated was 439. For the qualitative component, 6 participants across each TB key population idetentifed were purposively selected to participate in IDIs. Also, 9 participants for 1 FGD in each TB key population wre conductd to augument the study findings. Additional key stakeholders including a specialist paediatrician, a legal practitioner, an monitoring and valuation (M&E) officer from the Natioanl TB Programme (NTP), a regional TB coordinator, and a representative from the TB Voice Network were interview as well to provide key information in the study. Selection criteria included gender, age, duration of TB treatment, HIV co-infection status, and willingness to share experiences.

Study population and sampling

The study population included key populatons accessing TB care services within the five health districts. Key populations in this study were identified through stakeholder engagement based on five critical criteria: exposure risk, biological risk, barriers to accessing and completing care, potential for generating new insights, and estimated impact on the country’s TB disease burden.

A sampling frame was obtained from all the TB registers in the study health districts. Participants were selected using systematic random sampling with a k th value of 2 until the sample size was achieved. Participants who declined to participate in the study were skipped to the next available participants while keeping the interval. Children receiving TB care had guardians interviewed instead.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) accessed health care services at the TB clinic for at least 6 months, and (2) mentally sound to consent to participate. PWTB who were severely sick (admitted or hospitalized) were excluded.

Study variables and measurement

The primary dependent variable was treatment adherence, measured using the validated Medication Adherence Rating Scale (MARS). 18 The MARS is a 10-item self-report tool where the total score ranges from 0 to 10. The scoring logic was determined by the directionality of adherence for each specific question. For questions 1-6 and 9-10 (where a “Yes” indicates non-adherence), a “No” response was coded as 1. Conversely, for questions 7 and 8 (where a “Yes” indicates adherence), a “Yes” response was coded as 1. Higher aggregate scores indicated better adherence.

The key independent variable, lack of tailored services, was a binary variable (Yes/No) answered by participants if they felt current TB services met their specific needs. Other covariates included alcohol Use History which was ascertained via self-report and categorized into five levels (Never, Light, Moderate, Heavy, Abusive) based on reported frequency and quantity of consumption. Residential Status was defined based on the participant’s self-reported living location (Rural, Sub-urban, Urban). Preferences was ascertained using a multiple-response checklist of potential DSD interventions. Other variables such as age, sex and educational level were self reported during the interviews. The structured questionnaire was reviewed by a panel of five experts (two clinicians, one epidemiologist, one public health specialist, and one qualitative researcher). The Item-Level Content Validity Index (I-CVI) and Scale-Level Content Validity Index (S-CVI) were calculated. All items achieved I-CVI ≥ 0.80, and the S-CVI was 0.92.

Data collection

Quantitative data were collected using a structured questionnaire (see supplementary file 1) designed online in the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) server. Qualitative data were collected through 6 IDIs and 6 FGDs across each TB key population to ensure thematic saturation. All interview guides were developed based on the study objectives and validated through pilot testing. IDIs lasted approximately 20-30 minutes, while the FGDs lasted 30-60 minutes. All interviews were audio-recorded with participants’ consent. The audio recordings were stored on password-protected drives and will be permanently deleted in accordance with data retention protocols. To ensure data quality, interviews were transcribed verbatim. Data collection was conducted in a private environment by trained research assistants fluent in local languages, who were specifically trained to minimize power dynamics between themselves and the participants.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using Stata/SE version 16.1. 19 Prior to analysis, the normality of continuous variables, including the adherence score, was assessed using using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms. Central limit theorem assumptions were applied 20 due to large sample size. Descriptive statistics were computed for key variables, including frequencies for categorical data and means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges for continuous data, as appropriate. To assess the association between “lack of tailored services” and “treatment adherence score”, a Poisson generalized linear model was used. The dependent variable was the total score on the Medication Adherence Rating Scale (MARS). 18 Responses consistent with non-adherence were coded as 0, while responses consistent with adherence were coded as 1. For MARS questions 1-6 and 9-10, a “No” response indicated adherence and was coded as 1. For questions 7 and 8, a “Yes” response indicated adherence and was coded as 1. The total MARS score could range from 0 to 10, with higher scores reflecting better adherence. The key independent variable was lack of tailored services (yes/no). LASSO regression was applied to a candidate pool of covariates (age, sex, education, alcohol use, residential status, key population category) to select the most parsimonious set of confounders for the adherence score. The model was adjusted for variables such as key population category, age, sex, alcohol use history, educational level and residential status. Relative risks and 95% confidence intervals were reported for the unadjusted and adjusted models.

Qualitative data from in-depth interviews and focus group discussions were first transcribed verbatim and translated into English. The transcripts were then imported into Atlas.Ti software for coding using both deductive and inductive approaches as described by Colaizzi. 21 The deductive analysis was guided by the study objectives and key domains from the quantitative data, while inductive coding allowed additional themes to emerge from the data. The coding process involved reading through the transcripts, highlighting significant statements, formulating meanings, and clustering them into themes. To ensure the robustness of the qualitative findings, the analysis process utilized reflexivity and maintained audit trails during the thematic analysis process. The qualitative findings were used to triangulate and provide deeper context for the quantitative results. Objectivity was maintained through verbatim transcription and triangulation of qualitative insights with quantitative results.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Committee on Human Research, Publications & Ethics at Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (KNUST) in Ghana (Reference number: CHRPE/AP/858/23), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Permission to access the five selected health districts was obtained from the Ashanti Regional Ghana Health Service unit. Written informed consent was obtained from all adult participants. For children, consent was obtained from their guardians. The data collectors explained the study procedures and assured participants of the confidentiality of the information provided.

Result

Socio-demographics

This study identified 439 participants across six diverse key TB population group in Ghana. This included children (n=77,17.54%), informal miners (n=77,21.41%), persons with HIV (PWH) (n=100,22.78%), prisoners (n=14,3.19%), rural poor (n=93,21.18%), and smokers (n=61,13.90%). The demographic characteristics of the study participants reflected the varied nature of these key populations, as shown in Table 1. Gender distribution among the study population showed a predominance of males among miners (n=91, 29.17%) and smokers (n=61,19.55%), contrasting with a higher representation of females among PWH (n=56,44.09%). Age patterns also differed significantly across groups. While all children were, by definition, under 15, other groups spanned a wider age range. The rural poor had the highest mean age at 42.72 years (±14.49), while prisoners were generally younger, averaging 36.07 years (±12.84). The overall mean age was 35.7 years (SD = 17.5). There was no statistically significant difference in mean age across key population groups (Kruskal–Wallis test, p = 0.42). Educational attainment was generally low across all groups, with the rural poor showing the highest proportion of individuals without formal education (n=23 29.49%).

The study further sought to assess the influence of the lack of tailored TB service on treatment adherence score. The Poisson regression analysis revealed that lack of tailored TB services was significantly associated with lower medication adherence scores, both in the unadjusted and adjusted models (Table 3). In the unadjusted model, participants who reported a lack of tailored services had a 22% lower adherence score compared to those who did not (RR: 0.78, 95% CI: 0.66, 0.92). After adjusting for key population category, age, gender, alcohol use history, educational level and residential status, the association remained significant, with those experiencing a lack of tailored services having a 18% lower adherence score (aRR: 0.82, 95% CI: 0.68, 0.98). When examining the effect of key population category on adherence, prisoners had significantly lower adherence scores compared to PWH in both the unadjusted (RR: 0.49, 95% CI: 0.28, 0.83) and adjusted models (aRR: 0.52, 95% CI: 0.28, 0.88).

Situation of the untailored facility based TB services

Participants and stakeholders described the current TB service delivery model as largely one-size-fits-all, where patients with TB regardless of their specific vulnerabilities undergo standardized processes at health facilities. While stakeholders noted that services are technically available nationwide, they further highlighted significant structural and interpersonal barriers.

I think TB management is done everywhere. So, as I said earlier, once you have TB, you are not considered a key population of what you targeted disease. [KII, Regional TB coordinator]

The qualitative interview confirmed that with the exception of some screening for PLHIV, there are limited special services for other key populations. A stakeholder noted,

…for PLHIVs, we also do monthly screening. Whenever they come in for their drugs, they are screened for cough. And if they have cough, they are tested. If diagnosed with TB, they are put on treatment. If they are not diagnosed with TB, they are put on TB Preventive Therapy (TPT) for three months. [KII, M&E officer, NTP]

This rigidity creates friction for populations like miners and the rural poor, who struggle to align their livelihoods with facility schedules. As one stakeholder explained,

With access to TB diagnosis and treatment, basically, we see that the services are currently being centralised at this point in time. The services are not in the community. It does not get down to the communities, to the doorsteps of the vulnerable groups that I just mentioned. [KII, TB Voice Network]

The current facility-based approach was reported by participants as one that lacks sufficient socio-economic support, specifically the removal of the nutritional support they used to receive. Participants consistently highlighted that the strong side effects of TB medication require adequate food intake, which many cannot afford. This “hunger-induced non-adherence” was a recurring theme. A rural poor participant explained,

Because of money issues when you get the disease you won’t get food to eat. Sometimes money to buy gari to eat becomes a problem. We tend to stop the medication making the disease to come again. [FGD, Rural poor]

Preferences for TB care services to meet needs

Regarding preferences for TB care services to meet KPs needs, the study participants showed strong interest in community-based and decentralized care models, as illustrated in Table 3. Peer educators or community health workers were widely supported (n= 382,87.02% overall), particularly among PWH (n=87,22.77%), Rural Poor (n=84,21.99%) and informal miners (n=81,21.20%).

Qualitative findings explain this strong quantitative preference as a response to the stigmatizing attitudes encountered in standard facilities. For instance, PWH participants described fear of being scolded or looked down upon by nurses, whereas peer educators often TB champions provide a non-judgmental, supportive environment.

When the health service providers shouts at you. This could prevent people from accessing care. [FGD, PLHIV].

Mobile clinic visits were also highly favored across all groups (n=365,83.14% overall), with informal miners (n=81,23.01%) and the rural poor (n=77,21.10%) showing the strongest preference. Support groups specific to key populations were desired by 44.87%(n=106) of participants, with the highest interest among the rural poor (n=50,25.38%) and informal miners (n=47,23.86%), as shown in Table 4. This quantitative preference directly mirrors the structural barriers identified in the qualitative interviews. Miners reported that the illegal nature of their work and the risk of job loss prevent them from visiting facilities during standard hours. A miner in a focus group emphasized this need for on-site care:

“If we could have a health worker come to our mining site for check-ups and medication refills, it would really help us stay on treatment.” (Miner, FGD participant)

Integration of mixed-methods finding

Table 5 presents an integrated mixed-methods synthesis, where quantitative effect estimates are triangulated with qualitative narratives to generate meta-inferences. The findings consistently demonstrate that medication adherence is not solely a function of individual behavior but is strongly shaped by structural, socio-economic, and health system factors. In particular, lack of service tailoring, food insecurity, and centralized care delivery models emerge as critical barriers, while decentralization and service integration are identified as key facilitators of improved adherence outcomes.

Discussion

The findings of this study underscores the necessity of tailored, population-specific approaches to TB care in Ghana to improve treatment adherence and subsequently their treatment outcome. The results revealed that a lack of tailored TB services was significantly associated with 18% lower medication adherence scores, highlighting a critical misalignment between health system goal and the lived realities of vulnerable groups. This quantitative effect is directly confirmed by qualitative narratives in which participants described the current model as a rigid, one-size-fits-all approach that forces patients to adapt to the system rather than the system adapting to them. This further emphasizes the importance of adapting care delivery to meet the unique needs of each key populations. The association between lack of tailored services and suboptimal medication adherence aligns with the Socio-Ecological Model, which posits that health behaviors are not merely individual choices but are constrained by institutional and policy-level factors. 16 In this study, the current one-size-fits-all approach acts as an institutional barrier, requiring patients to adapt to the health system rather than the system adapting to them. This rigid structure is particularly detrimental to the rural poor and informal miners, whose livelihoods depend on labor-intensive activities that often conflict with centralized facility schedules.24,25 Similar challenges have been documented in India, where the implementation of differentiated care via the TN-KET initiative significantly improved TB outcomes by identifying and mitigating such structural risks. 6 The findings also aligns with evidence from HIV DSD models, which have demonstrated improvements in adherence and viral suppression through customized service delivery.8,26

Furthermore, the quantitative data indicated that 23.92% (n=105) of participants reported that current services do not meet their specific needs. Qualitative inquiry revealed a recurring theme of hunger-induced non-adherence, wherein participants reported discontinuing medication due to inability to afford adequate food to manage the potent side effects of TB medication. This qualitative theme explains the quantitative finding that lack of tailored services drives lower adherence due to the failure of the health system to address the socio-economic preconditions of treatment success. Participants emphasized that the potent side effects of TB medication require adequate nutrition, which many cannot afford. This finding suggests that a tailored service must look beyond clinical dispensing to include social protection mechanisms, such as nutritional support. Poor nutrition worsens TB outcomes, while TB further deteriorates nutritional status, creating a vicious cycle in low-resource settings. 27 Nutritional support improves immune recovery, reduces morbidity, and enhances treatment adherence. 27

The significantly lower treatment adherence scores among prisoners compared to PLHIV highlight the unique challenges faced by incarcerated individuals in accessing appropriate TB care. Qualitative data confirmed that centralized services rarely reach incarcerated populations, generating the meta-inference that standard facility-based care models are structurally incompatible with populations facing restricted mobility and institutional constraints. While PLHIV in Ghana benefit from integrated TB/HIV services and existing DSD models for antiretroviral therapy, prisoners face unique constraints, including restricted mobility and high levels of institutional stigma. 28 This finding is consistent with previous studies that have identified barriers to TB treatment adherence in prison settings, such as inadequate health infrastructure, stigma, and competing priorities.29,30 This underscores the need for tailored interventions that address the specific constraints and needs of prisoners, such as prison-based treatment programmes, peer support, and post-release care coordination. 28 By adapting tailored service delivery to the unique needs and preferences of key populations, there is potential to improve case detection, treatment adherence, and ultimately, TB outcomes8,9 as seen in other jurisdiction. 6

Also, health-seeking behavior was found to be affected by the quality of provider-patient interactions from the qualitative findings of this study. Reports of being “shouted at” by facility staff drives a strong preference for peer educators and community-based care (n=382, 87.02%) per the quantitative findings. This preference aligns with findings in Uganda demonstrating that community-based and differentiated service delivery models reduce TB burden by minimizing stigma, travel costs, and structural barriers to care, thereby improving case detection and treatment adherence.7,31

Additionally, the high preference for mobile clinics, and peer support groups among our participants from the quantitative assessment aligns with evidence supporting the effectiveness of these DSD strategies in improving TB care access and outcomes.32,33 Qualitative data clarified that this preference is motivated by the logistical burden of separate appointments. In the context of child health, a guardian expressed how great it would be to have TB services integrated with other child health services in order to avoid multiple trips to the health facility. This echoes the growing recognition of the importance of integrated, patient-centered care in addressing the complex needs of individuals affected by TB, particularly those with co-morbidities or belonging to multiple key population groups.34–36 Future DSD models for TB should prioritize the development and evaluation of integrated service delivery approaches that optimize patient outcomes and health system efficiency.

Strenghts and limitations

This study utilized a convergent parallel mixed-methods design; however, the quantitative component relied on cross-sectional data collection, which restricts the ability to establish causal inferences regarding how preferences and adherence behaviors evolve over time. Additionally, while the qualitative inquiry provided rich contextual insights, the sample size of in-depth interviews and focus group discussions was relatively small, which may limit the broader transferability of the thematic findings to all TB key populations in Ghana. Finally, the reliance on self-reported data for the MARS adherence scale introduces potential recall and social desirability biases, as participants may have over-reported adherence or under-reported barriers to appear compliant.

Conclusion

The findings of this convergent parallel mixed-methods study generate robust meta-inferences that demonstrate the current untailored, facility-based TB care model in Ghana is structurally misaligned with the lived realities of key populations and is significantly associated with lower treatment adherence. Across all five meta-themes, the integration of quantitative effect estimates with qualitative narratives reveals that effective TB service delivery must transcend clinical customization and address the multi-level determinants of adherence. Participants expressed a strong preference for DSD models, specifically community-based interventions involving peer educators and mobile clinics to bridge the gap between health facilities and patients. Implementing these tailored, patient-centered strategies, along with integrated TB-HIV services, is essential to improve adherence outcomes and effectively reduce the TB burden in Ghana.

Implication for policy and practice

The convergence of the quantitative and qualitative findings of this study with this international evidence tells that the current untailored, facility-based model in Ghana is structurally ill-suited to the realities of key populations, and the preferences expressed by participants are not merely aspirational but are evidence-based demands that mirror successful interventions elsewhere. For Ghana specifically, our findings support the consideration for expansion of DSD beyond PLHIV to include informal miners, prisoners, the rural poor, children, and smokers through: (i) deployment of routine mobile clinics to mining sites and rural communities; (ii) integration of TB services with HIV and child health platforms; (iii) institution of nutritional support packages; (iv) establishment of peer educator programs tailored to prisoners and the rural poor; and (v) policy amendments to enable multi-month dispensing and community-based directly observed therapy (DOT).
