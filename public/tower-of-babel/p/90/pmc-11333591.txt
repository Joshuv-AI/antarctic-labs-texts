Formation processes and mechanisms of a fault-controlled colluvial landslide in the Qinling-Daba Mountains, China
Authors: Wenbo Zheng, Yanbo Cao, Wen Fan, Xin Liang, Shaoqing Yuan, Wenwei Gao, Jikai Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFaults play a crucial role in shaping the formation and damage patterns of landslides in the mountainous region, particularly in Qinling-Daba (Qinba) area in China. On 6 October 2022, following a 4-day rainfall event totaling 221.5 mm, a landslide occurred in Hanwang Town, Shaanxi Province. The left boundary of the landslide coincided with a fault, which influence the formation and movement development of the landslide. To further understand and quantified the formation process and damage mechanism of the landslide, a comprehensive study was conducted, incorporating field investigations, local rainfall data, and various methods including unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), numerical simulations, and laboratory test. The results indicate that fault dictate the formation of the Lijiaping landslide by influencing the mechanical strength of the rock mass and the catchment topography in the landslide area. Due to fault, the rock mass in the landslide area is high fragmentation, with a softening coefficient of about 0.52. Weathering resulted in numerous residual and slope sediments in the landslide area, providing ample material for the landslide. Meanwhile, the fault activity led to a wedge-shaped topography in the landslide area, with an average Terrain Wetness Index (TWI) of 3.43, significantly higher than the Hanwang Township average of 1.47. This creates a hydrogeological structure favorable for landslides. Numerical simulations revealed that the maximum velocity of the landslide reached 5.05 m/s and the maximum displacement was 53.18 m, both occurring in the central part of the landslide. These findings offer crucial scientific insights for understanding and preventing similar geological hazards.Keywords: Fault-controlled landslide, Hydrogeological structures, Topography, Formation process, Failure mechanismsSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Hydrology, Natural hazards

Introduction

Landslides, a common type of geological disaster, have caused significant impacts including heavy casualties, property losses, and ecological damage1,2. Landform changes play a crucial role in the formation of landslides, often influenced by fault activities3–5. In fault zones, crustal dynamism is more pronounced and can significantly impact the terrain, geomorphology, and stratigraphic formations6,7. This variability can directly or indirectly trigger landslides in surrounding areas. Fault displacement may cause uneven settling or lifting of strata, leading to varied erosion levels and altering surface topography8–10. Topographic changes typically involve alterations in surface elevation, slope gradient, aspect, and catchment area, which influence the effects of gravity and hydrodynamic forces on soil movement and sliding11–13. In this context, there is a close association between terrain changes caused by fault activity and the occurrence of residual colluvial layer landslides. Therefore, it is highly necessary to study such landslides from the perspective of faults.

Increasing evidence suggests that faults are crucial in the initiation and occurrence of landslide hazards14–18. For instance, the 9 October 2019 landslide in Sataun, India, was primarily due to a fault traversing the area, resulting in the deterioration of rock mass properties19. These studies mainly focus on the impact of faults on rock landslides. However, there is still a significant gap in existing research regarding how faults alter the terrain and subsequently control colluvial layer landslides, especially concerning the effects on the thickness of residual colluvial layers and hydrological boundaries. Residual colluvial layers differ from rocks; they are characterized by loose, unconsolidated materials, and their behavior under stress conditions differs from that of solid rock masses20,21. They are more susceptible to geological disasters influenced by terrain and water accumulation. Long-term, understanding the formation and failure patterns of colluvial layer landslides in fault scenarios is crucial. This knowledge is vital for devising effective prevention and control strategies to reduce the risks associated with these disasters. The Lijaping landslide in the Qinba Mountains serves as a typical example of this.

The Qinba Mountain region, acting as the natural boundary between the north and south of China, has become become an area of high incidence of geological hazards due to the activity of various types of faults22–24. These fault activities have caused local uplift or subsidence, altering the topographical relief, and play a crucial role in the formation of geological disasters25,26. Previous research in this region has primarily focused on the triggering role of rainfall in landslides27,28, while studies on the controlling role of faults in landslides have been relatively scarce, and such research has mainly concentrated on the impact of faults on rock landslides29–31. However, with the increase in extreme rainfall events, landslides in colluvial layers controlled by faults are becoming more frequent. Consequently, gaining a thorough understanding of the formation process and failure patterns of these types of landslides is crucial.

From October 3 to 6, 2022, Hanwang Town recorded a cumulative rainfall of 221.5 mm, constituting continuous rainy weather that exceeded the 'rainstorm' level. This persistent rainfall triggered a colluvial layer landslide, formed due to topographical changes caused by fault activity. The landslide severely damaged transportation roads and destroyed a residential house at the base of the slope. This study, based on field surveys, UAV aerial photography, numerical simulation, and laboratory tests, aims to comprehensively analyze the formation process and failure mechanism of the Lijiaping landslide under the control of faults. First, through field surveys and borehole detection, we obtained information on the geological structure and stratum occurrence of the landslide and surrounding areas, revealing the transformative effect of faults on regional geology. Then, combined with rock mass mechanics tests, we assessed the damage effects of faults on rock masses and their control over the thickness of residual colluvial layers at the landslide site, thereby uncovering the relationship between fault activity and residual colluvial layers. Additionally, using remote sensing technology and Geographic Information Systems (GIS), we analyzed high-resolution Digital Elevation Models (DEM) to quantify the impact of fault activity on terrain changes and hydrological conditions. Finally, by combining numerical simulation with laboratory tests, we determined the mechanical properties of the soil through rapid shear tests and simulated the landslide deformation process using the discrete element method (PFC2D). We particularly focus on the control of faults on the thickness of the colluvial layer and the catchment topography in the landslide area. Numerical simulations revealed details of the landslide dynamics,while rapid shear tests in the laboratory and analysis of triggering factors enhanced our understanding of its failure mechanism.

Regional settings

Geological settings

The geologic structure of Hanwang town is complex, largely due to significant tectonic activity. The mountains show obvious uplift, and the topography varies significantly. The highest elevation, located at Phoenix Mountain in the north, is 1859.43 m, while the lowest elevation is 320.69 m (Fig. 1a). The Hanwang Fault Zone, located at the junction of the North China Plate and the Yangtze Plate, is a crucial component of the South Qinling thrust nappe tectonic belt. This fault zone formed during the Himalayan orogeny and further developed during the neotectonic period. It consists of a series of imbricate faults with a general northwest-southeast orientation, extending approximately 24 km through the entire Hanwang Town within the study area. This ancient fault zone has experienced multiple phases of tectonic activity, resulting in rock compression and fragmentation along the fault, characterized by intense mylonitization, brecciation, and fracture cleavage. The north wall of the fault consists of the Yaolinghe Formation from the Late Proterozoic, and the south wall comprises Cambrian and Ordovician strata, together forming a regional brittle fault32. Concurrently, a series of secondary fault structures emerged in the fault zone (Fig. 1a). The investigation revealed a strong correlation between the left boundary of the Lijaping landslide and the adjacent fault, which has significantly influenced the topography and geomorphology of the area.

Meteorological conditions

Hanwang Town is located in the Qinba Mountain area, part of the north subtropical humid monsoon climate region. The high elevation of the Phoenix Mountains in the north not only blocks the cold northwestern flow but also traps warm and humid air from the southwest, leading to high annual rainfall in the area33. The average precipitation in the years 2001–2022 was 1083.3 mm. However, this precipitation is unevenly distributed throughout the year. The monthly average precipitation during summer and autumn constitutes 90.01% of the annual total. Daily rainfall data was collected for one month prior to the landslide (Fig. 2). The inducing rainfall began on October 3, with the peak single-day rainfall reaching 98.6 mm. By the time of the landslide, the accumulated rainfall totaled 221.5 mm, exceeding the threshold for heavy rainfall. Previous research has extensively studied the intensity and duration of rainfall in Qinba area34.

Characteristics of the Lijiaping landslide

The landslide occurred within the Hanwang fault zone. The height of the landslide ranges from 420 to 441 m, with a slope gradient of 23° for the mass and an overall slide angle of 265° (Fig. 1b). This is a small soil shallow landslide, approximately 75 m long, 45 m wide, with a thickness of 9–10 m, and a total volume of about 33,750 m3. In this survey, detailed measurements were made of the lengths and orientations of the cracks. Based on the survey data, the landslide area was divided into four subdomains: A—the deformation failure zone at the landslide's rear boundary, B—the extrusion deformation failure zone on the right side of the landslide, C—the severe deformation area of the catchment gully, and D—the sliding mass accumulation area (Fig. 3a).

At the rear edge of the slope in the deformation and failure area (Area A), slope sliding resulted in two fault steps and numerous tension cracks. The heights of the fault steps vary from 0.2 to 0.49 m, with orientations ranging from 176° to 234°. The widest tension crack is up to 0.96 m and the deepest is 0.76 m, oriented at 176° and spanning the entire area. These cracks, perpendicular to the direction of slope movement and parallel to the steep slope formed after the landslide, confirm the overall sliding of the soil mass at the rear edge (Fig. 3i). Numerous shear cracks crossing the road caused severe damage to the transportation route. The road subsided by 1.5 m and moved forward by 1.27 m due to slope sliding (Fig. 3h). Shear cracks on the retaining wall surface varied in length from 0.3 to 1.9 m. The left side of the road suffered more severe subsidence due to water accumulation, with cracks up to 2.85 m long oriented at 274° (Fig. 3g). In Area B, on the right side of the landslide, pressure from the soil mass in Area A caused an uplift of about 0.73 m and created numerous echelon shear cracks, ranging from 0.46 to 0.66 m in length. Some of these cracks, oriented between 273° and 278°, connected with the shear cracks on the road surface(Fig. 3c). In the central area, several fault steps with heights from 0.3 to 1.4 m and orientations between 172° and 194° developed (Fig. 3b). In this area, slate outcrops with an attitude of 236°∠38°. The sliding of the lower part of the slope caused soil subsidence and the development of parallel tension cracks ranging from 0.6 to 3 m in length, oriented between 270° and 274°, with visible water erosion marks. Area C, showed the most pronounced deformation and failure, especially in soil sliding and subsidence. Multiple parallel fault steps were observed on the left side of the area, with a height difference of about 0.25 m for the first level, 0.8–1.2 m for the second level, and 0.4–0.9 m for the third level, all oriented at 263° (Fig. 3d). A gully, oriented at 272°, formed in the central area due to intense water accumulation, with a top width of 1.7 m, depths of 2 and 2.1 m, and a bottom width of 3.3 m. Moreover, the combined effects of water accumulation and slope sliding and subsidence resulted in numerous echelon tension cracks during the sliding process, ranging from 0.4 to 2.3 m in length and oriented between 250° and 253°. In the landslide deposition area (Area D), the slope moved forward by about 4.3 m, causing damage to a residential house. The traces of soil sliding on the side wall of the house were clearly visible (Fig. 3e), and the retaining wall on the right side of the base of the slope was damaged (Fig. 3f).

To determine the subsurface structure and topography of the landslide area, 11 boreholes were established in the landslide zone, totaling 169.7 m in depth, and four geological cross-sections were chosen (Fig. 4). Engineering geological prospecting reveals that the strata in the landslide area are divided into three layers, from top to bottom. The top layer consists of Quaternary Holocene colluvial deposits (Q4dl+el) with thicknesses varying between 1.4 and 9.6 m and primarily made up of high-viscosity silty clay with broken stones. The content of broken stones ranges from approximately 30–45%, increasing with the depth of burial. The middle layer is predominantly slate from the Cambrian Jianzhuba Formation (Єj), with a thickness ranging from 3 to 13.8 m and high water content. Due to tectonic effects, this layer is fractured and thinly laminated, feeling powdery and muddy. As depth increases, the rock core transitions from a loose state to a more fractured block state. The bottom layer is diabase, an intrusive rock in the landslide area, characterized by a low degree of weathering and a block-like rock core. On the left side of the landslide, an outcrop of the diabase dyke is visible. Figure 5 illustrates the cross-section of the landslide area.

Field investigations and borehole data indicate that the landslide mass predominantly comprises upper layer colluvial silty clay and a minor portion of highly weathered, fractured slate. Fault activity has reshaped the terrain of the landslide area into a wedge shape, thus creating an area conducive to water accumulation. Concurrently, a thick layer of loose, expansive, viscous soil, exceeding the thickness in surrounding areas, has formed in the landslide zone, supplying substantial material for the landslide. The landslide area is characterized by widespread tension cracks, shear cracks, fault steps, and evidence of water erosion. Considering the characteristics and interrelationships of various cracks, including their length, width, and orientation, the slope demonstrates further signs of instability and a heightened risk of destabilization.

Impact of fault activities on initiating landslides

In the Lijiaping landslide area, despite the fault outcrop not being very apparent, we found fault breccia and diabase intrusions along the left boundary of the landslide (Fig. 6e,f). These intrusions were predominantly in structurally weak areas (Fig. 6g), such as folds, and underwent deformation and uplift along with the mountain, suggesting multiple periods of tectonic activity in the landslide area. Significant changes were also observed in the attitude of the rock strata on both sides of the landslide. The rocks in the landslide area are primarily composed of slate, originating from shallow marine facies of argillaceous limestone, categorized as low-grade metamorphic rocks formed due to regional metamorphism36. From these surveys, we inferred that the fault crosses the left side of the Lijiaping landslide. The influence of fault has led to a rock strata attitude of 210°∠35° in the landslide area and its northern side, and 212°–215°∠72°–75° on the southern side, signifying a notable variation in the dip angles of the rock layers. We constructed a regional geological cross-section that includes the fault, based on field-measured data, with the profile line arrangement as shown in the Fig. 6b. It is evident from the diagram that the area is significantly influenced by the fault, with notable changes in the stratum attitude, leading to the formation of a wedge-shaped terrain in the landslide area (Fig. 6a). The landslide area is located at a lower topographical node, constituting a geological environment with high potential for runoff convergence.

We carried out field surveys in and around the landslide area to assess the impact of faulting on rock mass structure. About 350 m north of the landslide, an outcrop of the central fault was discovered (Fig. 6c,d). The fault caused parallel unconformable contact between the rock masses on the north and south sides: the north side consisted of tuffaceous slate from the Lower Sinian Yaolinghe Formation, while the south side was comprised of slate from the Cambrian Jianzhuba Formation. Numerous wrinkles were observed in the rock layers, and the attitude of the fault plane was 220°∠43°, with a fracture zone width of about 3 m. The Lijiaping landslide area was influenced by both the central fault and the secondary fault along its left boundary. The faulting enhanced the damaging effect on the rock mass, leading to an increased number of structural planes in the landslide area, with highly developed joints, fissures, and other permeable structural planes. In the landslide area, 64 structural planes were surveyed, with their pole density map (lower hemisphere projection) depicted in the Fig. 7. The map indicates four dominant structural planes in the landslide area, identified as J1: 99°∠28°, J2: 41°∠58°, J3: 18°∠59°, J4: 335°∠70°, all classified as shear joints. When superimposing the orientations of the dominant structural planes, it is evident that their great circles intersect at wide angles. This intersection pattern indicates that the structural planes segment the rock mass into fragmented blocks, considerably diminishing its integrity.

Uniaxial compressive strength reflects the mechanical properties of rock masses, while the softening coefficient indicates the weathering resistance of rock masses under the influence of water. Comparing these two mechanical parameters can elucidate the changes in the mechanical properties of rock masses in landslide areas caused by fault zones. This helps to explain the formation mechanism of thick residual colluvial layers in landslide regions. Comparative experiments were carried out on rock samples from the landslide area and from areas without visible faults (Fig. 8). The results, detailed in the Table 1, show that the uniaxial compressive strength of the rock mass in the landslide area is 16.74 MPa when dry and 8.63 MPa when saturated, yielding a softening coefficient of 0.52. Compared to rocks from other areas, the rock mass in the landslide area exhibits a lower compressive strength, softening coefficient and shear strength. This suggests that the mechanical strength of the rock mass in the landslide area has significantly decreased under the influence of faulting. Fault activity led to the fracturing of the rock structure, resulting in highly developed joints, fissures, and other permeable structural planes. These structural planes served as effective infiltration paths and channels for rainfall. The lower softening coefficient suggests increased susceptibility of the rock mass to weathering under continuous water accumulation. Therefore, after weathering, the rock mass forms thick residual colluvial layers at the landslide site. These loose residual colluvial layers provide an abundant material source for landslides, which can be easily triggered under conditions of heavy rainfall and water accumulation.

Our survey results lead us to infer that on one hand, the infiltration and accumulation of water due to prolonged rainfall accelerated rock weathering, leading to the formation of a residual soil layer in the landslide area. On the other hand, field investigations showed that the slope angle in the landslide area is 23°, which is significantly gentle than the upper part of the slope. Consequently, rock debris weathered from the upper part of the slope was transported by water flow or gravity to the landslide area, forming a colluvial soil layer. The combined effects of these processes created a thicker surface soil layer in the landslide area compared to surrounding regions, reducing stability of the slope. Furthermore, fault activity led to significant terrain changes, situating the landslide area at a topographical low point conducive to water convergence. These two factors together constituted the fundamental conditions for landslide occurrence. Analysis of topographical feature changes

The impact of fault activity on altering the topography and geomorphology of the landslide area is significant. In order to quantitatively assess the effect of fault activity on hydrological conditions in the landslide area and the topographical changes before and after the landslide, we utilized high-precision Digital Elevation Model (DEM) data obtained before and after the landslide event. Studies by37 and38 suggest that DEM, Topographic Wetness Index (TWI), and other geomorphic factors are highly effective in analyzing catchment conditions in landslide areas. We utilized UAVs to acquire high-precision DEM data both before and after the landslides. The pre-landslide data was collected using a JOUAV CW-15 drone equipped with a Sony 7RM4 full-frame camera on July 15, 2022, with a DEM data precision of 0.1 m (Fig. 10a,b). Post-landslide data was gathered using a DJI M300 drone with a Raybur Five lens, also achieving a DEM data precision of 0.1 m (Fig. 4). Eight indices were selected for analysis, including elevation, slope gradient, slope direction, curvature, Terrain Ruggedness Index (TRI), Topographic Position Index (TPI), TWI, and Stream Power Index (SPI), to examine the topographic changes.

(a) Drone flight path configuration. (b) Hanwang Town orthophoto and DEM. (c) Hanwang town TWI. (d) Landslide area TWI. (e) Landslide area SPI.

High-resolution DEM data from drones revealed that the topography and geomorphology influenced the formation of a catchment area of approximately 13,600 square meters behind the landslide mass. The catchment area is more than four times the size of the landslide area. Additionally, the direction of surface runoff highly corresponds with the inclination of the rock layers and the orientation of the mountain range. The rainfall distribution map (Fig. 2) indicated a time lag of about 1.4 days between the landslide occurrence and peak rainfall. This time lag is directly associated with the catchment area characteristics of the mountain39. Intense rainfall resulted in substantial surface and subsurface runoff, supplying ample water for the landslide. Continuous rainfall led to the landslide's delayed occurrence compared to the peak rainfall, and water accumulation on the left boundary of the landslide formed a gully up to 2 m deep.

A comparison of TWI values indicates that the average TWI of the landslide area is 3.43, which is markedly higher than the average of 1.47 in Hanwang Town and the average of 0.53 in the watershed where the landslide is located (Fig. 10c). This suggests that faulting significantly increases the potential for water convergence in the landslide area compared to other areas40. Areas exhibiting high TWI values predominantly line the hydrological pathways of the landslide, with the highest values noted on its left side. This observation is consistent with the on-site investigation findings of the landslide. Heavy rainfall leads to concentrated water flow on the left side of the landslide, causing a swift rise in groundwater levels, increased soil moisture, and eventual soil saturation. Drainage gullies on the left side of the landslide corroborate this observation (Fig. 10d,e).

DEM were used to compare and analyze topographical and geomorphological changes before and after the landslide. The results are shown in Table 2. The analysis revealed significant changes in elevation, curvature, SPI, and TWI, all of which are closely linked to hydrological structures. High-resolution DEM analysis demonstrated increasing trends in SPI and TWI before and after the landslide. This implies that the post-landslide terrain is more prone to water retention, potentially creating conditions favorable for future landslides, especially during heavy rainfall. The increases in TPI and TRI suggest significant fault displacement and cracking post-landslide, aligning with the deformation features observed in field surveys. Analysis of landslide movement process and failure mechanism

To conduct a comprehensive analysis of the failure mechanism of the landslide, we first recreated the movement process of the Lijiaping landslide. we used UAVs to obtain topographic data before the landslide and gathered field investigation data afterwards. Section 3-3, which exhibited the most obvious deformation, was selected for analysis. The discrete element method in PFC2D software was used to simulate the landslide's deformation process, effectively collecting data on sliding velocity, distance, and scope of influence. According to42 numerical modeling of the Yongjia County landslide, the PFC2D calculated results closely matched the actual data. The sliding mass primarily consisted of overlying loose silty clay from the Quaternary period, characterized by high viscosity. Consequently, the contact bond model was chosen for this simulation. The model included 11,215 particles, with the bottom width of the sliding source region measuring 70 m. The actual elevation, as measured in the field investigation, was 20 m (Fig. 12c). Nine parameters were selected for the simulation, including particle density, porosity, friction coefficient, normal bond strength, tangential bond strength, stiffness ratio, deformation modulus, and damping ratio. The damping value was set at 0.7 to quickly balance the simulation. In the triaxial compression test, we set the model's width to 200 m and height to 400 m to ensure a sufficient number of particles to eliminate size effects. Considering that the pre-landslide rainfall intensity of 178.07 mm has already exceeded the regional threshold of 135.7 mm, and based on the field survey results, the soil was approaching saturation at this time. To accurately reconstruct the landslide's movement process, we chose to use the parameters of the soil in a saturated state as the reference standard. After a series of tests, we confirmed that the model particles have a cohesion of 23 kPa and an internal friction angle of 18.2°. These results are very close to the soil cohesion and internal friction angle obtained from laboratory tests (Fig. 12a,b). Figures 13 and 14 depict the simulation process of the Lijiaping landslide. The simulation results show that initially, at 0 s, the slope was in a steady state. Subsequently, the back of the slope began to deteriorate and slide, causing numerous tension cracks at the trailing edge and increased porosity between soil particles. By 30 s, landslide debris had accumulated at the slope's base, leading to road damage and sinking. Afterwards, the soil mass on the rear side of the slope slid slowly, but the sliding speed in the middle section changed drastically due to increased water accumulation. At 65 s, movement on the side slope stopped, with soil accumulated at the bottom, and the entire side slope appeared to have sunk. This scene closely matched the post-landslide photograph taken by a UAV. Additionally, the velocity cloud map simulation shows that in the landslide's first five seconds, the monitoring sphere's speed spiked, then slowed and fluctuated within a smaller range until the end of the landslide. Specifically, the No. 4 monitoring sphere indicated a peak speed of 5.05 m/s. At 30 s, this sphere's speed slightly increased, suggesting severe slope deformation. Furthermore, the accumulated displacement cloud map shows the longest displacement occurred in the middle section of the slope (No. 4 and No. 6 monitoring spheres), explaining the significant deformation in this area. The simulation results indicate a significant increase in the porosity between soil particles. Field surveys reveal multiple parallel tension cracks at the rear of the slope, providing ideal infiltration pathways for rainfall. The displacement cloud map further indicates that the landslide is not in a stable state. In this state, further landslides may be triggered by extreme rainfall, potentially threatening structures at the slope's base. Therefore, the simulation results align closely with field investigation findings on the landslide's accumulation form, affirming the simulation's accuracy.

The landslide simulation demonstrated that soil saturation leads to reduced shear strength, resulting in a landslide. This suggests a strong correlation between increased soil moisture content and decreased physical strength in the Lijiaping landslide area. The reddish-brown soil in the landslide area exhibits characteristics of expansive soil. A thorough experimental analysis of soil samples from the Lijiaping landslide area was conducted. Test results indicated a porosity ratio of 0.721 for the soil samples, which were rich in clay particles and demonstrated strong hydrophilic properties. At 30% soil saturation, the moisture content was 6.6%, cohesion 115 kPa, and internal friction angle 32.3°. At 100% saturation, the moisture content rose to 23.7%, cohesion decreased to 26 kPa, and internal friction angle fell to 19.4°. The decreases in cohesive force and internal friction angle were 77.4% and 39.9%, respectively. This indicates that the shear strength of the soil in the landslide area significantly decreases after absorbing water, increasing the risk of liquefaction, which helps explain the formation of the drainage gully at the left boundary of the landslide. After absorbing water and expanding, the soil increased both the hydrostatic and hydrodynamic pressures in the slope mass. The additional gravitational forces increased the vertical stress, thereby enhancing the shear stress43–45. Moreover, when the soil approached a saturated state, its shear strength significantly decreased, ultimately triggering the Lijiaping landslide event. Therefore, the changes in topography and geomorphology caused by faulting, combined with water accumulation under conditions of heavy rain, were the fundamental causes of this landslide.

Discussion

The formation process and failure mode of the Lijiaping landslide are jointly influenced by fault activity and heavy rainfall. First, fault zone activity significantly alters the stratification in the landslide area, forming wedge-shaped terrain that makes the landslide zone a good water convergence area and controls the hydrological boundaries of the landslide area. Second, ongoing fault activity continually deteriorates the rock mass. Field surveys have identified five sets of high-angle intersecting joint fractures, which fragment the rock mass, increasing the infiltration pathways for rainfall. Simultaneously, the fractured rock mass has lower mechanical strength. Experimental data show that, due to the influence of the fault, the uniaxial compressive strength of rocks in the landslide area decreased by 25.2% and 31.1% under dry and saturated conditions, respectively, and the softening coefficient dropped to 0.52. Continuous moisture infiltration accelerates the weathering of the fragmented rock mass, ultimately resulting in the accumulation of thick residual colluvial layers at the landslide site (Fig. 9). Finally, heavy rainfall rapidly increases the soil moisture content. Influenced by the terrain, water converges in the landslide area, significantly reducing the physical strength of the soil, eventually causing the slope to deform from the rear edge, forming numerous parallel tension cracks and stepped faults. The sliding lasted a total of 65 s, causing road damage and subsidence on the slope surface. Deformation was most pronounced in the central area of the landslide due to water convergence, with the highest sliding speed and largest displacement, and a 2-m deep water erosion gully formed on the left boundary of the landslide. The overall sliding of the slope damaged the retaining wall and a house at the foot of the slope, with a large amount of landslide debris visible.

The Lijiaping landslide, unique in its nature, occurred as a colluvial layer landslide significantly influenced by faulting. Its formation process is notably complex, dictated by a specific geological structure and hydrological environment. This includes a combination of fault-induced topographic and geomorphological changes, coupled with water accumulation. Its disaster model significantly differs from other fault-controlled landslides46–48. Multiple periods of tectonic activity have subjected the Lijiaping landslide area to extensive fragmentation of rock masses, resulting in well-developed joints, fissures, and other permeable structural planes. Additionally, faults modify the attitude of rock strata, impacting the topography and geomorphology. These changes lead to surface uplift, increased slope gradients, and uneven terrain, altering water flow pathways and catchment area distribution. These changes affect both water flow concentration and distribution and accelerate weathering in the fragmented rock mass49. This results in the accumulation of thick colluvial layers at the Lijiaping landslide site, impacting slope stability. Additionally, Hanwang Town receives high annual rainfall, primarily concentrated in the summer and autumn months. The surface material of the Lijiaping landslide is primarily composed of colluvial soil and rubble, characterized by a loose structure. The high permeability of this material suggests that under high-intensity rainfall, it tends to form saturated zones in areas with effective water collection, which can promote displacement within the landslide mass.

Landslides of the Lijiaping type often exhibit geological concealment, primarily because the characteristics of fault activity are not readily apparent, and underground water flow changes are complex50,51. The Lijiaping landslide took place in the Qinba Mountain area, which has experienced multiple orogenic periods since the Indosinian era, resulting in the extensive development of various fault types52,53. Fault activity, which often occurs underground, may not have a direct and visible impact on the surface. In such scenarios, topographical changes due to fault activity may not be immediately visible on the surface, instead accumulating underground and posing potential landslide risks. Furthermore, fault activity indirectly influences groundwater flow direction and distribution by altering rock strata orientation54. Observing this influence directly from the surface is often challenging, particularly in mountainous areas with complex terrain. Underground water flow changes can occur without evident surface indicators, leading to soil saturation and variations in water pressure, eventually reaching a critical point that triggers a landslide55,56. Consequently, early stages of fault-induced landslides may not be detectable until they develop to the point of causing a noticeable landslide. Given the drastic changes in precipitation due to climate change and the increasing intensity and persistence of regional rainfall57, continuous monitoring and detailed geological surveys are crucial in geologically sensitive areas like the Qinba Mountains. This approach is essential to identify and mitigate potential geological disaster risks before the occurrence of landslides. Considering the similar geological background, faults have a comparable transformative effect on the regional rock masses. Therefore, other landslides in the Qinling-Daba Mountains, caused by terrain and landscape changes due to faults, may have similar formation mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study investigates the evolutionary characteristics and failure mode of the Lijiaping landslide under fault control. These analyses contribute to a better understanding of the relationship between fault activity and changes in slope stability. The main conclusions are as follows:

The Hanwang Fault Zone significantly alters the local terrain, forming wedge-shaped topography that enhances the water convergence capacity of the landslide area. The significantly increased Topographic Wetness Index (TWI) in the landslide area highlights the crucial role of the fault in altering hydrological pathways and concentrating water flow, thereby increasing the susceptibility to regional landslides.

Fault activity leads to extensive fragmentation and weakening of the mechanical properties of rock masses in the landslide area. Laboratory tests show that rocks affected by the fault have significantly reduced uniaxial compressive strength and softening coefficient compared to rocks in non-faulted areas. These deteriorated rocks, after weathering and accumulation, form thick residual colluvial layers, exacerbating slope instability during heavy rainfall.

This study delves into the formation and failure processes of colluvial landslides controlled by faults. In areas with complex geological and hydrological interactions, identifying landslide risk areas based on fault activity and hydrological conditions can inform land use planning and disaster management, thereby reducing the impact of geological hazards on local communities. Considering that future extreme rainfall events may trigger similar landslides, continuous monitoring and early warning systems are necessary. Additionally, constructing drainage facilities in these areas may have a significant effect on mitigating such landslide disasters.
