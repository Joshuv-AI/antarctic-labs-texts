Effects of Music Therapy on Perioperative Anxiety and Postoperative Sleep Quality in Patients Undergoing Surgery for Lumbar Disc Herniation
Authors: Hong Ding, Bin Du, YuJia Wei, ChaoJuan Yuan, Yu Fang, YuTing Xiao, YaPing Gao, Na Liu, WenJuan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose:This study aimed to investigate music therapy effects on perioperative anxiety and postoperative sleep quality in patients undergoing minimally invasive surgery for lumbar disc herniation (LDH).Methods:This retrospective study included patients who underwent percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy or laser disc decompression between January 2020 and January 2025. The patients were divided into music therapy group (n = 108) and routine care group (n = 92) according to the clinical management they received during hospitalisation. The music therapy group received music therapy and standard care, and the routine care group received standard care. The primary outcomes were state–trait anxiety inventory (STAI) and Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI). The secondary outcomes consisted of physiological parameters, stress biomarkers, pain levels, patient satisfaction, complication rate and recovery parameters.Results:At the 30-minute pre-operation timepoint, the music therapy group showed remarkably smaller increases in anxiety scores compared with the routine care group. At the 3rd day of hospitalisation, the music therapy group demonstrated significantly greater reductions in STAI and PSQI scores compared with the routine care group (both P < 0.05). The visual analogue scale scores for pain level were greatly reduced in the music therapy group (P < 0.05), whereas the patient satisfaction questionnaire-18 scores were higher (P < 0.05). Although the frequencies of individual complications are similar (all P >0.05), there are differences in the overall incidence of complications (P = 0.009).Conclusion:Music therapy represents an effective adjunctive caring method associated with improvements in perioperative anxiety, postoperative sleep quality and recovery outcomes in patients undergoing minimally invasive surgery for LDH. The findings support the integration of music therapy into standard perioperative care protocols to optimise patient experience and clinical outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Lumbar disc herniation (LDH) is one of the most common spinal disorders, affecting approximately 1–3% of the general population.[1] This condition typically manifests with localised back pain, radicular symptoms, sensory disturbances and, in severe cases, motor deficits impairing patients’ quality of life and functional capacity.[2] Surgical intervention becomes necessary when persistent or progressive neurological symptoms occur despite conservative treatments.[3] In recent decades, minimally invasive surgical techniques, including percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy (PELD) and percutaneous laser disc decompression, have been applied for LDH management.[4] These procedures offer fewer surgical injuries and shorter recovery times.[4] Despite these advancements, the perioperative experience remains challenging for many patients, with significant psychological stress, anxiety and sleep disturbances commonly reported throughout the perioperative period.[56] Standard perioperative care protocols typically focus primarily on the physical aspects of recovery and often inadequately address the psychological dimensions of the surgical experience.[6]

Music therapy represents a promising nonpharmacological intervention that has demonstrated efficacy in various clinical contexts. As a complementary therapy, music modulates autonomic nervous system activity, reduces stress hormone secretion and promotes relaxation.[7] Previous studies documented the beneficial effects of music therapy in reducing preoperative anxiety and improving perioperative outcomes across diverse surgical populations.[89] However, the literature gaps remain regarding the application of standardised music therapy protocols specifically for patients undergoing minimally invasive interventions for LDH. Evidence addressing anxiety reduction and sleep quality improvement within this population is limited. While this technique is generally well tolerated, potential contraindications to music therapy include severe hearing impairment, misophonia and incompatibility with certain hearing aids. These factors were considered in our exclusion criteria.

This study aimed to investigate the effects of music therapy on perioperative anxiety and postoperative sleep quality in patients undergoing minimally invasive surgery for LDH. By evaluating psychological parameters and physiological markers, this research sought to provide a comprehensive assessment of music therapy’s potential as an adjunctive caring method to enhance the perioperative experience and optimise the recovery outcomes in this patient population.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design

This retrospective clinical study examined the effects of music therapy on perioperative anxiety and postoperative sleep quality in patients who underwent minimally invasive surgery for LDH at The First People’s Hospital of Baiyin City from January 2020 to January 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The First People’s Hospital of Baiyin City (approval number: YL-KY-2025-053), and all the patients signed informed consent forms. The patients were assigned to either the music therapy group or routine care group according to the clinical management they received during their hospitalisation. The music therapy group (n = 108) received music therapy in addition to routine care, and the routine care group (n = 92) received only standard postoperative care according to hospital protocols.

Patient Selection

A total of 235 patients were assessed for eligibility between January 2020 and January 2025. Among them, 22 were excluded because of incomplete data, 10 because of American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status > III and 3 because of preexisting sleeping disorders. Ultimately, 200 cases were enrolled in the final analysis.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age between 18 and 75 years; (2) confirmed diagnosis of LDH based on clinical symptoms and imaging findings; (3) scheduled for first-time PELD (previous cervical or thoracic procedures were permitted); (4) able to understand and comply with the study procedures; (5) complete medical records and (6) hospitalisation for at least 3 days.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) emergency surgeries; (2) severe hearing impairment; (3) preexisting psychiatric disorders or cognitive impairment; (4) use of antipsychotic medications, anxiolytics or sleep medications within 2 weeks before surgery; (5) previous spine surgeries; (6) ASA physical status >III; (7) diagnosed sleep disorders prior to hospitalisation; (8) alcohol or substance abuse; and (9) intraoperative requirements for earplugs or headphones that would interfere with music therapy delivery.

Care Methods

The routine care group (n = 92) received the standardised perioperative care protocol established by our hospital. The routine care group received no music therapy, background music or other nonpharmacological sound-based therapies throughout their hospitalisation. Prior to the surgery, the patients received standard education regarding their condition, surgical procedure, expected outcomes and perioperative care routines from the attending physician and nursing staff during clinic visits and upon hospital admission. This education included verbal explanations, printed materials and demonstration of postoperative activities. Additionally, the patients underwent routine preoperative examinations including blood tests, electrocardiogram, chest X-ray and any additional tests indicated by their medical condition. On the day before the surgery, the patients received preoperative skin preparation instructions and fasting guidelines. On the day of the surgery, the patients were transported to the operating room where they received standard monitoring (electrocardiography, non-invasive blood pressure and pulse oximetry) and intravenous access establishment. After the operation, the patients were monitored in the post-anaesthesia care unit until stable and then transferred to the ward. Pain management followed a multimodal approach using nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and opioid analgesics as needed. Early mobilisation was encouraged within 24 hours post-surgery as permitted by patient condition. Standard wound care, vital sign monitoring and assessment for potential complications were performed in accordance with the departmental protocols. Discharge planning began upon admission and included patient education on home care, activity restrictions, medication management, wound care and follow-up appointments.

The music therapy group (n = 108) received the same standard perioperative care protocol as the routine care group, with the addition of music therapy. For this approach, a hospital-curated therapeutic music library comprising 45 instrumental tracks (mean duration 5.34 ± 1.42 minutes) was utilised. The library featured diverse genres, including classical compositions (Bach, Mozart and Debussy); traditional Chinese instrumental music; ambient nature sounds with instrumental accompaniment and modern relaxation melodies. The selection criteria emphasised consistent tempos (60–80 beats per minute), minimal rhythmic variation, predictable harmonies and absence of lyrics to maximise relaxation potential. Upon admission, the patients completed a music preference questionnaire and selected preferred genres from the library via a tablet-based interface, with individualised playlists created on the basis of these preferences. Music was delivered through noise-cancelling headphones (Bose QuietComfort 45, USA) calibrated to maintain consistent volume levels between 40 and 45 dB, and individual volume adjustment was permitted within this range for comfort. Sound levels were calibrated using a Type 2 sound level metre (BSWA 308, Beijing Sound Level Meter Factory, China). Calibration was performed weekly and documented in equipment logs. The metre itself underwent annual factory calibration with certification. Presession spot checks ensured consistent delivery within the 40–45 dB range (±2 dB tolerance). Nursing staff delivering the music therapy completed a standardised 12-hour training program comprising the following: theoretical foundations of music therapy (4 hours), practical application techniques (4 hours), equipment operation and calibration (2 hours) and supervised practice sessions (2 hours). Interrater reliability was assessed through standardised delivery checklists and achieved a Cohen’s kappa of 0.85, indicating substantial agreement. The caring method was administered in the following three distinct perioperative phases:

(1) Preoperative phase − Starting from hospital admission until surgery, music sessions were conducted twice daily (09:00–09:30 and 20:00–20:30) in the patient’s private room.

(2) Intraoperative phase − Continuous music playback from patient entry into the operating room until transfer to recovery, excluding critical communication periods (anaesthesia induction and emergence, surgical timeout procedures, critical surgical steps requiring enhanced communication between surgical team members and any emergency situations requiring immediate team coordination).

(3) Postoperative phase − Twice-daily sessions (10:00–10:30 and 21:00–21:30) from the day of surgery until discharge. Each 30-minute continuous music playback session was conducted by the trained nursing staff who ensured environmental optimisation (background noise < 55 dB, dimmed lighting during evening sessions) and documented adherence through a standardised checklist confirming device placement, music delivery and session completion.

Observational Indicators

Baseline Data

Baseline demographic and clinical data were collected upon hospital admission through a combination of electronic medical record (EMR) system review and patient self-report. The following information was extracted from the EMR system: age; gender; body mass index; comorbidities; medication use and clinical characteristics specific to LDH (disease duration, herniation level, herniation type, preoperative neurological symptoms and previous treatments). Additionally, laboratory values, magnetic resonance imaging and computed tomography scan results documenting the location and degree of disc herniation and surgical planning details were obtained. All baseline data were collected by trained research nurses using standardised data collection forms and entered into a secure electronic database.

Primary Outcomes

The patients completed assessments in private settings as instructed by trained research nurses who were blinded to the participants’ group allocation, and the data were electronically entered to minimise transcription errors.

(1) The state–trait anxiety inventory (STAI) was used to assess anxiety levels.[1011] STAI is a 40-item self-report instrument that measures state anxiety (temporary condition) and trait anxiety (general tendency). The state anxiety scale (STAI-S) consists of 20 items that evaluate current feelings of tension, nervousness and worry on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 4 (very much so), with total scores ranging from 20 to 80. The trait anxiety scale (STAI-T) also contains 20 items measuring relatively stable aspects of anxiety proneness, scored on the same 4-point scale. High scores on both scales indicate great anxiety levels. STAI demonstrated Cronbach’s α of 0.91 for STAI-S and 0.86 for STAI-T. STAI was assessed at three time points as follows: upon admission (baseline), 30-minute before operation and 3rd day of hospitalisation.

(2) The Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI) was employed to evaluate sleep quality at baseline and on the 3rd day of hospitalisation.[1213] PSQI has seven domains as follows: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication and daytime dysfunction. Each domain is scored on a 3-point Likert scale from 0 (no difficulty) to 3 (severe difficulty), yielding a global score ranging from 0 to 21. High scores indicate poor sleep quality, with total scores exceeding 7 indicating clinically significant sleep problems. The Chinese version of PSQI has an overall Cronbach’s α of 0.84.

Secondary Outcomes

(1) Physiological Parameters: Heart rate (HR) was measured using standard electrocardiogram monitoring or pulse oximetry. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were assessed using calibrated automatic oscillometric devices (Omron HEM-7320, Japan). Blood pressure monitoring was performed at 4-hour intervals throughout hospitalisation, and three consecutive measurements were taken at 2-minute intervals after the patient had been resting in a seated or supine position for at least 5 minutes. The average of these three measurements was recorded as the final blood pressure value for that time point. Vital signs were recorded at baseline (admission) and on the 3rd day of hospitalisation.

(2) Stress-related biomarkers: Salivary cortisol was measured as a biomarker of stress response. Saliva samples were collected using standardised Salivette® collection devices (Sarstedt AG & Co., Germany) at two time points: baseline (admission day at 8:00 AM) and 3rd day of hospitalisation (8:00 AM). The patients were instructed to refrain from eating, drinking or brushing teeth for at least 30 minutes before sample collection. The samples were immediately refrigerated, centrifuged within 24 hours and stored at −80 °C until batch analysis. Cortisol levels were determined using ELISA kits (IBL International GmbH, Germany) with a detection range of 0.02–3.6 µg/dL. All assays were performed in duplicate by laboratory technicians blinded to group allocation.

(3) Pain assessment: Pain intensity was evaluated in a resting position after at least 10 minutes of rest using the visual analogue scale, a unidimensional measure consisting of a 10 cm horizontal line representing pain intensity from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst possible pain).[1415] The patients marked the point on the line corresponding to their current pain level. Measurements were taken at baseline and on the 3rd day of hospitalisation.

(4) Patient satisfaction: Patient satisfaction was evaluated using a modified version of the patient satisfaction questionnaire (PSQ-18),[1617] a validated 18-item instrument measuring satisfaction across the following six domains: technical quality (four items), interpersonal manner (two items), communication (two items), time spent with healthcare providers (two items), accessibility (four items) and environment comfort (four items). Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with high scores indicating great satisfaction. The total score is within the range of 18–90 points. The Chinese version of PSQ-18 used in this study demonstrated good internal consistency reliability with Cronbach’s α = 0.87. The patients were asked to finish the questionnaire on the 3rd day of hospitalisation.

(5) Recovery parameters: Time to first ambulation was recorded as the duration (in hours) from the end of surgery until the patient successfully completed the first postoperative ambulation with or without assistance, as documented by physical therapists or nursing staff. Postoperative complications, including wound complications (infection, dehiscence and hematoma); neurological complications (new-onset motor or sensory deficits); urinary retention requiring catheterisation; postoperative nausea and vomiting (frequency and severity); respiratory complications and any other adverse events, were systematically monitored and recorded. All complications were graded according to severity and relationship to the surgical procedure, with serious adverse events reported to the institutional review board following standard protocols.

(6) Overall complication rate was defined as the proportion of patients experiencing at least one postoperative complication during the hospitalisation period, calculated as: (number of patients with ≥1 complication / total patients in group) × 100. This binary outcome (presence/absence of any complication) was distinct from the individual complication incidence rates.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed by utilising IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and tables were generated using Microsoft Excel 2021 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed via Shapiro–Wilk tests. Continuous data following normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using independent samples t-tests. For repeated measures continuous variables at three or more time points within a group, repeated measures ANOVA was performed to examine time effects, group effects and time × group interaction effects. For continuous variables with only two time points, paired t-tests were used for within-group comparisons. Categorical variables were described using frequency counts and percentages, with analyses conducted using χ2 tests. A two-sided testing approach was used, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

DISCUSSION

Patients with LDH encompass a wide age spectrum and exhibit varying degrees of disease severity.[3] Although surgical interventions such as PELD can effectively alleviate neurological compression symptoms, perioperative stress responses and psychological disturbances remain critical factors influencing patient prognosis.[18] Conventional nursing protocols typically focus on vital sign monitoring and wound management, with limited caring methods targeting patients’ psychological states. This study attempted to implement music therapy as a perioperative caring method for this patient population to examine the effects of music therapy on perioperative anxiety and sleep quality in patients with LDH.

Perioperative anxiety is prevalent, with at least 30% of patients experiencing perioperative anxiety.[19] Insufficient social support, misconceptions about surgery and fear can all potentially increase the risk of perioperative anxiety.[19] Perioperative anxiety imposes psychological burden on patients and adversely affects surgical outcomes and recovery.[20] Patients with high anxiety levels required significantly increased propofol doses during surgery, and their postoperative analgesic requirements were also markedly elevated, suggesting that perioperative anxiety may enhance patients’ pain sensitivity and reduce pain tolerance.[21] Goyal et al.[22] found that patients’ anxiety levels peaked on the day of surgery, followed by the day before surgery, and began to decline postoperatively. The results of the present study demonstrated that both groups of patients exhibited high anxiety levels on admission day, with further increases on the day of surgery. However, the music therapy group showed lower anxiety scores than the routine care group, and this difference between the groups persisted postoperatively. These findings suggested that perioperative music therapy can effectively improve patients’ anxiety levels. This influence may be attributed to music’s ability to increase brain levels of endogenous opioids and dopamine, neurotransmitters associated with pleasure and reward mechanisms, thereby reducing anxiety.[23] Additionally, anxiety and surgical stress activate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis signalling pathway, upregulating the secretion of stress hormones such as cortisol and further exacerbating negative emotions.[24] The present results showed that the cortisol levels in the music therapy group were significantly lower than those in the routine care group on the 3rd day of hospitalisation. Music therapy can activate the parasympathetic nervous system, leading to decreased HR and blood pressure, and subsequently reduce the secretion of stress hormones such as cortisol.[25] At the 3rd day of hospitalisation, although the blood pressure levels in both groups remained within normal ranges due to surgical treatment requirements for blood pressure control, the music therapy group exhibited lower HRs and blood pressure levels. These differences further confirmed the above regulatory mechanism. Analysis of pain levels at the 3rd day of hospitalisation also demonstrated that music therapy can effectively alleviate patients’ postoperative pain. This phenomenon may be related to its ability to inhibit and modulate pain sensation by activating the descending pain modulatory system which acts on pain afferent nerve fibres, thereby reducing the transmission of neural impulses and pain perception.[26] Music therapy can also mask unpleasant external stimuli such as noise, divert patients’ attention and reduce focus on pain.[27]

Sleep quality plays a crucial role in postoperative recovery, and good sleep facilitates physical recovery and psychological stability.[28] Poor postoperative sleep quality is associated with prolonged hospitalisation, and postoperative sleep disorders can lead to increased pain sensitivity and even elevate the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events.[29] This study demonstrated that music therapy improved patients’ sleep quality across multiple dimensions. In addition to the aforementioned effects of music on emotion and parasympathetic nervous system regulation, slow and gentle music can stimulate the brain to produce alpha waves, which are associated with relaxation and calm states and help induce sleep.[30] Listening to music before bedtime can significantly increase the proportion of rapid eye movement sleep and deep sleep (N3 stage), thereby improving sleep architecture.[31] In this study, the individual complication rates showed no significant differences between groups, but the proportion of patients experiencing at least one postoperative complication was significantly lower in the music therapy group. This finding suggests that music therapy may contribute to overall good perioperative outcomes, possibly through multiple mechanisms including stress reduction, improved immune function and enhanced pain management.

The cost-effectiveness of music therapy warrants consideration for clinical implementation. With minimal equipment requirements (headphones and music library) and no need for specialised personnel beyond trained nursing staff, music therapy represents a low-cost caring method with potentially high clinical utility. The reduction in overall complications and earlier mobilisation observed in our study may translate to decreased healthcare costs through short hospital stays and reduced resource utilisation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. The retrospective design may have introduced selection bias and limited the ability to control for all confounding variables that could influence patient outcomes. The relatively short follow-up period of 3 days may not fully capture the long-term effects of music therapy on recovery and quality of life indicators. Additionally, this study was conducted at a single institution, which may limit the generalisability of findings to other healthcare settings with different patient populations or care protocols. The lack of assessment of patients’ musical preferences and cultural backgrounds could have influenced individual responses to the caring method. Potential acoustic contamination between groups represents a methodological limitation, though the use of institutional design standards and headphones likely minimised this effect. Future research should conduct large-scale, multicentre randomised controlled trials to validate these findings across diverse populations and healthcare systems. Investigating the optimal duration, frequency and timing of music therapy sessions would help establish evidence-based protocols for clinical implementation. Furthermore, exploring the neurobiological mechanisms underlying music therapy’s effects on pain perception, sleep regulation and stress response could provide deep insights into its therapeutic potential. Long-term follow-up studies examining the sustained effects of perioperative music therapy on functional outcomes, quality of life and healthcare utilisation would strengthen the evidence base for its widespread adoption in minimally invasive spine surgery protocols. Given the cost-effectiveness, non-invasive nature and ease of implementation of music therapy, healthcare institutions should consider integrating standardised music therapy protocols into routine perioperative care pathways for minimally invasive spine surgery.[32] Additionally, training specialised nursing staff in music therapy implementation techniques and developing individualised music treatment plans based on patients’ musical preferences would help maximise therapeutic outcomes and ensure consistent delivery of this caring method across different clinical settings.

CONCLUSION

This study suggested that music therapy may serve as a beneficial adjunctive caring method for patients undergoing minimally invasive surgery for LDH. The findings indicated potential improvements in perioperative anxiety levels, postoperative sleep quality, physiological parameters and pain management outcomes among patients receiving adjuvant music therapy compared with those receiving routine care alone. Additionally, music therapy appears to be associated with enhanced patient satisfaction and accelerated recovery markers. These observations support the consideration of integrating structured music therapy protocols into standard perioperative care pathways.
