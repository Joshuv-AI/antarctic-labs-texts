Remission with residuals, but no cure
Authors: Othman Al-Sawaf
Published: 2026

In this issue of Blood Advances, Bazinet et al1 report long-term rates of measurable residual disease (MRD) in patients treated >15 years ago with fludarabine, cyclophosphamide, and rituximab (FCR) for previously untreated chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL).

For most patients with CLL, contemporary treatment aims to achieve optimal disease control by relieving CLL-related symptoms, restoring bone marrow function with normalization of peripheral blood counts and reducing MRD levels. A subgroup of patients with mutated immunoglobulin heavy-chain variable region (IGHV) genes has been observed to experience very long disease control following FCR, with progression-free survival (PFS) extending beyond 15 years in some reports.2, 3, 4 These observations have raised the question of whether a subset of patients with CLL may achieve cure with chemoimmunotherapy. However, a shared limitation of most long-term FCR studies is the potential bias introduced by loss to follow-up. Only a small number of patients remain under observation to define the tail of long-term remission, reflected by heavy censoring of Kaplan-Meier curves at later time points. Moreover, biological samples from these long-term responders are rarely available for analysis.

Across different treatment modalities including chemotherapy and fixed-duration targeted therapies, depth of response has consistently emerged as a key predictor of outcome. Patients who achieve undetectable measurable residual disease show substantially longer PFS than those with detectable MRD. Yet the durability of undetectable MRD and the long-term dynamics of residual disease in patients with sustained remission remain largely unknown.

Bazinet et al now address this question by examining the MRD status of long-term survivors treated with FCR. In their single-center cohort, the investigators identified 808 patients who had received FCR-based therapy. Peripheral blood samples suitable for next-generation sequencing–based MRD testing could ultimately be obtained from 17 patients who remained in clinical remission and had identifiable baseline immunoglobulin sequences, enabling sensitive MRD tracking to the level of 1 leukemic cell in 1 million cells. The median interval between FCR therapy and MRD assessment was 15.7 years.

At the end of treatment, most patients in this cohort had achieved deep remission, with 76% classified as having undetectable MRD using conventional flow cytometry assays. However, this analysis revealed that more than a decade later most patients had detectable residual disease when assessed with more sensitive sequencing methods. Overall, 76% had detectable MRD at the level of 1 in 1 million cells and 38% had MRD levels detectable at the less sensitive threshold of 1 in 10 000 cells. Notably, these molecular relapses occurred in the absence of clinical disease progression.

These findings are important because they challenge the long-held question of whether FCR may truly cure a subset of patients with CLL, particularly those with mutated IGHV (see figure). By a strict definition, the detection of residual leukemic cells argues against complete eradication of disease. In clinical medicine, cure is typically defined by the absence of detectable pathogen or malignant cells rather than by clinical improvement alone. One possible explanation is that residual CLL cells enter a state of limited proliferative capacity. Such cells may remain dormant for extended periods or may be kept under immune control, thereby preventing clinically relevant expansion. The stability of MRD levels observed in patients with serial sampling in this study supports this possibility. Importantly, almost half of the patients who were analyzed had unmutated IGHV, a genetic feature usually associated with more aggressive disease yet remained progression free for more than a decade despite detectable residual clones.

The main limitation of this study is the highly selected nature of the analyzed cohort. Only a small fraction of the original 808 FCR-treated patients could be included due to death, disease progression, or the practical challenges of contacting long-term survivors and obtaining suitable biological material. As a result, the cohort is enriched for younger patients with favorable outcomes, and statistical adjustment for other prognostic variables is not possible. The findings should therefore be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

Nevertheless, the extraordinary duration of follow-up provides a rare window into the long-term biology of CLL after therapy. These observations should motivate deeper investigation of the biological mechanisms that allow residual CLL cells to persist without causing clinical relapse. Potential determinants may include intrinsic cellular programs that limit proliferation, immune-mediated control of leukemic cells and microenvironmental signals that restrain growth. A better understanding of these mechanisms may reveal strategies to reinforce such dormant states or to induce them therapeutically.

Ultimately, the goal may not necessarily be eradication of every leukemic cell but rather the induction of a stable equilibrium in which residual disease remains permanently controlled. Insights gained from long-term survivors treated with FCR could therefore inform strategies aimed at achieving a functional cure of CLL in a broader group of patients.
