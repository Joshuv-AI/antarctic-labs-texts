Adult intussusception as the initial presentation of celiac disease: a rare case report and literature review
Authors: Mahdi Aljamal, Zainah Amjad Issa, Ali Shakhshir, Mohammed Maree
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCeliac disease is an autoimmune disease rarely presenting as intestinal intussusception in less than 1% of all cases.Case presentationWe report a case of a 34-year-old female patient who presented with recurrent abdominal pain and vomiting for 7 months. Imaging revealed intestinal intussusception of 2 small bowel segments. The patient underwent upper endoscopy, colonoscopy, and a diagnostic exploratory laparotomy with excellent postoperative recovery. Pathological investigation of the intestinal biopsy revealed duodenal villous atrophy and intraepithelial lymphocytosis, highly suspecting the diagnosis of celiac disease, which was confirmed with the presence of anti-transglutaminase antibodies.ConclusionThis case emphasized a prime example of delayed celiac disease diagnosis attributed to the atypical and sole presentation of intestinal intussusception, an exceptionally rare co-occurrence.

Introduction and background

Celiac disease, an autoimmune small intestinal disorder causing mucosal inflammation, crypt hyperplasia, and villous atrophy in response to gluten exposure [1]; and intestinal intussusception, characterized by an invagination of an intestinal segment into itself [2], are two entities that rarely co-occur. The typical clinical signs of celiac disease in an adult are malabsorptive signs like diarrhea with steatorrhea, weight loss, and abdominal pain [3] or anemia, osteoporosis, and arthralgia [4]. However, early diagnosis of celiac disease can be quite tricky due to commonly presenting vaguely with iron deficiency anemia, aphthous ulcers, or amenorrhea. Complications of celiac disease include adenocarcinomas, lymphomas, osteopenia, and intestinal intussusception [5]. Nevertheless, intussusception of the small intestines has been the sole clinical manifestation in 1% of all celiac disease patients [1, 2], whose prevalence is between 0.5% and 1% [1]. This case report presents an adult patient with celiac disease who was diagnosed solely based on the manifestation of intestinal intussusception, without exhibiting any other typical signs or symptoms of the condition.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old female patient presented with recurrent abdominal pain and vomiting for a duration of 7 months. The pain was colicky in nature, localized in the epigastric and right upper quadrant (RUQ) regions, and associated with vomiting of undigested food, watery diarrhea, and an unintentional weight loss of 10 kg over a period of 6 months. The pain was usually relieved after the episodes of vomiting. The patient denied having a history of hematemesis or melena. The patient previously underwent an abdominal X-ray (without contrast) that was normal, and her symptoms were attributed to psychological reasons.

On physical examination, the abdomen was mildly distended with RUQ tenderness, without guarding, palpable masses, or umbilical herniation. The patient’s vital signs were stable and laboratory investigations of complete blood count and kidney function tests were within normal limits. Radiological assessment

A contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis revealed an 11 cm jejuno-jejunal small bowel intussusception with subacute intestinal obstruction and mild proximal bowel dilatation and an additional segment of small bowel to small bowel intussusception in the lower abdomen, as shown in Fig. 1. Diagnostic challenges

A major diagnostic challenge in this case was the nonspecific and recurrent nature of the patient’s gastrointestinal symptoms, along with an initially unrevealing abdominal X-ray and prior attribution of symptoms to psychological causes, which contributed to delayed recognition of the underlying organic pathology.

Surgical management

Upon admission for acute symptoms, including vomiting and abdominal pain, the patient experienced a significant increase in abdominal pain, was unable to tolerate an oral diet, and developed signs of a surgical abdomen, leading to surgery being performed before the diagnosis of celiac disease due to concerns about a pathological lead point and subacute obstruction. Given the radiological suspicion of subacute bowel obstruction; the possibility of an underlying pathological lead point, including malignancy; and the need for an intra-abdominal assessment, an open exploratory laparotomy was preferred over a laparoscopic approach through an upper midline incision. Intraoperatively, dilated jejunal loops were identified with evidence of previously existing jejuno-jejunal intussusceptions, which had spontaneously reduced at the time of exploration. The involved segments were located in the proximal and mid-jejunum. No identifiable pathological lead point, mass lesion, or intraluminal abnormality was detected. The bowel appeared viable, with no signs of ischemia, necrosis, or perforation. In addition, multiple enlarged mesenteric lymph nodes were observed, and a representative lymph node measuring approximately 2 cm was excised for histopathological evaluation. No bowel resection or anastomosis was required. The remainder of the abdominal exploration was unremarkable.

Endoscopic evaluation

Postoperatively and after recovery from the surgery, the patient underwent upper endoscopy and colonoscopy for further evaluation. Upper endoscopy revealed a normal esophagus, but diffuse nodular gastritis in the gastric body and antrum and duodenitis with villous atrophy. Colonoscopy revealed a normal colon and terminal ileum. Biopsies of the gastric antrum and duodenum were positive for Helicobacter pylori and demonstrated total villous atrophy with markedly increased intraepithelial lymphocytes, consistent with the Marsh III classification of celiac disease.

Further diagnostic work-up and final diagnosis

Following the identification of duodenal villous atrophy consistent with the Marsh III classification, a targeted serological work-up for celiac disease was performed. during which serum anti-tissue transglutaminase (anti-tTG) IgA antibodies were found to be markedly elevated (> 100 AU/ml). strongly supporting the diagnosis of celiac disease. Meanwhile, to exclude IgA deficiency, total serum IgA levels were requested and were within normal limits.

By combining the characteristic histopathological findings on duodenal biopsy, positive celiac-specific serology, and the absence of an identifiable pathological leading point for the intussusceptions established, the final diagnosis of celiac disease was established, typically presenting with recurrent GI symptoms and small bowel intussusception.

Outcome and follow-up

Postoperative recovery was unremarkable; she resumed oral intake gradually and was discharged home in stable condition. Following confirmation of the diagnosis, she was referred to a gastroenterologist clinic and received detailed dietary counseling with strict initiation of a gluten-free diet.

During the 12 months after the diagnosis, during multiple follow-up visits, the patient described significant improvement, including complete resolution of abdominal pain, cessation of vomiting, and progressive weight gain. No recurrent episodes of bowel obstruction or intussusception were reported, and she remained clinically stable on dietary management alone, with no need for further surgical interventions.

The impact of atypically presented celiac disease on its diagnosis

The process of diagnosing celiac disease in patients presenting with persistent abdominal pain and unexplained weight loss [10] starts with the patient’s clinical presentation, which is then supported by laboratory investigations, imaging, and endoscopy. Proper and timely establishment of a celiac diagnosis reduces the risk of serious complications following undiagnosed celiac disease, including bowel wall edema, intestinal wall dysmotility, ulcers, strictures [1], osteopenia, and malignancies like small intestinal adenocarcinoma and lymphoma [5]. However, rare cases may present atypically, an intestinal intussusception being a prime example, delaying the diagnosis of celiac disease until the incidence of the intussusception and increasing the risk of complications. The delay in diagnosing unsuspected celiac disease partially owes to its vague clinical presentation and laboratory findings like leukocytosis with neutrophilic predominance [1] and hypoalbuminemia [3]. Further delay is attributed to intussusceptions not prompting a celiac workup because of the rarity of them being the initial and sole presentation of undiagnosed celiac disease [1, 9], For that, the suspicion of a celiac diagnosis in such patients is only reached after endoscopy and biopsy examination that prompts a celiac laboratory workup for confirmation.

Diagnosis process

Imaging modalities like computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are appropriate next steps to coming closer to a definitive diagnosis after inconclusive history, physical exam, and laboratory findings. Abdominal and pelvic CT scans with contrast have revealed jejunojejunal intussusception [8, 9] with mesenteric lymph node involvement identified by their enlargement [9]. In other cases, CT scans have only shown thickened intestinal walls with mesenteric lymph node involvement as well [11]. This case utilized an abdominal contrast-enhanced CT scan that revealed intussusception in two small intestinal segments along with subacute intestinal obstruction and mild bowel wall dilatation. Quera et al. [8] also made use of MRI, which revealed jejunal intussusception with radiological abnormalities favoring the possibility of celiac disease, while Redondo et al. [2] utilized barium examinations, which also showed evidence of intestinal intussusception. Despite being less sensitive than CT, abdominal ultrasonography (US) is also able to show intussusceptions by identifying the pathognomonic target sign of intussusception [1]. With those findings on imaging, endoscopy is deemed necessary for further evaluation of the obvious intestinal abnormalities and intussusceptions and for collecting appropriate specimens for pathologic investigation. Pathology revealed villous atrophy, lymphocytic infiltration, and inflammatory changes in this case and in previously reported cases [6, 9], which are consistent with celiac disease, thus, prompting the need to circle back to laboratory investigations to confirm the disease by the presence of autoantibodies. Celiac disease can involve positive antibody presence like anti-transglutaminase, anti-deamidated gliadin IgA, and anti-endomysial antibodies, with this case having positive anti-transglutaminase antibodies of over 100 AU/mL. With a criteria of positive serology, celiac-consistent histology, human leukocyte antigen (HLA) typing in genetically predisposed individuals [5], and responsiveness to a gluten-free diet, celiac disease can be successfully confirmed [5, 8]. However, negative serology does not exclude a celiac diagnosis, as presented by an antibody-negative but HLA-DQ2-positive case in the report by Mitchell et al. [9]. An important differential diagnosis in this context is enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma (EATL), a known but rare complication of celiac disease [5, 8]. In the present case, lymphoma was considered and effectively excluded based on the absence of a mass lesion or pathological lead point on imaging, lack of intraoperative evidence of infiltrative or neoplastic lesions, and benign histopathological findings of the excised mesenteric lymph node. Furthermore, the patient demonstrated marked clinical improvement following initiation of a gluten-free diet, with no evidence of recurrence, further supporting a non-malignant etiology.

Management of intussusception and celiac disease

The management of small intestinal intussusception depends on the case. The majority of cases have been managed conservatively, considering the spontaneous resolution of intussusception non direct intervention [2, 4, 6–8, 11]. However, other cases required an open laparotomy for reducing the intussusception [3, 5, 9, 10]. One case reported reduction of the intussusception laparoscopically, portraying potential in noninvasive management techniques that lower the risks accompanied by open procedures [1]. In certain cases, a diagnostic exploratory laparotomy may be indicated to further investigate the etiology of the intussusceptions in unclear situations, as done in this case. After confirmation of celiac disease, the definitive treatment is following a gluten-free diet, as gluten consumption can increase morbidity and lead to recurrence of intussusception [1, 5, 10]). In this case, an open surgical approach was selected over laparoscopy due to concern for bowel obstruction, the possibility of malignancy, and the need for complete abdominal exploration. This reflects the diagnostic uncertainty commonly encountered in adult intussusception.

This case highlights an important clinical implication: adult small-bowel intussusception in the absence of an identifiable lead point should prompt consideration of underlying celiac disease, even in patients lacking classic malabsorptive symptoms. Early recognition of this association may facilitate timely diagnosis and prevent unnecessary delays or invasive interventions.

Strengths and limitations

This case has several strengths. It highlights a rare and clinically important presentation of adult intussusception with an underlying diagnosis of celiac disease, emphasizes the value of maintaining suspicion for organic pathology in patients with recurrent nonspecific gastrointestinal symptoms, and illustrates the importance of a stepwise diagnostic approach combining imaging, operative findings, and targeted postoperative evaluation. However, this report also has limitations. As a single case, the findings are not generalizable and cannot establish causality between celiac disease and intussusception. In addition, some aspects of the earlier external evaluations were based on retrospective history, and detailed long-term follow-up laboratory/endoscopic reassessment was not available in this report.

Conclusion

Adult small-bowel intussusception without an identifiable lead point should raise suspicion for underlying celiac disease, even in the absence of classic symptoms. This case emphasizes the importance of considering celiac disease in atypical presentations to enable early diagnosis and appropriate management.

Patient perspective

The patient reported that the recurrent symptoms had significantly affected her daily life and caused frustration because the diagnosis was not clear for a long time. She expressed relief after the diagnosis was established and surgery was performed, and she reported improvement in her symptoms after starting a strict gluten-free diet.
