Cortical functional hierarchy disruption following subcortical stroke
Authors: Ke Wu, Gang Liu, Xinyu Liang, Jinsheng Zeng, Gaolang Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubcortical structures are pivotal for cortico-subcortical communication, yet are highly vulnerable to stroke. Damage to these structures can produce widespread cortical dysfunction, but how subcortical stroke reshapes the macroscale hierarchical organization of cortical networks remains poorly understood. Clarifying this relationship is critical for understanding subcortical contributions to brain-wide communication and for identifying reliable neural markers of clinical outcome.MethodsWe analyzed resting-state fMRI data from two independent cohorts of patients with subcortical stroke (discovery, 55 patients, 49 controls; replication, 23 patients, 26 controls). Cortical functional organization was mapped using diffusion map embedding to derive connectivity gradients, and group differences were quantified at both global and regional levels. Gradient metrics were further compared with conventional functional connectivity strength and tested for predictive utility using multivariate ridge regression against neurological outcome measures.ResultsAcross cohorts, patients consistently exhibited compression of the principal unimodal-to-transmodal gradient, reflecting diminished hierarchical differentiation between sensory and association cortices. These alterations were stable over the first three months of recovery and spatially aligned with normative maps of dopaminergic, GABAergic, glutamatergic, serotonergic and cannabinoid systems. Background

Subcortical structures, such as the basal ganglia and thalamus, play a pivotal role in orchestrating distributed cortical networks through cortico-subcortical loops [1–4]. Network analyses highlight subcortical nucleus gates information flow between unimodal sensory regions and higher-order association networks [5–7]. Anatomically, however, subcortical territories are supplied by small perforating arteries with limited collateral circulation, rendering them especially vulnerable to ischemic injury [8, 9]. Given this vascular fragility and their central role in information gating, lesions in subcortical regions can precipitate widespread disruption of cortical networks and heterogeneous neuropsychological deficits, ranging from motor impairment to cognitive dysfunction [10–14]. Understanding how large-scale cortical networks reorganize after subcortical stroke, and how such reorganization relates to behavioural outcomes, is therefore critical both for elucidating subcortical contributions to brain-wide communication and for establishing reliable neural markers to inform clinical interventions.

Resting-state functional connectivity (FC) has been widely used to investigate cortical reorganization following subcortical stroke [15–18]. Analyses at the level of individual region pairs or within/between functional networks have both been employed, yet their findings remain inconsistent. Some studies report decreased interhemispheric FC with increased default mode network (DMN)–sensorimotor/attention network coupling [12, 19–22], whereas others describe enhanced interhemispheric FC and reduced DMN-sensorimotor network connectivity in patients with extensive structural disconnection [11, 23–25]. Such discrepancies likely reflect the heterogeneity of lesion sites across cohorts as well as methodological variability—including differences in parcellation schemes, network definitions, preprocessing pipelines, and data quality [26, 27]. More fundamentally, both pairwise- and network-based FC indices are discrete, context-dependent measures that may fail to capture overarching principles of cortical functional organization and its reconfiguration after injury.

Functional connectome gradient analysis provides a principled alternative for mapping cortical functional organization [28–31]. By embedding the entirety of interregional connectivity into continuous low-dimensional axes, gradient methods integrate information across distributed systems and yield a unified characterization of the cortex’s macroscale functional hierarchy with reduced dependence on arbitrary parcellation or network choices. The principal gradient delineates a robust unimodal-to-transmodal spectrum, extending from sensorimotor cortices with domain-specific connectivity to association regions that integrate long-range projections from distributed brain systems. Alterations along this axis, often manifesting as reduced separation between unimodal and transmodal extremes, have been observed in autism, schizophrenia, temporal lobe epilepsy and Parkinson’s disease [32–35]. Gradient-based approaches have also been applied in stroke, for example by mapping lesions onto normative gradient spaces or by computing gradients directly from patient data, but these studies have yielded limited or inconsistent results, likely due to high heterogeneity in lesion locations across cohorts [36, 37]. How subcortical stroke, in particular, impacts cortical hierarchy therefore remains poorly understood, and it is unclear whether cortical gradients provide a more consistent and mechanistically informative marker of functional reorganization than conventional resting-state FC approaches.

To address this gap, we applied functional gradient analysis to resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data from two independent cohorts of patients with subcortical infarction. We hypothesized that focal subcortical injury would lead to compression of the principal cortical unimodal-to-transmodal gradient, reflecting disrupted hierarchical interactions between low-level sensory-motor and higher-order association systems. It was further predicted that these cortical gradient alterations would relate to cognitive impairment and, given the central role of the subcortical nuclei in neurotransmitter systems, would spatially align with normative maps of dopaminergic, GABAergic, glutamatergic, serotonergic and cannabinoid distributions [38–42]. By testing these hypotheses across independent cohorts, we sought to determine whether cortical gradients provide a more robust and mechanistically informative marker of post-stroke reorganization than conventional rs-FC metrics. Accordingly, this work provides a novel characterization of cortical network reorganization in subcortical stroke and positions gradient analysis as a systems-level marker of subcortical injury with implications for mechanistic insight and clinical translation.

Methods

Participants

Discovery cohort

We recruited 55 patients (35 males; mean age = 53.0 ± 12.1 years) from the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. Patients underwent multimodal MRI and clinical assessments at three post-stroke timepoints: 2 weeks, 4 weeks, and 12 weeks (Table 1). Inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) unilateral first-onset supratentorial subcortical infarcts in the anterior circulation without cortical lesions, confirmed by T1-, T2-, and diffusion-weighted native imaging within 7 days of onset; (ii) no major intracranial arterial occlusion as assessed by MR angiography or computed tomography angiography; and (iii) age 18–75 years. Exclusion criteria were: (i) unstable vital signs; (ii) history of any comorbid neurological disorders (e.g. dementia, Parkinson’s disease, epilepsy, or traumatic brain injury); (iii) evidence of alcohol abuse or psychiatric disorder; (iv) aphasia, apraxia or systemic medical conditions; and (v) contraindications to MRI (e.g. pacemaker, metallic implants). For comparison, 49 right-handed healthy controls (24 males; mean age = 55.6 ± 13.5 years) with no history of neurological or psychiatric illness were recruited.

Replication cohort

For replication, we used data from the Washington University Stroke Cohort [10, 12, 43]. This cohort included 132 patients with first-ever symptomatic stroke (age 19–83 years), representative of the clinical stroke population at large and not preselected by behavioral profile or lesion location. To ensure a strictly subcortical sample, we excluded all patients with cortical lesions; consequently, only 23 patients (9 males; mean age = 51.6 ± 11.7 years) with unilateral supratentorial subcortical infarcts were retained for analysis. These patients were assessed at 2 weeks (n = 23) and 3 months (n = 13) post-stroke (Table 1). Inclusion and exclusion criteria were broadly consistent with those of the discovery cohort. Importantly, lesion size did not differ significantly between the discovery and replication cohorts (two-sample t-test: t = 1.64, p = 0.11; Fig. 1). The replication dataset also included 26 age-matched healthy controls (10 males; mean age = 53.7 ± 11.8 years) who underwent neuroimaging and comprehensive neuropsychological assessment.

Imaging acquisition

Discovery cohort

Imaging was performed on a 3 T Siemens Trio scanner equipped with a 12-channel head coil. Structural images were acquired using a T1-weighted sagittal magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MP-RAGE) sequence (repetition time [TR] = 2530 ms; echo time [TE] = 3.45 ms; inversion time = 1100 ms; flip angle = 7°; field of view [FOV] = 256 × 256 mm2; voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3; 192 slices). Resting-state functional images were acquired using echo-planar imaging (EPI) (TR = 2000 ms; TE = 30 ms; flip angle = 90°; FOV = 220 × 220 mm; voxel size = 3.44 × 3.44 × 4.6 mm3; 32 contiguous axial slices, 4 mm thickness; 240 volumes). Lesions were manually segmented on structural MR images using FSLeyes, with T1-weighted and T2-weighted scans simultaneously inspected to ensure accurate delineation.

Replication cohort

Neuroimaging data were acquired on a 3 T Siemens Trio scanner with a 12-channel head coil. Structural scans included sagittal T1-weighted MP-RAGE (TR = 1950 ms; TE = 2.26 ms; flip angle = 90°; voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3) and transverse T2-weighted turbo spin echo (TR = 2500 ms; TE = 435 ms; voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3). Resting-state functional scans were acquired with a gradient-echo EPI sequence (TR = 2000 ms; TE = 27 ms; 32 contiguous slices, 4 mm thickness; in-plane resolution 4 × 4 mm2). Each subject underwent 6–8 runs of resting-state fMRI, each comprising 128 volumes. Lesions were manually segmented using the Analyze biomedical imaging software, with T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images jointly reviewed to ensure accurate delineation.

Clinical assessments

Neurological examinations in both cohorts were conducted by a trained neurologist blinded to neuroimaging results, on the same day as MRI acquisition. Stroke severity and motor deficits were quantified using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) and the Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA). The NIHSS provides a standardized measure of global stroke severity across motor, sensory, language, and consciousness domains. The FMA evaluates motor recovery in the upper and lower extremities after stroke. In the discovery cohort, two patients with missing NIHSS scores at the 2-week timepoint were excluded from all subsequent analyses involving this measure.

fMRI processing

Discovery cohort

Structural and resting-state fMRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep [44]. For T1-weighted images, preprocessing included intensity non-uniformity correction, skull stripping, tissue segmentation, and cortical surface reconstruction. Resting-state fMRI preprocessing included head motion estimation, slice-timing correction, susceptibility distortion correction, and co-registration to the T1-weighted image. Functional time series were then projected onto the 32k_fs_LR surface.

The fMRIPrep outputs were further post-processed as follows: (i) linear detrending; (ii) nuisance regression including 24 motion parameters (6 rigid-body estimates, their first-order temporal derivatives, and their quadratic terms), mean white matter, cerebrospinal fluid, and global signals; (iii) bandpass temporal filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz); (iv) scrubbing of volumes with framewise displacement > 0.5 mm; and (v) spatial smoothing with a 4-mm full-width-at-half-maximum Gaussian kernel. To validate that our main results were not driven by the specific cortical template used, we additionally projected functional signals onto the onavg-ico32 surface [45].

Replication cohort

Structural and resting-state fMRI data were preprocessed using the Human Connectome Project (HCP) minimal preprocessing pipeline [46]. For T1-weighted images, preprocessing included gradient distortion correction, skull stripping, tissue segmentation, and cortical surface reconstruction. Resting-state fMRI preprocessing included intensity normalization, head motion estimation, slice-timing correction, and co-registration. Functional time series were then projected onto the 32k_fs_LR surface.

The same post-processing steps as in the discovery cohort were applied to the HCP outputs.

Functional connectivity

Preprocessed fMRI time series were downsampled from the 32k_fs_LR surface to a custom 10k_fs_LR template (10,242 vertices per hemisphere) [47]. After excluding the medial wall, the cortical surface comprised 18,708 vertices. For each participant, vertex-level FC matrices were constructed by computing Pearson correlations between all vertex pairs, followed by Fisher’s z-transformation.

Functional connectivity strength (FCS) was then derived from these matrices. Global FCS was defined as the row-wise mean of the 18,708 × 18,708 matrix. Between-network FCS was calculated as the mean connectivity between each vertex and all vertices outside its assigned network, whereas within-network FCS was the mean connectivity with all vertices within the same network. Cortical network assignments followed Yeo’s seven-network parcellation [48], comprising the visual network (VN), somatomotor network (SMN), dorsal attention network (DAN), ventral attention network (VAN), limbic network (LIM), frontoparietal network (FPN), and default mode network (DMN).

Connectivity gradients

Connectivity gradients were estimated from the FC matrices using diffusion map embedding, implemented with the BrainSpace MATLAB toolbox [49]. This method projects high-dimensional connectivity profiles into a low-dimensional manifold, positioning vertices with similar connectivity patterns closer together. For each participant, the first ten gradients were computed using validated parameters: a row-wise 90% threshold (retaining the top 10% of connections), α = 0.5 (balancing local versus global data structure; α = 0 maximizes local influence, α = 1 minimizes it), and t = 0 (diffusion time scale). These parameters were chosen for consistency with prior studies and to maximize robustness while preserving global relationships [28, 47]. To enable group-level comparisons, individual gradients were aligned to a group template—derived from the average FC matrix of all healthy controls—using Procrustes rotation.

Neurotransmitter density mapping

Group-averaged Positron Emission Tomography (PET) maps of neurotransmitter receptor and transporter densities were obtained using the neuromaps toolbox (https://github.com/netneurolab/neuromaps). Fourteen targets spanning seven neurotransmitter systems implicated in subcortical nucleus modulation were included: dopamine (D1 [50], D2 [51], DAT [52]), GABA (GABAA [52]), cannabinoid (CB1 [53]), metabotropic glutamate (mGluR5 [54]), opioid (MOR [55], KOR [56]), histamine (H3 [57]), serotonin (5-HT1a [58], 5-HT1b [57], 5-HT2a [58], 5-HT4 [58], 5-HTT [59]). All PET maps, originally derived from healthy volunteers, were projected onto the 10k_fs_LR cortical surface using linear interpolation. Spatial associations between neurotransmitter distributions and stroke-related gradient alterations were quantified with vertex-wise Spearman correlations.

Statistical analysis

Group differences between 2-week stroke patients and healthy controls were first assessed in the discovery cohort using vertex-wise two-sample t-tests on functional gradient scores. Secondary gradients were analyzed in the same manner. Resulting significance maps were corrected for multiple comparisons across 18,708 vertices using the false discovery rate (FDR; p < 0.05 corrected). System-level comparisons were additionally performed by averaging gradient scores within each of Yeo’s seven canonical networks.

To evaluate reproducibility, identical patient-control comparisons were performed in the replication cohort. The spatial correspondence of group-difference maps between cohorts was quantified using Spearman correlations with spatial permutation tests (spin-tests) with 10,000 iterations, preserving cortical geometry and hemispheric symmetry. For comparison with conventional measures, parallel patient-control analyses were conducted on whole-cortex, within-network, and between-network FC strength, and the cross-cohort correlations of these maps were assessed in the same manner.

We next examined the predictive utility of brain features for clinical severity at two weeks. Ridge regression models with leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) were trained separately on gradient and FC strength features [60]. Clinical outcomes included NIHSS (global severity) and FMA (motor impairment). Within each LOOCV fold, features significantly correlated with behavioral scores (p < 0.01) were retained, and the ridge penalty parameter (α) was tuned across 2–10 to 25 to minimize prediction error. Model performance was evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient and mean absolute error (MAE) between observed and predicted scores. A permutation test was applied to determine whether the prediction performance (the correlation r between predicted and actual scores) was significantly better than expected by chance. The entire prediction procedure was repeated 1000 times. In each iteration, the behavioral scores were randomly shuffled across samples to break the true correlation between brain features and clinical scores, and the model was re-trained and cross-validated on this shuffled data. The p-value was calculated as the proportion of permutation iterations in which the correlation coefficient exceeded or equaled the value obtained from the original data. Generalizability was assessed by training on the full discovery cohort and testing in the replication cohort, applying the same feature selection and weight estimation procedures.

Longitudinal stability of gradient organization was then tested in both cohorts using linear mixed-effects models (LMEMs). Post-stroke time (days from onset) was entered as a fixed effect, with subject identity as a random intercept, and significance maps were corrected for multiple comparisons at FDR-corrected p < 0.05.

Finally, to assess neurochemical associations, the spatial pattern of gradient alterations (two-sample t-map of patient-control differences) was correlated with normative PET maps of 14 neurotransmitter receptors/transporters. Spatial correlations were evaluated using vertex-wise Spearman coefficients, and significance was determined with spin-tests (10,000 permutations).

Results

Compression of the principal cortical gradient following subcortical stroke

We decomposed group-level FC matrices to identify the principal components of cortical organization in patients 2 weeks after supratentorial subcortical stroke and in healthy controls. The principal gradient explained 27% of connectome variance in patients and 26% in controls, delineating the canonical unimodal-to-transmodal axis anchored by primary sensorimotor cortices at one end and higher-order association regions at the other (Fig. 2A), consistent with previous reports in healthy adults. In patients, this distribution was compressed, with attenuated separation between unimodal and transmodal extremes. The secondary gradient accounted for 21% and 23% of variance in patients and controls, respectively, capturing a visual-to-sensorimotor axis and showing no appreciable group differences (Fig. 2B).

A The principal connectivity gradient in both patients and healthy controls delineated the canonical unimodal-to-transmodal axis, extending from primary sensorimotor cortices to higher-order association regions; relative to controls, patients showed higher gradient scores in unimodal regions and lower scores in transmodal regions. B The secondary gradient captured a visual-to-sensorimotor axis and showed no appreciable differences between groups. C Global measures of the principal gradient demonstrated reduced range and variation in patients, whereas no group differences were observed for the secondary gradient. D Surface-based statistical maps illustrate vertex-wise alterations of the principal gradient, with reduced values (green) in association cortices and increased values (yellow) in sensorimotor regions in patients. E Functional network-level comparisons, based on Yeo’s seven-network parcellation, revealed reduced gradient scores in transmodal networks (DMN and FPN) and increased scores in unimodal networks (SMN and VN) in patients

To quantify compression, we compared global properties of gradient distributions. Stroke patients exhibited significantly reduced range (t = − 2.70, p = 0.008) and variation (t = − 2.26, p = 0.026) of the principal gradient relative to controls, whereas no differences emerged for the secondary gradient (Fig. 2C). Surface-based analyses revealed increased principal gradient scores in visual and somatomotor cortices and decreased scores in association cortices, particularly the inferior parietal lobule and temporal pole (Fig. 2D; Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Functional network-level analysis confirmed significant reductions within the DMN and FPN and increases within the VN and SMN (FDR-corrected p < 0.05; Fig. 2E). Importantly, our findings were robust to the choice of cortical template (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). Together, these results demonstrate a compression of the principal cortical gradient following subcortical stroke, reflecting disrupted cortical functional hierarchy.

Functional gradients show higher cross-cohort replicability than FC strength

Group-level alterations in cortical gradients were robustly replicated across the discovery and replication cohorts. In the replication cohort, patients again exhibited compression of the principal gradient, with significantly reduced range (t = − 2.23, p = 0.039) and variation (t = − 2.48, p = 0.017) relative to controls (Fig. 3A, B). Vertex-wise maps of patient-control differences show a spatial pattern broadly consistent with the discovery cohort (Fig. 3C), and these maps were strongly correlated across cohorts (Spearman ρ = 0.60, pspin < 0.001; Fig. 3D).

A In the replication cohort, the principal connectivity gradient delineates a unimodal-to-transmodal axis, and stroke patients show higher gradient scores in unimodal regions and lower scores in transmodal regions compared with healthy controls. B Global gradient range and variation are significantly reduced in patients relative to controls, replicating the findings from the discovery cohort (see Fig. 2). F Spatial correlations of FCS difference maps across cohorts are weak and non-significant

In contrast, conventional FC strength metrics (whole-cortex, between-network, within-network) exhibited limited cross-cohort correspondence. In the discovery cohort, patients showed widespread increases in FC strength (Fig. 3E), but these effects did not replicate. Cross-cohort correlations of FC strength difference maps were weak and non-significant (whole-cortex: ρ = − 0.10, pspin = 0.132; between-network: ρ = − 0.08, pspin = 0.240; within-network: ρ = 0.02, pspin = 0.771; Fig. 3F). These findings indicate that functional gradient alterations provide more replicable cross-cohort effects than FC strength metrics.

Principal gradient scores predict stroke severity with strong generalizability

We next evaluated the clinical relevance of cortical features by predicting behavioral outcomes. At 2 weeks post-stroke, the principal gradient robustly predicted NIHSS scores (r = 0.66, MAE = 0.68, p < 0.001; Fig. 4A), whereas between-network FC strength achieved only moderate prediction (r = 0.40, MAE = 0.76, p = 0.003). Whole-cortex and within-network FCS provided weaker predictions (r = 0.14, MAE = 0.88, p = 0.33; r = 0.29, MAE = 0.83, p = 0.03). None of the tested metrics reliably predicted motor-specific impairment measured by the FMA (Additional file 1: Fig. S3).

Cross-dataset validation confirmed superior generalizability of gradient features. Models based on principal gradient scores maintained predictive accuracy in the replication cohort (r = 0.62, MAE = 0.61; Fig. 4B), whereas between-network FCS failed to generalize (r = –0.45, MAE = 0.93; Fig. 4C). Cortical regions contributing positively to NIHSS prediction included left-lateralized SMN, VN, DAN, and VAN regions, whereas negative weights were localized to right-lateralized DMN, FPN, and medial VAN regions (Fig. 4B). Overall, gradient features provided stronger and more generalizable markers of stroke severity than FCS metrics.

Temporal stability of cortical gradients during early recovery

We next assessed the longitudinal stability of cortical gradients. In the discovery cohort, the principal gradient retained highly similar spatial organization across the 2-, 4-, and 12-week timepoints (Fig. 5A). Comparable stability was observed in the replication cohort across the 2- and 12-week timepoints (Fig. 5B). Global gradient measures (range and variation) showed no significant longitudinal changes in either dataset (all p > 0.1; Fig. 5C). Vertex-wise mixed-effects models also revealed no significant changes over time. These findings suggest that compression of the principal cortical gradient emerges early and remains stable over the first 3 months of recovery.

Spatial alignment of cortical gradient alterations with neurotransmitter systems

Finally, we tested whether cortical gradient alterations were spatially coupled with neurotransmitter systems. After controlling for multiple comparisons, patient-control differences in the principal gradient showed significant spatial correlations with normative PET maps of receptor and transporter densities, including dopamine (D1: ρ = 0.38, pspin = 0.011; D2: ρ = 0.45, pspin = 0.008), GABAA (ρ = 0.50, pspin = 0.002), cannabinoid CB1 (ρ = 0.47, pspin < 0.001), glutamate mGluR5 (ρ = 0.43, pspin = 0.004), mu-opioid receptor (MOR) (ρ = 0.61, pspin < 0.001), and serotonin (5-HT2A: ρ = 0.49, pspin < 0.001; 5-HT4: ρ = 0.53, pspin < 0.001) (Fig. 6). These results suggest that reorganization of the cortical functional hierarchy following subcortical stroke is constrained by the architecture of key neuromodulatory systems.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that subcortical infarcts induce a compression of the principal cortical gradient, reflecting diminished differentiation between unimodal and transmodal systems. This alteration was robustly replicated in an independent cohort, remained stable during the first three months of recovery, and showed strong spatial correspondence with normative maps of neurotransmitter systems innervated by subcortical nuclei. Importantly, gradient-based metrics reliably predicted overall neurological impairment (NIHSS scores) with cross-cohort generalizability, whereas conventional FCS measures showed poor reproducibility. These results establish the principal gradient as a robust, mechanistically grounded, and clinically meaningful candidate biomarker of cortical reorganization following subcortical stroke.

Although prior neuroimaging studies of subcortical stroke have largely focused on sensorimotor systems, the broader impact on cortical functional organization has been comparatively underexplored [18, 22, 61, 62]. We observed a compressed cortical hierarchy centred on sensorimotor and frontoparietal systems, suggesting reduced segregation between unimodal and transmodal domains. This likely arises from the disruption of cortico-subcortical loops, such as cortico-basal ganglia-thalamic (CBG) and cortico-thalamo-cortical loops [1, 2]. These parallel circuits regulate large-scale cortical networks, such as the motor loop linking sensorimotor cortices with the putamen, or the dorsolateral prefrontal loop connecting the prefrontal cortex with the caudate to support executive control. By disrupting thalamic relay, lesions may weaken segregation and enhance maladaptive coupling between sensory and association systems [63, 64]. This interpretation is consistent with disorders featuring selective CBG pathology, such as Parkinson’s disease, which show increased sensory-association coupling [65–67] and compression of the principal gradient [35]. Together, these results indicate that focal subcortical damage reverberates across the cortex, reshaping fundamental principles of cortical hierarchy. By focusing on cortical organization rather than lesion sites or pairwise connectivity, this work highlights how subcortical regions regulate cortical networks at the systems level.

Longitudinal analysis revealed that cortical gradient compression persisted at 2, 4, and 12 weeks post-stroke, despite concurrent behavioral improvements. This finding suggests that while behavioral recovery may proceed through adaptive plasticity, the disruption to whole-brain architecture does not normalize in the short term. Physiologically, such stability may reflect a new equilibrium imposed by disrupted cortico-subcortical loops and constrained by relatively stable neurotransmitter architectures. The brain may prioritize maintaining core network frameworks rather than restoring hierarchical differentiation, consistent with adaptive strategies of functional compensation and structural repair [68–70]. Clinically, the persistence of abnormal gradients underscores two points: first, early detection may help predict recovery trajectories; and second, the absence of spontaneous reversal highlights the need for targeted interventions during the subacute window to actively reshape cortical hierarchy. At the same time, this stability renders gradient compression a reliable biomarker for longitudinal tracking. Whether these alterations remain fixed or evolve at later stages remains unresolved, requiring long-term studies.

Gradient compression may represent a transdiagnostic signature of altered cortical organization. Similar patterns have been reported in previous studies of autism, schizophrenia and Parkinson’s disease, where compression correlates with symptom severity [32, 33, 35]. In our stroke cohort, the compressed gradient pattern also predicted NIHSS scores. Decreased gradient values in higher-order association cortices (e.g. frontoparietal regions, precuneus) and increased values in unimodal cortices (e.g. somatosensory, visual) were both associated with worse outcomes. This bidirectional shift suggests that a loss of segregation between sensory and cognitive networks underlies broad functional deficits [71]. While gradient metrics significantly predicted NIHSS scores, they did not significantly predict specific motor function scores. This finding highlights that gradient markers are particularly sensitive to the widespread network disruption that underpins multi-domain impairment [71, 72]. While gradients are excellent markers for the global network burden of stroke, they should be complemented by local metrics when assessing domain-specific recovery. Furthermore, gradient-based models not only outperformed FC-based approaches in predicting NIHSS scores, but also generalized successfully across independent datasets, which is an essential requirement for clinical application. This suggests that gradient compression may provide a robust marker that can bridge the gap between neurobiology and prognosis, offering a promising avenue for tailoring rehabilitation strategies.

Our findings highlight the superior reproducibility and robustness of gradient metrics compared to FCS. This discrepancy likely stems from the fact that FCS is a node-centric measure that sums the connectivity of a specific region and is highly sensitive to signal noise and the spatial heterogeneity of stroke lesions [29]. In contrast, functional gradients are system-level topological measures derived from dimensionality reduction. They represent the low-dimensional embedding of the functional connectome and tend to attenuate local noise to reveal the underlying manifold. Neurobiologically, while FCS reflects the local hubness of specific regions, functional gradients capture the macroscale hierarchy of the whole cortex, which is evolutionarily conserved [30]. These differences enable gradient analysis to capture systematic shifts in global topology that remain consistent across individuals, rather than the variable local fluctuations captured by FCS.

Importantly, the spatial distribution of gradient alterations corresponded with neurotransmitter systems, including dopamine, GABA, glutamate, cannabinoid, opioid, and serotonin. These systems regulate excitatory-inhibitory balance, synaptic plasticity, and network dynamics [39, 73–75]. Dopaminergic signaling modulates cortico-basal ganglia-thalamic loops critical for motor and cognitive control; glutamatergic transmission enables synaptic plasticity; GABAergic inhibition stabilizes cortico-basal ganglia-thalamic circuitry; cannabinoid receptors fine-tune dopaminergic and glutamatergic signaling; and mu-opioid and serotonergic systems modulate neural circuits underlying mood regulation. By contrast, neuromodulatory systems without significant associations with gradient alterations (e.g. histamine, certain serotonergic subtypes) may exert a more diffuse or weaker modulation on cortico-subcortical interactions. This selective association suggests that cortical hierarchy may be preferentially constrained by neuromodulatory systems most engaged in cortico-subcortical circuits, offering a neurochemical framework for how focal subcortical lesions reshape global cortical organization.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, although robustness was confirmed across two independent cohorts, modest sample sizes limited statistical power. Larger multicentre studies will be necessary for replication and extension. Second, our longitudinal observations were restricted to the first three months post-stroke, leaving the longer-term trajectory of cortical gradients unresolved. Future follow-up studies are needed to determine whether early stability reflects a permanent plateau or a precursor to further reorganization. Third, our analysis was confined to a functionally and anatomically heterogeneous group of subcortical strokes. Future work should differentiate the specific impacts of lesions involving distinct structures (e.g. basal ganglia and thalamus) on cortical hierarchical organization. Moreover, extending gradient analyses to other subtypes, including infratentorial (brainstem/cerebellar) and focal cortical lesions, will better characterize network-level disruptions and test the generalizability of our findings across cerebrovascular disease. Finally, methodological constraints regarding spatial alignment warrant consideration. While we employed standard surface-based registration to ensure cross-study comparability, the surface structure of functional cortical topographies exhibits considerable variability across individuals [76]. To address this limitation, functional alignment methods, such as hyperalignment, offer a promising avenue by projecting neural patterns into a common, high-dimensional information space [77–80]. Future work should explore whether advanced hyperalignment frameworks can enhance the inter-subject comparability of functional gradients, potentially improving the sensitivity to detect brain disease-related alterations.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that subcortical stroke disrupts cortical functional hierarchy, manifesting as a robust compression of the principal gradient between unimodal and transmodal systems. These alterations were stable during early recovery and closely aligned with the distribution of key neurotransmitter systems, highlighting how focal subcortical injury constrains large-scale cortical organization through specific neurochemical pathways. Beyond establishing a system-level marker of subcortical dysfunction, our findings emphasize that the cortical consequences of subcortical stroke extend well beyond local motor circuits, reshaping integrative principles of cortical communication. By linking cortical hierarchy to both behavioral deficits and neuromodulatory substrates, this work provides new insight into the mechanisms of post-stroke reorganization and underscores the potential of gradient-derived markers to guide prognosis and targeted neurorehabilitation.
