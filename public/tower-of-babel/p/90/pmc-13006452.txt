Neonatal ventilator-associated pneumonia: a tale of three cities in Canada West
Authors: Alexandra Kowalczyk, Celine Balay, Bao-Nghi Nguyen, Mimi T.Y. Kuan, Claire Hamilton, Adel M. Elsharkawy, Jonathan Wong, Joseph Y. Ting
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Preterm infants on invasive breathing support are at increased risk for acquired infections called ventilator-associated pneumonias (VAP). Wide heterogeneity exists in diagnostic criteria and management of VAP. The objective of this study is to determine the demographic characteristics and prevalence of VAP according to three different existing criteria used in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). We conducted a retrospective study including infants born at < 33 weeks’ gestational age (GA) from 3 different NICUs in Western Canada who have been diagnosed with VAP during 2018–2022. The incidence of VAP was categorized based on physicians’ discretion, Canadian consensus, and National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) criteria. There were 185 neonates diagnosed to have VAP at physicians’ discretion, with incidences of 4.6%, 8.9%, and 5.7% in the three NICUs. Among the VAP per physicians’ diagnoses, 18.2–55.0% of infants fulfilled the VAP criteria of Canadian consensus, and 10.9–12.5% of them fulfilled the NHSN criteria. Conclusion: We found that 4.6–8.9% of infants born at < 33 weeks GA had VAP diagnosed during their NICU stay. About one-tenth of these cases fulfilled the NHSN criteria. Therefore, there is an urgency to develop neonatal-specific and validated diagnostic criteria for VAP. However, there is significant heterogeneity in the diagnostic criteria and management of VAP, which have not been validated in the neonatal populationWhat is New:• 4.6-8.9% of infants are diagnosed with VAP during their NICU stay. Introduction

Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) is one of the most frequently diagnosed hospital-acquired infections in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) [1]. In Canada, approximately 3000 very-low-birthweight (VLBW; birth weight < 1500 g) infants are born annually, with around 60% requiring life-saving invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) via an endotracheal tube (ETT) [2]. The use of IMV predisposes these infants to airway colonization by potential VAP pathogens, particularly after 48 h of intubation.

The diagnostic criteria and preventative protocols for neonatal VAP are largely extrapolated from those developed for older children and adults [3, 4], not able to account for critical neonatal factors such as the use of smaller ETTs and the prolonged duration of IMV required for extremely premature infants. Furthermore, differentiating VAP from other neonatal etiologies of respiratory insufficiency poses a challenge. These limitations are significant as they may lead to delayed diagnosis, inadequate treatment, or overtreatment of neonates with antibiotics.

Reported VAP rates vary widely, ranging from 2.7 to 37.2 episodes per 1,000 ventilator days or affecting 16–24% of ventilated infants in Canadian NICUs, depending on the criteria used, population studied, and geographic location [5]. Internationally the incidence of VAP ranges from 1.4 to 7 episodes per 1000 ventilator days in developed countries and from 16.9 to 89 episodes per 1000 ventilator days in developing countries [6]. The aim of this study was to review the characteristics of patients treated for VAP across three Level 3 NICUs in western Canada and to examine the incidence of VAP using three currently employed diagnostic criteria.

Material and methods

We conducted a multicenter retrospective study conducted across three tertiary NICUs affiliated with the University of Alberta, the University of Calgary, and the University of British Columbia. This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethics approvals for the University of Alberta (Pro00125900) and the University of British Columbia (H23-02671) were obtained, while the project was regarded as part of a quality-improvement initiative from Foothills Medical Center (i.e., ethics board review not required).

The study population comprised infants born at less than 33 weeks’ gestational age (GA) who were treated for VAP between 2018 and 2022. The gestational-age cutoff of 33 weeks was determined based on the criteria used by the Canadian Neonatal Network (CNN) in its annual report on very preterm infants, thereby maintaining consistency with national standards for benchmarking neonatal outcomes. Exclusion criteria included infants who received fewer than four days of antibiotic therapy, those without respiratory specimens collected, and those who were intubated for less than 48 h. Data collection was performed using existing neonatal databases, following standard clinical care practices and the CNN Abstractor Manual [7].

A data collection tool was developed to calculate VAP incidences by collection of the following variables: (1) clinical aspects—clinical features (including temperature instability, bradycardia or tachycardia and change in sputum appearance); (2) radiographic changes on chest X-ray or ultrasound lung (including infiltration, consolidation, cavitation, pneumatocele, atelectasis, and pleural effusion or pulmonary edema); (3) ventilation—changes in mean airway pressure (MAP) and fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) within the week of diagnosis of VAP; (4) change in biochemical markers, including leukocyte count and C-reactive protein; (5) microbiology—identity of pathogens and susceptibility pattern; and (6) antimicrobial treatment.

Secondary objectives included identifying the median GA of patients diagnosed with VAP, identifying recurrence rates of VAP, identifying the incidence of bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), defined as the need for oxygen or respiratory support at 36 weeks postmenstrual age, and identifying the top pathogens implicated in VAP diagnosis.

Demographic characteristics were described using medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables and proportions for categorical variables, as appropriate. Analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.2.1).

Based on physicians’ discretion, 4.6–8.9% of the cohort were diagnosed with VAP, with recurrence rates ranging from 13.6% to 23.8%. Among these cases, only 27.6% met the Canadian Consensus definition, while just 9.7% fulfilled the NHSN criteria. Using the physicians’ discretion criteria, the incidence of BPD was 61.4% (Site I), 70.3% (Site II), and 85.0 (Site III), in contrast to the general BPD rates of 38.0%, 28.8% and 28.8% of all patients born at < 33 weeks in individual sites, respectively.

The most frequently identified pathogens across the study sites were Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella species, and Enterobacter cloacae complex. Resistance of S. aureus to oxacillin/cloxacillin ranged from 0.0% to 7.9%. Among E. coli isolates, a high resistance was observed against ampicillin, with rates ranging from 57.1% to 100%. Resistance to gentamicin/tobramycin was recorded in 16.7% to 50.0% of isolates, while up to 14.2% exhibited resistance to third-generation cephalosporins. No E. coli isolates demonstrated resistance to meropenem. Similarly, no resistance to gentamicin/tobramycin or meropenem was observed among K. pneumoniae isolates.

Discussion

VAP remains a significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenge for healthcare providers in NICUs. The absence of a consistent, neonatal-specific definition hinders accurate assessment of its prevalence and impedes the development of effective strategies to mitigate its burden and associated treatment consequences. Our pilot study found that the incidence of VAP varied between 4.6% and 8.9% across the three centres, with only 4.5% to 12.5% of cases meeting the NHSN criteria.

The NHSN definition was developed for a heterogeneous pediatric population, spanning from neonates to 18-year-olds, without subgroup analyses specific to preterm infants [8]. The ‘Canadian Consensus’ definition, although formulated through expert consensus and implemented in several Canadian Neonatal Network sites, remains unvalidated [5]. We are not aware of studies which directly compared different diagnostic criteria. In clinical practice, broad-spectrum antimicrobials are often initiated early by practitioners due to concerns about missing treatable conditions. Overdiagnosis and overtreatment of VAP are perceived as widespread, largely due to its non-specific clinical features in preterm infants [1]. This approach to clinical practice, in combination with the extensive and stringent NHSN criteria, contributes to the discrepancy of VAP incidence across different diagnostic definitions. Excessive use of broad-spectrum antibiotics contributes to increased mortality and morbidity, gut dysbiosis, and the emergence of antibiotic-resistant organisms, such as extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing organisms and vancomycin-resistant enterococci [9]. VAP has been identified as an independent predictor of mortality in VLBW infants, extending NICU admission and IMV duration while significantly increasing BPD prevalence [3].

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the incidence of VAP in Western Canada using three existing diagnostic frameworks. However, this pilot study has several limitations, including a relatively small sample size drawn from only three centers. Variations in clinical practice, rather than true differences in incidence, may account for some of the observed discrepancies among the centers, although such variations are challenging to quantify. Furthermore, the findings may not be generalizable to other regions. Microbiological samples were obtained from ETT aspirates, which preclude differentiation between tracheitis and pneumonia. All positive ETT aspirates, when accompanied by clinical, radiographic, and/or laboratory evidence of infection, were classified as VAP at the discretion of the medical team. We did not investigate the serial changes of radiolucency on CXR or ultrasound lung suggestive of the diagnosis of VAP. Differences in clinical judgment and institutional practices may also influence how healthcare teams distinguish between colonization and true infection within the respiratory tract.

Despite these limitations, our study highlights the urgent need for ongoing collaboration within the neonatal research community to address existing gaps in VAP diagnostics. Future research should prioritize the development of neonatal-specific diagnostic criteria that incorporate relevant outcomes, such as BPD, to improve detection accuracy. Large-scale, multicenter prospective studies will be essential to refine diagnostic criteria and develop targeted prevention and treatment strategies for this vulnerable population.

In summary, although up to 8.9% of neonates admitted to the NICU were diagnosed with VAP, only approximately one-tenth of cases fulfilled the NHSN criteria. Our study thus highlights the critical importance of establishing which rate is truly representative of the clinical burden.
