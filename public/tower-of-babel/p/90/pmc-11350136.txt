Hybrid nanocomposites impact on heat transfer efficiency and pressure drop in turbulent flow systems: application of numerical and machine learning insights
Authors: Hai Tao, Mohammed Suleman Aldlemy, Raad Z Homod, Muammer Aksoy, Mustafa K A Mohammed, Omer A Alawi, Hussein Togun, Leonardo Goliatt, Md Munir Hayet Khan, Zaher Mundher Yaseen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research explores the feasibility of using a nanocomposite from multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) and graphene nanoplatelets (GNPs) for thermal engineering applications. The hybrid nanocomposites were suspended in water at various volumetric concentrations. Their heat transfer and pressure drop characteristics were analyzed using computational fluid dynamics and artificial neural network models. The study examined flow regimes with Reynolds numbers between 5000 and 17,000, inlet fluid temperatures ranging from 293.15 to 333.15 K, and concentrations from 0.01 to 0.2% by volume. The numerical results were validated against empirical correlations for heat transfer coefficient and pressure drop, showing an acceptable average error. The findings revealed that the heat transfer coefficient and pressure drop increased significantly with higher inlet temperatures and concentrations, achieving approximately 45.22% and 452.90%, respectively. These results suggested that MWCNTs-GNPs nanocomposites hold promise for enhancing the performance of thermal systems, offering a potential pathway for developing and optimizing advanced thermal engineering solutions.Keywords: Machine learning, Multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), Graphene nanoplatelets (GNPs), Heat transfer, Pressure drop, Turbulent flowSubject terms: Computational science, Mechanical engineering, Fluid dynamics

Introduction

Background and purpose of study

Heat transfer and pressure drop mechanisms are critical in various applications, such as thermal management in electronics and energy conversion systems1. Nanofluids, solid particles in nanoscale suspended in conventional base fluids, have been considered promising solutions for enhancing heat transport and thermal conductivity2. The thermal performance of nanofluid depends on critical factors such as nanoparticle type (organic, inorganic, or metal), size (nanoparticle diameter or dimensions), nanoparticle volumetric or mass fraction, hosting fluid temperature and flow conditions (laminar, turbulent, or conjugated). The heat transfer and pressure drop properties using nanofluids can be investigated through experimental work, numerical simulations (ANSYS, MATLAB, or COMSOL software), and data-driven models3–5.

Experimental and numerical literature review

In the last decade, numerous researchers worldwide have focused on carbon-based nanostructures due to their outstanding optical, thermal, and electrical properties in various engineering applications. Interestingly, carbon allotropes such as graphene (Gr), graphene oxides (GO), reduced graphene oxide (rGO), graphene nanoplatelets (GNPs), and carbon nanotubes (single-walled (SWCNT), double-walled (DWCNT), and multi-walled (MWCNT)) possess high thermal conductivity and large specific surface area (SSA), which is advantage over classic nanomaterials for heat transfer applications. The general trend from the open literature shows that carbon-based nanofluids can significantly improve the heat transport phenomenon compared to traditional fluids6–9.

Various experimental work and numerical studies have used single and nanocomposite carbon-based nanofluids to improve heat transport in different test sections. For example, a study synthesized graphene nanoplatelet/platinum (GNPs-Pt) hybrid nanofluids to examine their impact on heat transfer capacity. Their results showed that the maximum increment in the average Nusselt number (Nuavg) was 28.48% relative to H2O. Meanwhile, an increase in the friction factor (f) was observed under the testing conditions of 0.1 wt.% at Re = 17,50010. The thermal conductivity of Iron oxide/Graphene (Fe3O4/Gr) hybrid nanofluid was improved by 14–32% at 20–40 °C compared to H2O, and the convective heat transfer coefficient was enhanced by 14.5% for Reynolds numbers of 2000–500011. In addition, the thermal and hydrodynamic properties of clove-treated multi-walled carbon nanotubes (C-MWCNTs)/H2O nanofluids were numerically tested in a concentric annular heated pipe, which demonstrated a notable increase in convective heat transfer coefficient without a significant change in the friction factor12. Moreover, the ANSYS computer program tested covalently functionalized graphene nanoplatelets (C-GNPs) nanosuspensions to examine heat transfer and frictional resistance properties in a horizontal heated tube. Their results showed an average variation in pressure drop (ΔP) and average heat transfer coefficient (htc) of 8.02% for 0.025 wt.%, 6.82% for 0.075 wt.%, and 5.94% for 0.1 wt.% and 8.11% for 0.025 wt.%, 1.87% for 0.075 wt.%, and 6.02% for 0.1 wt.%, respectively13. Also, C-GNPs nanofluids were produced for cooling working fluids, showing significant improvements in thermophysical properties with minor rises in frictional resistance and pumping power14. The effect of MWCNTs/turbine meter oil nanofluid concentration on the thermophysical properties of lubricants was examined experimentally and numerically15. The authors showed that, the highest increment in the friction factor was 10.41% under the testing conditions of 0.3 wt.% nanoparticles to pure oil and inlet velocity = 0.069 m/s. In another study, the combination of Al2O3-MWCNTs/H2O nanofluids was used in the annular passage of various eccentricities. The average thermal conductivity enhancements were 3.102% for 0.1 wt.%, 1.829% for 0.075 wt.%, and 1.158% for 0.05 wt.%, with corresponding maximum enhancements in convective heat transfer coefficient of 6.49%, 4.86%, and 2.98%16. MWCNTs and titanium dioxide (TiO2) nanoparticles were added to turbine meter oil to prepare nano lubricant in the presence of oleic acid and Triton × 100 as surfactants17. They showed that, increasing 0.3 wt.% of TiO2 and MWCNTs in the oil caused an improvement in viscosity index. The simulation and experimental data for the pressure drop were closer together and indicated a minor error, with the maximum error being less than 10%. A green approach was used with gallic acids (GA) to produce stable (MWCNTs) nanofluids. Their approach enhanced the thermal conductivity by about 7.30%, but a minimal increase in relative pumping power was observed18. In a different research paper, a novel heat exchanger system fitted with ultrasonic transducers increased long-term stability and heat transfer enhancement, with a 300% increase in the heat transfer coefficient19. Moreover, the pool boiling of MWCNTs/H2O nanofluids on smooth and groove surfaces was investigated20. According to the experimental results, the configuration S4 with a 30 mm deep circular groove inclined at a 45° angle and 33% higher than the base fluid on the smooth surface enhanced the boiling heat transfer coefficient. The thermal performance of a shell-and-tube heat exchanger in the presence of graphene/H2O nanofluid was experimentally investigated21. Nanofluid at 0.06 wt.% had good performance, with an increase of 22.47% in the heat transfer coefficient and 8.88% in the thermal performance. Thermal properties of novel synthesized ZnFe2O4 and TiO2-doped MWCNTs nanocomposites in transformer oil (TRO) were assessed22. The heat transfer properties were enhanced from 9 to 21% under forced convection conditions.

Machine learning literature review

Artificial intelligence approaches such as machine learning or deep learning have been gradually used to predict heat transport and pressure drop with nanofluids in recent years. Some famous models appeared in the open literature, such as decision tree (DT), linear regression models (LR), artificial neural networks (ANNs), and support vector machine (SVM). Such models were trained to demonstrate strong correlations between heat transport variables, nanofluid velocity, and other variables, increasing thermal performance prediction accuracy in different test sections.

Limited studies were conducted using numerical and data-driven approaches to discuss the effects of nanofluids on heat transport and pressure drop, for instance, a horizontal heated pipe comprising twisted-tape (TT) inserts using Graphene-Oxide (GO) nanofluids23. Their results indicated that, GO nanofluid at high vol.% and Re improved heat transfer mechanisms. The optimization approaches were based on a genetic algorithm (GA), resulting in a Nuavg of 263.57 and a friction factor of 0.0725. Another study examined the thermal performance of MWCNTs + Fe3O4/H2O hybrid nanofluids inside a flat plate solar collector (FPSC)24. The heat transfer coefficient increased by 26.3%, with a minimal reduction in pressure loss due to a friction factor of 18.9%. Boosted Regression Tree (BRT) and Extreme Gradient Boosting (EGB) models were developed to predict the heat transfer properties, hydrodynamic properties, and thermal efficiency. The R-squared values ranged from 0.9619 to 0.9997, with mean squared error (MSE) values from 0.000081 to 9.11. Another study used an ANN algorithm to estimate the thermal conductivity of triple hybrid nanofluids containing MWCNTs-TiO2-ZnO suspended in a mixture of water/ethylene glycol (DW + EG)25. The ANN calculated the maximum number of neurons to be 26, with a maximum absolute error of less than 0.018 in a dataset of 102 points. Similarly, an ANN predictive model was developed to determine the thermal conductivity of MWCNTs + WO3 nanocomposites suspended in engine oil26. The maximum number of neurons for the ANN was 7, which resulted in an absolute error of zero in many data points and smaller error values compared to the empirical correlations method. The density and dynamic viscosity of rGO-Fe3O4-TiO2 triple nanocomposites suspended in EG were predicted with BRT, SVM, and ANN algorithms27. BRT modeling showed the highest accuracy, with R-values of 0.9989 for density and 0.9979 for dynamic viscosity and the lowest uncertainty compared to ANN and SVM. Finally, an optimized ANN algorithm accurately predicted the thermophysical properties of the combinations between MWCNTs and different metallic oxides of Al2O3, TiO2, ZnO, and CeO2, all suspended in DW28. The results showed high accuracy, with R2 > 0.999, MSE of < 0.001, and deviations of < ± 5%. To conclude, recent studies have highlighted the potential of AI and simulation methods to effectively predict the thermophysical properties and optimize the configuration of thermal systems that utilize nanofluids.

Research objectives and novelty

This study uses ANSYS simulation and machine learning to evaluate the heat transfer and pressure drop characteristics of MWCNTs/GNPs hybrid nanofluids in a heated system. Testing different volume fractions and inlet temperatures enhances understanding of these properties in hybrid nanofluid mixtures. An artificial neural network model was developed using nine input parameters: fluid type, Reynolds number, turbulent intensity, velocity, mass flow rate, and various temperatures. This method provided extensive data, offering valuable insights for optimizing thermal engineering systems. The novelty of this research lies in its unique combination of ANSYS simulation and machine learning to predict heat transfer and pressure drop in “MWCNTs + GNPs” nanofluids. This innovative approach has practical implications for improving efficiency in industrial applications such as power plants and HVAC systems.

Simulation methodologies

Physical model and solution methods

The current research paper aimed to examine the convective heat transfer properties of MWCNTs + GNPs hybrid nanofluids suspended in H2O at Tin ranging from 293.15 to 333.15 K and concentrations between 0.01 and 0.2 vol.%. The study aimed to determine the influence of these nanofluids on fully developed turbulent convective heat transfer and pressure drop within a Reynolds number range of 5000–17,000. A 20 mm diameter and 900mm length heated pipe with smooth walls (as depicted in Fig. 1) was simulated under a constant heat flux boundary condition of 58,866 W/m2 to ensure steady-state conditions.

Schematic drawing of the physical model and computational grid.

The ANSYS FLUENT 2022R2 software was utilized to solve a set of partial differential equations (PDEs) of the convective heat transfer for MWCNTs-GNPs/H2O nanofluids using a pressure-based solution and the finite volume method (FVM). The PDEs included pressure, momentum, turbulent kinetic energy, specific dissipation rate, and energy. The velocity equation was solved at the inlet boundary condition of the heated pipe, and the pressure equation was solved at the outlet boundary condition with a non-slip wall boundary assumption. The PDEs were integrated into the control volumes in the FVM to obtain a set of algebraic equations, which were solved using numerical methods such as iterative methods or direct solvers. The 2nd order Upwind SIMPLE algorithm was utilized for the pressure–velocity coupling scheme.

Moreover, absolute criteria and under-relaxation factors were implemented with a convergence condition of 10–6 for continuity equations, x-velocity, y-velocity, z-velocity, k, omega, and energy to ensure solution stability and performance convergence. Additionally, a grid independence test (GIT) was recommended by simulating the thermal model at various structured hexahedral grids to determine the optimal grid used in the following simulation cases.

Mathematical expressions

Heat transfer simulations using numerical software offer a variety of advantages over traditional laboratory experimental methods. Five significant benefits can be addressed here. First, simulations are not costly as they do not require expensive equipment, materials, or chemicals. Second, simulations can model hazardous or destructive scenarios in a safe and controlled environment, eliminating the risk of harm to people or the environment. Thirdly, simulations allow for precise manipulation and testing of various conditions and variables, giving the researcher complete control over the experiment. Fourthly, simulations can be repeated similarly, resulting in more reliable and accurate data. In conclusion, simulations can be conducted much faster than experimentations, allowing the researcher to obtain more data in a shorter period.

Nanofluids are novel liquids produced by adding a single or combination of nanomaterials into the base fluids. According to the thermal system, the primary fluids could be pure water, mineral oil, or ethylene glycol. Furthermore, a small amount of the nanomaterials is sufficient to produce well-dispersed, homogamous, and long-term stability nanofluids. The thermophysical properties of nanofluids are enhanced relative to the base fluids; for this reason, the nanofluids demonstrated better heat transport and higher heat dissipation. As a result of these improved thermal properties, nanofluids have been considered for use in numerous purposes, such as heat exchangers and heating/cooling systems, where efficient heat transport is fundamental29,30.

The current research considers several assumptions and limitations when solving the numerical system using nanofluids. Machine learning methodologies

Artificial neural network (ANN)

The concept of Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) is based on the function of biological neural complex systems. ANNs are commonly used in many classification or prediction fields. In this context, the ANN was used to predict the heat transfer and pressure drop properties of MWCNTs-GNPs/H2O hybrid nanofluids35–40.

Validation/grid independence test

In this research Grid Independence Test (GIT) was used as numerical validation method for the concept of CFD. It works based on the determination of the minimal of grid size computation and it required to acquire a converged solution in simulations. Usually, it consists of grid incrementally refinement and compares the solution with a coarser grid. This is for the sake of ensuring a grid size independent results. In this study, four different grids were generated at 5000 Reynolds number using different inlet temperatures and assessed using four different parameters (T, TW, htc and P) (See Table 1). The grid selection was based on the validity of the Dittus–Boelter and Blasius equations for heat transfer and pressure drop properties, respectively. As the number of grid cells increased, the average error in the Dittus-Boelter equation increased, while the average error in the Blasius equation decreased. The trend across all grids indicates that the average error increases with increasing temperature. Grid-2 was chosen for the subsequent simulation cases, demonstrating a lower average error than Grid-1 and Grid-4.

Carbon-based nanostructures, such as graphene nanoplatelets (GNPs) and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), exhibit unique thermal and physical characteristics that enhance heat transport mechanisms in nanofluids. The heat transfer enhancement in these nanostructures can be attributed to several key factors48,49:

(i)High Thermal Conductivity CNTs and graphene possess exceptionally high thermal conductivities, exceeding 3000 W/m-K for CNTs and 5000 W/m-K for graphene.

As a result, nanofluids containing MWCNTs and GNPs, whether used individually or in hybrid forms, demonstrate significant potential for various thermal management applications50. However, adding these nanomaterials also increases the fluid’s resistance to flow, leading to a higher pressure loss. This increased fluid resistance results from the larger fluid volume and the increased friction caused by the nanomaterials, ultimately reducing the overall thermal efficiency of the heating or cooling system. Therefore, while incorporating MWCNTs and GNPs enhances the heat transfer coefficient, it is crucial to consider the resulting pressure drop, especially when it poses a significant concern for the system’s performance.

The discrepancies between the CFD simulations and ANNs predictions for heat transfer and pressure drop in MWCNTs-GNPs hybrid nanofluids are primarily due to the temperature-dependent properties of nanofluids, such as thermal conductivity and viscosity, which become more complex to model accurately at higher temperatures. Nanofluids exhibit non-linear behaviors like nanoparticle aggregation and Brownian motion that are challenging to capture in models. CFD simulations often rely on simplifications that may not fully represent these behaviors, while ANNs require extensive and diverse training data to generalize well. The cumulative errors observed, especially in pressure drop predictions at higher temperatures and concentrations, highlight the limitations of current models in accurately capturing the intricate dynamics of nanofluids under varying conditions.

Conclusion and remarks

The current study compared the heat transfer and pressure drop properties of carbon-based hybrid nanofluids using two computational approaches: ANSYS software and ANNs. The hybrid nanofluid, consisting of MWCNTs/GNPs in pure water, was tested in a pipe subjected to constant heat flux at various volume concentrations and inlet temperatures. The integration of ANSYS simulation and machine learning provided a more comprehensive understanding of the performance of the hybrid nanofluid. This study on MWCNTs-GNPs nanocomposites for thermal engineering shows promise but has several limitations. The narrow concentration range (0.01–0.2%), specific Reynolds numbers (5000–17,000), and limited temperature range (293.15–333.15 K) restrict its applicability. Validation relied on empirical correlations rather than direct experiments. Computational models may oversimplify nanocomposite behavior, and practical issues like increased pressure drop, material costs, and long-term stability are not addressed. Additionally, potential environmental and health impacts and performance under multiphase flow conditions were not considered. Future research should address these aspects for a more comprehensive understanding.
