Central Corneal Epithelial Wounding Accelerates Aniridia-Associated Keratopathy in PAX6-Deficient Mice
Authors: Yedizza Rautavaara, Israa Sefawi, Meri Vattulainen, Petros Moustardas, Dina Javidjam, Ava Dashti, Neil Lagali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate corneal healing in a Pax6+/− mouse model of aniridia-associated keratopathy (AAK) when challenged with a mild epithelial wound.Methods Pax6+/− small-eye (Sey) mice (n = 15) and Pax6+/+ wild-type mice (n = 4) on the 129S1/SvImJ background underwent 1 mm central corneal mechanical epithelial debridement. Corneal status before and four weeks after wounding was evaluated by slit-lamp imaging. Longitudinal corneal thickness was measured using optical coherence tomography (OCT), and AAK was graded in Sey mice. Wounded corneas were additionally compared to unwounded contralateral corneas for AAK status. Immunofluorescence staining was used to assess inflammation, wound healing, and the presence of corneal blood and lymphatic vessels.ResultsIn wild-type mice, the central epithelial wound healed normally. In contrast, wounded corneas in Sey mice exhibited significantly accelerated AAK progression at 4 weeks compared to unwounded contralateral corneas (P = 0.011), with a corresponding increase in mean AAK grade relative to pre-wounding levels (P = 0.001). AAK grade correlated positively with increased central corneal thickness in wounded (P = 0.031) and unwounded (P = 0.0006) Sey mice. Wounded corneas in Sey mice exhibited strong hemangiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis and altered epithelial phenotype with decreased CK12 and elevated MUC5AC expression, along with an increase in F4/80, IL-6, and IL-1β inflammation markers. Aberrant epithelial differentiation and stem/progenitor maintenance (CK15, SOX9), and persistent tissue repair (Ki-67) were present and were consistent with disease progression.ConclusionsCentral epithelial wounding accelerates AAK progression in Sey mice, highlighting the epithelial fragility in Pax6-aniridia and providing an efficient and robust accelerated model of AAK progression for preclinical studies.

Congenital aniridia is a rare genetic disorder typically characterized by the partial or complete absence of the iris.1 It is predominantly caused by mutations in the PAX6 gene, a key regulator of eye development. Vision impairment manifests early in life due to congenital anomalies such as foveal and iris hypoplasia.2 As the disease progresses, secondary complications, including cataract, glaucoma, and keratopathy, contribute to significant and often worsening deterioration of vision, in severe cases leading to complete blindness.3–5

Aniridia-associated keratopathy (AAK) is one of the most prevalent clinical manifestations of aniridia, affecting at least 80% of individuals with the condition,3 with detailed examinations revealing corneal pathology at the tissue level in virtually all cases of PAX6-aniridia.6 AAK is characterized by a painful and progressive loss of corneal function and transparency that is associated with limbal stem cell insufficiency, leading to neovascularization.6,7 The extent of corneal opacification and vascularization is a key indicator of AAK severity, which is graded based on these manifestations during clinical assessments.8 In patients with severe AAK, the corneal epithelium becomes increasingly unstable, leading to recurrent epithelial erosions, chronic inflammation, and conjunctivalization of the corneal surface.9,10 This is accompanied by abnormal corneal wound healing and the loss of a functional protective barrier.11

AAK typically manifests during the first to second decades of life and progresses slowly into early adulthood.11–13 Surgical procedures, however, risk triggering or worsening the AAK.14,15 In preclinical research, the slow onset of disease in animal models is desirable for faithfully representing the human course of AAK, which occurs over decades.7 Nevertheless, slow progression poses a challenge, requiring long observation periods in preclinical models and thus limiting the ability to evaluate novel therapeutic interventions. In addition, understanding the dynamics of wound healing and AAK progression in the context of preclinical models is essential for guiding the development of effective therapies.

Here, we examined the corneal response to a controlled, mild central epithelial injury in a Pax6-deficient mouse model to recapitulate epithelial damage arising from corneal erosions or surgical incisions. It was observed that a mild central epithelial wound, which heals normally in wild-type corneas, significantly and rapidly accelerates AAK progression in the mutant mouse model. This highlights the impaired wound healing in the Pax6-deficient cornea and offers a convenient, novel, and reproducible method to accelerate the phenotype in mouse models for preclinical therapy development.

Methods

Ethics Statement

The study was conducted after receiving ethics approval from the Linköping Regional Animal Ethics Review Board, Linköping, Sweden (Application No. 06826-2024). The study conformed to the EU Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes, and animals were treated in accordance with the ARVO statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

Animal Models

Mice with the 129S1/SvImJ background, including both Pax6+/+ wild-type (WT) and Pax6+/− mutant (Sey) genotypes, were bred and housed under standardized conditions at Linköping University, following the protocols described in detail elsewhere.7 Mouse genotyping was outsourced to TransnetYX (Cordova, TN, USA) using ear punch samples collected from the mice upon weaning. A total of 19 mice were included in the study, assigned to the following experimental groups: WT (wounded, n = 4) and Sey (wounded, n = 15). The injury was performed on one eye of each animal, and the contralateral, uninjured eye of each mouse was observed for comparison.

Central Epithelial Wounding Approach

All mice were anesthetized intraperitoneally with a combination of 100 mg/kg ketamine (50 mg/mL stock; Orion Pharma AB, Espoo, Finland) and 11.2 mg/kg xylazine (20 mg/mL stock; Pfizer AB). Tetracaine-HCl 0.5% eye drops (Bausch & Lomb, Rochester, NY, USA) were administered as a topical anesthesia before performing the injury. A sterile 0.5-mm corneal rust ring removal tool (Algerbrush II, cat no. BR2-5; Alger Company, Inc., Lago Vista, TX, USA) was used to remove the epithelium from the central cornea in a 1mm diameter circular zone, without penetrating through to the stroma. After gentle epithelial removal with the Algerbrush, one drop of ethanol 30% was placed on the wound for 30 seconds using a circle-shaped filter paper with a diameter of 1 mm to assist with epithelial cell removal, limiting alcohol exposure to only the wounded area. This was followed by a second gentle pass with the Algerbrush to aid in complete epithelial cell removal. After the injury, the eyes were rinsed with sterile water for injection (B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany). Antibiotic eye ointment (Fucithalmic 10 mg/g; Amdipharm UK Ltd., Advanz Pharma, London, UK) was placed on the wound daily for three days to minimize the risk of infection. To reverse anesthesia, mice received 200 µL of subcutaneous atipamezole solution (1.8 mg/kg), prepared by diluting Antisedan (5 mg/mL stock; Orion Pharma AB) in a 1:22.2 dilution with sterile PBS to a final concentration of 0.225 mg/mL. Buprenorphine (0.12 mg/kg) was co-administered with atipamezole for analgesia. The analgesic solution was prepared by diluting Temgesic (0.3 mg/mL stock; Indivior) 1:20 in PBS containing atipamezole, resulting in a final concentration of 0.015 mg/mL. Mice recovered in warmed cages until fully awake. Pain relief was further given in the form of subcutaneous injection of buprenorphine every 12 hours for three days in total.

In Vivo Ocular Imaging

Rodent slit-lamp (Micron III; Phoenix Technologies, Bowling Green, OH, USA) examination and fluorescein staining were performed before and immediately after epithelial wounding to confirm complete central epithelial removal. Slit lamp and digital SLR macro photography were used to determine the AAK grade, according to the classification scheme in Figure 1 (example images provided in Supplementary Figure S1). Tropicamide drops (Bausch & Lomb) were applied to dilate the eyes for better visualization of vascularization. AAK status was assessed pre-wounding (time = 0), at two weeks, and four weeks after wounding to compare the disease trajectory in wounded and contralateral unwounded eyes of Sey mice, as well as to evaluate corneal healing in WT controls. Additionally, the iVue 100 optical coherence tomography (OCT) system (Visionix USA, Lombard, IL, USA) was used to measure central corneal thickness in the corneal pachymetry mode. Corneal thickness was measured in three regions of the central cornea, excluding areas with small localized lenticular adhesions in Sey mice. Quantitative analysis of OCT images was conducted using the integrated OCT analysis software (version 3.2.1.8).

Corneal Sample Processing

At the four-week experimental endpoint, whole-eye bulbs were extracted and processed for histology. Whole eyes were rinsed with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution at room temperature for two hours, and preserved in 50% ethanol until further processing into paraffin blocks. Immunohistochemical analyses were performed on 5 µm tissue sections. Corneas designated for whole-mount staining were fixed in 1.3% paraformaldehyde for one hour and stored in PBS until further processing. Before staining, isolated corneas were cut to yield four leaflets. Details of the cornea sampling and preparation protocol are available elsewhere.7

Histology and Immunostaining

Histology with hematoxylin and eosin staining was conducted according to standard protocols. For immunohistochemistry (IHC), sections were deparaffinized by two rounds of xylene treatment (two minutes each), followed by a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 80%, 70%) and a final rinse in tap water (two minutes). Antigen retrieval was performed using the DAKO PT LINK 200 system with low pH citrate buffer. Tissue permeabilization was achieved using 0.3% Triton X-100 in 1× PBS for 30 minutes at room temperature. To block nonspecific binding, tissue sections were incubated with a blocking solution containing 3% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich Corp., St. Louis, MO, USA; Merck, Kenilworth, NJ, USA), 10% goat serum (CST-5425S; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), and 0.1% Tween-20 in PBS (PBS-T). Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies, followed by washing and two hours’ incubation at room temperature with appropriate secondary antibodies. Antibodies were diluted in a blocking solution (1.5% BSA, 5% goat serum, and 0.1% Tween-20 in PBS). All washing steps included sections being washed three times for five minutes each in PBS-T. Hoechst 33342 (1 mg/mL, 1:1000; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) or DAPI combined with SlowFade Diamond Antifade mountant (15451244; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used for nuclear staining and mounting. Hematoxylin and eosin-stained corneas were mounted with Eukitt quick-hardening mounting medium (Sigma-Aldrich, Merck; 03989). For consistency, images were acquired from the central cornea within a 1 mm region encompassing the wound site, with additional limbal images taken for epithelial stem/progenitor markers. For whole-mount histopathology, full corneas were imaged to assess vascularization (CD31) and lymphangiogenesis (LYVE-1). A comprehensive list of all antibodies used in the study is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, Inc., White Plains, NY, USA) or the Olympus Slideview VS200 system (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical Analysis

Comparisons of AAK grade across either time or between groups were made using GraphPad Prism v.8.3.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA) by performing a pairwise Mann-Whitney rank test. Linear regression was performed on the corneal thickness versus AAK Grade data, and Pearson's correlation analysis was performed. Two-tailed tests were conducted, with a P value of < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

Central Corneal Epithelial Wounding in Sey Mice Promotes AAK Progression

Epithelial removal was performed effectively in all mice using the AlgerBrush II (Fig. 2A). Slit lamp images confirmed successful central epithelial wounding with fluorescein uptake only within the injury site (Fig. 2B). Slit-lamp imaging revealed minimal changes in AAK Grade over four weeks in unwounded contralateral corneas of Sey mice, whereas the wounded corneas exhibited pronounced neovascularization following injury (Fig. 2C). A significant increase in AAK Grade from pre-wounding was observed at four weeks after wounding in Sey mice (P = 0.011), although no change in AAK Grade was detected in the contralateral corneas over the same period (P = 0.93) (Fig. 2D). The longitudinal AAK grade change in the same eye was significantly greater in wounded corneas from Sey mice (median grade change 0.75, range 0–3, P = 0.001) compared to unwounded contralateral corneas (median grade change 0, range 0–0.5). All wounded WT controls, by contrast, had restored epithelial integrity and a normal-appearing transparent cornea (Figs. 2E, 2F). Whole-mount staining at the four-week time point demonstrated progressive hemangiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis in wounded corneas from Sey mice. WT controls exhibited no signs of corneal vascularization or lymphatic vessel formation post-wounding, highlighting their effective wound-healing response (Fig. 3).

Central Corneal Thickness as a Quantitative Indicator of AAK Severity

OCT imaging revealed variability in central corneal thickness in wounded and unwounded contralateral Sey groups (Fig. 4A). Correlation analysis demonstrated a strong association between changes in AAK grade and corneal thickness in wounded (Pearson P = 0.031) and unwounded (P = 0.0006) corneas of Sey mice (Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that, regardless of wounding status, central corneal thickness positively correlates with the severity of AAK, with thicker corneas corresponding to higher disease grades and vice versa. Sey mice with AAK Grade 2 or higher had corneas that were, on average, 73.1 % thicker (P < 0.0001) than those with AAK below Grade 2 (Figs. 4A, 4C).

Central corneal thickness (CCT) correlates with AAK severity in Sey mice. (A, C) Central corneal thickness was significantly increased in AAK Grade ≥2, exhibiting a 73.1% increase relative to Grade <2 (P < 0.0001). (B) A strong positive association between CCT and AAK grade was observed in both wounded (P = 0.031) and unwounded corneas (P = 0.0006) in Sey mice (n = 34), indicating that increased corneal thickness is highly correlated with disease progression. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.

Persistent Epithelial Disruption in Sey Corneas After Central Epithelial Removal

Histology images of corneas four weeks after injury revealed distinct epithelial healing patterns between Sey and WT mice. In wounded corneas from Sey mice, further epithelial thinning and disorganized stratification of cell layers were observed. The incomplete recovery of transparency noted in slit lamp images was consistent with cell infiltration into the stroma observed by histology. By contrast, wounded WT corneas exhibited complete epithelial restoration and full transparency in slit lamp images, which in histology corresponded to maintenance of multiple well-defined epithelial layers and normal stromal cells (Fig. 5). Immunohistochemical analysis at the experimental endpoint confirmed differences in epithelial integrity, differentiation, and homeostasis (Fig. 5). PAX6 was consistently expressed across unwounded WT central corneal epithelial layers and was found to be expressed more basally after wounding. Similarly, PAX6 expression in the corneas of Sey mice decreased following wounding, particularly in the superficial layer. Corneal epithelial-specific cytokeratin-12 (CK12) was expressed across all epithelial layers in WT corneas but was absent in superficial cells of Sey corneas, with almost complete suppression after wounding. No conjunctival MUC5AC expression was detected in either unwounded or wounded WT corneas. By contrast, unwounded contralateral corneas of Sey mice showed slight MUC5AC expression in the epithelium at low AAK Grades, which increased after wounding and shifted toward the superficial layers (Fig. 5A). SOX9, a nuclear marker for limbal stem/progenitor cells, was expressed in the limbal but not central epithelium in the unwounded WT cornea, with limbal expression reduced after wounding in the WT central cornea. In Sey mice, SOX9 was not expressed in the central cornea and very weakly in the limbus in the unwounded state but became slightly activated in the limbal epithelium following wounding (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Fig. S2). CK15, a limbal/conjunctival stem cell marker, was expressed in the corneal limbus in all groups but in the central cornea only in Sey mice after wounding (Fig. 5B).

Chronic Inflammation and Impaired Wound Healing After Wounding

Immunostaining for inflammatory markers revealed low levels of IL-6 and IL-1β expression in the corneas of WT mice after wounding, with IL-6 primarily localized to the basal epithelium. In Sey mice, both IL-6 and IL-1β were already present in the unwounded cornea, and their expression intensified throughout the epithelium following wounding (Fig. 6). Macrophage marker F4/80, although absent in the WT cornea, was present in the unwounded cornea of Sey mice and was more prominent after wounding, indicating the presence of central stromal macrophages. Ki67-positive proliferating cells were scarcely detected in the corneal epithelium of uninjured Sey and WT mice. After injury, Sey corneas exhibited a marked increase in epithelial cell proliferation, primarily localized to the basal layer, indicating active regeneration. In contrast, injured WT corneas exhibited a very low level of Ki67 expression four weeks after wounding (Fig. 6).

Discussion

There is a great need for therapies to delay or halt the progression of AAK, which is the most vision-limiting aspect of congenital aniridia. The typical slow development of AAK before the emergence of phenotypic progression in early adulthood represents a therapeutic window,13 where changes to the corneal and limbal stem cell environment may still be reversible. Testing new therapies for AAK in a clinical setting, however, is challenging, given its progression on the time frame of decades. Mouse models that faithfully mimic the development of AAK, with its slow progression from a clear cornea to a fully vascularized state, are therefore invaluable.7,14 However, even mouse models exhibit slow disease progression over several months, making drug optimization and administration studies challenging. This underscores the need for accelerated and optimized disease models to support therapeutic development. Furthermore, clinical data have shown that invasive ocular procedures, such as cataract or glaucoma surgeries, often exacerbate corneal pathology and correlate with higher AAK stages after surgery.15,16 In this context, our study demonstrates that even a mild central corneal epithelial injury can significantly accelerate the progression of AAK in the Pax6+/− (Sey) mouse model.

Our method of epithelial wounding involves using the Algerbrush to gently remove the corneal epithelium. This technique causes minimal disruption to the underlying stroma compared to chemical methods and presents a valuable approach for preclinical drug testing.17,18 Remarkably, despite the superficial nature of the injury, the response triggered a robust inflammatory and wound-healing response in wounded corneas of Sey mice. Furthermore, a significant correlation between central corneal thickness and AAK grade was found, consistent with previous observations in human patients.6 Importantly, central corneal thickness may serve as a reliable indicator of AAK progression and severity, given its strong correlation with disease grade. The conjunctival pannus that invades the cornea in advanced stages of AAK is a thick fibrovascular tissue whose ingrowth may also trigger vascular ingrowth into the corneal stroma, vascular leakage, corneal edema, and lymphangiogenesis, as seen previously in patients with aniridia.12,19 Our targeted disruption of the corneal epithelium, which may have amplified pathological thickening and inflammatory remodeling, underscores that epithelial integrity may be a more important factor in AAK progression than previously recognized.

In our model, central corneal epithelial debridement revealed key molecular dysregulations at the protein level underlying impaired wound healing in Sey mice in comparison to WT mice. PAX6, a transcription factor essential for corneal epithelial identity, directly regulates CK12 expression.9 In the present study, PAX6 expression was absent from the superficial epithelium in both WT and Sey corneas after injury, suggesting a wound-induced shift toward basal expression likely associated with regeneration and progenitor cell activation.20 Furthermore, CK12 expression was moderately reduced in WT mice after injury but was markedly diminished in the corneas of wounded Sey mice, indicating compromised epithelial integrity and a loss of differentiated corneal phenotype. Previous studies have shown that Pax6+/− mice exhibit increased epithelial cell loss9 and lack proper epithelial cell morphology, collagen-IV production, and an epithelial basement membrane,7 suggesting a fragile epithelium in the PAX6-deficient cornea. This fragility is attributed to the combined disruption of cytokeratin expression, cell adhesion mechanisms, and glycoconjugate integrity, all of which impair epithelial cell migration during wound healing.21,22 Notably, corneas in Sey mice with post-injury AAK Grades 1.5 and 2 were included in immunostaining analysis. Given this, previous studies have shown CK12 expression may vary depending on the AAK severity;23 its retained expression in unwounded contralateral corneas likely reflects the relatively mild disease state in some of the samples examined.

After corneal wounding, the corneal epithelium in Sey mice more strongly expressed MUC5AC, a goblet cell marker known to be upregulated after injury.24 This suggests increased conjunctivalization and a shift in epithelial identity,25 similarly seen in aniridia patients12,26 and likely contributing to epithelial barrier disruption and disease progression. By contrast, wounded WT mice lacked MUC5AC expression, indicating preserved corneal epithelial identity without conjunctivalization. CK15, a limbal/conjunctival stem and epithelial cell marker, was expressed only in the limbus27,28 in WT mice and in the central cornea only in wounded Sey mice, providing further evidence of a shift to conjunctival epithelial phenotype with AAK progression after wounding.

CK15 has been reported to be regulated by SOX9,29 which is highly expressed in limbal stem cells, helping maintain their stem/progenitor state.29–31 As these cells divide, migrate toward the central cornea, and transition into transient amplifying cells, SOX9 expression progressively declines.31 Our results show very low levels of SOX9 in the limbus of unwounded Sey mice that are increased after wounding, indicating a propensity for limbal stem/progenitor cell activation in AAK progression, but this is aberrant, producing conjunctival-like cells expressing MUC5AC and CK15 instead of CK12+MUC5AC- CK15- central corneal epithelial cells.

Wounding in the Sey mice exacerbated the preexisting inflammatory state in the cornea. In the unwounded contralateral corneas of Sey mice, IL-6, IL-1β, and macrophages confirmed the pre-existing inflammation, which worsened after wounding. Strong expression of IL-6 and IL-1β in the epithelial and stromal cells coincided with the increased infiltration of F4/80+ macrophages, particularly in the stroma, further reinforcing immune activation. Notably, macrophages, a well-known source of both IL-6 and IL-1β,32 are known to populate the stroma in this Sey model.

The role of IL-1β in the corneal epithelium during wound healing beyond four weeks after a noninvasive injury remains unexplored. Notably, sustained IL-1β expression has been reported at the four-week time point in models of more severe corneal injury, such as alkali-induced damage.33 Our results suggest that IL-1β, which is known to induce early myofibroblast apoptosis following corneal epithelial healing to restore transparency, may continue to play a role at four weeks after injury, although at a low level.34–36 Ki67 expression was minimal but detectable in the corneal epithelium of both unwounded WT controls and contralateral corneas of Sey mice. These observations align with previous findings that show extremely low but consistent Ki67 expression in the basal epithelial layer of the healthy WT cornea.37,38 After injury, Ki67 expression was elevated primarily in the corneas of Sey mice, indicating an ongoing and uncontrolled wound healing response,7 which is closely related to the inflammatory and neovascularization response, and the related corneal thickness increase observed with AAK progression.

In conclusion, we establish an efficient accelerated model of AAK progression in Pax6+/− mice triggered by a controlled corneal injury, leading to uncontrolled wound healing, epithelial remodeling, inflammation, and neovascularization. The findings underscore the importance of maintenance of corneal epithelial integrity in mitigating AAK progression. The superficial central wounding model, moreover, presents a valuable tool for rapid experimental testing of interventions aimed at stabilizing the epithelium and delaying or preventing AAK progression in PAX6-related aniridia.
