Understanding donkey value chain in Kenya: mapping, governance, challenges, and equity
Authors: James Mutiiria Kithuka, Timothy Muthui Wachira, Maurice Karani Murungi, Joshua Orungo Onono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDonkeys (Equus asinus africanus) play a critical role in rural and peri-urban livelihoods in Kenya, supporting transportation, agriculture, and income generation. Despite their economic and social importance, donkeys remain underrepresented in national policies and development strategies. Limited data exist on the governance structures, operational challenges, and opportunities for upgrading the donkey value chain. This study aimed to map the donkey value chain, assess governance frameworks, identify constraints, and explore upgrading opportunities across diverse Kenyan production systems. A cross-sectional study was conducted in seven purposively selected counties—Bungoma, Kitui, Nairobi, Kiambu, Narok, Turkana, and Nakuru—to capture variation across agro-ecological zones and rural-urban divides. Data were collected through Key Informant Interviews and Focus Group Discussions with government officials, donkey owners, transporters, and other value chain actors. Thematic analysis was performed using Taguette software, while quantitative data were summarised to show key value chain nodes and trends. Findings revealed that the donkey value chain is largely informal, with minimal regulatory oversight and fragmented markets. Inputs such as, veterinary services, and feed were accessed primarily through informal networks, with market dynamics influenced by seasonal labour demands. Donkeys were predominantly used for transporting farm produce, firewood, water, and waste, while their manure was used for agricultural fertilization and pest control. Donkey milk, though not widely consumed, was used in some communities for treating respiratory ailments such as asthma and tuberculosis. In Turkana County, donkey meat consumption persisted as a cultural practice, contrasting with other regions where donkeys were primarily working animals. Weak governance structures facilitated illegal donkey trade and theft, contributing to a rapid decline in donkey populations, particularly following the establishment and subsequent closure of donkey abattoirs that fuelled demand for skin. Sustaining the donkey sector requires strengthening regulatory frameworks to enhance animal welfare, control illegal trade, and improve service delivery. Formal integration of donkeys into national livestock policies is crucial for unlocking investment in donkey-related research, improving access to veterinary care and quality inputs, and scaling up the productive and medicinal use of donkey-derived outputs such as milk and manure.Keywords: Donkey, Value chain, Animal welfare, Governance, Policy

Introduction

The domesticated donkey (Equus asinus africanus) is considered an equid under the order of odd-toed ungulates and has historically been used to facilitate trade due to its traction and durability. The donkey is one of the key working animals supporting livelihoods across various communities, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions of Kenya. Donkeys are primarily used for transport, especially in areas with poor infrastructure, and are vital for fetching water, and firewood, and transporting farm produce. They also contribute indirectly to agricultural productivity through manure and income generation. The increased use of donkeys for milk and meat poses public health concerns. The increased demand for the donkey skin threatens the donkey population, with a possibility of an entire species decimation (Salari et al. 2020, Aroua et al. 2024, Zhang et al. 2024).

Donkey-related data with respect to demography and use is limited as equids are not considered as important as other domestic livestock (Zhang et al. 2024). Nevertheless, the global data trend indicates a 19% population increase from 1997 to 50,451,887 donkeys by 2018. However, the global mule population has decreased by 53% to 8,522,982 (Norris et al. 2021). Ethiopia, with 8,542,747 donkeys, has overtaken China as the global leader in donkey population. Three countries within Africa, namely Sudan, Chad, and Zimbabwe, have recorded the highest donkey and mule population growth (> 80%) (Norris et al. 2021).

There exists country and region-level differences in the donkey population trends, and many socio-economic dynamics are involved. Agricultural mechanisation has limited the use of donkeys in developed countries. However, in countries such as Brazil, despite robust industrialisation, there’s still significant reliance on smallholder producers for food security (Graeub et al. 2016). Significant population decrease has however been observed in different countries following the increased demand for donkey skin used to produce eijao. Eijao is a traditional Chinese medicine whose history is more than 5000 years, and the demand is on the rise as it is a luxury product that is associated with upper middle class (Mainka and Mills 1995). Population increase in other countries has been attributed to the demand for construction materials and mining activities in Low- and Middle-income countries or deliberate breeding programs to satisfy the demand for eijao (Sanctuary 2017, Ozturkoglu-Budak et al. 2021).

Kenya does not report donkey statistics to the FAO (Norris et al. 2021), but the Kenya National Bureau of Statistics (KNBS) in 2019 reported the donkey population to be 1,176,374, reflecting a decrease from 1,832,519 in 2009. Before the establishment of donkey abattoirs in 2016, the donkey population growth was estimated at 1.04%. However, following the establishment of four donkey abattoirs between April 2016 and November 2018, a total of 301,977 donkeys were slaughtered − 15.4% of the total donkey population (KNBS 2019, Karanja–Lumumba et al. 2019). Donkey farming to meet this demand is unsustainable due to their low fertility, long reproduction cycles, low welfare awareness, poor practices of care and management, and illegal utilisation such as within country illegal slaughter and cross-border smuggling (Sanctuary 2017).

Donkeys in Kenya serve as silent yet vital contributors to community well-being and environmental health, operating beyond their roles in transportation and agriculture (Valette 2015). However, the health and welfare of donkeys in Kenya often remain overlooked, with implications for both the animals’ well-being and the communities and environments they inhabit (McLean and Gonzalez 2018). Donkeys in Kenya, in addition to providing draught power, are considered food animals and donkey meat, although unpopular, is consumed in some parts of Kenya. Ethnographic studies have reported that among the Maasai community in Kenya, warm freshly milked donkey milk is traditionally given to children suffering from severe coughs or pneumonia (Fernando et al. 2000, Rono et al. 2018, Kenya 1977). Donkey milk has historically been considered a therapeutic food in both Eastern and Western cultures, with early descriptions of its potential medicinal properties attributed to Hippocrates (460–370 BC (Adams 1886). Owing to mechanization and limited use of donkeys for their draught power, most studies are evaluating alternative uses of the donkey. Donkey milk for instance is drawing traction following its reported medicinal properties. One study reveals that donkey milk-derived lysozyme and lactoferrin demonstrate unique ability to be used as food and food product preservatives (Ozturkoglu-Budak et al. 2021). Donkeys have also been slaughtered for their meat, with literature citing different benefits of the same (Aroua et al. 2024, Zhang et al. 2024, Hassan et al. 2022). There are however glaring welfare gaps especially during donkey slaughter in countries such as Ghana where stunning is not conventional practice, subjecting donkeys to extreme pain and suffering (Fletcher et al. 2024).

A value chain is a sequence of related enterprises conducting activities to add value to a product from its primary production, through its processing and marketing to the final sale of the product to consumers. The functions of each link in the chain involve sourcing inputs, making/producing, and then delivering/selling products to the next link in the chain (Macfadyen et al. 2012). There exists literature on value chain mapping for other livestock/ livestock products (beef, dairy, small ruminants, poultry, and pigs) that provides qualitative and quantitative insights through a fragmented approach that describes animal/animal product sourcing, stakeholder interaction, governance and operational challenges, food safety concerns, business flow, operation, and estimated market share (Kiambi et al. 2020, Carron et al. 2017, Onono et al. 2018, Alarcon et al. 2017, Murungi et al. 2021). There is however scanty to no data describing the donkey value chain in Kenya.

While studies on donkey welfare and socio-economic roles are emerging, there remains limited research on the broader donkey value chain, particularly in terms of governance systems, challenges, and opportunities for upgrading. The lack of comprehensive analysis leaves a significant knowledge gap and excludes donkeys from national economic planning agendas. Furthermore, the sector faces governance challenges, weak institutional support, and significant upgrading constraints that hinder efficiency and sustainability. This study addresses these gaps by mapping the donkey value chain in selected counties in Kenya, assessing governance structures, and identifying opportunities for upgrading, while examining sourcing patterns, utilisation practices, and gender dynamics associated with donkey keeping.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study area comprised seven Kenyan counties purposively selected to capture variations in donkey ownership patterns and to ensure representation of the main agro-ecological zones, including both rural and urban areas where donkeys are kept.The study sites confer ethnic diversity, reflect significant donkey presence and hence activities, and different weather patterns that determine donkey usage. These counties include Bungoma, Kitui, Nairobi, Kiambu, Narok, Turkana and Nakuru and are as shown in Fig. 1. Other counties were excluded as selected counties were either already representative or there were little to no donkey populations.

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted to map the donkey value chain, assess governance structures, and determine the operational challenges, and opportunities for upgrading in key counties in Kenya. The study participants were drawn from diverse stakeholder groups across the donkey value chain. These included donkey owners, donkey users, commercial donkey operators, donkey breeders, donkey traders, donkey transporters, donkey service consumers, veterinary paraprofessionals, agrovet operators, farriers (equine hoof care specialists), abattoir owners, and abattoir workers. These groups participated in Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) conducted across the study counties. Key Informant Interviews (KIIs) were conducted with a diverse group of institutional and administrative stakeholders, including county and sub-county veterinary officers, deputy county directors of veterinary services, veterinary paraprofessionals, chiefs, representatives from the Association of Donkey Owners in Kenya (ADOK), NGO staff (such as those from Farming Systems Kenya and KSPCA), and the chairperson of a donkey users’ group. The tool used to guide these interviews is provided in Appendix 1.

The study participants were recruited with the help of the respective county governments and NGOs working with donkeys and domiciled in the different counties, who contributed invaluably in kind. These organisations include Caritas-Kitui County, Life Skill Promoters-Kiambu and Nairobi Counties, Farming Systems Kenya-Narok and Nakuru Counties, Ripple Effect- Bungoma County and APaD Kenya-Turkana County. The interviews were led by the lead researcher (JMK) and supported by (MKM) both veterinarians and researchers. The KII lasted on average 40 min while the FGD’s lasted an average of 90 min. Participants were provided with a modest reimbursement to compensate for their time and transport costs.

Data collection

A mixed-method approach was used to gather both qualitative and quantitative data on the donkey value chain. The primary data collection methods included Key Informant Interviews (KIIs) where semi-structured interviews were conducted with government officials (e.g., veterinary officers, area chiefs and representatives from NGOs engaged in donkey welfare and advocacy. The interviews focused on eliciting information on donkey sourcing, product movement and use of trade networks, health management, regulatory enforcement, and market dynamics and challenges. The specific research questions guiding data collection across the donkey value chain are outlined in Appendix 2.

Secondly, we conducted a total of 13 focus group discussions (FGDs) with various value chain actors (Table 1), including donkey owners, transporters, and slaughterhouse workers (where applicable). The discussions explored perceptions on governance, market access, welfare issues, challenges, and potential interventions. Before FGD and KII sessions, consent was obtained after participants read (or were read to) the consent form, which detailed the research objectives, collaborating institutions, and data privacy guarantees. All interviews were conducted in either English or Kiswahili. In one focus group discussion (FGD) with donkey owners in Turkana, where neither language was suitable, a translator was engaged to facilitate communication. During the FGDs, participants were encouraged to actively share their views. In cases where differing opinions arose, participants were given additional time to deliberate and reach a consensus. Where consensus could not be achieved, the number of participants supporting and opposing a given view was recorded. FGDs and KIIs were conducted in venues familiar and accessible to participants, including community halls, church compounds, and open spaces near chief offices, depending on the county and local context.

Data analysis

We analysed qualitative data using thematic analysis where FGDs and KIIs were transcribed and analysed using Taguette software (Taguette, v 1.4.1 2023) to identify common themes related to governance, market operations, health management, and challenges. Key themes were categorised into governance structures, value chain operations, challenges, and upgrading opportunities. The relationships between stakeholders, governance actors, and regulatory enforcement were mapped to understand power dynamics and decision-making structures. Recorded contents were translated verbatim. These transcriptions were transferred to Taguette software for further thematic analysis. Proportions (%) were derived by counting the number of participants reporting that operation in each FGD or KII, then aggregated to determine the overall proportion for each theme per county.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approvals were obtained from the University of Nairobi BAUEC 2024/549 and NACOSTI/P/24/36,654. The study adhered to Data Protection and Privacy Laws as described in the Kenya Data Protection Act of 2019. County-level approvals were obtained from the Ministry of Agriculture and Livestock Development while local administrative authorities were notified of the ongoing study. Informed consent was obtained from all the study participants who were also guaranteed anonymity throughout data analysis and publication of findings - the report includes aggregated data.

Results

Mapping & donkey sourcing

A total of 138 participants (92 males and 46 females) took part in the focus group discussions conducted across seven counties, namely Bungoma, Kiambu, Kitui, Nairobi, Nakuru, Narok, and Turkana. On average, 11 participants were engaged in each of the 13- focus group discussion. In addition to the FGDs, 21 Key Informant Interviews (KIIs) were conducted, involving 23 respondents (15 males and 8 females). A summary of the number of FGDs conducted, as well as the demographic and institutional profiles of both FGD participants and KII respondents across counties, is presented in Table 1.

Donkey ownership was found to be influenced by several factors, including the availability of land, household economic status, cultural practices, and the utility needs of the families. Integrated livestock systems were common among participants, with many donkey owners also keeping cattle, goats, sheep, or poultry.

Donkeys were acquired through a range of sourcing pathways across the study counties. Informal livestock markets remained the predominant source of donkeys, accounting for between 50% and 100% of acquisitions depending on the county. In Bungoma, 50% of respondents obtained their donkeys through purchases, 31.3% through dowry exchanges, and 18.8% through gifts. In Narok, gifting accounted for 25% of donkey acquisitions, while in Turkana, gifts contributed 17.8%. Dowry exchanges were a notable acquisition pathway in Turkana and Narok, contributing 20% and 12.5% respectively, reflecting the cultural significance attached to donkeys. In Kitui, 36.8% of donkeys were sourced through charitable organizations such as Caritas-Kitui and the Vincent Munywoki Foundation. Additionally, barter trade was reported in Turkana, where 13.3% of donkeys were exchanged for livestock or traditional valuables, such as goats, cattle, or beaded ornaments.

The distribution of donkey acquisition methods across the study counties is illustrated in Fig. 2. In urbanized settings, particularly Nairobi, donkey acquisition was more dynamic, with all respondents (100%) relying on purchases to meet the fluctuating demand associated with their livelihoods. Concerns about purchasing stolen donkeys were frequently mentioned by urban respondents, underscoring the informal and sometimes precarious nature of the urban donkey trade.

The number of donkeys owned per household varied considerably across the study sites. In peri-urban and urban areas like Nairobi, most households kept between one and two donkeys. In contrast, pastoralist regions such as Turkana South reported household donkey herds as large as twenty animals.

Socio-economic & cultural significance of the donkey

Donkeys play a multifunctional role in supporting household livelihoods across diverse Kenyan contexts. The most commonly cited function was transportation, with respondents across the study counties reporting donkeys as critical in hauling farm produce, firewood, water, garbage (particularly in urban areas), and construction materials. Migration-related uses, such as carrying children, the elderly, small stock, and household goods during dry seasons, were also noted. Transport-related uses were reported by the majority of respondents, ranging from 11.8% in Bungoma to 55.6% in Narok County.

In addition to their transport role, donkeys contributed to security, with owners highlighting the donkey’s braying behavior as an alarm against intruders. This use was particularly noted in Bungoma, where 5.5% of respondents mentioned security functions. Donkeys were also reported to assist in agricultural production, primarily through ploughing fields, although this use was less common and mostly confined to rural areas such as Kitui and Bungoma counties.

Manure production emerged as another important utility. Donkey dung was used by respondents for agricultural fertilisation, pest control, briquette-making, and even fish feed. In Nakuru and Bungoma counties, manure use was cited by between 5% and 10% of participants. In Bungoma, there was a unanimity among donkey owners, on the superiority for donkey manure compared to cow manure, this perception was varied in Nakuru county where 50% of participant disputed this assertion. This disconnect would suggest an opportunity for research on the efficacy of donkey dung as a manure and provide evidence. A cultural misconception associating donkey dung with tetanus was reported in some areas, leading certain residents to restrict donkeys from grazing on their land.

Although donkey milk is not legally recognized under the Kenya Dairy Industry Act, it is consumed in select communities for its perceived medicinal benefits, particularly in managing respiratory conditions such as asthma and tuberculosis. Consumption of donkey milk was reported in Bungoma and Turkana counties, albeit at low levels (less than 5% of respondents). Orphaned infants and elderly individuals were identified as key consumers, and some healthcare practitioners reportedly prescribed donkey milk for medicinal purposes. Nevertheless, cultural beliefs and the difficulty in milking aggressive donkeys limit its widespread adoption. In many areas, it is believed that donkey milk should only be consumed by foals.

The acceptance of donkey meat followed a similar pattern. Despite being legal under the Meat Control Act Cap 356, donkey meat consumption remains culturally sensitive and is largely avoided in many counties. However, in Turkana, donkey meat is a popular delicacy, especially during traditional weddings, first-born daughter marriages, and elder ceremonies. Over half (55.7%) of respondents in Turkana reported consuming donkey meat. Slaughtering of sick or old donkeys for food was common practice, and there was a belief that donkey meat is most flavorful within 24 h post-slaughter. In contrast, in urban centers such as Nairobi and Nakuru, donkey meat consumption remains rare and is often stigmatized.

Income generation through donkey-based activities was also highlighted. Donkeys are commonly hired out for income-generating transport services, including water vending, haulage services, and general transport tasks such as ferrying people or construction materials, thereby providing households with additional income streams. This was particularly notable in Bungoma and Nairobi counties. Donkey ownership thus plays an important role in enhancing household resilience and supporting small-scale enterprises.

Culturally, donkeys hold significant value in rituals and social transactions. They are included in dowry payments and are ritually buried in some communities as a sign of gratitude for their service. In parts of Western Kenya, it is customary to bury donkeys with coins — amounts ranging from 10 Kenyan cents to 50 Kenyan shillings — symbolizing the wealth generated by the animal during its lifetime. Ethnoveterinary practices were also reported, with some owners using pumpkins as natural anthelmintics for their donkeys.

In urban areas such as Nairobi, donkey ownership presents a different dynamic. Donkeys are largely exploited for their commercial value, particularly in water vending and market deliveries. However, donkey operators in urban settings often face stigma and discrimination, with donkeys perceived as disruptive in affluent neighborhoods. Additionally, donkey meat and milk consumption remain largely unpopular in urban areas, except in cases where products are misrepresented by unscrupulous vendors.

Feeding, watering, & welfare practices

The management of donkeys follows a structured daily routine across the counties surveyed. Owners typically conduct morning health inspections, checking hooves for debris and wounds to prevent lameness before the animals commence their day’s work. Donkeys generally work between four to six hours daily, predominantly in the morning, after which they are rested and fed. However, in some areas such as Nairobi and Kitui, donkeys were reported to work from as early as 5:00 a.m. until late evening, suggesting risks of overwork. One male respondent in Nairobi noted, “Our donkeys leave with us at sunrise and return only when the sun has set; they hardly rest.”

Feeding practices vary with the agro-ecological setting. In rural areas like Nakuru, Kitui, and Narok, donkeys primarily rely on natural grazing, often supplemented with crop residues such as maize stover, pigeon pea and green gram pods, bean haulms, and banana peels. During dry seasons when forage becomes scarce, owners turn to market waste, household scraps, and in some cases, food collected from garbage dumps. “When the fields are bare, we feed them what we can — vegetable scraps from the market,” quoted a participant from Nakuru.

The use of commercial feeds was reported only occasionally due to the prohibitive costs. Some owners mentioned allowing ad libitum feeding, providing donkeys continuous access to food at night to recuperate from the day’s labour.

Watering practices also demonstrate regional variation. Donkeys typically access water from boreholes, rivers, or communal watering points. However, seasonal water shortages were a major constraint in Kitui and Turkana counties, forcing donkeys to walk long distances for water, sometimes only receiving water once a day. In Turkana, it was noted that donkeys were allowed to graze freely for several days and had developed the ability to intuitively locate and return to water points when necessary. “Even if we do not guide them, they find the water points themselves,” quoted a Turkana participant.

Shelter practices varied by environment. In rural counties, donkeys were commonly tethered under shaded trees or housed adjacent to cattle sheds, with mixed housing considered beneficial for security and animal companionship. Conversely, in urban areas such as Nairobi, donkeys were more often tethered near marketplaces or left to roam freely at night due to lack of formal shelters.

Veterinary care for donkeys remains limited across all counties. Many owners prefer traditional herbal remedies over conventional veterinary care due to cost and service availability. Common ailments cited included body and hoof lesions, bloat (colic), tetanus, helminthiasis, trypanosomiasis, and occasional cases of alopecia and abscessation. Some owners admitted delaying veterinary treatment until the donkey could no longer work. In Kiambu County, where donkeys are more commercially valued, owners showed higher willingness to seek veterinary care. Nevertheless, veterinary services are often prohibitively expensive or unavailable. A female participant from Kiambu lamented, “It’s easier to get a vet for my cow than for my donkey.”

Farriers play a critical role in maintaining donkey mobility by providing hoof care, although they typically operate informally and without standardized certification. During farm visits, farriers charge KES 250 to 300 (approximately USD 1.92–2.30) per donkey, but on community donkey clinic days, the fee can drop to KES 100 (USD 0.77). Despite their recognized importance, many farriers engage in donkey care only as a secondary occupation, limiting their availability and professionalism.

Animal welfare challenges emerged consistently across the study sites. Donkeys suffer from overwork, poor nutrition, and untreated injuries. Bestiality was mentioned as a serious but isolated welfare concern, particularly in Kitui County. Concerns over donkey theft and illegal slaughter were widespread, especially in Kitui, Kiambu, and Turkana. In Turkana, while donkey slaughter is culturally accepted during certain traditional ceremonies, most respondents indicated that illegal slaughter also occurs, often carried out at night without veterinary inspection. As one participant explained, “They slaughter them at night, with no inspection, you just have to trust the meat is clean” (male participant, Turkana). In Turkana, the market price for donkey meat was reported at approximately KES 200 (USD 1.54) per kilogram.

Urban donkeys face additional welfare risks. Donkey theft remains a significant threat in Nairobi and Nakuru, often driven by the clandestine trade in donkey skins and meat. Stolen donkeys are usually transported to other counties for illegal slaughter. Furthermore, donkeys in urban environments frequently encounter road accidents and injuries, and there is a lack of infrastructure to facilitate their safe movement. The cost of maintaining donkeys in urban settings is high, due to the expense of feed, water, and cart repairs. Donkey owners reported social discrimination, particularly in affluent neighborhoods, where donkeys are considered a nuisance, and rental housing often prohibits tenants from keeping them. Additionally, donkey handlers in urban areas frequently face harassment from law enforcement officers for operating in restricted zones.

Despite these challenges, examples of improved welfare practices were noted. In Nakuru, community awareness campaigns promoted the adoption of better-designed carts to reduce strain on donkeys and adjustments in feeding practices to improve nutrition. In Kitui, some community members advocated for stricter enforcement against donkey theft, overworking, and illegal slaughter. However, as one participant quoted, “Advocating for donkey welfare here is not safe; some people don’t like being told what not to do.”

Policy, litigation, and governance gaps

The donkey sector in Kenya has faced longstanding governance challenges, particularly related to slaughter regulation and animal welfare enforcement. Although legal donkey slaughterhouses were initially sanctioned under the Meat Control Act (Cap 356) in 1999, their operations significantly depleted donkey populations, creating concerns about sustainability. Following intense advocacy and growing public outcry, the government issued Legal Notice No. 63 of 20th April 2020, officially banning donkey slaughter in Kenya. Despite this ban, illegal slaughter continues to persist, facilitated by weak enforcement mechanisms and under-resourced regulatory agencies.

In some counties, proactive control measures have been implemented to curb illegal trade. For instance, Kiambu County introduced Certificates of Transport (COTs) and carcass stamping to prevent the illegal movement and sale of donkey products disguised as beef. However, enforcement has been inconsistent, and loopholes remain within the regulatory framework. A male participant in Kiambu quoted, “The law lists the donkey as a meat animal (Legal Notice No. 146 of 26th August 1999), but communities do not embrace it.” This ambiguity further complicates regulatory efforts at the community level.

Disease surveillance systems are similarly underdeveloped. The veterinary departments across counties largely rely on private veterinary practitioners to report disease outbreaks, often through informal channels such as phone calls, veterinary pharmacies, and clinic visits. This fragmented approach limits the effectiveness of donkey disease monitoring and response, leaving major health issues unaddressed.

The donkey trade operates informally in most counties, with limited oversight on sales, movement, and welfare standards. The absence of structured donkey markets has resulted in fluctuating prices and vulnerability to theft. Formal identification and tracking systems are notably absent; unlike cattle, donkeys are not routinely branded or subjected to movement permits. For example, in Bungoma County, participants indicated that donkeys could easily be moved and sold without formal documentation. The lack of traceability has historically facilitated cases of donkey theft, particularly during periods of external demand linked to Chinese infrastructure projects.

In Kitui County, the donkey trade is technically legal, as donkeys are gazetted as meat animals nationally. However, regulatory weaknesses persist. The now-closed Kithyoko donkey slaughterhouse, established at the request of a Chinese company, initially pledged to slaughter only old or injured donkeys. In practice, the abattoir’s operations expanded rapidly, leading to increased theft and contributing to the depletion of donkey populations. Although the slaughterhouse was eventually closed, illegal slaughter persists in the county. Efforts to formalize donkey welfare through the Draft Donkey Welfare Policy for Kitui County have stalled, and no comprehensive legislation specifically protects donkeys as a unique working species.

Across the study counties, governance relating to donkeys falls under multiple legislative instruments: the Meat Control Act (Cap 356), the Animal Welfare Act (Cap 360), the Hide, Skin and Leather Trade Act (Cap 359), and the Dairy Industry Act (Cap 336). Notably, the latter does not legally recognize donkey milk under current regulations, although reforms under the pending Dairy Industry Bill (2024) may address this gap.

The depletion of donkey populations prior to the slaughterhouse closures was exacerbated by the species’ long gestation periods and slow foaling intervals, making herd recovery challenging. As one participant quoted, “Even if we stop slaughtering today, donkeys can’t replace themselves fast enough — it takes too long.”

In Turkana County, the donkey trade operates with partial formal recognition. While the trade is culturally accepted and known to government authorities, it lacks structured regulation and veterinary oversight. Traders interact with Animal Health Practitioners (AHPs) when seeking movement permits and not during meat inspections. Local administrators such as chiefs and sale yard officers sometimes assist by verifying ownership and issuing notification letters during sales. However, there are no licensed slaughter facilities, designated veterinary officers, or formal market infrastructure. These gaps in enforcement and governance allow unregulated slaughter and the continuation of illegal trade activities. Donkeys also remain largely excluded from formal county livestock development policies. In urban areas like Nairobi, legislation has further complicated donkey ownership. County by-laws restrict donkey movement within central business districts, pushing donkey operators into peri-urban zones and informal settlements. Additionally, the lack of designated resting areas, water points, and veterinary services further marginalizes donkey owners and compromises donkey welfare.

Role of NGOs and stakeholder interventions

Non-governmental organizations (NGOs) have played a critical role in addressing the governance gaps affecting the donkey sector in Kenya. Organizations such as Ripple Effect Kenya (formerly Send a Cow), Farming Systems Kenya (FSK), Kenya Society for the Protection and Care of Animals (KSPCA), African Network for Animal Welfare (ANAW), Association of Donkey Owners in Kenya (ADOK), and Brooke East Africa have actively supported donkey welfare initiatives across the study counties.

Advocacy by these organizations has been instrumental in raising awareness of donkey welfare issues, culminating in the government’s closure of all four legal donkey slaughterhouses, and contributing to the African Union Inter-African Bureau for Animal Resources (AU-IBAR) 15-year moratorium on donkey slaughter. NGO interventions have included the distribution of educational materials on donkey treatment and care protocols, the introduction of sack harnesses to reduce physical strain on working donkeys, and the provision of grass for feed improvement programs.

Sensitization efforts have focused on enhancing the perception of donkeys as valuable livelihood assets rather than low-status animals. In Kitui, an animal health officer quoted, “Without the NGOs, there would be no clinics or training for owners. Donkey owners would just rely on traditional ways, good or bad.”

NGOs have also worked to expand access to veterinary services by supporting the training of Animal Health Practitioners (AHPs) and facilitating the establishment of veterinary networks specializing in donkey care. They have organized regular donkey clinics offering services such as deworming, vaccination, and hoof trimming. Capacity-building programs for farriers have been implemented, including hands-on training in hoof examination, trimming, and balancing, often supplemented by the distribution of farriery toolkits.

In addition to improving service delivery, NGOs have collaborated with law enforcement agencies to tackle illegal donkey slaughter through multi-agency operations and community education campaigns. Rescue and rehabilitation programs have been established to care for donkeys rescued from abusive or illegal slaughter situations.

Despite these efforts, not all donkey owners are organized into cooperative groups, limiting the reach and impact of NGO interventions. While organized groups are more accessible to training and support, many owners remain isolated, reducing the overall effectiveness of capacity-building programs. As one community leader in Bungoma quoted, “It’s not that the NGOs don’t try — it’s that many owners don’t come together to benefit from the programs.”

Gender equity

Gender roles in donkey ownership and use varied across the study counties, although overall, donkey ownership remains predominantly male-dominated. In 57% of the counties (Bungoma, Narok, Nakuru, and Turkana), men were recognized as the primary owners and decision-makers regarding donkey use and management. Women’s participation was largely limited to supportive roles such as feeding, watering, and utilizing donkeys for household tasks.

In Kiambu and Nairobi counties, representing 28% of the study areas, there was a greater degree of gender sharing in donkey use. In Kiambu, while men were officially listed as owners, women were the primary handlers, using donkeys extensively for fetching water and transporting farm produce. Similarly, in Nairobi, ownership and daily management tasks were shared between men and women, with women frequently renting donkeys for water vending and market deliveries.

A notable exception was Kitui County, where women and children were reported to take primary responsibility for the management and use of donkeys, despite men holding official ownership titles. Kitui accounted for 14% of the study areas and demonstrated a more female-influenced management structure. A female participant from Kitui quoted, “In our homes, it is the women and children who take care of the donkeys — feeding them, watering them, using them for firewood collection.”

Participants highlighted that donkey care was considered a physically demanding task, often requiring strength and resilience. In counties with male-dominated ownership, cultural attitudes and perceptions of donkey handling being ‘men’s work’ contributed to limited female involvement. However, participants in Narok also acknowledged that women are often highly skilled handlers of donkeys and play a critical role in their day-to-day management, even when “formal” ownership remains with men. As one female participant from Narok quoted, “Even if a woman is strong, many feel shy or afraid to handle donkeys; it’s just not what they are expected to do.”

Nevertheless, women were still key beneficiaries of the income generated from donkey-related activities. Across all counties, when a donkey was sold, the proceeds were typically pooled at the household level, with women having a significant role in determining how the income was utilized — often prioritizing food, school fees, and household needs.

Youth involvement in donkey ownership was also noted in Kitui and Nairobi, where younger individuals were increasingly managing donkeys for commercial purposes, such as water vending and construction material transport, rather than purely domestic use. Men across several counties expressed a preference for male donkeys over females, citing their perceived greater strength and reliability for labor, with concerns that female donkeys’ productivity was affected by pregnancy and foaling cycles.

While strides have been made in some regions toward greater gender equity in donkey use and management, persistent cultural barriers and the physically demanding nature of donkey handling continue to limit women’s direct ownership and control over this critical livelihood asset.

Discussion

Donkey ownership in Kenya is primarily driven by economic status, utility needs, and cultural beliefs where most households keep 1–2 donkeys. Sourcing of these donkeys follow numerous channels including direct purchases from adjacent households, acquisition from donkey traders or open-air markets. Nguekeng et al. (2022) however reported a higher donkey ownership in Northern Cameroon (1–15 per household). The informal nature of this trade has precipitated pricing and supply chain inconsistencies (Fig. 2). Nonetheless, the cost of a donkey is primarily dependent on age, sex, training, perceived utility, and castration status—castrated jacks fetch more in Turkana. Donkey pricing also fluctuates based on demand, seasonal variations, and the physical condition of the animal. The average pricing for pre-pubertal and adult donkeys compares well with a previous study that documented donkey pricing in similar region to range between USD 7.70–153.85 with a median pricing of USD 76.92 (Carder et al. 2019).

Owning donkeys is primarily male-dominated in Kenya, but this doesn’t limit donkey use to women and other family members. A study by Nguekeng et al. (2022) conducted elsewhere also reports similar gender disparities in donkey ownership. However, donkey handling is perceived as labour-intensive and therefore, women tend to be less engaged. Further reasons contributing to inequality in donkey ownership and use include cultural attitudes, and shyness and fearing of donkeys. Notably, women are involved in determining the course of donkey proceeds in the household—daily income or income from donkey sales. The donkey is therefore critical in providing for low-income earners or as described by Maggs, Ainslie and Bennett (Maggs et al. 2021) a pathway out of ultra-poverty as most donkey owners reported that donkeys would contribute 30–60% of the household’s total earnings.

Donkey use has assumed different forms depending on the region-specific culture, geography, and other spacio-temporal factors. Just as the current study documents, the donkey has historically been used a beast of burden world over (Carder et al. 2019, Maggs et al. 2021, Gichure et al. 2020). The current findings depict donkey use beyond the provision of draught power. Donkey manure is preferred to domestic ruminant manure in agriculture and isolated cases, donkeys provide security—following its braying ability. Uniquely, the use of donkey dung as a pesticide has been reported in the current study but more studies are required to provide scientific insights.

This resourceful beast also serves a cultural significance following its use in dowry payments and when buried, money as low as USD 0.08 cents occupy it, symbolizing its economic contribution throughout its productive lifetime. With the advent of mechanization, donkey use for its draught power in some parts of the world is significantly diminished. In Italy for instance, some horse breeders rear donkeys for mule production. The female mules are in turn used to protect new-born foals as it is argued that their instincts are stronger than the dam’s (Smith et al. 2000).

The use of donkeys for soil and landscape maintenance has also been described as the survival of endangered plant and insect species is favoured by their selective feeding nature (Kugler 2013). Other studies have documented additional uses of donkeys. In France, Switzerland, and Belgium for instance, donkey use for tourism and leisure is becoming popular as the number of professional hirers is growing. Further, onotherapy—donkey-assisted therapy has emerged as a viable alternative to equestrian therapy (Borioni et al. 2012).

Donkey milk, although unpopular was reported to have medicinal properties against some respiratory diseases such as Tuberculosis. This finding is consistent with previous studies that reported that donkey milk is endowed with lysozyme (1402.50 u/mL of milk), bioactive peptides n3 fatty acids, lactoferrin, and casein that have antimicrobial activity against most bacteria. Lysozyme in particular has been shown to hydrolyse polysaccharides, aiding the inhibition of bacterial proliferation and is highest in donkeys aged > 15 years. The spectrum of activity has also been reported to extend to viruses, parasites and fungi (Martini et al. 2019, Massouras et al. 2020, Vincenzetti et al. 2005).

Donkey meat, although unpopular, was reported in Turkana County, and is used to mark different traditional rites such as family unions. It has been argued that donkey meat has better taste and nutritional value than mutton and pork. Donkey meat contains a high-protein diet that lowers liver fat through the reduction of fatty acid biosynthesis and the inhibition of fat absorption, positioning it as a viable alternative in individuals who aim to lose weight (Xu et al. 2020). Despite this, donkey meat uptake remains significantly low in Kenya and elsewhere. In Southern Africa for instance, cultural beliefs and taboos surrounding food are followed strictly. These taboos are in turn passed down to future generations and social groups (Chakona and Shackleton 2019). Donkey slaughter for meat has however proven unsustainable as donkey population growth remains low given the negative energy balance (NEB) stemming from stress, food deprivation, and diseases. NEB consequently impairs reproductive performance hence, low birth rates (Bekuma and Galmessa 2019).

The donkey value chain in Kenya is plagued by a myriad of governance issues and insufficient policies that inhibit growth. Donkey trade in Kenya mostly operates informally as there are no structured donkey markets with minimal oversight on pricing. Notably, donkeys are not subjected to movement permits and these informal settings have precipitated illegal donkey trade, slaughter and theft. This finding is also consistent with a previous study in Kenya that noted that the government slackened in in passing laws and policies that regulate donkey trade, specifically donkey hides (Carder et al. 2019). Similarly, Gichure and Oladiye (Gichure and Olayide 2024) noted that there exist over 25 laws regulating the animal resource industry in Kenya whence most of them are outdated and only partially compliant with the WOAH animal welfare standards.

Gichure and Oladiye (Gichure and Olayide 2024) note additional flaws with the Kenyan policy and legislation that are as follows; The prevention of cruelty to animals’ act CAP 360 doesn’t specifically mention the donkey. Other legislation regulating animal welfare such as the National Livestock Policy of 2008, Draft Veterinary Policy of 2015, and Wildlife Management and Welfare Policy of 2015 do not specifically consider the donkey as livestock since they are not food animals even though working equines are technically livestock. Several other weak linkages include weak institutional frameworks, devolution of veterinary services and animal welfare roles to county governments that remained under-resourced, hence, implementation gaps.

Gaps in policy and governance regarding donkey trade has escalated insecurity within this sub-sector following numerous cases of donkey theft and illegal slaughter. In the current study, one of the study areas (Bungoma), donkey theft reportedly surged due to a Chinese workstation that was temporarily established. Donkey theft significantly subsided after the workstation closed, suggesting their involvement in the donkey skin trade. This allegation can’t however be dismissed as donkeys are utilized to produce eijao in China. The Chinese economy has recently witnessed a robust growth leading to more disposable income for its nationals who can afford the luxury product, driving up the donkey hides demand (Mainka and Mills 1995, Gichure and Olayide 2024).

The current study also reveals numerous welfare gaps including being overworked, irregular feeding and watering patterns in select study areas. Donkeys are freely grazed in rural areas but in urban settings the population pressure has led to low acreage available for grazing, necessitating the adoption of poor-quality feed. Poor nutrition therefore impacts donkey welfare negatively as different physiological conditions—foal growth, gestation, and lactation require specific diets (Salari et al. 2020). It is possible that henceforth, land utility will feature activities with high return on investment such as crop agriculture or alternative livestock with relatively short generation intervals, and efficient feed conversion efficiency with minimal land pressure—dubbed ‘climate smart’ animals (Maigari et al. 2020). Donkey diseases are one of the numerous challenges facing donkey owners in Kenya. The common health challenges reported in the study such as habronemiasis, lameness, helminths, pneumonia, alopecia, and abscessation. Some of these challenges are largely precipitated by poor animal welfare where donkeys are subjected to harsh environmental conditions, poor harnesses that constantly inflict wounds, animal cruelty involving malicious whipping, dilapidated shelters, and negligence.

Donkeys are nonetheless more resilient compared to their domestic equine counterparts as they have well-developed predator evasion mechanisms such as masking pain (Ashley et al. 2005). The donkey’s ability to mask pain contributes to animal cruelty, reinforced by the fact that most owners hold the donkey with little to no regard or empathy as demonstrated in an Ethiopian study by Geiger et al. (2023) where some parts of the country equated women to donkeys to demean them. Some proverbs coined by some of the donkey-keeping communities in Ethiopia in the same study such as ‘women and donkeys never fear a stick; they are tolerant’ reflect the societal position of the donkey and women and often used to justify inhumane treatment.

Conclusion

This study highlights the integral role of donkeys in supporting livelihoods in Kenya through transport, agricultural labor, and income generation. Donkey acquisition largely occurs through informal markets and exchanges, with limited regulatory oversight. This informality contributes to challenges such as theft, unregulated slaughter, and public health risks associated with informal meat trade.

Despite their economic importance, donkeys face welfare challenges, including inadequate feeding, shelter, and veterinary care. Existing policy frameworks provide limited protection, and enforcement remains weak. Gender disparities persist, with ownership typically male-dominated, although women benefit from donkey services for household activities.

The findings suggest the need for improved regulation of the donkey trade, better enforcement of animal welfare standards, and formal integration of donkeys into livestock development policies. Strengthening collaborations between NGOs and government agencies could enhance awareness and promote best practices in donkey management.

Further research is needed to validate traditional practices such as the medicinal use of donkey milk, the application of donkey dung as a pesticide, and the use of pumpkin seeds as anthelmintics. Evidence from such studies could inform sustainable practices and policy development.

Overall, a holistic approach incorporating policy reforms, stakeholder engagement, and targeted research will be essential to ensure the sustainability of donkey populations and the livelihoods that depend on them.
