In vitro and in vivo activities of scutellarein, a novel polyphosphate kinase 1 inhibitor against Acinetobacter baumannii infection
Authors: Yuping Song, Hongfa Lv, Lei Xu, Zhiying Liu, Jianfeng Wang, Tianqi Fang, Xuming Deng, Yonglin Zhou, Dan Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInorganic polyphosphate (polyP)-targeted polyphosphate kinase 1 (PPK1) has attracted much attention by virtue of its importance in bacterial pathogenicity and persistence, as well as its exclusive presence in microorganisms. However, only very few drugs have been found to be efficacious in inhibiting the Acinetobacter baumannii (A. baumannii) PPK1 protein.ResultsIn this study, we identified Scutellarein (Scu), a potent PPK1 inhibitor that could significantly influence PPK1-regulated motility, biofilm formation, and bacterial persistence, which was further validated by the results of transcriptome analysis. Mechanistic explorations revealed that Scu achieved its enzyme inhibitory activity predominantly through direct engagement with the active center of PPK1. Moreover, the survival rate of Galleria mellonella larvae was increased by about 35% with 20 mg/kg of Scu treatment. The remarkable therapeutic benefits of Scu were also observed in the mouse pneumonia model, shown mainly by reduced bacterial colonization, pathological lesions, and inflammatory factors.ConclusionOur results revealed that Scu could attenuate the pathogenicity and persistence of A. baumannii by interfering with its important kinase PPK1.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12934-024-02540-9.

Background

Acinetobacter baumannii (A. baumannii), a notorious human pathogen, is considered an urgent threat to public health on account of its ability to provoke mortality rates of 26% and 43% in hospital settings and intensive care units (ICUs), respectively [1]. Undeniably, the knotty antibiotic resistance issue is the main culprit behind this crisis. Although A. baumannii has already been ranked on the priority pathogens list for new antibiotics by the World Health Organization (WHO), the development process for new antibiotics, including designing new structures and exploring new targets, is financially expensive and time-consuming, which has placed urgent anti-infection therapy against A. baumannii in a bottleneck.

Bacterial persistence and virulence factors, which are equally important as antibiotic resistance in contributing to the difficulty of A. baumannii eradication, offer promising targets against A. baumannii. Persistent bacteria refer to the subpopulation that can survive high bactericidal drug concentrations or harsh stresses, including heat shock, cold stimuli, nutrient deprivation, and oxidative stress [2–4]. The persistence of A. baumannii in healthcare environments due to reduced sensitivity to various stresses, especially disinfectants, has become a challenging worldwide issue. Several virulence factors involved in motility, biofilm development, adherence, invasion, and host colonization [5–8] have been reported to play the vital role in the host pathogenicity of A. baumannii. Inspiringly, identifying upstream factors that can simultaneously inhibit bacterial pathogenicity and persistence is regarded as a promising strategy for controlling A. baumannii infection.

Inorganic polyphosphate (polyP), a biopolymer consisting of up to 1,000 phosphate monomers linked by phosphoric anhydride bonds [9], has been confirmed to be involved in bacterial pathogenicity and persistence by influencing bacterial motility, biofilm formation, stress tolerance, energy metabolism, colony colonization, and bacterial survival in cells [4, 10, 11]. The homeostasis of polyP was maintained by the polyphosphate kinase (PPK) family, including PPK1 and PPK2 [12]. Furthermore, the finding that the PPK family has no known homologs in higher eukaryotes provided tremendous appeal for PPK-targeted therapy [13]. Over the past decade, the importance of both PPK1 and PPK2 in the pathogenicity and persistence of Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Mycobacterium tuberculosis has been gradually recognized [14, 15]. However, in A. baumannii, more attention is currently being focused on PPK1 due to the lack of understanding of PPK2. As the key enzyme converting ATP to polyP, PPK1 has been confirmed to possess an equally indispensable position as polyP in bacterial virulence [4, 16], making it consistently viewed as a valuable target for A. baumannii treatment.

In recent years, various small molecule compounds, including kaempferol, phloretin, and quercetin [17–22], have been successively identified as effective and relatively safe drugs against microbial infections. Here, we revealed that Scutellarein (Scu), a flavonoid derived from the herb Scutellaria baicalensis, could efficiently attenuate the in vitro virulence and host lesions by targeting PPK1, which would offer a promising therapeutic tactic for troublesome A. baumannii infection.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, cell lines, and reagents

A. baumannii 17,978 was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The ΔppK1::Apr and ΔppK1::Apr/PJL02-ppK1 strains were constructed in our previous research [4]. Unless specifically stated, the above strains were all cultured in LB broth (BIOFOUNT) at 37 °C.

A549 (human NSCLC cell line), B2B (human bronchial epithelium cell line), and RAW (mouse macrophage cell line) cells were cultured in DMEM high glucose medium containing 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics (penicillin and streptomycin).

Scu was obtained from Chengdu Deruike Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China) and dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide for further study. Norfloxacin was bought from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). The Cytotoxicity Detection Kit (Roche) was purchased from Sigma Aldrich Inc. (St. Louis MO, USA). The Live/Dead BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit was bought from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Growth curve analysis

Overnight-cultured A. baumannii was diluted 1:100 with fresh LB medium and then continued to be cultured until OD600 nm reached about 0.2. Scu was subsequently added at the final concentrations of 0, 32, and 64 µg/mL. The bacteria were cultured in a shaking incubator (180 r/min, 37 °C) until reached a plateau. OD600 nm measurement was taken every hour to evaluate the growth state of each group.

Surface-associated motility assay

The surface-associated motility of A. baumannii was assessed using a previously described protocol with some modifications [4]. Briefly, 3 µL of A. baumannii in logarithmic growth phase (6 × 108 CFUs/mL) was inoculated in the center of LB agar plates (10 g/L tryptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, 5 g/L NaCl, 3 g/L agar, 1 g/L glucose, several concentrations of Scu). After the static incubation at 37 °C for 16 h, the motility diameters and images were measured and recorded.

Biofilm formation and qualification assay

The biofilm-forming capacity of bacteria was measured using an improved crystal violet staining method [23]. Briefly, 2 × 105 CFUs bacterial cells of A. baumannii were inoculated in 24-well polystyrene microtiter plates. Following the incubation at 30 °C for 48 h, the formed biofilm was analyzed by visual observation, crystal violet quantification, and colony colonization.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy

The biofilm formation assay was performed as described above after placing sterilized slides at the bottom of 24-well polystyrene microtiter plates. After staining with SYTO9 and propidium iodide (PI) dyes in the dark for 15 min, the biofilm of each group was visualized with confocal laser scanning microscopy. The morphology and thickness of the biofilm were analyzed and plotted using ImageJ software [24].

Kinase inhibition assay

The inhibitory effect of Scu on PPK1 activity was first verified by assaying the extracellular polyP synthesis ability as described previously with slight modifications [14, 25, 26]. Purified PPK1 protein was incubated with different concentrations of Scu for 15 min at room temperature in the presence of reaction buffer containing 50 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 40 mM ammonium sulfate, and 4 mM magnesium chloride. Then, the DAPI and substrate ATP were added at final concentrations of 40 µM and 1 mM, respectively. After incubation for another 10 min, a spectrofluorimeter was used to quantify the generated polyP content at an excitation wavelength of 430 nm and an emission wavelength of 550 nm. Notably, the fluorescence intensity of all samples was detected using black 96-well fluorescence microplates.

Quantification and fluorescence staining of intrabacillary polyP

To further verify whether Scu interfered with intracellular polyP generation, a DAPI-based method was used to detect the intrabacillary polyP level in the presence of nutrient deprivation [27]. In brief, bacterial cells (6 × 108 CFUs/mL) incubated with or without Scu were resuspended in low-phosphate MOPS buffer (0.2% glucose, 100 µM potassium phosphate, and 10 µM thiamine) and then incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to stimulate polyP synthesis. To further increase membrane permeability, pellets suspended in HEPES (50 mM, pH 7.5) were heated at 60 °C for 10 min. After that, bacterial cells were suspended in DPAI assay buffer (150 mM KCl and 20 mM HEPES-KOH) while adding DAPI at a final concentration of 10 µM. After incubation for 10 min without light, the polyP-DAPI content of the samples was measured as the indicator of the accumulated polyP level following excitation at 415 nm and emission at 550 nm on a spectrofluorimeter. Moreover, the polyP-DAPI level of each sample could be visually compared using a confocal microscope (Olympus) based on the property that DAPI could bind to both DNA and polyP.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observation

Ultrastructural images of A. baumannii were obtained using transmission electron microscopy based on the negative-staining technique. The bacteria were resuspended in sterile PBS after low-speed centrifugation. Ten microliters of sample were split on copper TEM grids and incubated at room temperature. Ten minutes later, bacterial samples were stained with uranyl acetate and observed using a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi).

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assays

The broth microdilution method was used in this study to determine the MICs of Scu and ciprofloxacin for A. baumannii referring to the modified standard [28].

Antibiotic persistence assay

A concentration-dependent persistence assay was first performed by treating exponential-phase bacteria with different concentrations of ciprofloxacin (× MIC) for 3 h. Then antibiotic-persistence capacity of A. baumannii subjected to different treatments was measured by time-dependent killing assay for 24 h [29, 30]. Briefly, overnight bacterial cultures were inoculated at 1:100 with 32 µg/mL of Scu added and grown to exponential phase. After normalization (OD600 nm = 0.6), 30 × MIC of ciprofloxacin was added and incubated (37 °C, 180 rpm). Every 3 h, survived bacteria were plotted on LB agar plates for colony count.

Flow cytometry and staining methods

Bacterial cultures treated with ciprofloxacin for 24 h were collected, washed twice, and resuspended in sterile PBS. The live or dead status of the tested bacteria of each group was observed with a flow cytometer after adding SYTO 9 and PI dyes of the LIVE/ DEAD BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit (Invitrogen) under the guidance of the manufacturer’s instructions.

Persistence of A. baumannii to several stringent conditions

Stationary-phase bacteria were subcultured into fresh LB broth with or without the addition of Scu (32 µg/mL) until the OD600 nm reached approximately 0.6. After normalization, 1 mL bacterial culture (approximately 6 × 108 CFUs) was centrifuged and resuspended in PBS, after which different stimuli were given separately [31, 32]. Regarding oxidative stress stimulation, H2O2 was added at a final concentration of 0.05 mM for 30 min incubation. For the heat shock stimulation, bacterial solutions were treated with 60 °C heating for 7 min. Persistent bacteria were quantified by colony colonization analysis.

For the starvation stimulation [33], bacteria inoculated into fresh LB broth were normalized and collected when the OD600 nm was 0.3. Subsequently, bacterial precipitations were re-suspended with an equal volume of MOPS minimal medium (0.2% glucose, 1.32 mM potassium phosphate, and 10 µM thiamine) and continued to be incubated (180 r/min, 37 °C) until reached plateau. The absorbance of each group was measured every hour and used for plotting images.

RNA extraction and transcriptome analysis

Overnight cultures of A. baumannii were subcultured into sterile LB broth with or without Scu (16 µg/mL) and continued to be cultured at 37℃ for 6 h. After centrifugation and washing twice, the bacterial cells were used to extract the total RNA according to the protocol of the manufacturer. The subsequent construction and sequencing of cDNA libraries, as well as the collection and comparison of transcriptome data, were conducted by Allwegene BioTech Co., Ltd (Beijing, China).

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay

SPR, the label-free detection method, is a suitable and reliable platform for monitoring biomolecular interactions. Briefly, SPR was performed on Biacore X100 using a dextran-coated CM5 chip. After equilibration, the CM5 chip was coated with PPK1 protein. Affinity was determined by monitoring different concentrations of Scu captured on the chip surface [34]. SPR data was exported and analyzed with Origin software.

Circular dichroism (CD) spectra for secondary-structure analysis

CD spectra of PPK1 proteins purified in the presence or absence of Scu (32 µg/mL) were measured by a CD spectrophotometer (MOS-500; Bio-Logic, France) as described in our previous study [35]. The cuvette quartz cell’s path length was 1 mm with a wavelength ranging from 190 to 250 nm at ambient temperature. Then, secondary structure analysis of PPK1 proteins was performed using the BeStSel web server [36].

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

The structures of Scu and PPK1 were downloaded from Pubchem and Alphofold, respectively. The prepared pdbqt files of Scu and PPK1 were docked using AUTODOCK Vina [37], from which the well-scored conformation was selected for subsequent MD study. MD simulation was performed using amaber18 software, during which ff14SB forcefield parameter was used for PPK1 proteins and gaff generic forcefield parameter was used for Scu. After loading the PPK1-Scu complex into the leap module, hydrogen ions and antagonist ions are automatically added to neutralize the charge. Then we chose the TIP3P water model and built a water box (the edges of the water box are at least 1 nm from the edge of the protein). The process of MD simulation consists of 4 steps, including energy minimization, heating, equilibrium, and MD simulation. After which, the binding free energy of the complex was calculated with the molecular mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann surface area (MM-PBSA) approach [38].

Affinity measurement by fluorescence quenching assay

The binding capacity of Scu to PPK1 proteins and its mutation was measured by fluorescence quenching assay. The fluorescence values were measured at 345 nm following excitation at 280 nm with an F-7100 Hitachi fluorescence spectrophotometer according to the protocol reported earlier [39]. Briefly, an appropriate amount of PPK1 protein was first added within 2 mL of PBS to obtain the initial fluorescence value (F0). Subsequently, the dynamic changes in fluorescence values (F) were monitored by gradually adding equal doses of Scu. Construction, expression, and purification of PPK1 and its mutants

The vectors containing mutated ppK1 genes were obtained with gene-specific primers (listed in Table S1) based on pET28a-ppK1 constructed in our previous study [4]. Before further induction of protein expression, the recombinant vectors were transferred into E. coli BL21(DE3), and the accuracy of the mutations was validated by sequencing. Finally, the recombinant proteins were expressed and purified following the recommended experimental procedures described before [40, 41].

Cytotoxicity assays

The cytotoxicity of Scu in vitro was assessed using a cytotoxicity detection kit (LDH; Roche). A549 cells, RAW macrophages, and B2B cells were inoculated in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/cell. After coincubation with different concentrations of Scu at 37 °C for 24 h (the final concentration of solvent DMSO was under 0.1%), the LDH level in the cell supernatant was determined as an indicator of cell damage. Notably, cells treated with solvent and 0.2% Triton X-100 were regarded as negative and positive controls, respectively. Infection models of A. baumannii in vivo

The Galleria mellonella larvae (250 ± 50 mg) infection model was first constructed for survival rate analysis in vivo. 3 × 105 CFUs of A. baumannii in the exponential phase were microinjected through the left pro-leg for model establishment. After infection for 2 h, different concentrations of Scu dissolved in formulated solvent (0.5% DMSO + 40% PEG 400 + 5% Tween 80 + 54.5% NaCl) were administered in the left second proleg for treatment. Meanwhile, we set up a solvent-treated group and a meropenem-treated group (5 mg/kg) as negative and positive control groups. Infected larvae were monitored for at least five days in a 37 °C incubator to analyze the protective effect of Scu against A. baumannii infection. The larvae that did not respond to any stimulus were considered dead.

To further explore the protective efficacy of Scu in mice, we purchased C57BL/6 female mice (six to eight weeks old, 20 ± 2 g) from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and conducted adaptive feeding for 5 days. For pneumonia model construction, the mice were intranasally inoculated with 3 × 109 CFUs (40 µL) of A. baumannii after anesthesia with isoflurane. 2 h later, the mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6 for each group) and subjected to different treatments: solvent control group (0.5% carboxy-methyl cellulose sodium), Scu (10 mg/kg) group, Scu (20 mg/kg) group, and meropenem (5 mg/kg) group. The Scu and meropenem were respectively administered orally and subcutaneously twice a day. At 48 h postinfection, we euthanized the mice and autopsied the livers and lungs for bacterial burden analysis. The left lungs were also dissected for macroscopic comparison and H&E staining. In addition, ELISA kits (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) were used to detect the cytokine levels in the supernatant of liver homogenates following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

The statistical significance of all data was analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1. At least three biological replicates of each experiment were executed independently to provide data for the mean ± standard deviation calculation. Data comparison between two independent groups was carried out using the Student’s t-test. Significance determination between more than two groups was conducted by one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s correction. P < 0.05 (*) was regarded as statistically significant, and P < 0.01 (**) was regarded as extremely significant.

Results

Scu significantly attenuated the enzymatic activity of PPK1 without affecting the bacterial viability

A robust PPK1-targeted screening platform was established to explore potent PPK1 inhibitors. Scu (Fig. 1a), a typical flavonoid, was identified with excellent PPK1 enzyme inhibitory efficacy. We dynamically monitored the changes of PPK1-mediated extracellular polyP levels in the presence or absence of nonbacteriostatic doses of Scu. The results indicated that the polyP level converted from ATP was dramatically reduced in samples with Scu treatment (Fig. 1c). For the intracellular inhibitory effects of Scu on polyP accumulation, we introduced ΔppK1::Apr and ΔppK1::Apr/PJL02-ppK1 strains constructed in our previous study [4] representing ppK1-deficient and ppK1-complemented strains derived from A. baumannii 17,978, respectively. The level of accumulated polyP in A. baumannii was measured following a shift to the phosphate-deprivation medium, which was reported to stimulate polyP production [14, 42]. As shown in Fig. 1d, the polyP content was significantly reduced in the ΔppK1::Apr strain, which was slightly restored in the ΔppK1::Apr/PJL02-ppK1, and similar inhibitory efficacies were detected in Scu-treated A. baumannii strains. A more visualized difference in accumulated polyP level was detected by confocal microscopy, evidenced by the weaker polyP fluorescence intensity (green) and the same DNA fluorescence intensity (blue) in the Scu-treated groups (Fig. 1e). There was no marked influence on the growth of A. baumannii when knocking out ppK1 or treated with Scu (≤ 64 µg/mL) (Fig. 1b), which was further confirmed by the results of MIC assay in Table S2 (MIC = 1024 µg/mL).

Scu inhibited surface-associated motility and biofilm formation in A. baumannii

Our previous study confirmed that the pilus-mediated motility and biofilm formation in A. baumannii were markedly inhibited after ppK1 knockout [4]. Here, we explored whether Scu inhibition of PPK1 activity could influence the virulence of A. baumannii by the surface motility inhibition assay, TEM, and antibiofilm assay. As Fig. 2a, b shows, the sliding diameter of ΔppK1::Apr strain on 0.3% agar-containing plate was significantly reduced by approximately 60% compared to that of the wild-type (WT) strain, which could be partially recovered in ΔppK1::Apr/PJL02-ppK1 strain. The horizontal analysis demonstrated that Scu could further weaken the sliding ability of each strain. In addition, the pilus in different treated strains of A. baumannii was observed by TEM. Inconsistent with the WT and ΔppK1::Apr/PJL02-ppK1 strains, the pilus structure was almost completely lost on the surface of the ΔppK1::Apr strain, and the inhibitory effect of Scu on pilus production was intermediate between that of the WT strain and ΔppK1::Apr strain (Fig. 2c).

The influence of Scu on biofilm formation was subsequently verified. As shown in Fig. 3a, b, formed pellicles stained by crystal violet were visually reduced by approximately 50% under Scu treatment (64 µg/mL), and the ΔppK1::Apr strain almost completely lost its biofilm-forming ability. ppK1 knockout and co-incubation with Scu also notably lowered the bacterial load on the biofilm (Fig. 3c). To visualize the alterations in biofilm thickness, we photographed the samples with confocal laser scanning microscopy. After converting the pictures into three-dimensional (3-D) views by ImageJ software, the thickness of Scu-treated biofilm was seen to be equally intermediate between that of the WT and ΔppK1::Apr groups (Fig. 3d). Altogether, Scu exhibited effective inhibitory effects on PPK1-regulated phenotypes.

Scu markedly disrupted PPK1-associated bacterial persistence

We further determined whether Scu could also alter PPK1-mediated bacterial persistence. As an antibiotic often used to induce persisters formation in A. baumannii [29, 30, 43], ciprofloxacin was chosen in our study for the subsequent antibiotic-related persistence assays, before which the MIC of ciprofloxacin against A. baumannii was determined (Table S2). We first performed an antibiotic-killing assay to determine the optimal concentration of ciprofloxacin for persisters induction. Figure 4a showed that a plateau of persistent bacteria occurred from 30 × MIC ciprofloxacin (MIC = 0.125 µg/mL). Subsequently, antibiotic-related persistence assays with 30 × MIC of ciprofloxacin were implemented for 24 h. Flow cytometry was then conducted at 24 h to detect the proportion of live/dead bacteria in each group after staining with the Live/Dead BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit. The results indicated that the “persisters” population of the Scu-treated group was significantly reduced compared to the control group but still higher than that of the ΔppK1::Apr strain throughout the assay (Fig. 4b). Flow cytometry images showed that ciprofloxacin-induced persisters were significantly reduced after co-incubation with Scu for 24 h, which manifested as decreased live bacteria and increased dead bacteria (Fig. 4c). In addition, the surviving bacteria in many other inhospitable environments were also indicators of persistence. As can be seen, the Scu-treated strain and ΔppK1::Apr strain both exhibited attenuated tolerance to the stimulations of hydrogen peroxide, heat, and starvation (Fig. 4d). Taken together, our results indicated that Scu could attenuate the persistence of A. baumannii for several stimuli by influencing PPK1.

Validation of Scu-regulated bacterial phenotypes by transcriptome analysis

To verify the reliability of the above results, we analyzed the transcriptomics of the WT strain with or without Scu treatment. Figure 5a showed that the genotypes of the two groups were not significantly altered. However, 157 expressed genes and 135 expressed genes were respectively upregulated and downregulated following Scu treatment (Fig. 5b), and then the differentially expressed genes were displayed in a clustered heat map (Fig. 5c). Subsequently, we performed GO enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes and plotted images. As expected, some downregulated genes were relevant to pilus-mediated motility and bacterial response to stimulus, which is consistent with our conclusion that Scu could inhibit bacterial motility, biofilm formation, and bacterial persistence (Fig. 5d).

Identification of the mechanism of the interaction between Scu and PPK1

As a well-advanced technique that could monitor the interactions between biomolecules, SPR was first employed to detect the real-time affinity between PPK1 and different concentrations of Scu. The results revealed that Scu exhibited a dose-dependent affinity for PPK1, with an affinity constant KD of 5.444 × 10− 5 M (Fig. 6a).

Then, CD spectroscopy, a well-established technique for analyzing the secondary structures of proteins, was used to characterize the impact of Scu on PPK1 conformation. As shown in Fig. 6b, c, a visible change in the secondary structure of PPK1 was found when induced by Scu, which was characterized by elevated α-helix 1, anti-1 conformations and reduced α-helix 2, anti-3, parallel conformations.

After determining the direct interaction between PPK1 and Scu, molecular docking and MD simulation were used to further explore the potential binding mode of the PPK1-Scu complex. During the 100 ns simulation, the PPK1 conformation was gradually stabilized after 20 ns. The Scu continuously adjusted its conformation and binding position and finally formed a stable binding complex (Fig. 6d). The 3-D binding mode of the PPK1-Scu complex was obtained after 100 ns of simulation under equilibrium. As shown in Fig. 6e, Scu is bound to the catalytic pocket of PPK1 by its shape-complementary property. Several residues of PPK1, including Leu114, Phe124, and Leu125, could form van der Waals interactions with the Scu benzene ring through hydrophobic interactions. In addition, the hydroxyl group on the Scu side chain could form hydrogen bonds with Asn280, Thr278, Phe124, and Arg126 of PPK1, greatly enhancing the binding stability. A strong binding capacity was detected between PPK1 and Scu with a ΔG binding of 20 kcal/mol according to the MM-PBSA approach. We then further ascertained the residues that contributed crucially to the complex by energy decomposition (Fig. 6f) and performed mutation experiments on them to confirm the reliability of the above analysis. The KA of fluorescence quenching was assayed as indirect indicators reflecting the changes in the binding ability of mutant-complex and WT-complex. However, an elevated tendency in binding constants was observed after the single-site mutation (Fig. 6g, i). Therefore, we constructed two-site mutants for the top-ranked residues. Scu exhibited attenuated binding ability to the two-site mutated PPK1 proteins compared to WT-PPK1 (Fig. 6h, i). In summary, we deduced that targeting the PPK1 protein directly is the main manner by which Scu inhibited the enzyme activity of PPK1.

Scu attenuated the virulence of A. baumannii in vivo

The in vivo efficacy of Scu was successively evaluated in different infection models, before which the cytotoxicity of Scu within the effective doses was excluded (Fig. 7a). As shown in Fig. 7b, Scu administration (20 mg/kg) could improve the survival rate of Galleria mellonella larvae by about 35%. In the mouse pneumonia model constructed by nasal perfusion, Scu also exhibited excellent protective effects, although not as significant as meropenem. The bacterial loads in the lungs and livers of Scu-treated mice (20 mg/kg) were significantly lower than those of solvent-control mice (Fig. 7c, d). The lungs of the control mice exhibited severe congestion, hemorrhage, and edema, which were significantly alleviated by Scu administration. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed that the pathological damages, including thickened alveolar septa and neutrophil infiltration, were extremely improved after Scu treatment (20 mg/kg) (Fig. 7e). Furthermore, the reduced levels of inflammatory cytokine expression in the Scu- treated group (including IL-1β, IL-6, IFN-γ, and TNF-α) further confirmed the protective efficacy of Scu in vivo (Fig. 7f). Collectively, our results indicated that Scu could achieve worthy protective efficacy against A. baumannii infection in vivo by targeting the bacterial PPK1 protein and thereby inhibiting bacterial pathogenicity and persistence.

Discussion and conclusion

In the context of the endless development of antibiotic resistance, targeting bacterial virulence factors and persistence are emerging therapeutic strategies that can disarm the infection of organisms more directly [44]. As one of the most successful indigenous pathogens in healthcare settings [45], A. baumannii is also known for diverse virulence factors and stubborn persistence in addition to its broad-spectrum resistance [46]. Among them, bacterial motility, biofilm formation, and persistent bacteria have been documented to be uniformly regulated by polyP. As such, PPK1, which catalyzes polyP synthesis and is found only in microorganisms, has been regarded as a favored target for A. baumannii treatment [14, 47]. Given the precedent of natural compounds against bacterial infection [48, 49], we successfully screened Scu as a PPK1-targeted promising candidate to confront A. baumannii infection. In-depth exploration revealed that Scu could bind within the surface pocket of PPK1 through multiple amino acid sites, including Thr278, Asn280, Pro306, Gly305, and Gln238, resulting in the alteration of secondary structure and the inhibition of enzyme activity. It is promising that the identification of PPK1 inhibitors would advance the addressing of A. baumannii-caused healthcare dilemmas.

Flavonoids, which are widespread in the plant kingdom, have been found to possess a wide range of biological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-carcinogenic, antiviral, and antifungal properties [50–52]. Recently, antimicrobial reports of flavonoids on inhibiting biofilm formation, bacterial motility, and persisters emergence of various pathogens, including A. baumannii, have continuously appeared, but their mechanisms are not yet well explored [45, 53]. Here, we confirmed the antimicrobial efficacy of flavonoids by screening for Scu and further validated the key targeting role of PPK1 protein during this process by innovatively comparing the phenotypes of the Scu-treated strain with those of the ΔppK1::Apr strain. As a flavonoid extracted mainly from Scutellaria baicalensis and Erigeron breviscapus, Scu has been shown to exhibit safe and favorable therapeutic effects in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), heart disease, cancer, inflammation, pulmonary fibrosis, etc [54–60]. In the study, we present for the first time that Scu is effective against A. baumannii infection by targeting the PPK1 protein, as evidenced by the inhibition of virulence phenotypes in vitro and the attenuation of colonization and organ damage in vivo. It is worth mentioning that Scu has been reported to possess a variety of properties, including DNA binding, ferroptosis inhibition, inflammation inhibition, etc [55, 58, 61]. , which may indicate that Scu could exert its efficacy by acting on more than one target, as many natural compounds do [62]. Regarding the truth of this speculation and whether there exists an upstream-downstream relationship between different targets, we will verify it in our future research.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that Scu alleviated the pathogenicity and persistence of A. baumannii mainly by inhibiting the activity of PPK1(Fig. 8). The above findings pointed out the bright prospect of PPK1-targeted treatment against A. baumannii infection and provided Scu as a promising therapeutic candidate.
