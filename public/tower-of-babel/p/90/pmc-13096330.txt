Simultaneous effect of ZnO nanoparticles and silica fume on the mechanical properties of the concrete
Authors: Mukesh Kumar, Manjeet Bansal, Bal Krishan, Rishav Garg, Abdullah Naser M. Asiri, Saiful Islam, Wahaj Ahmad Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work investigates the effects of silica fume (SF) and ZnO nanoparticles in enhancing the mechanical strength and durability of concrete. High-performance concrete specimens (w/b 0.34) were prepared, replacing cement with SF (4–16%) and combining 8% SF with ZnO (0.3–1.2%). Mechanical strengths and resistance to sulphate and chloride attack were assessed. Results identified 8% SF and 0.9% ZnO as the optimal dosage for ternary blends, yielding a 8.96% increase in compressive strength (p < 0.05) and reducing strength loss by 18.86% under sulphate exposure. Microstructural analysis (XRD and SEM-EDX) confirmed that ZnO acts as a nucleation agent, enhancing matrix densification following the initial retardation period, while SF consumes calcium hydroxide through long-term pozzolanic reactions. This synergistic interaction results in a refined, compact cementitious matrix, consistent with improved retention of mechanical performance under aggressive exposure conditions. Consequently, the optimized SF-ZnO composite offers a robust material for sustainable infrastructure in aggressive environments.

Introduction

Silica fume (SF) is a prominent supplementary cementitious material (SCM) widely used to increase concrete strength and durability and reduce the carbon footprint of cement production1–3. The SF is mainly composed of amorphous silica microparticles and alters the cementitious matrix through physical and chemical processes4. The micro-sized particles are used as fillers, reducing porosity. Chemically, they react with the by-products of cement hydration. Nanotechnology can be used to provide a revolutionary solution to increase binder reactivity and negate the possible dilution effects of SCMs9,10. Nanoparticles are highly reactive/surface energy and can speed up the rate of hydration, as well as densify the matrix, at the nanoscale11. Nano-oxides have distinctive functional advantages, and nano-ZnO is one of them, with antimicrobial and antioxidant properties12,13. The interplay between ZnO and cement particles, however, is complicated. It has been known to slow the initial hydration process by forming an amorphous zinc hydroxide coating on C-S-H, temporarily suppressing the dissolution of calcium silicate14,15. Only after this layer is transformed into crystalline calcium hydrozincate does hydration restart, as shown in Eq. 316.3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{ZnO}}\xrightarrow{{{{\mathrm{H}}_{\mathrm{2}}}{\mathrm{O}}}}\,{\mathrm{Zn(OH}}{{\mathrm{)}}_{\mathrm{2}}}\xrightarrow{{{\mathrm{CH}}}}{\mathrm{CaZ}}{{\mathrm{n}}_{\mathrm{2}}}\,{{\mathrm{(OH)}}_{\mathrm{6}}}$$\end{document}

The next stage of erosion of this impermeable veil promotes the rapid development of the calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) phase. After overcoming this hydration block, ZnO particles serve as powerful nucleation sites, facilitating the development of a more compact C-S-H structure and leading to a significant increase in compressive strength17,18. Although binary blends of cement with SF or ZnO have been studied before12,19, simultaneous use of these materials has been seen as a promising way to counter the conflicting effects of these two materials, namely, to leverage ZnO’s nucleation ability to offset SF’s long-term pozzolanic effect.

Although the advantages of single additives have been established, there remains a critical gap in understanding the synergistic interaction between micro-sized silica fume and nano-sized ZnO in ternary binder systems20. The vast majority of the available literature is dedicated to binary blends, and the joint effects of micro-pozzolanic reactions and nano-nucleation effects on long-term durability remain largely understudied21,22. This research fills this gap by proposing a multi-scale modification strategy: leveraging the nucleation potential of ZnO to hasten early hydration and efficiently counter the initial retardation commonly associated with SF, and using the pozzolanic reactivity of SF to densify it over the long term. The main input in this work is to streamline this tertiary system to maximize resistance to aggressive sulphate and chloride environments, and to provide mechanistic insight into the role of Zn-modified C-S-H phases in improving matrix integrity under aggressive ionic exposure.

The current study will examine the effects of nano-sized ZnO on the physico-mechanical characteristics and chemical reactions in SF-based concrete systems. The main goal is to establish the optimal doses of SF and ZnO that can be substituted to achieve better performance. It is done by a thorough testing of major mechanical properties, compressive strength, split tensile strength, and durability parameters, in both the normal and aggressive environments. To substantiate these results, microstructural studies of the cement matrix have also been incorporated, and a correlation between performance enhancements and changes in phase composition has been found.

Silica fume was incorporated as a partial cement replacement in the range of 4–16%, based on the commonly reported optimum range of 10–15% in the literature1. A sequential optimization approach was adopted: binary OPC–SF mixes were first evaluated to identify the optimum SF dosage, which was found to be 12%. In parallel, binary OPC–ZnO mixes with ZnO contents of 0.3–1.2% were prepared for comparison. Subsequently, ternary OPC–SF–ZnO mixes were developed by introducing ZnO nanoparticles (0.3–1.2%) while reducing the SF (8%) content relative to the binary optimum (12%), in order to examine whether ZnO could compensate for lower SF levels through filler and nucleation effects (Table 3). All replacement levels were calculated by weight of cement. Moreover, the fine and coarse aggregate content was held constant across all mixes to maintain a consistent granular skeleton and isolate the physicochemical effects of the binder paste modification on concrete performance.

First, coarse aggregate and half of the sand were dry mixed for 1 min. The remaining sand, cement, supplementary cementitious materials (SF and ZnO, wherever applicable), and approximately two-thirds of the mixing water were then added, and mixing was continued for a further 3 min. Finally, the remaining mixing water, along with the superplasticizer, was introduced, and mixing was continued for an additional 6 min until a uniform mixture was obtained.

The prepared concrete was then poured into standard steel moulds: 150 mm × 150 mm × 150 mm cubes to measure compressive strength, 150 mm D × 300 mm H cylinders to measure splitting tensile strength, and 100 mm × 100 mm × 500 mm beams to measure flexural strength25. The moulds were stored on a flat surface at a laboratory ambient temperature of 27 ± 2 °C after casting, surrounded by damp gunny bags to prevent moisture loss. The specimens were then demoulded and immersed in clean tap water curing tanks at 27 ± 2 °C until the time of testing, as per the requirements of IS 516–1959 (Reaffirmed in 2021)9.

Analysis of concrete specimens

The consistency (IS 4031–2019 part 4), initial setting time (IST), final setting time (FST) (IS 4031–2019 part 5), and flow of the cement paste (IS: 5512 − 1983) were analysed25. A slump test of concrete (IS 1199–1959, reaffirmed in 2013) was also performed9. Separate specimens were cast and tested independently for each mechanical property. The mechanical strengths were determined in three repetitions at 28, 56, 90, 180, and 365 days of curing, in accordance with IS 516–1959 (Reaffirmed in 2021), for compressive, split tensile, and flexural strength (with a 400 mm span and a loading rate of 1.8 kN/min.). The effects of sulfate and chloride exposure were evaluated under laboratory conditions at 23 ± 2 °C26. After 28 days of standard water curing, the specimens were surface-dried and separately immersed in 5% magnesium sulfate (MgSO₄) and 5% sodium chloride (NaCl) solutions. A sufficient solution-to-specimen volume ratio was maintained to prevent ion depletion. The pH of the immersion solutions was monitored at regular intervals, and the solutions were fully replaced with freshly prepared solutions at fixed intervals. To track durability-related degradation, the compressive strength was measured at 180 and 365 days and compared with the respective values for the control specimen27.

All tests were conducted in triplicate for each Mix × Age or Mix × Exposure combination (n = 3). Statistical analysis was performed on individual replicate values rather than on means, using a two-way ANOVA at the 95% confidence level (p < 0.05). Mechanical strength data were analysed with Mix and Age as fixed factors, while durability data were analysed with Mix and Exposure as fixed factors for each exposure duration. Data were checked for normality and homogeneity of variance prior to analysis. Post-hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey’s HSD test. Standard deviations are shown as error bars, and effect sizes are reported as partial eta squared (η²p).

The inner core of each specimen was sampled after 28 days of curing and crushed into a fine powder using a mortar and pestle. The powdered samples were analysed by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) using Panalytical’s X’Pert Pro diffractometer. The diffraction peaks were identified and indexed by using the Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS) database and relevant literature.

The concrete fragments were washed with acetone three times and oven-dried at 65 °C for 2 h. The samples were gold-coated and analyzed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JEOL JSM-6510LV) at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. The elemental composition (atomic %) was measured by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). Figure 2 shows important raw ingredients and some of the experimental processes.

Results and discussion

Fresh properties

The consistency, flow, setting time, and workability properties of binary (OPC-SF, OPC-ZnO) and ternary (OPC-SF-ZnO) binder systems were systematically tested, with the SF content maintained at 8% in the ternary mixtures to isolate the effect of adding ZnO. The normal consistency results shown in Fig. 3a indicate a linear increase in water demand with increasing SF or ZnO dosage, consistent with previous findings on fine- and nano-modified binders12,28. The water demand of the binary OPCSF system increased by 3.32%, 6.64%, 9.96%, and 13.28% (SM1, SM2, SM3, and SM4) compared with the control mix (MC0). This has been largely due to the ultrafine particle size and high amorphous silica content of SF, which significantly increase the total specific surface area of the binder system, thereby increasing the volume of water required to wet and lubricate the surface29. Also, the angular morphology and the high pozzolanic reactivity of SF result in high paste viscosity and reduced free water availability in the fresh state.

Fig. 3Variation in fresh properties (a) consistency, (b) flow, (c) initial and final setting time, and (d) slump for all mixes.

Variation in fresh properties (a) consistency, (b) flow, (c) initial and final setting time, and (d) slump for all mixes.

A similar trend was observed with the binary OPC-ZnO mixes, with increases of 5.18% (ZM1), 8.47% (ZM2), 11.76% (ZM3), and 15.04% (ZM4). The enhanced effect of ZnO can also be attributed to its nanoscale size and the high surface energy, which increase inter-particle attractions and enhance water adsorption at particle surfaces10. Additionally, ZnO nanoparticles alter the initial physicochemical environment of the cement paste by enhancing surface adsorption, thereby decreasing the free water available for flow. In the ternary OPC–SF–ZnO systems, the demand for water was also dependent on ZnO addition to a lesser extent: 0.00% (SZ1), 3.32% (SZ2), 6.64% (SZ3), and 9.96% (SZ4). This controlled reaction implies that the coexistence of SF and ZnO enhances the effectiveness of granular packing, allowing small particles to fill interstitial voids among cement grains and partly counteracting the increased surface wetting requirement, which is typically associated with single fine additions30.

Figure 3b shows the flow characteristics of the paste mixtures, which indicate the combined effects of particle fineness, surface area, and packing. The control mix (MC0) had the highest flow value, which served as the reference. Binary OPC-SF mixes exhibited gradual decreases in flow of 4.54% (SM1), 7.26% (SM2), 10.90% (SM3), and 14.53% (SM4), which are explained by the fact that SF particles are a high-surface-area and water-absorptive material that increases the viscosity of the paste. Binary OPC to ZnO mixtures showed similar or a little higher flow reduction of 5.45 (ZM1), 7.26 (ZM2), 9.08 (ZM3), and 10.99 (ZM4). Nanoscale ZnO particles cause internal friction and impede particle motion, slowing the paste’s flow even when it is not replaced with many particles31. Conversely, the ternary OPC-SF-ZnO blends showed significantly smaller decreases in flow, ranging from 0.91% to 3.63%. This is a sign that the synergistic nature of micro- and nanoscale particles enhances particle packing and reduces unnecessary loss of paste mobility relative to binary systems, despite the increased total surface area20.

Figure 3c provides additional evidence of the effects of SF and ZnO on early-age hydration behaviour. The initial and final setting times rose gradually with increasing proportions of SF in the binary OPC-SF mixes, increasing by 6.66–16.65% and 4.35–10.86% in IST and FST, respectively, compared to MC0. This latency is explained by the fact that cement clinker phases are diluted and the kinetics of the pozzolanic reaction are slower, so the reaction absorbs calcium hydroxide at later ages rather than contributing to early-age hydration32. Moreover, the large water-retention capacity also slows the development of the percolated hydration network.

In line with the literature, the use of ZnO nanoparticles also increased setting times, as expected for hydration-retarding nanoparticles. Such retardation is usually explained by the presence of zinc hydroxide or zincate layers on the cement grain surfaces, which prevent early dissolution of clinker phases and delay the nucleation of hydration products33. Based on this, the physicochemical retardation rather than the hydration acceleration is seen to be the driving force behind the setting behavior of OPC-ZnO mixes. The setting times in the ternary systems of OPC–SF-ZnO showed intermediation behavior between the two binary systems. Although ZnO changed the setting properties of SF-containing mixes partially, it could not completely counteract the retarding effect of SF34. The observed differences can be largely ascribed to physical filler effects, interactions between particles during packing, and surface adsorption phenomena as opposed to alterations in intrinsic hydration kinetics.

The trends of the slump characteristics of the concrete mixtures were similar to those of the paste-level (Fig. 3d). The demand of water increased and the free water available to lubricate was decreased, as all modified mixes had lower slumps than MC035. Slump reductions were significant with SF dosage in binary OPC-SF mixes with a high specific surface area (and water absorption capacity) of silica fume ranging between 6.79 and 19.97%. The reductions of 1.33–5.32% were smaller and systematic in the binary OPC-ZnO mixes, which is reasonable because they had lower replacement levels and nanoscale effects. The cumulative slump decreases of 7.99–15.65% were realized in the ternary systems, which shows the joint effect of SF and ZnO on fresh concrete rheology31. The results of this research highlight the need to ensure optimum dosage of superplasticizer in real life situations to sustain target workability in the introduction of fine and nano-scale supplementary materials.

Strength analysis

Compressive Strength Analysis of SF- and ZnO-Modified Concrete

Figure 4 shows the development of compressive strength with time when SF and ZnO were added as supplementary cementitious materials. There was a progressive increase in strength with age across all concrete mixes, confirming the benefit of continued hydration in refining the microstructure36. The minimum strength was always observed in the control mix (MC0) which indicates the contribution of SF and ZnO addition in the performance of concrete. Strength in SF-only mixes (SM1 to SM4) showed an increase in strength with increasing SF content to 12%. Mixtures with 12% SF (SM3) exhibited a compressive strength increase of approximately 4.17% in comparison with the MC0 at 365 days of curing (58.25 MPa vs. 55.92 MPa) and a similar tendency of strength gain was achieved at all curing periods. Although the magnitude of the increase is low, statistical analysis (ANOVA, p < 0.05) proved that the difference is significant (Table 4). This improvement has been attributed to the pozzolanic reaction of SF reacting with calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)2) released during hydration to form further C-S-H gel27. This reaction is responsible for matrix densification and better bonding in the interfacial transition zone. Further increase in SF content to 16% (SM4) resulted in a slight net gain of 0.11% over MC0 at 365 days of curing (55.98 MPa). Such limited improvement is suggestive of reduced efficiency at higher SF contents, which may be related to workability or dispersion-related problems, which may affect particle packing and compaction9. These results indicate that SM3 (12% SF) is the best SF-only formulation, being a balance between reactivity and fresh state performance.

Compressive strength development for all mixes up to 365 days.

The ZnO-only mixes (ZM1-ZM4) also had a higher compressive strength in comparison to MC0 with an increase in compressive strength being a function of the dosage of the nanoparticles. ZnO at 0.9% (ZM3) was the most effective with 5.59% increase over MC0 at 365 days (59.04 MPa). This behavior has been attributed to the ability of ZnO to favor early hydration and affect pore structure refinement37. However an increase in the ZnO content to 1.2% (ZM4) decreased the gain to 4.14% (58.24 MPa) which could be attributed to the excess Zn2+ ions that may interfere with the subsequent hydration reactions. These results showed that effect of ZnO on the compressive strength is positive but dose dependent, so with increased concentration it was less effective.

The ternary blends (SZ1 to SZ4), which is a combination of SF and ZnO, showed a higher compressive strength compared to all binary mixes. The optimized formulation SZ3 (8% SF + 0.9% ZnO) showed the highest one-year strength corresponding to an 8.96% increase on MC0 (60.93 MPa). This improvement is the sum of the effect of nucleation with ZnO and the long-lasting pozzolanic activity of SF12. After the initial retardation stage, ZnO nanoparticles offer more nucleation sites that contribute to the further development of the C-S-H network, while SF will continue to react with available Ca(OH)2.

SZ1 (8% SF + 0.3% ZnO) also exhibited a good performance with a strength increment of 7.55% (60.14 MPa), which indicated that the synergy effect still works at lower ZnO dosage. SZ4 (8% SF + 1.2% ZnO) and SZ2 (8% SF + 0.6% ZnO) showed the increase of 6.96% (59.81 MPa) and 6.71% (59.67 MPa), respectively. Although these mixes were better in comparison to their binary counterparts, their relatively lower gains suggest that non-optimal proportions of SF or ZnO are not making full use of the combined effect19. Thus, the observed results prove that SF and ZnO exhibit an individual enhancement in compressive strength through different mechanisms and when combined in suitable proportions result in incremental but constant improvements in compressive strength. SZ3 was therefore found to be the best formulation of mixes tested.

Split Tensile Strength Analysis of Modified Concrete

Figure 5 shows the effect of the addition of SF and ZnO on the concrete split tensile strength. As expected, strength increased over time slowly in all mixes with further hydration38. There was a progressive increase in strength with SF content up to 12% in the SF only mixes (SM1 - SM4). The blend with 12% SF (SM3) exhibited an increase of 2.4% in comparison to control mix (MC0) at 365 days (4.27 MPa vs. 4.17 MPa). This tendency is consistent with the results of compressive strength and follows the same mechanisms of pozzolanic densification and ITZ refinement discussed earlier. However, with an additional increase in the percentage of SF used (16%), the gain was lower (0.2% over MC0), suggesting a lower effectiveness when the replacement level was increased, which may be attributed to the decrease of workability and the presence of entrapped voids.

The ZnO-only mixes (ZM1 - ZM4) showed moderate variations in comparison to MC0. The best ZnO dosage was 0.9% in ZM3, and the improvement was 3.13% at 365 days (4.31 MPa). With additional increase in the ZnO content (1.2%, ZM4), the strength gain reduced to 1.92% (4.27 MPa) indicating diminishing strength gaining efficiency with increasing the nanoparticle dosage.

The ternary mixes (SF and ZnO) had the best performance of all the investigated systems (SZ1–SZ4). SZ1 (8% SF + 0.3% ZnO) followed with a 4.08% increase (4.34 MPa), indicating that the combined effect remains effective at slightly reduced ZnO content. SZ4 (8% SF + 1.2% ZnO) and SZ2 (8% SF + 0.6% ZnO) also showed improvements of 3.84% (4.33 MPa) and 3.6% (4.32 MPa), respectively. SZ3 (8% SF + 0.9% ZnO) exhibited the highest tensile strength, with a 4.8% increase relative to MC0 after 365 days (4.37 MPa). Statistical analysis (Table 4) confirmed significant main effects of mix composition and curing age (p < 0.05), whereas the Mix × Age interaction showed a comparatively small effect size (Table 4). These gains are associated with refinement of the interfacial transition zone (ITZ), which is more susceptible to tensile failure27. The effect possibly takes place through the combined influence of SF-driven pozzolanic reactions and ZnO-assisted nucleation effects. Thus, SZ3 remained the most effective formulation, providing the highest tensile strength among the tested mixes.

Flexural Strength Analysis of Modified Concrete

Figure 6 demonstrates the changes in flexural strength of modified concrete containing SF and ZnO. All mixes showed a gradual increase in strength with curing age37. Flexural strength exhibited a steady increase with increasing SF content in the SF-only mixes (SM1–SM4) up to a maximum of 12%. The SM3 mix (12% SF) achieved a 2.35% increase over the control mix (MC0) at 365 days (5.41 MPa versus 5.29 MPa), indicating the contribution of SF to long-term matrix strengthening. However, when the SF dosage was increased to 16% (SM4), the gain decreased slightly to approximately 0.23% (5.30 MPa), likely due to reduced compaction efficiency at higher replacement levels. The ZnO-only mixes (ZM1–ZM4) showed moderate strength improvements compared with MC0. The optimal ZnO content was 0.9% in ZM3, which resulted in a 3.3% increase at 365 days (5.46 MPa). Further addition of ZnO (e.g., ZM4 with 1.2%) did not enhance performance, with the gain remaining at approximately 2.35% (5.41 MPa). The ternary blends (SZ1–SZ4) incorporating both SF and ZnO exhibited higher flexural strength than the corresponding binary systems. SZ1 (8% SF + 0.3% ZnO) showed a notable improvement of 3.99% (5.50 MPa), indicating that the contribution of ZnO remains effective at moderate dosages when combined with an optimal SF content. SZ2 (8% SF + 0.6% ZnO) and SZ4 (8% SF + 0.6% ZnO) followed, showing gains of 3.52% (5.48 MPa) and 3.76% (5.49 MPa), respectively. ZnO primarily influences early hydration kinetics. On the other hand, SF contributes to long-term matrix strengthening and microstructural refinement. Their combined action restricts microcrack development and strengthens the paste–aggregate interface. SZ3 (8% SF + 0.9% ZnO) recorded the highest flexural strength among all mixes, with a 4.7% increase relative to MC0 at 365 days (5.54 MPa).

Similar to the other mechanical properties, the findings of ANOVA proved that these increases were statistically significant, which supported the role of the ternary binder system (Table 4). The compressive, tensile, and flexural strength were statistically significantly influenced by the mix composition and curing age (p < 0.05). In all cases, the curing age had the greatest effect size. These findings suggest that SF and ZnO combined use are useful in enhancing flexural performance with dense and high-performance matrix34. The increased reaction is the interplay of ZnO-assisted hydration acceleration and the subsequent pozzolanic reaction of SF that enhances further C-S-H formation and densification of the matrix to bear the flexural stress. SZ3 was the most effective mix of all that yielded maximum flexural strength with all curing ages and also showed the advantage of multi-component binder systems with optimization. Therefore, the findings give clear indication that an integrated incorporation of SF and ZnO results in strength reinforcement when the two materials are incorporated in equal proportions38.

Durability analysis

Residual compressive strength after sulfate exposure of SF- and ZnO-modified concrete

The effect of sulfate exposure was evaluated using residual compressive strength of specimens immersed in a 5% magnesium sulfate solution. Figure 7 shows the residual compressive strength after sulfate exposure of the investigated mixes. Out of the SF-only series (SM1–SM4), strength retention under sulfate exposure increased with the addition of SF content up to 12% (SM3). SM3 was the most effective in this category, with residual compressive strengths of about 11.76% and 11.26% greater than the control mix (MC0) at 180 and 365 days, respectively. A further increase of SF content to 16% (SM4) did not provide proportional improvement, likely because dispersion was problematic at higher replacement levels39.

In the case of the ZnO-only series (ZM1–ZM4), the addition of nanoparticles also improved strength retention under sulfate exposure compared with the control. The 0.9% ZnO (ZM3) mixture showed the highest performance in this category, with residual strengths 10.09% and 9.19% higher than MC0 at 180 and 365 days, respectively. The lower dosages (ZM1, ZM2) were less effective, while the highest dosage (ZM4, 1.2% ZnO) resulted in a slight reduction in retained strength compared with ZM3, which may be attributed to nanoparticle agglomeration.

The ternary blends (SZ1 - SZ4) showed higher residual compressive strength after exposure to sulfate solution than the binary systems. The optimum mix SZ3 (8% SF + 0.9% ZnO) resulted in maximum compressive strength retained and the values were 19.63% and 18.86% higher than MC0 at 180 and 365 days respectively. Other ternary mixtures also showed significant improvements with SZ1 and SZ2 showing improvements of about 13–14% while SZ4 (8% SF + 1.2% ZnO) performed slightly worse than SZ3 but also better than the binary mixes. The effect of composition and age was evident across all mixes (Table 4).

Magnesium sulfate exposure is known to be especially aggressive. It promotes decalcification of C-S-H that leads to progressive mechanical degradation of cement matrix40. The better strength retention in the ternary blend (SZ3: 8% SF + 0.9% ZnO) is attributed to the synergetic effect of the decreased availability of calcium hydroxide as a result of the incorporation of SF and the observed densification of hydration products associated with ZnO addition. These effects contribute to reduced mechanical deterioration during prolonged sulfate exposure, resulting in the superior retained strength of SZ3 compared with the other mixes.

Residual compressive strength after chloride exposure of SF- and ZnO-modified concrete

The effect of chloride exposure was evaluated using residual compressive strength of specimens exposed to a chloride-rich environment at 180 and 365 days (Fig. 8). In the silica fume series (SM1–SM4), strength retention under chloride exposure increased with rising SF dosage up to 12%. The SM3 mix (12% SF) exhibited the most favourable performance among the binary SF blends, with approximately 10.45% and 9.95% higher residual strength than the control mix (SM3: 49.55 MPa and 50.33 MPa) at 180 and 365 days, respectively. The lower (SM1, SM2) and higher (SM4) SF content mixes showed reduced strength retention compared with SM3, indicating that 12% is the optimal replacement level to achieve the highest performance under chloride exposure in binary systems.

A similar trend was observed in the ZnO series (ZM1–ZM4), where strength retention under chloride exposure increased with nanoparticle dosage up to 0.9% (ZM3). The best-performing mix in this series was ZM3 (0.9% ZnO), which exhibited 10.01% and 9.10% higher residual strength at 180 and 365 days, respectively (ZM3: 54.51 MPa and 54.91 MPa). Further increase in ZnO content to 1.2% (ZM4) resulted in a reduced gain (6.46% at 365 days, 53.58 MPa), indicating diminishing effectiveness at higher nanoparticle dosages, likely due to less efficient matrix integration.

The ternary blends (SZ1 - SZ4) had the best residual compressive strength after chloride exposure of all investigated mixes. For both sulfate and chloride, mix composition was a dominant influence while exposure duration was a lesser, but statistically significant influence. However, interaction effects were comparatively small with low η²p values (Table 4). The optimized mixture SZ3 (8% SF + 0.9% ZnO) exhibited the highest retained strength with the strength values 18.18% and 17.42% higher than MC0 at 180 and 365 days, respectively (SZ3: 58.56 MPa and 59.10 MPa). The rest of the ternary compositions (SZ1, SZ2 and SZ4) also exhibited significantly higher values for retained strengths than the binary systems, confirming the synergistic effect of the combined SF and ZnO incorporation.

Microstructural analysis

XRD Analysis and Implications for Hydration and Strength at 28 Days

XRD analysis was conducted to identify crystalline and semi-crystalline phases in the cement matrix after 28 days of curing. Four representative mixes MC0, SM3, ZM3, and SZ3 were examined. The diffractograms were refined using Rietveld analysis with background modelling to separate crystalline and amorphous contributions and are presented in Fig. 9. All mixes exhibit the typical phase assemblage of hydrated Portland cement, including residual alite (C₃S), belite (C₂S), portlandite (Ca(OH)₂), ettringite, quartz, and calcite. In addition, a broad diffuse intensity region between 29–32° (2θ) is observed in all samples and is attributed to the poorly crystalline or amorphous calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel41.

Fig. 9XRD patterns showing identified peaks for MC0, SM3, ZM3, and SZ3 mixes at 28 days.

XRD patterns showing identified peaks for MC0, SM3, ZM3, and SZ3 mixes at 28 days.

The control mix MC0 indicates relatively high residual C3S and strong portlandite reflections at about 18°, 34.2°, and 50.3°, which reflects a lower level of hydration and reduced secondary reactions10. This stage assembly is in line with its base position and is associated with its relatively low mechanical functioning. The fact that a significant amount of the unconsumed portlandite is available implies a lower refinement of the microstructure.

Within the SM3 mix, the intensity of the portlandite peak is minimized and the reflection of the residual clinker is minimized, which are indicative of effective pozzolanic use of Ca(OH)2 by silica fume and further hydration of clinker phases. The amplified hump of the 29-32o area is a confirming factor of the amplified formation of disordered C-S-H gel31. These increases in phase are related to the improvements in mechanical performance at subsequent curing ages. The quartz peak of the range at a position of about 26.7o is largely constant in all mixes and is an internal reference that ensures proper normalization and quality of refinement.

The ZM3 and SZ3 mixes have only distinct zinc-related features (Fig. 10). The most noticeable is the reflection at approximately 23o, which has been identified to be due to the zinc hydroxide-based hydration products and is evidently better in the zinc-containing mixes compared to MC0 and SM318. The secondary zinc-related contributions also appear at a comparable position of about 34.5o which partially overlaps the portlandite reflection and at about 30.5o, where amorphous C-S-H hump can coexist with calcium-zinc-silicate-type phases. Another contribution around the area of about 51o is observed especially in SZ3 though this area is overlapping with a secondary portlandite peak hence is interpreted with caution.

Fig. 10Refined XRD patterns for MC0, SM3, ZM3, and SZ3 at 28 days showing specific regions.

Refined XRD patterns for MC0, SM3, ZM3, and SZ3 at 28 days showing specific regions.

The amorphous C-S-H contribution is trend SZ3 = SM3 > ZM3 = MC0, which means that the hydration products were developed more in the modified mixes17. Conversely, additional portlandite intensity is not uniformly reduced in all systems indicating that the addition of zinc does not occur by mainly direct CH consumption but by the development of discrete zinc hydroxide phases and changes in the distribution of hydration products. The intensity of residual alite is high in MC0 and low in all modified mixes, which confirms the acceleration of hydration in the presence of SF and/or ZnO.

Therefore, the crystallized XRD data indicates that only ZM3 and SZ3 contain zinc as discrete zinc-bearing hydration complexes, with zinc hydroxide as the major crystalline form, and Zn-modified C–S–H–type hydration phases identified at 28 days. These zinc-containing phases were resolved by the use of Rietveld refinement and background modelling that was used to define the amorphous contributions. The cumulative decrease in residual clinker phases, the increase of the amorphous C-S-H contribution, and the generation of the secondary zinc phases are responsible as a whole to the enhanced mechanical and durability performance of the modified mixes.

SEM-EDX Analysis and Correlation with Strength Performance

In order to visualize the morphological effect of the phases obtained by XRD, the SEM analysis was performed. The microstructure properties of the cement-based composites, especially the distribution of calcium hydroxide (CH) plates and calcium silicate hydrate (CSH) gel, directly control the mechanical performance42,43. Complementing the mineralogical data, the microstructural characteristics were studied. The SEM micrographs in Fig. 11 show distinct morphological differences between the MC0, SM3, ZM3, and SZ3 specimens at 28 days. Quantitative SEM-EDX analysis (Fig. 12) also shows variations in the Calcium-to-Silicon (Ca/Si) ratio among the mixes17.

SEM micrographs of representative mixes at 28 days: (a) MC0 (b) SM3 (c) ZM3 and (d) SZ3.

Fig. 12EDX analysis of representative mixes (a) MC0 (b) SM3 (c) ZM3 and (d) SZ3 at 28 days representing Ca/Si atomic%.

EDX analysis of representative mixes (a) MC0 (b) SM3 (c) ZM3 and (d) SZ3 at 28 days representing Ca/Si atomic%.

The control mix MC0 exhibits a heterogeneous microstructure, as shown in Fig. 11a, with loosely distributed hydration products. Well-defined plate-like calcium hydroxide crystals and discrete ettringite needles are clearly visible. The observations are consistent with the strong portlandite reflections and higher residual clinker content observed in the XRD patterns (Fig. 12a). SEM–EDX measurements indicate a high Ca/Si atomic ratio of approximately 3.26, reflecting the presence of calcium-rich phases within the analyzed interaction volumes.

In SF-modified systems, the pozzolanic reaction plays an important role44. SF reacts with the CH generated during the hydration of ordinary portland cement to produce some additional C-S-H. In the SM3 mix, the microstructure shown in Fig. 11b appears comparatively more cohesive, with an increased presence of gel-like C–S–H and a reduced visibility of large portlandite plates. Residual CH and unreacted silica fume particles remain visible, indicating partial refinement at this replacement level. The measured Ca/Si ratio decreases to approximately 1.44, in agreement with the reduced portlandite intensity and the enhanced diffuse hump between 29–32° observed in the XRD patterns (Fig. 9), which reflects increased formation of disordered C–S–H17. SF inclusion in SM3 improves pore structure refinement and connectivity in support of Gupta et al. (2020), which linked SF replacement with a reduction in cumulative pore volume and pore diameter45.

The ZM3 mix exhibits a denser and more uniform distribution of hydration products, as shown in Fig. 11c. Interlinked C-S-H areas dominate the matrix, and less distinct CH plates are seen at the same magnification. The Ca/Si ratio of approximately 2.6 remains higher than that of SM3, which is in agreement with XRD results (Fig. 10). The addition of zinc does not influence the reduction of portlandite but primarily affects the action of the pozzolanic reactions. The SEM morphology is connected to localized refinement of the matrices that can be found in the presence of zinc-related hydration products, detected through diffraction46. This is associated with ZM3 high-strength retention that validates the dual action of ZnO. When incorporated in appropriate amounts, ZnO contributes to the hydration process in a two-fold manner; as a nanofiller, which contributes by enhancing the particle packing and as a nucleation site, which contributes through the acceleration of the initial hydration reactions47.

Ternary mix SZ3 exhibits the most refined and homogeneous microstructure among the examined samples, as shown in Fig. 11d. It is predominantly gel-dominated and contains very little visible portlandite, with reduced microstructural heterogeneity in the matrix. The Ca/Si ratio of 1.27 is the lowest one of all mixes in compliance with the combined XRD findings of the decreased portlandite intensity and the rise of amorphous C-S-H contribution (Fig. 9) and the zinc-bearing phases (Fig. 10). These SEM-EDX results indicate that the hydration products have a modified chemical composition with an overall refinement15. It is a two-fold action. SF reacts with surplus calcium hydroxide and provides long-term pozzolanic strength. Whereas, ZnO improves the hydration and nucleation of the particles. It results in a physically dense, yet chemically homogeneous matrix33.

Therefore, the enhanced performance of SF and ZnO modified cementitious systems, especially of ternary blends is not by chance but the direct outcome of the interactions between chemical and physical processes. The resulting product of the pozzolanic reactivity (SF) together with the hydration acceleration caused by nucleation (ZnO) is the increased proportion of the CSH, limit CH, and forms a compact cementitious structure. This cohesive mechanism leads to improved compressive, split tensile, and flexural strengths, as well as protection against sulphate and chloride exposure.

Conclusion

The present study reports the effect of partially substituting cement with SF and/or ZnO nanoparticles in binary and ternary blends to develop concrete with enhanced strength and durability. Based on the experimental findings, the following conclusions are drawn:

(1) Optimization of Dosage: The combined substitution of 8 wt% SF and 0.9 wt% ZnO (mix SZ3) yielded the most favorable balance of fresh and hardened properties, outperforming binary and control mixes.(2) Mechanical Performance: The optimized ternary blend exhibited statistically significant strength enhancements (p < 0.05) at 365 days, with increases of 8.96% in compressive, 4.80% in splitting tensile, and 4.70% in flexural strength relative to the control. These gains are attributed to the complementary effects of SF’s pozzolanic reaction and the nucleation sites provided by ZnO.(3) Durability: Resistance to aggressive environments was notably improved. The ternary matrix reduced the strength loss under sulphate attack by 18.86% and increased the residual strength under chloride exposure by 17.42%. These improvements are attributed to matrix refinement and reduced calcium hydroxide availability, which collectively enhance resistance to mechanical degradation under chemical exposure.(4) Microstructural Mechanism: SEM and XRD analyses confirmed that the performance improvements stem from a highly densified microstructure. Zn-modified C–S–H–type hydration phases were identified at 28 days, along with refined microstructural features associated with nanoscale filling effects. These characteristics are consistent with the observed durability performance, rather than direct measurements of transport inhibition.
