Upregulation of PTPRO by WT1 alleviates podocyte injury in diabetic nephropathy through inhibiting the c-Abl/p53 pathway
Authors: Hengjiang Guo, Li Wang, Yiru Tong, Yan Jiang, Xingmei Yao, Limin Lu, Rong Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPodocyte injury plays a central role in the pathogenesis of diabetic nephropathy (DN). Protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type O (PTPRO) has been implicated in glomerular disease, yet its specific role and regulatory mechanism in DN remain poorly understood. Here, we demonstrated that PTPRO expression was significantly downregulated in the glomeruli of DN mice and patients with DN, as well as in cultured podocytes exposed to diabetic stressors. Functional studies revealed that Ptpro knockdown induced podocyte injury, characterized by reduced Nephrin and Podocin expression, increased apoptosis, actin cytoskeleton disruption, enhanced migration, and reduced adhesion. Conversely, PTPRO overexpression ameliorated high glucose (HG)-induced podocyte damage. Mechanistically, PTPRO interacted with and dephosphorylated c-Abl, thereby suppressing the c-Abl/p53 signaling pathway. Pharmacological activation of c-Abl abolished the protective effects of PTPRO. Furthermore, we identified PTPRO as a direct transcriptional target of WT1, a podocyte master transcription factor downregulated in DN. WT1 overexpression restored PTPRO expression, suppressed c-Abl/p53 signaling, and ameliorated HG-induced podocyte injury in vitro. In vivo, podocyte-specific overexpression of WT1 via adeno-associated virus delivery in db/db mice attenuated proteinuria, glomerulosclerosis, and podocyte foot process effacement, accompanied by restored PTPRO expression and inhibition of the c-Abl/p53 pathway. Collectively, these findings establish that PTPRO functions downstream of WT1 to protect podocytes in DN by suppressing c-Abl/p53 signaling. Introduction

Diabetic nephropathy (DN), a major complication of diabetes mellitus, has become the leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) worldwide [1]. Despite extensive prevention and treatment efforts, DN remains a significant public health burden, underscoring the urgent need to elucidate new pathophysiological mechanisms and develop novel therapeutic strategies [2]. Clinically, DN is characterized by progressive proteinuria and glomerular damage, including glomerular basement membrane (GBM) thickening, mesangial expansion, and glomerulosclerosis [3]. Podocyte injury is believed to play a central role in this pathological process [4]. As highly differentiated epithelial cells, podocytes constitute a key component of glomerular filtration barrier (GFB); Consequently, podocyte injury results in disruption of the GFB and the development of proteinuria [4]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying podocyte injury in DN have not been fully clarified.

Protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor O (PTPRO), originally named glomerular epithelial protein 1 (GLEPP1), was first identified and cloned in a search for podocyte-specific proteins that might regulate glomerular structure and function [5]. It contains a single intracellular phosphatase domain that catalyzes tyrosine dephosphorylation, and a large extracellular domain containing eight fibronectin type III-like repeats [6]. In adult tissues, PTPRO was identified to be highly expressed in the kidney and the brain [6, 7]. Within the kidney, it is specifically localized to the apical membrane of podocyte foot processes [6], and loss of PTPRO expression has been observed in various proteinuric nephropathies, including primary focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) and IgA nephropathy (IgAN), as well as in experimental models of podocyte damage [8, 9]. Furthermore, mutations in PTPRO cause childhood-onset nephrotic syndrome [10] and PTPRO-deficient mice exhibit abnormal podocyte morphology, reduced glomerular filtration rate, and hypertension following uninephrectomy [11]. More recently, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified that a single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) in the PTPRO gene locus is associated with reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in diabetic patients [12]. Collectively, these findings establish an essential role for PTPRO in maintaining podocyte structure and function. However, nearly three decades after its identification in podocytes, the functional role of PTPRO in the development of DN and its underlying pathophysiological mechanisms remain largely unknown.

c-Abl, a member of the Abl tyrosine kinase family, regulates diverse cellular processes, including cell survival, proliferation, motility, adhesion, and morphogenesis [13]. Generally, c-Abl exerts its function by relying on kinase activity, which is associated with phosphorylation at Tyr245 and Tyr412 [14–16]. Under physiological conditions, c-Abl is maintained in an auto-inhibited, non-phosphorylated state. In response to growth factors, DNA damage, or oxidative stress, phosphorylation at these sites stabilizes c-Abl in an open and active conformation, enabling it to phosphorylate and bind multiple targets to regulate various signaling pathways. Emerging evidence highlights a critical role for c-Abl in podocyte injury during DN. Specifically, c-Abl contributes to glucose- or angiotensin II-induced apoptosis by regulating the p53 and Nephrin-Akt signaling pathways in podocytes [17, 18]. Nevertheless, the upstream signals that modulate c-Abl activity in podocytes remain unclear. Intriguingly, a recent study reported that PTPRO can dephosphorylate and inactivate c-Abl during retinal axon development, thereby identifying c-Abl as a novel substrate of PTPRO [19]. To date, whether PTPRO directly regulates c‑Abl signaling and mediates its function in podocytes remains unexplored.

As a widely recognized podocyte biomarker, the Wilms’ tumor-1 gene (WT1) encodes a podocyte-specific transcription factor in the kidney that directly regulates a large proportion of podocyte-specific genes [20]. Mutations in WT1 cause a spectrum of renal manifestations, including Wilms’ tumor [21, 22], Denys-Drash syndrome [23], and Frasier syndrome [24]. Notably, WT1 expression is downregulated during DN progression, and stabilization of WT1 expression can prevent podocyte injury in DN [25]. Recent chromatin immunoprecipitation-sequencing (ChIP-Seq) analyses have provided insight into the genomic DNA binding characteristics of WT1, revealing that nearly half of all podocyte-specific genes are direct WT1 targets [26, 27]. Examination of these ChIP-Seq datasets revealed a distinct WT1-bound peak within the PTPRO gene locus [26]. Supporting the functional relevance of this binding, PTPRO expression was significantly reduced in Wt1(+ KTS) knockout mice compared to wild-type mice [26]. Together, these data strongly suggest that PTPRO is a direct transcriptional target of WT1.

In the present study, we investigated the functional relevance of PTPRO, WT1, and c-Abl in high glucose (HG)-stimulated podocytes in vitro and in the diabetic db/db mouse model in vivo.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

The conditionally immortalized mouse podocyte cell line, originally generated by Dr. Peter Mundel (Massachusetts General Hospital, Harvard Medical School) and kindly provided by Professor Niansong Wang (Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital, China), was cultured as described previously [28]. Briefly, cells were propagated at the permissive temperature of 33 °C in RPMI 1640 medium (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and 20 U/mL recombinant mouse interferon-γ (IFN-γ; Peprotech, Cranbury, NJ, USA). To induce differentiation, podocytes were cultured in IFN-γ-free medium at 37 °C for 7 days prior to being used for experiments. Differentiated podocytes were serum-starved overnight in medium containing 1% FBS prior to stimulation with the following agents: (1) high glucose (HG; D-glucose at final concentrations of 20 or 40 mM) for 48 h, medium contained 5 mM D-glucose as low glucose (LG) control, mannitol (M, 25 mM mannitol + 5 mM D-glucose) was added in the medium as the osmotic pressure control; (2) palmitate (100 or 200 µM) for 24 h; (3) adriamycin (ADR; 0.5–1 µg/mL) for 24 h; and (4) tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α; 10 ng/mL) for 48 h.

Adenovirus-mediated overexpression, siRNA-mediated knockdown, and plasmid transfections

For PTPRO overexpression, podocytes were transduced with a recombinant adenovirus carrying the mouse Ptpro gene (Ad-Ptpro; NM_001164401.2) or a control adenovirus (Ad-NC), both constructed by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 20 for 24 h prior to treatment with HG. For gene silencing, podocytes were transfected with 50 nM short interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting Ptpro (si-Ptpro), c-Abl (si-c-Abl) or Wt1 (si-Wt1), or with a negative control siRNA (si-NC). For WT1 overexpression, cells cultured in 6-well dishes were transfected with 2.5 µg per well of a plasmid encoding the mouse Wt1 coding sequence (CDS) with a 3 × Flag tag (OE-Wt1), or an empty vector control (OE-NC). The plasmid and siRNA transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 and Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (both from Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), respectively, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The siRNAs targeting mouse Ptpro and Wt1 were designed and synthesized by OBIO Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and the siRNA targeting mouse c-Abl was synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The siRNA sequences used were listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Construction of substrate-trapping mutant of Ptpro

The full-length mouse Ptpro CDS (NM_001164401.2) was synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and cloned into the mammalian expression vector pcDNA3.4-3×Flag to generate the pcDNA3.4-Ptpro-3 × Flag construct. Generation of the substrate-trapping mutant of Ptpro was performed as previously described [29, 30]. The catalytic site cys1118 and the conserved asp 34 aa upstream of the catalytic site cys1118 were both mutated to improve PTPRO substrate-trapping efficiency. The double-point mutant fragment of Ptpro (C1118S/D1084A) were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The resulting mutant product was then swapped with the wild-type (WT) sequence in the pcDNA3.4-Ptpro-3×Flag construct, and the mutations (CS/DA) were confirmed by sequencing.

Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

For exogenous Co-IP, 1 × 107 HEK293T cells were co-transfected with 5 µg of pcDNA3.4-Ptpro-3 × Flag (WT or CS/DA mutant) and 5 µg of pcDNA3.1-c-Abl-HA using Lipo8000™ Transfection Reagent (C0533; Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Separately, podocytes were transfected with 5 µg of pcDNA3.4-Ptpro-3×Flag (WT or CS/DA mutant) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). At 48 h post-transfection, cell lysates were prepared using Cell Lysis Buffer for Western and IP (P0013J; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with protease inhibitors (4693132001; Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and phosphatase inhibitors (4906845001; Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The lysates were centrifuged at 8000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. The supernatants were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with 5 µg of the following specific antibodies: mouse anti-Flag (F1804; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), mouse anti-HA (sc-7392; Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX, USA), or matched isotype control IgG (A7028; Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Protein A/G beads were then added for 4 h. After washing with IP assay buffer, bound proteins were eluted by boiling in sample buffer for 5 min. The immunoprecipitated complexes were analyzed by Western blot with the indicated antibodies to assess the PTPRO-c-Abl interaction.

For endogenous Co-IP, 2 × 107 podocytes were harvested and lysed in the same Cell Lysis Buffer for Western and IP supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. PTPRO was immunoprecipitated using a rabbit anti-PTPRO antibody (PAB321Mu01; Cloud-clone, Wuhan, China).

For c-Abl dephosphorylation experiments, cell lysates of HEK293T cells co-transfected with pcDNA3.4-Ptpro-3 × Flag (WT or CS/DA mutant) and pcDNA3.1-c-Abl-HA were prepared as described above. c-Abl was immunoprecipitated using the anti-HA antibody (sc-7392; Santa Cruz) and then immunoblotted with an anti-p-c-Abl (Tyr412) antibody (#2865; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA).

Cell migration and wound-healing assays

For migration assays, Transwell inserts (8 μm pore size; Corning, NY, USA) were placed in 6-well plates containing RPMI 1640 medium. Podocytes (3 × 105 cells) were seeded in the upper inserts and allowed to migrate for 24 h at 37 °C. Non-migrated cells on the upper surface were removed with a cotton swab. Migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), stained with Crystal Violet Staining Solution (C0121; Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and counted in five random fields per membrane using a 20 × objective.

For wound-healing assays, podocytes (5 × 10⁵ cells/well) were cultured in 6-well plates until a confluent monolayer was formed. Cells were serum-starved in medium containing 0.1% FBS for 12 h. A linear scratch was made in the monolayer using a sterile 200-µL pipette tip. Debris was removed by washing with PBS, and the cells were cultured in fresh medium. Images of the same scratch area were captured at 0 and 24 h using an Olympus Inverted Microscope (BX53; Olympus, Japan) with a 10× objective. Wound closure was measured using Image J software (NIH, MD, USA) and calculated as: Percent wound closure = [(width at 0 h – width at 24 h) / width at 0 h] × 100%.

Cell adhesion assay

The adhesion assay using crystal violet staining was performed as previously described [31]. Equal amounts of differentiated podocytes were seeded on a collagen type I-coated 96-well plate at a density of 105 cells per well in five wells, and allowed to adhere for 1 h. After the medium and nonadherent cells were removed by tapping the inverted plate and washing gently twice with PBS, the adherent cells were fixed with 4% PFA and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 60 min at room temperature. Cells were washed and then incubated with 10% acetic acid on a shaker for 5 min. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a Tecan Spark multimode microplate reader.

Flow cytometry analysis

Podocyte apoptosis was evaluated using the Annexin V-PE/7-AAD Apoptosis Detection Kit (40310ES50; Yeasen, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were harvested with 0.25% trypsin, washed, and resuspended in binding buffer. The cell suspension was incubated with 5 µL of Annexin V-PE and 5 µL of 7-AAD in the dark for 15 min. Apoptosis was immediately analyzed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). Cells staining Annexin V-positive/7-AAD-negative and Annexin V-positive/7-AAD-positive were considered early and late apoptotic, respectively.

Cell cycle analysis was performed using Cell Cycle and Apoptosis Analysis Kit (40301ES50; Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Podocytes were harvested, washed once with ice-cold PBS, and then fixed at -20 °C overnight with 70% ethanol. The fixed podocytes were washed twice with PBS, then incubated with assay buffer containing RNase A and propidium iodide provided in the kit for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The samples were analyzed by CytoFLEX flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, USA). Cell cycle distribution was analyzed using FlowJo software (BD Life Sciences).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

ChIP assays were performed using a commercial kit (P2078; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, podocytes (2 × 10⁷ cells) were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min, and the reaction was quenched with 1.25 M glycine. Cells were lysed with the provided buffers, and the chromatin-containing supernatant was incubated overnight at 4 °C with 4 µg of either rabbit anti-WT1 antibody (ab89901; Abcam, USA) or rabbit control IgG (AC005; Abclonal, Wuhan, China). Protein A/G-conjugated agarose beads were added for 1 h to capture the immune complexes. After extensive washing, the chromatin was eluted from the beads, and crosslinks were reversed by incubation at 65 °C for 4 h. Proteins and RNA were then removed by sequential digestion with proteinase K and RNase A. The purified DNA was analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) using Bio-Rad CFX Opus 96 system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with the the primers specific for the Ptpro gene (Supplementary Table S3). The ChIP-qPCR protocal was: 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min.

Animals

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care Committee of Shanghai Children’s Hospital and conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines. 12-week-old male type 2 diabetic db/db mice (n = 24) and age-matched non-diabetic db/m littermates (n = 12) were purchased from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China). 6-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were obtained from Shanghai Shengchang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (License No. SCXK (Hu) 2021–0002). All animals were housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) facilities with free access to food and water, under a constant temperature of 25 °C and 50% humidity.

Streptozotocin/high-fat diet (STZ/HFD)-induced DN

A uninephrectomized, HFD-fed mouse model treated with STZ was used to accelerate the development of DN [28, 32]. 6-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were fed either a HFD (60 kcal% fat, D12492; Research Diets) or a control diet for 4 weeks and then underwent uninephrectomy. After a one-week recovery period, mice were fasted for 4 h and then injected intraperitoneally with STZ (80 mg/kg body weight) or vehicle (control) daily for 3 consecutive days to induce partial insulin deficiency. All mice were maintained on their respective diets for an additional 12 weeks with free access to food and water.

In vivo administration of adeno-associated virus (AAV)

Recombinant AAV9 (rAAV9) is the most efficient rAAV serotype for kidney gene delivery [33]. To achieve podocyte-specific gene delivery, a recombinant rAAV9 vector containing the Nphs1 promoter was used to drive expression of Wt1. 12-week-old db/db mice received in situ renal injections of either 2 × 10¹² genomic particles of AAV-Nphs1-Wt1-GFP (AAV-Wt1) or AAV-Nphs1-GFP (AAV-NC), prepared by Hanbio Biotechnology Inc. (Shanghai, China), at five independent points per kidney. At 8 weeks post-AAV delivery, all mice were euthanized, and blood samples and kidneys were harvested for biochemical and gene expression analysis and histological examination.

Measurement of physiological parameters and biochemical analysis

Mice were housed in metabolic cages for urine collection every 4 weeks to determine the urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR). Urinary albumin was measured using an Albumin ELISA Kit (CEB028Mu; Cloud-clone, Wuhan, China), and creatinine concentration was measured using a Creatinine Assay Kit (C011-2; Nanjing Jiancheng, Nanjing, China). Fasting blood glucose levels were monitored with the Omron HEA-230 Glucometer using one drop of tail blood. At the end of the study, blood samples were drawn from orbit and centrifuged for serum collection. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) was measured using a urea nitrogen measurement kit (C013-2; Nanjing Jiancheng, Nanjing, China). Serum HbA1c was measured using Mouse Glycated Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ELISA Kit (CEA190Mu; Cloud-clone, Wuhan, China). Serum insulin was measured using Mouse INS (Insulin) ELISA Kit (E-EL-M3119; Elabscience‌, Wuhan, China). Determination of Triglycerides (TGs) was performed by TG assay kit (A110-1-1; Nanjing Jiancheng, Nanjing, China). Total cholesterol levels were measured using a Total cholesterol assay kit (A111-1-1; Nanjing Jiancheng, Nanjing, China).

Renal histology

Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining was performed to assess renal pathological changes. Mouse kidneys were perfused with 4% PFA in PBS, and paraffin-embedded sections (5-µm thickness) were prepared. Sections were stained with a PAS staining kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Images were captured using an Olympus BX51 microscope at 40 × magnification. Mesangial matrix expansion was quantified as the PAS-positive area per glomerular area using Image-Pro Plus software. Glomerulosclerosis was semi-quantitatively scored using a previously described five-grade method [34].

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

For ultrastructural analysis, 1 mm³ pieces of renal cortex were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 72 h at 4 °C. Samples were washed with cacodylate buffer, post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide, stained en bloc with uranyl acetate, and embedded in Spurr’s epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and examined using a Hitachi 7700 transmission electron microscope (Tokyo, Japan). Foot process width was measured using ImageJ software as previously described [35].

TUNEL assay

Apoptotic cells in kidney tissues were detected using the ApopTag Plus Peroxidase In Situ Apoptosis Detection Kit (S7101; Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. TUNEL-positive cells were semi-quantified by counting 30 randomly selected glomeruli per mouse.

Immunofluorescence

For cultured cells, podocytes grown on coverslips were fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with bovine serum albumin (BSA). Cells were then incubated with primary antibodies against Podocin (ab50339; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), PTPRO (PAB321Mu01; Cloud-clone, China), Paxillin (R010349; Epizyme, Shanghai, China), β1 integrin (12594-1-AP; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), phospho-c-Abl (Tyr412) (sc-293130; Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX, USA), or c-Abl (sc-23; Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX, USA), followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 594-, Alexa Fluor 488-, or Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam, USA). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were obtained using a Leica laser scanning microscope (DM IRB, Germany).

For tissue analysis, frozen kidney sections (5-µm thickness) were fixed with pre-chilled acetone at -20 °C for 10 min, blocked with 5% BSA, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PTPRO (PAB321Mu01; Cloud-clone, Wuhan, China), Synaptopodin (sc-21537; Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX, USA), Podocin (ab50339; Abcam, USA), Desmin (16520-1-AP; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), α3 integrin (P103897; Epizyme, Shanghai, China), or β1 integrin (12594-1-AP; Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Following incubation with appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies, images were captured using the same Leica microscope.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded kidney sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval by autoclaving in citrate buffer (0.01 M, pH 6.0) for 15 min. After blocking with 5% BSA, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against WT1 (ab89901; Abcam, USA), PTPRO (PAB321Mu01; Cloud-clone, China), phospho-c-Abl (Tyr412) (sc-293130; Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX, USA), or phospho-p53 (Ser15) (28961-1-AP; Proteintech, China). Detection was performed using a Polymer HRP (Mouse/Rabbit) IHC Kit with DAB (BDAA0191; Biodragon, Jiangsu) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Images were captured, and the number of WT1-positive cells and the integrated optical density (IOD) for p-c-Abl and p-p53 were quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6 software (Media Cybernetics, USA).

Western blot analysis

Total protein from cultured podocytes or renal cortex was extracted using RIPA buffer (P0038; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PTPRO (PAB321Mu01; Cloud-clone, Wuhan, China), Nephrin (BA1669; BOSTER, Wuhan, China), Podocin (BA1688; BOSTER, Wuhan, China), Desmin (16520-1-AP; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), Paxillin (R010349; Epizyme, Shanghai, China), α3 integrin (P103897; Epizyme, Shanghai, China), β1 integrin (12594-1-AP; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), p-c-Abl (Tyr412) (#2865; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), c-Abl (sc-23; Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX, USA), p-p53 (Ser15) (28961-1-AP; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), p53 (sc-126; Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX, USA), and WT1 (ab89901; Abcam, USA). After washing, membranes were incubated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence solution (NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China) and quantified by densitometry using ImageJ software.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the renal cortex using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the Hifair® III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (11141ES60; Yeasen, Shanghai, China). qRT-PCR was performed using Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (11201ES08; Yeasen, Shanghai, China) on a Bio-Rad CFX Opus 96 system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The cycling conditions were: 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. The primer sequences for mouse Ptpro were listed in Supplementary Table S2. Gene expression levels were normalized to β-actin and calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method.

Phalloidin staining of F-actin

Podocytes cultured on collagen I-coated coverslips were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA for 30 min, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min. After blocking with 2% BSA in PBS for 30 min, cells were incubated with TRITC-conjugated phalloidin (Molecular Probes, USA) for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark to label F-actin. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. Coverslips were mounted, and images were acquired by confocal microscopy. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of F-actin fibers was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, MD, USA).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.4.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc., SanDiego, CA, USA). Comparisons between two groups were made using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were made using one-way ANOVA followed by multiple comparisons. P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

PTPRO expression is decreased in podocytes of DN models and patients

To investigate the expression pattern of PTPRO in DN, we first examined db/db mice, a spontaneous model of type 2 diabetes (T2D). qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses revealed that both mRNA and protein levels of Ptpro were significantly downregulated in the renal cortex of 16-week-old db/db mice compared to their non-diabetic db/m littermates (Fig. 1A and B). Immunofluorescence staining showed that PTPRO was exclusively localized in podocytes, as indicated by its co-localization with the podocyte-specific marker Synaptopodin in control db/m mice, while its expression was markedly reduced in db/db mice (Fig. 1C). Consistent results were obtained in the kidneys of STZ/HFD-induced diabetic mice, a model of advanced T2D (Fig. 1D–F). In vitro, PTPRO expression was significantly decreased in podocytes exposed to HG (Fig. 1G), palmitate (Fig. 1H), or TNF-α (Fig. 1I). In addition, the reduction of PTPRO expression was also observed in ADR-treated podocytes (Fig. 1J), suggesting that the decreased PTPRO expression may play an important role in podocyte injury under different pathological conditions.

To further assess PTPRO expression in human glomerular disease, we analyzed publicly available transcriptomic datasets from the Nephroseq database. PTPRO mRNA levels were significantly reduced in a range of glomerular diseases, including DN, FSGS, and IgAN (Fig. 1K–M). Moreover, PTPRO expression positively correlated with eGFR (Fig. 1N), suggesting a potential link between PTPRO downregulation and renal dysfunction.

Knockdown of Ptpro leads to podocyte injury

To investigate the impact of PTPRO downregulation on podocyte structure and function, we knocked down Ptpro in normally cultured mouse podocytes using siRNA interference. An effective Ptpro siRNA, si-Ptpro3#, was first selected (Fig. 2A). We subsequently found that Ptpro knockdown resulted in decreased expression of the podocyte marker proteins Nephrin and Podocin and the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, and increased expression of pro-apoptotic protein Bax (Fig. 2B). Consistently, flow cytometry analysis showed that Ptpro knockdown induced podocyte apoptosis (Fig. 2C). In addition, Ptpro knockdown disrupted the actin cytoskeleton (Fig. 2D). Podocyte cytoskeleton rearrangement is closely related with the altered cell adhesion and migration in disease state [36], therefore, we examined cell motility using a transwell assay and a wound healing assay. We found that Ptpro knockdown significantly enhanced podocyte migratory capacity (Fig. 2E and F). Adhesion of podocytes to collagen type I-coated plates was also studied using crystal violet staining. Compared with control podocytes, Ptpro-knockdown podocytes exhibited decreased adhesion as well as reduced expression of the adhesion-associated protein Paxillin, a focal adhesion protein, and α3β1 integrin, which serves as the primary integrin receptor mediating podocyte adhesion to the GBM (Fig. 2G-I). Together, these results suggest that PTPRO plays a critical role in maintaining podocyte morphology, adhesion and survival.

Overexpression of PTPRO ameliorates HG-induced podocyte injury

To investigate the role of PTPRO in HG-induced podocyte injury, we infected podocytes with Ad-Ptpro prior to HG exposure. Successful overexpression of PTPRO was confirmed by Western blot analysis (Fig. 3A). Functional assays revealed that HG induced a significant increase in podocyte apoptosis, whereas PTPRO overexpression significantly reduced this effect, as determined by flow cytometry (Fig. 3B). Phalloidin staining showed that HG induced derangement of the F-actin cytoskeleton, which was alleviated by PTPRO overexpression (Fig. 3C). Moreover, Transwell and wound healing assays showed that HG-induced podocyte migration was markedly suppressed upon PTPRO overexpression (Fig. 3D and E). Consistently, Western blot analysis indicated that HG reduced the protein levels of Nephrin, Podocin, and Bcl-2, while increasing the expression of desmin, a marker of podocyte injury, Bax, and Cleaved Caspase 3. However, these molecular alterations were significantly recovered by PTPRO overexpression (Fig. 3F). Additionally, podocyte adhesion was suppressed by HG, along with the downregulation of Paxillin and α3β1 integrin; however, these alterations were significantly prevented by PTPRO overexpression (Fig. 3G-I). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that overexpression of PTPRO protects against HG-induced podocyte injury.

PTPRO interacts with c-Abl and inhibits HG-induced activation of the c-Abl/p53 pathway

To elucidate the mechanism by which PTPRO protects against HG-induced podocyte injury, we searched the literature for potential downstream substrates of PTPRO. One study identified c-Abl as a novel substrate of PTPRO in neurons, prompting us to investigate whether a similar interaction occurs in podocytes. We first performed Co-IP experiments using lysates from HEK293T cells co-transfected with Flag-PTPRO and HA-c-Abl. Reciprocal pulldown with anti-FLAG or anti-HA antibodies followed by immunoblotting confirmed the binding between PTPRO and c-Abl (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, we performed Co-IP experiments using WT and a substrate-trapping CS/DA mutant of PTPRO, in which the conserved cysteine (C) and aspartic acid (D) residues in the PTP domain are converted to serine (S) and alanine (A), respectively. This mutant is deficient in phosphatase activity but retains the ability to recognize and stably bind substrates [37]. HA-tagged c-Abl along with Flag-tagged PTPRO was transiently expressed in H293T cells. PTPRO was immunoprecipitated using anti-Flag antibody, and the CS/DA mutant of PTPRO showed significantly enhanced binding to c-Abl compared with the WT (Fig. 4B). Conversely, c-Abl was immunoprecipitated using the anti-HA antibody. Immunoblot analysis of immunoprecipitated c-Abl and cell lysates with phospho-specific c-Abl (Tyr412) antibodies revealed that c-Abl co-expressed with PTPRO-WT was in a dephosphorylated state compared with c-Abl co-expressed with PTPRO-CS/DA or vector alone (Fig. 4C). These results indicate that c-Abl is recognized as a substrate of PTPRO. This interaction between PTPRO and c-Abl was further validated in podocyte lysates, where endogenous PTPRO co-immunoprecipitated with c-Abl (Fig. 4D). Confocal microscopy demonstrated that PTPRO and c-Abl colocalized at the foot process membrane under normal conditions. However, HG stimulation markedly reduced this colocalization (Fig. 4E), which was further confirmed by the Co-IP assay (Fig. 4F), suggesting that HG disrupts the interaction between these two proteins.

Given that c-Abl has been implicated in HG- and angiotensin II-induced apoptosis through p53 signaling, we examined the c-Abl/p53 pathway in HG-treated podocytes. Western blot analysis showed that HG induced a concentration-dependent increase in the phosphorylation of both c-Abl and p53, indicating activation of this pathway (Fig. 4G). c-Abl knockdown by siRNA remarkably inhibited p53 activation and HG-induced podocyte apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. S1A and B). At the molecular level, c-Abl knockdown significantly reversed the HG-induced downregulation of Nephrin, Podocin, and Bcl-2, while suppressing the HG-induced upregulation of Bax and cleaved caspase-3 (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Furthermore, c-Abl knockdown significantly inhibited HG-induced F-actin collapse, as well as the enhanced cell migration and reduced cell adhesion (Supplementary Fig. S1D-H). When PTPRO-WT or the CS/DA mutant were overexpressed in podocytes under HG condition, the binding between PTPRO and c-Abl was enhanced by the CS/DA mutation (Fig. 4H). Moreover, HG-induced p-c-Abl (Tyr412) level was significantly reduced by PTPRO-WT but enhanced by the PTPRO-CS/DA mutant (Fig. 4I). Furthermore, Ptpro knockdown elevated basal levels of phospho-c-Abl and phospho-p53, whereas PTPRO overexpression significantly attenuated HG-induced c-Abl and p53 phosphorylation (Fig. 4J and K). Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated that PTPRO overexpression blocked the HG-triggered expression and nuclear translocation of both phospho-c-Abl and phospho-p53, reinforcing the inhibitory effect of PTPRO on this signaling axis (Fig. 4L).

p53 is involved in several fundamental cellular signaling pathways, among which the most meaningful pathways are Bax/Caspase-dependent apoptosis regulation and p21-mediated cell-cycle arrest [38, 39]. Podocytes are terminally differentiated cells that remain in a post-mitotic state with limited regenerative capacity; re-entry into the mitotic phase triggers mitotic catastrophe and subsequent cell death. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that 13.6% of podocytes resided in the G2/M phase under low glucose (LG) conditions, whereas high glucose (HG) treatment increased this population to approximately 23.5%, indicating G2/M arrest. Notably, PTPRO overexpression abrogated this HG-induced G2/M arrest (Fig. 4M). p21, the first identified transcriptional target of p53, not only governs the G1/S checkpoint but also regulates the G2 checkpoint via Cyclin B [40]. HG treatment concurrently elevated p21 expression, an effect that was partially reversed by AdPtpro transfection, indicating that PTPRO mitigated cell cycle arrest at G2/M phase and podocyte mitotic catastrophe (Fig. 4N).

Collectively, all these findings indicate that PTPRO suppresses HG-induced podocyte injury by inhibiting the c-Abl/p53 pathway.

Activation of c-Abl blocks the protective effects of PTPRO against HG-induced podocyte injury

To determine whether c-Abl mediates the protective role of PTPRO in podocyte injury induced by HG, we treated podocytes with DPH, a specific c-Abl activator. DPH treatment significantly reversed the suppressive effect of PTPRO overexpression on the activation of the c-Abl/p53 signaling pathway under HG conditions (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, both immunofluorescence staining and Western blot analysis showed that DPH effectively reversed the restoration of Nephrin, Podocin and Bcl-2 expression and attenuated the suppression of Desmin and Bax mediated by PTPRO overexpression (Fig. 5B and C). Consistently, DPH treatment largely abolished the protective effects conferred by PTPRO overexpression against HG-induced apoptosis, F-actin cytoskeleton collapse, and impaired cell migration (Fig. 5D-G). Moreover, the protective effect of PTPRO against HG-induced adhesion loss was largely abrogated by DPH (Fig. 5H–J), and the inhibitory effect of PTPRO on HG-induced G2/M arrest and p21 expression was also largely abolished (Fig. 5K and L). These results suggest that activation of c-Abl counteracts the protective effects of PTPRO against HG-induced podocyte injury, indicating that c-Abl functions downstream of PTPRO in regulating podocyte integrity and survival under diabetic conditions.

WT1 transcriptionally activates Ptpro and ameliorates c-Abl/p53-mediated apoptosis in HG-stimulated podocytes

The downregulation of Ptpro mRNA in DN suggests regulation by upstream transcriptional factors. Analysis of publicly available ChIP-seq data revealed that Ptpro may be a target of WT1, a key podocyte transcription factor, with a distinct WT1-bound peak identified within the Ptpro gene locus (Chr6: 137294798 ~ 137295717) [26]. Bioinformatics analysis using the JASPAR database further predicted a potential WT1 binding motif within this region (Chr6: 137295383 ~ 137295392). ChIP-qPCR assays confirmed that WT1 bound to this site, and that HG treatment reduced WT1 enrichment at this locus (Fig. 6A). Western blot analysis revealed that HG decreased WT1 expression in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 6B), which correlated positively with PTPRO expression. Knockdown of Wt1 significantly downregulated Nephrin and Podocin expression, suppressed PTPRO, and activated the downstream c-Abl/p53 pathway (Fig. 6C and D). Conversely, Wt1 overexpression markedly inhibited PTPRO deficiency-mediated c-Abl/p53 activation in HG-incubated podocytes (Fig. 6E). Meanwhile, Wt1 overexpression significantly ameliorated HG-induced injury, as evidenced by restored Nephrin and Podocin expression, reduced apoptosis, recovery of the F-actin cytoskeleton, and restoration of normal cell migration capacity (Fig. 6F-J). Together, these findings demonstrate that the protective effects of Wt1 overexpression against HG-induced podocyte injury is associated with PTPRO/c-Abl/p53 signaling pathway.

To further investigate whether PTPRO is an important mediator of WT1-conferred effects on c-Abl/p53-mediated podocyte injury, we treated podocytes with Wt1 overexpression plus Ptpro siRNA under HG conditions. Ptpro knockdown partially reversed the effects of Wt1 overexpression on HG-induced activation of the c-Abl/p53 signaling pathway, and similarly attenuated the protective effects of Wt1 overexpression against HG-induced podocyte apoptosis, F-actin derangement, enhanced motility, and adhesion loss (Supplementary Fig. S2). These data suggest that PTPRO contributes to, but may not fully account for, the protective functions of WT1 in this setting.

Podocyte-specific Wt1 overexpression alleviates DN via the PTPRO/c-Abl/p53 pathway in db/db mice

We next investigated whether restoring Wt1 expression could attenuate DN by modulating the PTPRO/c-Abl/p53 pathway in vivo. To achieve podocyte-specific overexpression, 12-week-old db/db mice received a renal in situ injection of an adeno-associated virus carrying the Wt1 gene under the control of the Npsh1 promoter (AAV-Wt1; Fig. 7A). Biochemical analysis showed that while db/db mice developed progressive proteinuria, Wt1 overexpression significantly ameliorated this effect (Fig. 7B). Moreover, Wt1 overexpression significantly reduced blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels in db/db mice but had no significant effects on physiological parameters, including body weight, blood glucose, serum insulin, and serum lipids (Supplementary Table S4). Consistently, Wt1 restoration markedly reduced glomerulosclerosis, podocyte foot process effacement, and podocyte apoptosis and loss (Fig. 7C and D). At the molecular level, Wt1 overexpression significantly increased the expression of the podocyte markers Nephrin and Podocin as well as anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, while suppressing the injury marker Desmin and pro-apoptotic Bax expression in db/db mice (Fig. 7C-E). Immunofluorescence staining showed that the adhesive protein α3β1 integrin was markedly reduced in the glomeruli of db/db mice, whereas Wt1 overexpression effectively restored its expression (Supplementary Fig. S3). Furthermore, compared to db/m controls, the glomeruli of db/db mice exhibited downregulation of PTPRO and upregulation of activated c-Abl (p-c-Abl) and p53 (p-p53). Notably, these changes were reversed by Wt1 overexpression, which restored PTPRO levels and inhibited c-Abl and p53 activation in podocytes (Fig. 7C-H). Together, these results confirm the critical role of the WT1/PTPRO/c-Abl/p53 axis in preserving podocyte structure and function in the progression of DN.

Discussion

This study uncovered a previously unrecognized role of the WT1/PTPRO/c-Abl/p53 axis in podocyte injury during DN. We demonstrated that WT1 directly bound to the PTPRO gene and promoted its expression in podocytes. The restoration of PTPRO by WT1 restrained HG-induced activation of c-Abl and p53, thereby reducing podocyte apoptosis. In vivo, AAV-mediated Wt1 overexpression attenuated podocyte foot process effacement, reduced albuminuria, and preserved renal function. Collectively, these findings establish that WT1-driven PTPRO expression mitigates podocyte apoptosis via inhibition of c-Abl/p53 signaling, thereby delaying DN progression.

As a critical component for maintaining GFB integrity, podocytes are highly specialized, terminally differentiated epithelial cells that are particularly vulnerable to various pathological insults such as oxidative stress, inflammation, and hemodynamic changes. As a result, they undergo a series of changes ranging from hypertrophy, foot process effacement, detachment, to apoptosis [41]. Podocyte injury is recognized as an early event in DN, and the subsequent loss of these cells are key events that drive DN progression and proteinuria [42]. Correspondingly, the structural and functional integrity of podocytes relies on the tightly regulated expression of podocyte-specific genes. A recent study identified 92 such genes essential for podocyte integrity [43]. Among these, PTPRO has been well documented to play a critical role in maintaining podocyte structure and function [44]. PTPRO is mainly distributed on the apical membrane of podocyte foot processes and is the most abundant tyrosine phosphatase in podocytes [45]. Consistent with previous reports, we found that PTPRO was markedly reduced in podocytes of DN mice, accompanied with the downregulation of podocyte cytoskeletal marker Synaptopodin. This reduction aligns with podocyte injury in the diabetic kidney. Importantly, PTPRO downregulation was also observed in cultured podocytes exposed to HG, palmitate, TNF-α, or ADR, supporting the interpretation that reduced PTPRO reflects an injury-associated change within podocytes rather than only a consequence of podocyte loss. Analysis of transcriptomic datasets from the Nephroseq database further revealed that PTPRO mRNA levels were significantly decreased across a range of glomerular diseases, including DN, FSGS, and IgAN. Interrogation of human glomerular transcriptomic data further identified PTPRO as a hub gene strongly correlated with baseline proteinuria (r = − 0.67, P = 6.55 × 10–9) and eGFR (r = 0.59, P = 9.14 × 10–7) in DN patients [46]. Our further functional studies showed that basal knockdown of Ptpro was sufficient to induce podocyte injury, as demonstrated by reduced expression of podocyte markers, disruption of the F-actin cytoskeleton, enhanced podocyte motility, decreased adhesion, and increased apoptosis. Conversely, Ptpro overexpression effectively rescued HG-induced podocyte injury. All these results underscore the essential role of PTPRO in podocyte homeostasis.

We next investigated the potential mechanisms underlying the protective function of PTPRO in DN. As a member of the R3 subfamily of receptor protein tyrosine phosphatases, PTPRO dephosphorylates a family of tyrosine kinases [47], positioning it as a key regulator of multiple signal transduction pathways under various pathophysiological conditions. Previous studies have implicated PTPRO in metabolic regulation, including inhibiting insulin signaling by dephosphorylating specific sites on the insulin receptor [48] and suppressing lipid droplet growth in adipocytes through dephosphorylation of vimentin at Tyr117 [49]. PTPRO also functions as a tumor suppressor, such as inhibiting lymph node metastasis in esophageal carcinoma by dephosphorylating MET [50] and repressing breast oncogenesis through dephosphorylation of ERBB2 [51]. In addition, PTPRO plays a critical role in the central projection of retinal axons by dephosphorylating c-Abl [19].

A striking finding of our study is that, among the few known substrates of PTPRO, we identified c-Abl as a downstream effector of PTPRO in podocytes. c-Abl is a nonreceptor tyrosine kinase implicated in neuronal degeneration [52] and cancer [53]. In podocytes, c-Abl has been shown to mediate angiotensin II- and HG-induced apoptosis via activation of p53 [17, 54], as well as podocyte cytoskeletal remodeling via suppression of Nephrin-Akt signaling [18, 55]. In this study, we also observed that c-Abl was activated in podocytes under diabetic conditions, and that c-Abl knockdown inhibited p53 signaling activation and attenuated podocyte injury. Co-IP experiments confirmed the binding between PTPRO and c-Abl in HEK293T cells and podocytes. The PTPRO substrate-trapping mutants (CS/DA) trapped more c-Abl than WT-PTPRO. Furthermore, WT PTPRO, but not the CS/DA mutants, dephosphorylated c-Abl at Tyr412. In podocytes, PTPRO-c-Abl interaction was markedly reduced upon HG stimulation. Generally, it is believed that phosphatase-substrate interactions are generally transient and difficult to capture using traditional Co-IP. Several reasons may account for the observed interaction. One possibility is that high expression of PTPRO increases collision frequency and shifts the equilibrium toward enzyme-substrate complex formation. Indeed, in cell lines with endogenous high expression of PTPRO, its interactions with JAK2 and YAP can be detected by Co-IP [56]. Additionally, the complex compartmental structure and scaffold proteins within podocytes may play a role, as PTPRO and c-Abl may co-localize within a multiprotein complex, possibly through indirect interaction. Under HG conditions, WT-PTPRO significantly reduced c-Abl Tyr412 phosphorylation, while the CS/DA mutant enhanced it, further supporting c-Abl as a genuine substrate of PTPRO in podocytes. However, additional dephosphorylation sites on c-Abl targeted by PTPRO, beyond Tyr412, require further investigation.

Further study revealed that PTPRO promoted dephosphorylation of c-Abl at Tyr412 and prevented its translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus upon HG-induced podocyte injury. Furthermore, PTPRO inhibited the expression and activation of p53, a downstream effector of c-Abl. p53 induces apoptosis via transcriptional activation of pro-apoptotic genes (e.g., Puma, Bax, Noxa) and triggers cell cycle arrest by activating p21 transcription [57]. Beyond apoptosis, mitotic catastrophe is an emerging mechanism of podocyte loss in glomerular diseases. Healthy podocytes are arrested in G0 phase and do not proliferate, but pathological factors can drive them to re-enter the cell cycle. Due to their unique structural characteristics, podocytes cannot complete mitosis, resulting in G1/S or G2/M arrest. Entry into S and G2/M phases renders podocytes vulnerable and more prone to detachment from the GBM [58]. In our study, overexpression of PTPRO inhibited the c-Abl/p53/Bax/cleaved caspase-3-mediated apoptosis and prevented p21-mediated G2/M arrest in HG-stimulated podocytes, whereas pharmacological activation of c-Abl by DPH abrogated these protective effects. In addition, overexpression of PTPRO inhibited HG-induced F-actin disruption, enhanced cell motility, and reduced adhesion, effects that were largely abolished by DPH. These findings establish c-Abl as a critical downstream effector mediating the protective function of PTPRO in DN.

Another important finding of our study is the identification of Ptpro as a transcriptional target of WT1, with mapping of its binding site. WT1 is the predominant transcription factor in podocytes and is critical for glomerular development and function [20]. Podocyte-specific Wt1 knockout in adult mice leads to proteinuria, foot process effacement, and FSGS [59]. Yet, the downstream targets mediating WT1’s protective effects remain incompletely defined. Previous studies have shown that WT1 ameliorates podocyte injury by repressing EZH2/β-catenin signaling [25] and inhibiting p53 [60]. To identify novel transcriptional targets of WT1, we analyzed published ChIP-seq data and identified a WT1 binding site within the PTPRO gene locus (Chr6: 137295383 ~ 137295392), as predicted by the JASPAR database. ChIP-qPCR assay confirmed that WT1 directly bound to the PTPRO gene locus in podocytes, and HG stimulation reduced WT1 enrichment at this site. Under diabetic conditions, downregulation of WT1 paralleled the loss of PTPRO. Conversely, Wt1 overexpression significantly restored PTPRO expression levels, inactivated the c-Abl/p53 signaling pathway, and ameliorated podocyte injury and apoptosis. To confirm that PTPRO is a major downstream effector mediating WT1-conferred protection, we knocked down Ptpro in combination with Wt1 overexpression in HG-stimulated podocytes. The observation that Ptpro knockdown partially blocked WT1’s inhibitory effect on c-Abl/p53 pathway activation and podocyte injury supports this conclusion.

Finally, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of activating WT1-PTPRO signaling in db/db mice. Consistent with previous studies by Wan et al. [25] and Zhang et al. [60], AAV-mediated Wt1 overexpression specifically in podocytes markedly ameliorated albuminuria, podocyte foot process effacement, podocyte loss, and glomerulosclerosis in db/db mice. These improvements were accompanied by restored expression of the key podocyte markers Nephrin and Podocin and suppressed expression of the injury marker Desmin. Moreover, Wt1 overexpression upregulated PTPRO and inhibited the c-Abl/p53 signaling pathway in db/db mice. These results further indicate that the renoprotective effects of WT1 overexpression observed in DN are associated with PTPRO/c-Abl/p53 signaling.

There are some limitations to this study. First, our current in vivo experiments demonstrate that podocyte-targeted Wt1 restoration improves DN phenotypes and is accompanied by restored PTPRO expression. However, these findings do not directly prove that podocyte PTPRO is required for the observed protective effects in vivo. Future investigations using podocyte-specific Ptpro knockout and inducible overexpression mice in the diabetic model will be necessary to definitively determine the role of PTPRO in DN progression. Second, although we identified c-Abl as a key downstream effector, PTPRO likely has additional substrates. Future phosphoproteomics-based studies are therefore warranted to systematically identify other potential targets and effector molecules mediating the protective functions of PTPRO.

In conclusion, this study identifies the WT1-PTPRO-c-Abl-p53 axis as a critical regulatory pathway in podocyte injury during DN. Under diabetic conditions, suppressed WT1 expression leads to reduced transcription of PTPRO. As a tyrosine phosphatase, decreased PTPRO activity results in elevated phosphorylation of c-Abl at Y412, thereby enhancing c-Abl activation. This, in turn, activates the p53 signaling pathway and promotes podocyte injury (Fig. 8). These findings provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying podocyte injury in DN progression. Furthermore, targeting the WT1-PTPRO-c-Abl-p53 axis may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for the treatment of DN.
