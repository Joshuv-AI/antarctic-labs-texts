Harnessing fano-like line shape resonance in a rectangular waveguide for filtering applications
Authors: El-Aouni Mimoun, Ali Hennache, Ben-Ali Youssef, Essahlaoui Abdelouahed, Bria Driss, Mahmoud A Zaky, Zaky A Zaky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a comprehensive investigation of the transmission characteristics and filtering performance of a tailored one-dimensional rectangular waveguide structure (1D-RWS). The research employs a combined analytical–numerical approach. The system is first modeled theoretically using the Green’s function method (GFM) to elucidate the underlying physical mechanisms and then validated through rigorous finite element method (FEM) simulations. A key focus of the work is to explore the influence of critical geometric parameters, including resonator length, lateral position, and width, on the filter’s spectral response. The results demonstrate that the transmission spectra and resonant frequencies are highly tunable, exhibiting phenomena such as resonant frequency shifts, critical coupling, and variations in bandwidth with geometric modifications. These findings confirm that the proposed 1D structure provides a versatile platform for controlling electromagnetic wave propagation. Consequently, this waveguide can be considered a bandpass filter for applications in the radio-frequency spectrum, offering a design principle for integrated radio-wave circuits and communication systems.Keywords: Geometric and optical parameters, Ring, Resonator, Electromagnetic filter, Fano-like line shape resonanceSubject terms: Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

Waveguide systems with diverse geometries are fundamental to modern electromagnetic devices, significantly impacting the performance of optical sensors, communication systems, and selective filters1–7. These filters are particularly vital for telecommunications infrastructure, such as frequency-division multiplexing systems, where they enable the precise selection of specific frequencies to enhance signal integrity and transmission capacity8–10.

Initially developed for telecommunications, optical waveguides offer advantages such as flexible design and low-loss guidance, leading to their adoption in numerous other fields11. Optical fibers, for instance, form the basis of many compact and accessible sensors. However, their designs, often optimized for communication, can impose limitations for specialized sensing or filtering applications. This has driven recent interest in planar and channel waveguides, which provide greater design flexibility and potential for integration into circuits12–14.

Among integrated components, ring resonators (RRs) have emerged as critical elements. They function as narrowband filters in optical systems, capable of selecting or rejecting specific frequencies, a key requirement for multiplexing applications. Beyond filtering, their high sensitivity to changes in the local refractive index makes them suitable for detecting chemical, biological, thermal, and physical stimuli11–15. While much research has focused on the optical regime, waveguides also show considerable promise at lower frequencies, such as the megahertz (MHz) range, for applications in radio communications and integrated sensing. Wave propagation in these structures can be modeled using theoretical methods like Green’s function or transfer matrix approaches, often solved numerically to design compact, selective filters16–22.

Photonic and phononic devices have emerged as powerful platforms for sensing23–29 and filtering applications30–33, leveraging the interaction between light or acoustic waves and matter to achieve exceptional selectivity and sensitivity34–36. Optical filters, essential for isolating specific frequencies, form the critical front-end of sensing systems by rejecting noise and enhancing signal clarity37. When integrated into sensor architectures, these components enable the detection of minute physical, chemical, or biological changes with high precision38–40. The convergence of advanced filtering techniques with sensitive photonic transducers is therefore driving progress in fields as diverse as environmental monitoring, point-of-care diagnostics, and industrial process control.

Recent advances in photonic filter design have been dominated by innovations based on ring resonator architectures, which offer compact size, high quality factors, and strong wavelength selectivity. Modern implementations on silicon-photonic platforms enable ultra-compact, high-order filters with excellent out-of-band rejection ratios (up to 40 dB) and low insertion loss within submicron footprints41. More recently, microelectromechanically (MEMS)-tunable silicon ring resonators have achieved low-power, wide tuning ranges (over 500 pm) with sub-100 nW static power consumption, demonstrating energy-efficient reconfigurability for integrated photonic systems42. Hybrid photonic-electronic integration has also enabled high-speed operation, as seen in recent 112 Gb/s wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) receivers incorporating thermally controlled ring-resonator filters for wavelength demultiplexing43. Meanwhile, topological photonic ring-resonators have emerged as a new direction, achieving defect-immune, unidirectional signal routing for on-chip filtering44. Furthermore, cascaded configurations on 300 mm silicon photonics platforms have demonstrated high rejection filters exceeding 100 dB, paving the way for advanced quantum and communication applications45.

The principal novelty of this work lies in the integrated design and multifaceted analysis of a rectangular-ring resonator waveguide for advanced filtering applications. While electromagnetic filters are well-established, this study distinguishes itself by conducting a systematic exploration of the interdependence between multiple geometric parameters, specifically resonator length, lateral positioning, and width, and the resulting transmission characteristics in the MHz frequency regime. We demonstrate that through precise optimization of these structural parameters, our proposed filter achieves a high-performance profile, characterized by a narrow bandwidth, an exceptionally high-quality factor (Q-factor), and dynamically tunable resonant frequencies. A further significant aspect of our methodological novelty is the rigorous, dual-framework validation employed. By correlating results from the analytical GFM, which provides profound physical insight into the resonant states and coupling mechanisms, with comprehensive numerical simulations via the FEM, we ensure the robustness and accuracy of our findings. This synergistic approach not only confirms the filter’s performance but also establishes a generalizable design and analysis framework for engineering electromagnetic components with tailored spectral responses.

While the theoretical model presented in this section provides valuable insights into the Fano-like resonance behavior and high-Q performance of the proposed rectangular waveguide, certain practical assumptions and limitations must be acknowledged. The model is based on an idealized lossless dielectric material and assumes perfectly smooth waveguide walls, neglecting surface roughness, fabrication imperfections, and material dispersion within the simulated frequency range. Thermally induced variations and nonlinear effects at high power levels are also outside the scope of this analysis.

In real devices, we expect the resonant frequency and Q-factor to be influenced by these factors; however, within the microwave regime and for moderate power levels, the proposed geometry is anticipated to retain its key properties, namely, sharp Fano-like resonance and critical coupling, provided fabrication tolerances are maintained, and low-loss materials are used. The model is therefore most applicable in the design and conceptual validation phase, with experimental calibration recommended for performance optimization in practical implementations.

In Fig. 2, the transmission rate is illustrated for a proposed structure in Fig. 1 (with conditions m, , , , , and ). The results from the GFM (blue) are compared with the reference FEM solution (red). The root-mean-square deviation between the GFM and FEM transmission spectra over 0–500 MHz is 10.4% of the maximum transmission, while the maximum pointwise deviation reaches 92% at isolated resonant frequencies where very sharp features occur. We note that the two transmission spectra coincide, providing excellent validation of the analytical GFM by the numerical FEM. Both methods show the same transmission spectrum behavior, with transmission modes (peaks) and zeros aligned. The alternating transmission maxima and minima result from the coupling between the rectangular ring and the grafted resonator, which introduces resonant effects. This configuration generates interference between the propagation paths, resulting in both destructive (transmission zeros) and constructive (narrow peaks) resonances. The precision of the FEM method demonstrates that even the finest details of the frequency response are accurately considered, including those in highly dispersive areas. This figure not only confirms the validity of the FEM for modeling propagation in guided structures but also demonstrates the robustness of the model used to describe the frequency behavior of a rectangular ring system coupled to a resonator.

This interpretation is strongly supported by the behavior of the full width at half maximum (FWHM), a direct indicator of the resonance’s bandwidth and the system’s Q-factor. The FWHM reaches an exceptionally narrow minimum of approximately 0.0014 at the very same length, m. An extremely narrow FWHM signifies a very high Q-factor, meaning the resonator stores energy for a much longer duration. This combination of near-zero transmittance and a very sharp resonance profile is the clear indication of the critical coupling condition. In conclusion, the data effectively demonstrates that by simply tuning the resonator’s length, one can sweep through different coupling regimes and identify a precise geometry that induces critical coupling, a state of optimal energy confinement within the resonator.

The asymmetric line shapes observed in the transmittance spectra, particularly the sharp transition from high to low transmission at specific frequencies for different resonator lengths (e.g., the drastic dip at ), are characteristic of a Fano-like line shape resonance. This phenomenon arises from the interference between a discrete resonant state, the confined mode within the resonator, and a broad continuum of background scattering states. The system’s behavior, where transmittance does not follow a simple symmetric pattern but instead features a pronounced, narrow anti-resonance (the sharp transmission collapse) alongside broader peaks, is a classic fingerprint of such destructive interference. The exceptionally high Q-factor and minimal FWHM at the anti-resonance point signify the precise tuning where this interference becomes most pronounced, confirming that the interaction between the discrete resonator mode and the continuum pathway is the fundamental mechanism shaping the system’s spectral response.

The efficiency of the system, measured by its transmittance, reveals a more complex and insightful dependency on the resonator length. As clear in Fig. 4, the transmittance generally remains high but undergoes significant variation, culminating in a sharp and pronounced minimum. It rises to a peak value of 98.1% at a length of 0.26 m, indicating a state of optimal coupling where energy is transferred into and out of the resonator with minimal loss. However, at a specific length of m, the transmittance collapses dramatically to a mere 5.9%. This drastic reduction is characteristic of a phenomenon known as critical coupling, where the internal losses of the resonator precisely match the external coupling losses. At this unique point, energy is almost entirely dissipated within the resonator itself rather than being transmitted through it.

In a geometrically scaled system, the resonant frequencies shift inversely with the scaling factor when material properties and relative dimensions are preserved. The Q-factor is a key performance metric for resonant structures, defined as , where is the resonant frequency of the resonance peak. A high Q-factor indicates low energy loss and sharp spectral selectivity, which is crucial for sensing, filtering, and low-power applications. To contextualize the performance of our proposed rectangular waveguide design harnessing Fano-like line shape resonance, we compare it with recent resonator configurations reported in the literature, as summarized in Table 1. While conventional and optimized ring resonators, such as whispering gallery mode resonators49, slot microring resonators52, and plasmonic ring resonators53, typically achieve moderate to high Q-factors, they often require complex fabrication or precise geometrical tuning. In contrast, our design leverages a simple rectangular waveguide geometry excited in a Fano-like line shape resonance regime, which yields a high Q-factor and exhibits critical coupling without the need for cascaded or hybrid structures. This approach not only simplifies the design and fabrication process but also provides competitive performance in terms of resonance sharpness and energy confinement, as evidenced by the comparative Q-factor data. The proposed structure thus offers a compelling alternative to conventional ring-based resonators, particularly for radio and microwave applications where simplicity, robustness, and high selectivity are desired.

Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrates the design and optimization of a radio-wave filter based on a resonant structure incorporating a rectangular ring and a tunable resonator. The synergistic application of the analytical Green’s function method and the numerical finite element method provide a comprehensive framework for analyzing the system, establishing a clear correlation between its geometric parameters, specifically resonator length, lateral position, and width, and its filtering characteristics. The results strongly suggest that these geometric variations offer direct and precise control over key transmission properties, including the resonant frequency, bandwidth, and transmittance, by tailoring the underlying resonance modes and coupling conditions. Consequently, this work presents a design principle for engineering radio-wave filters with tailored spectral responses for advanced communication and sensing applications.
