Impact of plasma aluminum on neurocognitive outcomes at early school age in children with intestinal failure
Authors: Camille Maltais-Bilodeau, Rivka Green, Glenda Courtney-Martin, Daniela Gattini, Christina Belza, Anna Gold, Yaron Avitzur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAluminum (AL) is a known contaminant in parenteral nutrition (PN) and is associated with bone disease, and neurotoxicity. We aimed to evaluate the association between plasma AL concentration and neurocognitive outcomes at early school age, in children with intestinal failure (IF) receiving long-term PN.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included children diagnosed with IF before 2 years of age, with at least one measurement of plasma AL who completed a neuropsychological assessment between 4 and 8 years. Spearman correlation coefficient was used to explore the association between AL concentrations and neurocognitive scores.ResultsNineteen patients (12 males, median gestational age of 35 weeks) were included. The median number of PN years was 9.5 (IQR 7.4–11.8). Median and peak AL concentrations were 363 nmol/L (IQR 233–580) and 832 nmol/L (IQR 491.5–1361.5), respectively. Mean age at assessment was 6.32 + /-0.92 years. The mean full-scale intelligence quotient (FSIQ) was 95.8 + /-13.8. There was a moderate, inverse correlation between mean AL concentration before age three (- 0.56, p = 0.020) and FSIQ. For other domains, the strongest association was found between visual memory and the mean AL concentration before assessment (-0.67, p = 0.006).ConclusionsHigher plasma AL concentrations in children at early school age with IF were modestly associated with lower IQ and visual memory scores. Plasma AL monitoring allows early detection of abnormal plasma concentration, minimization of AL in PN and potentially prevention of neurotoxicity in young children with IF.Keywords: Pediatric intestinal failure, Parenteral nutrition, Aluminum, Neurocognitive outcomes

Introduction

Aluminum (AL) is the most common metal present in the environment. It is used in many foods and medications processes [1]. AL can enter the human body by ingestion, inhalation or contact with the skin [2], and then distribute in the bloodstream [3], [4]. Its primary mode of excretion is renal [1], [5]. However, depending on the type, the route of exposure and the clearance capacity [5], AL can accumulate in various tissues [3], [4]. It has not been found to have any role in biological processes in the human body and is not considered a needed micronutrient.

Long-term effects of exposure to high AL have been associated with metabolic bone disease, dialysis encephalopathy, hepatotoxicity, dyslipidemia and microcytic anemia in adults and children [6], [7], [8], [9]. There is increasing evidence that AL can deposit in the brain [3], [4] and may cause neurotoxicity [7], [10], [11], [12], [13], specifically by its deposition in the hippocampus, cerebellum and cortex [14], [15]. AL has been reported to potentially have a role in the development of neurodegenerative and neurodevelopmental disorders [7], [12], [13], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]. It is known that the brain of the fetus and growing child is vulnerable to neurotoxicity because of the ongoing cerebral development from the fetal life into childhood. It has been demonstrated that early exposure to some neurotoxic environmental agents, metals or elements in childhood, can contribute to the development of neurodevelopmental and neurocognitive disorders [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27].

AL can be found in all components of PN solutions from the container to the mixture [6] and present in excessive amounts in PN solutions particularly small volume solutions containing calcium gluconate or stored in glass containers [6], [28], [29]. A previous study in premature infants receiving an aluminum-depleted PN solution compared to a standard solution found that the mean neurodevelopmental score at 18 months of age was lower in the group receiving the standard AL [30]. It has also been demonstrated that children receiving long-term or home PN have higher plasma, serum and/or urine AL concentrations compared to healthy children [28], [31].

Given the above-mentioned findings and the known accumulation of AL in brain tissue, the aim of this study was to evaluate the association between plasma AL concentrations and neurocognitive outcomes at early school age in children with IF exposed to long-term PN. We hypothesized that high plasma AL concentrations during infancy and early childhood will be associated with lower neurocognitive scores in this patient group.

Methods

Study design and patients

This was a single centre, retrospective, observational study done at the intestinal rehabilitation program of The Hospital for Sick Children, Toronto, Ontario, Canada. Children with IF, defined as PN administration for more than 60 days or bowel length < 25% of expected length, diagnosed before 2 years of age, who had at least one blood plasma AL concentration measured and who underwent neurocognitive assessment at early school age were included in the study. Children were excluded if they were diagnosed with IF before 2014–01–01, received a liver or intestinal transplant before their neuropsychological assessment, required hemodialysis at any time prior to their neuropsychological assessment, or if they were unable to complete a standard neuropsychological assessment due to significant physical, sensory or language barrier.

Routine plasma AL concentration monitoring was initiated in our program from 2013 because of previous reports demonstrating negative effects of AL in patient on long-term PN. AL concentration above 293 nmol/L was defined as abnormal based on our laboratory reference range.

Neuropsychological assessment at early school age, between ages 4–8 years, is offered routinely as part of our program’s standard of care, to support and optimize school integration and advocacy. The early school age neuropsychological assessment was completed by an experienced psychologist (AG) or trained psychometrist under the supervision of a psychologist (AG). The battery of assessment included measures of intellectual functioning, memory, visual-motor skills, language and academics. The Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence (WPPSI-IV), Fourth Edition [32] or the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC), Fourth Edition [33] or Fifth Edition [34], depending upon age and ability level at time of assessment, was used to assess intellectual ability, and included Full-Scale Intellectual Quotient (FSIQ), the verbal comprehension index (VCI), and the nonverbal index (NVI). Assessment of verbal memory was completed using the Story Memory and Word List subtests gathered from the Children’s Memory Scale (CMS) [35] and/or California Verbal Learning Test for Children (CVLT-C) [36], and visual memory utilized the Dot Locations subtest from CMS. Standard scores were used with a mean of 100, and a standard deviation of 15 (85−115). Cognitive or learning diagnosis were provided based on the DSM-IV-TR [37] or DSM-V [38] criteria, where appropriate.

The primary outcome of the study was the association between the FSIQ at early school age and plasma AL concentrations in children receiving PN for IF. Secondary outcomes included association between VCI, NVI, verbal memory and visual memory score at early school age and plasma AL concentrations.

Medical and demographic data

The study was approved by The SickKids Research Ethics Board. All demographics, medical, and laboratory data were collected from the electronic hospitals records by a member of the study team (CMB). Demographic data collected included: sex, date of birth, gestational age and birth weight. Medical data collected included: diagnosis and type of IF, date of diagnosis of IF, small and large bowel length remaining after the initial surgery, date at the start and discontinuation of PN, length of hospital stay during the primary admission, number of sepsis episodes in the first year of life, age at the time of neuropsychological assessment and presence of neurologic comorbidities including diagnosis of seizures, intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), periventricular leukomalacia (PVL), cerebral palsy (CP), attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), learning disability, global developmental delay or autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Laboratory data collected included: date and values of AL concentration and presence of prolonged hyperbilirubinemia (defined as a conjugated bilirubin above 100 umol/L for a minimum of two weeks).

Statistical analysis

The sample size for the study consisted of available chart data based on inclusion criteria for review. All patients in our intestinal rehabilitation program meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria with an available plasma AL concentration who completed a neuropsychological assessment at early school age were analyzed.

Demographics, medical and laboratory characteristics or values were summarized using descriptive statistics (mean with standard deviation for normally distributed parameters, median with interquartile range for abnormally distributed parameters or proportions for categorial measures). The mean AL concentration was calculated for each patient individually if two or more AL values were available. If only one plasma AL concentration was available, this was considered as the value for the analysis. To investigate for possible correlations between AL concentrations and neuropsychological outcomes, we evaluated the median of all AL values reported before the neuropsychological assessment; the median of all AL concentrations reported before the age of three; and the AL peak concentration (highest value) before neuropsychological assessment. The normality of the distribution for the neuropsychological assessment data was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk. For the primary and secondary outcomes, we used the Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient to assess the association between the AL concentrations and the FSIQ score and the other neuropsychological subdomains. RStudio was used for statistical analysis [39]. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Seventy patients were screened for eligibility during the study period from January 1, 2014, to April 1, 2024, of which 50 patients met the medical and laboratory inclusion criteria. Thirty-one patients (44%) were subsequently excluded because they did not undergo neuropsychological assessment during the study period. Nineteen patients were finally included in the study and their data analyzed (supplement figure S1). The baseline characteristics of the study population are found in Table 1. Twelve patients (63%) were male and the median gestational age for the cohort was 35 weeks (IQR 34–37.50) with 13 patients (68%) born premature. The median birth weight was 2700 g (IQR 2100–3050). The etiology of IF was related to short bowel syndrome (n = 16) or congenital diarrhea and enteropathies (n = 3). The median number of PN days during the first year of life was 364 (IQR 363–364.5) and the median number of PN years was 9.5 (IQR 7.3–11.8) for the overall study period. Two patients started PN after their first year of life. Eleven patients (58%) were found to have neurologic comorbidities: seizures (n = 1), IVH (n = 1), CP (n = 1), diagnosis of ADHD (n = 4), Learning Disability (n = 2), Global Developmental delay (n = 1) and ASD (n = 2). Diagnoses are not mutually exclusive.

Plasma aluminum concentrations

Plasma AL concentrations were drawn as part of a routine monitoring in our cohort of patients on long-term PN. A total of 218 AL concentrations, for the 19 patients, were available during the study period with 141 assessments (65%) performed prior to the neuropsychological assessment (median of 9 values per patient, minimum = 1 and maximum = 14). Only one patient had one AL concentration drawn prior to the neuropsychological assessment while the other had multiple samples prior to the assessment. Two patients did not have AL concentration measured prior to three years of life. The median AL concentration for the cohort for all values prior to neuropsychological assessment was 363 nmol/L (IQR 233–580) and the median peak (highest values) of AL was 832 nmol/L (IQR 491.5–1361.5). All patients had at least one abnormal AL concentration prior to neuropsychological assessment. No patient had a peak AL concentration within the normal range.

Neuropsychological assessment results

Patients underwent neuropsychological assessment at early school age at a median age of 6.25 years (IQR 5.88–6.67). Thirteen patients (68%) were assessed with the WISC-V test, and six patients (32%) with the WPPSI-IV test. The mean FSIQ score was 95.79 (SD 13.81) with two patients below 1 SD and one patient below 2 SD for their age group. The mean score of the VCI was 98.36 (SD 18.50) with three patients below 1 SD and no patient with a score below 2 SD. The mean score of the NVI was 95.84 (SD 13.27) with three patients below 1 SD and one patient below 2 SD. The results of the verbal and visual memory scores are presented in Table 2. Fig. 1 shows a boxplot of the neuropsychological assessment results by subscale for each domain. Correlation tests results between the FSIQ and the other neuropsychological subscales and AL concentrations are shown in Table 3.

Primary outcome: association between plasma aluminum concentrations and FSIQ

Spearman’s correlation found a moderate, inverse, correlation between the FSIQ score and the mean AL concentration before age three (-0.56, p = 0.020). There was an inverse correlation that approached significance between the FSIQ score and the mean AL concentration before neuropsychological assessment (-0.43, p = 0.070), and the peak AL concentration before neuropsychological assessment (-0.44, p = 0.062).

Secondary outcome

For the secondary outcomes, a moderate, inverse, correlation was found between the VCI score and the mean AL concentration before neuropsychological assessment (-0.46, p = 0.048), the mean AL concentration before age three (-0.53, p = 0.030) and the peak AL concentration before neuropsychological assessment (-0.49, p = 0.034). For the NVI score, a moderate, inverse, correlation was found only for AL concentration before age three (-0.49, p = 0.048). The strongest association was found between the visual memory and the AL concentration before neuropsychological assessment (-0.67, p = 0.006), as shown in Fig. 2. No association was found between the verbal memory score, the NVI score and the AL concentration before neuropsychological assessment. In addition, no association was found between the peak AL concentration before neuropsychological assessment, and the visual memory score. Finally, there was no association between AL concentration before age three or the peak AL concentration before neuropsychological assessment and visual memory score.

Discussion

This retrospective study assessed for the first time the association between plasma AL concentrations measured during routine monitoring in patients on long-term PN for IF and neurodevelopment at early school age. In this cohort of 19 patients, all children had at least one abnormal AL concentration (> 293 nmol/L) prior to their neuropsychological assessment at early school age. Although the normal reference range of plasma AL is not well established, we assumed that concentrations above this threshold demonstrate a significant exposure to AL. We found a moderate, inverse correlation between mean plasma AL before age three and FSIQ at early school age in children with IF. For the other neuropsychological domains evaluated at early school age, the strongest association was found between visual memory and mean plasma AL concentration. The overall findings confirm previous reports that intravenous AL exposure leading to higher plasma AL concentrations from PN early in life is a risk factor for the development of neuropsychological deficits in later life.

Outcomes of children with IF have changed in the past years with improvement in the mortality and morbidity rates related to change in the medical, nutritional and surgical care as well as the introduction of multidisciplinary intestinal rehabilitation programs [40], [41]. However, children with IF are still at risk of various multisystemic complications including neurodevelopmental impairment and negative impact on quality of life [40], [42], [43], [44]. In a recent systematic review and meta-analysis [45] on the cognitive outcomes of children requiring PN for conditions affecting the small intestine, compared to the normal population, children with IF had lower developmental quotient/intelligence quotient (DQ/IQ) scores. This study also found that almost 30% of children with IF had a severe developmental delay or disability [45]. Multiple etiologies and risk factors can impact the developing brain in children with IF including gestational age, longer hospital stay specifically during the first year of life and increased number of procedures under general anesthesia [45]. Although, Vlug et al. did not find an association between duration of PN dependency and DQ/IQ, those findings argue that insults during the first months of life play a significant role in the neurodevelopmental outcomes of this population. Nutritional deficiencies as well as neurotoxins are known factors which negatively impact neurodevelopment in growing children. A recent systematic review of the association between heavy metal exposure and impact on neurodevelopmental performance showed that early exposure to heavy metals such as arsenic, lead, cadmium, mercury or manganese is associated with several neurodevelopmental outcomes. Exposure to those neurotoxins in childhood, may increase the risk of decreased neurodevelopmental performance [46].

The association between AL toxicity and neurodevelopmental delay was first suggested by Bishop et al. in 1997 [30]. They compared a group of premature infants receiving an aluminum-depleted PN solution to a standard solution and found that the mean neurodevelopmental scores at 18 months of age were lower in the group receiving the standard AL solution for more than 10 days compared to the infants in the aluminum-depleted solution group. The infants in the standard AL group were also more likely to have a Mental Development Index below 85, on the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development. The same authors published a follow-up study in 2011 [47], evaluating the cognitive outcomes of their cohort at 13–15 years of life. No difference between the two groups was found at that later timepoint. This may be partly attributable to the fact that those infants with early neuromotor impairment or with a lower Bayley score (< 85) were excluded from the follow-up assessment, suggesting a bias towards not assessing teens with a reduced early motor or cognitive risk.

The current study adds to the limited body of literature and expands it to a new patient population with much longer, chronic, ongoing exposure to PN and AL contamination. Moreover, we were able to measure AL concentrations and show that all 19 patients had at least one abnormal AL value. The PN exposure difference between the two populations, with a short and limited exposure to PN in the neonatal period in the Bishop et al. cohort, as well as natural variations in the cohorts can explain the discrepancies in long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes in the two cohorts.

AL is present in all the components of the PN solution, mainly in solutions such as calcium gluconate, inorganic phosphates and cysteine chloride [47], [48], [49], [50], [51], [52]. With an intention to reduce AL exposure from PN, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recommended in 2004 that small-volume PN solution bags include a warning label that patients with impaired kidney function, including premature neonates, who receive more than 4–5 mcg/kg/day may experience central nervous system and bone toxicity [53]. Despite this regulation, the ongoing concerning high concentration of AL in PN solutions in neonates and children has been demonstrated [28], [29], [54]. A previous study by our group compared plasma AL concentration between children with IF receiving long-term PN and age and sex- matched controls. It showed that the plasma AL concentration of children with IF was significantly higher due to PN exposure [28]. Although the excessive concentration of AL in PN solutions is described and known, it is currently challenging to reduce completely AL from PN solutions and future solutions to this problem are needed.

There are several limitations to this study. First, because AL is a contaminant, any concentration of AL in PN solutions could be considered abnormal, but there is no accepted laboratory threshold of plasma AL concentration in children that is clearly associated with toxicity. The cutoff value of 293 nmol/L chosen for this study was based on our laboratory reference range but is not specific to our pediatric population. In addition, it is not known if plasma concentration is the best method to assess AL exposure. Other neurotoxins such as lead can be tested in hair or urine and better represent toxicity for those metals. However, until such knowledge is available, we assumed that plasma AL concentration is a reasonable method to assess AL exposure in the human body. Secondly, the plasma AL concentrations available for the study were obtained at a different time points for every patient, based on clinically indicated monitoring and test availability. This led to a different number of samples for each patient. The sampling limitations have been mitigated by including only patients that had at least one AL value reported before the neurodevelopmental assessment and by using mean and peak concentration for each patient. Finally, our results and statistical analysis are limited to 19 patients. Although a higher number of patients met the medical and laboratory inclusion criteria, 44% of the patients screened were excluded because they did not undergo neuropsychological assessment. The reasons behind the absence of neuropsychological assessment are not all available. Some were due to a canceled appointment by the parents or because the patient was scheduled to be evaluated after the data collection time of the study. This could lead to a bias towards families who value closer monitoring of their child. The sample size also highlights the limited opportunities to identify large IF cohort that meets the inclusion criteria including availability of AL concentrations and the results require further verification in larger or similar size cohorts in other centers.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study found that higher plasma AL concentrations in children with IF are associated with lower measures of general intellect and visual memory scores at early school age. It suggests that monitoring of plasma AL concentrations in children with IF is an opportunity for early detection of abnormal concentrations and thus minimization of AL exposure in PN components. It could prevent neurotoxicity in this population and potentially neurodevelopmental deficiencies. Future prospective longitudinal studies with a larger population and structured AL monitoring are needed to confirm the findings of this study.
