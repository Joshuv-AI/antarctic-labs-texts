Inhibition of p65 nuclear translocation in decidual stromal cells underlies COS-induced supraphysiologic estrogen impairment of uNK cell function which drives placental abnormalities
Authors: Haikun Yu, Hui Mu, Yujing Xiong, Jiaqin Mao, Hui Lei, Xueyan Lu, Jie Dong, Ni Jin, Jie Lu, Shuqiang Chen, Xiaohong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundControlled ovarian stimulation (COS) induces supraphysiologic levels of estrogen (E2), which have been associated with low birth weight and placental abnormalities in infants conceived through assisted reproductive technologies (ART). Uterine natural killer (uNK) cells play a crucial role in regulating placentation. Elevated E2 has been shown to disrupt both the abundance and function of uNK cells. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying these effects remain incompletely understood.MethodsWe established mouse models for supraphysiological E2 exposure and pyrrolidine dithiocarbamate (PDTC) treatment. Placental and fetal development were evaluated through histological observation and analysis. The abundance, subtype distribution and cytokine secretion profiles of uNK cells were assessed using flow cytometry and Luminex assays. In vitro co-culture systems were employed to investigate the regulatory effect of decidual stromal cells (DSCs) on uNK cells. Additionally, we examined the roles of decidualization, NF-κB signaling, BMP2, and CXCL8 through pharmacological interventions and targeted inhibition.ResultsCOS-induced elevated E2 significantly altered the abundance, subsets and cytokine secretion profiles of uNK cells, leading to reduced fetal weight and placental deficiencies in mice. Notably, supraphysiological E2 did not directly affect uNK cells but rather influenced them via DSC-dependent paracrine signaling mechanisms. High levels of E2 inhibited NF-κB pathway activity in DSCs, resulting in impaired decidualization as well as altered cytokine secretion profiles; among these alterations was a confirmed modulation by CXCL8 on the migration of uNK cells. Mechanistically, NF-κB P65 directly binds to the promoter region of BMP2 to regulate decidualization while also modulating CXCL8 secretion by binding to its promoter region.ConclusionOur findings indicate that the low birth weight associated with COS may be attributed to elevated estrogen levels, which can adversely affect the differentiation and function of uNK cells. This disruption may lead to abnormal placental morphology and functionality. These findings may contribute to the prevention and intervention of adverse pregnancy outcomes related to COS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08587-z.

Introduction

The placenta serves as a critical interface for fetal development, orchestrating the exchange of nutrients, oxygen and waste products between the maternal and fetal circulations [1]. Despite its crucial role, the placenta is vulnerable to a range of adverse factors, including maternal stress and disruptions in endocrine and metabolic homeostasis. These influences can significantly impact both the structure and function of the placenta [2]. Growing evidence from global research suggests that placental dysfunction may have consequences that transcend immediate neonatal outcomes such as low birth weight. These effects appear to persist long-term, potentially predisposing individuals to an elevated risk of developing cardiovascular diseases, metabolic disorders including diabetes and neurological conditions in later life [1, 3, 4].

Assisted reproductive technology (ART) has emerged as a highly effective treatment for infertility, with over 8 million children born worldwide through these techniques [5]. However, during fresh embryo transfer cycles, controlled ovarian stimulation (COS) exposes gametes, embryos and developing fetuses to supraphysiological estradiol (E2) levels [6, 7]. These markedly elevated E2 concentrations can persist throughout the first trimester—a critical window for placental development [8, 9]. This aberrant endocrine environment has been associated with an increased risk of low birth weight [7, 8, 10]. Emerging evidence suggests that COS-induced supraphysiological E2 may also exert long-term consequences on offspring health. Studies have linked supraphysiological E2 exposure to glucose metabolic dysfunction, transient elevations in low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels and increased risk of thyroid dysfunction in offspring [6, 11, 12]. Notably, maternal serum E2 levels demonstrate an inverse correlation with offspring intelligence quotient (IQ) [13]. Furthermore, elevated maternal E2 concentrations have been shown to negatively impact verbal abilities in children conceived through ART [14].

Accumulating evidence indicates that COS can disrupt normal placentation processes [15–17], with the associated supraphysiological E2 levels serving as a key mediator of these placental alterations [18–20]. Our recent experimental findings demonstrate that elevated E2 levels directly impair placental development, manifesting as structural abnormalities and reduced fetal weight in murine models [21]. These observations strongly implicate COS-induced supraphysiological E2 in the pathogenesis of placental dysfunction, which may represent a primary mechanism underlying the increased incidence of low birth weight and associated health risks in ART-conceived offspring. Nevertheless, the precise molecular pathways through which elevated E2 levels during COS compromise placental development remain to be fully elucidated.

Uterine natural killer (uNK) cells constitute the predominant immune population in the decidua, representing approximately 70% of total decidual immune cells during early pregnancy [22]. These specialized lymphocytes play essential roles in multiple aspects of placental development, including extravillous trophoblast (EVT) invasion, trophoblast differentiation and placental vascular remodeling [23–25]. Emerging evidence indicates that the supraphysiological hormonal environment created by COS significantly compromises uNK cell function, particularly their capacity to promote EVT invasion [26]. Our experimental studies in murine models further demonstrate that COS induced supraphysiological E2 reduces uNK cell density and suppresses their production of critical angiogenic factors [27, 28]. These collective findings suggest that COS-induced supraphysiological E2 disrupts both the abundance and functional competence of uNK cells, thereby contributing to placental developmental abnormalities. Despite these observations, the precise molecular mechanisms by which elevated estradiol levels impair uNK cell activity remain to be fully characterized.

During each menstrual cycle, human endometrial stromal cells (hESCs) undergo progesterone- and estradiol-dependent proliferation and differentiation into specialized decidual stromal cells (DSCs), a process known as decidualization [29]. As the predominant cellular component of the decidua, DSCs play a pivotal role in shaping its unique immunomodulatory microenvironment [29]. Through their secretion of cytokines, DSCs critically regulate the differentiation and function of uNK cells [24]. Moreover, DSCs actively maintain uNK cell residency within the decidual tissue [30]. These observations collectively underscore the importance of bidirectional communication between DSCs and uNK cells for ensuring proper uNK cell differentiation and function during early pregnancy. While estrogen has been shown to directly influence uNK cells [31], the mechanisms through which COS-induced supraphysiological E2 affects uNK cell biology - whether through direct hormonal actions or indirect DSC-mediated pathways - remain to be fully elucidated.

In this study, we first demonstrated that COS-induced supraphysiological levels of E2 lead to low birth weight and morphological abnormalities in the placenta within murine models. Our subsequent analysis revealed that these effects were mediated through estrogen-dependent impairments in the differentiation and function of uNK cells, representing a crucial mechanistic link to the observed placental defects. Importantly, we established that the effects of estrogen on uNK cells were not cell-autonomous; rather, they occurred indirectly via DSCs. At the molecular level, supraphysiological E2 was found to suppress NF-κB signaling in DSCs, resulting in two critical consequences: (1) compromised decidualization and (2) dysregulated secretion of CXCL8 which modulates uNK cell activity. These findings offer new mechanistic insights into the role of COS-associated supraphysiological E2 in contributing to adverse pregnancy outcomes in offspring conceived through ART.

Materials and methods

Animal models

6-week-old ICR mice were purchased from Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China. Male and female mice were housed separately in a pathogen-free environment with a 12-hour light/dark cycle at 23–25 ℃. After 2-week acclimation, virgin female mice mated with normal male mice in a male: female ratio of 1:1. The onset of pregnancy was confirmed by the presence of vaginal plug observed on the following morning, which was regarded as gestational day (GD) 0.5.

Supraphysiological E2 mouse model: From GD 5.5 to GD 7.5, pregnant female mice were orally administrated with 100 µg/kg/day 17β-estradiol (MCE, USA) dissolved in corn oil (Glpbio, USA) as previously described [32, 33]. Control group (NC) mice were administrated with equal volume of corn oil. Maternal blood and decidua tissues were collected at GD 8.5. Placenta and fetal mice were collected at GD 12.5 for further study.

PDTC mouse model: We utilized pyrrolidine dithiocarbamate (PDTC, MCE, USA) to inhibit NF-κB pathway in this study. The dose of PDTC was established based on findings from previous studies [34]. Pregnant mice were given either saline or PDTC (dissolved with saline, 250 mg/kg/day) by intraperitoneal injection from GD 5.5 to GD 7.5. Decidua tissues were collected at GD 8.5.

Histological observation and analysis

Placentas were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (4 μm) were sliced and baked at 60 °C for 2 h prior to staining. Placental tissues were stained with a Hematoxylin–Eosin Stain kit (H&E) (ab245880, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and an antibody against CD31 (ab182981, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) for determination of microvascular density.

Detection of serum estrogen

Blood was collected at GD 8.5 via retro-orbital bleeding. It was allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min and then centrifuged to obtain serum. Serum estrogen concentration was measured using a commercial ELISA kit (CSB-E07280m, CUSABIO, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Serum estrogen concentration was measured using a commercial ELISA kit (CSB-E07280m, CUSABIO, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Human placental tissue collection

Human placental tissues were obtained from healthy women undergoing elective termination of early pregnancy between 6 and 8 weeks of gestation at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Tangdu Hospital. Tissue samples were collected using a pre-sterilized vacuum suction bottle containing phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Subsequently, the tissues were minced and subjected to erythrocyte lysis in a laminar flow cabinet. All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki (2000). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee of Tangdu Hospital, The Fourth Military Medical University (Approval No. 202203-027).

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspension obtained from decidual tissues of GD 8.5 or human placenta required erythrocyte lysis, followed by centrifugation and resuspension. Flow cytometry was performed according to protocol of Biolegend and raw data were analyzed with Novo Express software 1.4.1. The antibodies used in this study were listed in Supplementary Table 1. In the histogram overlay, the light gray and black peaks represent the isotype control and the experimental sample, respectively. To define the positive region, the gate was established strictly according to the isotype control’s fluorescence, with the threshold positioned to keep fewer than 1% of isotype control events in the positive gate.

Isolation of mouse and human uterine NK cell

This assay was performed as previously described [35]. Briefly, decidual tissues were minced and digested for 1 h at 37 ℃. Then the resulting cell suspension was filtered through 100 μm– 70 μm- 40 μm filters, layered onto Ficoll-Paque and then centrifuged at 400 g for 30 min. After centrifugation, the cells were cultured overnight to remove adherent cells. PE anti-mouse NK-1.1 antibody and Anti-PE MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) was used to isolate mouse uNK cells and the the purity of mouse uNK cells was (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Human uNK cells were isolated by human NK Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany), with their purity demonstrated in (Supplementary Fig. 1B).

Cell culture

The human endometrial stromal cell line (hESC) was kindly provided by Prof. Haibin Wang (Xiamen University) and maintained in phenol red-free DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% charcoal-stripped FBS (Thermo Scientific, USA), 100 µg/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 1% Insulin-Transferrin-Selenium (ITS-G, MCE, USA). To induce in vitro decidualization, hESCs were differentiated into decidual stromal cells (DSCs) by treatment with DMEM/F12 (phenol red-free) containing 2% CS-FBS, 0.5 mM 8-Bromo-cAMP (MCE, USA), and 1 µM medroxyprogesterone acetate (MPA, MCE, USA). After 6 days of treatment, the expression of decidualization markers, including prolactin (PRL) and insulin-like growth factor binding protein 1 (IGFBP1), was confirmed by reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) [36–38].

Co-culture and conditioned media (CM) preparation

Transwell chambers (0.4 μm pore size; Corning, USA) were established between decidual stromal cells (DSCs) and uNK cells. The lower chambers contained either untreated DSCs, treated DSCs, or cell-free DMEM/F12 medium. Purified human uNK cells were seeded into the upper chamber at a density of 1 × \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{10}^{6}$$\end{document} cells/well. After 24-hour of co-culture, uNK cells were further cultured alone in DMEM/F12 medium for an additional 24 h. Subsequently, uNK cells and supernatant were collected separately. The cells were subjected to flow cytometric analysis, while the supernatant was centrifuged and used as uNK cell-conditioned media (CM) for subsequent treatment of HUVEC and HTR-8/SVneo cells.

Invasion assay

The HTR-8/SVneo cell line, a well-characterized and widely accepted in vitro model for studying human extravillous trophoblast (EVT) biology [39], was employed in this study. This cell line is an immortalized trophoblast cell line originally derived from first-trimester placental villous explants. For the invasion assay, Transwell chambers (8.0 μm pore size, Corning, USA) were used. The upper chambers were pre-coated with 60 µL of Matrigel (diluted in RPMI 1640) and incubated at 37 °C for 3 h to allow gelation. Subsequently, HTR-8/SVneo cells were seeded into the upper chambers, while the lower chambers were filled with conditioned media from uNK cells (uNK cell-CMs). After 36 h of incubation, cells remaining in the upper chamber were fixed, washed, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (MCE, USA) for 15 min. Non-invading cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed with a cotton swab. Images of invaded cells were captured from randomly selected fields using an inverted microscope, and the number of invaded cells was quantified using ImageJ software.

Scratch assay

HTR-8/SVneo cells were plated in 6-well plates at 2.5 × \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{10}^{6}$$\end{document} cells/well and cultured overnight. A linear scratch was scratched using a 200 µl pipette tip. After washing with PBS, cells were treated with uNK cell CMs for 48 h. Wound images were acquired at 0 h and 48 h by an inverted microscope, and percentage of migration area was calculated by Image J software.

In vitro tube formation assay

Growth factor-reduced Matrigel (Corning, USA) was thawed at 4 °C overnight, aliquoted into 48-well plates (100 µL/well), and polymerized at 37 °C for 1 h. HUVECs were resuspended in uNK cell CMs and incubated for 3–6 h. Tube formation was imaged by an inverted microscope (Leica, Germany), and total tube length and branch points were quantified with ImageJ.

RNA isolation, cDNA preparation and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from decidua tissues or hESCs by TRIzol regant (Invitrogen, USA). The quality and concentration of RNA were evaluated by Nanodrop 2000 (NanoDrop Technologies, USA). 1000 ng RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the Prime Script RT Regent Kit (TaKaRa, Japan). Real-time polymerase chain reaction was performed with SYBR Premix ExTaq (TaKaRa, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The relative expression of every gene was calculated using 2−∆∆CT method and Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the internal control. The primer sequences used in this study are displayed in Supplementary Table 2.

Immunofluorescence assay

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 and blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Beyotime, China). Subsequently, samples were incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight and with fluorescent secondary antibody for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, the slides were sealed with DAPI-containing anti-fade medium. Images were captured and analyzed by Leica laser scanning confocal microscope STELLARIS 5. The following antibodies and reagents were used in this study:

NF-κB p65: Rabbit monoclonal antibody (clone D14E12; Cat. #8242; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA);

Secondary antibody: Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Cat. No. PF00001; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA);

F-actin labeling: Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated phalloidin (Cat. No. PF00001; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), a high-affinity probe that specifically labels filamentous actin;

Nuclear staining: 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Cat. No. PR30021; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), included in the mounting medium.

Luminex assay

Based on a literature review, a panel of 15 cytokines and related factors known to be secreted by human endometrial stromal cells (hESCs) or decidual endometrial stromal cells (DSCs) was selected for analysis. After treatment, cell culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 min to remove debris. Cytokine levels were measured using a Human Luminex® Discovery Assay (Bio-Techne, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The panel comprised the following factors: CCL2, CXCL16, IFN-γ, IL-1β, CXCL8, IL-11, IL-18, MMP-2, MMP-3, CCL5, HGF, IL-6, IL-10, IL-15, and LIF.

Protein extraction and western blotting

Cells and tissues were lysed using RIPA buffer. Nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were isolated using a commercial extraction kit (Beyotime, China). Protein concentration was determined with a BCA assay (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein (15 µg) were separated on 4–20% SDS-PAGE gels (Bio-Rad, USA) and transferred onto 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad, USA). After blocking with 5% BSA, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, namely P65, P-P65, β-actin and Histone H3 (Cell Signaling Technology, USA). Subsequently, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using a ChemiDocXR+ Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). Relative protein expression was normalized to β-actin or Histone H3.

siRNA transfection

hESCs were seeded with a confluency of 60%–70% the night prior to transfection. Transfection of siNC or siP65#1–3 was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48–72 h of transfection, cells were collected for subsequent experiments. All siRNA sequences used in this study were provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Public database analysis

To identify potential downstream target genes of p65, we utilized the following three publicly available databases:

TRRUST (Transcriptional Regulatory Relationships Unraveled by Sentence-based Text Mining): This database (http://www.grnpedia.org/trrust/) contains 8,015 human and 6,552 mouse transcription factor (TF)-target regulatory relationships derived from literature, including information on the mode of regulation (activation or repression) and TF cooperativity.

GTRD (Gene Transcription Regulation Database): This resource (http://gtrd.biouml.org/) comprises uniformly processed ChIP-seq, ChIP-exo, and DNase-seq data related to gene transcription regulation.

hTFtarget: This comprehensive database (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/hTFtarget) provides extensive information on human transcription factor-target regulations, integrating 7,190 ChIP-seq samples from 659 TFs and high-confidence binding sites for 699 TFs.

These databases were queried to predict and cross-validate p65-regulated genes for subsequent experimental validation.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay (ChIP)

This assay was performed with SimpleChIP® Plus Sonication Chromatin IP Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, USA) in accordance with manufacturer’s protocol. Enriched DNA fragments were analyzed by RT-qPCR assays. The primer sequences used are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistical analyses

GraphPad Prism 8.3.0 was used for statistical analysis, and the data were expressed as means ± standard error of mean (SEM). Student’s two-sample t-test was used to assess differences between two groups, while one way analysis of variance (one way ANOVA) was used for more than two groups. Every experiment was performed at least three times and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Elevated estradiol level reduces fetal weight and impairs placental angiogenesis in mice

To investigate the effect of estradiol (E2) on pregnancy, we established a supraphysiological E2 mouse model as outlined in Fig. 1A [40]. Naturally conceived mice that received no exogenous hormone treatment served as the control group (NC group). Serum E2 levels were significantly higher in the E2 group than in the NC group (Fig. 1B), confirming successful establishment of the mouse model. At gestational day (GD) 12.5, fetal mice and placentas were collected and weighed. Both fetal and placental weights were significantly lower in the E2 group than in the NC group (Fig. 1C, D). However, placental efficiency (fetal weight/placental weight) did not differ significantly between the two groups (Fig. 1E). Further analysis revealed that, although no obvious morphological changes (e.g. the proportion of the labyrinth layer) were observed in the placenta, vascular density was significantly reduced in pregnant mice exposed to elevated E2 (Fig. 1F–I). Given that the placenta is a critical organ for fetal nutrient supply, this impaired angiogenesis may explain the observed reduction in fetal weight.

Supraphysiologic E2 alters uNK function via decidual stromal cell (DSC)-dependent paracrine signaling

We next explored how supraphysiological E2 impaired uNK cells. Firstly, we examined whether elevated E2 directly affected uNK cells. Purified mouse uNK cells were cultured for 24 h under two conditions: complete medium alone (uNK group, serving as the control) or complete medium supplemented with 1000 nM E2 (E2-uNK group) [40, 43]. The flow cytometry results indicated that direct exposure of 1000 nM E2 did not alter the percentage of three uNK subtypes, nor did it affect the expression level of VEGF, PLGF and IFN-γ (Supplementary Fig. 2). Similarly, direct exposure of human primary uNK cells to 1000 nM E2 did not affect the mRNA expression of these cytokines (Supplementary Fig. 3). These results suggest that supraphysiological E2 does not directly modulate uNK cell function.

Subsequently, we established a DSC-uNK co-culture system to investigate the role of DSCs in regulating uNK cells (Fig. 3A). The following groups were defined: uNK group (control), uNK cells cultured without DSCs in D/F12 medium alone; E2-uNK group, uNK cells cultured in D/F12 medium supplemented with 1000 nM E2; DSC-uNK group, uNK cells co-cultured with untreated DSCs; DSC+E2-uNK group, uNK cells co-cultured with DSCs pretreated with 1000 nM E2. Consistent with our expectation, 1000 nM E2 exposure couldn’t affect the secretion levels of these three cytokines in uNK cells. What’s more, we found that uNK cells co-cultured with normal DSCs secreted much more VEGF, PLGF and IFN-γ compared with uNK cells alone, confirming the modulatory effect of DSCs on uNK cells. However, the synthesis and secretion level of these three cytokines were notably reduced in uNK cells which were co-cultured with DSCs treated with 1000 nM E2 (Fig. 3B, C). To further assess the functional consequences of these changes, we collected conditioned medium (CM) from purified uNK cells after they had been subjected to the various co-culture conditions as defined above. This approach ensures that any observed effects on target cells are attributable solely to the uNK cells’ secreted factors, excluding direct contributions from DSCs or other components of the co-culture system. Using this CM, we evaluated its impact on HTR-8/SVneo trophoblast cell invasion and migration. CM from uNK cells previously co-cultured with untreated DSCs (DSC-uNK) significantly enhanced both HTR-8/SVneo cell invasion and migration compared to CM from uNK group or those E2-uNK group. In contrast, CM from uNK cells from DSC+E2-uNK group inhibited trophoblast invasion and migration relative to the DSC-uNK group (Fig. 3D-G).

We next examined the angiogenic potential of uNK cells using an in vitro tube formation assay with human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). As illustrated in Fig. 3H and I, DSC-uNK CM significantly enhanced both tube length and branch points compared to CM from control uNK cells or E2-exposed uNK cells. However, DSC+E2-uNK CM resulted in a marked reduction in these angiogenic parameters compared to the DSC-uNK CM group. These findings suggest that supraphysiological E2 indirectly impair the regulatory functions of uNK cells concerning the migration and invasion of EVTs, as well as angiogenesis, through paracrine signaling from DSCs.

Elevated E2 disrupts decidualization and cytokine secretion in DSCs

As decidualization is an essential process for endometrial stromal cells, we examined the effect of supraphysiological E2 on this process. Both molecular markers and morphological changes were evaluated. Compared to human endometrial stromal cells (hESCs, control), the mRNA levels of the decidual markers prolactin (PRL) and insulin-like growth factor binding protein 1 (IGFBP1) were significantly increased in both DSC and DSC+E2 group, confirming successful decidual induction. However, exposure of 1000 nM E2 during decidualization significantly suppressed the expression of both markers (Fig. 4A). Immunofluorescence result demonstrated that normal DSCs underwent a morphology transition from fibroblast-like to polygonal epithelial-like shapes; conversely, high E2-treated DSCs remained a fibroblast-appearance (Fig. 4B). DSCs secrete various cytokines and chemokines that modulate immune cell function. Therefore, we first conducted a literature search to identify cytokines produced by DSCs, which yielded 15 reported cytokines: CCL2, CXCL16, IFN-γ, IL-1β, CXCL8, IL-11, IL-18, MMP-2, MMP-3, CCL5, HGF, IL-6, IL-10, IL-15 and LIF. A Luminex assay was then performed to evaluate the effects of supraphysiological E2 on the secretion of these cytokines by DSCs. Among these, the concentrations of five cytokines, IL-1β, IL-18, CCL5, IL-10 and LIF, secreted into the culture medium were too low to be analyzed, falling below the detection range of the standard curve. For the remaining cytokines, no significant differences were observed between the control group (DSCs) and the high-estrogen group (DSCs+E2) for CXCL16, IFN-γ, MMP-3, and HGF (Supplementary Fig. 4). In contrast to these largely unaffected factors, supraphysiological E2 treatment significantly reduced the secretion of six other cytokines: IL-6, CXCL8, IL-11, IL-15, CCL2 and MMP-2 (Fig. 4C). In the control group, their levels were 10.25 pg/mL, 39.30 pg/mL, 1.96 ng/mL, 2.62 pg/mL, 15.64 ng/mL and 9.47 ng/mL, respectively, which decreased to 3.46 pg/mL, 17.51 pg/mL, 1.03 ng/mL, 2.05 pg/mL, 2.46 ng/mL and 9.10 ng/mL following high-estrogen exposure. Collectively, these results suggest that elevated E2 may impair uNK cell function by affecting decidualization, cytokine secretion, or a combination of both processes.

Elevated E2 attenuates NF-κB pathway in DSCs

The NF-κB signaling pathway plays a critical role in endometrial receptivity, embryo implantation, and decidualization during early pregnancy [44]. However, it remains unclear whether elevated E2 affects this pathway in DSCs. Western blot analysis revealed that supraphysiological E2 exposure suppressed the phosphorylation of the NF-κB p65 subunit (P-p65/p65) in murine decidual tissue, compared with the naturally conceived (NC) control group (Fig. 5A, B). Consistently, the protein level of P-p65 was significantly lower in E2-treated DSCs than in untreated DSCs (control group) (Fig. 5C, D). We further examined the nuclear translocation of p65 using western blot and immunofluorescence. The results demonstrated that supraphysiological E2 decreased both p65 protein levels and fluorescence intensity in the nucleus compared with untreated DSCs (Fig. 5E–G). Together, these findings suggest that supraphysiological E2 suppresses the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway in DSCs.

NF-κB pathway regulates decidualization and cytokine secretion in DSCs and thus affects function of uNK cells

To further investigate the role of NF-κB in decidualization, we used PDTC to block the NF-κB pathway in hESCs [45], prior to inducing decidualization in vitro. After decidualization, ELISA showed a significant decrease in PRL secretion of the PDTC-treated group compared with normal DSCs (control group) (Fig. 6A). Consistently, the mRNA levels of PRL and IGFBP1 were markedly lower in DSC+PDTC group (Fig. 6B). Moreover, PDTC also suppressed the secretion levels of a series of cytokines, including IL-6, CXCL8, IL-11, IL-15, IFN-γ, MMP2, CCL2 and CXCL16 (Fig. 6C). We next transfected hESCs with siP65 to knock down endogenous P65 expression, and the knockdown efficiency was confirmed by western blot. Among the three siRNAs tested, siP65#1 exhibited the highest interference efficiency and was selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. 6D, E). Compared with the siNC-transfected group, siP65#1-transfected DSCs showed significantly reduced mRNA expression of PRL and IGFBP1 (Fig. 6F). Immunofluorescence images revealed that deficient NF-κB disrupted cytoskeletal reorganization and morphological transformation during decidualization, so that siP65#1-DSC remained fibroblast-appearance (Fig. 6G). These results demonstrated that NF-κB participated in regulating decidualization and cytokines secretion. Subsequently, we established a DSC-uNK co-culture system again, which was performed as previously described. We found that uNK cells co-cultured with siNC-transfected DSCs (control group) secreted higher levels of VEGF, PLGF and IFN-γ than uNK cells alone (blank group). However, when uNK cells were co-cultured with siP65-transfected DSCs, the synthesis and secretion levels of these three cytokines were notably reduced (Fig. 6H, I). Taken together, these findings confirm that NF-κB regulates decidualization, and that DSCs with deficient NF-κB activity impair uNK cell function.

NF-κB P65 directly binds to the promoter region of BMP2 and CXCL8 to modulate decidualization and expression of CXCL8

The above results demonstrated that blocking NF-κB activity in hESCs impaired both decidualization and cytokine secretion. Among the affected cytokines, CXCL8 has been reported to modulate conventional NK cells [46]. We therefore hypothesized that CXCL8 may serve as a key paracrine factor through which DSCs regulate uNK cell function. To test this hypothesis, we performed a migration assay using a Transwell system as illustrated in Fig. 7A. The results showed that co-cultured with either DSCs or recombinant CXCL8 significantly enhanced the migratory capacity of uNK cells compared to uNK cells cultured alone (control). In contrast, addition of an anti-CXCL8 neutralizing antibody to the uNK-DSC co-culture system significantly suppressed uNK cell migration (Fig. 7B). To explore the underlying molecular mechanism, we searched the TRRUST, GTRD and hTFtarget databases to identify potential target genes of the p65 subunit of NF-κB. By intersecting the results from these three databases, we obtained 84 candidate target genes. After filtering for genes related to decidualization, we identified six genes: BMP2, CXCL8, CCL2, CXCL2, IL-6 and MMP9 (Fig. 7C). BMP2 is a well-established regulator of decidualization [47]. It acts as a downstream target of progesterone, promoting decidualization via the Wnt4–FOXO1 and C/EBPβ–STAT3–LIF axes [48, 49]. Given its pivotal role, we selected BMP2 for further analysis. RT-qPCR showed that mRNA levels of both BMP2 and CXCL8 were significantly reduced in siP65#1-DSCs compared with siNC-DSCs, suggesting transcriptional regulation by P65 (Fig. 7D). ChIP-qPCR confirmed direct binding of P65 to the promoter regions of both BMP2 and CXCL8 (Fig. 7E). Together, these results demonstrate that NF-κB P65 directly binds to the BMP2 promoter to regulate decidualization, and also modulates CXCL8 secretion by binding to its promoter.

Discussion

The Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) hypothesis posits that perturbations during early mammalian embryogenesis and fetal development—driven by alterations in the microenvironment—can exert long-term consequences on offspring health in adulthood [50, 51]. Against this backdrop, ART has become a critical public health consideration, with over 2.5 million ART cycles performed annually, resulting in more than 500,000 births worldwide [5, 52]. ART involves multiple procedural steps, such as COS, in vitro fertilization (IVF), embryo culture and embryo transfer, each of which introduces significant modifications to gametes and the embryonic microenvironment [53]. Notably, COS induces supraphysiological E2 levels during the periconceptional period and early pregnancy following fresh embryo transfer. This supraphysiologic E2 environment has been associated with adverse neonatal outcomes, including low birth weight and small-for-gestational-age (SGA) births [7, 8, 10]. Furthermore, emerging evidence links elevated maternal E2 exposure to metabolic and neurodevelopmental disorders in offspring, such as glucose intolerance, dyslipidemia, thyroid dysfunction and impaired verbal abilities later in life [6, 11, 12, 14]. Given these risks, it is essential to elucidate the mechanistic pathways through which elevated E2 levels adversely affect offspring health. Additionally, developing strategies to enhance the safety of ART remains a critical priority.

The placenta acts as the critical interface between the maternal and fetal systems, playing a pivotal role in fetal growth and development [1]. Growing evidence suggests that dysfunctional placental adaptations may serve as a key mechanistic link between maternal environmental exposures and the developmental programming of chronic diseases in offspring later in life [54, 55]. For instance, studies indicate that impaired placental growth can disrupt fetal cardiac development, predisposing individuals to chronic heart failure in adulthood [56]. Furthermore, both placental weight and thickness exhibit an inverse correlation with the risk of hypertension in later life [57]. These structural changes are thought to reflect functional placental alterations, thereby mediating the impact of maternal environmental disturbances on long-term disease susceptibility. Of particular significance to ART, supraphysiological levels of E2induced by COShave been demonstrated to disrupt normal placentation [18, 19, 21]. In this study, we observed that elevated E2 levels led to reduced placental weight, impaired placental angiogenesis and intrauterine fetal growth restriction (IUGR) in mice. These findings suggest that elevated E2 conditions during COS may induce placental morphological and functional abnormalities, contributing to low birth weight and predisposing offspring to long-term health complications. Identifying the mechanisms by which COS-derived supraphysiological E2 compromises placental function will be vital for optimizing ART safety and offspring health.

However, it remains unclear whether uNK cell abnormalities result from direct E2 exposure or indirect mechanisms. Although uNK cells were historically considered steroid hormone-insensitive, they express ER-β, a lower-affinity estrogen receptor variant [59]. To address this, we treated isolated uNK cells with high-dose E2 in vitro and observed no significant impact on either the uNK subset distribution or on the secretion of VEGF, PLGF or IFN-γ. These results imply that maternal supraphysiologic E2 influences uNK cells indirectly. Given the physical proximity of DSCs and uNK cells, it is likely that DSCs mediate this effect. Factors secreted by DSCs, including interleukin IL-11, IL-15 and IL-33, play a crucial role in the proliferation and differentiation of uNK cells [24]. Furthermore, DSCs are known to secrete a diverse array of chemokines that facilitate the recruitment of uNK cells. Using a DSC-uNK co-culture system, we demonstrated that supraphysiologic E2 alters uNK function via DSC-dependent paracrine signaling. We also discovered that elevated E2 significantly downregulated the angiogenic and trophoblast-invasive capabilities of uNK cells through DSCs. Furthermore, to investigate which cytokines produced by DSCs mediate the effects of supraphysiologic E2 on uNK function, we performed a literature search. According to published reports, DSCs can secrete 15 cytokines, including CCL2, CXCL16, IFN-γ, IL-1β, CXCL8, IL-11, IL-18, MMP-2, MMP-3, CCL5, HGF, IL-6, IL-10, IL-15 and LIF. Using these 15 cytokines, we designed a Luminex assay panel to evaluate the effects of supraphysiological E2 on the secretion profile of DSCs. Among these, IL-1β, IL-18, CCL5, IL-10 and LIF were secreted into the culture medium at levels too low to be reliably analyzed. No significant differences were observed between the control group (DSCs) and the high-estrogen group (DSCs+E2) for CXCL16, IFN-γ, MMP-3 and HGF. In contrast to these largely unaffected factors, supraphysiological E2 treatment significantly reduced the secretion of six other cytokines: IL-6, CXCL8, IL-11, IL-15, CCL2 and MMP-2. These findings suggest that the paracrine signaling from DSCs to uNK cells is selectively altered by supraphysiologic E2. Our findings establish that COS-derived supraphysiological E2 disrupts uNK cell function primarily by compromising DSCs secretory activity, rather than through direct actions on uNK cells. However, the precise mechanism through which elevated E2 influence the decidualization and cytokine secretion function of DSCs requires further elucidation. It should be noted that the panel of cytokines identified as being suppressed by supraphysiologic E2 was selected on the basis of a literature review. Consequently, our assay may not have detected other important factors that were also downregulated by supraphysiologic E2 in DSCs.

NF-κB is a pivotal transcriptional regulator of cytokine networks essential for endometrial receptivity, embryo implantation and decidualization during early pregnancy [60–62]. Immunohistochemical analyses demonstrate that nuclear phosphorylated p65 (the active form of NF-κB) is markedly upregulated in the secretory phase of the human endometrium compared to other menstrual cycle stages [60], consistent with its functional role in stromal cell differentiation. Both in vitro and in vivo studies confirm that NF-κB signaling directly governs the expression of decidualization markers [63, 64]. Notably, elevated E2 levels induced by COS can suppress NF-κB activation in murine endometrial tissues [65], suggesting a mechanistic link between supraphysiological E2 and impaired decidualization. We proposed that COS-induced supraphysiological E2 attenuates NF-κB activity in endometrial stromal cells, disrupting decidualization and altering the secretion of cytokines critical for uNK cell maturation and function. This study supports this hypothesis: high E2 levels inhibit NF-κB signaling, as evidenced by reduced p65 nuclear translocation. We further examined whether pharmacological inhibition of NF-κB or genetic knockdown of p65 in endometrial stromal cells affects the expression of IL-6, CXCL8, IL-11, IL-15, CCL2 and CXCL16—cytokines that were suppressed by supraphysiological E2. Similarly, we found that pharmacological inhibition of NF-κB suppressed the expression of these cytokines. Concordantly, DSCs with impaired NF-κB activity reduce secretion levels of VEGF, PLGF and IFN-γ in uNK cells. Collectively, these findings indicate that COS-induced E2 elevation suppresses NF-κB activity in endometrial stromal cells, leading to defective decidualization and dysregulated cytokine expression, which in turn disrupts uNK cell polarization and angiogenic function.

To further elucidate the role of NF-κB in decidualization, we identified bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2) as a key downstream effector in this process [66, 67]. Notably, genetic silencing of p65 in endometrial stromal cells not only impaired decidualization but also significantly downregulated BMP2 expression. Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays confirmed direct binding of p65 to the BMP2 promoter region, establishing a novel transcriptional regulatory axis wherein NF-κB activation maintains BMP2 levels essential for decidualization. Consistent with the well-established role of NF-κB in regulating cytokine and chemokine expression [68, 69], our study demonstrated that NF-κB suppression significantly downregulated a panel of mediators—namely IL-6, CXCL8, IL-11, IL-15, IFN-γ, CCL2 and CXCL16—most of which have been previously implicated in uNK cell biology [70–75]. In contrast, although CXCL8 has been shown to modulate peripheral NK cell recruitment [76], direct evidence for its role in regulating uNK cell function particularly recruitment remains limited. To investigate the molecular mechanism underlying decidual endometrial stromal cell-mediated modulation of uNK cells via NF-κB, we focused on CXCL8 as a representative candidate. Our results showed that p65 knockdown effectively reduced CXCL8 expression in hESCs. Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays further confirmed direct binding of p65 to the CXCL8 promoter region. Importantly, functional assays revealed that CXCL8 enhanced uNK cell migration, suggesting its potential involvement in stromal cell–uNK cell crosstalk.

We finally validated the above findings in vivo using a mouse model. Consistent with our in vitro results, pharmacological inhibition of NF-κB in pregnant mice suppressed the expression of the decidualization marker Dtprp and the decidualization regulator BMP2. Furthermore, NF-κB inhibition significantly impaired uNK cell secretion of the angiogenic factors VEGF and PLGF, thereby recapitulating the uNK cell dysregulation observed under COS-like supraphysiological E2 conditions. These findings demonstrate the broad potential applicability of our study; however, further exploration in humans is still required. A key limitation of this in vivo approach is that mice lack the Cxcl8 gene [77], precluding direct examination of the complete CXCL8-mediated mechanistic cascade in this species. Nevertheless, the observed alterations in three uNK cell subsets as well as reductions in VEGF, PLGF and IFN-γ secretion by uNK cells mirror those seen under supraphysiological E2 conditions, suggesting that NF-κB-dependent regulation of uNK cell phenotype is conserved despite species-specific differences in downstream chemokine effectors. Future studies employing humanized mouse models or non-human primates are warranted to further bridge this translational gap.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the conclusion that supraphysiologic E2 impairs angiogenic activity—derived from an in vitro tube formation assay—is primarily based on morphological parameters, including tube length and branch point counts, without molecular validation of endothelial cell specification. Future studies incorporating immunofluorescence or RNA-based approaches to assess tip and stalk cell markers (e.g., DLL4, VEGFR2 and neuropilin) would provide deeper mechanistic insight into how uNK cell-derived signals under distinct hormonal conditions modulate endothelial cell behavior during angiogenesis. Second, although our findings demonstrate that supraphysiologic E2 suppresses NF-κB activity by inhibiting nuclear translocation of the p65 subunit, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this inhibition remain to be fully elucidated. Unbiased high-throughput approaches, such as transcriptomic and proteomic profiling, could offer a more comprehensive view of the signaling networks and downstream effectors involved. Recent studies have successfully employed such strategies; for example, proteomic analysis combined with gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) identified the FABP4/PPARγ/NF-κB pathway as a key therapeutic target in chronic colitis and acute pneumonia [78, 79], highlighting the utility of these approaches in uncovering context-dependent regulatory mechanisms. Future investigations employing similar methodologies, particularly under varying estrogen concentrations, are warranted to systematically delineate how supraphysiologic estrogen suppresses NF-κB activity. Addressing these questions will extend our current findings and may ultimately reveal novel therapeutic targets for pregnancy complications associated with COS.

Conclusion

Our research indicates that supraphysiologic E2 induced by COS inhibit the NF-κB signaling pathway in endometrial stromal cells, thereby suppressing their decidualization and the expression of multiple cytokines essential for uNK cell functions. Consequently, this inhibition affects the recruitment and differentiation of uNK cells. The abnormal differentiation and function of uNK cells resulting from elevated E2 may significantly contribute to impaired placental development and function (Fig. 9). These findings provide novel mechanistic insights into how COS-associated supraphysiological E2 contributes to adverse pregnancy outcomes while simultaneously identifying NF-κB signaling in DSCs as a potential therapeutic target for mitigating the increased risks of low birth weight and postnatal complications in offspring conceived through ART.
