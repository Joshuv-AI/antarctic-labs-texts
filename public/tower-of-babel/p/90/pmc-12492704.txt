What makes a confident surgeon? Perspectives from surgical trainees and surgeons in Kenya, China, and Mali
Authors: Sidney Moses Amadi, Rakshit Vadher, Oscar Mandizadza, Chen Qi, Joan Ruth Waeni Kivai, Boubacar Sidiki Ibrahim Drame, Cheick Ahmed Tidiane Koné, Zhifei Wang, Jing Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSurgical training effectiveness varies significantly worldwide. Understanding the key determinants of trainee confidence and career preference across diverse healthcare systems is crucial for optimizing educational strategies. This study examines surgical training experiences from trainees and surgeons across Kenya, Mali, and China that shape confidence and career preference identifying common associated challenges and potential solutions.MethodsAn anonymous 38-item questionnaire incorporating multiple response formats was distributed online to surgical trainees and professionals across Kenya, China, and Mali (n = 274) between December 2023 and March 2024. The survey assessed demographics, training duration, working patterns, operative experience, assessment methods, perceived pre-operative mastery, and confidence/preference regarding surgery. Data were analysed using descriptive statistics, univariate tests, binary logistic regression (for confidence), and mixed-effects logistic regression (for preference, accounting for country). This study adheres to CHERRIES guidelines.ResultsAnalysis of 274 respondents revealed notable variations in professional composition and working patterns across countries. Only 28.8% reported confidence in performing gastrointestinal surgery independently after training. Multivariate analysis demonstrated that making a specialty decision (OR = 2.00, p = 0.035), experience as primary surgeon (OR = 3.07, p = 0.009), assessment during training (OR = 2.86, p = 0.011), and pre-operative mastery (OR = 3.43, p < 0.001) were key predictors of surgical confidence. Country-specific factors included professional status in Kenya, pre-operative mastery in China, and practical examinations in Mali. Regarding interest in surgery as a career, weekly participation in operations (OR = 1.94, p = 0.031), understanding procedures beforehand (OR = 2.13, p = 0.013), and effective team communication (OR = 2.15, p = 0.030) were significant factors.ConclusionThis multinational survey reveals that surgical training effectiveness is primarily determined by quality factors (pre-operative preparation, hands-on experience, and formal assessment) rather than training duration. Implementation of structured learning approaches emphasizing these elements could substantially improve surgical education, regardless of resource availability or regional differences.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07778-4.Keywords: Surgical education, Surgical training, Surgical confidence, Kenya, China, Mali

Introduction

Surgery is a critical healthcare field addressing diseases and conditions across body systems, profoundly impacting patient outcomes and quality of life [1]. However, access to safe, timely, and affordable essential surgical care remains a major global challenge, with approximately five billion people lacking adequate access [2]. This deficit disproportionately affects low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), contributing significantly to preventable morbidity and mortality, reflected in approximately 28% of Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) lost annually [3–7]. Addressing this requires a sufficient, well-distributed, and adequately trained surgical workforce, a goal highlighted by international benchmarks like those from The Lancet Commission on Global Surgery (LCOGS) suggesting a minimum of 5,000 operations per 100,000 population are needed for robust health outcomes [8–10].

However, surgical training varies considerably worldwide in duration, structure, supervision, resources, and assessment methods [11–15]. While traditional surgical training often followed the Halstedian model [16] influenced later by reforms like those prompted by Flexner [17, 18], modern approaches are increasingly evolving towards competency-based medical education (CBME) integrating simulation, and structured mentorship [19–21], Despite these advancements, significant heterogeneity in training duration, structure, resources, and evaluation methods globally persists. Concerns remain that some curricula may still lack sufficient emphasis on progressive responsibility or structured competency-based feedback [22], suggesting completion of a set training duration does not inherently guarantee competence or confidence for independent practice [23–25].

Defining training success requires looking beyond procedural logs to include crucial outcomes like trainee confidence, defined as their ability to perform a task which relates to readiness for independent practice [21, 26, 27], and interest in surgery as a career referring to overall career satisfaction, which influences surgery as a career choice and workforce sustainability, particularly relevant given concerns about declining surgical interest globally [28–30]. While confidence is implicitly taught via traditional apprenticeship [31], its development during training and its precise impact on performance warrant further investigation [32] Understanding the factors that build confidence and sustain preference from the trainees’ perspective is thus essential for program improvement.

Despite the importance of these factors, there is limited comparative data identifying the key drivers of surgical confidence and preference across highly diverse training environments, particularly concerning the relative importance of experiential factors (like operative participation and roles), preparation (pre-operative mastery), and evaluation (assessments) versus traditional metrics like training duration.

This study aimed to address this gap by investigating factors associated with self-reported surgical confidence and preference among trainees and professionals across three distinct settings: Kenya (with its devolved system, resource variability, and focus on producing surgeons for immediate independent practice [33, 34], China with its standardized, research-intensive system, where trainees face competitive pressures related to publication requirements and specialization pathways [35], and Mali a West African LMIC environment characterized by limited resources, reduced subspecialty access, and early surgical autonomy [36–38]. We sought to identify both universal predictors and context-specific nuances, hypothesizing that factors related to the quality of training experiences would be significant predictors of confidence and preference, potentially outweighing the influence of training duration alone.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study surveyed medical trainees and professionals across Kenya, Mali, and China using a cross-sectional survey design to recruit approximately 100 participants from each country for a total target sample size of 300. The survey targeted surgical trainees and professionals across diverse healthcare contexts that included university-affiliated hospitals and their respective university.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Zhejiang Provincial Peoples Hospital. Local facilitation was provided by the University of Nairobi and the China Medical Team in Mali. The survey was administered anonymously. Participants were informed about the study's purpose, voluntary nature of participation, anonymity, and data confidentiality before proceeding with the questionnaire; consent was implied by voluntary completion and submission of the survey. No personal identifiers were collected. Data were stored in password-protected files accessible only to the core research team.

Participants and recruitment

Participants included postgraduate medical trainees (surgical residents, medical officers on surgical rotation), medical interns who had completed a surgical rotation, surgical fellows, and supervising consultants involved in surgical practice or training in Kenya, China, or Mali. Inclusion required having completed at least a basic surgical rotation internship post-graduation. We aimed to capture both real-time trainee perspectives and retrospective reflections from career professionals, which is particularly relevant in settings where training pathways and roles can be fluid and early-career independence is common. The sampling frame was defined by three collaborating institutions and their affiliated training networks: The University of Nairobi (Nairobi, Kenya), Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital (Hangzhou, China), and Hospital du Mali (Bamako, Mali) all of which are primary hubs for surgical training in their respective regions. These institutions served as the primary sites for survey dissemination, reflecting a convenience sampling strategy. Participants were invited to participate via links or QR codes shared through medical trainee social media groups (e.g., WhatsApp, WeChat, Tencent, Twitter) and professional society networks. This was an open survey accessible via the shared links, with no incentives offered for participation. The target sample size was approximately 300 (100 per country); a total of 302 responses were received. (Fig. 1)

Study Flowchart design

Survey design, development, and pretesting

A 38-item questionnaire incorporating multiple-choice, Likert-scale, yes/no/maybe, and open-text response formats was developed for this study (Supplementary Material S1). Instrument development involved an initial literature review and consensus process by two surgeons (SM, ZJ). The draft questionnaire underwent iterative refinement and was piloted among surgical trainees and international medical interns in China. To ensure cross-contextual relevance, collaborators in Kenya and Mali also reviewed the instrument. In Mali, input was obtained from French-speaking clinicians to ensure clarity and appropriateness in the Francophone context. Translation accuracy and conceptual equivalence were verified by bilingual experts, back-translation was used. The questionnaire covered domains including: participant demographics and professional role, duration and structure of surgical training, workload characteristics, frequency and nature of operative participation, specific procedural experience (including gastrointestinal surgery), exposure to assessment methods, perceived pre-operative understanding and mastery, learning resources used, supervision, feedback, and team communication. The estimated completion time was 10–15 min.

Data distribution and collection procedures

The survey was administered online and configured for anonymous data collection. Microsoft Forms® and Google Forms® were used in Kenya and Mali, while Questionnet App® was used in China, where access to some Western-hosted platforms is restricted. Participants accessed the survey via direct links or QR codes. No institutional login or registration was required. To minimize multiple entries from the same individual, survey platforms limited submissions to one per IP address. Questions were presented in a fixed order without randomization or adaptive questioning (skip logic). Responses were required for key fields to ensure completeness; only fully completed surveys were submitted and analyzed. Once submitted, participants could not return to previous questions or alter their responses. This restriction was enforced through survey platform settings. Data collection occurred between December 2023 and March 2024.

Variables

Full details referenced in Supplementary Material S3.

Outcome Variables.

Surgical Confidence: Assessed using Question 18 (“Do/Did you feel confident performing some Gastro intestinal surgery operations independently after your surgical training?“). Responses were dichotomized for analysis: ‘Yes’ coded as Confident, ‘No’ and ‘Maybe’ coded as Not Confident.

Surgical Career Interest; Assessed using Question 23 (“How much do/did you like surgery after your surgical training?“). Responses (‘More’, ‘Less’, ‘Same as before’) were dichotomized for analysis: ‘More’ and ‘Same as before’ coded as Higher/Sustained Preference, ‘Less’ coded as Lower Preference.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 27.0) and R (version 4.4.3). Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize participant characteristics. Univariate analyses employed Chi-squared tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and independent samples t-tests, ANOVA, or (Mann-Whitney U test, Kruskal-Wallis test) for continuous/ordinal variables, as appropriate based on data distribution.

Multivariable logistic regression models were developed to identify independent predictors for each primary outcome:

Surgical Confidence: Binary logistic regression was used. Models were built using a forward likelihood ratio method, first including core training-related predictors, and subsequently adding demographic variables, as presented in the results. Adjusted Odds Ratios (ORs) and 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs) were reported.

Surgical career interest: Mixed-effects binary logistic regression was used, with Country included as a random intercept to account for potential clustering. Model fit was compared against a fixed-effects model using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and the Likelihood Ratio Test. The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was calculated from the final mixed-effects model. Adjusted ORs and 95% CIs were reported for the final adjusted model.

Country-specific multivariable logistic regression analyses were also conducted for both outcomes within each of the three countries. p-value < 0.05 was considered significant for all analyses. Minor missing data for specific variables were handled using mean imputation where appropriate and feasible; otherwise, complete case analysis was performed for regression models [Regression analyses used complete case data for variables included in each model]. Further details on specific tests and model diagnostics are provided in Supplementary Material S2. Figures were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0.0).

Reporting guidelines

This study adheres to the Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys (CHERRIES) guidelines, full checklist provided in Supplementary Material S2.

Results

A total of 302 responses were received. After excluding 22 responses from Mali and 6 from Kenya, 274 complete responses were included in the final analysis. See Fig. 1.

Respondents’ biographical and basal characteristics information

Analysis of 274 respondents across Kenya, China, and Mali see Table 1 revealed substantial variation in professional composition. Medical Officers/Interns were the predominant group in Kenya (61.7%, n = 58) and China (49.6%, n = 56), whereas Mali had the highest proportion of Fellows/Consultants (43.3%, n = 29). Short training periods (1–3 months) were most common across all three countries (Kenya: 56.4%, n = 53; China: 64.6%, n = 73; Mali: 49.3%, n = 33). However, Kenya uniquely reported individuals with 1-year (5.3%, n = 5) and 3-year (5.3%, n = 5) durations and had the highest percentage with > 5 years of training (29.8%, n = 28). Working patterns also differed: most Kenyan (53.2%, n = 50) and Malian (47.8%, n = 32) respondents reported working > 10 h daily, contrasting with Chinese respondents who predominantly worked 10-hour shifts (53.1%, n = 60).

Surgical confidence

Overall, 28.8% (79/274) of respondents reported confidence in performing gastrointestinal surgery independently after training. Initial analysis identified associations between confidence levels and; professional identity (p = 0.001) with Medical Officers/Interns reporting the highest proportion (40.6%) and Residents the lowest (18.3%). Confidence also increased with training period duration (p = 0.016). Additionally other factors associated with higher confidence in univariate analysis (all p < 0.05) included; deciding on a specialty, weekly operative participation, acting as primary surgeon/assistant, receiving assessments or examinations, understanding steps beforehand, and pre operative mastery of operation. (Full details in Supplementary Material S3).

Binary logistic regression using a forward likelihood ratio method identified four key independent predictors of surgical confidence: making a specialty decision (OR = 2.00, 95% CI: 1.05–3.81, p = 0.035), having experience as primary surgeon or assistant (OR = 3.07, 95% CI: 1.32–7.14, p = 0.009), being assessed during training (OR = 2.86, 95% CI: 1.27–6.45, p = 0.011), and reporting mastery of the operation before starting (OR = 3.43, 95% CI: 1.90–6.17, p < 0.001). (Fig. 2.)

Discussion

These study results challenge the traditional reliance on training duration as the primary benchmark for surgical training success. Across diverse settings in Kenya, China, and Mali, our findings reveal a nuanced picture where the quality and nature of the training experience encompassing direct operative engagement, cognitive preparation, and structured assessment more potently shapes surgical trainees’ confidence and preference for surgery, than time spent during the training period. For example, Parker et al. have shown that years in training do not reliably predict surgical preparedness in sub-Saharan Africa, reinforcing the need for competency-based benchmarks [39]. This distinction is critical, particularly as only 28.8% of respondents reported confidence performing gastrointestinal surgery independently post-training, suggesting a significant gap between program completion and perceived readiness. A similar disconnect is reported in a study by Mattar SG. et al. that residency completion often yields insufficient confidence for advanced procedures, frequently necessitating further fellowship training [24, 25].

After multivariable analysis. The strong predictive power of hands-on operative roles (e.g., primary surgeon/assistant, weekly participation) underscores the irreplaceable value of experiential learning. This aligns with self-efficacy theory, where mastery experiences build capability beliefs [40], and resonates with studies linking operative autonomy to skill development and reduced hesitation [41]. The findings imply that simply observing or performing minor tasks offered insufficient impact on perception of performance or whether learning occurred; meaningful engagement is key. Furthermore, while confidence is difficult to measure, it may serve as a surrogate for ability in this setting; reduced trainee self-confidence can diminish supervisor confidence, potentially leading to fewer opportunities for autonomy and creating a challenging feedback loop [42], reduced self-confidence reduces performance this leads to reduced opportunities for autonomy for the trainee creating a challenging feedback loop [31, 43]. Similarly, the significance of pre-operative mastery and understanding procedural steps highlights cognitive readiness as potentially crucial alongside technical exposure. Effective training must cultivate not only motor skills but also the planning and anticipatory understanding essential for navigating complex procedures, potentially enhanced through methods like mental practice [44] Progressive operative autonomy is critical to confidence development. Parker RK et al. demonstrated that graduated responsibility can be achieved safely in LMIC settings [45], as exemplified by Tenwek Hospital’s structured supervision model [46]. Our findings echo this, as hands-on roles significantly predicted confidence.

Beyond direct experience and preparation, structured assessment emerged as a distinct and significant contributor to surgical confidence. Far from being merely a hurdle, receiving assessments and particularly practical exams for Malian respondents likely provides essential feedback, benchmarks progress, and helps calibrate self-perception against external standards [47]. As Harden and Laidlaw emphasized, assessment is a component students cannot easily bypass to achieve qualification [48]. Critically, effective evaluation appears integral to developing realistic self-efficacy and mitigating the risk of inflated confidence developing from unsupervised or unevaluated experiences [23, 32, 49]. Our findings on assessment align with Beard’s assertion that volume alone is inadequate and that formative assessment is central to competence development [50]. Similar recommendations by Rickard et al. reinforce the value of feedback and structured evaluation [37].

While these core principles held broadly, the distinct systemic, resource, and cultural contexts across the countries clearly modulated which factors carried the most weight: In Kenya, the concurrent significance of both ‘professional status’ and ‘pre-operative mastery’ appears to reflect its diverse training landscape. Kenya is a lower-middle-income country [14] that operates within a complex, devolved healthcare system featuring 47 counties and a 6-tiered facility structure [33, 34] In Kenya, the finding that Medical Officers/Interns reported higher confidence odds than Residents is counterintuitive. While this could be attributed to residents’ “conscious incompetence” a greater awareness of their knowledge gaps, a more compelling explanation is the Kenyan system’s demand for early independence. Medical Officers must often assume significant operative autonomy out of necessity, a factor that likely accelerates practical confidence more rapidly than the structured supervision typical for residents. This dynamic is further complicated by the two predominant, yet distinct, surgical training pathways in Kenya: the university-based Master of Medicine (MMed) and the regional College of Surgeons of East, Central and Southern Africa (COSECSA) program, which offer different training experiences. Our study was not designed to differentiate between these groups, representing a significant nuance that our sampling could not capture. This highlights that the nature of operative experience, particularly early autonomy, as a powerful determinant of confidence in this setting. Graduates in contexts like rural Kenya often enter independent practice immediately, without the safety net of readily available senior mentorship or fellowship opportunities common in high-resource settings, making robust self-efficacy essential for safe practice [51]. The strong association of pre-operative mastery with confidence is consistent with findings from modular education interventions such as the regionally developed online curriculum evaluated by Parker AS et al., which demonstrated significant improvements in knowledge acquisition and satisfaction [45].

In China, the singular predictive strength of ‘pre-operative mastery’ is striking. This could stem from the highly standardized nature of the Surgical Specialist Training (SST) program implemented since the late 1980 s, typically housed within higher-level (Level 2 or 3) hospitals. Distinctive features include a variable training duration (3–5 years) dependent on the trainee’s prior academic degree (Bachelor’s, Master’s, or Doctorate), mandatory publication requirements, and a period of rural service [52]. This might reduce variability in other factors like assessment exposure or basic operative frequency, leaving individual preparation as the key differentiator. Alternatively, it might reflect the intense pressure for research output, driven by high stakes institutional rankings, financial incentives, and overarching state policy goals linking biomedical achievement to national prestige and rejuvenation [35, 52]. This creates significant tension for trainees balancing clinical duties with research demands, often within a system where they report limited agency over specialty choice or research projects, feeling dependent on mentors and ‘luck’ [35], potentially fostering a culture where meticulous preparation and perceived flawlessness (i.e., mastery) are paramount for building confidence.

In Mali, the strong descriptive association between confidence and the completion of a practical examination likely highlights the profound impact of formal assessment in a resource-constrained This finding likely highlights the profound impact of formal assessment in a resource-constrained West African LMIC context [36], where factors such as curriculum delivery, access to specialized cases sometimes necessitating simulation or rotations, supervision quality, and intense trainee workloads reports of > 100 h/week [53–55]. create significant challenges [56, 57]. High stress levels, and difficulties meeting ancillary requirements like research due to inadequate support [36, 58, 59], successfully passing a formal practical examination may serve as a powerful external validation and benchmark of competence. This perhaps provides a boost to confidence that surpasses the impact of day-to-day experiences in this specific setting, potentially also reflecting alignment with regional standards influenced by bodies like the West African College of Surgeons [37].

While the study’s multinational scope and focus on crucial trainee-centered outcomes represent significant strengths, several limitations temper our conclusions. First, our reliance on a non-probabilistic, convenience sampling strategy via social media and professional networks has significant implications. This approach prevented the calculation of a response rate and introduces a high risk of selection bias, as our cohort may over-represent individuals who are more digitally connected or more motivated to comment on their training experiences. With efforts to improve confidentiality, we did not examine the impact of gender Meyerson et al. demonstrated the importance of gender on operative autonomy [60]. As more women are choosing surgical disciplines globally this is an important avenue for further investigation. Additionally, it is important to note that these results represent perceived rather than objectively measured training outcomes, corresponding to Kirkpatrick Level 1 (reaction). We acknowledge that confidence is a multifaceted construct and is not synonymous with objective competence. As prior work by Fonseca et al. has shown, trainee self-assessment may not reliably reflect technical ability. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as a measure of the trainees’ perceived readiness for independent practice, a critical factor for professional development and workforce retention rather than a direct proxy for their surgical skill [61]. The cross-sectional design as previously described [32, 60] precludes causal inference, and the convenience sampling approach limits generalizability. Reliance on self-reported data introduces potential biases (recall, social desirability) Further study with expert observers could help to reconcile this limitation. Baseline variation in professional identity across countries reflects real structural differences in training systems. While this limits direct comparison, we addressed this through adjusted multivariable regression models and separate country-specific analyses to reduce potential confounding. These country-specific analyses are limited by smaller subgroup sizes, which result in less stable estimates and wider confidence intervals for certain predictors (e.g., Mali’s practical examination completion: OR = 16.500, 95% CI: 3.376–80.638). These findings should therefore be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution, avoiding overgeneralization to entire national training systems. Furthermore, a primary limitation is the marked heterogeneity of our respondents, which spanned from interns to consultants. This inclusion weakens the coherence of the findings, as the factors driving confidence in a trainee are not directly comparable to those of a practicing surgeon, an issue that statistical adjustment cannot fully resolve. With efforts to enhance interpretability, the dichotomization of outcome and predictor variables, while simplifying the interpretation of regression models, may have reduced statistical sensitivity. masking more nuanced variations, a point particularly relevant for the ‘surgery as a career choice’ outcome, where subtle shifts are educationally significant. Future studies employing methods that preserve the full range of ordinal data would be valuable to validate and expand upon these findings. As well as understanding whether confident trainees are more likely to sustain interest in surgical careers could provide valuable insights for recruitment and retention strategies, particularly in LMIC settings where surgical workforce gaps are substantial. Future research should examine this relationship to better understand how confidence-building interventions might simultaneously address both competency and workforce retention challenges.

Nonetheless, the implications for surgical education are profound, strongly advocating for an accelerated shift towards competency-focused training models. Optimizing surgical training necessitates actively structuring opportunities for meaningful operative roles, implementing robust pre-operative preparation strategies, and integrating regular, multifaceted assessments. Ultimately, this study argues that building the next generation of competent, confident, and committed surgeons hinges less on training duration and more on the deliberate design and quality of their formative experiences. Addressing the global surgical deficit requires not just more surgeons, but surgeons trained effectively through approaches prioritizing mastery, preparation, and evaluated practice.

Conclusion

Across diverse training settings in Kenya, China, and Mali, factors reflecting preparation (pre-operative mastery), experience (operative participation/lead role), and evaluation (assessment/examinations) emerged as key drivers of surgical confidence, outweighing the impact of training duration. These findings advocate for competency-focused surgical education strategies that emphasize these specific experiential and assessment components to improve training effectiveness globally.
