Acquired Vulvar Lymphangiomatosis: A Rare Condition After Cervical Cancer Treatment
Authors: Mário Moura, Inês Monteiro Brás, Cristina Alves, Tiago Aguiar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVulvar lymphangiomatosis is a rare but important clinical consideration in the context of cervical cancer history. The differential diagnosis between this condition and vulvar cancer through histopathological confirmation is essential. Despite concerning visual appearances, lymphangiomatosis is a benign condition without malignant potential and therefore, patients can be reassured.Keywords: acquired vulvar lymphangiomatosis, cervical carcinoma, vulvar benign lesions, vulvar lymphangioma

The vulva can be affected by a wide variety of lesions. When multiple lesions appear years after cervical cancer, physicians should be aware that this can represent a recurrence of the disease. But what if the culprit is the treatment rather than the cancer itself?

We present a case of an 80‐year‐old woman, who attended the emergency department with vulvar lesions that had progressively appeared within the last 10 months, as well as vulvar discomfort in the last few weeks. She had been treated for cervical squamous cell carcinoma with radical hysterectomy and pelvic lymphadenectomy and radiotherapy 25 years before. The lesions occupied the anterior portion of both major labia and the mons pubis. They consisted of several spread out heterogeneously sized (the largest with 8 mm) translucent papules and vesicles on an erythematous base (Figure 1A) One of the lesions had a hyperkeratotic appearance and was the target of a punch biopsy for histopathological assessment (Figure 1B). Of note, left lower limb edema was observed and had been present for more than 5 years, according to the patient.

(A) Global appearance of the vulvar lesions: Several spread out heterogeneously sized translucent papules and vesicles, resembling “frogspawn.” (B) highlight of the hyperkeratotic lesion that was biopsied.

The histopathological exam revealed lymphatic vessels proliferation in the superficial dermis without endothelial cell atypia or signs of dysplasia. These results represent the histological pattern of lymphangiomatosis (Figure 2). Thus, the diagnosis of acquired vulvar lymphangiomatosis (AVL) was established. The patient was offered outpatient follow‐up and topical anesthetics and barrier ointment to treat the vulvar discomfort. At the first follow‐up appointment, 3 weeks after, vulvar discomfort had significantly improved. As the patient showed symptomatic relief, we decided to proceed with watchful waiting of the lesions.

Histopathological features of lymphatic vessels proliferation in the superficial dermis (asterisks) without endothelial cells atypia [left panel]. Immunohistochemistry study was positive for D2–40 (lymphatic vessels marker) [middle panel] and negative for CD34 (blood vessels marker) [right panel].

AVL is a very rare condition characterized by benign cutaneous tumors [1]. A 2023 systematic review reported only 133 patients with this condition. Lesions usually consist of vesicular or verrucous papules, and are sometimes described as resembling “frogspawn” when there are grouped clear fluid‐filled vesicles. AVL is almost always a consequence of lymphatic damage and can be related to chronic inflammatory conditions, as well as to surgical intervention or radiation therapy for malignant tumors. Cervical carcinoma is the most well‐known associated neoplastic disease. Chronic infection and Crohn's disease are also known associations [2].

The differential diagnosis includes condyloma acuminatum or vulvar premalignant and/or malignant lesions (primary or metastatic). This differentiation can be challenging because lymphangiomas can have hyperkeratotic appearances and vulvar neoplastic lesions can mimic lymphatic lesions. So, histological sampling should be performed [1, 2]. AVL requires follow‐up because it can lead to genital lymphedema and/or chronic limb edema, although these lesions do not undergo malignant transformation [1, 3]. Due to the inherent benign nature of AVL, despite its potentially concerning visual features, diagnosis may provide patients with relief regarding cancer reoccurrence concerns. Outpatient follow‐up should be offered to ensure early management of complications should these arise. If lesions increase in number or size or remain very symptomatic, surgical or laser excision may be performed; however, there is limited outcome data about the best excision treatment modality [2].
