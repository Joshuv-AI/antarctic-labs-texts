Oncolytic virotherapy with chimeric rVSV-NDV causes tumor-specific immunity in a DC-activation-dependent manner
Authors: Lorenz Hanesch, Nina Hartmann, Sonja Glauß, Tianle Feng, Jennifer Altomonte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, activation of the immune system has been recognized as a key mechanism in supporting the therapeutic outcome of oncolytic virotherapy for cancer. Most studies have focused on T cells in the characterization of immune responses to therapy; however, the role of dendritic cells (DCs) in priming these cytotoxic T cell responses has been far less explored. rVSV-NDV is a chimeric oncolytic virus that causes a highly immunogenic cell death in infected tumor cells. In tumor-bearing mice, this leads to increased levels of circulating and tumor-infiltrating tumor-specific CD8+ T cells upon rVSV-NDV treatment. Here, we show that the rVSV-NDV-mediated immunogenic cell death leads to the activation of the dendritic cell subtypes, cDC1 and cDC2, in vitro and in vivo. Using wild-type (WT) and cDC1-deficient (BATF3KO) mice implanted with B16 melanoma lesions, we observed that the increase in circulatory, tumor-specific CD8+ T cells and the survival benefit conferred by rVSV-NDV tumor therapy depends on a functional cDC1 population. Finally, we demonstrate that rVSV-NDV therapy can be significantly augmented by the combined treatment with adoptively transferred cDC1 cells that were ex vivo activated and antigen-loaded by co-culture with rVSV-NDV-infected tumor cells.Keywords: oncolytic virus, cancer immunotherapy, dendritic cell, cancer vaccine, immunogenic cell death

Introduction

Conventional dendritic cells (cDCs) represent a crucial link between the adaptive and innate immune system. Upon exposure to danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), they become activated alongside other innate immune cells and locally produce cytokines like interleukin (IL)-12 and the chemokines CXCL9 and CXCL10.1,2,3,4 They are also able to take up antigens, process them into peptides, migrate to tissue-draining lymph nodes, and present the antigen-derived peptides alongside costimulatory factors like CD80 and CD86 on major histocompatibility complex class I and class II.5,6,7,8

cDCs can be further divided into subtypes, which play differing roles. The cDC2 subtype presents peptides on MHC class II complexes and stimulate maturation of various CD4+ T cell groups, including TH2 and TH17 helper cells.9,10 Meanwhile, cDC1s are able to cross-present peptides on their MHC class I complexes, stimulating CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and initiating many of the most direct cytotoxic immune effects.11,12 In addition, cDC1s are the primary producers of CXCL9 and CXCL10 in the tumor microenvironment2,13 and contribute to the activation of TH1 helper cells.10,14

Oncolytic viruses (OV) are viruses that specifically infect and replicate in cancer cells by exploiting the downregulation of antiviral signaling caused by the malignant transformation of the cell. These viruses offer an attractive approach to combat various cancers due to their ability to cause direct tumor-specific cell lysis and to drive immune-mediated anti-tumor effects.15 The immune responses to OV therapy are caused by the release of danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) during cell death16 and the antiviral response to the virus itself,17 both of which attract immune cells to the tumor microenvironment (TME) and activate them. This can lead to a transition from an immunologically “cold” to an immunologically “hot” TME,18 which can in turn activate systemic immune responses.

The chimeric virus, rVSV-NDV, consists of the backbone of the vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV), with its glycoprotein having been replaced by the two envelope proteins of the Newcastle disease virus (NDV): the hemagglutinin-neuraminidase protein (HN) and a variant of the fusion protein F, which had been modified previously to improve its fusogenic properties [F3aa(L289A)] to ease spread within the tumor.19,20 This recombinant hybrid virus was engineered with the aim of combining the beneficial aspects of each parental virus, while eliminating the respective safety concerns of each.19 In addition to providing an excellent safety profile for the recipient and for the environment, rVSV-NDV has been shown to lead to an efficient and highly immunogenic cell death (ICD).19 In the in vivo B16-OVA melanoma mouse model, this leads to a strong anti-tumor immune response characterized by an increased systemic activation of tumor-specific CD8+ T cells and their infiltration into directly treated and distant tumors.21,22,23 It was further shown that significantly increased numbers of activated DCs were present in the tumor-draining lymph nodes after rVSV-NDV treatment,21 and in vitro studies showed that co-culture with rVSV-NDV-infected tumor cells leads to activation of murine cDC2s and human, monocyte-derived DCs.22,24

Previous reports have highlighted a specific link between the DC subtype cDC1 and the therapeutic success of different oncolytic viruses using cDC1 knockout mouse models, but an analysis of the state of cDC1 activation in vivo during viral therapy has not been reported.25,26,27 While one study did describe an increase of tumor infiltrating cDC1s and an activation of the general intratumoral DC population in response to oncolytic virotherapy using a vaccinia virus expressing a programmed death-ligand 1 (PDL-1) inhibitor and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), in vivo tumor antigen cross-presentation data or information on the cDC1 state in tumor-draining lymph nodes, where their most crucial interactions with CD8+ T cells take place, was not described.28

Based on the existing prelimina, via OV-mediated ICD is an important contributing factor to the previously observed T-cell-based immune responses in VSV-NDV therapy of solid tumors in mice. In this work, we perform an in-depth analysis of DC activation in response to rVSV-NDV-mediated tumor cell lysis in vitro and in vivo. We also demonstrate that the presence of the cDC1 subset is necessary for the CD8+ T-cell-based anti-tumor immune response previously observed in response to rVSV-NDV therapy and the corresponding survival benefit. Finally, we show that ex vivo activation of cDC1s via co-culture with VSV-NDV-infected oncolysates can be used as a method to generate an effective DC vaccine to enhance rVSV-NDV therapy.

Results

The immunogenic cell death caused by rVSV-NDV infection of B16-OVA leads to DC activation in vitro

As we had previously reported the characterization of rVSV-NDV infection in the murine melanoma cell line, B16-OVA,22,23 which included virus replication kinetics and cytotoxicity analysis, we began by performing a qualitative analysis using microscopy in order to confirm susceptibility of this cell line. In vitro infection of B16-OVA with rVSV-NDV-GFP (expressing the green fluorescence protein reporter) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01 led to obvious signs of extensive syncytia formation and green fluorescence at 24 h post-infection (Figure 1A). In order to investigate whether this infection leads to an ICD, we analyzed cell surface expression of calreticulin and extracellular ATP as representative markers of ICD. Upregulation of cell-membrane-localized calreticulin was evident on living cells by flow cytometry analysis at 24 h after VSV-NDV infection compared to uninfected cells (Figures 1B and S1), and an increase in extracellular ATP was observed at the same time point after infection (Figure 1C). These results are consistent with our previous observations in human hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells.19

Having established the infectability and post-infection immunogenicity of B16-OVA, we investigated the activation profile of murine DCs in response to co-culture with B16-OVA cells infected with rVSV-NDV, as a functional readout of ICD. After 24 h of co-culture of cDC1 (MHC-II+ CD11c+ CD103+) and cDC2 (MHC-II+ CD11c+ CD103− CD11b+) cells with B16-OVA cells previously infected at an MOI of 0.01 (Figures 2A and S2A), we analyzed the expression of surface activation markers on the DCs by flow cytometry. These data indicated a statistically significant upregulation of critical co-stimulatory factors like CD80, CD86, and CD40 and the upregulation of MHC class I, which is critical to antigen cross-presentation and serves as an activation marker, on both cDC subtypes (Figures 2B, 2C, and S2B). Furthermore, we demonstrated an upregulation of CCR7, a receptor that modulates the migration of cDC1s from tissue to lymph node after activation29,30 in cDC1 and to a lesser degree also in cDC2 (Figures 2D, 2E, and S2C), as well as an upregulation of IL-12, which is critical to immune stimulation both in the TME and in the lymph node3,31 (Figures 2F, 2G, and S2D). In the case of cDC1, we were also able to observe increases in mRNA levels of CXCL10, which contributes to T cell recruitment into the tumor2,13,32 (Figure 2H). No significant increases in CXCL10 expression were observed in cDC2, and no increase of CXCL9 was observed in either cDC subtype (data not shown).

Finally, we measured presentation of the OVA-derived peptide, SIINFEKL, on MHC class I by cDC1 cells after 24 h of co-culture with infected B16-OVA in order to demonstrate antigen processing and presentation after co-culture with infected cells. A significant increase in cells presenting SIINFEKL in an MHC-class-I-restricted manner could be observed on cDC1s co-cultured with infected B16-OVA cells compared to those co-cultured with uninfected tumor cells (Figures 2I and S2E).

Treatment of B16-OVA tumors in vivo leads to an increase in DC activation in the tumor-draining lymph nodes

To test whether the in vitro observations translate into an in vivo setting, subcutaneous B16-OVA tumors were implanted bilaterally into the flanks of C57BL/6 mice and treated by intratumoral injection of 1 × 107 TCID50 rVSV-NDV or PBS into the tumor on the right-hand side, while the contralateral tumor was left untreated in order to investigate abscopal effects. Animals were euthanized 2 days later for analysis of DC contents in the tumor and tumor-draining lymph nodes (Figure 3A). While both subtypes demonstrated a trend towards a reduction in the number of DCs present in the directly treated tumors themselves after rVSV-NDV treatment (Figures S3A and S3B), the number of cDC1 and cDC2 in the tumor-draining lymph nodes of rVSV-NDV-treated mice increased significantly on the directly treated tumor side compared to the non-treated side in the same mice, as well as the lymph nodes draining the PBS-treated tumors in the control group (Figure 3B). In these lymph nodes, we also observed an increase in activation markers on cDC1, with significant increases in CD86, MHC class I, and CCR7 expression and trends toward higher expression of CD80 (Figure 3C). Among cDC1 cells in these lymph nodes, we also observed an increase in SIINFEKL presentation on MHC class I complexes (Figure 3D), suggesting the potential of these cDC1 to initiate an anti-tumor CD8+ T cell response. Meanwhile, cDC2 cells in the same lymph nodes showed significant upregulation of the CD80, CD86, and MHC class I activation markers and a trend indicating an increase in CCR7 (Figure 3E).

The anti-tumor CD8+ T cell response observed after rVSV-NDV treatment is cDC1-dependent

To investigate the role of cDC1 activation in the in vivo treatment response to rVSV-NDV, we implanted B16-OVA tumors bilaterally into the flanks of C57BL/6 (wild type [WT]) and BATF3KO mice and monitored survival in response to rVSV-NDV therapy. BATF3KO mice have the C57BL/6 background but lack exons 1 and 2 of the Batf3 gene, disabling the function and cross-presentation of antigens by cDC1 and, therefore, their interaction with CD8+ T cells.12 Six days post-implantation, both mouse groups were then treated with PBS or 1 × 107 TCID50 rVSV-NDV intratumorally into the right flank tumor, followed by additional treatments 3, 6, and 14 days later and analysis of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) on day 8 after the start of treatment (Figure 4A). While treatment of WT mice led to a small but statistically significant (p = 0.04) prolongation of survival, this treatment effect was weakened (p = 0.15) in the BATF3KO mice (Figures 4B and S4). Despite survival prolongation in response to virus therapy in the WT context (Figure 4B), individual tumor volumes varied across treatment groups, and therefore, no statistically significant differences could be determined (Figure S4). An analysis of the peripheral blood collected on day 8 revealed a statistically significant increase in effector CD8+ T cells, as defined by the CD44+ CD62L− phenotype, in WT mice treated with rVSV-NDV, compared both to WT mice treated with PBS and to BATF3KO mice treated with rVSV-NDV (Figures 4C, S5A, and S5B). The CD8+ T cells of the same mice also showed a statistically significant upregulation of the T cell activation marker, programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) (Figures 4C and S5C). In WT mice, we observed a statistically significant increase in CD8+ T cells expressing OVA-specific T cell receptors (TCRs) in mice treated with rVSV-NDV compared to mice treated with PBS (Figures 4D and S5D). While an increase in CD8+ T cells with OVA-specific TCRs was also observable in several BATF3KO mice after rVSV-NDV treatment, this effect was smaller and not statistically significant (Figure 4D).

Among these CD8+ T cells with OVA-specific TCRs, the activation effect was even more pronounced than in the general population (Figures 4E and 4C). In WT mice treated with rVSV-NDV, nearly 100% of these cells presented the CD44+ CD62L− effector phenotype, a statistically significant increase compared to the same cells in WT mice treated with PBS and BATF3KO mice treated with rVSV-NDV (Figure 4E). The expression of PD-1 on these cells on WT mice treated with rVSV-NDV was similarly increased by a statistically significant margin (Figure 4E). These data suggest that the anti-tumor immune reaction triggered by rVSV-NDV therapy, which contributes to its therapeutic benefit, is dependent on the presence of functional cDC1 cells.

Together with our previous work showing that depletion of CD8+ T cells abrogates the ability of rVSV-NDV to inhibit tumor growth,21 this hints toward an important role of the cDC1-CD8+ T cell axis of acquired immunity during rVSV-NDV therapy. A similar experiment using anti-NK1.1 antibodies to transiently deplete natural killer (NK) cells was performed as well; however, in this context, no notable difference in the therapeutic efficacy of rVSV-NDV was observed compared to control mice treated with an antibody isotype control over the course of virus treatment (Figure S6). These results suggest that NK cells play only a minor role in rVSV-NDV therapy in this tumor model.

rVSV-NDV therapy can be enhanced by combination with a cDC1 vaccine generated by ex vivo co-culture with rVSV-NDV-lysed tumor cells

After demonstrating that a lack of a functional cDC1 population inhibits the anti-tumor immune response and survival benefit resulting from rVSV-NDV therapy and that rVSV-NDV-oncolysates can effectively activate cDC1s in vitro, we explored the potential of rVSV-NDV-oncolysate-activated cDC1s as a complimentary therapy to boost the immune response and survival in the context of rVSV-NDV therapy in tumor-bearing mice. To that end, subcutaneous B16-OVA tumors were implanted into the flanks of C57BL/6 mice and treated intratumorally with rVSV-NDV or PBS as in the previous experiment. Additionally, cDC1 cells were co-cultured with infected B16-OVA cells in vitro as depicted in Figure 2A and then sorted and injected subcutaneously above the tumor-draining inguinal lymph node on days 1 and 7 post-treatment initiation in additional cohorts of PBS- or rVSV-NDV-treated mice (Figure 5A). While the DC vaccine on its own did not influence survival, rVSV-NDV treatment led to significantly prolonged survival (p < 0.01), which was even further enhanced when administered in combination with the DC vaccine (p < 0.05) (Figure 5B). These survival data were reflected in the tumor growth kinetics, although a high degree of variation within each treatment group prevented statistical significance of these findings (Figure 5C).

Compared to the PBS control group, significantly more circulating CD8+ T cells of the CD44+ CD62L− effector phenotype or expressing the activation marker, PD-1, could be observed in both groups receiving rVSV-NDV treatment, although this effect was not further enhanced by combination with the DC vaccine (Figure 5D). This trend was similarly reflected in the overall percentage of CD8+ T cells with OVA-specific TCRs (Figure 5E) and within their subsets exhibiting an effector phenotype or showing PD-1 upregulation (Figure 5F). Interestingly, the difference between the combination therapy and the rVSV-NDV monotherapy in the peripheral blood became more pronounced at day 15, with trends toward a higher percentage of overall and OVA-specific effector cells and more PD-1 upregulation in the combination treatment group although the difference remained non-significant (Figures S7A and S7B). Similarly, more CD8+ T cells with OVA-specific TCRs were in circulation in mice treated with the combination therapy (Figure S7C).

In order to determine how this upregulation in tumor-specific T cell responses compares to the response to the virus itself, we analyzed the percentage of CD8+ T cells expressing TCRs specific for rVSV-NDV (specifically the VSV nucleoprotein). We observed a statistically significant (p < 0.01) upregulation of virus-specific CD8+ T cells in both rVSV-NDV-treated groups compared to the PBS-treated groups (Figures 5G and S7D); however, the percentage substantially decreased by day 15 (around 4% on day 15 versus around 10% on day 8), and there was no difference detectable, at either time point, between mice that had received the rVSV-NDV monotherapy and those that had received the combination therapy (Figures 5G and S7E).

The immunogenic cell death caused by rVSV-NDV infection of HCC cells leads to DC activation in vitro

In order to rule out the possibility that the observed DC activation in response to rVSV-NDV treatment is tumor cell line or tumor entity specific, we investigated cDC1 and cDC2 activation in vitro using the setup described in Figure 2A, but this time using two murine hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines. We established infectability of RIL-175, a murine HCC cell line (Figure S8A), and a second, novel murine HCC cell line called HCC c7.1 (Figure S8B). As these cell lines are less susceptible than B16-OVA to rVSV-NDV, we infected them for a slightly longer duration (48 h) with rVSV-NDV at a higher MOI (MOI 1), before co-culturing them with DCs for 24 h, as before. Significant upregulation of the co-stimulatory markers CD80, CD86, and CD40, as well as MHC class I, was observed on both cDC1 and cDC2 cells when co-cultured with infected RIL-175 or HCC c7.1 compared to those co-cultured with uninfected tumor cells (Figures 6A, 6B, S9A, and S9B). CCR7 and IL-12 were also clearly upregulated on cDC1s co-cultured with both infected tumor cell lines (Figures 6A, 6B, S9A, and S9B), with a notably lesser degree of upregulation observable in case of cDC2. Corresponding to the lower susceptibility, lower levels of ICD markers were also observed. Extracellular ATP levels 48 h after infection were not significantly increased compared to uninfected cells (Figures 6C and S9C), while cell surface calreticulin levels were increased (p < 0.05) (Figures 6D and S9D), although to a lower degree than than previously observed in response to infection of B16-OVA.

rVSV-NDV-mediated cell death in HCC cell lines causes a stronger DC activation than pulsing with virus particles or uninfected tumor lysates

In order to investigate the contribution of the direct virus infection of DCs on their activation status, we compared activation markers on DCs co-cultured with infected or uninfected tumor cells with DCs directly infected with an equivalent amount of virus in the absence of tumor cells. While culture of DCs with infected B16-OVA tumor cell lysate (called oncolysate hereafter) led to an activation that was equivalent to, or often less than, that achieved by direct infection, the co-culture with HCC oncolysates did lead to mostly significantly higher activation than that achieved with uninfected cells or the virus on its own (Figures S10 and S11). It must be noted, however, that the viral titers produced by B16-OVA were several orders of magnitude higher than those produced by the HCC cells, which could explain why the direct DC infection control for B16-OVA generated a substantially higher activation than that for the HCC cell lines. To put DC activation by the virus alone and the oncolysates into perspective, activation marker upregulation by lipopolysaccharide (LPS),33,34 a cytokine-based activation cocktail,35,36 and the RIG-I agonist 3p-RNA37,38 is also shown.

To rule out the possibility that an additive effect of pulsation with virus particles and tumor cell particles is responsible for the increased DC activation upon HCC co-culture, rather than the effect of DAMPs released by the HCC cells in response to infection, we treated both infected and uninfected HCC cell lines with UVB light to inactivate the virus. We then created two additional controls by adding UVB-inactivated virus to UVB-treated uninfected cells or to naive DCs, at a titer equivalent to the titer in the infected tumor cell wells (Figure 7A). We observed statistically significant upregulation of the costimulatory factors, CD80 and CD86, as well as upregulation of MHC class I on cDC1 and cDC2 treated with HCC-cell-line-based oncolysates compared to DCs co-cultured with UVB-treated tumor cells with and without additional UV-inactivated virus particles (Figures 7B, 7C, S12A, and S12B). DCs stimulated with HCC oncolysates also tended to be more activated than naive DCs stimulated with UVB-inactivated rVSV-NDV. This difference was significant for both co-stimulatory activation markers in the case of HCC-c7.1-based oncolysates used to treat both cDC1 and cDC2, as well as for MHC class I in treated cDC1 cells (Figures S12A and S12B). In the case of RIL-175, this difference was statistically significant for CD80 and CD86 on cDC2 but only for CD80 on cDC1 (Figures 7B and 7C).

Discussion

Within the last years, oncolytic viruses have been increasingly accepted into clinical application, especially since the Food and Drug Administration (FDA)s approval of T-Vec, an attenuated herpes simplex strain modified to express GM-CSF in infected cells, for the treatment of melanoma.39,40 Since then, numerous novel oncolytic viruses have made the transition from preclinical to clinical trials, and a second recombinant herpes simplex vector has been approved for the treatment of glioblastoma in Japan.41,42 Simultaneously, the ability of oncolytic viruses to act as facilitators of anti-tumor immunity has been increasingly recognized to be of crucial importance to their utility as effective cancer therapies.15,43 This has resulted in detailed investigations of the tumor-directed T cell responses mediated by treatment with most common oncolytic viruses, including rVSV-NDV,21,41,44,45 and has resulted in their combination with therapies further enhancing T cell function, especially immune checkpoint inhibitors.22,46,47

The research into the mechanisms behind these T cell responses has been less thorough. Detailed work investigating the ability of oncolytic viruses to change the immune characteristics of the TME has been published, describing their ability to turn immunogenically “cold” tumors “hot,” allowing for easier invasion of the tumors by T cells and other immune cells and decreasing the rate at which they become exhausted.18 Less attention has been paid to the mechanism of initiation of the T cell response, in which DCs are hypothesized to play a crucial role.48 DCs are able to transition an innate immune response to a systemic immune response by taking up tumor antigens, migrating to the lymph node and presenting tumor-antigen-derived peptides to naive T cells. The cDC1 subset is of special interest in this context due to their ability to not only present peptides to CD4+ T cells on MHC class II but also cross-present peptides on MHC class sI directly to cytotoxic CD8+ T cells.11,12

Importantly for their potential to activate DCs, the ability of oncolytic viruses to reshape the TME not only facilitates tumor invasion by T cells but also contributes to mechanisms that can counteract DC-suppressive signaling often prevalent in the TME.49,50,51 Among these factors capable of activating DCs is type I interferon signaling by immune cells or even tumor cells in the TME upon viral infection, which we previously observed in murine melanoma tumors treated with rVSV-NDV,21,52 or the presence of viral particles or viral RNA itself.53,54,55,56 In addition to those, we had previously shown that the syncytia-based, highly ICD caused by rVSV-NDV also releases DAMPs like HSP70, HSP90, HMGB1, and extracellular ATP.19,57 In this work, we confirmed the increase in extracellular ATP in B16-OVA and two murine HCC cell lines after rVSV-NDV infection and showed that infection leads to increased expression of cell surface calreticulin, which acts as an eat me signal, facilitating phagocytosis of the infected cells by antigen-presenting cells like macrophages and DCs.58,59,60

The classical view of T cell activation by DCs involves three signals. To investigate whether rVSV-NDV infection of tumor cells leads to functional DC activation, we analyzed DCs in the context of these three signals after treatment in vitro and in vivo. The first signal is antigen presentation, which is facilitated by upregulation of MHC class I,61 which we also demonstrated in vitro (Figures 2B, 2C, 6A, 6B, S9A, and S9B) and in vivo (Figures 3C and 3E) in response to rVSV-NDV treatment. Direct antigen presentation of chicken ovalbumin, used as a model antigen in the B16-OVA cell line, was observed on cDC1s via its cross-presentation on MHC class I, both in vitro and in vivo (Figures 2I and 3D). The second signal consists of co-stimulation of the T cells by co-stimulatory factors like CD80, CD86, and CD40,62,63 whose upregulation on cDC1 and cDC2 was shown using multiple tumor cell lines in vitro (Figures 2B, 2C, 6A, 6B, S9A, and S9B) and in vivo (Figures 3C and 3E). The third signal usually consists of inflammatory cytokines, with IL-12, whose upregulation we showed in vitro (Figures 2F, 2G, 6A, 6B, S9A, and S9B), being of special importance for the activation of CD8+ T cells.3,31

To better model in vivo conditions, we co-cultured specifically differentiated cDC1 and cDC2 cells with infected tumor cells in our in vitro setup instead of only directly adding virus to the DCs (Figures 2, 6, and S9), as performed in most previous publications characterizing DCs in response to oncolytic virus infection.25,26,27 To determine the source of the DC activation signal, we exposed DCs directly to live virus at doses equivalent to the viral titers produced by infected tumor cells and compared the resulting responses with those observed following co-culture with infected cells (Figures S10 and S11). DC activation induced by infected B16-OVA cells was comparable to that elicited by direct infection with the corresponding viral dose. In contrast, infected HCC-derived cell lines induced stronger activation than could be explained by the lower viral titers they produced, as evidenced by multiple activation markers. Together, these results indicate that direct DC infection contributes to activation in a dose-dependent manner, while additional factors associated with infected HCC cells may further enhance DC activation.

In addition to the correct signaling, DCs also need to be co-localized with naive T cells in order to activate them. This usually requires migration from the TME into the tumor-draining lymph node, which is mediated by CCR7 expression on DCs.29,30 CCR7 acts as a chemotaxis sensor, following CCL19 and CCL21 gradients into the lymph node, specifically toward the T cell area in the lymph node paracortex.64,65,66,67,68 We demonstrated upregulation of CCR7 on cDC1 upon co-culture with rVSV-NDV-infected tumor cells, both in vitro (Figures 2D, 6A, and S9A) and in vivo (Figure 3C). In addition, a decrease in tumor-resident DCs (Figure S3B), corresponding with the observed increase in lymph node resident DCs (Figure 3B) 2 days after tumor treatment in vivo, is a further indicator of DC migration into the lymph node.

CXCL10 plays a similarly important but inverse role: if expressed by tumor-resident cDC1s, it attracts CXCR3+ effector CD8+ T cells into the TME,2,13,32 and we demonstrated its upregulation in cDC1 upon co-culture with rVSV-NDV-infected B16-OVA cells in vitro (Figure 2H). In contrast to previous work showing an increase of intratumoral cDC1s after treatment of colon adenocarcinoma with a modified vaccinia virus,28 we observed a decrease of intratumoral cDC1s on day 2 after rVSV-NDV treatment of the B16-OVA melanoma model (Figure S3B). This highlights the differences between different viruses acting in different tumor entities. We also cannot rule out that an increase in cDC1s had occurred at an earlier time point, before migrating to the tumor-draining lymph nodes. While a decrease in the cDC1 population in the TME could lead to reduced levels of CXCL10 production, and thereby impair T cell infiltration, our previous work demonstrated that intratumoral rVSV-NDV treatment of the same tumor model led to increased CXCL10 expression and increased invasion of effector CD8+ T cells into the tumor after rVSV-NDV treatment.21 This suggests that other immune cells in the TME are able to make up for the reduced numbers of CXCL10-producing cDC1s.69,70

Our data suggest that DCs could play an important role in activating T cells during in vivo therapy with rVSV-NDV. To test this hypothesis, the use of BATF3KO mice allowed us to investigate survival and T cell response to rVSV-NDV treatment in the absence of functional cDC1 cells in comparison to WT mice. While a significant survival benefit of the viral therapy was only observed in WT mice, a high degree of variation in tumor volumes over the course of therapy across treatment groups prevented statistically significant differences in comparing rVSV-NDV therapy in WT versus BATF3KO mice. The systemic anti-tumor CD8+ T cell response after rVSV-NDV treatment was indeed abrogated in most cDC1 knockout mice, leading to worse survival outcomes (Figure 4) and mirroring the results of similar experiments performed using different oncolytic virus platforms.25,26,27 Taken together with the previously demonstrated negative impact of CD8+ T cell depletion on the ability of rVSV-NDV to restrict tumor growth,21 and the lack of an effect on the virotherapeutic outcome by NK cell depletion (Figure S6), this indicates a crucial role of the cDC1-CD8+ T cell axis on immune responses after rVSV-NDV treatment. However, the observation of a partial anti-tumor CD8+ T cell response and the corresponding survival prolongation in a minority of the BATF3KO mice after rVSV-NDV treatment indicate the presence of cDC1-independent mechanisms of T cell activation. There are reports that other DC subtypes like cDC2 and moDCs and macrophages are also capable of cross-presentation, although to a lesser degree than cDC1s.71,72,73

Experiments using DCs activated in vitro by co-culture with rVSV-NDV oncolysates as a DC-based cancer vaccine demonstrated that these DCs alone were not sufficient to initiate an anti-tumor (or anti-rVSV-NDV) immune response (Figure 5). This could be due to a need for optimizing dosing or utilizing an adjuvant. The vaccine was, however, able to enhance rVSV-NDV treatment to prolong survival (Figures 5B and 5C), although this did not lead to significant changes in peripheral T cell activation (Figures 5D–5G). Interestingly, although it is likely that a majority of the adoptively transferred cDC1s were presenting viral antigens, this apparently did not counteract the oncolytic viral therapy and potentially even supported it by enhancing T cell infiltration into the infected tumor. It will be important to investigate the mechanism of the enhanced therapeutic effect in the combination therapy in follow-up studies, particularly by characterizing the immune cell signatures in the tumor and tumor-draining lymph nodes. Nevertheless, the data indicate that rVSV-NDV could potentially serve as an adjuvant to support DC-mediated activation of tumor-specific T cell expansion and therapeutic success. These results are supported by similar findings in which oncolytic NDV or oncolytic Alphavirus M1 was used to complement an NDV- or M1-mediated oncolysate-based DC vaccine, respectively.74,75,76,77,78 Notably, and in contrast to the previously reported oncolysate-based vaccines, our approach uses specifically differentiated and sorted cDC1 cells that have potential therapeutic advantages over the predominantly cDC2-based DC approaches.

The data reported here demonstrate that DCs, and particularly the cDC1 subset, play a crucial role in the immune-stimulatory mode of action of oncolytic rVSV-NDV therapy. We have shown that rVSV-NDV-mediated oncolysis of two distinct tumor entities is capable of activating cDC1s and cDC2s and leads to the efficient processing and presentation of model tumor peptides via MHC class I molecules. We furthermore showed that the absence of a functional cDC1 population in tumor-bearing mice leads to a severe abrogation of the therapeutic effects of rVSV-NDV therapy. Finally, it was demonstrated that ex vivo activation of cDC1 cells using rVSV-NDV oncolysates is a promising method for generating an anti-cancer DC vaccine. Although this approach was not effective as a monotherapy in the conditions utilized here, optimization of dosing and injection route could lead to enhanced therapeutic effects and will be investigated in future studies. However, the significant survival benefit of the DC vaccine approach administered in combination with intratumoral viral therapy was an exciting outcome and further validates the critical role of cDC1s in oncolytic virotherapy using rVSV-NDV. These data provide an important mechanistic insight into the previously observed activation of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells mediated by the chimeric virus and support the further development of the ex vivo oncolysate approach for pulsing cDC1 cells as a promising personalized DC cancer vaccine.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and virus

Murine B16-OVA and HCC c7.1 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal calf serum (FCS), 1× penicillin-streptomycin, 1× non-essential amino acids, and 1× sodium pyruvate. RIL-175 cells were obtained from Lars König (Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich, Germany) under a Material Transfer Agreement from Tim Greten (National Institute of Health) and cultured in DMEM containing 10% FCS and 1× penicillin-streptomycin. All cells were cultured at 37°C under humidified conditions at 5% CO2.

Virus stocks were produced in adherent AGE1.CR.pIX cells (ProBioGen, Berlin, Germany), purified by ultracentrifugation over a sucrose gradient as previously described,79 and resuspended in PBS after a further ultracentrifugation step for long-term storage at −80°C. Viral titers were determined by TCID50 using AGE1.CR.pIX cells.

To generate the novel HCC cell line, HCC c7.1, HCC tumors were induced in Albumin-floxstop-Tag (AST) mice by intravenous injection with 1.25–5 × 108 plaque-forming units (pfu) of adenovirus expressing Cre recombinase (Ad.Cre), which was kindly provided by Martina Anton (Institute for Molecular Immunology, Technical University of Munich). After around 8 weeks of tumor growth, primary AST tumors were extracted, minced, digested with Liberase TM (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and then plated; single cell clones and sub-clones were isolated, among them HCC c7.1.

Extracellular ATP measurement

Twenty-four hours after infection of B16-OVA at MOI 0.01 and 48 h after infection of HCC c7.1 or RIL-175 at MOI 1, supernatants were collected and subjected to extracellular ATP measurement using the Real-Time-Glo extracellular ATP assay (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and using a GloMax Discover microplate reader.

Immune cell generation, co-cultures, and stimulation

Murine DCs were generated in vitro from bone marrow (BM) cells and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FCS and 1× penicillin-streptomycin (DC-M). For cDC2, 5 × 106 BM cells were seeded onto 10 cm dishes containing 10 mL DC-M and 20 ng/mL murine GM-CSF (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Ten milliliters of the same medium was added at day 3, and half of the medium was refreshed on day 6. Cells were harvested for co-cultures and stimulation on day 7.

For cDC1, 1.5 × 107 BM cells were seeded onto 10 cm dishes containing 10 mL DC-M supplemented with 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), supernatant containing CHO-Flt3L to a final concentration of 82.5 ng/mL murine Flt3L (Flt3L production cell line provided by Jan Böttcher, Institute for Molecular Immunology, Technical University of Munich), and supernatant containing murine GM-CSF to a final concentration of 2 ng/mL (cDC1-M). Five milliliters of DC-M supplemented with 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol was added on day 5, and the non-adherent cells were transferred into new 10 cm dishes in 10 mL cDC1-M on day 9. Cells were harvested for co-cultures and stimulation on day 14 as previously described.80

After infection of tumor cells at an MOI of 0.01 or 1, cells were cultured in DCM for 24 h in case of B16-OVA and 48 h in case of RIL-175 and HCC c7.1. The DC concentration was adjusted to 1 × 106/mL, and the suspension was added to infected or uninfected cells at a 2:1 DC-to-tumor cell ratio (1 × 105 DCs per 96 well, 5 × 105 DCs per 24 well). DCs and tumor cells were co-cultured for 24 h for DC activation studies. For intracellular staining for flow cytometry, Brefeldin A 1000x buffer (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) was added 4 h before the end of the co-culture period at a final concentration of 5 μg/mL.

For the direct DC infection control, DCs were infected with rVSV-NDV at titers equivalent to the titers obtained in the supernatants of the infected tumor cell wells. For the LPS control, 100 ng/mL LPS from Escherichia coli O111:B4 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added to the DC wells. For the cytokine cocktail, an adapted version of a previously published DC activation cocktail was used35,36: 250 ng/mL prostaglandin E2 (BioGems, Westlake Village, CA, USA); 1 μg/mL R848 (Resiquimod) (InvivoGen, San Diego, CA, USA); 5,000 U/mL rh interferon gamma (IFN-γ); 2,000 U/mL rh IL-1β; 16 ng/mL rh IL-4; 1,100 U/mL rh tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α); and 20 ng/mL rm GM-CSF (all Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For the RIG-I agonist control, 1 × 105 DCs were transfected with 1.4 μg 3pRNA (provided by Nadia El Khawanky, Department of Internal Medicine III, TUM Klinikum rechts der Isar) using Lipofectamin 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

To generate UV-inactivated virus lysates, infected cells, uninfected cells, and rVSV-NDV at titers equivalent to the titers in the infected tumor cell wells were inactivated for 10 min using UVB light, which was confirmed to completely eliminate infectivity of the virus. One additional control was set up by adding UV-inactivated rVSV-NDV to UV-inactivated uninfected tumor cells directly prior to DC co-culture. DC concentrations were adjusted to 1 × 106/mL, and the DC suspension was added to these three setups at a 2:1 DC-to-tumor cell ratio. A fourth condition was created by adding UV-inactivated rVSV-NDV at titers equivalent to the titers in the infected tumor cell wells to naive DCs.

Quantitative RT-PCR

RNA was isolated from DC-tumor cell co-cultures using the innuPREP RNA mini kit (Analytik Jena, Jena, Germany). RT-qPCRs were performed using the GoTaq 1-step RT-qPCR Kit (Promega) and a 7500 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using the following primer pairs: GAPDH forward (5′- GCC TTC TCC ATG GTG GTG AA -3′), GAPDH reverse (5′- GCA CAG TCA AGG CCG AGA AT -3′), CXCL10 forward (5′- CCA AGT GCT GCC GTC ATT TTC -3′), and CXCL10 reverse (5′- GGC TCG CAG GGA TGA TTT CAA -3′).

Mouse studies

Mouse studies were approved by the institute’s Center for Preclinical Research (ZPF) and the regional government commission for animal protection (Regierung von Oberbayern, Munich, Germany). Female C57BL/6J mice (Janvier Labs, France) were used at 7–9 weeks for tumor implantation studies, and female C57BL/6J mice at 7–30 weeks were used to isolate BM cells for DC generation. BATF3KO mice were provided by Jan Böttcher (Institute for Molecular Immunology, Technical University of Munich) and used at 7–9 weeks for tumor implantation studies.

A total of 2.4 × 105 B16-OVA cells were implanted subcutaneously into the flanks of mice. In the case of bilateral implantation for later tumor and lymph node isolation, the same number of cells were implanted into each flank. In the case of bilateral implantation for the survival study using C57BL6/J and BATF3KO mice, 2.4 × 105 B16-OVA cells were implanted into the right flank, while 1.2 × 105 cells were implanted into the left flank. During survival experiments, width and length of the subcutaneous tumors were measured using calipers, and tumor volume was approximated with the following formula:

Mice were euthanized when a tumor diameter exceeded 15 mm in one dimension or when the tumor ruptured, according to the requirements of local regulatory agencies. Survival times with respect to the start of treatment were plotted as Kaplan-Meier curves, and median survival times were calculated.

Six days after tumor implantation (day 0), mice were treated with 1 × 107 TCID50 rVSV-NDV injected into the tumor on the right flank. On day 2, tumors and the tumor-draining inguinal lymph nodes were isolated from the mice, and single-cell suspensions for flow cytometry were generated. For survival studies, additional tumor treatments were applied on days 3, 6, and 14. Blood samples were collected from the facial vein in EDTA-microvettes (Sarstedt, Nümbrecht, Germany) on days 8 and 15. Red blood cells were lysed with RBC buffer (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) to attain leukocyte suspensions for flow cytometry. For the NK cell depletion study, 25 μg of anti-NK1.1 antibodies (BioXCell, Lebanon, NH) or isotype control were injected intraperitoneally on the day before treatment start and then readministered twice a week for the duration of the survival experiment, while rVSV-NDV treatment was administered as described above.

For the DC vaccine study, cDC1s were generated and activated as described below, sorted to achieve a high cDC1 purity, and washed twice in PBS. These cells were then injected subcutaneously in close proximity to the tumor-draining inguinal lymph node. Mice were monitored daily and euthanized when the tumors ruptured or exceeded 15 mm in diameter, according to the requirements of local regulatory agencies.

Flow cytometry and cell sorting

All cells were stained for viability using Viobility 405/520 (Miltenyi Biotech, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) or the LIVE/DEAD Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and treated with FcR Blocking Reagent, mouse (Miltenyi Biotech, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany).

To detect cell surface calreticulin, cells were trypsinized (PAN Biotech, Aidenbach, Germany) 24 h after infection with rVSV-NDV at MOI 0.01 in case of B16-OVA or 48 h after infection with rVSV-NDV at MOI 1 in case of the HCC cell lines. These infected and uninfected cells were stained with rabbit anti-Calreticulin (Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom), fixed using fixation buffer (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), and then stained with the secondary antibody, goat anti- Rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 488) (Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom).

DC subtypes for in vitro characterization are identified using MHC class II antibody, anti-mouse, REAfinity (Miltenyi Biotech, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) or anti-mouse I-A/I-E antibody (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), together with anti-mouse CD11c, anti-mouse/human CD11b, and anti-mouse CD103 antibodies (all BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). Activation markers were stained using anti-mouse CD80, anti-mouse CD86, anti-mouse H-2Db antibody, anti-mouse CD197 (CCR7), and anti-mouse CD40 (all BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). Cross-presentation was measured using the OVA257-264 (SIINFEKL) peptide bound to H-2Kb monoclonal antibody [eBio25-D1.16 (25-D1.16)] antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Anti-mouse IL-12/IL-23 p40 (monomer, dimer, and heterodimer) antibody was used for IL-12 intracellular staining after DC lineage staining and treatment with fixation buffer and Intracellular Staining Permeabilization Wash Buffer (10×) (all BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA).

DC subtypes for the B16-OVA in vivo studies were characterized using anti-mouse F4/80, anti-mouse I-A/I-E, anti-mouse CD11c, anti-mouse CD172a (SIRPα), and anti-mouse/rat XCR1 antibodies (all BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). Activation markers were stained at the same time using anti-mouse CD80, anti-mouse CD86, anti-mouse H-2D b antibody, and anti-mouse CD197 (CCR7) (all BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). Cross-presentation was measured as described above. DC counts in lymph nodes and DC concentrations in tumors were determined using Precision Counting Beads (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA).

cDC1s for in vivo injection were stained using the LIVE/DEAD Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), along with anti-mouse CD11c and anti-mouse CD103 antibodies (both BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). Sorting was performed on an MA900 Multi-Application Cell Sorter (Sony Biotechnology, Champaign, IL, USA).

Leukocyte suspensions derived from blood samples were first stained for OVA- and VSV-specific TCRs using the H-2kb OVA Tetramer-SIINFEKL (MBL International, Woburn, MA, USA) and the H-2kb VSV-nucleoprotein Tetramer-RGYVYQGL provided by the NIH Tetramer Core Facility (contract number 75N93020D00005), respectively. A second staining was performed using anti-mouse NK-1.1, anti-mouse CD4, anti-mouse CD279 (PD-1), and anti-mouse/human CD44 antibodies (all BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), as well as CD3 anti-mouse, CD8a anti-mouse REAfinity, and CD62L anti-mouse REAfinity antibodies (all Miltenyi Biotech, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany).

Flow cytometry results were analyzed using FlowJo v.10.10 software (BD Life Sciences, Franklin Lakes, N.J., USA) and further processed using R.81

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism v.10.5.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). For tests comparing two groups in in vitro settings, paired, two-sided t tests were performed, as each datapoint represents the mean of three technical replicates. When comparing two groups in vivo, unpaired tests were used, as each datapoint represents one individual mouse. For tests comparing more than two metrics, one-sided ANOVAs were used, as before in the paired form in case of in vitro settings and the unpaired form in in vivo settings. If all groups were compared to one control group, Dunnett's multiple comparison test was used, and in all other cases Šidák’s multiple comparison test was used. Survival data were analyzed using the log rank (Mantel-Cox) test. In the case of all tests, significance was defined as p < 0.05.
