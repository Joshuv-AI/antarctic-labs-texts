Concurrent chemoradiotherapy plus nimotuzumab versus chemoradiotherapy alone for locoregionally advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma with a suboptimal response to induction chemotherapy: a randomized phase 2 trial
Authors: Li-Ting Liu, Xue-Song Sun, Ting-Ting Quan, Xiao-Yun Li, Ling Guo, Hao-Yuan Mo, Shan-Shan Guo, Sai-Lan Liu, Ying Huang, Dong-Hua Luo, Rui Sun, Guo-Dong Jia, Ji-Bin Li, Qing Liu, Pan Wang, Yu-Jing Liang, Jie Chen, Yi-Fu Li, Hui Cheng, Wei-Xiong Xia, Fang Qiu, Jin-Hao Yang, Qi Yang, Dong-Xiang Wen, Jin-Jie Yan, Chong Zhao, Qiu-Yan Chen, Lin-Quan Tang, Hai-Qiang Mai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Induction chemotherapy (IC) followed by concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) is the standard treatment for locoregionally advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma (LA-NPC); however, patients with a suboptimal response to IC, defined as detectable Epstein–Barr virus DNA and/or stable or progressive disease after IC, remain at high risk of treatment failure. Here we report an open-label, randomised, phase 2 trial evaluating whether adding nimotuzumab, a humanised anti–epidermal growth factor receptor antibody, to CCRT improves outcomes in this high-risk population. A total of 246 patients with untreated, non-keratinising, stage II–IVA LA-NPC were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive CCRT with or without nimotuzumab. The primary endpoint was 2-year progression-free survival (PFS); secondary endpoints included overall survival, distant metastasis–free survival, locoregional relapse–free survival, short-term response rate, and safety. At a median follow-up of 47 months, the 2-year PFS was 81.0% (90% confidence interval [CI], 74.3–86.1) in the nimotuzumab plus CCRT group and 80.8% (90% CI, 74.2–85.7) in the CCRT-alone group (hazard ratio, 0.93 [90% CI, 0.63–1.37]; p = 0.70). Survival outcomes were similar between groups, while low-grade rash occurred more frequently with nimotuzumab. These findings indicate that adding nimotuzumab to CCRT does not improve survival in patients with LA-NPC with a suboptimal response to IC, underscoring the need for predictive biomarkers and alternative therapeutic strategies. Trial registration: NCT04223024.

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), an Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)-associated malignancy endemic to East and Southeast Asia, is treated with concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) as the cornerstone for locoregionally advanced (LA-NPC) disease. Recently, several phase 3 trials have confirmed the superiority of adding induction chemotherapy (IC) to CCRT over CCRT alone1–3. Consequently, IC plus CCRT is now recommended as the standard of care (Category 1A) in the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines4.

Nevertheless, 20–30% of patients developed locoregional or distant recurrence after IC plus CCRT. Patients with detectable EBV DNA levels or an unsatisfactory radiological response (stable disease [SD] or disease progression [PD]) after IC are at an increased risk for locoregional and/or distant failure5–7. Thus, enhancing the therapeutic efficacy for patients with suboptimal response to IC has become a significant challenge for clinicians.

Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) is a member of the erythroblastic oncogene B family of receptor tyrosine kinases8,9. Adding EGFR inhibitor cetuximab to chemotherapy (EXTREME and TPEx regimens) demonstrated favorable survival benefits for recurrent or metastatic head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (RM-HNSCC)10,11 and metastatic NPC (mNPC)12. Concurrent cetuximab plus radiotherapy (RT) improved locoregional control and reduced mortality in LA-HNSCC13,14. However, its combination with CCRT has failed15,16. These negative results are likely due to toxicities from cetuximab or panitumumab, which often force cisplatin dose reductions and radiation interruptions. Despite this, a trend toward improved outcomes was observed in high-risk (HPV-negative and T4) subgroups. In LA-NPC, previous studies showed that >89% of patients express EGFR, and the overexpression of EGFR is implicated in poor prognosis17. A phase 2 single-arm study demonstrated promising results with cetuximab plus CCRT; however, cetuximab increased the need for nasogastric feeding and cisplatin dose reduction18. The efficacy of EGFR inhibitor remains controversial in LA-NPC. Nimotuzumab, a humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody targeting EGFR19, also mediates tumor cell death via antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity and complement-dependent cytotoxicity. Although cetuximab and nimotuzumab have similar mechanisms, they have different molecular and biological impacts20. In LA-HNSCC, nimotuzumab combined with CCRT improves progression-free survival (PFS) and locoregional control with mild toxicity21. In recurrent/metastatic NPC, it shows potential efficacy and good tolerance when combined with cisplatin–fluorouracil22. Several retrospective studies indicate that CCRT with nimotuzumab is more effective than CCRT alone in reducing relapses in LA-NPC23–26. A retrospective study27 and two single-arm phase II trials suggest that adding nimotuzumab to CCRT after IC is effective, especially for patients with poor IC responses28,29. However, all related results supporting nimotuzumab plus CCRT come from retrospective or single-arm studies with small sample sizes and unselected patients, and its superiority in LA-NPC with unfavorable response to IC has not been validated in randomized trials. For these high-risk patients (detectable EBV DNA and/or SD/PD after IC), adding nimotuzumab to CCRT to increase the treatment intensity may improve locoregional and/or distant control.

Here, we report a phase 2 randomized controlled trial to compare CCRT plus nimotuzumab with CCRT alone, hypothesizing that nimotuzumab could improve the primary endpoint of 2-year progression-free survival in LA-NPC with suboptimal response to IC.

Results

Patients and tumor characteristics

Between 31 January 2020 and 8 February 2021, 510 patients with stage II–IVA disease were screened for eligibility, of whom two (0.4%) refused to participate, one (0.2%) had insufficient hematological function, one (0.2%) had insufficient renal function, seven (1.4%) had a negative EGFR expression, 309 (61.9% of 499) patients achieved CR/PR, 190 (38.1%) achieved SD, 253 (50.7%) achieved both CR/PR and had undetectable EBV DNA levels, and 246 (49.3%) had detectable plasma EBV DNA levels and/or SD after two cycles of IC. A total of 246 eligible patients were enrolled (from 31 January 2020 to 12 March 2021) and randomly assigned to the CCRT plus nimotuzumab group (n = 121) or the CCRT group (n = 125; intention-to-treat population; Fig. 1). The median age was 45 years (interquartile range [IQR], 21–70 years), with 175 (71.1%) males and 71 (28.9%) females. Overall, 32 (26.4%) of 121 patients in the CCRT plus nimotuzumab group and 33 (26.4%) of 125 patients in the CCRT group showed a strongly positive EGFR expression. Of the 121 patients in the CCRT plus nimotuzumab group, 64 (52.9%) had detectable EBV DNA levels after IC compared with 60 (48.0%) of 125 patients in the CCRT group. Additionally, 93 (76.9%) patients in the CCRT plus nimotuzumab group and 97 (77.6%) in the CCRT group achieved SD after IC. Furthermore, 35 (28.9%) and 30 (24.0%) patients in the CCRT plus nimotuzumab and CCRT groups, respectively, had detectable EBV DNA levels and SD after IC. Baseline characteristics were well-balanced between the groups (Table 1). Efficacy

In the intent-to-treat population, as of data cutoff (31 August, 2024; median follow-up 47 months [IQR 44–50]), the primary endpoint was not met. The 2-year PFS rates were 81.0% (90% confidence interval [CI], 74.3–86.1) and 80.8% (90% CI, 74.2–85.7) in the CCRT plus nimotuzumab and CCRT group, respectively (stratified HR, 0.93 [90% CI, 0.63–1.37]; log-rank p = 0.70; Fig. 2). The results were consistent in the per-protocol analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1). Subgroup analyses were consistent with the primary outcome. As of the data cutoff, the 2-year overall survival (OS) rates were 99.2% (95% CI, 94.3–99.6) in the nimotuzumab group and 97.6% (92.7–99.2) in the CCRT alone group (stratified HR, 0.67 [95% CI, 0.29–1.57]; p = 0.40). The 2-year cumulative incidence of distant metastasis was 11.3% (95% CI, 6.6–19.5) vs 12.8% (95% CI, 7.7–21.2) for the nimotuzumab and CCRT alone groups, respectively (HR 0.83 [95% CI, 0.44–1.57]; p = 0.56). For locoregional relapse, the rates were 11.4% (95% CI, 6.6–19.6) vs 7.6% (95% CI, 3.9–14.6), respectively (HR 1.05 [95% CI, 0.55–2.01]; p = 0.89; Fig. 2). Additional data on survival outcomes, response rates, post-relapse treatments, treatment compliance, dose modifications and cycles of each treatment received by group details are available in Fig. 1 and the Supplementary Tables 2–5.

Dose reductions during treatment

Dose reductions were reported in 20 (16.5%) of 121 patients in the CCRT plus nimotuzumab group and 22 (17.6%) of 125 patients in the CCRT group during the concurrent phase. Notably, 42 (34.7%) and 43 (34.4%) patients in the CCRT plus nimotuzumab and CCRT groups, respectively, discontinued treatment most frequently due to patient refusal (22/121 [18.2%] vs. 25/125 [20.0%]) and adverse events (18 [14.9%] vs. 17 [13.6%]). The most frequent adverse events leading to discontinuation were leukopenia (12 [9.9%] vs. 10 [8.0%]) and thrombocytopenia (4 [3.3%] vs. 5 [4.0%]). Patient refusal (12 [9.9%]), adverse events (5 [4.1%]), and the coronavirus 2019 pandemic (2 [1.7%]) were the reasons for nimotuzumab discontinuation.

Adverse events

In total, 241 patients were included in the safety analysis. Grade 3 or worse adverse events occurred in 56.3% of patients in the CCRT plus nimotuzumab group and 51.6% in the CCRT group. The most common grade 3–4 events were mucositis (20.2 vs. 18.0%), leukopenia (19.3 vs. 17.2%), vomiting (11.8 vs. 12.3%), nausea (11.8 vs. 13.1%), and neutropenia (6.7 vs. 9.8%; Table 2). Grade 1–2 rash was more frequent with nimotuzumab (12.6 vs. 4.9%). Late adverse events were similar, with similar instances of dry mouth (61.3 vs 61.4%) and hearing impairment (26.9 vs. 26.2%). Grade 3 late events occurred in 10.9% and 8.2% of patients, respectively. No treatment-related deaths occurred. Discussion

This randomized phase 2 controlled trial evaluated the addition of nimotuzumab to CCRT in high-risk LA-NPC patients with a suboptimal response to IC. The results showed no significant survival benefit, with 2-year PFS rates of 81.0% in the nimotuzumab arm versus 80.8% in the CCRT alone arm.

IC plus CCRT is the standard treatment for LA-NPC30. However, not all patients benefit from IC. A phase 3 trial showed that patients with SD/ PD (HR, 12.83) after induction gemcitabine and cisplatin (GP) had significantly higher mortality risks than CR/PR responders (HR, 3.53)31. Our previous studies indicate that patients with detectable EBV DNA and/or SD/PD after IC face a higher risk of progression and need more intensive treatment5. The present study focused exclusively on this population with a suboptimal IC response, which explains why the 3-year PFS in our control arm (72.8%) was lower than that reported in pivotal trials enrolling all-comers (80–85.3%)1,2. Our findings underscore that these patients represent a distinct, high-risk group with inherent treatment insensitivity1,2. TPF and GP are both NCCN-recommended IC regimens (category 1 evidence) for EBV-associated LA-NPC. A 2023 meta-analysis comparing taxane-based (TPF), GP, and PF regimens demonstrated that taxane-based IC followed by CCRT was associated with the highest progression-free survival and overall survival in LA-NPC. This analysis included data from 8,214 patients, supporting taxane-based regimens as a top-tier therapeutic option32. To date, there is no consensus on the optimal number of IC cycles. Although three-cycle ICs are more commonly used, 2 or 3 cycles are both recommended by the CSCO and ASCO guideline3. A two-cycle approach mitigates the substantial toxicity of three-cycle regimens and, critically, allows for early identification of patients with a suboptimal response1,2,33. Although our SD/PD rate was higher than in three-cycle studies (~10%)2, it is comparable to other two-cycle cohorts (20–30%)5,34,35. More importantly, our prior research showed that for poor responders, additional IC cycles were detrimental compared to a direct transition to radiotherapy36. The EP-SEASON study further provides evidence in support of early transition to radiotherapy for patients who achieve a suboptimal response with detectable EBV DNA to IC after two cycles37. Accordingly, a two-cycle induction strategy was adopted to limit cumulative chemotherapy-related toxicities and enable early identification of suboptimal responders, thereby allowing timely initiation of definitive radiotherapy rather than prolonging potentially ineffective additional induction chemotherapy. Consistent with this rationale, our recent phase III trial comparing IC + CCRT with CCRT + AC showed a higher locoregional relapse rate in the IC + CCRT group (three cycles of TPF IC followed by CCRT) than in the CCRT + AC group (HR 4.34)38. These findings indirectly suggest that, in a subset of patients with intrinsic resistance to induction chemotherapy, prolonged IC may delay the initiation of radiotherapy, potentially promoting accelerated tumor cell repopulation39–41 and increasing the risk of locoregional recurrence. For such patients, early initiation of radiotherapy may therefore be critical. Therefore, we infer that tumor heterogeneity is the main factor determining patients’ intrinsic sensitivity to treatment, rather than specific chemotherapy regimens, and that various platinum-based IC regimens can be used to screen for these high-risk patients.

EGFR is commonly overexpressed in NPC and HNSCC, making anti-EGFR antibodies an effective targeted therapy, especially when combined with chemotherapy or radiotherapy11,42–47. While anti-EGFR monotherapy has shown modest activity, it is more effective when combined with cytotoxic chemotherapy19. In RM-HNSCC, the addition of an anti-EGFR antibody to chemotherapy, such as cisplatin–fluorouracil or cisplatin–docetaxel, has been shown to improve survival outcomes compared with chemotherapy alone10,11. When added to the aforementioned regimens or carboplatin, anti-EGFR antibodies have also demonstrated favorable outcomes in recurrent and metastatic NPC12,22,48. For LA-NPC, adding nimotuzumab to CCRT after IC has shown promising efficacy and safety in retrospective studies23,24, and a single-arm phase 2 trial reporting an 83.5% 2-year PFS28. A phase 3 trial comparing CCRT with low-dose cisplatin (30 mg/m2 per week) plus nimotuzumab with CCRT alone in LA-HNSCC also showed that CCRT plus nimotuzumab improved PFS and locoregional control 21.

However, in the present randomized controlled trial, it was disappointing to find that adding nimotuzumab to CCRT had no significant effect on all endpoints. Similar results were observed in trials with cetuximab (RTOG 0522) and panitumumab (CONCERT-1) in LA-HNSCC15,16. These negative outcomes were likely caused by poor treatment compliance. For instance, in the RTOG 0522 trial, adding cetuximab led to radiotherapy interruptions (26.9%) and incomplete dosing (26.4%). Similarly, in the CONCERT-1 study, panitumumab-related toxicities forced a ~25% reduction in the cisplatin dose, undermining the regimen’s efficacy.

To avoid RT interruption and cisplatin dose reduction caused by the additional toxicity of anti-EGFR antibodies, we chose nimotuzumab plus chemoradiotherapy as the experimental arm. Nimotuzumab, a humanized antibody with a unique safety profile, has shown good tolerability when combined with chemotherapy in prior studies19,21. In our study, the compliance with CCRT was not negatively affected by nimotuzumab, with 95.0% of patients receiving ≥200 mg/m2 of cisplatin, which is comparable to that of 93.6% of patients who received CCRT alone. However, a non-significant trend towards a benefit with nimotuzumab was observed in female patients, those with strong EGFR expression, and the small subgroup of patients with both detectable EBV DNA and SD after IC. However, the wide CI crossing 1 indicates that the clinical benefit remains uncertain.

Several factors may explain negative outcome. First, the high proportion of patients receiving a cumulative cisplatin dose exceeding 200 mg/m² in our control arm (93.6%)—notably higher than in the HNSCC trial (78.2%)21—may have maximized the efficacy of CCRT, potentially masking an additional benefit from nimotuzumab. Second, platinum and nimotuzumab share similar mechanisms in inhibiting the repair of radiation-induced DNA damage, thereby enhancing RT sensitivity. While synergistic in unselected populations, this effect may be diminished in a cohort specifically selected for a suboptimal response to IC. Tumors showing a poor response to TPF-based chemotherapy likely harbor clones with reduced sensitivity to platinum. Given the convergent mechanisms, they may also respond poorly to nimotuzumab, negating any added benefit. This hypothesis is supported by a Hong Kong study in a platinum-insensitive population, where cetuximab plus carboplatin yielded a low objective response rate (12%), suggesting that overcoming this state with EGFR inhibitors is challenging48. Future strategies should focus on identifying novel drugs with distinct mechanisms, exploring new targets in resistant patients, and combining these with immunotherapy to enhance the IC regimen. Additionally, using these drugs during the concurrent or adjuvant phases (NCT05201859) should be explored to overcome resistance.

In terms of toxicity, the most common grade 3–4 acute toxicities were leukopenia, mucositis, nausea, and vomiting in both groups. Consistent with the results of previous studies, no serious adverse events were observed in the CCRT plus nimotuzumab group22. However, the incidence of grade 1–2 adverse events, including mucositis and skin rash, was higher in the CCRT plus nimotuzumab group than in the CCRT alone group, although they were generally manageable.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, all patients enrolled in the study were from a single center in an endemic region; therefore, whether the findings can be applied to non-endemic regions remains to be validated. Second, whether the combination of CCRT and nimotuzumab is effective in keratinising NPC remains to be determined. Third, three cycles of IC may be more aligned with routine clinical practice. Fourth, the GP induction regimen has emerged as the global reference standard following the demonstration of a survival benefit by Zhang et al.1. However, this trial was initiated before the widespread availability of GP for the treatment of LA-NPC. Additionally, the global standardization of plasma EBV DNA measurements is still needed, as this could affect the variability in the viral DNA copy numbers detected.

In summary, this trial demonstrated that adding nimotuzumab to CCRT did not significantly improve outcomes for patients with LA-NPC who had a suboptimal response to IC. Further investigation into predictive biomarkers and novel therapeutic combinations is warranted to optimize outcomes for this high-risk population.

Methods

Study design and participants

This open-label, parallel-group, randomized phase 2 trial was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Centre and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) guidelines were followed, and the trial was completed. Eligible male or female patients had histologically confirmed, non-keratinising, EGFR-positive (protocol in Supplementary Materials) stage II–IVA NPC according to the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer classification system. The key inclusion criterion was a suboptimal response to two cycles of IC, defined as either detectable plasma EBV DNA levels and/or a radiological response of SD/PD. Other criteria included an age of 18–70 years, an ECOG performance status of 0–1, and adequate organ function. Patients with histologically confirmed WHO type I tumors, those who received treatment with palliative intent, those with a previous malignancy (except adequately treated basal cell or squamous cell skin cancer) or in situ cervical cancer, or those with a severe coexisting illness, and lactating or pregnant patients were excluded. The complete inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in the protocol (Supplementary Materials). Sex and age were documented from medical records. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Pretreatment assessments

Pretreatment assessments included patient history, physical examination, nasopharyngeal endoscopy, histopathological diagnosis, hematology and biochemistry profiles, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT) of the nasopharynx and neck (CT for MRI contraindication), chest and abdomen CT, skeletal scintigraphy or whole body 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT; preferred), and plasma EBV DNA load tested via quantitative polymerase chain reaction.

Plasma EBV DNA Assay

Extraction from plasma samples

Samples of peripheral blood (5 mL) were collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tubes from all participants and centrifuged at ×1600 × g for isolation of plasma. Plasma samples were carefully transferred into plain polypropylene tubes and stored at −80 °C until further processing. DNA from the plasma samples was extracted with the QIAamp Blood Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) using the blood and body fluid protocol, as recommended by the manufacturer. A total of 500–1000 µL of each plasma sample was used for DNA extraction per column, with a final elution volume of 50 µL from the extraction column. Exact amounts were documented to calculate the target DNA concentration. Real-time quantitative EBV DNA polymerase chain reaction (PCR): The real-time quantitative PCR system was developed for EBV DNA detection toward the BamHI-W region. The system consisted of amplification primers W-44F (5′-AGT CTC TGC CTC CAG GCA-3′) and W-119R (5′-ACA GAG GGC CTG TCC ACC G-3′) and the dual-labeled fluorescent probe W-67T (5′-[FAM] CAC TGT CTG TAA AGT CCA GCC TCC [TAMRA]-3′). In this study, real-time quantitative PCR for the β-actin gene was used as a control due to the amplifiability of plasma DNA. The β-actin gene primer sequence was forward, 5′-ACA GGC ACCA GGG CGT GA TGG-3; and reverse, 5′-CTC CAT GTC GTC CCA GTT GGT-3′; the dual-labeled fluorescent probe sequence was (5′-[FAM] CAT CCT CAC CCT GAA GTA CCC CAT C [TAMRA]-3′) 49–51.

Randomization and masking

Randomization was performed at the Clinical Trials Centre of SYSUCC (Guangzhou, China). After two cycles of IC, participants with detectable EBV DNA levels and/or SD/PD responses were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive either CCRT plus nimotuzumab or CCRT alone. Randomization was performed using a computerized random list generator and block randomization, with a block size of six (known only to the statistician Q Liu) and stratified by the overall stage (II, III, or IVA). After completing all screening procedures, the investigators contacted the study coordinator (PW) to obtain the treatment assignment. The statistician and study coordinator had no clinical involvement in the remainder of the trial. The patients and investigators were not blinded to the treatment group assignment.

Procedures

Pretreatment assessments were detailed in the Supplementary Materials. The TPF (taxane-PF) regimen was selected for IC in this study. This choice is supported by its category 1 recommendation in the NCCN guidelines for EBV-associated NPC and further corroborated by a network meta-analysis that identified taxane-based IC as the most effective strategy32. Eligible patients received two cycles of IC with a taxane-PF (TPF) regimen (paclitaxel liposome [delivered without corticosteroids] 135 mg/m2 intravenously on days 1 and 22, cisplatin 25 mg/m2 intravenously on days 1–3 and 22–24, and fluorouracil 3.75 g/m2 via continuous intravenous infusion for 120 h on days 1–5 and 22–26) every 3 weeks52,53. A two-cycle duration was chosen, in line with CSCO and ASCO guidelines3, to reduce cumulative chemotherapy-related toxicities and facilitate an early tumor response assessment33, thereby enabling a timely transition to subsequent RT without delay for patients with a suboptimal response36.

After IC, response was assessed via MRI/CT, and plasma EBV DNA testing was performed 7 days after completion of the second cycle of IC. Patients with undetectable EBV DNA and a complete response (CR) or partial response (PR) received standard CCRT. Patients with detectable EBV DNA and/or SD/PD were randomly assigned to receive either CCRT plus nimotuzumab or CCRT alone. Cisplatin 100 mg/m2 was intravenously administered every 3 weeks on days 1, 22, and 43 for three concurrent cycles with RT in both groups. Nimotuzumab was intravenously administered on day 1 of RT and then weekly in the CCRT plus nimotuzumab group as a 200-mg flat dose mixed in 250-mL normal saline over a period of 60 min without premedication. Details of the drug dose adjustment plan and the management of toxicities are shown in the Supplementary Materials. All patients underwent intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) following a previous study54,55. Details of the RT plan are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Treatment responses were assessed using Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors, version 1.1, with follow-up every 3 months for the first 3 years and every 6 months thereafter until death (Supplementary Table S2). Acute toxic effects were assessed by the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0. Late radiotherapy toxicities were scored by the Late Radiation Morbidity Scoring Scheme of the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (Supplementary Materials).

Study end points

The primary endpoint was the 2-year PFS, which was defined as the time from randomization to a documented local or regional relapse, distant metastasis, or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Secondary endpoints included overall survival (OS), distant metastasis–free survival (DMFS), locoregional relapse-free survival (LRRFS), short-term response rate (assessed at 16 weeks with RECIST, version 1.1), and safety. The definition and methods used to assess these endpoints are described in the protocol (Supplementary Table 7).

Dose modification of chemotherapy and nimotuzumab

The National Cancer Institute Common Toxicity Criteria, version 5.0, were used to assess chemotherapy and acute radiation toxicity. Chemotherapy dose adjustments were allowed for subjective/objective evidence of hematological or non-hematological toxicity during treatment. In case of hematological toxicity, chemotherapy was withheld until the nadir values were 1500 cells per μL or higher for the absolute neutrophil count and 100,000 per μL or higher for the platelet count. For renal toxicity, chemotherapy was withheld until creatinine clearance was higher than or equal to 60 mL/min.

In patients with grade 3 or 4 hematological, grade 3 or 4 non-hematological, or other protocol-specific toxic effects, cisplatin treatments were interrupted. Chemotherapy must not be administered until prechemotherapy toxicity is reduced to grade 1 or eliminated (except for hair loss, fatigue, and nail changes). Chemotherapy should be terminated if chemo-induced treatment delay exceeds 2 weeks. Dose modifications were intended to be permanent for hematological and non-hematological toxicities (i.e., if a patient’s cisplatin dose was reduced to 80 mg/m², it remained at the reduced dose for the duration of their treatment) and were based on the nadir blood counts and interim toxicities of the preceding cycle.

Dose modifications during concurrent chemotherapy

Cisplatin was decreased to 80 mg/m² if the absolute neutrophil count was 500–1000 cells per μL, platelet count was 50,000–75,000 per μL, or creatinine clearance was 40–60 mL/min. Cisplatin was decreased to 60 mg/m² if the absolute neutrophil count was less than 500 cells per μL or the platelet count less than 50,000 per μL.

Dose modifications during induction chemotherapy

Reductions in the dose of paclitaxel liposome were planned for neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, impaired liver function, severe diarrhea, or mucositis. Paclitaxel liposome decreases to 110 mg/m2 if patients occurred neurotoxicity of grade 3, the absolute neutrophil count was 500–1000 cells per μL, platelet count was 50,000–75,000 per μL, or creatinine clearance was 40–60 mL/min, or more than 2.5–5.0 times the upper limit of normal of aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase and alkaline phosphatase, or first episode of grade 4 diarrhea, or second episode of grade 3 diarrhea, or grade 4 mucositis. Paclitaxel liposome was decreased to 75 mg/m2 if the patients occurred grade 4 neutrophil decrease, grade 3 platelet decrease, or creatinine clearance was less than 40 mL/min, or more than 5.0 times the upper limit of normal of aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, and alkaline phosphatase. Dose modifications of cisplatin were planned for neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, nephrotoxicity, or neurotoxicity. Cisplatin was decreased to 60 mg/m2 if patients had neurotoxicity of grade 3, the absolute neutrophil count was 500–1000 cells per μL, platelet count was 50,000–75,000 per μL, or creatinine clearance was 40–60 mL/min or grade 4 mucositis. Cisplatin was decreased to 45 mg/m2 if the absolute neutrophil count was 0–500 cells per μL, platelet count was less than 50,000 per μL. Fluorouracil was decreased to 3000 mg/m2 if the absolute neutrophil count was 500–1000 cells per μL, platelet count was 50,000–75,000 per μL, grade 4 mucositis, first episode of grade 4 diarrhea, or second episode of grade 3 diarrhea. Fluorouracil was decreased to 2200 mg/m2 if the absolute neutrophil count was 0–500 cells per μL, platelet count was less than 50,000 per μL.

Dose modifications of nimotuzumab

Dose modifications of nimotuzumab were not permitted but could be interrupted or delayed for a maximum of 2 weeks during the CCRT phase.

Description of the guidelines for intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) plan

All the patients were immobilized in the supine position using head, neck, and shoulder thermoplastic masks. Two sets of images with and without contrast were obtained from the CT simulator for treatment planning. All patients were scanned using serial 3-mm slices from the vertex through the clavicles. Inverse IMRT planning was performed using the Corvus system (version 3.0; Peacock, Nomos, Deer Park, IL, USA), and a MiMi multileaf collimator (Nomos, Sewickly, PA, USA) was used for planning and treatment.

The patients in both groups were irradiated using intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT). The primary and nodal gross tumor volumes (GTV-nx and GTV-nd, respectively) included all gross diseases of the primary tumor volume, enlarged retropharyngeal nodes, and cervical lymph nodes visualized on CT and/or MRI. The delineation of the GTV was based on the pre-induction tumor extent. The high-risk clinical target volume (CTV1) was defined as the gross tumor volume of the nasopharynx plus a 5–10 mm margin (2–3 mm posteriorly if adjacent to the brainstem or spinal cord) to encompass the high-risk sites of microscopic extension and the entire nasopharynx. The low-risk clinical target volume (CTV2) was defined as the high-risk clinical target volume plus a 5–10 mm margin (2–3 mm posteriorly if adjacent to the brainstem or spinal cord) to encompass the low-risk sites of microscopic extension, including the skull base, clivus, sphenoid sinus, parapharyngeal space, pterygoid fossae, posterior parts of the nasal cavity, pterygopalatine fossae, retropharyngeal nodal regions, and elective neck area from level IB to level V. The planning target volume (PTV) was created based on each volume with an additional 3-mm margin to allow for setup variability. Critical normal structures, including the brainstem, spinal cord, parotid glands, optic nerves, chiasm, lens, eyeballs, temporal lobes, temporomandibular joints, mandible, and hypophysis, were contoured and set as OARs during optimization.

Dose–volume histograms of the treatment targets and critical normal structures were evaluated. The prescribed doses were 70 Gy to the PTV of the GTVnx (PTVnx), 60 Gy to PTV1, 54 Gy to PTV2, and 70 Gy to the PTV of the GTVnd (PTVnd) in 33 fractions. For the GTV and clinical target volume (CTV), the target volumes receiving 95% of the prescribed dose were used to reflect target coverage, and the maximal, minimal, and mean doses delivered to the target volumes were also calculated. For critical organs with functional subunits organized in series, such as the brainstem, optic chiasm, and optic nerves, a dose of 5% of the volume was examined. The dose constraints for critical organs with functional subunits organized in parallel are listed in Supplementary Table 8. Dose distribution was also examined slice-by-slice on the CT images.

Tumor relapse assessment

Tumor relapse was assessed via MRI or CT of the nasopharynx and neck, nasopharyngoscopy, chest radiography or CT, abdominal sonography or CT, skeletal scintigraphy, plasma EBV DNA load, and ¹⁸F-FDG PET/CT in patients with detectable plasma EBV DNA levels or those with a suspicion of locoregional disease or distant metastasis. When necessary, locoregional or distant relapse was confirmed by fine-needle aspiration or biopsy. All endpoints, including safety, were assessed and confirmed by the attending physician. After the completion of the assigned treatment, further anticancer treatments (e.g., chemotherapy, re-irradiation, surgery, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy) were determined at the physician’s discretion for patients with persistent disease or documented relapses, whenever possible.

Statistical analysis

This study was designed to evaluate whether nimotuzumab plus CCRT improves PFS compared with CCRT alone. The study was designed with 92% power to detect a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.49 for PFS with a two-sided significance level of 0.1056. This calculation assumed an improvement in 2-year PFS from 74.2% in the CCRT group5 to 86.2% in the nimotuzumab group. To achieve the required 60 events, and accounting for a 10% dropout rate, a total enrollment of 238 patients (119 per group) was planned.

Initially, the trial was designed with 80% power (which required 39 events and 150 patients), which increased to 92% in December 2020. The enrollment rate was high (150 patients were enrolled from 31 January 2020 to 12 November 2020), and after being evaluated by statisticians (Prof. Q Liu and JB Li), it was recommended to continue the enrollment, and the updated protocol was submitted for ethical approval; therefore, a power increase was authorized without extending the accrual period. The sample size was calculated using Power Analysis and Sample Size software (version 15; NCSS, Kaysville, UT, USA).

All efficacy analyses were performed using the intention-to-treat population (all randomized patients). In a prespecified analysis, PFS was also analyzed in the per-protocol population, which included patients who did not violate the eligibility criteria and who started randomly assigned treatments (completed 2–3 cycles of concurrent cisplatin [≥200 mg/m2] chemotherapy in both groups and 6–7 cycles of nimotuzumab in the CCRT plus nimotuzumab group). Only patients who received at least one dose of the protocol-specific treatment were included in the safety analysis for adverse events. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical variables between the two groups, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables. Kaplan–Meier curves (with no informative censoring) were used to present all time-to-event data, and the two groups were compared using log-rank tests stratified according to the overall stage (II vs. III vs. IVA). Under the proportional risk assumption confirmed by the Schoenfeld residual57, HRs were calculated using a Cox proportional risk model to estimate the efficacy of the experimental treatment stratified by overall stage. The cumulative incidences of locoregional relapse and distant metastasis were presented using Nelson–Aalen cumulative risk curves and estimated using the Fine-Gray subhazards model, with deaths regarded as competing events for locoregional and distant metastasis failure58. Missing time-to-event data (due to loss to follow-up or no events observed at the time of the predefined primary analysis) were censored. Right-censored data were used for survivorship estimates, and the follow-up duration for patients lost to follow-up was defined as the date from randomization to the date of the last follow-up visit59. Interaction analysis for the primary outcome was performed to explore whether the effect of experimental treatment varied in the subgroups defined according to sex (men vs. women), age (<45 years vs ≥45 years), ECOG performance status (0 vs. 1), overall stage (II vs. III vs. IVA), T stage (T1–2 vs. T3 vs. T4), N stage (N0-1 vs N2 vs N3), pretreatment EBV DNA test (<2000 vs. ≥2000 copies per mL), EGFR expression (positive vs. strong positive), EBV DNA test after IC (undetectable vs. detectable), response to IC (CR/PR vs. SD/PD), and combined response to IC (detectable EBV DNA levels or SD vs. detectable EBV DNA levels and SD after IC). An interaction analysis was conducted using a treatment-by-covariate interaction test based on the Cox proportional hazards model60. All analyses were performed using SPSS (version 24.0; SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) and STATA (version 18.0; Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA). All statistical tests were two-sided; p < 0.10 was considered significant for the primary endpoint, and p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance for all the other tests. This study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov, number NCT04223024.
