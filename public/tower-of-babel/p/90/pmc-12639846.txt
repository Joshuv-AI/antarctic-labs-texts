Synthesis of pH-sensitive hydrogel beads for controlled delivery of ketorolac tromethamine: computational evaluation and in vivo pharmacokinetic study
Authors: Hamid Ullah, I-Hui Chiu, Rahime Eshaghi Malekshah, Muhammad Suhail, Pao-Chu Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The current study aimed to develop pH-responsive hydrogel beads based on sodium alginate (SA) and acrylic acid (Aa), crosslinked in the presence of calcium chloride, for the controlled delivery of ketorolac tromethamine. The hydrogel beads were fabricated using the ionic gelation technique and subsequently characterized for their structural properties, surface morphology, and thermal stability. Additional investigations, including sol-gel analysis, drug loading efficiency, and drug quantification, were conducted to evaluate the physicochemical attributes of the prepared beads. Furthermore, Monte Carlo simulations using the adsorption locator module in Materials Studio were conducted to investigate the adsorption behavior of ketorolac on the SA/Aa hydrogel beads. The negative adsorption energy obtained from the simulations suggested that the interaction between the drug and polymer was both spontaneous and exothermic, indicating a thermodynamically favorable binding mechanism. The pH responsiveness of the beads was evaluated through swelling and drug release studies under different pH conditions (pH 1.2 and 7.4). The results indicated markedly significant swelling and drug release at pH 7.4 as compared to 1.2, thereby confirming the pH-responsive behavior of the formulated beads. The toxicity of the hydrogel beads was evaluated using the Hen's Egg Test on Chorioallantoic Membrane (HET-CAM), which revealed no signs of irritation or toxicity. An in vivo pharmacokinetic study in rabbits further demonstrated that the drug-loaded hydrogel beads achieved higher plasma concentrations of ketorolac tromethamine compared to the drug solution, supporting their potential for improved bioavailability. Hence, the developed SA/Aa-based hydrogel beads exhibited favorable physicochemical, biocompatibility, and pharmacokinetic profiles, making them promising candidates for controlled drug delivery systems.

Introduction

Polymers hold a significant position in the pharmaceutical industry due to their remarkable ability to encapsulate and deliver therapeutic agents to different regions of the body. The importance of employing a polymer is to delay drug dissolution and to minimize the exposure rate of drug molecules in the gastrointestinal tract. (Suhail et al., 2022b). Hydrogels represent an important category of polymeric materials due to their favorable properties for potential use in biomedical, pharmaceutical, and environmental applications. They are three-dimensional, hydrophilic polymer networks capable of absorbing substantial amounts of water. In recent years, hydrogels have been engineered with additional functionalities, such as the capacity to swell or contract in response to external stimuli, and are termed ‘smart’ hydrogels. Among these, the most widely investigated are those responsive to variations in environmental conditions, commonly referred to as environmentally sensitive hydrogels (Yin et al., 2018). Stimuli-responsive hydrogels constitute a distinct category of hydrogels that play a crucial role in drug delivery by reacting to specific stimuli. Among these, pH-responsive hydrogels are the most investigated and applied. The pH of the surrounding environment influences their swelling and deswelling behavior. Such hydrogels incorporate acidic or basic functional groups within their polymeric structure that ionize at specific pH values. Hydrogels containing acidic groups swell when the pH exceeds their acid dissociation constant, whereas those with alkaline groups swell when the pH drops below their base dissociation constant. Due to these characteristics, pH-responsive hydrogels are extensively employed in biomedical and biotechnological applications, particularly in controlled drug delivery systems (Singh and Nayak, 2023). Consequently, numerous researchers have designed various pH-sensitive hydrogels for controlled drug delivery applications. For instance, Neethu Das and Govind Raj (2024) developed chitosan-based graphene oxide‑sodium alginate hydrogel beads for the controlled release of amoxicillin. (Neethu Das and Govind Raj, 2024). Similarly, Jing et al. (2024) prepared pH-sensitive Zingiber officinale polysaccharide-based hydrogel beads for insulin delivery to the colon (Jing et al., 2024).

Naturally occurring polymer-based hydrogels are highly desirable due to their non-toxicity, biodegradability, biocompatibility, and broad availability (Zhu et al., 2021). Sodium alginate (SA), a naturally occurring anionic polysaccharide, is widely accepted for its effectiveness in encapsulating bioactive compounds, attributed to its biodegradability, non-toxicity, water solubility, and excellent biocompatibility. Therefore, it has been widely used as a pH-sensitive hydrogel in drug delivery systems (Wang et al., 2023). The main property of SA is its capability to form a three-dimensional gel network, often described by the “egg-box” model, upon interaction with divalent cations such as calcium (Ca2+), barium (Ba2+), or strontium (Sr2+). This gelation mechanism, which is central to microencapsulation in drug delivery systems, involves electrostatic connections between the carboxylate groups (COO−) of SA and the cations charge group present in the cross-linking medium (Touzout et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2017). Previously, sodium alginate has been used with the carboxymethyl chitosan for the controlled release of lactoferrin (Jing et al., 2022).

Acrylic acid (Aa) is a synthetic monomer that exhibits strong pH-responsive behavior, significantly affected by the surrounding environmental pH. The presence of carboxylic acid functional groups imparts this pH sensitivity, allowing Aa to respond to variations in pH (Al-Tabakha et al., 2021). At low pH values, these carboxylic groups remain protonated, whereas at higher pH values, they become deprotonated, thereby enabling the pH-responsive characteristics of Aa. Khalid and co-workers used Aa as a monomer with sodium alginate to synthesize a hydrogel for controlled delivery of loxoprofen sodium (Khalid et al., 2016). Density functional theory (DFT) and Monte Carlo (MC) simulations are powerful computational approaches for investigating molecular-level interactions between drug molecules and polymer matrices. DFT provides detailed understandings of the electronic structure, binding energies, and preferred conformations of interacting species, enabling the prediction of stable interaction sites and the nature of chemical bonding. In parallel, MC simulations facilitate the exploration of adsorption phenomena and thermodynamically favorable configurations by statistically sampling molecular orientations and positions within the polymeric network. Together, these techniques offer a complementary framework for elucidating the physicochemical interactions governing drug–polymer compatibility, stability, and loading efficiency, thereby guiding the rational design of advanced drug delivery systems (Ighnih et al., 2024).

Ketorolac tromethamine (KT) is one of the non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). It possesses a broad spectrum of therapeutic activities, including pain and fever management, inhibition of postoperative ocular inflammation, and reduction of conjunctivitis without affecting corneal opacity; however, it has been associated with a potential increase in intraocular pressure. KT functions as a nonselective cyclooxygenase (COX) inhibitor and is commercially available as a racemic mixture. It can be administered through oral, intravenous, intramuscular, or topical ophthalmic routes. Marketed formulations include tablets (10 mg), topical ophthalmic solutions (0.4–0.5 % w/v), and injectable solutions (15–30 mg/mL), with an elimination half-life of approximately 5.5 h (El Moussaoui et al., 2021; Silva et al., 2023). Consequently, the relatively short half-life of KT needs frequent dosing to maintain therapeutic plasma concentrations. However, multiple daily administrations are associated with adverse effects, including acute renal failure and gastrointestinal ulceration, and can significantly reduce patient compliance. To overcome the limitations associated with frequent dosing and to enhance patient adherence, the development of a polymer-based delivery system is needed to control KT release. Hydrogels represent an ideal polymeric platform for the controlled delivery of KT due to their pronounced swelling capacity, high drug-loading efficiency, ability to sustain drug release, and low cytotoxicity. In this context, the crosslinking between SA and Aa in the fabricated hydrogel beads effectively prolonged the controlled release of KT over an extended duration (Suhail et al., 2021a). Patil and his co-workers (2020) synthesized chitosan-based nanoparticles encapsulating KT, showing a sustained release profile lasting up to 12 h (Patil et al., 2019). Tuğba Çopur and his co-workers (2025) design PLGA Nanoparticles having KT for Glioblastoma treatment and reported KT release of 90 % within 6 h (Çopur et al., 2025).

The novelty of the present work lies in developing a controlled-release system of hydrogel beads for KT, aiming to mitigate the adverse effects of KT and enhance patient compliance. The pH-responsiveness of the polymeric hydrogel beads was enhanced through the incorporation of a pH-sensitive polymer and monomer, enabling protection of the drug from degradation in the acidic gastric environment while simultaneously shielding the gastric mucosa from the drug's adverse effects. This hydrogel-based delivery system enables the site-specific, controlled release of KT without harming surrounding cells and tissues.

In the present study, the authors developed an SA/Aa hydrogel bead system using the ionic gelation technique for sustained delivery of KT. Further analyzed the synthesized hydrogel beads using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, sol-gel analysis, scanning electron microscope, thermogravimetric analysis, swelling studies, drug loading assessment, in vitro drug release testing, kinetic modelling, HET-CAM toxicity evolution and in vivo pharmacokinetics study. Also, to predict the interactions, energy and adsorption behavior, MC simulation was applied to adsorb the drug molecule on SA/Aa hydrogel beads. Accordingly, evaluations of swelling behavior, drug release kinetics, irritation potential, in vivo performance, and other relevant parameters demonstrated that the developed hydrogel is suitable for the controlled delivery of KT.

Materials and methods

Also, acrylic acid (Aa) (98 % extra pure) was gained from Acros (Carlsbad, CA, New Jersey, USA). Keten was obtained from Everest Pharm., Industrial Co., Ltd. (TourChyan Ind.) Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) ≥ 95 % was obtained from ThermoFisher (Waltham, United States).

Synthesis of SA/Aa hydrogel beads

Hydrogel beads composed of SA and Aa were fabricated using the ionic gelation method. Initially, a predetermined amount of SA was dissolved in distilled water under continuous magnetic stirring to obtain a uniform and homogeneous solution. Subsequently, a specified concentration of Aa, as shown in Table 1, was added dropwise to the SA solution under constant agitation to ensure proper mixing and homogeneity. The resulting polymeric solution was loaded into a 60 mL syringe and introduced dropwise at a controlled rate of 0.5 mL/min into a pre-prepared calcium chloride (CaCl₂) solution under gentle magnetic stirring. The divalent calcium ions in the solution facilitated the ionic crosslinking of SA and Aa, leading to the formation of spherical hydrogel beads. The formulated hydrogel beads were kept in the CaCl₂ solution for some time to achieve a proper morphology. After that, the beads were gently removed from the solution and washed with a 50:50 (v/v) ethanol-distilled water mixture to eliminate any unreacted residual materials. Subsequently, the washed beads were air-dried at ambient temperature for 24 h, then further lyophilized in an oven at 40 °C for 2 days to ensure complete moisture removal before proceeding to characterization. For the preparation of drug-loaded hydrogel beads, 5 % of the drug was first dissolved in deionized-distilled water and incorporated into the SA solution before the addition of Aa. The subsequent steps for bead formation and post-treatment were performed as described above. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR spectroscopy has been used to analyze the molecular interactions and confirm the chemical structure of the unloaded and drug-loaded SA/Aa hydrogel beads. The spectra were analyzed in comparison with the spectra of the individual components, including pure sodium alginate, acrylic acid, and the pure drug. Prior to analysis, all samples were finely ground to an appropriate particle size to ensure uniformity. The FTIR spectra were noted in the 4000–500 cm−1 range using an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory on a NICOLET 380 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Ishioka, Japan) (Yin et al., 2018).

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

The surface of the lyophilized hydrogel beads was examined using a field emission scanning electron microscope (JSM-6330 model from JEOL in Tokyo, Japan). The sample was evenly distributed on a double paste tape affixed to an aluminum stub. Gold has been deposited onto the stub using a gold sputter module in an argon environment. The bead sample underwent scanning at various levels of magnification.

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) and mapping analysis

EDX, also known as EDS, is an elemental analysis technique commonly coupled with Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM). It is employed to determine the elemental composition and distribution within a sample surface or cross-section.

Elemental composition of the optimized hydrogel beads was determined by EDX attached to a field emission scanning electron microscope (JSM-6330, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) operated at 20 kV accelerating voltage. Dried hydrogel beads were mounted on aluminum stubs using double-sided conductive carbon tape and sputter-coated with a thin gold layer (∼10–15 nm) using a Quorum sputter coater to prevent surface charging. The samples were analyzed under high vacuum, and elemental spectra were recorded from selected regions of interest. Both point analysis and elemental mapping were carried out to confirm the presence and uniform distribution of elements such as C, O, Na, Ca, and Cl, corresponding to sodium alginate, acrylic acid, and calcium crosslinking components within the hydrogel matrix (Gholamali and Yadollahi, 2020).

Thermal analysis (TGA)

The TGA test was conducted for SA, KT, loaded, and unloaded hydrogel beads using (Thermal Analyzers Mettler-Toledo, 2-HT) Columbus, Ohio, USA. A small quantity of sample, weighing between 5 and 6 mg, was deposited into an exposed pan linked to a microbalance. Heat flow ranged from 20 to 600 °C and was maintained for all samples with a rising rate of 20 °C/min. Nitrogen gas was utilized continuously during the experiment at a flow rate of 30 mL/min (Suhail et al., 2021c).

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

The thermal behavior of the polymer components, pure KT, and both drug-loaded and unloaded SA/Aa hydrogel beads was evaluated using elevated temperature thermal analysis. Approximately 5 mg of each sample was precisely weighed and transferred into standard aluminum pans. The samples were then analyzed using a controlled thermal program, heating from 50 °C to 400 °C at a uniform rate of 20 °C/min, under a dynamic nitrogen atmosphere with a constant flow rate of 30 mL/min (Ullah et al., 2019b).

Measurement of the swelling behavior

Pre-weighted hydrogel beads were immersed in beakers containing the corresponding buffer solutions and allowed to swell for predetermined time intervals. At each designated time point, the beads were carefully retrieved, gently blotted with the help of soft tissue to remove attached buffer, and immediately weighed. This process was repeated at regular intervals until the hydrogel beads reached a constant weight, indicating the achievement of swelling equilibrium. All measurements were performed in triplicate to certify reproducibility and the statistical consistency of the results (Jing et al., 2022; Suhail et al., 2023a).

Drug quantification

The drug content within the loaded beads was analyzed using an extraction-based approach. Precisely weighted dried hydrogel beads were deeply put in 100 mL of phosphate buffer solution (pH 7.4) and subjected to gentle agitation. At specific time points, an aliquot of the buffer was taken for analysis and put back the same volume of buffer to maintain sink conditions and ensure complete drug extraction. This procedure was continued until no further drug release was observed. The concentration of the extracted drug in each sample was quantified using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer (U-5100, 3 J2 0014, Tokyo, Japan) at 280 nm. Cumulative drug release

To compare release performance and infer relative efficacy potential, dissolution testing was performed for hydrogel beads containing KT and an equivalent dose of KT reference (marketed immediate-release product). Tests were conducted under physiologically relevant pH conditions that simulate gastric and intestinal transit. Results from cumulative release and kinetic modelling were used to compare the release rate and mechanism. A 200 mg load of hydrogel beads was used for the dissolution study. The experiments were carried out using 500 mL of dissolution medium in a USP Dissolution Apparatus II (SR8 Plus Dissolution Test Station, Hanson Research, Chatsworth, CA, USA) maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C with a paddle rotation speed of 50 rpm. At specific time intervals of 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 and 24 h, 5 mL aliquots of the dissolution medium were collected and immediately replenished with an equal volume of fresh medium to maintain sink conditions. The drug concentration in the collected samples was quantified spectrophotometrically at 280 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. All measurements were performed in triplicate to ensure reliability and reproducibility. The release profiles were subsequently fitted to the Korsmeyer-Peppas kinetic model to elucidate the underlying mechanism of KT release from the formulated hydrogel bead matrix (Wang et al., 2023).

Kinetics models

The release mechanism of KT was evaluated using zero-order, first-order, and Higuchi models, with model suitability determined by the correlation coefficient (r2). Additionally, the Korsmeyer–Peppas model was applied to characterize the diffusion mechanism, where the release exponent (n) indicates the mechanism: n < 0.42 denotes Fickian diffusion, 0.42 < n < 0.86 corresponds to anomalous (non-Fickian) transport, and n > 0.86 reflects erosion-controlled release (Costa and Sousa Lobo, 2001).

HET-CAM irritation test

The Hen's Egg Test on the Chorioallantoic Membrane (HET-CAM) assay is a well-established in vitro model extensively utilized for evaluating the anti-irritant potential and any toxic effect, including ocular toxicity of various chemical and biological substances. This assay provides a sensitive and ethically preferable alternative to conventional in vivo methods, enabling the assessment of irritation and inflammatory responses induced by test compounds without the use of live animals (Ozturk et al., 2020).

To evaluate the potential toxicity of the developed hydrogel beads, the Hen's Egg Test on the (HET-CAM) assay was conducted in the present study. A 300 μL aliquot of the medium obtained from the swollen hydrogel beads was applied directly onto the membrane of an egg with a live chicken. The membrane was then monitored at predetermined time intervals for any signs of irritation or damage. The primary parameters assessed included hemorrhage, coagulation, and vasoconstriction. Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) 1 % was used as the positive group to induce irritation, whereas sodium chloride (0.9 %) was used as the negative group to represent non-irritant conditions (Hsieh et al., 2021).

Theoretical assumptions and modelling

DFT-D calculations

The optimization of KT and the SA/Aa hydrogel beads was performed using DFT-D methods as implemented in the DMol3 module of materials studio (Fig. 1) (Ighnih et al., 2024). The calculations were performed employing the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) framework (Ullah et al., 2025). A double numerical basis set with polarization functions (DND) was employed, providing reliable accuracy for systems containing metal surfaces (Salahshoori et al., 2024). An orbital cutoff radius of 3.3 Å was applied, and a Fermi smearing value of 0.005 Ha was used to improve the computational convergence and efficiency.Fig. 1Optimized structures of KT (A) and the SA/Aa hydrogel beads (B) were obtained using DFT-D methods implemented in the DMol3 module of Materials Studio.Fig. 1

Optimized structures of KT (A) and the SA/Aa hydrogel beads (B) were obtained using DFT-D methods implemented in the DMol3 module of Materials Studio.

Monte Carlo calculations (MC)

Theoretical calculations using Monte Carlo (MC) simulations were performed to identify the most constant structural arrangement and evaluate the energetic characteristics of KT adsorbed on the SA/Aa hydrogel beads, using 3:1 and 5:1 M ratios of ketorolac to copolymer. Adsorption energy values (Eads) were calculated to determine the loading efficiency (Karim et al., 2025).

The Universal Force Field (UFF) was applied to model the ketorolac/SA/Aa system. Simulations included 10 annealing cycles and employed the Group-based method with a 12.5 Å cutoff to compute nonbonding interactions, including electrostatic and van der Waals forces (Ighnih et al., 2023).

In-vivo study

Male New Zealand White rabbits (weighing 2.4–3.5 kg) were procured from the Livestock Research Institute, Council of Agriculture, Executive Yuan, Taiwan. The protocol of the study was reviewed and approved by the Kaohsiung Medical University Committee on Animal Use (Approval No. 109008), and all experimental procedures were performed by institutional guidelines and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 8023, revised 1978).

The rabbits were kept under controlled environmental conditions and provided with a standard diet. All rabbits were restricted overnight with free access to water before drug administration. Six rabbits were randomly assigned to two groups, with three animals in each group (n = 3). Group I (control group) received a drug solution (10 mg/kg), Group II (test group) received drug-loaded SA/Aa hydrogel beads (42 mg/kg) equivalent to 10 mg/kg of drug. Oral administration was carried out using a feeding kit, followed by rinsing with 5 mL of distilled water. All animals were identified and housed in individual cages during the study period. Blood samples of 1 mL were collected from the marginal ear vein at pre-set time points. The samples were transferred into heparinized polypropylene tubes and centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min to separate plasma. The collected plasma was stored at controlled temperature conditions until further analysis.

The plasma concentration of KT was quantified using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; HITACHI). Separation was performed on a LiChrospher® 100 RP-18 endcapped column (250 × 4 mm, 5 μm, Merck). The mobile phase, consisting of phosphoric acid (pH 3.0) and acetonitrile (50:50, v/v), was delivered at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. For sample preparation, 200 μL of plasma was mixed with 200 μL of internal standard (ketoprofen in acetonitrile, 400 μg/mL) and vortexed for 1 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 12 min at ambient temperature. KT was detected at 313 nm, with retention times of 5.2 min for KT and 7.2 min for the internal standard.

Statistical analysis

The SPSS Statistics program was used to present the results as the mean ± SD for each experiment. Statistical significance was assessed using the Student's t-test, yielding a p-value of less than 0.05.

Results and discussion

Characteristics of SA/Aa hydrogel beads

Hydrogel beads composed of SA and Aa were synthesized via the ionic gelation method by dropping the polymer mixtures into a calcium chloride (CaCl₂) solution. This method promoted the creation of hydrogen bonding interactions between the polymers and the monomer under acidic conditions, contributing to the structural integrity of the hydrogel network. The addition of the SA and Aa blends to the CaCl₂ solution led to rapid precipitation, forming beads with a uniform spherical form and with a smoothly surface. The newly synthesized SA/Aa hydrogel beads are shown in Fig. 2. The beads displayed a spherical shape and a white color. The transparency of the beads increased when the concentration of SA was increased, signifying an enhanced compactness of the three-dimensional network structure. This phenomenon was consistent with the behavior observed in SA/Aa hydrogel beads cross-linked by calcium ions (Lin et al., 2005). Upon adding SA and its solutions to a CaCl₂ solution, monovalent sodium ions are exchanged for divalent calcium ions, resulting in the formation of SA/Aa hydrogel beads through ionic interactions (Wang et al., 2023b). As shown in Fig. 2, the hydrogel beads exhibited a uniform size of approximately 2–3 mm, which decreased to about 1 mm after drying while maintaining their spherical morphology.Fig. 2Physical appearances of (A) SA/Aa hydrogel beads after washing, (B) Beads size after drying, (C) Beads size after swelling.Fig. 2

Additionally, the peak observed at 860 cm−1 is associated with the vibrational mode of the Na—O bond (Samanta and Ray, 2014). SEM

The SEM of SA/Aa hydrogel beads is presented in Fig. 4. The SA/Aa hydrogel beads exhibit a spherical shape with a rough and wrinkled surface, because of the shrinkage during the drying process. At higher magnification, macro-pores within the gel network were observed, which facilitate water diffusion through the polymer matrix and enhance the rate of swelling. The loose and spongy structure of the hydrogel beads provides an ideal environment for the entrapment of KT molecules. Shifts were observed in both the exothermic and endothermic peaks of pure SA and KT in the crosslinked hydrogel beads. The displacement of these peaks toward higher temperature ranges indicates an enhancement in the thermal stability of the developed SA/Aa hydrogel beads compared to their individual components.

Likewise, the DSC thermogram for KT displayed a first endothermic peak at 86 °C, indicating water loss. These minor variations in the KT peaks suggest the successful incorporation of the KT into the hydrogel bead network. The DSC thermograms of unloaded and drug-loaded SA/Aa hydrogel beads exhibited similar thermal profiles, with only minor variations attributed to the presence of the encapsulated drug.

TGA

The TGA thermograms of SA, KT, and both unloaded and drug-loaded SA/Aa hydrogel beads are illustrated in Fig. 6B. The TGA for pure SA exhibited an initial mass loss of approximately 10 %, which occurred at temperatures below 10 °C, likely due to the evaporation of water. And the SA primary structural degradation, accounting for approximately 40 % of the mass, commenced around 200 °C. This degradation is attributed to the cleavage of the mannuronic acid and glucuronic acid segments. (Sun et al., 2020). As the temperature rose to 360 °C, SA degradation started and continued until complete degradation occurred. The unloaded SA/Aa hydrogel beads can withstand high temperatures. When the temperature rises from 100 °C to 170 °C, 10 % of its weight is lost. Further, when the temperature rises from 170 °C to 310 °C, almost 40 % degradation occurs, and finally, 10 % weight loss is observed from 350 °C to 550 °C. Comparing both results of SA and unloaded hydrogel beads suggests that crosslinking enhanced the thermal stability of pure hydrogel bead components. The TGA analysis for KT shows that only 20 % weight loss occurred within the temperature range 190 °C to 250 °C. A further 60 % degradation of the drug's main structure was observed as the temperature increased from 300 °C to 380 °C. The TGA thermogram of the drug-loaded hydrogel beads exhibited a thermogram similar to that of the unloaded beads, with only minor deviations resulting from the incorporation of the drug. These findings indicate that the grafting and crosslinking of SA and Aa enhanced the thermal stability of the resultant drug delivery system (Suhail et al., 2021b).

Swelling study

Swelling studies were performed to assess the pH responsiveness of the synthesized hydrogel at two distinct pH levels: 1.2 and 7.4, as shown in Fig. 7. The synthesized hydrogel beads exhibited a higher swelling index at pH 7.4 than at pH 1.2, demonstrating the pH-dependent properties of the bead formulation. The hydrogel formulation's functional groups may have been deprotonated, which is why swelling is more pronounced at pH 7.4. Carboxylate (COOH) functional groups were present in the SA and Aa molecular structures. At an acidic pH of 1.2, the carboxylate group of SA and Aa underwent protonation and then, through hydrogen bonding, they form a conjugate with opposite ions. At pH 1.2, there was a little swelling because the hydrogen bond interaction lowered the charge mass of ions in the same group. The carboxylate groups of SA and Aa have been deprotonated when the pH increases from 1.2 to 7.4, resulting in a rise in the charge density of the associated ions. Therefore, the hydrogel bead networks exhibited an expansion due to greater electrostatic repulsive forces. Therefore, significantly increased swelling of hydrogel networks was detected (Suhail et al., 2022c). As seen in Fig. 7 (B–C), a rise in COOH groups causes swelling, which is proportional to the concentration of SA (SAAF 4, SAAF 5, and SAAF 6), rising at pH 1.2 and pH 7.4. Swelling is high because an increase in SA concentration causes the gel's pores to widen, which improves water penetration (Chang et al., 2009). Monomer concentration also affected the swelling rate as we increased acrylic acid concentration more pendent ionizable groups became available, resulting in more swelling. Same finding was noted by authors during formulation of pH dependent preparation of Eudragit based hydrogel system for controllable delivery of losartan potassium (Mahmood et al., 2019).Fig. 7Effect of (A) pH (B) SA (C) Aa on dynamic swelling and (D) appearances of SA/Aa hydrogel beads.Fig. 7

Drug quantification

Drug loading in the hydrogel beads was quantified using an extraction-based method. The efficiency of drug incorporation was dependent on the compositional ratios of sodium alginate SA and Aa, exhibiting a trend consistent with the swelling behavior. As presented in Table 2, increasing the concentrations of SA and Aa led to higher drug loading capacities. This effect is attributed to the greater availability of carboxylic functional groups in both the natural polymer (SA) and the synthetic monomer (Aa), which led to the relaxation of the hydrogel beads matrix through enhanced water uptake, thereby facilitating more drug entrapment within the polymer matrix.

Drug release study

The in vitro drug release behavior of the SA/Aa hydrogel beads and the commercial product Keten was evaluated under simulated gastrointestinal conditions to assess the potential of SA/Aa hydrogel beads as sustained-release carriers for KT. It was observed that the polymer composition strongly influenced the release rate; formulations with higher SA and Aa content exhibited more prolonged drug release, attributed to the denser crosslinked network and reduced matrix porosity that hindered the rapid diffusion of the drug molecules. The results indicated a significantly higher drug release (97 %) at pH 7.4, associated with pH 1.2. This enhanced release under alkaline conditions can be attributed to the deprotonation of carboxylic acid (-COOH) groups present in both SA and Aa, which increases the ionization of the polymer matrix. The resulting electrostatic repulsion leads to greater polymer relaxation and swelling, thereby facilitating enhanced drug diffusion and release Fig. 8A. This pH-responsive behavior is consistent with the swelling characteristics observed under similar conditions. The process of deprotonation of carboxylate groups resulted in an increased density of charged ions within the same group. This, in turn, caused electrostatic repulsive forces, leading to a more swelling and greater release of the drug. In contrast to the alkaline pH 7.4, minimal drug release was observed at the acidic pH 1.2. The lower drug release (29 %) observed at pH 1.2 is consistent with the expected behavior of SA/Aa hydrogels beads because the carboxyl groups are predominantly protonated at gastric pH, reducing polymer charge density and osmotic swelling and producing a tighter network due to the creation of strong hydrogen bonds with counter ions, which reduced the charge density of similarly charged ions that limits drug diffusion. Moreover, ketorolac (pKa ≈ 3.5) is largely unionized and less soluble at pH 1.2; hence, such effects reduced drug release compared with pH 7.4, where polymer deprotonation increases swelling and ketorolac ionization enhances solubility and release (Rizwan et al., 2017). Consequently, reduced swelling and drug release occurred at pH 1.2. Ullah et al. developed a polymer-based hydrogel system for oxaliplatin delivery and reported a similar enhancement in drug release under alkaline pH conditions (Ullah et al., 2019a). Hence, the developed SA/Aa hydrogel beads exhibited drug release of 29 % and 97 % at pH 1.2 and 7.4 by 24 h. Similarly, a drug release study was carried out on the commercially available product Keten (Fig. 8B) under both acidic and alkaline pH conditions. Drug release occurred over 90 % during the first 3–5 h at pH 7.4, but at pH 1.2, the release of the drug surpassed 80 % by 24 h. A comparison of the drug release profile of the commercially available product Keten with that of the synthesized hydrogel beads revealed that the formulated beads exhibited sustained and controlled drug release behavior. This indicates the potential of the developed hydrogel beads for prolonged therapeutic action compared to the immediate release profile of the commercial formulation. The drug release characteristics, consistent with the swelling response, were markedly dependent on the feed ratios of the hydrogel components. Enhanced release was obtained at elevated concentrations of SA and Aa, as depicted in Fig. 8C–D. The observed increase in drug content percentage can be credited to the heightened charge density, which induces stronger electrostatic repulsive forces, thereby promoting maximal swelling and facilitating enhanced drug release. The increased release associated with higher concentrations of Aa Fig. 8D is due to the higher production of COOH groups, which, owing to their higher charge density, render the hydrogel more pH-sensitive. This finding is supported by our previous work on the formulation of a microgel system for sustained drug delivery (Suhail et al., 2021c).Fig. 8Result of (A, B) pH on SA/Aa hydrogel beads and Keten, and (C) SA and (D) Aa affecting drug release from SA/Aa hydrogel beads.Fig. 8

HET-CAM irritation test

The safety profile of the SA/Aa hydrogel beads was tested by using the (HET-CAM) of Hen's Egg, as illustrated in Fig. 9. The membrane of fertilized chicken eggs was treated with a highly concentrated swollen medium derived from the hydrogel beads and observed at intervals of 0, 0.5, 2, and 5 min post-application. The test aimed to detect any signs of vascular response indicative of irritation, such as vasoconstriction, hemorrhage, or coagulation. For comparison, 0.9 % sodium chloride (NaCl) was used as a negative control, which is not expected to elicit any toxic or vascular reactions. In contrast, 1 % sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), known to induce irritation, was used as the positive control and exhibited clear signs of vasculature damage, including hemorrhage, coagulation, and vasoconstriction. The negative control demonstrated an irritation index (II) of 0, whereas the positive control showed a markedly higher value of 8.46. In comparison, the SA/Aa hydrogel beads exhibited an extremely low irritation index of 0.093, indicating negligible toxicity and confirming their biocompatibility. In-vivo study

In vivo pharmacokinetic evaluation was performed in six healthy rabbits to compare plasma drug concentrations and pharmacokinetic parameters following administration of the drug solution and the drug-loaded SA/Aa hydrogel beads. As shown in Fig. 11, the plasma drug concentration achieved by the loaded SA/Aa hydrogel beads was significantly higher and more sustained over time compared to that of the drug solution. This indicates a prolonged drug release profile from the hydrogel formulation. The enhanced sustained release behavior of the hydrogel beads can be attributed to the gradual relaxation and swelling of the polymeric network, resulting in controlled drug diffusion. Notably, significant differences were observed in key pharmacokinetic parameters among the two tested groups, as presented in Table 4. The drug solution exhibited a shorter Tmax, indicating rapid absorption, while the SA/Aa hydrogel beads demonstrated a delayed Tmax, suggesting slower absorption and a sustained release pattern. Despite containing the same drug concentration, the Cmax values were higher for the hydrogel bead formulation than for the drug solution, indicating that the hydrogel bead matrix maintained therapeutic plasma concentrations over an extended period. Conclusion

In this study, pH-responsive hydrogel beads based on SA and Aa were synthesized by the ionic gelation method for the sustained release of KT. The gel content appears to increase with the increasing bead component ratio. Similarly, an increase in KT loading amount was observed when the concentrations of sodium alginate and acrylic acid increased. A higher swelling ratio and increased drug release were observed at an alkaline pH of 7.4 compared to an acidic pH of 1.2, demonstrating the pH-responsive nature of the developed hydrogel beads. FTIR confirmed the development of the hydrogel beads. Similarly, TGA and DSC analyses results indicated that the hydrogel beads produced had higher thermal stability than their pure contents. SEM with EDX and mapping represents the successful structural development and elemental distribution in the hydrogel bead structure. The results of the HET-CAM assay demonstrated that the beads are safe, as no signs of irritation or vascular damage were detected, indicating their suitability and safety for the oral administration of ketorolac tromethamine. Stable adsorption configurations of ketorolac on the SA/Aa hydrogel beads were obtained using the adsorption locator module. The adsorption energy was negative, indicating that the process is spontaneous and exothermic. The in vivo assessment revealed that the hydrogel beads provided higher bioavailability of ketorolac tromethamine relative to the drug solution. Conclusively, these findings indicate that the prepared hydrogel beads are safe and effective for oral administration and may serve as a promising controlled-release delivery system.
