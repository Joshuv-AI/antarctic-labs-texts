Mobile ICU-supported ECMO rapid response team: A paradigm for regional critical care optimization
Authors: Zewei Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryExtracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is a cornerstone therapy for refractory cardiopulmonary failure, but its effectiveness depends on rapid coordination, initiation, and safe inter-hospital transfer. We evaluated a mobile ICU-supported ECMO rapid response team designed to reorganize ECMO delivery into a standardized, parallelized regional workflow. In a single-center retrospective cohort of 151 adults treated between 2021 and 2025, this model significantly shortened team response, circuit priming, ECMO initiation, and transfer times compared with conventional ad hoc care, while reducing mechanical complications and improving weaning rates, although without statistically significant survival benefit. Efficiency gains were consistent across age, sex, and diagnostic subgroups, underscoring the conceptual value of system-level redesign rather than new technology. These findings demonstrate the feasibility of a mobile ICU-based, hub-and-spoke ECMO network to enhance regional critical care efficiency and coordination, and provide a scalable framework for future prospective and multicenter evaluation.Subject areas: Intensive care medicine, hospital in health services research, emergency care system

Introduction

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) provides temporary cardiopulmonary support for critically ill patients with severe organ failure and has been associated with improved survival across multiple conditions.1,2,3,4 Although several countries have established mature and highly efficient mobile ECMO teams and centralized programs that achieve excellent regional coverage,5 the accessibility, standardization, and inter-hospital coordination of ECMO services remain markedly insufficient in many resource-limited settings.6,7 Many lower-tier hospitals still lack unified initiation criteria and standardized protocols, resulting in technical challenges, safety concerns, and continued dependence on higher-level centers for interfacility transfer.8,9 Large regional variations in ECMO-related in-hospital mortality, ranging from 20% to 70%, further highlight disparities in experience and capability, with high-volume centers consistently achieving better outcomes.10,11 These issues underscore the need for more coordinated regional ECMO systems and improved emergency response capacity.

As ECMO use expands, its high-risk nature, technical complexity, and reliance on multidisciplinary teams have become increasingly concerning,12 particularly during inter-hospital care and long-distance transfers. Traditional centralized ECMO models, based on fixed-site operation and passive patient reception, no longer meet modern critical care demands for rapid response and closed-loop workflows.13 Evidence shows that transferring critically ill patients to specialized ECMO centers reduces complications and improves survival, emphasizing the need for mobile ECMO teams capable of immediate deployment.12,14 However, no standardized framework guides ECMO team development. Institutions vary widely in team composition, activation protocols, and procedural standards, limiting reproducibility and scalability.15,16,17 Technical infrastructure and operational pathways for mobile ECMO systems also remain underdeveloped, and achieving efficient regional collaboration with streamlined referral processes and unified practices remains challenging. Within this landscape, the mobile intensive care unit (mobile ICU) has emerged as a promising platform for critical care transport and bedside intervention, offering invasive monitoring, therapeutic support, and integrated data capabilities to extend advanced life support beyond the hospital.

In recent years, several medical centers in China have begun exploring ECMO rapid-response models built on mobile ICU platforms, in which a fully equipped transport vehicle and a trained multidisciplinary team perform on-site cannulation, initial stabilization, and escorted transfer to a central ECMO center. Although similar systems have shown early success in Western countries,5,18 systematic evaluation in China remains limited, particularly regarding operational efficiency, safety, and clinical impact. Moreover, the uneven distribution of medical resources across regions underscores the need for a standardized, region-based ECMO collaboration framework supported by mobile platforms. Since 2021, our institution has been developing such a mobile ICU-ECMO rapid-response team, laying the groundwork for the present study.

Based on this experience, we established a citywide ECMO collaborative network supported by a mobile ICU rapid response team. The present study aims to evaluate this model by assessing its operational efficiency, including activation speed, bedside standardization, transfer timeliness, and technical reliability. Rather than introducing new technology, the study reorganizes existing workflows into an integrated rapid-response and regional-coordination system. This work addresses a major gap in research on regional ECMO collaboration and provides a practical framework for cities with developing critical-care capacity.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

The main baseline characteristics of patients in the intervention group (n = 98) and the control group (n = 53) were summarized in Table 1. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of sex, age, length of hospital stay, primary diagnostic categories, or clinical outcomes (all p > 0.05). In terms of gender distribution, the intervention group included 79 males (80.6%) and 19 females (19.4%), while the control group included 44 males (83.0%) and 9 females (17.0%) (p = 0.716). The median age was 57.5 years (IQR: 47–69) in the intervention group and 57 years (IQR: 50–68) in the control group (p = 0.766). The median length of hospital stay was 4 days (IQR: 1–14) and 5 days (IQR: 1–11), respectively (p = 0.641). Cardiogenic diseases were the predominant diagnosis in both groups, accounting for 79.6% and 83.0%. The distribution of other diagnostic categories, including cerebral injury, multiple trauma, and miscellaneous conditions, also did not differ significantly (p = 0.231). Regarding clinical outcomes, the intervention group had 38 deaths (38.8%), 33 cases of no improvement (33.7%), 21 improvements (21.4%), and 6 recoveries (6.1%), while the control group had 23 deaths (43.4%), 17 cases of no improvement (32.1%), 13 improvements (24.5%), and no recoveries (0%) (p = 0.308). Overall, the two groups demonstrated comparable baseline characteristics before intervention.

The results showed that the intervention group and the control group differed across multiple key efficiency indicators (Table 2). The total time to ECMO initiation was 68.05 ± 10.96 min in the intervention group and 100.11 ± 12.55 min in the control group (p < 0.0001) (Figure 1C). Effect of the standardized process intervention on clinical outcomes and hospital stay

The mortality rates in the intervention and control groups were 38/98 (38.8%) and 23/53 (43.4%), respectively, with no statistically significant difference (p = 0.705) (Figure 2A). The ECMO weaning success rates were 34/98 (34.7%) in the intervention group and 13/53 (24.5%) in the control group, and the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.270) (Figure 2B). The median hospital stay was 4 days in the intervention group and 5 days in the control group, with no significant difference (p = 0.641) (Figure 2C).

Subgroup analysis demonstrates consistent and stable intervention effects across populations

To further evaluate the applicability and robustness of the intervention across different patient subgroups, analyses were performed based on age (≤60 years vs. > 60 years), primary diagnosis (cardiogenic vs. non-cardiogenic), and gender (male vs. female). The results showed that mortality, ECMO weaning success, hospital stay, and ECMO-related efficiency indicators did not differ between the intervention and control groups in either age subgroup (all p > 0.05), indicating that the intervention effect was similar in younger and older patients (Table S1). Stratified analysis by diagnostic category showed that although the proportion of male patients was higher in the cardiogenic subgroup (p = 0.030), the intervention effect on clinical outcomes and efficiency indicators remained comparable between the cardiogenic and non-cardiogenic groups (Table S2). Likewise, gender-based subgroup analysis showed no differences in clinical or process-related outcomes between male and female patients (Table S3). Overall, the intervention model demonstrated consistent performance across age groups, diagnostic categories, and genders.

Discussion

This study reviewed 151 ECMO cases from 2021 to 2025 and found that a mobile ICU-supported ECMO rapid response team markedly improved key workflow metrics—including team activation, priming, cannulation, and interfacility transport. The total time to establish ECMO was reduced by 32 min (p < 0.0001), providing a critical therapeutic window for severely ill patients. Although decannulation success and mechanical complication rates did not reach statistical significance, both trended favorably. Compared with traditional ad-hoc multidisciplinary activation,19 fixed team composition, parallelized workflows, and integrated equipment preparation enhanced process consistency and organizational efficiency, supporting the structural value of a system-based activation model. These findings provide a basis for assessing alignment with international time-based standards such as the ELSO 20/40-min benchmark.

This study further showed that the mobile ICU-supported ECMO team significantly compressed time-critical intervals, moving system performance closer to the ELSO-recommended window for ECPR. The ELSO consensus advises team activation within 20 min of the initial call and ECMO flow within 40 min,20 with delayed ECMO initiation strongly associated with increased mortality.21,22 In the traditional model, team response and total cannulation times were 65.0 and 100.1 min, respectively, whereas the intervention model shortened them to 39.0 and 68.1 min. Although these times remain outside the 20/40-min benchmark, the gap has substantially narrowed, indicating meaningful system-level improvement. These gains stem from structured implementation of the “team readiness” concept, including fixed personnel, parallel task initiation, and one-touch dispatching, all of which reduce organizational delays inherent to temporary multidisciplinary coordination.19 Integration of the mobile ICU further ensured ICU-level support during transport, reinforcing continuity across the ECMO care chain.

Despite marked improvements in time metrics, mortality did not differ between groups—a finding consistent with prior evidence indicating that ECMO outcomes are driven predominantly by pre-cannulation physiologic status, comorbidity burden, and complications rather than process speed alone.23,24 Long-term survival is influenced chiefly by multiorgan recovery and post-ECMO management quality.25,26 In this study, most patients received VA-ECMO and presented with profound baseline illness severity; even after workflow optimization, the cannulation time (∼68 min) remained well beyond the 35-40-min low-flow threshold associated with survival in ECPR cohorts.22,27,28 Accordingly, no survival benefit would be expected in such a critically ill, heterogeneous population. The absence of systematic no-flow/low-flow documentation and the small proportion of patients meeting true ECPR criteria further limited time-to-outcome analyses. The slightly lower decannulation rate in the intervention group likely reflects higher baseline acuity and more complex out-of-hospital cannulation cases rather than diminished process effectiveness. Collectively, these results align with established ECMO outcome mechanisms: improvements in workflow alone are insufficient to alter survival in high-acuity VA-ECMO patients unless the time-critical ECPR window can be reached.

Nevertheless, the mobile ICU-supported ECMO response model provides significant structural value for regional systems of care. Given the complexity and resource intensity of ECMO, many local hospitals cannot independently initiate ECMO and rely on referral to higher-level centers. Existing expert consensus highlights the importance of establishing regional ECMO networks, with standardized pathways and resource concentration to improve overall system performance.7,29 The “hub-and-spoke” configuration used in this study—linking a core ECMO center with alliance hospitals through a mobile ICU platform—enabled rapid, unified activation, and seamless interfacility rescue. High-capability tertiary hospitals may substantially strengthen regional response capacity through fixed ECMO teams and dedicated mobile ICU units, whereas peripheral hospitals should focus on optimizing ECMO recognition and transfer protocols to achieve a closed-loop collaborative system.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective pre-post design is susceptible to temporal confounding and cannot fully account for changes in team experience, technology, or overall care quality over time. Second, although demographic and diagnostic characteristics were similar between groups, inconsistent documentation of key severity markers (e.g., SOFA, APACHE II, hemodynamic parameters, duration of shock or cardiac arrest) prevented robust multivariable adjustment, leaving residual confounding. Third, the sample size and number of outcome events were limited, reducing statistical power for major clinical endpoints, including decannulation success and mortality. Fourth, the cohort’s overall high acuity likely attenuated the detectable impact of process improvements on short-term outcomes. Fifth, a learning-curve effect cannot be excluded, as increased team proficiency over time may have contributed to improvements in efficiency metrics. Lastly, the absence of economic and cost-effectiveness analyses limits assessment of the model’s feasibility and scalability for broader implementation.

Future studies should incorporate prospective or multicenter designs to validate these findings; systematically collect baseline severity indicators (e.g., SOFA, APACHE II, and hemodynamics) to enable multivariable and causal analyses; expand sample size and conduct disease- and risk-stratified subgroup analyses; and investigate causal pathways linking workflow efficiency, cannulation timing, and clinical outcomes. Economic and resource-utilization assessments will be essential for evaluating long-term sustainability. Integration of digital tools—such as AI-assisted dispatch, teleconsultation, and mobile physiologic monitoring—may further support the development of scalable, reproducible regional ECMO systems.

This study provides comprehensive evidence that a mobile ICU-supported ECMO rapid response model can substantially improve process efficiency and strengthen interfacility coordination within a regional emergency care system. Although no statistically significant improvements were observed in major clinical endpoints, potential benefits warrant further confirmation in larger, prospective studies. This work represents the first structured regional implementation of an ECMO rapid response system, offering a foundational model for building efficient, standardized regional critical care networks. Given its reliance on specialized resources, including mobile ICU platforms, coordinated prehospital systems, and a mature ECMO center, its broader applicability requires context-specific evaluation. Future adaptation should account for regional heterogeneity to support the transition of critical care delivery from a centralized approach toward an integrated, system-level model of coordinated response.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its single-center, retrospective pre-post design introduces potential selection bias and temporal confounding, as improvements in efficiency may partly reflect accumulating team experience or concurrent system-level changes rather than the intervention alone. Second, the sample size and number of outcome events were relatively limited, reducing statistical power to detect differences in major clinical endpoints such as mortality and ECMO weaning success. Third, incomplete and non-uniform documentation of key pre-cannulation severity variables (e.g., duration of shock or cardiac arrest, low-flow/no-flow time, and detailed hemodynamic parameters) precluded robust multivariable adjustment and causal inference. Fourth, the predominance of high-acuity VA-ECMO cases may have attenuated the observable impact of workflow optimization on survival outcomes. Finally, the study did not include cost-effectiveness or resource-utilization analyses, which are important for assessing the scalability and sustainability of the mobile ICU-supported ECMO model in other regions.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study population and population

This single-center retrospective cohort study completed all data extraction and analysis in June 2025, with March 31, 2025 as the cutoff date for data collection. The study included and analyzed clinical data from adult patients who received ECMO therapy at our institution between January 2021 and March 2025. To evaluate the impact of implementing a mobile ICU-supported ECMO rapid response team, patients were divided into two groups based on the date of team establishment (Figure 3).

Flowchart of patient enrollment for ECMO treatment

A total of 162 patients who received ECMO treatment between January 2021 and March 2025 were screened. In the control group (n = 59), 2 patients under 18 years of age, and 4 patients with ECMO support duration <24 h were excluded, resulting in 53 included cases. In the intervention group (n = 103), 4 patients under 18 and 1 with a support duration <24 h were excluded, resulting in 98 included cases. A total of 151 patients were included in the final analysis.

The control group consisted of 53 patients treated between January 2021 and December 2022 by a conventional hybrid ECMO team, which comprised staff from both the Department of Critical Care Medicine and the Department of Emergency Medicine. The intervention group consisted of 98 patients treated between January 2023 and March 2025 by a newly established mobile ICU-supported ECMO rapid response team, primarily staffed by emergency physicians and nurses.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Receipt of ECMO therapy with appropriate clinical indications; (2) Age ≥18 years; (3) Signed informed consent obtained from the patient or their legal guardian before treatment, with agreement to be transferred to our institution for ongoing care. Exclusion criteria: ECMO support duration <24 h.

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Clinical Ethics Committee of Jiaxing University Affiliated Hospital (Jiaxing First Hospital) (No. 2024-LP-147). The study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all enrolled patients or their legal representatives prior to participation.

Method details

Sample size estimation and data collection

This study was retrospective in design, and all clinical data were obtained from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. The sample size was determined based on the ECMO case volume at our institution over the past three years, as well as similar studies published previously.30,31 A total of 151 patients were ultimately included in the analysis.

Data extraction was performed independently by two researchers and cross-validated for consistency. A third investigator resolved any discrepancies to ensure data accuracy and reliability.

ECMO team operational models before and after intervention

Before the establishment of the mobile ICU-supported ECMO rapid response team, the ECMO team was composed of a hybrid group of physicians and nurses from the Departments of Critical Care Medicine and Emergency Medicine. A conventional referral process was followed: partner hospitals submitted a transfer request to our institution, after which team members—including a team leader, one assistant, and one perfusionist—were individually contacted and assembled. The team would then transport a mobile ECMO device to the referring hospital (without the support of a dedicated mobile ICU), initiate ECMO therapy on-site, and transfer the patient to our institution for continued treatment.

Following the establishment of the mobile ICU-supported ECMO rapid response team, a standardized activation protocol and centralized dispatch system were implemented, enabling team members to be deployed in parallel and arrive rapidly at the target facility. Leveraging the mobile ICU and an integrated transportation coordination system, both response and transfer times were significantly reduced, leading to enhanced efficiency and procedural stability. Establishment and management of the mobile ICU-supported ECMO rapid response team

Team composition and management mechanism

The mobile ICU-supported ECMO rapid response team was officially established in January 2023 under the overall coordination of the Director of the Emergency Department. The team comprises 15 core members, including 4 team leaders, 6 assistants, and 7 perfusionists. All members completed standardized ECMO-specific training and passed qualification assessments. The team is fully integrated into the hospital’s emergency management platform, enabling centralized dispatching and efficient collaboration (Figure 4, right panel).

Comparison of dual-track organizational structures in traditional and mobile ECMO Teams

Optimization of activation mechanism

The team’s response protocol was developed based on the Clinical Guidelines for the Application and Management of Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation and integrated into the hospital’s emergency management system, featuring a “one-click activation” mechanism designed to initiate a response within 20 min (Figure 5). Upon receiving an ECMO request, the team leader immediately proceeds to the referring hospital for on-site evaluation and consultation. Simultaneously, the assistant dispatches a dedicated ambulance equipped with ECMO devices, while the perfusionist prepares the equipment and completes circuit priming in parallel. This multithreaded workflow enables simultaneous patient assessment, equipment preparation, and personnel mobilization, effectively reducing pre-procedural response time.

Dispatch protocol for the mobile ICU–Supported ECMO rapid response team

The dispatch protocol for the mobile ICU–supported ECMO rapid response team is designed to standardize the activation, mobilization, and execution of extra-hospital ECMO support to ensure rapid, safe, and reproducible clinical response. When a partner hospital within the regional ECMO collaboration network submits a support request through the designated emergency communication platform, the team leader first performs a remote assessment based on vital signs, imaging findings, and arterial blood gas analysis to confirm that the patient meets the ECMO indications of our center. Once approved, a “one-click activation” mechanism is triggered in which the hospital emergency command center simultaneously broadcasts alerts through internal communication systems, group messaging, and app-based warning platforms. All core team members receive a standardized notification instructing them to assemble at the designated mobile ICU departure site within 15 min.

Upon activation, the team responds in parallel. The team leader immediately contacts the requesting hospital to provide early resuscitation guidance and organizes task allocation. Two assistants proceed to the mobile ICU parking area to start the vehicle, activate the onboard power system, and conduct rapid equipment checks. One to two perfusionists retrieve pre-primed ECMO circuits, cannulation kits, and emergency supply boxes before joining the mobile ICU. Within 5 min, the mobile ICU must complete vehicle and equipment readiness, including initiating the engine, powering ventilators, monitors, infusion pumps, and verifying the availability of essential medications such as vasoactive agents, sedatives, and heparin.

After departure, the dispatch center notifies the municipal traffic command platform to request prioritized transit support, including green-wave optimization for the vehicle’s travel route. Throughout transport, the team leader maintains continuous communication with the requesting hospital to obtain real-time clinical updates and refine the operational plan. Upon arrival, the team follows a pre-defined workflow: the leader conducts bedside assessment and determines cannulation strategy; assistants establish airway or vascular access as needed and prepare medications; perfusionists complete ECMO circuit assembly and final priming. Once ECMO is successfully initiated and the patient reaches physiological stability, the team evaluates transport suitability. If eligible, the patient is transferred onto the mobile ICU platform, and the receiving ICU at our center is notified in advance to prepare for admission.

After completing patient handover, the mission formally concludes. All equipment and consumables used during the mission must be replenished and restored within 24 h to maintain readiness. A structured debriefing is conducted within 72 h to document key time points, operational challenges, and opportunities for improvement as part of continuous quality assurance.

Dual-track team structure and role definition

The team adopted a dual-track organizational structure with clearly defined roles (Figure 5). The team leader was responsible for clinical decision-making and procedural guidance; the assistant handled transport coordination and provided procedural support; and the perfusionist focused on setting up ECMO circuits and performing technical maintenance. This structured approach contrasts sharply with traditional ECMO teams, which often suffer from ad hoc composition, overlapping responsibilities, and procedural delays (Figure 4, left panel).

Development of the “mobile ICU” and inter-hospital transport system

To improve inter-hospital transport efficiency, the team collaborated with the Municipal Emergency Medical Center to retrofit a standard ambulance into a “mobile ICU” equipped with advanced critical care monitoring and an integrated ECMO system. After ECMO initiation, patients were directly transferred to the mobile ICU for transport. The city’s traffic management authority coordinated green-light traffic signal priority through a centralized command system to ensure smooth and uninterrupted transfer (Figure 5, Post phase), significantly reducing delays and wait times compared to the traditional approach (Figure 5, Pre phase).

To standardize and enhance the quality of inter-hospital ECMO transfers, a structured protocol for ECMO patient transport has been developed. This protocol covers critical stages including pre-transfer assessment and preparation, intra-transfer monitoring and support, and post-transfer handover and verification (Figure 6). The workflow clearly specifies personnel allocation for each task—for example, two personnel are assigned to operate the ECMO device, one to manage the circuit, and two to handle stretcher transport. These measures ensure continuity, stability, and safety throughout the entire transfer process, enabling the patient to be securely transported to the receiving hospital.

Inter-hospital transfer process for ECMO patients

Quality control and continuous improvement

A monthly case review and process analysis system was established. Regular review meetings were conducted to optimize procedural workflows, identify and address issues, and enhance overall team coordination, involving both medical and nursing teams.

Training system and emergency drills

Team training encompassed the following four components: (1) Systematic study of the Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) Training Manual to strengthen theoretical knowledge; (2) Standardized procedural training and competency assessments to ensure technical proficiency; (3) Quarterly case-based experience sharing sessions to enhance practical skills; (4) Semi-annual emergency drills covering the entire ECMO response workflow—including activation, transport, and cannulation—to improve real-time performance and strengthen team coordination.

Cannulation and ECMO initiation procedure

Operators of equivalent qualification levels performed cannulation procedures in both groups to minimize operator-related bias (Figures 7 and 8). Under ultrasound guidance and local infiltration anesthesia using 1% lidocaine, femoral artery and femoral vein cannulation were performed. Upon successful vascular access, 15/17F MAQUET arterial cannulas and 21/23F MAQUET venous cannulas were inserted. Circuit priming was completed using the MAQUET ECMO BE-PLS 2050 extracorporeal circulation kit. All patients were managed using the veno-arterial (V-A) ECMO configuration.

Schematic diagram of the standardized procedure for ECMO cannulation performed by the clinical team

For anticoagulation, unfractionated heparin was used in all cases. The initial dosage ranged from 50 to 100 IU/kg, with activated clotting time (ACT) maintained between 180 and 220 s. ECMO flow rates were initially set at 2.2–2.6 L/min/m2 based on body surface area, and dynamically adjusted according to the patient’s blood pressure, oxygenation status, and lactate levels to ensure target perfusion and reproducibility of support.

Outcome measures and evaluation indicators

Demographic and Clinical Information: Patient data, including gender, age, diagnosis, and diagnostic category, were collected. Hospital length of stay and clinical outcomes (i.e., death or non-recovery) were recorded to evaluate the baseline comparability between the two groups.

ECMO operational efficiency indicators: Including team activation time (from the initial call to full readiness of team members and equipment), circuit priming time (time required from the start to the completion of priming by the perfusionist), ECMO establishment time (total time from team activation to successful initiation of ECMO support), and inter-hospital transfer time (total duration from the beginning of transport preparation to the patient’s arrival at our hospital). These indicators were used to evaluate differences in efficiency between the two models.

Safety and clinical outcome indicators: Including ECMO mechanical failure rate (the proportion of patients experiencing mechanical failure during ECMO support) and ECMO weaning success rate (the proportion of patients who were successfully weaned from ECMO and survived for more than 48 h). These indicators were used to evaluate the safety and preliminary effectiveness of ECMO treatment under different models.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1). Continuous variables were described according to their distribution: those with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x¯ ± s), and comparisons between groups were conducted using independent samples t-tests; non-normally distributed variables were presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) [M (P25, P75)] and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and group comparisons were performed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All tests were two-sided, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

As there were no statistically significant differences between the intervention and control groups in the primary outcome variables (mortality and ECMO weaning success rate), and given the relatively small sample size and limited number of events, the conditions for multivariable logistic regression analysis were not met. Therefore, multivariate analysis was not conducted in this study.

Based on the primary analysis, subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential effect modifications across different populations. Stratification variables included: (1) Age (≤60 years vs. > 60 years); (2) Primary diagnosis type (cardiogenic vs. non-cardiogenic); (3) Gender (male vs. female). Within each subgroup, differences between the intervention and control groups were compared in terms of mortality, ECMO weaning success rate, hospital stay, and efficiency indicators (response time, circuit priming time, total ECMO establishment time, and inter-hospital transfer time), using the same statistical methods as in the main analysis.
