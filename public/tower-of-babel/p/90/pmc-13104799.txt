Effect of Once‐Daily Macitentan 75 mg on the Pharmacokinetics of Sildenafil, Riociguat, or Rosuvastatin in Healthy Male Participants
Authors: Dénes Csonka, Cynthia Gargano, Navin Goyal, Marc De Meulder, Vitaly Yartsev, Juan Jose Perez Ruixo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMacitentan is an oral, dual ETA/ETB endothelin receptor antagonist, currently approved in adults for the treatment of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) at a once‐daily dose of 10 mg. Pre‐clinical and clinical data suggest that greater ETB receptor inhibition may result in greater efficacy. This hypothesis is being tested in the Phase 3 UNISUS study (NCT04273945), comparing once‐daily macitentan 75 mg versus macitentan 10 mg.The present Phase 1 study (NCT04211272) evaluated the pharmacokinetics and safety of concomitant administration of once‐daily macitentan 75 mg at steady state with substrates of either breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP) transporter (e.g., riociguat and rosuvastatin) or cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4) enzymes (e.g., sildenafil and tadalafil) in healthy male adults. Macitentan was administered once daily at 10 mg on Days 1 and 2, 37.5 mg on Days 3‐5, and 75 mg on Days 6‐13. Participants received a single oral dose of sildenafil (20 mg) on Days −4 and 13, riociguat (1 mg) on Days −3 and 10, or rosuvastatin (10 mg) on Days −4 and 10.Geometric mean ratios for the maximum concentration and area under the curve of the substrate and 90% confidence intervals showed no clinically relevant effects on exposure for sildenafil, riociguat, or rosuvastatin when administered alone or with macitentan 75 mg. Co‐administration of macitentan and the substrates was generally well tolerated. Due to the lack of clinically relevant drug–drug pharmacokinetic interactions, no dose adjustment is deemed necessary when co‐administering once‐daily macitentan 75 mg with BCRP or CYP3A4 substrates.Keywords: drug interactions, macitentan, pulmonary arterial hypertension, riociguat, rosuvastatin, sildenafil

Introduction

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a progressive, fatal vascular disease with no cure that is characterized by restricted blood flow in the pulmonary arteries, resulting in high pulmonary arterial pressure and, eventually, right heart failure and death. 1 , 2 , 3 Current therapies for PAH target four key signaling pathways: endothelin‐1 (ET‐1), nitric oxide, prostacyclin, and activin. 4 , 5 Despite the efficacy associated with targeting these pathways, the mortality rate of PAH remains high (median survival of 5‐7 years), highlighting the need for new and/or improved treatment strategies. 2 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9

Macitentan is a highly effective, oral dual endothelin receptor antagonist that inhibits the binding of ET‐1 to endothelin receptors A (ETA) and B (ETB). 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 The approved dose of once‐daily macitentan 10 mg is the recommended standard of care for adults with PAH, where it has been shown to reduce the risks of disease progression and hospitalization. 1 , 10 , 12 , 13 , 14 In a Phase I, single‐ascending dose study in healthy volunteers, macitentan was absorbed slowly, reaching peak plasma levels after 8‐30 h, with a less‐than‐dose‐proportional exposure (at doses of 25 mg and above); elimination half‐life (t1/2) was approximately 16 h. In a Phase I multiple‐ascending dose study (1‐30 mg/day), peak plasma levels occurred at approximately 8 h, t1/2 was 14‐19 h, and steady state was achieved within 3 days. 16

While the 10‐mg dose predominantly inhibits ETA receptors, the 75‐mg dose achieves maximal inhibition of ETB receptors. 17 Pre‐clinical and early‐phase clinical data suggest that greater ETB receptor inhibition may result in greater efficacy, 17 , 18 , 19 a hypothesis being tested in the Phase 3 UNISUS study (NCT04273945), which compares the effect of once‐daily macitentan 75 mg versus 10 mg on time to first morbidity/mortality event, the study's primary endpoint.

Therapeutic management of PAH often involves the use of combination therapy targeting the four pathways described above. 4 , 5 , 20 Macitentan is frequently co‐administered with agents targeting the nitric oxide pathway, such as the phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors, sildenafil and tadalafil, 21 and the soluble guanylate cyclase riociguat. 22 In addition, many patients with PAH have cardiovascular risk factors requiring co‐prescription of other therapies, including statins. 23

Pre‐clinical, in vitro studies suggest potential interactions between macitentan and substrates of both breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP) transporter (e.g., riociguat and rosuvastatin), as well as cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4) enzymes (e.g., sildenafil and tadalafil). Pharmacokinetic (PK) studies of once‐daily macitentan 10 mg in healthy male volunteers showed no effects on the PK of riociguat or rosuvastatin. 24 , 25 Macitentan has been shown to inhibit BCRP transporter substrates with a 50% inhibitor concentration of 1.0 µM. At this macitentan concentration, CYP3A4 mRNA is increased by up to 11‐fold, and CYP3A4 enzyme activity is increased by up to five‐fold (data on file). Following repeated oral administration of macitentan 75 mg during steady state, peak plasma concentrations of macitentan and its active metabolite, aprocitentan, in patients with PAH are predicted to be around 5 and 14 µM, respectively (data on file). Due to high protein binding, free plasma concentrations of macitentan and aprocitentan are estimated in the range of 0.02 and 0.07 µM (data on file), which are not anticipated to result in any inhibition of BCRP in the liver or kidney, or induction of CYP3A4 enzyme in the liver (data on file). However, intestinal concentrations of macitentan may be higher, and its effect on intestinal BCRP and CYP3A4 enzymes at the 75‐mg dose is unknown. Therefore, the potential for drug‐drug interactions at higher doses of macitentan requires evaluation.

The aim of this Phase 1 study was to characterize the effect (PK and safety) of once‐daily macitentan 75 mg at steady state on the PK of a single 20‐mg dose of sildenafil (and if needed tadalafil), a single 1‐mg dose of riociguat, and a single 10‐mg dose of rosuvastatin in healthy adult males.

Methods

Ethical Approval

This study (NCT04211272) was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The study protocol was approved by an independent ethics committee, and participants provided informed consent prior to any study‐related activity.

Study Design and Participants

This was a Phase 1, single‐sequence, open‐label, PK study in healthy male participants, conducted at a single center in Belgium (Clinical Pharmacology Unit, Lange Bremstraat 70 Merksem, 2170) between January 17, 2020, and April 19, 2021 (Figure 1). The study was divided into two parts: Part A of the study was to investigate the effect of macitentan 75 mg on the PK of sildenafil and riociguat, and Part B was to investigate the effect of macitentan 75 mg on the PK of rosuvastatin and tadalafil. The interaction of macitentan 75 mg with tadalafil was only to be assessed in Part B if macitentan decreased the area under the plasma analyte concentration‐time curve of sildenafil by >50% in Part A. As this was not the case, tadalafil was not administered in this study.

Study design. Part A examined the separate interaction between macitentan (up to 75 mg) and sildenafil 20 mg or riociguat 1 mg. Part B examined the interaction between macitentan (up to 75 mg) and rosuvastatin 10 mg. For sildenafil, blood samples were taken at 0 (pre‐dose), 20, and 40 min and at 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 16 h post‐dose. For riociguat, blood samples were taken at 0 min (pre‐dose) and at 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 16, 24, 48, and 72 h post‐dose. For rosuvastatin, blood samples were taken at 0 min (pre‐dose) and at 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, 4.5, 5, 5.5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 12, 16, 24, 48, and 72 h post‐dose. For macitentan, blood samples were taken at 0 min (pre‐dose) and on Days 3, 5, 7, and 9; prior to substrate dosing; and at 16 h post‐substrate dosing in Part A and at 48 and 96 h post‐substrate dosing in Part B. EOS, end of study; FU, follow‐up.

Both parts of the study included a 21‐day screening phase before the first drug was administered, followed by an open‐label treatment phase consisting of five (Part A) or three (Part B) treatments in a fixed sequence (Figure 1). The total study length was 26‐30 days, excluding screening and follow‐up.

Healthy men aged ≥18 to ≤55 years with a body mass index of ≥18.0 to ≤30.0 kg/m2 and a body weight of ≥50 kg were eligible. Participants who had received a known CYP inhibitor/inducer or transporter inhibitor/inducer were excluded from the study based upon the duration of the inhibitor/inducer effect, or for up to five terminal half‐lives of the drug, vitamin, or herbal supplement received to ensure its elimination.

Study Drug and Dosing Schedules

Oral macitentan, formulated as 10‐, 37.5‐, and 75‐mg film‐coated tablets, was provided by the sponsor (Johnson & Johnson). Commercially available sildenafil (Revatio, Viatris Inc, PA, USA), formulated as a 2‐mg film‐coated tablet; riociguat (Adempas, Bayer Pharmaceuticals AG, Berlin, Germany), as a 1‐mg film‐coated tablet; and rosuvastatin (Crestor, AstraZeneca, GmbH, Wedel, Germany), as a 10‐mg film‐coated tablet were used in the study.

In both Part A and Part B, macitentan was administered under fed conditions on non‐substrate days over Days 1‐13 and under fasted conditions on the days on which the substrates (sildenafil and riociguat in Part A; rosuvastatin in Part B) were co‐administered concomitantly. Although in clinical practice, sildenafil, riociguat, and rosuvastatin can be taken with or without food, there are some effects of food reported for these substrates 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 ; hence, for consistency, all were administered in the fasted state. Macitentan 75 mg should be taken with food (data on file). Macitentan was dosed once daily at 10 mg on Days 1‐2, 37.5 mg on Days 3‐5, and 75 mg on Days 6‐13 (Figure 1). In Part A, participants were administered a single oral dose of sildenafil (20 mg) on Days −4 and 13, and a single oral dose of riociguat (1 mg) on Days −3 and 10. In Part B, participants were administered a single oral dose of rosuvastatin (10 mg) on Days −4 and 10. Based on their prescribing information, riociguat, sildenafil, and rosuvastatin were studied at the clinically relevant doses used in patients with PAH (maximum approved doses were not selected to ensure safety and tolerability in the event that any increases in the concentrations would be observed).

The macitentan dose regimen was implemented after a protocol amendment. Under the original study protocol, macitentan 75 mg was administered without the intermediate 10‐ and 37.5‐mg steps. This dosing regimen was poorly tolerated by the first 11 participants, and the study was halted due to the sponsor's decision. The protocol was then amended (Figure 1), and the study resumed, enrolling new participants.

Endpoints

The primary objective was to evaluate the effect of once‐daily macitentan 75 mg at steady state on the following PK parameters of a single 20‐mg dose of sildenafil, 1‐mg dose of riociguat, or 10‐mg dose of rosuvastatin: maximum plasma analyte concentration (Cmax), time to maximum plasma analyte concentration (tmax), area under the concentration‐time curve (AUC) up to the last quantifiable time point (AUClast), AUC to infinite time (AUC∞), and half‐life (t1/2).

Secondary objectives included the assessment of safety and tolerability of concomitant administration of macitentan/riociguat, macitentan/sildenafil, or macitentan/rosuvastatin; the PK effect of macitentan 75 mg on desmethylsildenafil (main metabolite of sildenafil) and M1 (main metabolite of riociguat); and determination of the trough concentrations of macitentan and aprocitentan that lead to steady‐state and a Day 13 PK profile.

The pharmacodynamic effect of macitentan 75 mg on the 4β‐hydroxycholesterol/cholesterol ratio was measured at multiple time points following initial dosing and at steady state to assess CYP3A4 induction. This endogenous biomarker reflects CYP3A4‑mediated cholesterol metabolism and has been shown to increase in response to CYP3A4 induction. 30 In Part A, the pharmacodynamic effect of once‐daily macitentan 75 mg on ET‐1 concentrations was investigated as an exploratory objective to confirm ETB receptor inhibition.

Safety and tolerability were monitored throughout the study. Adverse events (AEs), treatment‐emergent AEs (TEAEs), serious AEs (SAEs), clinical laboratory tests (blood samples for serum chemistry and hematology; urine samples for urinalysis), electrocardiograms, vital signs, and physical examinations were recorded. TEAEs were coded according to the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities Version 23.1.

PK Sampling and Bioanalysis

PK profiles were collected before the first dose of macitentan and after macitentan 75 mg and its active metabolite (aprocitentan) reached steady state at Day 13 (Figure 1). A 24‐ and a 72‐h PK profile for sildenafil and riociguat and a 96‐h PK profile for rosuvastatin were collected while macitentan continued to be administered. In parallel with the 24‐h PK profile for sildenafil, a 240‐h PK profile was collected for macitentan and aprocitentan.

Plasma concentrations of macitentan, aprocitentan, sildenafil, desmethylsildenafil, riociguat, M1, and rosuvastatin were determined using validated, specific, and sensitive liquid chromatography coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS) methods, with lower limit of quantifications (LLoQ) within plasma as follows: 1 ng/mL for macitentan and aprocitentan, 0.02 ng/mL for sildenafil and desmethylsildenafil, 0.2 ng/mL for riociguat and M1, and 0.1 ng/mL for rosuvastatin. Plasma concentrations of cholesterol and 4β‐hydroxycholesterol were determined using validated, specific, and sensitive LC‐MS/MS methods, with LLoQ values of 5 ng/mL for 4β‐hydroxycholesterol and 25 µg/mL for cholesterol. ET‐1 concentrations were measured pre‐dose and at several on‐treatment time point using a Simple Plex assay on the ELLA automated immunoassay platform. 16 , 31

Statistical Analysis

A formal sample size calculation was not performed; however, it was estimated that with a sample size of 16 evaluable participants in Parts A and B, the lower and upper bounds of the 90% CI for the geometric mean ratio of co‐administered treatment/treatment alone for Cmax and AUC0‐∞ would range from 0.81 to 1.23 if the estimated ratio was 1 (Table S1). These estimated ratios were based on assumed intra‐individual coefficients of variation (% CV) for sildenafil, riociguat, and rosuvastatin of 30%, 20%, and 34% for Cmax, respectively, and 18%, 20%, and 21% for AUC0‐∞, respectively, from historical data. Eighteen participants were targeted for enrollment in each part to account for potential dropouts.

The PK analysis set comprised all participants who received at least one dose of the study drug and had at least one plasma concentration data value after study drug administration. The safety analysis set comprised all participants who received at least one dose of macitentan, sildenafil, or riociguat in Part A or macitentan or rosuvastatin in Part B. If a PK parameter of interest was missing or not estimable for a given participant for one or more drugs, that participant was excluded from the statistical analysis of that particular PK parameter. Pharmacodynamic data (4β‐hydroxycholesterol/cholesterol ratio and ET‐1 assessments) were only available for Part A and are presented for all participants who had at least one data value available. As the ET‐1 analysis was exploratory, no statistical comparisons were made.

For each analyte of interest, log‐transformed PK parameter (AUCs and Cmax) data were analyzed using a mixed‐effects model with drug as a fixed effect and participant as a random effect. The estimated least square means and intra‐individual %CV from the mixed‐effects model were used to construct 90% CIs for the difference in means on the log scale for each drug pairing. The limits of the CIs were back‐transformed using antilogarithms to obtain 90% CIs for the geometric mean ratios for each drug pair. PK data were analyzed separately for Parts A and B.

Results

Patient Population

In both Part A and Part B, 18 male participants received at least one dose of the study drug (i.e., safety analysis set). Overall demographics and baseline characteristics of these participants are listed in Table 1. For Part A and Part B, the median age (range) was 43.5 (20‐55) years and 37.0 (23‐54) years, respectively, with a median (range) weight of 78.2 (59.6‐88.1) and 75.6 (61.9‐96.1) kg, respectively. In Part A, one participant (5.6%) withdrew from dosing after receiving a single dose of sildenafil 20 mg and withdrew from the study the day after sildenafil administration due to orthostatic hypotension. In Part B, one participant (5.6%) withdrew from dosing on Day 8 (after having received a single dose of rosuvastatin 10 mg and escalating doses of macitentan up to two administrations of macitentan 75 mg) and withdrew from the study due to headache. Following exclusion of these two participants, the PK statistical analysis sets for Part A and Part B each comprised 17 participants.

PK of Macitentan and Aprocitentan

In Part A, mean macitentan and aprocitentan plasma concentrations peaked at 3‐ and 10‐h post‐dose, respectively, based on steady‐state profiles on Day 13, following once‐daily macitentan administration (Figure 2). Both compounds exhibited a biphasic decline thereafter. On average, plasma concentrations of macitentan fell below the LLoQ after 6 days (144 h), while plasma concentrations of aprocitentan remained quantifiable at the last sampling time point on Day 9 (216 h). Steady‐state concentrations for macitentan and aprocitentan were reached after 9 days of once‐daily macitentan, based on individual and mean trough plasma concentrations (Figure 3A). PK parameters for macitentan and aprocitentan are summarized in Table 2. The PK characteristics of macitentan and aprocitentan in Part B are shown in Figure 3B. Of note, macitentan trough concentrations on Days 13 and 14 in Part B were comparable (1180 and 1140 ng/mL, respectively) with those measured on Days 13 and 14 in Part A (1030 and 936 ng/mL, respectively).

PK Interactions Between Macitentan and Sildenafil, Riociguat, or Rosuvastatin

Administration of sildenafil 20 mg alone or with once‐daily macitentan 75 mg at steady state resulted in similar plasma exposures of sildenafil (Table 3; Figure 4A,B). The geometric mean ratio (90% CI) of combination to single treatment for Cmax, AUClast, and AUC∞ were 97.3% (79.9%‐118.4%), 98.7% (91.4%‐106.7%), and 95.8% (89.5%‐102.7%), respectively; median tmax was similar with both regimens. Intra‐participant % CV across these parameters ranged from 10.7% to 33.7%. For desmethylsildenafil, the main metabolite of sildenafil, plasma exposure decreased following co‐administration with once‐daily macitentan 75 mg at steady state: AUClast decreased by 17% (geometric mean ratio [90% CI]: 83.2% [76.4%‐90.6%]), and AUC∞ decreased by 21% (79.2% [71.1%‐88.3%]). Cmax and tmax were similar with both regimens. Intra‐participant % CV across these parameters ranged from 14.1% to 19.9%.

Co‐administration of riociguat 1 mg with once‐daily macitentan 75 mg at steady state resulted in a 24% decrease in the AUClast and AUC∞ of riociguat compared with riociguat alone, with geometric mean ratios (90% CI) of 75.8% (70.7%‐81.2%) and 75.8% (70.8%‐81.1%), respectively (Table 3; Figure 4C). Geometric mean Cmax decreased by 18% (81.6% [75.5%‐88.2%]), while median tmax was similar with both regimens. Intra‐participant % CV across these parameters ranged from 11.4% to 14.9%. Co‐administration of riociguat with macitentan 75 mg increased plasma exposure of M1, the main metabolite of riociguat, by 9% for AUClast (geometric mean ratio [90% CI]: 108.7% [103.0%‐114.8%]) and AUC∞ (109.3 [102.4%‐116.6%]) and by 17% for Cmax (116.7 [108.8%‐125.2%]) compared with riociguat alone (Table 3; Figure 4D). Median tmax for M1 was reached 6 h earlier when riociguat was co‐administered with once‐daily macitentan 75 mg at steady state compared with riociguat alone. Intra‐participant % CV across these parameters ranged from 8.5% to 11.8%.

Co‐administration of rosuvastatin 10 mg with once‐daily macitentan 75 mg at steady state resulted in rosuvastatin increases of 7% for AUClast (geometric mean ratio [90% CI]: 107.4% [93.6%‐123.4%]), 11% for AUC∞ (110.8% [101.9%‐120.4%]), and 16% for Cmax (115.8% [102.0%‐131.4%]) compared with rosuvastatin alone (Table 3; Figure 4E). Median tmax of rosuvastatin was reached approximately 1.5 h earlier after co‐administration with once‐daily macitentan 75 mg at steady state compared with rosuvastatin alone. Intra‐participant % CV across these parameters ranged from 11.9% to 23.4%.

Pharmacodynamics: Effect on 4β‐Hydroxycholesterol/cholesterol Ratio

In the 17 participants who completed Part A, once‐daily macitentan had a negligible impact on the 4β‐hydroxycholesterol/cholesterol ratio, with a mean (standard deviation, SD) change from baseline to Day 13 of 0.105 (0.475). Mean values were relatively stable throughout the study (Figure S1).

Pharmacodynamics: Effect on ET‐1 Levels

In Part A (n = 17), ET‐1 plasma concentrations increased from a geometric mean of approximately 1.7 pg/mL at baseline to a plateau of 3.7 pg/mL by Day 10 (Figure S2). After Day 14 (i.e., 24 h after the last dose of macitentan 75 mg), ET‐1 plasma concentrations started to return to the levels observed before macitentan administration, with mean levels at Day 20 of 2.2 pg/mL).

Safety

Co‐administration of once‐daily macitentan 75 mg with a single dose of sildenafil, riociguat, or rosuvastatin was generally well tolerated in healthy adult males. No SAEs were reported, and most AEs were mild in severity.

TEAEs were reported for all study drugs. The most common TEAE was headache (Part A: n = 13 [76.5%], Table 4; Part B: n = 17 [94.4%], Table 4). In Part A, five participants (27.8%) receiving sildenafil, six (35.3%) receiving riociguat, and 15 (88.2%) receiving macitentan reported at least one TEAE (Table 4). In Part B, five participants (27.8%) receiving rosuvastatin and 18 (100%) participants receiving macitentan experienced at least one TEAE (Table 4). Treatment‐emergent laboratory abnormalities were reported in two participants in Part A (high alanine aminotransferase [ALT] on Day −3, n = 1; high ALT and aspartate aminotransferase [AST] on Day 9, n = 1) and one participant in Part B (high creatinine levels on Day 19). Treatment‐emergent vital sign abnormalities were reported in two participants in Part A (hypotension on Day 1, n = 1; hypertension on Day 16, n = 1) and one participant in Part B (pyrexia on Day 11). No other clinically relevant changes in safety outcomes were recorded.

Discussion

The efficacy, safety, and tolerability of once‐daily macitentan 10 mg have been demonstrated in clinical trials and further established in real‐world settings. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 In contrast, data on the investigational dose of once‐daily macitentan 75 mg remain limited. In this study of healthy male volunteers, we showed that at steady state, once‐daily macitentan 75 mg does not clinically affect the PK of sildenafil, a CYP3A4 sensitive substrate, or riociguat or rosuvastatin, both BCRP substrates. Since tadalafil is a less sensitive CYP3A4 substrate than sildenafil, 32 , 33 it was not administered in this study (per protocol), and no change in its exposure is expected upon co‐administration with once‐daily macitentan 75 mg.

Minor PK changes were observed when once‐daily macitentan 75 mg was co‐administered with single doses of sildenafil 20 mg, riociguat 1 mg, or rosuvastatin 10 mg, consistent with findings from similar studies of once‐daily macitentan 10 mg, where minor, non‐clinically relevant PK interactions were reported. 24 , 25 The reduction in riociguat exposure observed with macitentan 75 mg (24% decrease in AUC) was comparable to that reported with bosentan (27% decrease in AUC) 34 and omeprazole (26% decrease in AUC). 35 Given riociguat's high interindividual variability (∼60%), 27 the observed 24% decrease is likely attributable to random variability rather than a meaningful effect on its pharmacokinetics. Additionally, once‐daily macitentan 75 mg at steady state was shown to have a negligible effect on the 4β‐hydroxycholesterol/cholesterol ratio, suggesting that this dose does not induce the CYP3A4 enzyme. In line with the United States Food and Drug Administration guidelines, 36 the PK effects of macitentan can be considered limited; thus, our findings suggest that dose adjustment of BCRP or CYP3A4 substrates (beyond that of individualized dosing) is not required. Concomitant administration of once‐daily macitentan 75 mg with sildenafil, riociguat, or rosuvastatin was generally well tolerated and resulted in AE profiles similar to those associated with the individual agents. Given the lack of clinically relevant PK interactions and overall favorable tolerability profile, once‐daily macitentan 75 mg can be co‐administered with sildenafil, tadalafil, riociguat, or rosuvastatin. To further enhance tolerability, a stepwise dose‐escalation approach for macitentan was implemented in this study (10 mg, then 37.5 mg, followed by 75 mg), which improved tolerability and has been adopted as a 4‐week dose‐escalation period in the UNISUS Phase 3 trial.

The current study was conducted in male individuals only; however, as sex has no clinically relevant effect on the pharmacokinetics of macitentan or aprocitentan, 37 sildenafil, 38 riociguat 39 or rosuvastatin, 40 the results should be generalizable to females. As no clinically relevant differences in the PK of macitentan have been reported in healthy volunteers versus patients with PAH, with similar overall exposure at steady state to macitentan and aprocitentan, the present findings can be extrapolated to patients with PAH. 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 An interaction between macitentan and the activin signaling inhibitor, sotatercept, is also not expected, as the potential for drug‐drug interactions with fusion proteins is very low. 45 Thus, based on available data, once‐daily macitentan 75 mg can be co‐administered in patients with PAH receiving all types of background PAH‐therapy.

Since clearance of ET‐1 from the circulation is primarily mediated by the ETB receptor, ET‐1 plasma levels can serve as a pharmacodynamic marker of ETB receptor inhibition. 31 In this study, ET‐1 plasma concentrations increased with escalating macitentan doses, reaching a plateau with macitentan 75 mg by Day 10. These findings are consistent with previous observations 31 and are aligned with the mechanistic rationale of macitentan 75 mg providing greater ETB receptor blockade than the 10‐mg dose. 17 Additional support comes from the observation that escalating the macitentan dose from 10 to 75 mg did not produce further changes in blood pressure, which are side effects mediated by ETA receptors (blockade of ETA receptors inhibits vasoconstriction effects). 38 Hence, these findings suggest that ETA receptors are already maximally inhibited at the 10‐mg dose, and any additional efficacy (and safety) effects observed with macitentan 75 mg are likely due to ETB receptor inhibition. The hypothesis that macitentan 75 mg provides greater ETB receptor blockade is being investigated in the aforementioned UNISUS trial, the first head‐to‐head superiority study in PAH.

Conclusions

In summary, concomitant administration of once‐daily macitentan 75 mg with a single dose of sildenafil, riociguat, or rosuvastatin was well tolerated by healthy male participants. The effects of once‐daily macitentan 75 mg on the PK of sildenafil, riociguat, and rosuvastatin were not considered clinically relevant. These conclusions are also considered applicable to tadalafil. Hence, concomitant administration of these drugs, representing CYP3A4 and BCRP substrates, with once‐daily macitentan 75 mg does not require any dose adjustments.
