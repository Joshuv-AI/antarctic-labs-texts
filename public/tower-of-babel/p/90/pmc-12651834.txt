Non-invasive cerebral pulse rate monitoring during two low-cardiac-output events after birth – A case report
Authors: Lukas P. Mileder, Christoph Schlatzer, Bernhard Schwaberger, Gerhard Pichler, Nariae Baik-Schneditz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Pulsatile near-infrared spectroscopy (pNIRS) allows measurement of cerebral pulse rate.•Cerebral pulse rate measurement correlates with heart rate and cardiac output.•In this patient, pNIRS guided chest compression delivery after birth.•Cerebral pulse rate holds promise as feedback parameter for neonatal resuscitation.

Introduction

Chest compressions (CC) should be initiated after 30 s of effective ventilation in neonates whose heart rate remains below 60 beats per minute.1, 2 In the rare situation of postnatal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), the recommended ratio is three CC followed by one ventilation breath, targeting at least 90 compressions and 30 ventilations per minute.1, 2 CC should be delivered using the two-thumbs technique over the lower third of the neonate’s sternum.1, 2 However, for evaluating either the quality of delivered CC or the return of spontaneous circulation, guidelines state that “current evidence does not support the use of any single feedback device in a clinical setting”.1 A scoping review by the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation Neonatal Life Support Task Force found 16 studies of CC feedback devices, twelve of which were manikin studies.3 Among the reviewed parameters, end-tidal carbon dioxide (etCO2) and arterial blood pressure were the only real-time physiological feedback parameters of CC performance in neonates.

The primary goal of CC during neonatal resuscitation is to ensure adequate end-organ perfusion. Hence, non-invasive, continuous measurement of cerebral perfusion during neonatal resuscitation could help optimizing the quality of CC. Here, we describe the clinical utilization of the recently developed pulsatile mode of the near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) device NIRO 200NX (Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan)4 during postnatal resuscitation of a neonate.

Case presentation

A female neonate was delivered at 40 + 3 weeks of gestation via vaginal birth, complicated by shoulder dystocia. She was brought to the resuscitation table apnoeic, with no audible heart rate and severely impaired muscle tone. Electrocardiogram (ECG) confirmed asystole. Despite 30 s of effective mask ventilation, her heart rate remained below 60 beats per minute, and CPR was initiated at a ratio of 3:1. As part of an ongoing observational study with deferred consent (ethics committee number 36-193 ex 23/24), using pulsatile NIRS measurements as an adjunct to standard monitoring in bradycardic neonates after birth, a neonatal NIRS sensor was applied to the neonate’s forehead. The pulsatile mode revealed a cerebral pulse rate matching the CC frequency of around 100 per minute, suggesting an effective CC rate with sufficient perfusion pressure. At this time, pre-ductal pulse oximetry monitoring did not show a reliable and stable signal. After approximately 45 s of CPR, the neonate’s heart rate increased to above 100 beats per minute, and, therefore, epinephrine was not administered. She was subsequently intubated due to minimal spontaneous breathing efforts. Following successful video laryngoscopy-guided endotracheal intubation, the fraction of inspired oxygen was quickly reduced to room air.

Two minutes after intubation, the neonate developed broad complex tachycardia with a heart rate between 200 and 220 per minute. Despite this, she remained haemodynamically stable with palpable pulses at both upper and lower extremities. While a defibrillator was prepared as a precaution, the pulsatile NIRS (pNIRS) mode again detected cerebral pulse rate, indicating stable cerebral perfusion and reassuring that emergency defibrillation was not required (Fig. 1). Tachycardia ceased spontaneously after less than 30 s and the neonate was transferred to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). There, the neonate required minimal ventilator support and was haemodynamically stable without inotropic support. However, she exhibited neurological impairment, with severely reduced muscle tone, absent spontaneous movements and cerebral low-voltage pattern. Within six hours after birth the neonate’s neurological presentation improved, with the amplitude-integrated electroencephalography showing a predominantly discontinuous pattern. After successful extubation and initiation of non-invasive respiratory support on the second day of life, she experienced two episodes of seizures mainly affecting the right upper and lower extremities, which were successfully treated with phenobarbital. Magnetic resonance imaging, performed during the second week after birth, revealed an eight-millimetre ischaemic lesion in the left thalamus. Furthermore, reduced spontaneous movements of the upper extremities led to the diagnosis of bilateral Erb's palsy, while transient left-sided phrenic nerve palsy gradually improved. The neonate was weaned from non-invasive respiratory support by the eleventh day after birth. Phenobarbital treatment was discontinued during the NICU stay, and no further seizure events occurred. She was discharged at three weeks of age, without signs of neurological impairment, except for ongoing motor function recovery from the brachial plexus injury sustained at birth. The infant had regular follow-up examinations in our neurodevelopmental outpatient clinic and showed normal motor and cognitive development at one year of age.

Discussion

Postnatal CPR is a rare event, with an incidence of 0.2 % in term born neonates5 and 2.7 %6 in moderate preterm neonates. In cases of bradycardia and subsequent low cardiac output, high-quality CC are essential to maintain cerebral and myocardial perfusion, aiming for a quick return of spontaneous circulation. However, deviations from guidelines frequently occur during CPR events in the delivery room. In the study by Boldingh et al.7 only six out of 29 neonates receiving CC postnatally actually required CPR based on video review. Achieving the adequate CC depth remains challenging,8 and delivered CC rates show a high variation and often exceed recommendations, even among experienced providers.9 When considering the high-stakes environment of the delivery room and the vulnerability of neonates after birth, these challenges are particularly concerning.

For infants and children requiring resuscitation, the American Heart Association states that monitoring including etCO2 and arterial blood pressure “may be used to assess and guide the quality of CPR”.10 In a study from the Collaborative Pediatric Critical Care Research Network including 164 resuscitated children, 60 % of whom were below one year of age, maintaining a mean diastolic blood pressure of 25 mmHg or higher during CPR was associated with improved survival and with favourable neurological outcomes.11

Blood pressure does not necessarily equal cerebral and coronary blood flow. etCO2, on the other hand, serves as a surrogate of cardiac output and pulmonary ventilation. A retrospective cohort study by Stine et al.12 found that an etCO2 threshold of 17–18 mmHg provided the highest sensitivity and specificity for predicting heart rate recovery above 60 beats per minute during CPR in neonates and children with a median age of 30 days. Correspondingly, Chandrasekharan et al.13 reported a significant relationship between etCO2 levels and CC adequacy in their lamb model of cardiac arrest.

However, there are several limitations both with etCO2 and arterial blood pressure as physiological feedback parameters during resuscitation in the delivery room. First, in the majority of neonates who require CPR after birth, CC are commenced before endotracheal intubation has even been performed.14 Additionally, (invasive) airway leakage, airway positioning, and common drugs during CPR such as epinephrine may affect etCO2 values.15, 16 Second, continuous arterial blood pressure monitoring to guide CC delivery would require rapid arterial line placement shortly after birth, which is difficult to achieve for monitoring purposes during postnatal CPR.

Based on the non-invasive measurement of relative concentrations of oxygenated and de-oxygenated haemoglobin, NIRS facilitates continuous monitoring of tissue oxygen saturation. Regional cerebral tissue oxygen saturation is influenced by oxygen content in the blood, cerebral perfusion, and tissue oxygen extraction,17 offering more diagnostic information than arterial oxygen saturation and blood pressure alone. Several studies have shown that cerebral tissue oxygen saturation monitoring can be successfully implemented into postnatal care.18 Moreover, a recent multi-centre randomized controlled phase 3 trial found an increase in survival without cerebral injury by 4.3 % in preterm neonates below 32 weeks of gestation when cerebral tissue oxygen saturation monitoring with dedicated interventions was added to standard stabilization practices after birth.19

In addition to cerebral oximetry, the pNIRS mode allows for real-time assessment of cerebral perfusion. pNIRS facilitates cerebral pulse rate measurement, which correlates with heart rate and cardiac output.4 For the measurement, the standard NIRS sensor has to be placed on the neonate’s forehead, which can be performed in short time and does not require invasive interventions such as endotracheal intubation, placement of a supraglottic airway or arterial catheterization. While there is no direct quantification of cerebral blood flow or perfusion pressure available, assessment of pNIRS waveforms may at least provide indirect information about the current state of brain perfusion.

In our patient, pNIRS was a valuable diagnostic tool during two critical events after birth. First, it confirmed that CC were effectively delivered, including a compression rate of around 100 per minute as currently recommended.1, 2 Furthermore, the cerebral pulse rate indicated sufficient perfusion pressure, based on the regular and normofrequent cerebral pulse rate. Second, during the episode of broad complex tachycardia, normofrequent pNIRS readings in combination with equally palpable peripheral pulses suggested uncompromised haemodynamics despite ventricular tachycardia.

Conclusions

So far, only single-patient case reports and small case series in adult emergency medicine have investigated and described the use of pNIRS during CPR.20 This clinical case highlights the potential of pNIRS monitoring also during postnatal resuscitation and low-cardiac-output events in neonates. While cerebral pulse rate as a physiological feedback parameter for guiding and optimizing neonatal CPR requires further investigation, we believe it holds promise for reducing neonatal mortality and morbidity.
