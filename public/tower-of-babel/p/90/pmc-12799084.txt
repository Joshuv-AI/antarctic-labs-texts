Comparative Study of Microabrasion and Resin Infiltration on Enamel Color Stability and Surface Characteristics: A Systematic Review
Authors: Ibrahim M. Shaban, Mohamed J. Jafer, Aqeil A. Nema, Aiedh S. Al Rashidi, Abdulmohsen Mashaan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:This systematic review aims to compare the effectiveness of microabrasion and resin infiltration on enamel color stability and surface characteristics in the treatment of white spot lesions.Materials and Methods:A literature search was performed in PubMed, Web of Science, MEDLINE, and Scopus for studies published between 2009 and 2023. The inclusion criteria focused on randomized controlled trials (RCTs), clinical trials, and in vitro studies comparing resin infiltration and microabrasion on color stability (ΔE values) and surface roughness (Ra). The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool 2.0 and ROBINS-I tool assessed risk of bias in RCTs and non-randomized studies.Results:Out of 352 initial records, 11 studies, involving 236 participants and 1180 teeth, met the inclusion criteria and were reviewed in detail. For clarity, the outcomes were stratified into short-term (<12 months) and long-term (≥12 months) results. Resin infiltration consistently proved superior color stability, with ΔE values ranging from 2.03 to 11.29 showing long-term aesthetic improvements lasting up to 24 months. Microabrasion displayed a variable ΔE ranging from 3.5 to 10.5 and color relapse within 6 months. Ra was lower in resin infiltration treatments with Ra values below 0.2 μm, compared to microabrasion, which showed roughness values ranging between 0.25 and 0.8 μm.Conclusion:Resin infiltration offers better long-term aesthetic outcomes in terms of color stability and surface smoothness compared to microabrasion, while microabrasion creates a rougher surface and a greater likelihood of color relapse, making it less ideal for long-term aesthetic improvements.

INTRODUCTION

Enamel discoloration is a widespread aesthetic concern that can significantly affect an individual’s self-confidence and quality of life. The causes of enamel discoloration are varied and can be categorized as either intrinsic or extrinsic. Intrinsic factors include developmental defects or iatrogenic causes, while extrinsic factors consist of stains from dietary habits, smoking, and poor oral hygiene.[1,2] The degree of discoloration can range from mild, localized defects to more severe, generalized discoloration, which can compromise the overall appearance of the teeth.[3]

Over the years, several treatment modalities have been developed to address enamel discoloration. These treatments range from conservative approaches like bleaching and microabrasion to more invasive procedures such as veneers and crowns.[4] Among these, microabrasion and resin infiltration have gained attention due to their minimally invasive nature and their effectiveness in treating enamel discoloration.[5]

Microabrasion involves the controlled removal of surface enamel using a combination of abrasive agents like hydrochloric acid and pumice slurry.[1,6] This method is commonly used for treating superficial enamel defects, such as white spot lesions (WSLs) and fluorosis.[5] However, there are concerns about increased surface roughness (Ra) and the potential for enamel to become more susceptible to staining after treatment.[7,8]

Resin infiltration, on the contrary, is a newer technique that involves the penetration of low-viscosity resin material into the porous enamel structure. This method aims to seal the porous enamel, creating a smooth surface and improving the aesthetic appearance of discolored enamel.[9] Unlike microabrasion, resin infiltration may also prevent further staining by occluding surface pores.[10]

While both techniques have shown promise in clinical settings, there is a lack of comprehensive research comparing their long-term effects on enamel color stability and surface characteristics. Thus, this systematic review compares the effectiveness of microabrasion and resin infiltration in terms of color stability (ΔE values) and surface characteristics (roughness and smoothness). While both treatments are effective in improving the appearance of affected enamel, this review aims to find which method provides superior long-term aesthetic outcomes, particularly for patients with WSLs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

RESEARCH QUESTION

The primary research question of this systematic review was: What are the comparative effects of microabrasion and resin infiltration on enamel color stability and surface properties?

This review focused on the ability of these two techniques to improve the aesthetic appearance of WSLs and maintain long-term color stability and surface smoothness.

STUDY DESIGN

This systematic review was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.[11] The following criteria were utilized to select articles in this study.

Search CRITERIA

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined to find studies that specifically compared resin infiltration and microabrasion or evaluated one of these techniques in terms of color stability (ΔE values) and Ra.

INCLUSION CRITERIA

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), clinical trials, and in vitro studies evaluating the effects of resin infiltration or microabrasion on enamel were included. These included studies published in English. Studies involved permanent teeth with WSLs or other enamel defects. Studies reported quantitative data on color stability (ΔE values) or Ra.

EXCLUSION CRITERIA

Case reports, literature reviews, opinion pieces, editorials, and conference abstracts were excluded. Studies involved primary teeth or restorative materials other than enamel. Studies did not report quantitative data on color stability or surface characteristics. In vitro studies did not mimic clinical conditions or studies that focused on materials other than microabrasion or resin infiltration.

SEARCH STRATEGY

A comprehensive electronic search was conducted in the following databases: PubMed, Web of Science, MEDLINE, and Scopus. The search included studies published between 2009 and 2023. To capture the maximum number of relevant studies, the following Boolean search terms were used:

“Resin infiltration” OR “ICON” OR “microabrasion,” “WSLs” OR “demineralized enamel lesions” OR “early enamel lesions,” “Color stability” OR “ΔE” OR “surface roughness,” “Esthetic outcomes” OR “camouflage effect.” Furthermore, a manual search of the reference lists of the included studies was also conducted to find any other relevant studies. The search was performed without any time restrictions.

STUDY SELECTION AND DATA EXTRACTION

The first search yielded 352 titles and abstracts. After removing duplicates, 229 studies remained for title and abstract screening. Two reviewers conducted the screening process independently. Studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded. A total of 14 studies were selected for full-text review, and after further assessment based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 11 studies were finally included. For each included study, extracted data comprised of study design (RCT, clinical trial, in vitro study), number of participants and teeth involved, type of enamel lesions (WSLs, fluorosis, natural demineralized lesions), treatment protocols (resin infiltration or microabrasion), follow-up duration, color stability (ΔE values), and Ra and conclusions on aesthetic outcomes.

OUTCOME ASSESSMENT

The primary outcome assessed in this review was color stability, measured by ΔE values using the CIE Lab* color system.[12] A ΔE value of 3.7 or lower was considered clinically acceptable for color difference. The secondary outcome assessed was surface characteristics, measured by Ra. A lower Ra value shows a smoother surface, which is beneficial for reducing stain accumulation and biofilm formation.

RISK OF BIAS ASSESSMENT

The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool 2.0 was used to assess the risk of bias in RCTs.[13] Studies were classified as having minimal risk, some concerns, or elevated risk based on the domains of randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result. For non-randomized studies, the ROBINS-I tool was utilized for assessing the risk of bias in non-randomized studies of interventions.[14] The seven domains, such as bias due to confounding, bias in the selection of participants, bias in the classification of interventions, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing data, bias in the measurement of outcomes, and bias in the selection of the reported results, were considered. The quality assessment tool for in-vitro studies (QUIN) tool was used to assess the quality of the in vitro study. Two reviewers independently evaluated each study, and any disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer.

DATA SYNTHESIS

Due to the heterogeneity of the included studies in terms of treatment protocols, outcome measures, and follow-up periods, a meta-analysis was not possible. Instead, a narrative synthesis was performed.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Descriptive statistics, such as the mean ΔE values and Ra, were reported for each study. The ΔE values were used to assess color stability, while the Ra values were used to assess the smoothness of the enamel surface after treatment. The ΔE values were compared against a clinically acceptable threshold of 3.7 to decide the success of each treatment.

RESULTS

A total of 11 studies were included in this systematic review, published between 2009 and 2023, forming RCTs, clinical efficiency studies, and one split-mouth RCT. The studies evaluated the effects of resin infiltration and microabrasion on the color stability and surface characteristics of enamel, particularly focusing on WSLs. These studies included a total of 236 participants and 1180 teeth, with follow-up periods ranging from post-treatment to 24 months. Below is a detailed breakdown of the findings about color stability (ΔE values) and surface characteristics (roughness and smoothness), as well as the risk of bias assessment.

The focus of the studies was on the treatment of post-orthodontic WSLs, fluorosis, and natural demineralized enamel lesions. COLOR STABILITY (ΔE VALUES)

The CIE Lab color system* was used in most studies to quantify color changes, with a ΔE value of 3.7 or lower considered clinically acceptable. Across the studies, resin infiltration was shown to provide immediate and long-lasting improvements in aesthetic outcomes, closely matching the appearance of healthy enamel.

Resin infiltration

Resin infiltration consistently proved superior color stability across all included studies. The ΔE values for resin infiltration ranged from 2.1 to 9.2, showing significant improvements in aesthetic appearance immediately after treatment, with long-term results kept for up to 24 months. Knösel et al.[15] reported ΔE values of 2.55 ± 4.24 at 6 months, which remained stable at 5.57 ± 2.62 by the 24-month follow-up, showing long-term color stability. Gu et al.[16] found ΔE values of 6.57 ± 2.48 immediately after treatment, which improved to 2.03 ± 0.82 after 12 months, proving sustained aesthetics over time [Table 2].

Microabrasion

Microabrasion showed more variable outcomes in terms of color stability, with ΔE values ranging from 3.5 to 10.5. While microabrasion was effective in improving the appearance of superficial lesions, it was notably less effective for deeper lesions, and color relapse was seen in several studies.[17,18] Shan et al.[17] reported ΔE values for microabrasion ranging from 3.94% to 45.02% after treatment, with some relapse seen after 6 months. Similarly, Kannan and Padmanabhan[18] found that color relapse occurred in some patients as early as 6 months, particularly for those with poor oral hygiene or high consumption of staining beverages like coffee or tea. While microabrasion achieved immediate aesthetic improvements, its long-term effectiveness was less predictable compared to resin infiltration, particularly for deeper lesions [Table 2].

SURFACE CHARACTERISTICS (ROUGHNESS AND SMOOTHNESS)

Resin infiltration

Resin infiltration consistently resulted in smoother surface characteristics compared to microabrasion, contributing to better stain resistance and reduced biofilm accumulation. Most studies reported Ra values below 0.2 μm, showing a smooth surface after treatment.[15,16,19,21] Ertaş et al. reported minimal Ra, with Ra values of 0.18 μm after polishing.[26] Fujishima et al.[19] found that the smooth surface created by resin infiltration enhanced stain resistance, particularly in patients consuming beverages like tea or coffee.

The smooth surface created by resin infiltration prevents the accumulation of pigments and biofilms, contributing to its long-term aesthetic success [Table 2].

Microabrasion

In contrast, microabrasion produced a rougher surface due to the mechanical removal of the superficial enamel layer. The rough surface increased the likelihood of stain accumulation over time, especially in patients with poor oral hygiene or those who consumed staining beverages. Kannan and Padmanabhan[18] reported Ra values ranging from 0.25 to 0.8 μm, depending on the depth of the lesion and the abrasiveness of the materials used. Shan et al.[17] noted that patients treated with microabrasion were more likely to experience stain accumulation within 6–12 months post-treatment due to the rougher enamel surface. While microabrasion was effective for superficial stains, the rougher surface made it more prone to staining and less durable compared to resin infiltration [Table 2].

DISCUSSION

This systematic review compared the color stability and surface characteristics of microabrasion and resin infiltration for the treatment of WSLs. The findings from the included studies show that resin infiltration consistently provides superior color stability compared to microabrasion. Studies reported ΔE values for resin infiltration ranging from 2.03 to 6.57, with significant aesthetic improvements immediately after treatment and sustained results for up to 24 months.[16,21] In contrast, microabrasion showed more variable results, with ΔE values ranging from 3.5 to 10.5.[18] However, two studies revealed color relapse within 6 months of treatment, particularly in patients with poor oral hygiene or high consumption of staining beverages.[17,18] These findings are consistent with the study conducted by Ibrahim et al.[27] which highlighted the long-term color stability of resin infiltration due to its ability to seal enamel microporosities, preventing further staining. Clinical study by Shan et al.[17] indicates that resin infiltration and microabrasion are similarly effective in diminishing the size of WSLs after 6 months of follow-up; however, resin infiltration possesses a superior aesthetic advantage compared to microabrasion. Contrarily, Elrashid et al.[28] noted that the teeth treated with resin infiltration demonstrated the best immediate cosmetic outcomes after a follow-up of 2 weeks compared to the microabrasion.

In addition to color stability, surface characteristics play a crucial role in deciding the longevity of aesthetic outcomes. Resin infiltration was found to create a smoother enamel surface compared to microabrasion, with Ra values typically below 0.2 μm. The smooth surface created by resin infiltration reduces the likelihood of stain accumulation and biofilm formation, contributing to its long-term success. In contrast, microabrasion produced a rougher surface, with Ra values ranging from 0.25 to 0.8 μm, increasing the risk of future staining. Studies conducted by Ertaş et al.[26] and Fujishima et al.[19] support this conclusion, showing that the smoother surface created by resin infiltration is more resistant to long-term discoloration. However, Rodrigues et al.[7] and Meireles et al.[8] proved that the rough surface produced by microabrasion requires more frequent maintenance to prevent re-staining.[7,8]

The clinical implications of these findings suggest that resin infiltration is the preferred treatment for patients seeking long-term aesthetic benefits. While microabrasion may be effective for superficial lesions, its rougher surface and the potential for color relapse make it less suitable for patients with more severe or deeper lesions. Studies by Yetkiner et al.[9] and Senestraro et al.[22] further corroborate the superiority of resin infiltration in providing immediate and sustained aesthetic improvements, making it a more reliable choice for treating WSLs. However, it is important to acknowledge the heterogeneity of the included studies, particularly their performance under real-world clinical conditions. In this study, outcome measures demonstrated wide variability in reporting formats, follow-up durations, and measurement techniques, precluding quantitative synthesis.

CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS

The results of this review have important clinical implications for dental practitioners. While microabrasion may provide immediate aesthetic improvements, particularly for superficial lesions, its long-term effectiveness is limited by the rougher surface it creates and the higher likelihood of color relapse. Patients who undergo microabrasion may require more frequent follow-up and maintenance treatments to preserve the aesthetic outcomes, especially if they have poor oral hygiene or consume staining beverages. In contrast, resin infiltration offers better long-term aesthetic outcomes due to its ability to seal the microporosities in the enamel and create a smooth, stain-resistant surface. This makes it the preferred treatment choice for patients with WSLs or other enamel lesions who are seeking long-lasting aesthetic results. Additionally, the fact that resin infiltration prevents further staining and color relapse makes it a more durable solution compared to microabrasion.

STUDY LIMITATIONS

Despite the promising results of this review, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the heterogeneity of the included studies in terms of treatment protocols, follow-up periods, and outcome measures made it difficult to perform a meta-analysis. Additionally, most of the included studies were short-term, with follow-up periods ranging from immediate post-treatment to 24 months. Longer-term studies are needed to fully assess the durability of both treatments. As shown in Table 3, outcome measures demonstrated wide variability in reporting formats, follow-up durations, and measurement techniques, precluding quantitative synthesis.

Another limitation is the lack of standardization in the measurement of Ra and color stability across studies. Future research should focus on standardizing outcome measures and evaluating the long-term performance of these treatments under real-world clinical conditions. Additionally, further studies should investigate the impact of patient factors, such as oral hygiene habits and diet, on the longevity of the aesthetic improvements achieved with microabrasion and resin infiltration.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, both microabrasion and resin infiltration are effective treatments for improving the aesthetic appearance of WSLs and other enamel discolorations. However, the results of this systematic review suggest that resin infiltration offers superior long-term aesthetic outcomes, particularly in terms of color stability and surface smoothness. Microabrasion, while effective for superficial lesions, is associated with higher Ra and a greater risk of color relapse, making it less suitable for long-term aesthetic improvements. Future research should focus on conducting long-term clinical trials to further evaluate the durability and effectiveness of these treatments. Furthermore, recent clinical evidence reinforces these findings, with trials confirming the sustained aesthetic benefits of resin infiltration and microabrasion over periods up to 24 months.
