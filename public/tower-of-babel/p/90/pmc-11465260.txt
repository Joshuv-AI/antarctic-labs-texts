Influence of Supercritical Conditions on Isothermal Adsorption Capacity Calculation of Methane and Model Optimization
Authors: Zhe Li, Baobin Gao, Wenjie Lei, Shujun Ma, Hao Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study of isothermal adsorption models for coal and methane under supercritical conditions helps us to understand the gas content distribution in coal seams and improve gas management in coal mines. Coal samples from the Hebi mine and Longshan coal mine were selected for this research. Using the weight method, isothermal adsorption curves of supercritical methane at temperatures of 308, 313, and 318 K were measured. The adsorption phase density of supercritical methane was obtained by using the intercept method and model fitting, and the absolute adsorption capacity of methane was calculated. Simultaneously, the pore volume and specific surface area of the coal samples were tested, and the isothermal adsorption model for supercritical methane was studied and optimized. The results indicate that using mercury intrusion, low-temperature N2, and low-temperature CO2 adsorption methods for testing coal sample pore structures allows for multiscale characterization of the coal pore structure. Based on the Gibbs excess adsorption theory, the phase density of methane obtained from the intercept method and model fitting effectively represent the isothermal adsorption curve of supercritical methane. By combination of the specific surface area and pore volume distribution of the coal with the absolute adsorption capacity of methane, the number of methane adsorption layers on the coal surface was calculated to be between 1.11 and 1.3 layers. This suggests that the isothermal adsorption model for supercritical methane on coal is not solely micropore filling or single molecular layer adsorption but primarily single molecular layer adsorption with concurrent micropore filling adsorption. Based on this adsorption mechanism, an L-DA isothermal adsorption model for supercritical methane on coal was established. The L-DA isothermal model shows good fitting results and effectively explains the adsorption characteristics of supercritical methane on coal.

Introduction

Methane, as the primary component of coalbed methane, predominantly exists in two forms: free and adsorbed, with adsorbed methane constituting approximately 80% to 90% of the coal mass.1 Given methane’s distinctive presence in coal and its inherent combustion and explosion traits, coal and gas accidents have emerged as a significant constraint on coal resource production and development in China.2−4 When temperature surpasses 190.6 K and pressure exceeds 4.59 MPa, methane within the coal seam transitions from a subcritical to a supercritical state.5 In supercritical conditions, methane behavior undergoes alterations, rendering traditional isothermal adsorption models inadequate for describing coal’s methane adsorption characteristics.6 Investigating the adsorption model of coal for supercritical methane can substantially enhance gas control in deep mines under complex conditions and furnish a robust theoretical foundation for averting coal and gas outbursts as well as for the development and prediction of coalbed methane resources.

During supercritical isothermal adsorption experiments, the isothermal adsorption curve of coal to methane often exhibits an “inflection point” phenomenon, characterized by an initial rise followed by a decline.7−9 This occurrence stems from the oversight of the excess adsorption of methane induced by environmental buoyancy during experimentation.10 When investigating the isothermal adsorption model of coal for supercritical methane, it is crucial to accurately understand the variations in the methane adsorption phase density. Three common methods for calculating adsorption phase density exist: the intercept method, the empirical equation method, and the model fitting method. The intercept method entails fitting the linear relationship between methane’s excess adsorption capacity at high pressure and gas phase density, determining the intersection point between the line and the gas phase density axis as methane’s adsorption phase density.11,12 The model fitting method utilizes established adsorption models like Langmuir, DA, and DR, leveraging Gibbs excess adsorption theory to simulate methane’s adsorption phase density for the isothermal adsorption curve.13−16 The empirical equation method involves direct or indirect computation of adsorbed methane density under supercritical conditions using approaches such as the liquid phase density method17 and Ozawa equation method.18 The isothermal adsorption model of coal for supercritical methane obtained through these methods demonstrates a satisfactory fitting under specific conditions. Nevertheless, given the intricate pore structure characteristics of coal rock and the unique adsorption behavior of methane molecules, some researchers solely assess the fitting effect of the supercritical methane isothermal adsorption curve without scrutinizing the model’s rationality and applicability.

To address the aforementioned concerns, this study employs the mercury intrusion method, the low-temperature N2 adsorption method, and the low-temperature CO2 adsorption method to characterize the specific surface area and pore volume distribution of coal samples. Isothermal adsorption experiments on coal samples under various temperature and pressure conditions were conducted to determine the absolute adsorption capacity of methane, utilizing the intercept method, model fitting method, and liquid phase density method. Leveraging the specific surface area and pore volume distribution characteristics of coal, the L-DA isothermal adsorption model for coal rock adsorption of supercritical methane has been proposed. Utilizing the L-DA model to elucidate the isothermal adsorption curve of coal supercritical methane offers a more rational approach.

Pore Structure Test of Coal Samples

As a porous medium comprised of macromolecular frameworks, coal rock inherently possesses pore and fracture structures, facilitating channels for methane adsorption, desorption, diffusion, and seepage within coal.19−21 Investigating the pore structure characteristics of coal and rock is vital for enhancing the assessment of coalbed methane resource development and deepening our comprehension of methane storage and migration phenomena. To comprehensively understand pore size distribution across various scales, coal samples were categorized into micropores (≤2 nm), mesopores (2–200 nm), and macropores (>200 nm). Mercury intrusion methods, low-temperature CO2 adsorption, and low-temperature N2 adsorption experiments were employed to delineate the pore distribution of coal samples with varying particle sizes. The mercury intrusion experiment utilized the Autopore IV9500 mercury injection instrument to measure the pore surface area and volume of coal samples. This instrument features two high-pressure stations and four low-pressure stations, with a mercury injection pressure range of 0.5–33000 psia and a measured pore size range of 30 nm to 1000 μm. Analysis of the mercury intrusion curve enables the determination of pore volume and specific surface area distribution in coal. The low-temperature N2 adsorption method was conducted using the V-Sorb 2800tp specific surface area and pore size analyzer, employing liquid nitrogen at 77 K as the adsorbent. The measured pore size range is 2–375 nm. During the experiment, the BET method was applied to determine coal’s specific surface area, while the BJH method was used to analyze pore volume and size distribution. The low-temperature CO2 adsorption method operates on the same principle as the low-temperature N2 method. Using the V-Sorb 2800tp specific surface area and pore size analyzer, CO2 gas at a temperature of 273 K was employed as the adsorbent. Compared to the low-temperature N2 adsorption method, low-temperature CO2 exhibits superior adsorption and diffusion rates at 273 K, effectively identifying micropores with diameters less than 2 nm.

Measurement of Pore Structure Using Low-Temperature N2 Adsorption Method

The type of isothermal adsorption curve can to some extent explain the pore structure properties of coal.22 According to the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) classification,23 the isothermal adsorption curves of two types of coal samples for liquid nitrogen belong to Class II adsorption isotherms. The entire adsorption process can be roughly divided into three stages: when the relative pressure is below 0.2, the isotherms of the three coal samples all protrude upward, indicating the presence of a certain amount of micropores in the coal samples. When the relative pressure is between 0.2 and 0.8, the adsorption capacity of the three coal samples shows a slow upward trend with the increase of pressure. When the relative pressure is greater than 0.8, the adsorption isotherm is significantly convex and rises rapidly, indicating the occurrence of capillary condensation within the pores of the coal sample and the presence of a certain amount of mesopores and macropores in the coal sample. Due to the influence of the inherent properties of low-temperature N2 during the testing process, there are certain defects when testing large pores with a pore size of 300 or more, which cannot be used to characterize the pore characteristics of some large pores.

Analysis of Isothermal Adsorption Experiment Results

Experimental Results of Isothermal Adsorption of Supercritical Methane by Coal

When isothermal adsorption experiments were conducted, LS and HB coal samples were taken as the research objects, and methane with a purity of 99.999% was used as the experimental gas. The experimental temperatures were 308, 313, and 318 K. A total of 13 adsorption equilibrium pressure points were set up in the experiment, with pressures of 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, and 24 MPa. The experimental pressure gradually increases from a vacuum to ensure sufficient adsorption equilibrium time. The isothermal adsorption curve characteristics of coal samples for supercritical methane under different temperature and pressure conditions were examined, and the results of isothermal adsorption experiments on different coal samples under different temperature and pressure conditions are shown in Figure 6.

Isothermal adsorption results of the gravimetric method.

Analysis of experimental results reveals that coal’s adsorption capacity rises with low-pressure increments. However, for identical coal samples under the same pressure, coal’s adsorption capacity diminishes with escalating temperature. At this juncture, the coal’s methane adsorption curve demonstrates an I-type characteristic. Notably, when the pressure hovers around 4–5 MPa, the adsorption capacity of HB and LS coal samples peaks. With further pressure elevation, methane transitions into a supercritical state. Additionally, during verification, coal’s excess adsorption wanes gradually. Elevated pressure prompts a linear decline in methane’s excess adsorption by coal, potentially leading to negative values, indicative of “reverse adsorption” phenomena. Equations 9 and 10 suggest that under low adsorption environment pressures, methane’s adsorption phase density (ρa) surpasses the gas density (ρg) in the adsorption environment, rendering the excess adsorption capacity akin to the absolute adsorption capacity. As the gas-phase methane density escalates in the coal sample environment, adsorption phase methane density (ρa) mirrors this increment. Upon reaching maximal discrepancy, the excess adsorption curve peaks. As the adsorption environment gas pressure approaches critical levels, adsorption phase methane density gradually saturates with increasing environmental pressure, stabilizing ρa at a constant value. At the point of gas-phase methane density (ρg) alignment with the adsorbed phase methane density, excess adsorption capacity becomes null. Continued environmental pressure elevation drives an upsurge in free methane density. When ρg falls below ρa, experimental excess adsorption amounts manifest as negative. Methane’s curve features an initial ascent followed by descent. Under sufficiently high environmental pressures, negative adsorption phenomena emerge.

Coal Adsorption Model for Supercritical Methane

The existing isothermal adsorption models for supercritical methane in coal mainly consist of Langmuir, L–F, DR, and DA models. The parameter fitting results for these models are listed in Table 6. Although these models demonstrate good applicability, their fitting effectiveness relies solely on the isothermal adsorption curve of supercritical methane without considering the impact of pore volume and specific surface area of coal samples during supercritical methane adsorption. To address this limitation, optimizing the isothermal adsorption model of coal for methane under supercritical conditions by incorporating the specific surface area and pore volume distribution characteristics of coal is warranted.

Determination of the Number of Adsorption Layers of Coal

By comprehensively understanding the absolute adsorption capacity, pore characteristics, and specific surface area distribution of coal samples, the adsorption mode of methane within the coal body can be elucidated. The joint characterization results of specific surface area and volume of coal samples presented in Table 2 indicate that the total pore volumes of HB and LS coal samples are merely 0.0865 and 0.0855 cm3/g, respectively, suggesting that micropore filling alone is insufficient to account for the adsorption characteristics of coal for supercritical methane. Assuming monolayer adsorption when methane molecules reach saturation in the high-pressure segment, it is postulated that methane molecules are uniformly distributed on the coal body’s pore surface, arranged in a hexagonal dense packing configuration (refer to Figure 11). In a regular hexagon with “o” as the center, a total of three methane molecules reside. Given the molecular dynamics diameter (D) of methane, the area occupied by each methane molecule in the single-molecule adsorption layer can be calculated as √(3/2)d2. By utilization of the specific surface area of the coal sample and the area occupied by a single methane molecule, the number of methane molecules necessary for achieving single-layer adsorption can be determined, thereby establishing the number of adsorption layers of methane on the coal surface (see eq 16).

Methane Adsorption Method Based on L-DA Model

As a porous medium, coal has a more abundant pore structure and developed specific surface area compared to conventional rocks. When coal adsorbs supercritical methane, its adsorption mechanism may entail a blend of Langmuir monolayer adsorption and microporous filling adsorption. Adsorption arises from the interaction between the molecules on the coal pore surface and methane molecules. The attractive force exerted by coal molecules on free methane molecules causes them to adhere to the coal surface. Coal features prominently developed micropores, abundant specific surface area, elevated surface free energy levels, and markedly higher methane adsorption capacity compared with other rocks. Methane molecules exhibit a preference for monolayer adsorption on the coal surface with the adsorption quantity increasing rapidly under low pressure. As the pressure escalates, methane molecules progressively occupy high-energy adsorption sites on the coal surface, eventually infiltrating adsorption sites within pores possessing lower energy levels. Upon reaching saturation, methane molecules primarily adhere to high-energy adsorption sites on the coal surface via single-layer adsorption, achieving saturation in their single-layer adsorption state. Concurrently, influenced by the potential energy field of select micropores, methane molecules undergo filling adsorption between the pores.

Conclusions

(1)The micropore structure of coal samples was assessed using the mercury intrusion method, low-temperature N2 adsorption method, and low-temperature CO2 adsorption method. Specifically, the mercury intrusion method evaluates the specific surface area and pore volume of macropores (>200 nm), while the low-temperature N2 adsorption method assesses the specific surface area and pore volume of mesopores (2–200 nm), and the low-temperature CO2 adsorption method characterizes the specific surface area and volume of micropores (<2 nm).

(2)High-pressure isothermal adsorption experiments using the gravimetric method were conducted to elucidate the adsorption behavior of coal for methane under supercritical conditions. Under such conditions, methane adsorption by coal demonstrates excess adsorption, and the density of adsorbed methane can be determined through the intercept method and model parameter fitting method. With the methane density of the adsorption phase established, the absolute adsorption capacity of the HB coal sample at 308, 313, and 318 K is 25.83, 25.18, and 24.01 cm3/g, respectively, while that of the LS coal sample at the same temperatures is 35.17, 33.27, and 31.66 cm3/g.

(3)Based on the specific surface area and pore volume test results of coal samples, the L-DA model for coal adsorption of supercritical methane was developed. It was proposed that the adsorption mechanism of coal for supercritical methane involves both single molecular layer adsorption and microporous filling adsorption.
