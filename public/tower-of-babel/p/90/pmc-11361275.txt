Forecasting Photo-Dissolution for Future Oil Spills at Sea: Effects of Oil Properties and Composition
Authors: Danielle
Haas Freeman, Robert K. Nelson, Kali Pate, Christopher M. Reddy, Collin P. Ward
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Photo-dissolution, the photochemical production of water-soluble species from oil, can transfer oil-derived dissolved organic carbon (DOC) from floating surface slicks to the underlying seawater. Photo-dissolution was likely a quantitatively relevant fate process for the Macondo crude oil spilled during the 2010 Deepwater Horizon spill, but the importance of photo-dissolution for other oils is poorly constrained. This study evaluated the photo-dissolution reactivities (apparent quantum yields) and modeled rates for oils with diverse physical properties and chemical compositions, including an ultra low sulfur fuel oil (ULSFO). Photo-dissolution from UV (310 nm) light was strongly positively correlated with the fraction of small, gas-oil range compounds (<C25), resulting in faster rates for lighter crudes. However, photo-dissolution rates and importance to oil mass balance varied as a function of both reactivity and properties that govern slick thickness and light absorbance. Thicker slicks (∼1 mm) of light and heavy crudes produced more DOC by photo-dissolution compared to thin slicks due to higher rates of light absorbance. However, the mass lost from thin slicks (∼1 μm) was quantitatively relevant for calculations of oil mass balance, with a modeled ∼5% loss for a simplified, hypothetical spill after 1 day of sunlight exposure. The ULSFO was unusual in its exceptionally low photo-reactivity, suggesting distinct fates for this high-spill-risk product. The results show that photo-dissolution is a relevant fate process for a wide range of oil products and that it is controlled by oil properties and composition, making possible predictions of oil fate and effects for future spills at sea.

Introduction

During marine oil spills, photo-dissolution, or the photochemical production of water-soluble species from oil, can transfer oil-derived compounds from floating surface slicks to the underlying seawater, where they join the dissolved organic carbon (DOC) pool.1−8 Photo-dissolution likely affected the mass balance of the light, sweet “Macondo” well oil spilled during the 2010 Deepwater Horizon spill (DwH) in the Gulf of Mexico (GoM), resulting in a loss of ∼8% of surface oil.1 It may also have been a contributor to the DOC anomaly detected in GoM waters over a year after DwH.9 By affecting surface oil mass balance, photo-dissolution may reduce the load of oily material remaining on the sea surface that washes ashore—a principal concern of responders. By producing DOC, it mediates the exposure of marine ecosystems to oil-derived compounds.

The next big oil spill may involve an oil with very different physical properties and chemical composition from Macondo, but we have little insight into the importance of photo-dissolution for other oils. Previous studies have shown that other oils can be susceptible to photo-dissolution,3,4,10 but these laboratory studies did not normalize the results to the amount of light absorbed by the different oils (i.e., the photon dose). This step is necessary to determine apparent quantum yields (AQYs; mol product per mol photons absorbed),11−13 parameters which quantify photo-reactivity.1,14 Without calculating AQYs, photochemical rates determined under different experimental conditions15 are not comparable. Rates observed for different oils could vary due to differences in intrinsic photo-reactivity (AQYs), but they may also vary due to differences in the slick thickness that defines the path length for light absorbance (influenced by viscosity)16,17 or differences in optical properties (i.e., light attenuation coefficients). With AQYs, comparable photochemical rates can be determined for different oils with different optical properties, present at different slick thicknesses, and exposed to different light sources.1,14 Moreover, AQYs can be used to model the photo-dissolution rates (DOC production) and effects on oil mass balance (%C dissolved) for hypothetical future spills.1

Photo-dissolution AQYs likely vary for different oils. Higher-density, heavier oils have higher abundances of aromatic compounds, polar resins, and high molecular weight asphaltenes, which strongly absorb light in the solar spectrum (280–700 nm) and are known to be photo-reactive,8,18−22 suggesting that these oils could have higher overall photo-reactivity. However, asphaltenes can also quench photochemically produced reactive oxygen species, shutting down reaction pathways for other oil compounds.23,24 This phenomenon could explain why higher overall compound degradation rates, bulk oxidation, and DOC production have been observed for lower-density, lighter oils, even without normalizing to the lower light absorbance and thinner slicks expected for these oils.3,10,19,20 Nevertheless, this hypothesis is complicated by the variety of photochemical endpoints measured in the literature, which may not be driven by the same reaction pathways.10,19,20 For example, different oil compounds may be involved in photo-polymerization reactions that combine small compounds into high molecular weight photo-products8,25 compared to photo-dissolution.2,4,8

This study investigated photo-dissolution reactivities for eight oils and modeled their photo-dissolution rates. The goal was to assess how reactivity and rates vary across oil types. First, we evaluated photo-dissolution AQYs as a function of oil physical properties and chemical composition. Then, we modeled the rate of photo-dissolution (DOC production) and effects on mass balance (%C dissolved) for a hypothetical spill. The results show that photo-dissolution affects a wide range of oil products, which may spill at sea, but that reactivities, rates, and effects on oil mass balance vary substantially as a function of oil properties and composition.

Methods

Sample Preparation

This study investigated a suite of crude and refined oil products with diverse geographical origins, uses, physical properties, and chemical compositions (Table 1). The suite included six crude oils and two refined products: a 2018 Shell Ultra-Low Sulfur Fuel Oil (ULSFO) from The Netherlands and a Cold Lake diluted bitumen. Several of these oils have been involved in major spills: Macondo well oil spilled during DwH,26 Alaskan North Slope (ANS) during the 1989 Exxon-Valdez spill,27 and Harmony during the 2015 Refugio Beach spill.28,29 The ULSFO is a ship fuel compliant with the recent (2020) International Maritime Organization (IMO) requirements intended to reduce sulfur emissions,30 making it a high-spill-risk product, as demonstrated by the recent spill of another IMO-compliant fuel (2020 Wakashio spill).31 The ULSFO is a lower-sulfur product with more high molecular weight material compared to the Wakashio oil.32

All fresh oil samples were evaporatively weathered in the dark at 65 °C with stirring to remove hydrocarbons ≤ C12, similar to the degree of weathering expected in the first days of a marine spill.33 The percent mass loss required to achieve evaporation up to this threshold for each oil was predetermined by a high-temperature simulated distillation (HTSD) analysis of the fresh oils completed by Triton Analytics (Houston, TX) (Table 1, Figure S1, Section S1).34

Oil Characterization

The evaporatively weathered oils were characterized by several basic and advanced analytical techniques commonly used in oil characterization, fingerprinting, and modeling,17,35−37 including measurement of oil density (g/mL; 25 °C)38 and dynamic viscosity (cP; 25 °C),38 diffusion-ordered spectroscopy nuclear magnetic resonance (DOSY NMR),39 comprehensive two-dimensional gas chromatography with flame ionization detection (GC × GC-FID),6,31,32,35,40 a saturates, aromatics, resins, and asphaltenes (SARA) analysis conducted by Triton Analytics using a column chromatography method modified from ASTM D200741 for saturates, aromatics, and resins and percent insoluble in heptane (IP 143) for asphaltenes,42 and optical (UV–vis absorbance) spectroscopy.1,14 Average molecular weight (Da) was determined from DOSY NMR39 and the fractions (%) of saturates and aromatics in the GC-amenable portions of each oil (C8–C38) from GC × GC-FID (Figure S2).32,35 Naperian attenuation coefficients were determined in 1 nm increments from 250 to 700 nm from UV–vis spectroscopy for photon dose calculations and photochemical rate modeling, and values at 300 (a300) and 450 nm (a450), and their ratio (a300/a450), were selected for the regression analysis discussed below (Table 1, Figure S3). Compound size distributions of fresh oils were also determined from HTSD (Figure S1).34 These data were used to calculate the cumulative weight % of each evaporatively weathered oil corresponding to small compounds (<C25 or <C15), intermediate size compounds (C25–C45) and large compounds (>C45). The C25 cutoff is commonly used to distinguish between light, “gas-oil” compounds versus “residual” compounds in fuel oils, but we also assessed a fraction with a lower cutoff of C15.36 The C45 cutoff is used to designate high molecular weight compounds that are not GC-amenable.36 Comprehensive details of oil characterization are provided in Sections S2–S8.

Irradiation

Neat oil films (590 μL, 300 μm thick) prepared on 5 cm diameter glass disks were irradiated using custom-built LED photoreactors that provide narrow (∼20 nm) wavebands of light in the UV and visible region (310, 368, 406, 450, and 514 nm) (Table S1, Figure S4).1,13 The experimental conditions ensured that the same photon dose was absorbed by the oil under each LED, allowing for a direct comparison of reactivity across wavelengths (Figure S4).1 The photon dose was determined experimentally by accounting for the irradiance at the location of the samples (radiometry), distance from the light source, exposed surface area, and slick thickness (Section S9, Supporting Information Excel File).1,11−13

Dissolution

The irradiated oil was equilibrated with filtered, autoclaved seawater (125 mL) for 24 h at 30 °C and 100 rpm alongside dark controls (Section S10).1 After equilibration, the water-insoluble oil was removed by filtering the leachate through Sterivex filters with a nominal 0.2-μm pore size.1 The filtered samples were acidified with trace-metal-grade concentrated HCl (Fisher Chemical) to pH 2–3 and the dissolved organic carbon (DOC) quantified as CO2 on a Shimadzu TOC-L CPH Total Organic Carbon Analyzer (Section S11).1,38,43

AQYs for photo-dissolution were determined by calculating the difference in DOC production for irradiated versus dark samples (Table S4) and normalizing to the amount of light absorbed (i.e., the photon dose) (eq 1). AQYs at 310 nm (AQY310) were chosen as representative of UV photo-reactivity, while AQYs at 450 nm (AQY450) were chosen as representative of visible photo-reactivity. The uncertainty analysis used for AQYs is shown in Section S12.1

Regression Analysis

A correlation analysis (Figure 1) and individual linear regressions (Figure S5–S10) were used to determine relationships between oil physical properties and chemical composition, optical properties (a300, a450, a300/a450), and photo-reactivity (AQY310, AQY450, and the ratio AQY310/ AQY450). The analyses were completed in MATLAB, using the corrcoef function44 to calculate Pearson correlation coefficients and the fitlm function45 to determine simple linear regressions at the 95% confidence level.

AQYs were plotted as a function of wavelength and fit with exponential and linear regressions; the regression with the best fit (lowest p-value) for each oil was selected. Results

Oil Properties and Composition

Evaporatively weathered oil properties and chemical composition varied widely (Table 1, Section S15), with measurements largely falling between those of Agbami (the least dense, lightest oil) and Harmony or Cold Lake (the most dense, heaviest oils). These measures were well-correlated across oil type, aligning on a spectrum from low to high density. Oil ln(viscosity), non-GC-amenable fraction (>C45), average molecular weight, fractions of resins and asphaltenes, and UV (a300) and visible (a450) attenuation coefficients were positively correlated with oil density (Figure 1). The gas-oil fraction (<C25 or < C15), fraction of SARA saturates, and the spectral ratio (a300/a450) were negatively correlated with density (Figure 1). Density was the best predictor of oil light absorbance, as quantified by a300 and a450 (p = 3 × 10–5, R2 = 0.95 and p = 4 × 10–4, R2 = 0.89 respectively) (Figures S5–S6). In contrast, the gas-oil fraction (<C25) was the best predictor of a300/a450 (p = 2 × 10–4, R2 = 0.91) (Figure S7).

A few measures of oil chemical composition did not vary predictably across oil types. The SARA fraction of aromatics, GC-amenable fractions of aromatics and saturates, and the fraction of midrange hydrocarbons (C25–C45) were largely uncorrelated with other measures of properties and composition (Figure 1, Section S16).

Photo-Dissolution Reactivities

All oils were susceptible to photo-dissolution under both UV and visible light (Figure 2, Table S4, Figure S11). Photo-dissolution AQYs varied from 0–5 mmol DOC per mol photons absorbed, with most AQYs falling in a range of 0.5–2 across wavelength and oil type. Most of the oils had nonzero AQYs up to 514 nm (green light). The exceptions were Hibernia and the ULSFO, which had nonzero AQYs up to 450 nm (blue light). The lighter crude oils (e.g., Agbami, Macondo) were the most reactive in the UV region, while the heavier crude oils (e.g., Harmony) had lower UV reactivity but similar or slightly higher visible reactivity. The ULSFO was the least reactive oil at all wavelengths. The AQY at 310 nm (AQY310) of Agbami, the oil with highest UV reactivity, was 20x that of the ULSFO. The AQY at 450 nm (AQY450) of Cold Lake, the oil with the highest visible reactivity, was 8x that of the ULSFO (Table 1).

The shapes of the AQY spectra, a selection of which are shown in Figure 2, varied across oil type. For the lighter crude oils (e.g., Agbami and Macondo), the best regression fits were exponential decays (R2 ≥ 0.97), in which AQYs decreased from the UV to the visible region. For the heavier crude oils (e.g., Harmony), AQYs did not have a monotonic relationship with wavelength. Instead, AQYs peaked in the UV-A or visible region for these oils. When the low UV-B AQY was excluded, there was a significant exponential decay relationship between AQY and wavelength for Harmony, but not for the other heavier oils (Figure 2, Figure S11). The refined products showed distinct relationships between the AQY and wavelength (Figure 2, Figure S11). The ULSFO had a significant decreasing linear relationship between AQY and wavelength (Figure 2), while the Cold Lake AQYs increased with increasing wavelength (Figure S11).

Photo-dissolution reactivity in the UV region was correlated with oil properties and composition, with lighter oils exhibiting higher reactivity. AQY310 was negatively correlated with density, midrange hydrocarbon fraction (C25–C45), non-GC-amenable fraction (>C45), and average molecular weight (Figure 1). AQY310 was positively correlated with the gas-oil fraction (<C25 or < C15) and the spectral ratio a300/a450 (Figure 1). The best predictors of AQY310 were the gas-oil range fractions quantified as < C25 (p = 8 × 10–4, R2 = 0.87) or < C15 (p = 3 × 10–4, R2 = 0.90) (Figure S8). Thus, heavier oils with high fractions of small compounds had the highest UV reactivity.

Photo-dissolution reactivity in the visible region did not have strong relationships with any of the measures of oil properties or composition (Figure 1). All linear regressions of AQY450 and oil properties or composition yielded insignificant relationships except for one: AQY450 had a weak positive correlation with the fraction of asphaltenes (p = 0.03, R2 = 0.58, Figure 1, Figure S9). Thus, heavier oils with a higher asphaltene content had higher reactivity under visible light.

The ratio of reactivities AQY310/AQY450 was higher for lighter oils (Figure 1). The best predictor of the ratio AQY310/AQY450 was a300/a450 (p = 3 × 10–4, R2 = 0.91, Figure S10). Thus, lighter oils had higher UV reactivity compared with visible reactivity, while visible reactivity was more important for heavier oils.

Photo-Dissolution Rates

Photo-dissolution rates and the effect of photo-dissolution on oil mass balance for a hypothetical spill were calculated for four oils: Agbami, Macondo, Harmony, and the ULSFO. These oils were included because (1) their AQY spectra revealed significant relationships between AQY and wavelength, and (2) their AQYs, properties, and composition span the dynamic range of the data set (Table 1). The spill scenario assumed 1 day of clear-sky summer sunlight at 30°N (representative of DwH conditions), 30 °C water temperature (as in DwH), and slick thicknesses ranging from 1 μm to 1 cm.

This range of thicknesses was chosen to quantify the sensitivity of rates to photochemical path length. However, not all thicknesses are equally likely for every oil because the minimum (terminal) thickness to which an oil can spread on the sea surface is influenced by its viscosity.16,17 Based on the formula for thickness in the widely used oil spill model, the Spill Impact Model Application Package (SIMAP), the low viscosities of Agbami and Macondo (<100 cP, Table 1) afford spreading to minimum slick thicknesses <1 μm.17 In contrast, due to the high viscosities of Harmony (115,000 cP, Table 1) and the ULSFO (9700 cP, Table 1), these oils have higher minimum slick thicknesses of ∼2.5 cm and ∼81 μm (Figure 3).17 These thresholds are marked by dotted lines in Figure 3.

Overall modeled photo-dissolution rates (integrated from 280 to 700 nm) increased with slick thickness (Figure 3A) due to higher overall light absorbance, especially in the visible region (Figure S3). For the lighter Agbami and Macondo, which are expected to form a range of slick thicknesses at sea, rates for 1-μm slicks were ∼3 mmol DOC m–2 d–1, but rates for 1 cm slicks were 10x higher (39 and 24 mmol DOC m–2 d–1 respectively). For heavier Harmony and the ULSFO, rates for slicks <81 μm and <2.5 mm (respectively) were considered unlikely to form at sea, but rates for 1 cm slicks were 48 mmol DOC m–2 d–1 and 4 mmol DOC m–2 d–1, respectively. Thus, rates for thick slicks of the light and heavy crudes were similar, but the ULSFO had notably lower photo-dissolution. Indeed, the ULSFO 1 mm rate was similar to the Agbami and Macondo 1-μm rates despite a thousand-fold longer path length.

Thin slicks, which are more likely for lighter oils, lost a greater fraction of their mass to photo-dissolution compared to thick slicks (Figure 3). For lighter Agbami and Macondo, the fractions dissolved from a hypothetical 1-μm slick were ∼4.5 and ∼4.1%, respectively, but <0.1% from hypothetical 1 cm slicks. For heavier Harmony and the ULSFO, the fractions dissolved from 1-μm slicks were considered “unlikely,” and the fractions dissolved from 1 cm slicks were <0.1%.

Discussion

Light to Heavy: Trends in Oil Properties and Composition

The evaporatively weathered oils in the data set spanned the range of typical crude oil properties and chemical composition.37,49,50 Further, the properties and composition varied consistently as a function of oil density. The lower-density, lighter oils (e.g., Agbami, Macondo) were less viscous, consisted of smaller, more saturated compounds, and had lower overall light absorbance but higher absorbance in the UV relative to the visible region. In contrast, higher density, heavier oils (e.g., Harmony) were more viscous, consisted of larger, more aromatic, and more polar compounds (e.g., resins and asphaltenes), and had higher overall absorbance in the UV and visible regions. The ULSFO was an exception to these correlations, having a low asphaltene content of 1% despite other characteristics of heavy oils (e.g., high viscosity), a finding likely attributable to the refining process that removes S-containing asphaltene compounds.31

The positive correlations (Figure 1) between attenuation coefficients and measures of properties and composition typical of heavy oils (i.e., high density, viscosity, non-GC-amenable fraction, resins, and asphaltenes) are expected based on the light absorbance properties of polar and highly conjugated compounds such as resins and asphaltenes (Section S17). These findings suggest that optical properties can be used as proxies for select oil physical properties and chemical compositions, a hypothesis that should be validated with a broader suite of crude and refined oil products.

Photo-Dissolution Precursors and Reaction Pathways

The relationships between oil reactivity and measures of oil physical properties and chemical composition provide insight into the compounds and reaction pathways involved in photo-dissolution. We found that UV reactivity (AQY310) was positively correlated with characteristics of light oils, such as higher fractions of small, gas-oil range compounds (<C25), but negatively correlated with characteristics of heavy oils, such as high non-GC-amenable fractions (>C45), density, average molecular weight, and attenuation coefficients (Figure 1). These results suggest that, in the UV region, the molecular precursors for water-soluble photo-products are small compounds, which are more abundant in lighter oils. The absence of significant relationships between AQY310 and any of the SARA fractions or GC-amenable fractions of saturates and aromatics (Figure 1, Figure S8) suggests that the size of the compound is more important than its structure. Future work should investigate specific compound classes as possible controls on photo-dissolution AQYs.

We hypothesize that UV-driven photo-dissolution occurs when photochemical oxygenation and fragmentation of small compounds generate polar photo-products that are more water-soluble compared to their precursors.2,22 Because photo-dissolution in the UV region appears agnostic to compound structure (e.g., aromaticity) and has an inverse relationship with light attenuation (a300) (Figure 1), these oxygenation reactions likely occur via indirect photochemical pathways in which reactive oxygen species nonselectively oxygenate small compounds.40,51 Due to their small size, low levels of oxygenation may increase the polarity of small compounds enough to make them substantially water-soluble.2 Larger compounds such as asphaltenes may also be oxygenated and fragmented.8,23,24 However, these photochemical reactions are likely less efficient at producing water-soluble products because of the large initial size of the parent compound, which requires greater oxygenation to increase its polarity and water-solubility.2,52 Asphaltenes are also known to physically quench reactive oxygen species, a process that shuts down reaction pathways for both the asphaltenes themselves and smaller compounds that could otherwise result in water-soluble species.23,24

The absence of significant relationships between photo-dissolution AQYs in the visible region and oil properties/composition suggests that different reaction pathways dominate at different wavelengths. AQY450 had a weak relationship (R2 = 0.58) with the fraction of asphaltenes and no significant relationships with other measures of oil properties or composition (Figure 1, Figure S9). Visible-light-absorbing asphaltenes affected by extensive fragmentation and oxygenation reactions8,22,53 may thus be precursors of water-soluble photoproducts in this wavelength range. However, the weak relationship between asphaltene content and visible light reactivity indicates that abundance alone is not the control on reactivity and implies that the molecular composition of asphaltenes, which can vary widely across oil types,54 may govern reactivity. Future work should explore relationships between photo-dissolution AQYs of asphaltenes and their molecular-level properties,8 potentially leading to a predictive understanding of the photochemical fate of asphalt-covered surfaces in the built environment (e.g., roads, parking lots, roof shingles).

These observations are specific to the photochemical production of seawater-soluble species from oil. Measurements of other photochemical endpoints, such as bulk compound depletion,18,19,21,55 photo-polymerization products,8,25 and changes to physical properties,38,56,57 might highlight different reaction pathways and different relationships with oil properties and composition.

Forecasting Photo-Dissolution: Implications for Spill Modeling

Photo-dissolution has two major effects relevant to oil fate, spill response, and ecological impacts. First, photo-dissolution transfers oil-derived compounds from the oil phase into the marine DOC pool. During DwH, photo-dissolution likely contributed to a detectable DOC anomaly in the GoM.9 Oil-derived DOC may differ in lability5,58 and toxicity3,59 from native compounds, but the effects on overall DOC quality and ecosystem health are little explored. Second, photo-dissolution acts as a removal process from floating surface slicks. As such, this process decreases the amount of oil left at the sea surface, potentially resulting in less oil to which humans are exposed, which must be recovered or burned by responders, or which could wash ashore in sensitive coastal ecosystems. During DwH, photo-dissolution was a quantitatively relevant removal process, likely resulting in a loss of ∼8% of the floating surface oil.1

The strong correlations observed between photo-dissolution reactivity and oil properties/composition represent a major step forward in our predictive capability for oil photochemistry. Currently, no oil spill model includes a parametrization of photo-dissolution for any oil, let alone the diversity of oil products, which may spill at sea.17 The relationships observed in this study between photo-dissolution reactivity and measures of oil composition (e.g., HTSD), make incorporation into oil spill models a powerful and feasible next step. For example, in the aftermath of a spill, measurements of the gas-oil fraction (<C25) from an HTSD analysis could be used as a proxy for UV photo-dissolution reactivity during a spill (Figure S8). Ultimately, the relationships between reactivity and oil properties/composition could facilitate rapid, robust predictions of oil photo-dissolution rates (DOC produced) and the fractions of oil C dissolved in future spills.

Moving beyond Deepwater Horizon: Implications for Future Oil Spills

The photon-dose-normalized results of this study show that Macondo oil spilled during DwH was not uniquely reactive to photo-dissolution. Accordingly, photo-dissolution will likely play an important role in future spills of other oils, but the effects will vary depending on intrinsic reactivity (AQYs) and properties, that control photon dose (i.e., optical properties, slick thickness). The importance of slick thickness controlling photo-dissolution (Figure 3A,B) highlights (1) the need to account for photon dose (by calculating AQYs) in experiments comparing the photo-reactivity of diverse oils and (2) the need to advance remote sensing methods for accurate determination of oil slick thickness in spill scenarios,47,60 as discussed previously.1 When the amount of light absorbed is not accounted for,15 calculations or comparisons of photo-dissolution rates should be treated with caution because rates can vary substantially as a function of slick thickness even for the same oil (Figure 3A,B). By determining AQYs, this study disentangled the effects of intrinsic reactivity and slick thickness on photo-dissolution for a range of oil types and spill scenarios.

When comparing AQYs, the Agbami crude oil stands out for its exceptionally high intrinsic photo-reactivity, with AQY310 nearly 3× higher than the next reactive oil, Macondo, and 20× higher than the least reactive, the ULSFO (Figure 2). Agbami is a Nigerian crude similar in composition to others from the Niger Delta,61 where spills are chronic and insufficiently responded to.62,63 The high photo-reactivity of this oil suggests that photochemistry may be an important fate process for spills of native oils in Nigeria and offshore waters (Section S18).

In contrast, the ULSFO stands out for its exceptionally low photo-dissolution AQYs (Figure 3). There is a high likelihood that future oil spills will involve ULSFOs due to the 2020 IMO requirement that shipping vessels use these fuels to reduce atmospheric sulfur emissions.30 Indeed, an IMO-compliant fuel spilled during the Wakashio disaster.31 Thus, understanding the susceptibility of these oils to photochemistry is highly relevant to the current and future landscape (or seascape) of marine oil spills. Our results indicate that photo-dissolution may not be an important fate process for low sulfur fuels, consistent with the analysis of field samples of the very low sulfur fuel spilled during Wakashio.31 The generalization of this result from one ULSFO to low-sulfur fuels of varying compositions should be explored in future research.

The AQYs discussed above were determined for a single photon dose corresponding to a time scale from days to weeks of exposure to 20 nm “slices” of sunlight (similar to the waveband width of the LED photoreactors) for an intermediate oil slick (100 μm).1 AQYs can vary as a function of photon dose, with higher photon doses resulting in lower AQYs.1 Thus, the estimates of photo-reactivity in this study may be low for oil exposed over short time scales (lower photon dose) and may be high for slicks exposed over long time scales (higher photon dose).

While photo-reactivities (AQYs) provide insight into which oils are likely to be affected by photo-dissolution, the role of photo-dissolution during a spill is strongly dependent on slick thickness. Due to higher rates of sunlight absorption, photo-dissolution results in much higher DOC production from thick oil slicks (Figure 3). For example, the photo-dissolution rate of a 1 cm Agbami slick (39 mmol DOC m–2 d–1) is 14× higher than that of a 1-μm slick (3 mmol DOC m–2 d–1), resulting in more total DOC produced (Figure 3A,B). However, photo-dissolution results in higher DOC production relative to the total oil carbon in thin slicks (higher %C dissolved) because light penetrates through the entire path length.1,14 In contrast, self-shading in thick slicks (attenuation as light moves deeper in the slick) results in a plateau of photo-dissolution rates at a high thickness (>1 mm) that limits DOC production relative to the total amount of oil in the slick.1,14 Thus, photo-dissolution has a far greater impact on the mass balance of thin versus thick slicks. Accordingly, the %C dissolved from a 1 μm Agbami slick (4.5%) is 700× higher than the %C dissolved from a 1 mm slick (0.006%; Figure 3).

The dependence of photo-dissolution on slick thickness has implications for spills of light versus heavy oils. Due to high viscosity, heavy oils have higher terminal slick thickness compared to light oils,16,17 meaning that they are more likely to form thick slicks. Thus, in a spill of heavy oil with high AQYs (i.e., Harmony), photo-dissolution may generate high levels of DOC but have a negligible impact on oil mass balance. In contrast, due to their low viscosity, light oils can form thick slicks but can also spread into much thinner slicks (∼1 μm). Further, light oils have high intrinsic photo-reactivity due to their high UV AQYs (Figure 2). Thus, in a spill of light oil, photo-dissolution could have a quantitatively relevant impact on oil mass balance. The interactions between photo-dissolution and other fate processes (e.g., biodegradation) and how these interactions ultimately affect the mass balance for any given spill should be further investigated (Section S19).

Overall, the results from this study provide both a conceptual and a mathematical framework to understand the relevance of photochemistry for spills of diverse oil products. The results show that photo-dissolution is a relevant fate process for a wide range of oils that may spill at sea and that the impact on oil fate varies depending on oil properties and composition. Incorporation of these results into oil fate models and further investigation of the effects of photo-dissolution DOC will improve our predictive capability for oil fate and effects, facilitating more accurate damage assessments and more judicious spill responses moving forward.
