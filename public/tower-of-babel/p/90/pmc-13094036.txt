Impact of disability and selected factors on the quality of life in the older people in southeastern Poland - a cross-sectional study
Authors: Agnieszka Sozańska, Bernard Sozański, Anna Wilmowska-Pietruszyńska, Agnieszka Wiśniowska-Szurlej
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPopulation ageing is associated with an increase in the prevalence of disabilities. Improving the quality of life (QoL) of older people has become an inevitable requirement of civilisational and social progress. This study aimed to analyse the relationships between areas of disability, QoL domains, and selected socio-demographic factors.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study conducted in a representative population of people aged 65 and older living in south-eastern Poland. The primary outcomes were the level of disability measured by the WHODAS 2.0 questionnaire and the level of QoL in the physical health, psychological, social relationships and environment domains measured by WHOQOL-BREF. Information on selected sociodemographic factors was also collected.ResultsA total of 3276 complete questionnaires were included in the analysis. The highest average level of disability was noted in the domain of Life activities: household (34.80), while the lowest QoL was in the physical domain (58.31). Women generally had lower QoL compared to men. Increasing age was significantly associated with lower QoL in the physical domain. Lower education was associated with lower QoL in the physical, social, and psychological domains. Single individuals generally had lower QoL in the psychological, social, and environment domains. Physical activity of at least 150 min/week and regular exercise were associated with significantly better QoL in all domains.ConclusionsData on the relationship between QoL and functional disability are particularly important in the face of ageing population and the related challenges for countries worldwide. The results of our study could serve as a reference point for formulating health and activation programmes for older people to promote active and healthy ageing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26898-0.

Background

In recent years, there has been a steady increase in the number of older adults and people with disabilities across Europe and worldwide. The main reasons are the rise in average life expectancy and the decline in birth rates [1–3]. This trend experienced a slight deceleration during the COVID-19 pandemic; however, it remains evident and ongoing [4]. In Poland, the percentage of people aged over 60 is expected to increase significantly, reaching 10.8 million by 2030 and 13.7 million by 2050, accounting for about 40% of the total population [5].

The prevalence of disability among older adults in Europe is about 14.0%, with Poland being one of the countries with the highest disability rates [6]. Disability affects the quality of life (QoL) of older adults, influencing various aspects of their daily functioning, social integration, and overall well-being [7–9].

Research from different parts of the world indicates that disability limits the ability to perform both complex and basic daily activities to varying degrees and is often associated with older age, low income, low physical activity, multimorbidity, and depression [10–15]. Studies also highlight the role of factors such as gender and age, showing that older women have a higher prevalence of disability than older men [16]. The incidence of disability among older adults increases with age and becomes particularly evident after the age of 80 [17].

Another factor significantly impairing the functioning of older adults is multimorbidity, which occurs in nearly 70% of individuals aged 65 and over. Multimorbidity significantly increases the risk of functional limitations and worsens disability in older adults [18, 19]. Increasing disease burden is associated with higher disability levels and poorer quality of life, particularly when somatic and mental disorders such as depression coexist [18]. Multimorbidity and disability form a vicious cycle whereby functional decline accelerates health deterioration and reduces overall quality of life [20, 21]. Disability among older adults leads to a significant increase in healthcare costs [22]. Older adults with disabilities tend to reduce their physical and instrumental activity levels, which consequently leads to social exclusion [11, 23–25].

During the COVID-19 pandemic, older adults with multiple diseases and functional limitations may have experienced a further deterioration in their quality of life. The negative effects of social isolation, limited access to medical care, and difficulties in continuing treatment for chronic conditions further exacerbated the interaction between disease and disability [26]. Studies have shown that multimorbidity increases sensitivity to stress associated with lockdowns, which was linked to greater deterioration in mental and physical functioning [27]. In addition, the pandemic revealed differences in the resilience of older adults with multimorbidity to the negative effects of isolation, which had a significant impact on their well-being and quality of life [28].

The principles of the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) provide a framework for analysing the relationship between disability and quality of life. In the ICF model, disability is defined as limitations within elements of the biopsychosocial framework (body functions and structures, activity, participation, and environmental and personal factors) that may negatively affect quality of life [29–31]. The use of tools based on ICF categories, such as WHODAS 2.0, enables a comprehensive assessment of the level of disability and its impact on QoL, ensuring a consistent and holistic approach to the health and well-being of older adults.

Studies have shown that factors associated with higher levels of disability in older adults negatively affect their QoL, both directly and indirectly [32–35]. Limitations in social participation, mobility, and social interactions can adversely impact QoL [36]. As the global population continues to age, there is an increasing need for population-based studies to clarify the complex interactions between health-related QoL and disability [37].

Given the ongoing ageing of societies worldwide, it is necessary to expand research aimed at introducing systemic changes to improve the functioning and quality of life of older adults. A thorough understanding of the issues related to functioning, disability, and quality of life among older adults in relation to various sociodemographic and health-related factors is crucial. This understanding is particularly important for planning and implementing medical, care, social, and other support programmes for older adults. Therefore, our study aimed to analyse the relationships between the domains of disability measured by WHODAS 2.0 and QoL assessed by WHOQOL-BREF, as well as the impact of selected sociodemographic factors on the domains of QoL measured by WHOQOL-BREF.

Methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional survey conducted among a representative sample of older adults living in southeastern Poland, in the Podkarpackie Voivodeship.

Setting and procedures

The study was conducted using the Random Route method among individuals aged 65 and over residing in the Podkarpackie Voivodeship in southeastern Poland. Participants were selected through a multi-stage cluster sampling method. The first clusters were chosen based on the region of residence and the size of the locality. Next, single clusters and individual households were randomly selected. The Podkarpackie Voivodeship was divided into four subregions [38], from which large, medium, and small towns and villages were randomly chosen. Localities were coded as rural vs. urban according to the official TERYT urban–rural classification of Statistics Poland (GUS), where rural areas comprise rural gminas and rural parts of urban–rural gminas. In each randomly selected town and village, an initial address was drawn, which served as the starting point for the survey. The starting address in each locality was randomly selected from the official municipal register/list of streets and addresses (local address registry). The interviewer began the survey from this address. Subsequent addresses were selected by moving to the right from the first address, choosing every fourth address until the estimated number of interviews was completed.

Participants

The study was conducted among individuals aged 65 and over living in the Podkarpackie region in southeastern Poland. The inclusion criteria were: age 65 and over, normal cognitive function (Abbreviated Mental Test Score, AMTS > 6 points), and informed consent to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria were: age below 65, cognitive impairment (AMTS ≤ 6 points), lack of informed consent, and residence in institutional or healthcare facilities. Cognitive status was assessed during the home visit using the AMTS administered by trained interviewers at the beginning of the interview. Only participants scoring > 6 points were considered cognitively intact and included in the study; individuals with AMTS ≤ 6 were excluded from further participation.

The Podkarpackie Voivodeship has 304,600 residents aged 65 and over [39]. The sample size assumptions included a 95% confidence level (0.95) and a maximum estimation error of 2%. It was assumed that the total planned number of participants should be at least n = 3000. Ultimately, a total of 3500 individuals were examined, and after verifying the completeness of the collected data, 3276 questionnaires were included in the analysis (Fig. 1 – Flow diagram). Procedure

The study was conducted using the Paper and Pencil Interview (PAPI) method between 2021 and 2024. Interviews were carried out by appropriately trained interviewers at the participants’ places of residence. Individuals residing in care or healthcare facilities were not included in the study.

Variables

Information was collected on age, gender, place of residence, education, marital status, and monthly income per capita (PLN). Data were also collected on physical activity causing at least light fatigue (e.g. housework, gardening, brisk walking, sports) for a minimum of 30 min a day (at least 150 min per week), as well as on physical exercises aimed at strengthening muscles and improving physical fitness and endurance, and on social activities (participation in senior clubs, religious or cultural organisations, etc.).

Older adults were asked about diseases diagnosed by a physician in accordance with the ICD-10 classification, pain intensity (assessed using the VAS scale: 0 – no pain, 10 – unbearable pain), and underwent height and weight measurements.

The primary outcomes analysed were the levels of disability measured using the WHODAS 2.0 questionnaire and the levels of QoL in the physical health, psychological, social relationships, and environment domains measured using the WHOQOL-BREF.

Measurement

To assess disability among the participants, the 36-item version of the WHODAS 2.0 was used. To evaluate participants’ QoL, the shortened version of the WHOQOL-BREF was applied. In addition, metric, sociodemographic, and health-related data were collected, as described in the Variables section.

World Health Organization Disability Assessment Schedule 2.0 (WHODAS 2.0)

The 36-item WHODAS 2.0 was used to assess disability among the participants. WHODAS 2.0 is a standardised and uniform tool based on the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) framework. This instrument enables a general assessment of disability across six domains: Cognition (D1), Mobility (D2), Self-care (D3), Getting along (D4), Life activities – household (D5.1), and Participation (D6).

The domain of Life activities – work/school was not analysed in this study, as most participants were no longer employed. The WHODAS 2.0 uses a five-point response scale, where 1 = no problem, 2 = mild problem, 3 = moderate problem, 4 = severe problem, and 5 = extreme problem. The results were converted to a scale ranging from 0 to 100. Disability levels for the entire population can be presented on a qualitative scale according to the ICF: 0–4% (no disability), 5–24% (mild disability), 25–49% (moderate disability), 50–95% (severe disability), and 96–100% (extreme disability) [40].

In a previous study, we confirmed the high psychometric properties of the WHODAS 2.0 in a survey of older adults in Poland [41].

World Health Organization Quality of Life – BREF (WHOQOL-BREF)

The WHOQOL-BREF was used to assess participants’ quality of life (QoL). It consists of 26 questions and enables assessment across four domains: physical health, psychological, social relationships, and environment. Responses were rated on a five-point scale indicating the level of difficulty or problem. The results were calculated according to the user manual, on a scale ranging from 0 to 100, where 0 indicates the worst quality of life and 100 the best [42]. The WHOQOL-BREF has been validated in the Polish population and is available for download from the WHO website [43].

Sociodemographic and health-related data were collected using a specially designed interview questionnaire. The following variables were included in the analysis: age, gender, marital status, place of residence, education, physical activity, physical exercise, social participation, number of diagnosed chronic conditions, pain, height, and weight. Physical activity was assessed by self-report as engaging in any activity causing at least light fatigue (e.g., housework, gardening, brisk walking, sports) for ≥ 30 min per day and was dichotomized as active (≥ 150 min/week) vs. inactive (< 150 min/week). Social activity was defined as self-reported participation in any organized social activities (e.g., senior clubs, religious or cultural groups) and coded as socially active vs. socially inactive (no participation).

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the University of Rzeszów (Resolution No. 8/05/2021). All participants were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study and were advised of their right to withdraw at any stage. Prior to the interview, participants provided written informed consent to take part in the study.

Statistical methods

The collected data were analysed using TIBCO Software Inc. (2017) Statistica (data analysis software system), version 13. Descriptive statistics were used for the preliminary analysis. The normality of the distribution of quantitative variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. To compare differences in the distribution of specific variables across age groups, the Kruskal–Wallis test and the chi-square test of independence were applied.

The direction and strength of the relationship between the level of disability and QoL were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (due to the non-normal distribution of variables). Linear regression models were used to determine the effects of sociodemographic factors on participants’ QoL in specific domains. The level of statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Lifestyle factors showed consistent associations across all QoL domains. Physical inactivity (< 150 min/week) was linked to lower QoL in physical (–2.382), psychological (–1.229), social (–0.986), and environmental (–0.844) domains. Not performing strengthening/fitness exercises was similarly associated with poorer QoL, with the largest deficit in the environmental domain (–3.437). Social inactivity was related to reduced QoL in every domain (B ≈ –2.0 to –2.5).

Health burden was another strong predictor: each additional chronic condition decreased QoL in all domains (most notably physical: –1.250, and environmental: –1.045). Higher BMI was associated with lower QoL across domains, with the strongest effect in physical QoL (–1.852 per 1 kg·m⁻2). Pain intensity affected QoL only in the physical domain (–0.156 per VAS point) (Table 4).

Discussion

As societies are ageing, we sought to expand our understanding of the relationship between various areas of disability and QoL, as well as the influence of different factors on the domains of QoL.

In the 65–74 age group, the strongest significant correlation was observed between the level of disability in Do2. Mobility and Do6. Participation, measured by WHODAS 2.0, and QoL in the physical domain (–0.614). Mobility is an important predictor of disability and QoL in older people [44]. Studies show that mobility limitations, such as slow walking speed, correlate with poorer quality of life and reduced life space in older people [45, 46].

Participation in social life is associated with better QoL and overall physical well-being. Older people may face environmental barriers that preclude full participation in social activities, negatively affecting their health-related QoL [47]. Active participation in social life generates more physical activity and is closely linked to a sense of physical health within the physical domain [48, 49].

In addition, social factors such as community support and belonging enhance motivation and enjoyment of physical activity, which can lead to sustained participation and better QoL in the physical domain [50]. Social groups have their own dynamics. The presence of active individuals in a group increases activity among group members [51]. Moreover, social activity can reduce feelings of isolation and loneliness, further encouraging physical activity [50].

In the age groups 75–84 and 85 and older, the strongest significant correlation was observed between the level of disability in the domain Do2. Mobility and QoL in the physical domain (–0.635 and –0.731, respectively). Mobility is a crucial factor affecting QoL in older adults, especially those aged 75 and older. Mobility limitations affect about 35% of people aged 70 and older, and most older adults over 85. Mobility restrictions are associated with an increased risk of falls, hospitalisation, and reduced QoL [52, 53]. Maintaining physical fitness, especially the ability to walk safely, significantly contributes to better health perception and QoL in older age. Apart from efforts to maintain and improve the physical activity of older adults, implementing technological solutions for personal use and in social spaces is necessary to support the mobility of older adults [54, 55].

Our study confirmed that women generally had a lower QoL compared with men, particularly in the psychological, social, and environment domains. The tendency for older women to report lower psychological well-being than men is also confirmed by other research findings. Women achieve lower scores in self-acceptance, autonomy, having a purpose in life, and environmental control [56]. Studies indicate that women receive lower scores in mental health assessments and are more susceptible to experiencing psychological stress and symptoms of depression [57–59].

Older women are more sensitive to factors arising from the social environment than men. Women who feel low social support more often report poor self-assessment of health status compared with men. Therefore, this suggests that social support is a significant factor affecting their overall well-being [60]. Other reasons for the poorer QoL in older women within the social domain may result from more frequent health problems, negatively affecting their ability to engage in social activities [61, 62], or poorer economic conditions limiting participation in social life [63]. Moreover, social isolation, often associated with the loss of a spouse, may also contribute to poorer social QoL in older women [64, 65].

The lower QoL of older women in the environment domain compared with men is also confirmed by other studies [66]. This situation particularly affects women living alone or with others (excluding a spouse) [67]. Moreover, previously mentioned key factors such as chronic illnesses and impairments in body functions and structures are also crucial, particularly in environments with poor accessibility for people with special needs [35, 68, 69].

Our results show that the age of the participants significantly affected quality of life scores in the physical domain, where with each additional year, QoL scores in this domain decreased by an average of 0.406 points. This decrease can be attributed to age-related deterioration in muscle strength, physical and cognitive fitness, the presence of chronic diseases, and more frequent psychological and emotional problems, leading to a poorer perception of health and living conditions [70–74]. Studies have shown that maintaining higher levels of physical activity in older adults effectively improves physical fitness and related QoL despite increasing age [73, 74].

Our research also confirmed the link between the level of education and QoL. Lower education was associated with lower QoL among older adults in the physical, psychological, and environmental domains. Studies by other authors confirm that a lower level of education is associated with poorer health parameters, more frequent disability and chronic diseases, and lower levels of physical activity, all negatively affecting health-related QoL [75–77]. Education contributes to better social engagement, which is crucial for maintaining a higher quality of life. Higher education levels are associated with better psychosocial functioning and less social withdrawal [78]. A lower level of education is linked to higher stress levels and lower subjective well-being. Research shows that older adults with lower education report higher levels of depression and cognitive stress, adversely affecting their psychological QoL [77, 79]. Higher education is associated with better cognitive health and lower rates of cognitive decline, which also affects QoL in the psychological domain [80]. This relationship is also evident in the environmental domain. People with lower levels of education are more likely to experience limited access to environmental resources such as social infrastructure, healthcare, public spaces, and communication technologies, which translates into a lower sense of security and satisfaction with living conditions [81]. Research indicates that higher education is associated with a better perception of quality of life in the environmental domain, explained by greater knowledge, health awareness, and the ability to use available services and public spaces [82–84].

Our study results indicate that single individuals had, on average, lower QoL scores in the psychological (by 1.338 points), social (by 1.712 points), and environment (by 0.829 points) domains compared with those living with a spouse/partner. Loneliness is associated with poorer mental health, lower physical activity, fewer social interactions, and consequently, social isolation, all strongly correlated with lower QoL [85–88].

We confirmed in our study that maintaining an appropriate level of physical activity and regular exercise significantly influences the assessment of QoL. Older adults whose physical activity was less than 150 min/week had, on average, QoL scores 2.382 points lower in the physical domain, 1.229 points lower in the psychological domain, 0.986 points lower in the social domain, and 0.844 points lower in the environment domain than those whose physical activity was at least 150 min/week. A similar pattern was observed among those exercising less than three times per week. The results strongly highlight the role of both overall physical activity and exercise in maintaining a higher QoL for older people. To achieve a significantly higher level of QoL, substantial engagement in physical activity is required. Research by other authors confirms that regular physical activity is associated with better physical health outcomes, including a reduced risk of numerous chronic diseases and cognitive and emotional disorders [89]. Better health also provides opportunities to maintain stronger social bonds [90].

In our study, we demonstrated that socially inactive individuals generally had lower QoL scores across all domains compared with socially active individuals, with the average difference in each domain exceeding two points. Other researchers confirm the role of social activity in achieving higher QoL scores in various areas, including physical health, mental well-being, and social relationships [91]. Participation in social activities such as religious groups and formal group classes is positively associated with better overall QoL.

The incidence of chronic diseases in the studied group was high, with an average of 4.73 conditions per participant. We confirmed that with each additional disease, there was a significant decrease in QoL across all domains. The subjectively perceived level of pain affected QoL in the physical domain, where QoL decreased by an average of 0.156 points for each 1-point increase in pain level. Other studies also confirm a strong association between the occurrence of chronic diseases and lower satisfaction with life and overall quality [92].

All the factors analysed above, related to disability and QoL among older adults, interact with one another. The findings observed in our study, as well as in studies conducted by other authors, indicating significant levels of disability and low QoL in this social group, are concerning. Analysing the interactions between the key factors affecting QoL and implementing initiatives aimed at optimally influencing the health and functioning of older adults, using the biopsychosocial model promoted by the WHO, is crucial.

Because the study was conducted between 2021 and 2024, the interpretation of our findings takes into account the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic and the subsequent post-pandemic period. Lockdowns and social distancing measures reduced physical activity and social contact among older adults, which European studies have linked to a decline in quality of life assessed with the WHOQOL-BREF, particularly in the physical and social domains [93, 94]. This may explain the strong association observed in our study between social participation and physical quality of life in the 65–74 age group, as well as the dominant role of mobility in older cohorts, consistent with documented declines in activity and physical fitness during the pandemic [95, 96]. Interruptions in healthcare services and unmet health needs among individuals with multiple chronic conditions may also have reduced quality of life across all domains [97]. Even after restrictions were lifted, many older adults continued to limit social contact and remained cautious, which may have contributed to persistently lower quality of life in the post-pandemic period. Overall, our findings reflect not only the usual challenges associated with ageing but also the additional burdens related to the pandemic, highlighting their public health and social relevance.

Benefits and limitations

The study has several important strengths. Firstly, it included a large, representative sample of older adults (over 3,200 respondents), which increases the reliability of the findings. The use of WHO-recommended instruments, such as WHODAS 2.0 and WHOQOL-BREF, ensured high measurement accuracy and allowed for the comparison of results with other international studies. In addition, the inclusion of numerous sociodemographic factors and consideration of the COVID-19 pandemic context enabled a comprehensive analysis of the quality of life and functioning of older adults.

At the same time, the study has certain limitations. Its cross-sectional design prevents causal conclusions from being drawn. Older adults living in care facilities or social welfare homes were not included, which limits the generalisability of the findings. The lack of information on the season during which the study was conducted may be relevant given the potential seasonal fluctuations in well-being, quality of life, and physical activity. Data on the variable ‘monthly income per person’ were incomplete, as some participants chose not to answer this question due to its sensitive nature, which to some extent limited the analysis of socioeconomic status.

The regression models also did not account for all potential confounding factors, such as medication use, social support structures, or recent life events, although it should be noted that many important accompanying variables were included. The study also did not include objective measurements taken with devices (e.g. anthropometric data or physical activity levels) and was based mainly on self-reported information.

In light of the advantages and limitations presented, it is also worth indicating directions for future research. Firstly, future studies should incorporate both WHO-recommended instruments and other scales that allow for a detailed assessment of selected aspects of mental, social, and spiritual well-being. Expanding the scope of sociodemographic data (e.g. household structure, economic status, access to healthcare, and use of digital technologies) could enhance the analysis of factors differentiating the quality of life of older adults.

Longitudinal studies are also recommended to monitor changes over time and analyse the long-term effects of multimorbidity and functional limitations, including within the context of crises such as pandemics. Future projects should also include older adults in institutional care, as well as somatic measurements (e.g. waist circumference, body composition, and eating habits) and a more multidimensional assessment of pain and other conditions affecting quality of life.

Additionally, future research should consider seasonal variation and assess functioning and quality of life in relation to different times of the year, as these factors may significantly influence the well-being and daily activities of older adults.

Conclusions

Based on this cross-sectional study, disability was associated with lower quality of life in older adults, particularly in the physical, psychological, and social domains. The strongest links between disability and quality of life were found for social participation and mobility, underscoring the central role of these areas in older people’s well-being. Sociodemographic characteristics—gender, age, education level, and marital/partnership status—remained significant correlates of quality of life, while both physical and social activity were consistently related to higher quality of life across all domains. In contrast, multimorbidity and obesity were associated with poorer quality of life, highlighting the importance of including these health burdens in future research. Overall, our findings provide evidence that may inform the design of health and activation programmes for older adults, supporting active and healthy ageing.
