Quantitative 161Tb SPECT/CT imaging for dosimetry using a ring-based digital CZT camera
Authors: Eline Zoetelief, Marcel Segbers, Johannes Hofland, Anita A Harteveld, Tessa Brabander
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground161Tb-labeled compounds are emerging as promising alternatives to 177Lu-labeled compounds for radioligand therapy (RLT). The assessment of RLT safety and efficacy rely on accurate dosimetry, which necessitates quantitative activity measurement from multiple SPECT acquisitions. However, no studies to date have assessed 161Tb quantification using the ring-based cadmium-zinc-telluride (CZT) digital SPECT/CT systems, nor have optimal reconstruction parameters been established. This study aimed to optimize 161Tb SPECT reconstruction parameters for a CZT-based system.MethodsA phantom study was performed using the NEMA imaging quality phantom with the standard sphere set (diameters 10–37 mm), with the background prepared either with a 10.5:1 activity ratio or without activity. Imaging was conducted on a StarGuide (GE Healthcare) ring-based CZT digital SPECT/CT system using the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. Scans were repeated over activity levels up to 2.2 GBq. Three reconstruction methods were evaluated: OSEM, Q.Clear without regularization term (QClearN) and Q.Clear with regularization term (QClearR), each tested with multiple iteration-subset combinations. To compare the reconstruction methods, recovery coefficient (RC), noise levels and spatial resolution were assessed.ResultsRC values increased with sphere size and number of updates. At 864 updates, RC ranged from 0.56 to 0.95 for OSEM, 0.52–0.92 for QClearN, and 0.52–0.92 for QClearR. Noise levels, quantified by the coefficient of variation, in OSEM increased from 0.07 to 1.16 with increasing number of updates, while the noise levels of QClearN and QClearR ranged from 0.07 to 0.40 and 0.07 to 0.38, respectively. Spatial resolution was assessed using the full with at half maximum (FWHM) derived from matched-filter analysis of a digital phantom, with values of 7.9–17.0 mm for OSEM, 8.7–17.7 mm for QClearN, and 8.8–17.8 mm for QClearR. Gibbs artifact appeared in all methods, intensifying with higher number of updates.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated the feasibility of quantitative 161Tb imaging using a ring-based CZT SPECT/CT system. Recommended number of updates are 48–96 for OSEM and approximately 200 for QClearN and QClearR. With these settings, 161Tb achieved higher RC values and improved spatial resolution compared to published 177Lu data, supporting its potential as a substitute for 177Lu for image-based dosimetry in RLT.Keywords: Terbium-161, Cadmium-zinc-telluride (CZT), SPECT/CT, StarGuide, Radioligand therapy (RLT)

Introduction

For patients with a neuroendocrine tumor or prostate cancer, treatment with radioligand therapy (RLT) using 177Lu-labeled compounds can prolong progression free survival, but unfortunately, patients are rarely cured [1–3]. This may be attributed to the physical characteristics of lutetium-177 (177Lu), which emits medium-energy β−-particles that predominantly deposit their radiation dose outside the target cell. As a result, its efficacy in eradicating micrometastases and single tumor cells is reduced, potentially limiting curative outcomes [4–6]. Moreover, the growing number of clinical therapies using 177Lu is contributing to an increased demand, which might surpass its production capacity. Hence, there is a need to further improve RLT, for example by exploring alternative radionuclides.

The radionuclide terbium-161 (161Tb) is attracting increasing interest as a potential alternative to 177Lu, given their comparable chemical and physical properties (Table 1), as well as similar pharmacokinetic and biodistributions profiles demonstrated in preclinical studies [7]. Despite their similarities, certain differences between these radionuclides could make 161Tb more advantageous for therapeutic use. Notably, 161Tb has higher abundance of low-energy conversion- and Auger electrons. These electrons have a short tissue penetration, which theoretically results in a higher absorbed dose within the target cell [4, 5]. As a result, the same absorbed dose could be delivered using an approximately 28% lower activity of 161Tb compared to 177Lu [8].

The first clinical studies using RLT with 161Tb have already been performed [9–12], and additional phase I/II and prospective studies are currently running [13–15]. A pivotal element of these studies is the use of dosimetry to evaluate safety and efficacy of the new radioactive drug [10, 16]. Dosimetry requires SPECT/CT imaging at multiple timepoints following the administration of RLT. Currently, different types of SPECT/CT system are commercially available. To date, clinical research involving 161Tb has primarily used conventional SPECT/CT systems with sodium iodide (NaI) scintillation detectors for imaging. Emerging ring-based SPECT/CT systems employ cadmium-zinc-telluride (CZT) detectors and fixed dual-channel tungsten collimators. This system is equipped with twelve swiveling CZT-based detectors positioned around the patient. The digital CZT-based detectors can directly convert gamma photons into an electrical signal, potentially resulting in a superior sensitivity, higher energy resolution and higher spatial resolution compared to conventional NaI-based SPECT systems [17–22]. In addition, the ring-based CZT SPECT/CT system implemented at our center (StarGuide, GE Healthcare) is equipped with the block sequential regularized expectation maximization (BSREM) reconstruction algorithm (Q.Clear), already known as a reconstruction method applied for PET images [23–25]. The advantages of BSREM over conventional ordered subset expectation maximization (OSEM) reconstruction is the ability to control noise while running full convergence, by using a penalty regularization term that balances spatial smoothness and preservation of edges [26–28]. Two types of penalty regularization terms can be applied: the relative difference prior (RDP) and median root prior (MRP). Both types aim to suppress noise by comparing each voxel values to adjacent voxels, with RDP using the relative differences and MRP using median differences [29].

While imaging protocols for 177Lu have been extensively studied using the new ring-based digital CZT SPECT/CT system [17–19], comparable investigations for 161Tb remain limited thus far. In clinical studies exploring RLT, accurate dosimetry is essential, relying on precise quantification of the activity of the radionuclide using SPECT/CT imaging. To our knowledge, no studies have evaluated the feasibility of quantifying 161Tb using a ring-based digital CZT SPECT/CT system, nor established an optimal acquisition or reconstruction protocol for this purpose. Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine optimal SPECT reconstruction parameters for 161Tb on a ring-based digital CZT SPECT/CT system for imaged-based dosimetry application by investigating several parameters related to image quality and image quantification, including noise, spatial resolution and recovery coefficients.

Materials and methods

Activity calibration

Prior to this study, the VIK-202 dose calibrator (Comecer, Castel Bolognese, Italy) was calibrated using unopened batches of 161Tb provided by the vendor, based on the vendor-specified activity reference time, as previously detailed by Ntihabose et al. [30]. Measurements were performed using the 99mTc channel (scale 1.0, factor 236) and a conversion factor of 1.10. The 161Tb was supplied by TerThera (Breda, the Netherlands) in a 10 mL quartz-coated vial. An identical vial type was used for both the calibration of the dose calibrations and the preparation of doses for the phantom experiments.

Phantom preparation

A Flangeless 6.2 ± 0.1 L Deluxe Jaszczak phantom without inserts (inside diameter 209 mm and height 188 mm) was used for calibration. The Jaszczak phantom was filled with a solution of 0.36 MBq/mL 161Tb. To prevent radioactivity from sticking to the phantom the 161Tb was labeled with DTPA.

A NEMA imaging quality phantom (NEMA IQ) with sphere set (10, 13, 17, 22, 28 and 37 mm) and without lung insert was used to investigate quantitative and qualitative parameters. The spheres were positioned counterclockwise in decreasing size order with largest sphere (37 mm) at the top center. The spheres were filled with water containing 2.1 MBq/mL DTPA-labeled 161Tb, supplemented with 0.4 mL sodium acetate (2.5 M, pH 5) per 100 mL water to prevent sticking. The NEMA IQ phantom was scanned with (hot) and without (cold) background activity. The hot background was prepared with water containing 0.20 MBq/mL DTPA-labeled 161Tb, supplemented 0.4 mL sodium acetate (2.5 M, pH 5) per 100 mL water, resulting in a sphere-to-background ratio of 10.5:1.

Image acquisition

The phantoms were scanned in a ring-based digital CZT SPECT/CT (StarGuide, GE Healthcare, Haifa, Israel). For each acquisition, a low-dose CT scan was performed first, directly followed by the SPECT acquisition of one bed position. SPECT acquisition parameters were selected based on the manufacturer’s recommended settings for 161Tb, which included: continuous sweep mode and energy window centered at 74 keV photopeak (± 12% width) combined with a low scatter window at 59 keV (± 10% width) and a high scatter window at 92 keV (± 9% width). For the low-dose CT scan the parameters were set to 120 kV, 512 × 512 × 112 matrix, and a resolution of 1.0 × 1.0 × 2.46 mm3.

The SPECT acquisition time was 15 min for the Jaszczak calibration scan and 30 min for all the NEMA IQ phantom acquisitions, with the acquisition time kept constant across the different activity levels. The NEMA IQ phantom with hot background was scanned at multiple timepoints corresponding with different activity levels of 161Tb. The included activity levels were 100, 50, 25, 12.5, 6.8 and 3.4% of the activity during the first scan, corresponding to approximately 2200, 1100, 550, 225, 113 and 57 MBq, respectively. The scan was repeated three times with 100 and 12.5% of initial activity to assess the reproducibility of the scans.

Image reconstruction

The SPECT data was reconstructed using the SmartConsole interface (GE Healthcare, version 1.0.12). Three different reconstruction methods were included: OSEM, Q.Clear without regularization (QClearN) and Q.Clear with RPD regularization (QClearR). Q.Clear with and without regularization was included to assess the efficacy of the regularization term. In this study, the regularization term RDP (γ = 1 and β = 0.005) was used. The parameters were obtained from the manufacturer-recommended settings for 177Lu. Furthermore, β was weighted according to a sensitivity map derived by the CT-based attenuation map by using the ‘By Sens’ setting [17]. To ensure consistency, all remaining reconstruction parameters were standardized across the three methods: voxel size of 1.0 × 1.0 × 2.46 mm, attenuation correction using low-dose CT, applied scatter correction, disabled tail function, and no post reconstruction filters applied [31].

For each reconstruction method, SPECT images were reconstructed using different number of iterations (i) and subsets (s), where the product of i and s corresponds to the number of updates. In this study, the number of iterations varied between 6 and 864 (denoted as 6i to 864i), each using a single subset (1s). Subsequently, the impact of varying the number of iterations and subsets for the same number of updates of 96i1s was assessed using combinations 6i16s and 12i8s. This selection of number of updates was adopted from Danieli et al. [17].

Image analysis

Image analysis was performed with an in-house developed Python script based on the SimpleITK framework, including manual delineation of all volume-of-interests (VOIs) on the CT images [32, 33]. Delineated VOIs were: a spherical volume with diameter around 15 cm in the Jaszczak phantom (VOIjas), and large spherical volume with diameter 80 cm within the background of the NEMA IQ phantom with hot background (VOIbackground), and a separate VOI for each sphere within the NEMA IQ phantom with cold and hot background (VOIsphere). Following delineation on CT images, VOIs were applied to the corresponding SPECT images. All reconstructed SPECT images were visually checked for the presence of artifacts prior to quantitative analysis, including background homogeneity, CT-SPECT alignment, and clear delineation of the spheres. Quantitative metrics included the calibration factor (CF), recovery coefficient (RC), noise, and spatial resolution. Each metric is described in more detail below.

The recommended number of updates was determined by identifying the number of updates at which the increase in RC became smaller than the corresponding increase in noise. To visualize this point, noise was plotted against RC curves for each sphere in the NEMA IQ phantom with cold background, across all reconstruction method.

Spatial resolution

Spatial resolution in this study was represented by the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the reconstructed images. The FWHM was obtained through a matched-filter analysis, in which a reconstructed image was compared to a series of digital phantom images convolved with 3D Gaussian kernels of varying widths. The FWHM of the kernel that resulted in the smallest sum of squared differences between the digital phantom image and the reconstructed image was considered to represent the spatial resolution [35]. The spatial resolution was calculated in millimeters and evaluated for each reconstruction method across multiple iterations with one subset and varying combinations of iterations and subsets.

Quantitative metrics – NEMA IQ phantom scans with cold and hot background

Background RC values across different energy levels ranged overtime from 0.95 to 1.01 for OSEM and 0.96–1.07 for both QClearN and QClearR. RC values in the spheres demonstrated a consistent increase with both rising number of iterations and sphere sizes across all reconstruction methods (see Fig. 3). For the highest number of updates (864i1s), RC varied across sphere sizes in the ranges of 0.56–0.95 for OSEM, 0.52–0.92 for QClearN, and 0.52–0.92 for QClearR reconstructions. Beyond 96 iterations, OSEM yielded higher RCs compared to both Q.Clear reconstruction methods. The RC curves for both QClearN and QClearR were similar, regardless of sphere size or number of iterations. Notably, the 13 mm sphere yielded lower RC curves for reconstruction exceeding 100 iterations compared to the preceding 10 mm sphere.

Figure 4 shows that varying the subset configurations while maintaining a constant number of updates (n = 96) had minimal impact on the RCs of OSEM. The Q.Clear reconstructions with 6i16s and 12i8s configurations resulted in higher RCs compared with single-subset configuration.

Discussion

This study showed that 161Tb can be quantitatively imaged using the ring-based digital CZT SPECT/CT system StarGuide™, with high RCs and high spatial resolution. The recommended number of updates was determined by evaluating a trade-off between the RC and noise in the four largest spheres, in the absence of objective criteria. Based on this trade-off, the recommended number of updates for the OSEM reconstruction ranged between 48 and 96 updates, and around 200 for the Q.Clear reconstructions. Throughout the study, Q.ClearN and Q.ClearR produced comparable results, for example with similar noise suppression at lower activity levels. For Q.Clear, the performance was affected by the configuration of iterations and subsets, with improved results observed when fewer iterations were used for a fixed number of updates. In all reconstruction methods, the Gibbs artifact was observed which became more pronounced as the number of updates increased. In addition, as the activity dropped to 225 MBq after three half-lives, measurement variability increased markedly. The reduced RC values observed in the 13 mm sphere compared to the smaller sphere immediately preceding was attributed to an elevated pixel intensity in the 10 mm sphere, as shown in Fig. 1.

The activity levels used in this study were intended to simulate those observed in clinical practice. The VIOLET trial [12] demonstrated that an administered dose of 7.4 GBq 161Tb-PSMA-I&T is safe to use in patients. During the initial phantoms scans with a hot background, the activity was approximately 2.2 GBq, which in our opinion aligned with upper limit of activity levels observed in patients undergoing posttherapy at 4 h.

In this study, only subtle differences were observed between the Q.Clear reconstruction methods with and without a regularization term. The parameters β and γ in the regularization term were fixed and selected based on recommendations for 177Lu. However, the regularization term did not influence the quantitative values in this study. Further investigation is warranted to optimize the regularization term for 161Tb.

The recommended number of updates for both OSEM and QClearR reconstructions aligned with the values proposed by Danieli et al. [17] for 177Lu. Although the number of updates used is comparable, comparison of the outcome values demonstrates differences. The RC values for 161Tb are closer to one across all reconstruction methods, with approximately 0.95 observed in the largest sphere, compared to 0.7 for 177Lu. Additionally, spatial resolution for 161Tb is better compared to 177Lu, with values of 7.7 vs. 9.4 mm, respectively, in the OSEM reconstruction. Moreover, the CF values for 161Tb differ from those for 177Lu. Across all reconstruction methods at 96 updates, this study reports a CF of 102 cps/MBq of 161Tb, compared to 94 cps/MBq for 177Lu as reported by Danieli at al. [17]. These differences in RC and CF values suggest radionuclide-specific differences within the same CZT system, likely arising from limitations associated with the fixed collimator design. Further investigation into scatter corrections on CZT SPECT is also warranted.

During image reconstruction, only the 74.6 keV gamma emission peak of 161Tb was utilized, despite the higher abundance of the 48.6 keV peak. The use of the lower-energy peak presented several challenges, primarily related to the limitation of the ring-based digital CZT SPECT/CT system. Specifically, the 48.6 keV peak lay near the lower detection threshold of the CZT detectors of approximately 40 keV. Consequently, scatter correction could not be performed, as the lower scatter window was not applicable [22]. Furthermore, the study of Marin et al. [36] demonstrated artifacts during the reconstruction of the 48.6 keV peak in conventional dual head SPECT, although comparable artifacts have not yet been reported in CZT-based systems. More recent work by Westerbergh et al. [37, 38] indicates that the 48.6 keV peak may still hold value in later scan phases or low-uptake areas, owing to its 3–4 fold higher sensitivity compared to the 74.6 keV peak. Therefore, additional research is needed to elucidate the potential benefits and challenges associated with incorporating the 48.6 keV peak in practice.

Earlier research using conventional SPECT/CT systems for 161Tb imaging has demonstrated clear dead-time effect at higher activities for 161Tb [16, 37]. In this study, background recovery was assessed across multiple activity levels, showing stable values between 0.95 and 1.01 for OSEM and 0.96–1.07 for Q.Clear reconstructions. These findings do not indicate a clear dead-time effect. It has been shown that CZT SPECT/CT detector response remains constant at count rates up to 760 kcps [22]. Nonetheless, further investigation is required to verify this observation and to further explore differences between CZT and conventional SPECT/CT systems.

A limitation of this study is the 30-minute acquisition time of one bed position used for the NEMA IQ phantom scans. Such a duration is not practical for routine dosimetry, particularly given the need for multiple time-point acquisitions with multiple bed position. This study indicated that lowering the activity with a constant scan time, effectively mirroring a reduced scan time at higher activity levels, resulted in increased measurement variability. Further studies should aim to determine the minimum scan duration that maintains acceptable quantitative accuracy. Besides, as the recommended reconstruction protocols in this study are derived solely from phantom data, additional research is necessary to assess their performance in patient imaging.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that 161Tb can be quantitatively imaged using a ring-based digital CZT SPECT/CT camera. Based on a trade-off between RC and noise, the recommended number of updates for the OSEM and Q.Clear reconstruction methods are between 48 and 96 and around 200, respectively. 161Tb achieved higher RC values and improved spatial resolution compared to available data from literature for 177Lu scanned on a similar SPECT/CT system.
