Sleep Deprivation During Clerkship: Hidden Curriculum or Educational Gap?
Authors: Morgan Soffler
Published: 2025

The medical student clerkship experience is not just about practical application of knowledge learned in the pre-clerkship years. Starting on the first day in the clinical learning environment, students will start to learn, through both implicit and explicit lessons, which behaviors are frowned upon and which are rewarded, how they should prioritize the varying demands of the day, how to balance their personal and professional lives, and much more.

The hidden curriculum refers to the implicit lessons and values that medical students absorb outside of formal teaching, often through observation and interpersonal interactions in the clinical learning environment (1). Lessons learned through the hidden curriculum help shape professional identity and attitudes toward patients, colleagues, and the healthcare system. The hidden curriculum can reinforce positive behaviors or, conversely, contribute to cynicism, burnout, and moral distress if it conflicts with students’ core values. Recognizing and addressing the hidden curriculum is essential to fostering a supportive and reflective learning environment. One of these “hidden lessons” in the clinical environment is the prioritization, or lack thereof, of one’s own basic needs, including the need for sleep.

In this issue of ATS Scholar, Ge and colleagues evaluated the self-reported sleep habits of medical students during their internal medicine clerkship at two academic medical centers in New Jersey (2). Using both qualitative and quantitative research methods, the study highlights the presence of sleep deprivation among the majority of students participating in the study (using a definition of sleep deprivation as “the condition that occurs when a person does not get enough sleep to support optimal health and functioning.”) The study found that out of the 222 students surveyed (which represented a 70% response rate), nearly two-thirds (64%) of students reported sleeping less than 7 hours per night. More than half of the cohort surveyed (56%) reported that inadequate sleep impacted their performance or engagement on their rotation at least 1–2 days per week. A shocking 37% of the student cohort reported drowsy driving at least once per week. When asked open-endedly about contributing factors to these sleeping patterns, details about sleep hygiene and clerkship responsibilities were analyzed (Kappa values 0.7 and 0.8, respectively). The study has some limitations that render the conclusions vulnerable to bias, including a lack of objective data about sleep hours and absence of objective sleepiness or performance measures. Nevertheless, how we respond to the knowledge that medical students are sleep deprived and are engaging in the dangerous behavior of drowsy driving is a hidden lesson. What do we want that lesson to be?

The study also surveyed students about whether they had received formal sleep education in medical school, with the overwhelming majority (88%) sharing that they had not received any. Interestingly, only one-third of students believed they would benefit from such an educational intervention. What would we teach during such a session? And would we have to add a “do as I say not as I do” disclosure? As the authors point out, there is no standardized approach at any level of training, despite the presence of the requirement for personal sleep-related education by the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (3).

The negative effects of sleep deprivation are well studied and acknowledged by the sleep medicine community. These effects are far-reaching and are known to have an impact on learning, performance, emotional regulation, and mental and physical health (4). Many of the students surveyed in the study shared that they felt sleep deprivation impacted their learning and performance (2). Outside of performance issues, there are health and safety concerns we must contend with. It is known that there is a disproportionate risk of mental health problems and substance use for medical students (5, 6). Although never shown to directly correlate, one can imagine that the known effects of sleep deprivation on impulsivity and poor decision making may increase these risks. In a large study from Brazil, authors studied the impact of sleep quality, daytime somnolence, and sleep deprivation on medical students’ quality of life, anxiety and depression symptoms, and perception of the educational environment (7). They found a significant association between sleep deprivation and daytime sleepiness with a poor perception of quality of life and educational environment among medical students. The students who were less sleepy had lower scores of depression and anxiety symptoms, with a dose–response relationship.

The reports of drowsy driving are disturbing. Sleep deprivation has been shown to impair cognitive and motor performance in ways comparable to alcohol intoxication (8). Impaired judgment, slower reaction times, reduced attention, and increased likelihood of errors have all been demonstrated in the context of sleep deprivation.

The authors of this study have highlighted the issue of sleep deprivation during clerkship training that has been largely overlooked in the undergraduate medical education community and has received greater regulation and attention in the graduate medical education world, predominantly because of patient safety concerns. If we truly believe in the importance of sleep for our graduate medical education trainees, why not make this a focus upon their entrance into the clinical world as clerkship students? What we choose to teach and evaluate, as well as what we choose to leave out, is part of our hidden curriculum.
