Negative-pressure wound therapy for perineal wound complications after abdominoperineal resection/extralevator APR: Systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: X Su, L Chen, X Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClosed‑incision negative-pressure wound therapy (ciNPT), or incisional negative-pressure wound therapy (NPWT), has been used prophylactically to reduce perineal wound morbidity after oncologic abdominoperineal resection (APR) and extralevator abdominoperineal excision (ELAPE). However, published evidence is heterogeneous and includes prophylactic incisional use as well as therapeutic/intracavitary applications. We conducted a systematic review and meta‑analysis of NPWT strategies for perineal wound outcomes following oncologic APR/ELAPE.MethodsA systematic search was executed across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library from inception to July 2025, including studies that evaluated the role of NPWT in postoperative perineal wounds after oncologic APR/ELAPE. We included both prophylactic closed-incision NPWT (ciNPT) after primary closure and therapeutic NPWT for open/infected perineal wounds; quantitative pooling was limited to comparable comparative studies. The outcomes of interest were the perineal wound complications and infection, surgical revision, and hospital stay. Data were synthesized through a DerSimonian–Laird random effect meta-analysis model, with risk ratios (RR) or mean differences with their 95% confidence interval (CI), pooling the dichotomous or continuous outcomes, respectively.ResultsOur review included eight studies with three comparative studies pooled with a total of 270 patients. Although the raw incidence of perineal wound complications was lower in the NPWT group relative to the control group (36.5% versus 53.6%), the pooled analysis revealed no statistical significance (RR = 0.79, 95% CI [0.42–1.48], p = 0.46). Similarly, no significant differences between the two groups were observed in terms of perineal wound infection (p = 0.35), surgery revision (p = 0.53), and hospital stay (p = 0.77).ConclusionsThis meta-analysis demonstrated no significant clinical benefit for NPWT in managing postoperative perineal wound after oncologic APR/ELAPE. Further randomized controlled trials with standardized protocols are needed to clarify the role of NPWT in this area.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10151-026-03329-1.Keywords: Negative-pressure wound therapy, NPWT, Perineal wounds, Meta-analysis

Background

Perineal wound complications are among the most common and morbid adverse events following abdominoperineal resection (APR) or extralevator APR (ELAPE) for rectal cancer [1]. These complications range from surgical site infection, wound dehiscence, and delayed healing to abscess formation and, in chronic cases, persistent sinus formation [2, 3]. They can lead to prolonged hospital stay, intensive postoperative wound care, readmissions, and, in severe cases, reconstructive surgery [4]. In wound-care terminology, malignant wounds (also called fungating/ulcerating cancer wounds) refer to lesions where a tumor infiltrates and breaks through the skin, creating a chronic ulcerated wound, most often in advanced cancer. In contrast, the present review addresses postoperative perineal wounds and perineal wound complications following oncologic abdominoperineal resection (APR) or extralevator APR (ELAPE), where common complications include infection/sepsis, dehiscence, delayed healing/persistent sinus, and perineal hernia.

Multiple anatomical and pathophysiological factors significantly impact these consequences. For instance, following APR or extralevator APR (ELAPE), a large pelvic defect appears, representing a reservoir for blood and fluid accumulation, facilitating bacterial overgrowth and delaying wound healing [5]. Additionally, other factors such as mechanical stress, due to sitting or lying down, exert shearing forces on the wound, creating an unfavorable environment for wound closure and healing [6]. Moreover, patient-related factors including malnutrition, smoking, diabetes, and obesity can also add further complexity to wound healing and heighten the risk of perineal wound complications [7–9].

Given this considerable burden, negative-pressure wound therapy (NPWT) has been introduced as a promising therapy to mitigate these significant complications. The World Health Organization (WHO) suggests prophylactic negative-pressure wound therapy, designed originally for open wounds, may be used on primarily closed surgical incisions in high-risk wounds, taking resources into account, for the purpose of preventing surgical site infection (conditional recommendation, low quality of evidence). Similarly, National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidance states that PICO™ negative-pressure wound dressings should be considered for closed surgical incisions in people at high risk of developing SSI [10, 11]. In the APR/ELAPE setting, NPWT has been used either prophylactically as closed-incision NPWT (ciNPT) to prevent SSI after primary closure, or therapeutically as vacuum-assisted closure (VAC)/NPWT for open or infected perineal wounds. NPWT works by applying sub-atmospheric pressure, typically −80 mmHg, thus enhancing fluid evacuation, reducing edema and accelerating angiogenesis and wound contraction. Furthermore, it also impacts the molecular healing pathways, such as cytokine modulation and mechanoreceptor activation, which prompts the extracellular matrix remodeling and granulation tissue formation [12].

Despite these encouraging mechanisms and early success in other surgical regions (e.g., sternotomy, vascular hernia, and trauma surgery), the evidence concerning the efficacy of NPWT in perineal wound healing is still limited and inconclusive [13, 14]. Several pilot studies revealed a potential reduction in surgical site infection and wound dehiscence with NPWT, but most of these studies encountered considerable limitations, including underpowered sample sizes, retrospective controls, and variability in NPWT protocols [15–17]. Due to the significant morbidity of postoperative perineal wound after oncologic APR/ELAPE, the potential therapeutic benefits of NPWT, and the limited quality of available evidence, there is a need for a review that summarizes all these findings. This study seeks to evaluate the efficacy of NPWT in reducing perineal wound complications following oncologic APR/ELAPE.

Methods

This study adhered to the methodological guidance in the Cochrane Handbook and the reporting standards created by the PRISMA statement [18, 19].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

With no restriction to a particular study design, we included all relevant studies that fulfilled the following criteria: (1) adult patients with perineal wounds that appeared following perineal or rectal cancer surgery and (2) patients who had received negative-pressure wound therapy. Study endpoints included perineal wound complications, perineal wound infection, surgery revision, and Clavien–Dindo classification grade. We excluded studies evaluating perineal wounds following benign disease surgery, conference abstracts, reviews, and foreign language studies. NPWT interventions were categorized as prophylactic closed-incision NPWT (ciNPT) after primary perineal closure versus therapeutic NPWT for open/infected wounds or intracavitary drainage/irrigation strategies; meta-analysis was restricted to clinically comparable comparative studies. We excluded studies of fungating/ulcerating malignant wounds caused by tumor invasion of the skin because our population of interest was those with postoperative perineal wounds after APR/ELAPE.

Literature search and selection process

The scope of our electronic search involved PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library from the origin until July 2025. The search strategy combined terms for negative-pressure wound therapy (including “incisional NPWT” and “closed-incision NPWT/ciNPT” and brand terms such as PICO/Prevena where applicable) with terms for oncologic APR/ELAPE and postoperative perineal wounds; the full database-specific search strategies are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

After exporting the literature search results, the articles were imported into EndNote (Clarivate Analytics, PA, USA) to identify and eliminate duplicates. Consequently, two independent authors, addressing their conflicts through discussion, evaluated the titles and abstracts of eligible studies using Rayyan Web [20], followed by an extensive full-text assessment for the retrieved reports. Furthermore, we manually searched the bibliography of the included studies and previous meta-analyses to ensure no relevant studies were missed.

Data extraction

Two blinded authors, addressing their discrepancies through discussion, used a pre-designed extraction sheet to extract the relevant summary and baseline information. The summary data included the study ID, country, time frame, total sample size, inclusion criteria, NPWT management, type of comparator, indication of surgery, type of surgery, duration of follow-up, and conclusion. The baseline information encompassed age, sex, gender, smoking, diabetes status, and use of neoadjuvant therapy. Primary outcomes were (1) perineal surgical site infection (SSI) and (2) overall perineal wound complications (composite). SSI was defined according to each study; SSI definitions were considered in relation to Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)/National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) criteria (superficial incisional, deep incisional, and organ/space) and surveillance periods. Overall perineal wound complications were treated as a composite outcome as defined by each study, typically including SSI/infection, dehiscence, perineal abscess/collection, seroma/hematoma, delayed wound healing, or persistent perineal sinus. When studies reported multiple time points for SSI, we extracted the earliest clinically relevant postoperative time point (typically 30 days) for quantitative synthesis, and recorded longer follow-up outcomes narratively when reported.

When reported, we also extracted neoadjuvant radiotherapy exposure, perineal reconstruction/closure technique (primary closure versus mesh versus flap), and device type/pressure/duration.

Risk of bias assessment

Two independent authors evaluated the risk of bias in double-arm observational studies using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [21]. The NOS assessed three critical methodological aspects: population and exposure selection, comparability between the two groups, and endpoint ascertainment. The cumulative judgment score determined the quality of each study, which was rated as good, moderate, or poor.

The National Institute of Health (NIH) was employed to assess the risk of bias of single-arm studies [22]. The NIH for single-arm cohorts encompasses several methodological items, such as the clear description of the study objective, the predetermination of eligibility criteria, and the sufficiency of the sample size to provide confidence in the findings. The final judgment score ranged from poor to fair to good quality. Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) was not applied to single-arm studies, because it is intended for estimating comparative intervention effects, while our single-arm evidence was used for narrative context rather than effect estimation.

The discussion approach was employed to address any emerging conflicts.

Statistical analysis

The risk ratio (RR) with its 95% confidence interval (CI) was used for pooling the categorical data, while the mean difference (MD) with its 95% CI was implemented for analyzing the continuous parameters. The DerSimonian–Laird random effect meta-analysis model was applied with a p-value less than 0.05, indicating statistical significance. Because only a small number of studies contributed to each pooled outcome, results were interpreted cautiously; for outcomes with zero-event cells, an appropriate continuity correction was applied when required. The heterogeneity was assessed using the chi-squared p-value and I2. Significant heterogeneity was identified when the chi-squared p-value < 0.1 or I2 > 50%. Publication bias was not formally assessed, because each pooled outcome included fewer than ten studies. All the statistical synthesis was performed using STATA 18 BE.

Results

Literature search

The initial search of electronic databases yielded 344 articles, and upon eliminating 106 duplicates, 238 were screened through the title and abstract screening stage. Consequently, 221 articles were removed through this phase, and 17 were retrieved for comprehensive full-text assessment. Ultimately, eight articles were included in this systematic review; four of them entered the meta-analysis [15–17, 23–27]. Figure 1 outlines the PRISMA flow diagram of the study selection process.

PRISMA flow diagram of the study selection process

Characteristics of the included studies

This systematic review and meta-analysis included 270 patients from six cohorts, one case–control study, and one case series study. The geographical distribution of this study was mainly dependent on two regions: five studies were conducted in Europe, and three were conducted in Asia. The negative-pressure regimen varied across studies, encompassing the application of 80, 100, or 125 mmHg of negative pressure for 3, 5, or 7 days following surgery. Negative pressure was predominantly applied for perineal wounds that appeared after surgery for rectal cancer, with abdominoperineal resection being the most common surgery implemented. Follow-up duration ranged from 3 to 48 weeks, with an average of 21.4 weeks across studies. Most of the population was male (71.4%), with a mean age of 66.75 years. Risk of bias

Five double-arm observational studies were evaluated using NOS; four were rated good quality, and one revealed fair quality, as detailed in Supplementary Table S2. According to the NIH tool, two single-arm studies showed fair quality, while one exhibited poor quality, as outlined in Supplementary Table S2. All eight included studies underwent risk-of-bias assessment; however, only four studies contributed data suitable for quantitative meta-analysis due to outcome definition and reporting differences.

Perineal wound complications

Three studies reported data on perineal wound complications, encompassing 63 patients in the NPWT group and 56 in the control group. The overall incidence of perineal wound complications was notably lower in the NPWT group compared with the control group (36.5% versus 53.6%). However, the pooled analysis showed comparable estimates of perineal wound complications between the NPWT and control groups (RR = 0.79, 95% CI [0.42–1.48], p = 0.46). The pooled studies revealed moderate nonsignificant heterogeneity (I2 = 40.89%, p = 0.18; Fig. 2).

Forest plot of perineal wound complications

Perineal wound infection

The overall incidence of perineal wound infection was 28.6% (22/77) in the NPWT group and 39.3% (33/84) in the control group. There was no statistically significant difference between the NPWT and the control group regarding perineal wound infection (RR = 0.79, 95% CI [0.47–1.31], p = 0.35). Mild heterogeneity was observed among the pooled studies (I2 = 8.26%, p = 0.35; Fig. 3).

Forest plot of perineal wound infection

Surgical revision

The pooled analysis of two studies, encompassing 62 patients, revealed comparable instances of surgical revision between the two groups (RR = 0.76, 95% CI [0.31–1.82], p = 0.53). The pooled results were homogeneous (I2 = 0%, p = 0.63; Fig. 4).

Forest plot of surgery revision

Hospital stay

The pooling of two studies, including 141 patients, showed no statistically significant reduction in the hospital stay with the NPWT relative to the control group (MD = −0.59, 95% CI [−4.59 to 3.41], p = 0.77), with associated significant heterogeneity (I2 = 74.83%, p = 0.05; Fig. 5).

Forest plot of hospital stay

Clavien–Dindo classification grade

Two studies, comprising 62 patients, reported the Clavien–Dindo classification grade. No significant differences were found between the NPWT and the control group regarding the Clavien–Dindo classification grade 1 (RR = 1.11, 95% CI [0.59–2.10], p = 0.75), Clavien–Dindo classification grade 2 (RR = 0.53, 95% CI [0.05–6.06], p = 0.61), Clavien–Dindo classification grade 3a (RR = 1.47, 95% CI [0.42–5.12], p = 0.55), and Clavien–Dindo classification grade 3b (RR = 0.72, 95% CI [0.30–1.74], p = 0.47; Fig. 6).

Discussion

This study-level meta-analysis synthesized the evidence from eight studies encompassing 270 patients receiving NPWT for postoperative perineal wound after oncologic APR/ELAPE. Although the crude incidence of perineal wound complications was lower in the NPWT group than in the control group, the meta-analysis revealed no statistically significant difference between the two groups. Similarly, the incidence of perineal wound infection was comparable between the two groups. Furthermore, the pooled analysis showed no significant differences between the two groups regarding surgery revision, hospital stay, and Clavien–Dindo classification across grades 1–3b.

A meta-analysis published in 2023 (Fan et al.) [28] evaluated NPWT after APR/ELAPE and incorporated trial sequential analysis. Compared with that work, our review emphasizes clear clinical separation of prophylactic closed-incision NPWT (ciNPT) versus therapeutic/open-wound NPWT and intracavitary drainage/irrigation strategies, and we explicitly state when pooled estimates are derived from only a subset of comparative studies. This framing aims to reduce clinical heterogeneity and improve interpretability for colorectal/perineal practice.

Negative-pressure wound therapy has been introduced as a mechanistically effective procedure for minimizing perineal wound complications after abdominoperineal surgeries for malignant cancers [15, 16, 24]. The pathophysiological effects of NPWT are diverse and strictly correlated with the fundamental challenges of perineal wound healing, especially in oncologic patients who usually receive neoadjuvant therapy and undergo substantial tissue resection. Prior investigations highlighted that NPWT eradicates the residual wound fluid, increases local oxygenation, and approximates wound margins, thus achieving adequate mechanical stability [29, 30]. NPWT also modifies cytokines toward an anti-inflammatory profile, as well as facilitating angiogenesis and extracellular matrix remodeling [31]. This superficial negative pressure may also enhance the epithelial closure and alter the underlying blood flow [29]. These mechanisms are especially advantageous in the perineal region, where deep cavities, less perfusion, and mechanical stress could delay wound healing. Despite these significant biological effects, they were not translated into significant clinical effects, as observed in our pooled meta-analysis. It revealed no statistically significant impact for NPWT regarding wound complications, infection rates, and surgery revision.

The clinical efficacy of NPWT could be influenced not only by the patients or surgery type but also by differences in device type, pressure dose, and technical feasibility in the perineal region. In this systematic review, several pressure regimens were observed encompassing −80 mmHg, −100 mmHg, and −125 mmHg. Higher negative pressures may be more effective in removing the excess fluid in deep pelvic cavities. Supporting this, Sumrien et al. [16] reported a statistically significant reduction in major wound complications (3% versus 40%, p = 0.01) when applying pressure of −125 mmHg. Conversely, Van der Valk et al. [15] employed −80 mmHg negative pressure and found no significant reduction in complication rates, which may be attributed to the suboptimal negative pressure, failing to reach the deeper pelvic lesions.

Device type may also play a pivotal role in the clinical efficacy of NPWT. For instance, portable systems such as PICO™ and Avelle™ encounter technical challenges encompassing vacuum failure, dressing saturation, and difficulty maintaining a seal in complex anatomical regions such as the perineum [15, 24, 29]. In a study by Salmenkylä et al. [24], who used the Avelle™ system, 62% of NPWT devices were removed early, and 33% failed due to vacuum failure. These limitations may be pronounced in female patients owing to their more challenging perineal area, as evidenced by Wiegering et al. [17], who reported that leakage at the vaginal introitus may compromise NPWT therapy in female patients.

The type of surgery may also represent a significant moderator for the efficacy of NPWT. Sumrien et al. [16] found that NPWT used in conjunction with ELAPE and biologic mesh significantly reduced major wound complications compared with standard APR with primary closure. In contrast, studies limited to APR did not observe clinical benefit [15, 24]. Given the broader dissection and more extensive cavity in ELAPE, NPWT may be more effective due to improved device fit and enhanced drainage potential. These findings suggest that NPWT’s clinical performance may be optimized in specific patient and surgical contexts, particularly ELAPE, and attenuated by low-pressure dosage and complex anatomy.

Strengths and drawbacks

This systematic review and meta-analysis summarizes available evidence on NPWT for postoperative perineal wound outcomes after oncologic APR/ELAPE; however, certainty is limited by predominantly observational designs, small sample sizes, and heterogeneity in intervention type and co-interventions. A key strength lies in the variability in geographical regions and population characteristics encompassed in this study, which supports the generalizability and credibility of our findings. Nevertheless, this study encounters several limitations. Most included studies were observational, which carries a potential risk of selection bias and residual confounding. Sample sizes were small, particularly in single-arm and feasibility studies, which may limit the statistical power to produce statistical significance. Also, we could not perform the planned subgroup analysis by neoadjuvant radiotherapy/chemoradiotherapy. This is clinically relevant because preoperative radiotherapy/chemoradiotherapy has been reported as a risk factor for perineal wound complications and perineal infection after abdominoperineal excision/resection. Additionally, a major limitation is clinical heterogeneity: NPWT was applied prophylactically to closed incisions in some studies, while others used therapeutic/open-wound NPWT or drainage/irrigation variants. Lastly, a significant heterogeneity was observed, which may be attributed to variations in NPWT protocols, follow-up duration, and outcome definitions.

Implications in clinical practice and future directions

While NPWT poses significant theoretical promise in perineal wound management due to its mechanistic effects, such as fluid evacuation, edema reduction, and angiogenesis stimulation, its clinical utility remains uncertain based on current evidence. The findings of this meta-analysis do not support routine use of NPWT in all postoperative perineal wounds after oncologic APR/ELAPE, especially in standard APR procedures using low-pressure portable devices. However, individual studies suggest that NPWT may confer meaningful benefit when combined with ELAPE and biologic mesh, particularly when higher pressures (e.g., 125 mmHg) are applied using robust, hospital-grade devices.

From a clinical point of view, careful patient selection and surgical context should guide NPWT deployment. For female patients or those with anatomically complex perineal regions, technical feasibility must be evaluated to ensure seal integrity. Device improvements tailored to the perineal anatomy are demanded.

Future research should prioritize randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with standardized outcome measures comparing NPWT regimens across pressure levels and surgery types. Exploring variable pressure protocols and assessing cost-effectiveness in high-risk subgroups could further clarify NPWT’s role in oncologic wound care. NPWT should be considered a selective adjunct rather than a universal standard in this setting.

Conclusions

Although NPWT is biologically plausible and technically promising, current comparative evidence does not demonstrate a clear reduction in perineal wound morbidity with NPWT/ciNPT after oncologic APR/ELAPE; certainty is low due to observational designs, small study numbers, and heterogeneity in intervention type and reconstruction strategy. There was no considerable advantage for NPWT regarding perineal wound complications, infection, surgery revision, and hospital stay. Current evidence suggests NPWT may reduce SSI, but certainty is low due to limited and heterogeneous studies. Well-designed RCTs are needed.
