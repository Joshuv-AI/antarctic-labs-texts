An educational intervention to bridge the gap between nurses and nurse assistants– a focus group study in Norway
Authors: Ann-Chatrin Linqvist Leonardsen, Renate Slang, Anna Pauline Pedersen, Helene Matri, Malin H. Westerlund, Trine Hovland, Kristin Myhre
Published: 2025

Background

Studies have found that a mixed staffing model where nurses and nurse assistants work as a team can deliver better care quality than standard nurse staffing models, if the ratio of nurses to nurse assistants is sufficiently high [1, 2]. Such models have shown to reduce the nonprofessional workload and overtime hours of nurses [3, 4]. Also, with the support of nurse assistants, nurses will potentially be able to focus on tasks that require a high level of professional knowledge [5].

Summary of existing literature

Norway is estimated to lack 28 000 nurses by 2035 [6]. The Norwegian government has claimed that newly educated nurses do not have the required competence to meet health service demands and the patients’ quality needs [7]. This is supported by studies indicating a gap between education and practice requirements [8–11]. As a solution, national guidelines for nursing education at a bachelor’s degree level have been regulated to ensure a quality, national standard of nurses [12]. No similar studies in nurse assistants have been identified.

Also, a Norwegian parliamentary notice suggested task shifting as a measure to alleviate the nursing shortage [13]. Task shifting is defined as shifting tasks from personnel with higher competence to personnel with lower competence [14], and nurse assistants (in Norway vocational school health educated) have been suggested used to relieve bachelor degree’ educated nurses from traditional nursing tasks such as insertion of peripheral vein catheters, administration of fluids or other practical procedures [15].

A 2024 comprehensive review of skill development and role integration between nurses and nurse assistants [16] emphasizes issues related to communication gaps, unclear job descriptions, hierarchical barriers, resource limitations, cultural and systemic factors that need to be solved to ensure an effective nursing workforce. Consequently, an intervention to bridge communication gaps, clarify the contents of nurses and nurse assistants’ job respectively and to decrease hierarchical barriers through increasing nurses and nurse assistants’ knowledge about the other’s competencies seemed appropriate.

Purpose

The purpose of the current study was to explore nurses’ and nurse assistants’ experiences with an educational intervention to bridge the gap between nurses and nurse assistants. The educational intervention comprised a lecture of 90 min and a simulation of 60 min within the subjects ‘respiratory issues’ and ‘sepsis’ respectively.

Methods

A qualitative study design, with focus group interviews, was used. Focus group interviewing uses group interaction as part of the method, where participants are encouraged to talk to one another: asking questions, exchanging and commenting on each other’s experiences. This approach is particularly useful for exploring people’s knowledge and experiences [17]. The study adheres to the Consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) [18].

Context

The study was conducted in a middle-sized hospital in southeastern Norway, with a catchment area of approximately 320 000 inhabitants. The area is affiliated with a university college with a bachelor’s degree program in nursing, as well as a vocational school with educational programs for nurse assistants. In 2022, the hospital had 355 positions for nurse assistants, and 1720 for nurses.

Four medical wards were included in the intervention, namely infection, neurology, geriatrics and an acute observation ward. The wards struggle with recruiting and retaining nurses, and a measure has been to establish nursing teams including nurses and nurse assistants.

Intervention

The intervention consisted of an education day arranged at the university college’s facilities, including a simulation center. The day was planned together with clinical nurses (n = 7) from the respective wards, and educators (n = 6) from the university college. It included a theoretical lecture on respiratory issues and sepsis, 90 min respectively. The theoretical lecture was followed by a simulation (60 min) on the same subject, using live markers. The simulation followed a script developed by a clinical nurse and an educator together, both experienced with scenario-development. The scenario time was approximately 25 min, followed by 20–25 min debrief. Learning outcomes in both simulations were (1) to assess a patient with a medical deterioration and to initiate actions, (2) to communicate using ‘closed loop’, (3) to clarify roles within the team. There were four to six participants in each simulation group, and each participant completed both scenarios. The participants were asked to take their normal role in the situation. Due to the group size, some of the participants ended up as observers. Table 1 gives an example of the education intervention agenda (groups varied between topics).

Agenda

The subjects were chosen due to feedback from nurses and nurse assistants indicating a need for more knowledge and training in respiratory issues and sepsis. Both the lectures and the simulation were led by one clinical nurse and one educator together.

In total, 74 nurses and 45 nurse assistants participated in one of three days arranged. Each day had about 40 participants, that were divided into ten groups. The groups were collated with nurses and nurse assistants mixed, and mixed across wards they worked in.

Sample

A purposive sampling strategy was used. All the nurses and nurse assistants participating in the education day were prospectively invited by professional development nurses in the four wards to participate in a focus group interview at the end of the day. Inclusion criteria were working clinically and being able to understand and express themselves in Norwegian. In total, 23 nurses or nurse assistants agreed to participate. None withdrew from the study after having consented to participate.

Interview guide

An interview guide was developed through discussions between the clinical nurses (n = 7) from the four wards, and educators (n = 6) from the university college, of which all but one were female. Of the educators, three were experienced with focus group interviews, one professor/PhD and two associate professors. The guide was piloted in nurses (n = 4) and nurse assistants (n = 2), to assess whether the questions were understandable and relevant. The pilot participants found the interview guide relevant, understandable and consistent. Table 2 presents the topics included in the interview guide.

Datacollection

The focus groups were composed of nurses and nurse assistants from the same ward. The interviews were conducted directly after each education day, in a separate room. A moderator experienced with focus group interviews was present. As such ACLL (professor) moderated three interviews, KM (associate professor) moderated two interviews and RS (assistant professor) moderated one interview. TH (assistant professor) and IH (associate professor) observed two of the interviews. Participants were prompted to freely discuss the presented “questions”/themes. The interviews were audio recorded through a secure cellphone application (Nettskjema Diktafon) and automatically transcribed in a safe platform developed and hosted by the University of Oslo (nettskjema@usit.uio.no). The transcripts were controlled for accuracy by one of the interviewers.

Analysis

The data were analyzed using a semantic, thematic, inductive analysis as described by Braun & Clarke [19]. This method is specifically recommended for novices in qualitative analysis, chosen due to including both novices and more experienced researchers. In the first step, all authors familiarized themselves with the data through reading the transcripts and/or listening to the recordings. Then, in step two, the moderator of each interview (ACLL, KM and RS) coded the transcript by identifying meaningful text that seemed relevant to the study purpose. These codes were then reviewed by the interview observer (TH or IH) or the project leader (ACLL), and codes were either revised or added until consensus was achieved. Then, the codes were collated in step three to preliminary themes, by identifying codes that related to the same issue. This was done by the same pair of researchers. In step four, five of the authors met (ACLL, APP, RS, TH, HM) to discuss preliminary themes. Through several iterations, constantly moving back and forth to the codes and text if needed, the themes were identified and finally named in step five. Analysis material consisting of codes, preliminary themes and final themes were also reviewed by two nurse assistants not taking part in the project, to assess the transparency and credibility of the interpretations. Ethical considerations and consent for participation

According to Norwegian legislations, no ethics approval is needed when interviewing healthcare personnel [20]. An approval was provided by the Norwegian Agency for Shared Services in Education and Research (SIKT, project no. 149238). The study was conducted in-line with the Declaration of Helsinki [21], and based on anonymity, confidentiality and informed, willing consent to participate. The participants were informed that they could withdraw their consent at any point without any negative consequences. All the participants provided their written consent to participate before the interview. To avoid recognition, the participants’ age, gender, occupation and ward are not combined.

Results

In total, six FGs were conducted in the period 7th to 21th May 2024. The FGs included 23 participants, of which 13 nurses and 10 nurse assistants, three were male. Their mean age was 33.4 years, and they had a mean of 7.3 years of experience from their occupation, representing three different hospital wards. The interview length varied between 25 and 55 min. Through analysis, two themes were identified, namely (1) Simulation: a love-hate relationship and (2) Increased insight into each other’s competence and understanding of each other’s focus.

Simulation: a love-hate relationship

Most of the participants across FGs reported to be sceptic in front of the simulations, due to finding it surreal and uncomfortable. This perception was described like this by one of the participants in FG 4: “I find it really uncomfortable to simulate.Horrible…But still, I always think afterwards that it´s good learning. It´s a love-hate relationship…”.

The negative feeling towards simulation was underlined in some way in all the FGs, for example in FG 2: “I feel is gets weird when acting…That I´m judged somehow, even if they say we aren´t supposed to do that. I may freeze in the situation, that I´m sure I had managed at work…”.

But, throughout the interviews, more positive elements of simulation were discussed, for example in FG 6, where one of the participants stated: “You get time to reflect in front…That´s not common at work…I think the scenario shouldn´t be detailed, because you don´t know so much in front in real life…”.

As a conclusion, all the participants across FGs acknowledged the positive effects of simulation on providing professional replenishment. Specifically, the combination of a lecture as preparation before simulation was emphasized as positive across groups. For example, in FG 1, one of the participants said: “You get instructions, and then you can use it. And the situation doesn´t appear out of the blue…Easier to imagine what to do…” (FG1).

The positive aspect of combining lecture and simulation was also reported in all the other FGs. The preparation lecture made the participants aware of specific issues that could occur in the simulation, also giving them an opportunity to ‘practice what they had learnt’ directly. Moreover, several of the participants in most FGs underlined that such preparation before simulation increased the feeling of mastery, as stated in FG3: “With the physiology as a basis, with the same case throughout… You get the theory, then the case, and then the simulation. You hear, you see and then you do…”.

Neither of these perceptions seemed to differ between nurses and nurse assistants or across FGs.

Even if some of the nurses, for example in FG 2 and 4, said that the lecture was quite basic and mostly repetition, they stated that input in front of the simulation was useful and created a common basis for the simulation across nurses and nurse assistants and across wards. In FG4, one of the participants expressed: “Even if it was mostly repetition, it was good repetition. I knew it before, still I haven´t used it…So, it was a good reminder…”.

Also, in two of the FGs the importance of debrief was underlined as promoting reflection and further learning. One of the participants in FG3 said: “It´s not really the simulation itself, but the debrief afterwards. It´s there the learning appears…Sitting down together and talking through what we did, what we thought, what we observed…”. Another participant in the same FG continued: “You add more flesh to the bones… What do these observations mean? That it´s not only a routine, and then you report further. But- what does this mean, and what can I do? “.

Some of the participants from different FGs also suggested improvement areas of simulation to increase their learning outcomes, such as ‘increasing the complexity of the case’ (FG2), simulating together with students to ‘recruit and learn together and from each other’ (FG3), or changing roles between simulations (FG4).

Increased insight into each other’s competence and understanding of each other’s focus

Most of the participants across FGs appreciated receiving lectures and simulating nurses and nurse assistants together. Several of the participants across FGs underlined that the common education day increased learning across occupational background, that would be useful in a clinical setting. For example, a nurse in FG5 declared:

And you get a NEWS presented, often by the nurse assistant…And the saturation is low, 91%, and then I think “so what”? How was the respiration? The respiration rate? What about blood pressure? Was the patient affected? To see this together….

This was supported by a nurse assistant in FG1, who said: “I feel that, as a nurse assistant, I learnt much more today…I´ll observe the patient´s skin status, clammy…And I can report my observations to the nurse, because I´ll understand that something´s wrong”.

Some differences between nurses and nurse assistants were identified. For example, all the nurse assistants across FGs reported to have “learnt a lot” in both the lecture and simulation sessions. Most of them stated that the themes were new to them, and that they had gained insight into the work of the nurses through the simulation. In contrast, some of the nurses stated that the themes mainly were repetition of what they already knew. However, the common intervention was seen to increase nurses’ insight into the nurse assistant’s competence. For example, a nurse in FG3 prompted: “It was nice simulating in pairs nurses and nurse assistants. Because this is how we work. And then we learn about each other’s role and competence. When simulating together, we become more aware of what each of us can do. And we also speak the same language across professions”. Another participant in FG3 underlined that “what the nurse assistants can do varies across wards. Some places they can put up a Ringer, or insert a peripheral vein catheter, other places they can´t”. Still, the common training was seen positive, as stated in FG1: “training together allows for developing common routines”.

Moreover, training across the wards was seen to increase the understanding about each other’s medical fields, namely neurology, geriatrics, and infection medicine. One of reported reasons, as stated in FG 6, was that all of them, regardless of occupational background or ward affiliation “focused on taking care of the patient”. To some, this seemed a surprise, as stated in FG1: “I was a bit sceptic at first…But then I felt it was nice to see how others work…We sometimes assist across wards, and then it is good to know how one may work”.

The common lectures and simulations made the participants aware of their different focus and specialties, as a participant in FG3 said: “You´re formed by the ward you work in. We focused on infection and sepsis, but the ones from the acute geriatric ward focused on ABC and anti-slip socks to prevent from falling”. Similar differences were underlined in several of the other FGs. Moreover, participants across FGs thought that common lectures and simulations could potentially increase understanding and focus on collaboration across wards. This was exemplified by a participant in FG5: “We may have different routines and things to follow up…And now we learn a common view…If we´re in a situation together, we’ll see the same things even if we´re from different wards”.

Several of the FG participants also provided positive feedback on the physical surroundings in the simulation center at the university college.

Discussion

This study shows that both nurses and nurse assistants were sceptic to simulation as an educational intervention. However, all appreciated the combination of preparational lectures and simulation, and reported that the education day had provided professional input. The theoretical level seemed appropriate for nurse assistants, while the level mostly was regarded as repetition by nurses. The education day increased insight into each other’s competence and understanding of each other’s focus across wards and specialties, and across nurses and nurse assistants.

Most of the nurses and nurse assistants across FGs reported to be sceptic in front of the simulations, due to finding simulation surreal and uncomfortable. The importance of relevant simulation scenarios is underlined in the Global consensus statement on simulation-based practice in healthcare [22]. Studies in nursing populations have found that simulation provides psychological and physiological arousal, which indicates simulation is demanding and stressful [23, 24]. Similarly, studies indicate that nursing students experience an increased physiological stress and anxiety during simulation practice [25, 26], and that this may hamper students’ learning outcomes [27]. However, throughout the interviews in the current study more positive elements of simulation were discussed, for example accessible time to reflect and discuss issues. There is extensive research on the education-practice gap in nursing [28] and on the use of simulation in nursing education [29]. However, more research is required as support for simulation to be used as a tool to specifically address this gap experienced in clinical practice [30].

In the current study, preparational lectures made the participants aware of specific issues that could occur in the simulation, also giving them an opportunity to ‘practice what they had learnt’ directly. This approach is supported by e.g. Zhao et al. [31] who found that, following a 90-min theoretical lecture, simulation-based training better enhanced the performance of medical students in the interpretation and short-term retention of 20 cross-sectional views. Similar positive outcomes of combining lecture and simulation have also been found in other studies in various healthcare personnel populations [32–34]. As such, combining different learning methods has been defined “blended learning”, as an effective teaching strategy that appears to have excellent long-term developmental potential [35, 36].

Most of the participants across FGs appreciated receiving lectures and simulating nurses and nurse assistants together, even if the theoretical level was seen as repetition for nurses. According to the participants, this increased learning across occupational backgrounds, that would be useful in a clinical setting. Moore et al. [37] also found that simulation activities successfully can be used to cross-train nursing staff. Moreover, they found that debriefing may lead to additional problem-solving beyond simply reviewing and evaluating the actual simulation. This was also the case in our study. A systematic review of health professionals’ experience of teamwork education in acute hospital settings [38] recommends that hospital team members should be encouraged to participate in teamwork education programs in order to foster a positive culture of learning and teamwork within the team. Our findings align with this, indicating an increased learning outcome across occupations, specialties and wards.

Finally, our study indicates that the education day increased the participants’ insight into each other’s role and competencies. A comprehensive review of skill development and role integration between nurses and nurse assistants [16] found that even if collaboration makes work easier: if there are too many people doing the same or similar job, confusion or rivalry arises within the staff. As such, even if interventions like the one in our study may increase insight into each other’s role, further emphasis is needed to meet these barriers and hereby facilitate a robust nursing workforce for the future.

Limitations

The study was conducted in a Norwegian hospital, and in a limited number of participants. Hence, the transferability of our findings may be discussed. The results have similarity with findings from other studies, even if these are from countries outside Europe. Due to differences both in healthcare service organization, education of nurses (and nurse assistants) and manning of hospital wards, the results may not necessarily be comparable. The trustworthiness of our results is supported by the transparent presentation of data collection and analysis. Moreover, since only nurses were involved in as researchers, the analytic steps and results were reviewed by two nurse assistants. There was a balance between nurses and nurse assistants in the sample, and with a range of age and experience. However, only two males were included. Still, the results across FGs mainly aligned, hereby strengthening the dependability of our findings. In retrospect, we should have involved nurse assistants in the planning of the education day, to include their perspectives also here.

Conclusion

Educational interventions between nurses and nurse assistants seem appropriate to decrease communication and collaboration gaps across these two occupations. Moreover, combining theoretical lectures and simulations seems like an appropriate approach. These perspectives are useful to include when aiming for sustainable nursing workforce in the future.

Implications for further research¨

Many of the studies identified were from a non-European setting, indicating a need for further research from European or Nordic settings.
