Automatic recognition of oral health status in mixed dentition via intraoral photography
Authors: Zhuoying Liu, Shen Zhao, Yikang Wang, Yina Cao, Huancai Lin, Liangyue Pang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildren in the mixed dentition stage are susceptible to dental caries, necessitating frequent examination and preventive measures. In this study, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) were used to develop a model for detecting oral health status in mixed dentition.MethodsIntraoral images of dentition spanning 2467 occlusal surfaces were collected from 7–9-year-old children for training and testing. The health status of all teeth, including caries, fillings, intraoral devices, pigments, and black stains, was tested. For the first permanent molar, indicator testing was conducted to determine the eruption conditions, fissure morphology, and sealing conditions. ResNet50 was trained for segmentation tasks, and TResNet was trained for detection tasks. The statistical analysis included calculations of the F1 score (F1) and area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC).ResultsThe model was able to correctly detect caries (F1, 0.777; AUC, 0.964), fillings (F1, 0.746; AUC, 0.893), pigmentation (F1, 0.836; AUC, 0.870), intraoral devices (F1, 0.800; AUC, 1.00) and black stains (F1, 0.867; AUC, 0.988). The model demonstrated good capabilities for detecting deep fissures (F1, 0.927; AUC, 0.951), completely erupted teeth (F1, 0.882; AUC, 0.944), and sealed teeth (F1, 0.947; AUC, 0.986), but it demonstrated a weak ability to detect incompletely erupted teeth (F1, 0.727; AUC, 0.871) and shallow fissures (F1, 0.632; AUC, 0.934).ConclusionsThe performance of the model was verified in detecting tooth status and screening teeth suitable for fissure sealants, but the scale of rare labels was also inadequate and requires optimization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06866-7.

Introduction

Dental caries are among the most prevalent chronic bacterial infections, imposing substantial threats to the oral and systemic health of individuals [1]. In China, the incidence of dental caries in children has continued to rise over the past decade [2, 3]. They not only physically and psychologically impact adolescents but also impose a significant economic burden on households. Promoting widely accessible oral examinations, healthcare services and primary and secondary prevention strategies can significantly improve the oral health of the population [4, 5], with high cost-effectiveness [6]. Oral healthcare resources are severely inadequate in China. The World Health Organization recommends a dentist-to-population ratio of 1:5000 [7]. Unfortunately, this ratio in China is 1:7768, which is far below the WHO’s recommended guidelines [7]. The insufficiency of dental practitioners dramatically affects the provision of diagnostic and therapeutic services to patients. However, emerging technologies, such as artificial intelligence (AI), have the potential to assist dentists, dental students and nurses in their training and initial screening of patients [8], which could offer substantial benefits with properly developed relational models.

AI leverages advanced image recognition technology and can be used to train automated identification models through transfer learning, utilizing large image datasets. This approach could assist dentists in processing image data from visual inspections, which are simple but crucial examination steps during the oral examination process and are cost effective, noninvasive, and require minimal equipment [9]. An accurate visual inspection result provided by an experienced examiner provides clinical evidence that can aid dentists in formulating preliminary assessments and diagnoses [9]. Numerous studies [10–13] have utilized intraoral photographs with AI and highlighted the merits of this diagnostic approach. Kühnisch [10] used intraoral photographs to distinguish among intact tooth surfaces, incipient carious lesions, and cavitated carious lesions, achieving accuracies of 90.6% for intact tooth surfaces, 85.2% for noncavitated carious surfaces, and 79.5% for cavitated carious surfaces. The reported mean intersection over union (MIoU) was 0.726, which was not significantly different from that reported by clinical experts. AI can also be used to detect gingival inflammation with intro-oral photographs of orthodontic patients, with a reported accuracy of 77.12% [14]. Another study employed an existing dataset of intraoral photographs for the identification of oral squamous cell carcinoma and reported an accuracy of 92.3% [15]. Existing AI methods have demonstrated significant potential for disease recognition when applied to intraoral image datasets. However, most research on intraoral image classification has focused on a single task [10, 11, 14]. In contrast, clinical practice often involves teeth with multiple concurrent features (e.g., caries, indirect restorations, and staining). may be limited in addressing complex clinical scenarios. There is still insufficient research on multiclass classification or differential diagnosis models for these more intricate dental presentations [16].

Additionally, AI research specifically focused on data from the mixed dentition period remains limited, with most studies concentrating on radiographic images rather than intraoral photography [17–19]. Intraoral images are easier to obtain than radiographs and are more valuable for large-scale screening. This developmental phase is characterized by newly erupted permanent teeth, especially first molars, which exhibit reduced mineralization, increased acid susceptibility, and deep fissures, making them more prone to dental caries [20]. Fissure sealing is the most cost-effective method for preventing pit and fissure caries [21, 22] during the mixed dentition phase. In China, PFMs are the primary targets for national public fissure sealant programs, due to their early eruption, complex occlusal morphology, and high susceptibility to caries. Therefore, timely identification of sealing indications is essential, as it can reduce unnecessary public healthcare expenditures and patient visits. Current research on AI application on fissure sealing has focused mainly on evaluating sealant retention conditions [11, 23]. However, studies investigating automatic detection on sealant indications are lacking. Manual surveys remain the primary method for screening children’s teeth in China, resulting in limited coverage due to insufficient medical resources.

As mentioned above, AI has demonstrated excellent ability in image recognition tasks in intraoral photos, making it possible to apply AI to more complex detection tasks. To our knowledge, no application for the automated detection of mixed dentition status or indications of fissure sealing has been developed and evaluated. Therefore, this study aimed to develop a multitask disease detection CNNs (Convolutional Neural Networks) model to identify complex tooth conditions and select teeth that meet the indications for fissure sealing of the first permanent molars in 7- to 9-year-old children with mixed dentition, based on intraoral photographs. The goal was to achieve a diagnostic accuracy of at least 0.9 for the test method [10].

Methods

This study obtained approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Project ID: KQEC-2022-34-01). We used the STARD checklist when writing our report [24].

Study population

For this study, children aged 7–9 years were recruited from three primary schools in Guangzhou, Guangdong Province, China. Informed consent was obtained from both the children and their guardians prior to participation. Children who were unable to cooperate during examinations or oral photography, such as those with mouth-opening restrictions, were excluded. A total of 196 children were included for analysis. The mean age of the participants was 8.02 years (SD: ±0.54), 46.4% (N = 91) were male and 53.6% (N = 105) were female.

Data collection

Data collection included clinical examination results and oral images.

Visual examinations were performed by two female specialists in preventive dentistry, Pang LY (6 years of experience) and Cao YN (4 years of experience) in the Preventive Dentistry Department of the Hospital of Stomatology, Guanghua School of Stomatology, Sun Yat-sen University, in 2022. The clinical examination results were anonymized using codes and recorded in a database, where they were subsequently used for partial label annotations. Caries examinations followed WHO criteria. Caries was recorded when a lesion exhibited a clearly discernible cavity, compromised enamel, or a softened area detected using a CPI probe. Teeth with temporary fillings, or teeth that were both sealed/filled and decayed, were also classified as carious. The differentiation of deep and shallow fissures followed Köning’s standards [25]. Shallow fissures appear with cusps forming a ‘V-angle’ greater than 70°, and the tip of a WHO-type periodontal probe can reach the base of the fissure, visible under good lighting. Deep fissures appear with cusps forming a ‘V-angle’ less than 70°, and the probe tip cannot penetrate the fissure. The bases of deep fissures are not visible even under good lighting.

Intraoral photographs were captured by a physician (Liu ZY), including four images showing the occlusal views of the upper right, upper left, lower left, and lower right dental arches. The teeth were dried, and all the photographs were taken via a digital camera (Canon EOS M200), in preset macro mode, employing autofocus, automatic ISO, and automatic white balance settings. The images were captured under indoor clinic lighting conditions. Patients were instructed to lie as flat as possible and open their mouths to achieve a 90- to 120-degree angle between the maxillary teeth and the floor. Mirrors, retractors, and reflectors were used as needed. A stable airflow was directed across the mirror surface to prevent condensation. To simulate various clinical situations, images featuring conditions, such as missing teeth, unerupted or partially erupted teeth, and orthodontic appliances or restorations, were excluded.

Before anonymization, all facial features were cropped out to protect the privacy of the subjects. To ensure high image quality, 52 photos with duplication, incomplete dental arches, out-of-focus targets, overexposure, dim lighting, foreign object obstructions, or saliva-contaminated occlusal surfaces were excluded. A total of 686 photos containing one-half to one-quarter of the dental arch were included. All images were saved in JPG format with a resolution of 6000*4000 pixels and 72 dpi, without compression.

Image annotation

The data annotation process included dental position segmentation and dental condition classification. All categories were manually labelled with labelme (version 5.1.1). Based on the segmentation mask, each original arch was cropped to obtain images with only one target tooth to eliminate interference from irrelevant tissue. The single-tooth images then underwent a secondary examination and then annotated. A total of 2349 images exhibiting duplication, incomplete occlusal surface, out-of-focus elements, overexposure, insufficient lighting, or saliva contamination were excluded. Finally, 2467 images were included, which was considered acceptable based on previously published studies [10, 11]. Since one tooth could present multiple conditions, a photograph may have had multiple labels. The distributions of all labels are shown in Table 1.

Two physicians (Pang LY and Liu ZY) with over 4 years of clinical experience conducted the image annotation. Labelling was performed on the same monitor and guided by patient records and visual assessments on the monitor. Prior to the formal study, both annotators received a 2-hour training session on the classification standards. To assess annotation consistency, an intra-examiner reproducibility test and an inter-examiner test with an experienced clinician were conducted. A total of 250 single-tooth images were evaluated for caries classification and 100 single-tooth images of first molar were evaluated for fissure morphology classification. There was a 14-day interval between the first and second rounds of annotation, during which the annotators had no access to the target images to prevent recall bias. The intraexaminer reproducibility of caries and fissure morphology were 0.879/0.851 (Liu ZY) and 0.889/0.847 (Pang LY), and the interexaminer reproducibility was 0.833/0.832. After calibration, all the images were pre-annotated by the same examiner (Liu ZY). An experienced dentist (Pang LY) conducted an independent review of all pre-annotated images solely on the basis of visual assessments on the monitor. This ensured that only images suitable for visual diagnosis were included in the final dataset. Images with discrepancies were excluded, and only those with full agreement across all classifications were included in the analysis.

The category of intraoral appliances was particularly noted, including devices fixed on teeth, such as crowns, inlays, and orthodontic attachments, which may hinder children’s oral hygiene. In addition to the tag “pigmentation”, this study introduces the subtag “black stain (BS)”. BS [26] manifests as black lines or spots distributed along the gingival margin in the lower third of the tooth. BS is possibly associated with a reduced incidence of caries [27–29]. Owing to their similar colour, many patients mistakenly perceive BS as dental caries. Parents should be reminded to implement appropriate hygiene measures and distinguish between pigmentation and dental caries to alleviate unnecessary anxiety.

Implement framework

Our proposed method encompasses two stages. In the first stage, pre-processed oral dentition images are input into the Mask R-CNN model for feature extraction. The output feature maps from Mask R-CNN are decoded to obtain oral tooth segmentation and classification. In the second stage, local regions are cropped into single-tooth images based on the segmentation and classification outcomes. Disease classifications are then performed according to the type of cropped image. The entire process and labels are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Stage 1: Identification and segmentation of teeth and positions

In the first stage, a multitask model identifies tooth types and regions in semiarch intraoral images. Accurate tooth type identification is crucial, as different types require specific recognition tasks to reduce model confusion.

Preprocessed oral images are input into a ResNet50 model for feature extraction. The resulting feature maps are then fed into a Region Proposal Network (RPN) to generate candidate bounding boxes. Regions of interest (ROIs) are then extracted for multitask learning. Using different decoder heads in Mask R-CNN [30], tooth type classification, segmentation, and bounding box prediction are performed. Based on the bounding box and classification results, the cropped dentition images are sorted into images containing a single focused tooth, forming a dataset of high-resolution individual tooth images (Fig. 1). These single-tooth images are then utilized for subsequent disease identification tasks.

Stage 2: Identification of dental status

Given the complexity of clinical classification issues and intricate and interactive relationships in oral image analysis, the design process incorporated clinical diagnostic and treatment protocols as prior knowledge. High-resolution cropped images were grouped, and the relationships between disease categories across different groups were modelled accordingly.

Two detection modules were employed. Module 1 was designed to identify the fundamental conditions of teeth, covering five classifications and one subcategory, across all included single-tooth images. Module 2 focused on detecting indications for fissure sealants specifically in images of the first permanent molars. Two additional steps were included to exclude certain images, such as images of teeth with caries, teeth with fillings, or incompletely erupting teeth. The final step involved assessing the morphology of fissures, which were categorized into three types: deep fissures, shallow fissures, and fissures with sealants.

TResNet [31] (Fig. 2), an optimized model of ResNet with excellent feature extraction capabilities, was applied in Stage 2. We applied Global Average Pooling (GAP) and a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) to the output feature maps. To facilitate multi-label learning, the sigmoid activation function was employed. This means that the model frames the task as multiple binary classification problems, allowing it to implicitly learn the correlations between different categories.Fig. 2Architecture of TResNet. Experiment

The first stage had a low-resolution requirement, with specific emphasis on the colour characteristics and positional relationships among different teeth. Thus, the semiarch intraoral images were down-sampled to one-quarter of their original size. After data cleaning, spatial data augmentation operations, including rotation (−10 ~ 10°), random translation (10%), random scaling (10%), and flipping, were applied to simulate various capture scenarios and expand the sample size. Additionally, imgaug (https://github.com/aleju/imgaug) was used to further enhance colour variations with contrast changes and random brightness offsets. The data were split randomly and independently into a training-to-test ratio of 242:44. During hyperparameter tuning, the training set was further divided into a training subset and a validation subset at a ratio of 194:48. Once the optimal hyperparameters were determined, the training and validation sets were merged into a new training set for retraining for utilizing more data [32], and only the results from the final epoch were recorded. The test set was used solely for model evaluation.

The second stage required high image resolution owing to the undeniable impact of local anomalies. A lower resolution may obscure regional abnormalities in the images. The segmented individual tooth images obtained from the previous stage are resized to 448 × 448 as the input size for the model. Spatial and intensity data augmentation, similar to that performed with the stage 1, was also applied. The dividing, merging, and evaluation methods of the dataset are consistent with the program used in the first stage. Finally, in Module 1, the training-to-test ratio was set at 1973:494, with the training set divided into training and validation subsets (1578:395) during hyperparameter tuning. In Module 2, all first permanent molars were used for training and testing (525/142), with no additional validation set created, because both Module 1 and Module 2 employed identical hyperparameters.

To expedite the model training process and enhance the ability to extract structural features from images, both the Mask R-CNN segmentation model and the TResNet disease detection model were pretrained on large datasets (COCO, OpenImagesV6). Notably, our second TResNet model incorporates the parameters from the first model. Due to the small sample size of the first molar tooth, fine-tuning based on the entire dataset of teeth yields better results. The Mask R-CNN model was re-trained for 10 epochs using the SGD optimizer (with a learning rate of 0.005, momentum of 0.9, and weight decay of 0.0005). TResNet was re-trained for 200 epochs using the Adam optimizer (with a learning rate of 0.0003, beta values of 0.9 and 0.999, and an epsilon of 1e − 8). L2 regularization was used in the loss function to penalize the complexity of the model, limit the size of the model weights, and thus reduce overfitting. All methods were implemented using PyTorch and trained on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 Ti graphics processing unit.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using the PyTorch and scikit-learn libraries in Python (https://pytorch.org, https://scikit-learn.org). The IoU was evaluated for the tooth segmentation model. The accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, F1-score, and ROC-AUC were used for the detection model. These results were utilized to evaluate the model’s performance and adjust the parameters for model improvement. To estimate the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for model performance metrics, non-parametric bootstrap for 1000 times resampling with replacement was performed.

CAM [33] was employed to generate heatmaps and locate the areas contributing most significantly to classification decision-making. These regions often point to characteristic areas determining the classification of tooth conditions.

Results

The model accurately segmented the tooth area, with an IoU of 0.95 for the test set. The model recognized tooth positions, achieving an average F1 score of 0.906 for the training set. The classification results were obtained for different tooth positions, with IoU > 0.7, and a confusion matrix was generated (Fig. 3). Discussion

Outcomes

This disease detection model adopted strict inclusion criteria, potentially limiting its applicability in real-world scenarios [10]. Oral health, especially during the mixed dentition phase, which is a period of high susceptibility to caries, presents unique complexities that require special attention. To address these issues, this study is the first to focus on the complex conditions of mixed dentition and fissure sealing indications. For research purposes, the study strictly limited the participants’ age range, ensuring a more clinically meaningful and representative dataset for model development. This approach likely captured the unique dental characteristics of this age group while excluding potential confounding factors from other age groups. Based on this dataset, an initial attempt was made to develop an algorithm tailored for multitask detection in mixed dentition. Given that accuracy (ACC) greater than 90% was achieved in 8 out of 11 categories, the initially formulated hypothesis was supported.

In the classification of caries, the model achieved an AUC of 0.964, closely approximating the results reported in a single-task study. J. Kühnisch et al. [10], who detected caries in clinical intraoral photographs, reported an accuracy of 0.925 and AUC of 0.964. However, owing to stringent methodological requirements, their research did not include diverse images with various restorations and pigmentations, potentially leading to low model performance in real-world scenarios. Eun Young Park et al. [34] developed a deep learning model for detecting caries using intraoral endoscopic images, incorporating images with various influencing conditions (e.g., multiple teeth in a single image, the presence of restorations, and tooth discoloration). Their model is comparable to that used in this study, as both models simulate real-world conditions. They reported a maximum average accuracy of 0.867 and an AUC of 0.837. In this study, the model achieved an AUC of 0.893 for filling identification, which was lower than that of the model proposed by Paula Engels et al. [35] for identifying posterior resin composites and cement restorations (AUC of 0.987). The probable reasons were sample imbalances across different classes and the limited number of true-positive cases.

The present study identified instances of oral pigmentation, including a specific type known as black stain (BS), in some children. While BS has not been reported to be associated with any functional impairments, it has a clear tendency towards high recurrence [36], posing aesthetic and sociopsychological risks. Many parents mistakenly perceive pigmentation as dental caries, leading to unnecessary concerns. To address this, the proposed model was developed to detect tooth pigmentation, including BS features for the first time. The dataset included images with and without dental caries for differentiating between dental caries and pigmentation. This research demonstrates a strong potential for identifying pigmentation, helping to alleviate concerns and encouraging patients to seek timely care and guidance.

In this study, fissure sealing indications were identified using a deep learning model for the first time. Not all healthy molars, especially those not fully erupted or with good self-hygiene surfaces, require sealants [37]. This model was developed for detecting indications aiming to prevent unnecessary sealing of healthy molars, reduce financial costs, and save resources. It distinguished deep fissures (meeting indications), shallow fissures, and the presence of sealant (not meeting indications). No identical research was found in the published literature. Schlickenrieder et al. [11] achieved an AUC of 0.996 in detecting sealant status (unsealed, intact, adequate, or inadequate) for healthy molars. The present results provide valuable insights, particularly regarding the need for secondary sealant application in cases of insufficient sealant, which should be included and emphasized in subsequent research designs.

Limitation

This study has several limitations.

First, the present study employed the WHO criteria for caries, which do not account for noncavitated lesions. This approach was initially chosen to ensure higher annotation consistency and model stability, particularly within the multi-task classification framework this study employed. However, preventive and noninvasive procedures are ideal strategies for caries management [38]. Therefore, greater attention should be directed towards non-cavitated lesions in caries detection models. Detecting and distinguishing between no caries, non-cavitated lesions (ICDAS Codes 1 and 2), and cavitated lesions (ICDAS Codes 3, 4, and 5) could increase the effectiveness of the study design [39, 40]. Future investigations should consider incorporating ICDAS-based annotation schemes for finer stratification and early-stage caries detection, particularly when larger datasets and higher-resolution imaging systems become available.

Second, the dataset utilized is relatively small and focused on a single population. The distribution of samples in this dataset did not match the proportions reported in the literature, as these samples were collected from a general population, whereas most of the published image datasets were derived from hospitals or for record-keeping and educational purposes. Consequently, the representation of rare labels, such as shallow fissures, is relatively inadequate. While data augmentation can partially address this issue [41], future research should prioritize the collection of rare populations and samples. This imbalance could negatively impact model training, as multilabel classification models tend to focus on categories with larger sample sizes, reducing attention to categories with sparse samples. Furthermore, the low diversity of small-sample data limits the data domain represented by the model, consequently decreasing its ability to recognize similar samples. Despite efforts to mitigate the effects of label imbalance during the model design process, effectively addressing this challenge remains difficult. Nevertheless, sample size for most categories in this study was sufficient to support effective model training. Although certain categories had fewer samples, their distinct features make them easier to classify (e.g., fissure sealants and intraoral devices), which is reflected in our model’s high accuracy (ACC > 0.90). In contrast, the low accuracy in predicting shallow fissures can be attributed to the limited number of positive samples and less distinguishable features. This is likely due to the low prevalence of shallow fissures in the study population. Augmenting rare samples could significantly enhance the model’s performance in such tasks.

Applications and future direction

This study presents a preliminary multi-task disease detection model designed for the mixed dentition population. The model aids in monitoring oral health in this group and supports large-scale preliminary screening for fissure sealant indications in primary care settings. As the dataset is further optimized and the model improved, it has the potential to lower the degree of technical barriers for oral health screening, reducing reliance on healthcare professionals and promoting healthcare equity. It is important to emphasize that the model is designed to facilitate timely access to medical services, not to replace clinical diagnosis.

To develop more accurate and generalizable model, data should be at the core of experimentation [16, 42]. Future research should prioritize collecting larger, more diverse samples to expand the dataset. Balancing different sample categories through appropriate oversampling or undersampling techniques is also critical. Currently, clinical research methods for collecting oral imaging data remain highly dependent on human resources and technology, which poses challenges for large-scale sampling. Efforts should focus on improving data acquisition methods or hardware to obtain larger and higher-quality datasets efficiently. The high requirements for intraoral equipment and photography techniques often limit image collection process to clinical situations, restricting the generalizability of the models. An optimized solution would be to promote the use of standardized, low-cost equipment and to simplify and standardize the data collection process, enabling sample collection by non-professionals while also improving robustness. The development of a stable, high-quality imaging device paired with a suitable model could address these challenges in the future.

Conclusion

This study introduces a novel multitask AI model designed to detect tooth status and assist dentists in identifying teeth suitable for fissure sealants. Compared to similar models in other studies, this research achieved a comparable level of high performance. These findings suggest that this model could assist in the preliminary screening of patients, encouraging them to seek professional dental care.
