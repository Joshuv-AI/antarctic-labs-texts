Predictors of Hospitalization due to Febrile Neutropenia in Cancer Patients Receiving Chemotherapy
Authors: Elif Değirmenci Aktaş, Dilek Yildirim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFebrile neutropenia (FN) is a serious and potentially life‐threatening complication of chemotherapy, frequently leading to hospitalization, treatment delays, and increased healthcare utilization. Identifying clinical factors associated with FN‐related hospital admission is essential for improving risk stratification and supportive care strategies.MethodsThis observational study evaluated cancer patients 2207 receiving chemotherapy who developed FN. Clinical, laboratory, and treatment‐related variables—including body mass index (BMI), hemoglobin level, absolute neutrophil count, chemotherapy cycle number, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, and granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF) prophylaxis—were analyzed. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to determine independent predictors of FN‐related hospitalization.ResultsFN‐related hospitalization was associated not only with neutrophil count but also with multiple clinical parameters. Lower hemoglobin levels, poorer ECOG performance status, lower chemotherapy cycle number, and BMI were identified as independent predictors. The inverse association between chemotherapy cycle number and hospitalization risk (OR = 0.96) supports the established pattern of FN occurring more frequently during early treatment cycles. Patients requiring hospitalization had a lower mean number of chemotherapy cycles, consistent with early‐cycle vulnerability. Although the model demonstrated statistical significance, its explanatory power was modest (Nagelkerke R 2 = 0.053), suggesting the contribution of additional unmeasured factors.ConclusionFN‐related hospitalization in patients receiving chemotherapy is influenced by multidimensional clinical factors beyond neutrophil count alone. Performance status, hematologic reserve, BMI, and treatment timing play important roles in risk determination. Early identification of high‐risk patients and implementation of individualized monitoring and prophylactic strategies may improve patient safety and optimize healthcare resource utilization. Prospective, multicenter studies are warranted to develop and validate risk‐based care models aimed at reducing FN‐related hospitalizations.Keywords: cancer, chemotherapy, febrile neutropenia, hospitalization, risk factors

Background

Cancer continues to be one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide. With advancements in early diagnostic approaches and improvements in therapeutic modalities, the number of patients receiving oncological treatment has steadily increased [1, 2]. Currently, chemotherapy is widely utilized in the treatment of solid tumors and hematologic malignancies, either as a single modality or in combination with other therapeutic approaches such as surgery, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy. However, in addition to targeting malignant cells, chemotherapy also affects rapidly proliferating normal cells, leading to various hematologic and systemic adverse effects. Among these, febrile neutropenia represents one of the most serious complications in terms of clinical outcomes and patient safety [1, 2, 3, 4].

Febrile neutropenia (FN) is a clinical condition that arises as a consequence of chemotherapy‐induced bone marrow suppression and is characterized by the presence of fever in the setting of severe neutropenia. It is typically defined by an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) of < 500/mm3, or an ANC < 1000/mm3 with an anticipated decline to < 500/mm3 within a short period of time [5, 6, 7]. Due to the reduction of neutrophils, one of the primary components of the host defense against infections, FN may progress rapidly and lead to life‐threatening complications. This condition increases the likelihood of hospitalization, may necessitate intensive care support, and can result in unfavorable consequences for oncologic treatment continuity, such as chemotherapy dose reductions or delays in treatment administration [2, 6].

FN is recognized as a major contributor to chemotherapy‐related mortality among patients with cancer. The literature reports mortality rates associated with FN ranging from 5% to 20%, with higher rates observed particularly in patients with hematologic malignancies, advanced age, and those who develop sepsis.

In addition, FN imposes a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems. Prolonged hospital stays, the use of broad‐spectrum antibiotics, diagnostic procedures, and the need for intensive care support significantly increase both the direct and indirect costs associated with FN [3, 5, 6].

The development of FN is a complex and multifactorial process that cannot be explained solely by the myelotoxic effects of the administered chemotherapy regimen. Patient‐related factors, including demographic characteristics, the type and stage of the underlying malignancy, comorbid conditions, nutritional status, and performance level, significantly influence the risk of FN. Advanced age, in particular, is associated with a reduced bone marrow reserve and diminished immune response, both of which contribute to an increased susceptibility to FN. Additionally, comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, and chronic pulmonary disorders may further elevate the risk of infection and predispose patients to more severe clinical manifestations of FN [6, 7, 8].

FN often occurs in the absence of a clearly identifiable infectious focus, with fever sometimes representing the only warning sign. Because the clinical manifestations of infection may be subtle in neutropenic patients, FN is considered an oncologic emergency in clinical practice; therefore, prompt initiation of empirical broad‐spectrum antibiotic therapy is recommended as soon as fever is detected. Delays in treatment are associated with an increased risk of sepsis, septic shock, and multiple organ failure.

Accurate risk assessment and the implementation of preventive strategies are critical for ensuring patient safety and maintaining treatment continuity. The use of granulocyte colony‐stimulating factors (G‐CSF) for primary or secondary prophylaxis has been shown to reduce the incidence of FN and its related complications, and current guidelines recommend primary prophylaxis in regimens associated with an FN risk of 20% or higher. However, real‐world data indicate that these recommendations are not always consistently implemented in clinical practice. A multidisciplinary approach, with nursing care playing a central role, is essential in the prevention and management of FN. Early recognition of symptoms, education of patients and caregivers, close monitoring, and effective implementation of infection control measures all contribute significantly to reducing FN‐related morbidity and mortality [8, 9, 10].

Although risk factors and management strategies related to FN have been extensively addressed in the literature, the determinants predicting FN‐related hospitalization may vary across centers due to differences in patient characteristics, chemotherapy practices, and supportive care approaches. In addition to the occurrence of FN itself, the ability to anticipate hospitalization secondary to FN is of critical importance in terms of patient safety, optimal resource utilization, and clinical outcomes [11, 12, 13]. In this context, identifying patient‐, disease‐, and treatment‐related factors that predict hospitalization due to FN in patients receiving chemotherapy will contribute to the development of risk‐based monitoring and preventive strategies. This study aims to investigate the predictors of FN‐related hospitalization among patients undergoing chemotherapy. The findings are expected to facilitate the identification of high‐risk patient groups and to support more robust clinical decision‐making processes.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This prospective, observational cohort study was conducted with 2207 cancer patients who developed febrile neutropenia. The research was carried out between February 2025 and February 2026 in the Oncology, Emergency, and Hematology departments of two different hospitals in Istanbul, Turkey.

Population and Sample

The study sample included patients aged 18 years and older who were receiving ongoing cancer treatment, had been diagnosed with febrile neutropenia (FN), were able to communicate effectively, could understand and respond to the questions, and voluntarily agreed to participate in the study. Patients with communication difficulties, psychiatric disorders, severe comorbid conditions involving other vital organ diseases, or advanced‐stage comorbidities were excluded from the study.

FN was defined as a single oral temperature of ≥ 38.3°C or a sustained temperature of ≥ 38.0°C lasting longer than one hour in the presence of an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) < 1.0 × 109/L.

Data Collection and Instruments

Data were collected using a standardized data collection form, the Participant Information Form, and the hospital electronic health record systems, from which relevant clinical and laboratory parameters were obtained. After providing detailed information regarding the purpose, scope, and procedures of the study, written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study forms were subsequently completed.

Participant Information Form

This form was developed in accordance with the existing literature. It includes questions related to sociodemographic characteristics such as age, sex, educational level, smoking status, and alcohol consumption [14, 15, 16].

Data Analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0. Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage) were used to summarize the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the participants, as well as the scale scores. Data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests, and the results indicated that the data were normally distributed.

To identify independent risk factors associated with hospitalization due to FN, a multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was conducted. Variables considered clinically relevant and those found to be associated in univariate analyses (body mass index, hemoglobin level, neutrophil count, number of chemotherapy cycles, ECOG performance status, age, sex, presence of liver metastasis, and G‐CSF prophylaxis) were included in the model. Variables were selected for multivariable logistic regression analysis based on clinical relevance and a univariate screening approach. Specifically, variables with a p‐value < 0.10 in univariate analyses, as well as factors deemed clinically important according to prior literature, were considered for inclusion in the final model. A stepwise approach was not used; instead, all selected variables were entered simultaneously into the model to avoid selection bias. Underlying disease characteristics were not included in the multivariable model due to their limited variability within the cohort and potential collinearity with treatment‐related variables. Multicollinearity was assessed prior to model fitting, and variables demonstrating high correlation were excluded to ensure model stability and reliable estimation of adjusted effects.

The results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical Approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Koç University Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (Date: 03/02/2025; Approval No: 2025.015.IRB2.011). In addition, detailed information about the study was provided to the participants, and both verbal and written informed consent were obtained prior to data collection. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Sociodemographic Characteristics of the Study Population

The study population consisted of 2207 patients. The average age was 53.6 years (SD: 16.4), with ages ranging from 18 to 90 years. The mean body mass index was 26.3 kg/m2 (SD: 5.0), indicating an overall overweight tendency in the cohort. In terms of gender distribution, males comprised slightly more than half of the sample (54.7%, n = 1208), while females accounted for 45.3% (n = 999). With respect to behavioral characteristics, more than one‐third of the participants were smokers (36.4%), whereas 63.6% reported no smoking history. Alcohol consumption was less common, reported by one‐fifth of the patients (20.3%), while the majority (79.7%) stated that they did not consume alcohol. Comparison Between Patients With and Without Febrile Neutropenia–Related Hospitalization

Comparisons between patients who were hospitalized due to febrile neutropenia and those who were not are presented in Table 4. The mean age did not differ significantly between the two groups (p = 0.640). However, body mass index was significantly lower among patients who experienced febrile neutropenia–related hospitalization compared with those who did not (26.1 ± 4.9 vs. 27.0 ± 5.5 kg/m2; p = 0.001). No significant differences were observed between the groups with respect to gender distribution (p = 0.545), solid tumors disease stage (p = 0.863), presence of liver metastases (p = 0.730). Platelet counts and estimated glomerular filtration rate did not differ significantly between the groups (p = 0.457 and p = 0.838, respectively). In addition, the mean number of chemotherapy cycles was significantly lower in patients who developed febrile neutropenia–related hospitalization compared with those who did not (6.0 ± 4.8 vs. 7.9 ± 7.7; p < 0.001).

After adjustment for potential confounders, body mass index (OR = 0.966, 95% CI: 0.943–0.990; p = 0.006), hemoglobin level (OR = 0.750, 95% CI: 0.668–0.840; p < 0.001), number of chemotherapy cycles (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.93–0.98; p < 0.001), and ECOG performance status (OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.01–1.51; p = 0.043) were identified as independent predictors of hospitalization due to febrile neutropenia.

Specifically, higher body mass index, increased hemoglobin levels, and poorer ECOG performance status were associated with an increased likelihood of febrile neutropenia–related hospitalization, whereas a higher number of chemotherapy cycles was associated with a reduced risk. Neutrophil count showed a trend toward significance but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.089). Age, sex, presence of liver metastasis, and use of G‐CSF prophylaxis were not significantly associated with hospitalization due to febrile neutropenia.

Discussion

In the study population, hematologic malignancies constituted the majority of cases (66%), representing the predominant diagnostic group. This distribution is consistent with trends reported in the literature, which indicate an increasing recognition and diagnosis of hematologic cancers [17, 18]. In our study, diseases such as leukemia and lymphoma were observed significantly more frequently than solid tumors, including gastrointestinal cancers (9.9%) and thoracic malignancies (6.3%). This finding is consistent with reports indicating that hematologic malignancies often predominate in cancer statistics [19, 20]. Moreover, FN is a significant complication observed in more than 80% of patients with hematologic malignancies and is associated with a mortality rate of approximately 10% [21]. In another study, the incidence of FN was reported to be 20.6% among breast cancer patients receiving chemotherapy without prophylaxis [22]. In another study, the incidence of FN was reported as 19.8% in patients with lung cancer, 15.5% in gynecological cancers, 13.3% in breast cancer, and 9.5% in colorectal cancer [23]. The high number of patients with hematologic malignancies in our sample group may be attributed to the fact that FN is a common complication in hematologic cancers.

The comparison between patients hospitalized due to FN and those managed without hospitalization revealed critical differences that may inform clinical practice. Notably, patients admitted for FN had a significantly lower body mass index (BMI) than those who were not hospitalized (26.1 ± 4.9 vs. 27.0 ± 5.5 kg/m2; p = 0.001). This finding is consistent with results reported in previous studies. In a study conducted to investigate the incidence of chemotherapy‐related FN in patients with early‐stage breast cancer and to evaluate its impact on chemotherapy administration, a lower body mass index (BMI < 23 kg/m2) was identified as being associated with an increased risk of FN [16]. Similarly, in a study conducted by [24], the incidence of FN was found to be significantly higher among patients with non–small cell lung cancer who had a BMI ≤ 22.509 kg/m2 [24]. In another retrospective study, lower BMI was found to be associated with the development of FN in patients with small cell lung cancer [25]. Consistent with the findings reported in the literature, our study also identified low BMI as one of the factors associated with an increased risk of FN.

In our study, patients hospitalized due to FN had significantly lower hemoglobin levels and absolute neutrophil counts. This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that low baseline hematologic parameters are associated with the development of FN and unfavorable clinical outcomes. In particular, current guidelines and risk assessment models emphasize that profound neutropenia is directly associated with infection severity, higher complication rates, and an increased need for hospitalization [24, 26]. Similarly, anemia has been reported to reflect reduced bone marrow reserve and overall clinical frailty, thereby potentially contributing to a more severe course of FN [27]. On the other hand, it is noteworthy that the mean number of chemotherapy cycles was lower among patients who were hospitalized due to FN. This finding may suggest that FN tends to occur more frequently during the early phases of treatment and that the development of severe neutropenic complications may compromise treatment continuity. Indeed, recent clinical analyses indicate that FN is more commonly observed during the initial chemotherapy cycles and that hospitalization may lead to reduced dose intensity or delays in subsequent cycles [9, 14, 15, 27]. When these findings are considered collectively, low hemoglobin levels, profound neutropenia, and hematologic toxicities emerging during the early phases of treatment may be regarded as potential contributors to FN‐related hospitalization. Therefore, careful evaluation of hematologic parameters prior to chemotherapy and closer monitoring of high‐risk patients may be of considerable clinical importance. It is important to distinguish FN from febrile neutropenia–related hospitalization (FNH). While FN is primarily a biologically driven event, FNH is a composite outcome influenced not only by infection risk but also by patient clinical condition, comorbidities, healthcare utilization patterns, and clinician judgment regarding hospitalization [26, 28, 29, 30, 31]. This distinction may introduce variability in FNH rates independent of underlying FN risk. Established risk stratification tools, such as the MASCC risk index [30], highlight the multifactorial nature of hospitalization decisions in FN management and support the need to interpret FNH outcomes within this broader clinical context.

Another important finding of the study was that lower body mass index, lower hemoglobin level, fewer chemotherapy cycles, and poorer ECOG performance status were identified as independent predictors of hospitalization due to FN. Following correction of the regression model coding, higher BMI and hemoglobin levels were found to be associated with reduced odds of hospitalization, whereas poorer performance status remained associated with increased hospitalization risk. These findings are consistent with the current literature. The high utilization rate of granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF) in our cohort (> 80%) may have contributed to attenuation of baseline differences in febrile neutropenia–related outcomes across cancer subgroups. This finding likely reflects the impact of effective supportive care strategies rather than intrinsic differences in biological risk, consistent with previous studies. An additional consideration in the interpretation of our findings is the heterogeneous nature of G‐CSF use. In this study, G‐CSF was analyzed as a binary variable; however, clinically relevant distinctions exist, including pegfilgrastim versus daily G‐CSF formulations, primary versus secondary prophylaxis, therapeutic use, and the timing of administration. These factors may substantially influence both the effectiveness of G‐CSF and subsequent clinical outcomes. The lack of granularity regarding G‐CSF administration therefore limits more precise interpretation of its association with febrile neutropenia and related outcomes. Previous studies have highlighted the clinical relevance of these distinctions in optimizing supportive care strategies [26, 29].

Similarly, the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) guidelines define poor performance status (ECOG ≥ 2) as a high‐risk feature for FN‐related complications and hospitalization [9]. In our study, each one‐unit increase in the ECOG score was associated with an increased risk of hospitalization (OR = 1.23), supporting the pivotal role of functional reserve in tolerating infectious complications. Similarly, the identification of hemoglobin level as an independent predictor underscores the impact of hematologic reserve on the clinical course. The updated G‐CSF guideline issued by the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) also states that baseline anemia and reduced bone marrow reserve may be associated with FN‐related complications [14, 15, 26]. Anemia may be considered not only a marker of reduced oxygen‐carrying capacity but also an indicator of bone marrow tolerance to myelosuppression. The inverse association observed with the number of chemotherapy cycles (OR = 0.96) is also consistent with the “early‐cycle febrile neutropenia” pattern described in the literature. Clinical analyses based on European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) guidelines have reported that FN frequently occurs during the initial cycles and that treatment continuity may be compromised in patients who develop severe neutropenia [9, 14, 15, 27]. This may explain why the mean number of chemotherapy cycles was lower in the hospitalized group in our study. The identification of body mass index as an independent predictor also suggests a potential influence of nutritional status and body composition on hematologic toxicity. In a recent analysis conducted in patients with lung cancer, BMI was shown to be associated with the risk of FN [24]. It has been proposed that body composition may affect chemotherapy pharmacokinetics as well as bone marrow reserve. The relatively low explanatory power of the multivariable model (Nagelkerke R 2 = 0.053) suggests that febrile neutropenia–related hospitalization is influenced by a broad range of clinical and treatment‐related factors beyond those included in this analysis. This is expected given the multifactorial nature of the outcome, which is affected by patient characteristics, disease severity, chemotherapy regimens, treatment intensity, and supportive care strategies. The absence of several key variables, including detailed treatment and supportive care data, likely contributed to the limited variance explained by the model.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

When the strengths and limitations of this study are considered together, the findings appear to be clinically meaningful; however, they should be interpreted with caution. The study has important methodological strengths, as it represents one of the large‐sample investigations examining hospitalization due to FN in patients receiving chemotherapy. The relatively high number of participants increased statistical power, and the systematic collection of clinical and laboratory parameters supported the reliability of the data. In particular, the simultaneous evaluation of clinically relevant variables—such as hemoglobin level, absolute neutrophil count, number of chemotherapy cycles, and ECOG performance status—within the same model highlights the multifactorial nature of FNH. The use of multivariable logistic regression analysis enabled adjustment for potential confounding variables and allowed the identification of independent predictors. Furthermore, the analysis of G‐CSF prophylaxis contributed to the assessment of supportive care practices in a real‐world clinical setting. A key limitation of this study is the absence of chemotherapy regimen‐specific risk stratification. Since FN risk is strongly influenced by the myelotoxicity and intensity of administered chemotherapy regimens, the lack of detailed regimen‐level data and guideline‐based FN risk categorization may have resulted in unmeasured confounding and reduced precision in risk estimation. In addition, the relatively low explanatory power of the model (Nagelkerke R 2 = 0.053) suggests that other important clinical and treatment‐related factors influencing FN‐related hospitalization were not captured in the analysis. Variables such as chemotherapy regimen myelotoxicity profiles, dose intensity, infectious focus, and microbiological pathogens were not available, which further limits the comprehensiveness of risk assessment. Another important limitation is the lack of detailed information regarding relative dose intensity (RDI), dose reductions, and treatment delays. These treatment modifications are known to influence the risk of both febrile neutropenia and FN‐related hospitalization and their absence may have introduced residual treatment‐related confounding. Similarly, the absence of detailed G‐CSF‐related data including timing of prophylaxis, dosing variations, formulation differences, and adherence limits a more granular evaluation of supportive care interventions and their impact on outcomes. Finally, although the study included a heterogeneous population of patients with different malignancies, this heterogeneity reflects real‐world oncology practice but may also have introduced variability in clinical characteristics and treatment‐related outcomes. In particular, the inclusion of multiple solid tumor types with distinct biological behaviors and treatment regimens represents an important source of clinical heterogeneity that may influence FN risk and hospitalization patterns. In addition, the study was conducted in selected centers, and the relatively high proportion of hematologic malignancies may limit generalizability to broader solid tumor populations. Overall, while these limitations should be considered, the study provides clinically relevant insights into FNH in a large real‐world cohort.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that hospitalization due to FN in patients receiving chemotherapy is associated not only with neutrophil levels but also with multidimensional clinical factors, including body mass index, hemoglobin level, number of chemotherapy cycles, and ECOG performance status.

The findings indicate that poor performance status and reduced hematologic reserve, in particular, may increase the risk of hospitalization. Moreover, the observation that FN occurs more frequently during the early cycles of treatment suggests that high‐risk patients should be monitored more closely from the outset of therapy.

These results underscore the importance of developing individualized risk assessment models to prevent FN‐related hospitalization. In clinical practice, careful evaluation of performance status and hematologic parameters, along with closer surveillance, early intervention, and appropriate prophylactic strategies in high‐risk patients, may enhance patient safety and promote more efficient use of healthcare resources. Future multicenter, prospective, and interventional studies are warranted to support the development of risk‐based care models aimed at reducing FN‐related hospitalizations.
