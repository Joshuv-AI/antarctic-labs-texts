Stress among parents of preterm infants admitted to neonatal care units of public hospitals in Nepal: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Tumla Shrestha, Archana Pandey Bista, Chandrakala Sharma, Kalpana Silwal Raut, Bishnu Panta, Muna Rana Thapa, Isabel Lawot
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdmission of preterm infants (PTIs) to neonatal care units (NCUs) is stressful for parents, potentially affecting infant–parent attachment, parents’ mental health and parenting behaviour. Identifying stressors is essential for designing interventions that reduce this burden. However, evidence on parental stress among parents of PTIs in the Nepali context is limited. We aimed to assess parental stress and its associated factors among parents of NCU-admitted PTIs.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted among 305 parents of PTIs admitted to NCUs in five randomly selected public hospitals in Nepal. Data were collected through in-person interviews using a 5-point Likert scale-based Parental Stress Questionnaire (1=not stressful to 5=extremely stressful). Stress levels were categorised as mild (<3), moderate (3–4) and severe (≥4) based on median scores. Descriptive and inferential statistics, including multivariable logistic regression, were applied.ResultsParents had moderate levels of stress (median score and IQR: 3.0 (2.6–3.4)). Their most common stressor was separation and altered parental role 3.3 (2.6–3.6), which was significantly higher among mothers (p<0.001). Stress level showed significant associations with parental variables (age, education, family income and antenatal clinic check-up status) and infant variables (mode of birth, birth weight and hospitalisation duration) (p<0.050). Parents whose infants were hospitalised for ≥15 days were 2.4 times more likely to experience higher stress compared with those with ≤14 days of stay (adjusted OR 2.415; 95% CI 1.420 to 4.081; p<0.001).ConclusionsNCU admission of PTIs imposes considerable stress on parents. Separation and altered parental roles are the main stressors, particularly among mothers. Longer hospitalisation significantly increases stress levels. Interventions that minimise parent–infant separation and promote parental involvement in care may help to mitigate parental stress.Keywords: Neonatology, Low and Middle Income Countries, Caregivers, Psychology

Introduction

Globally, the estimated preterm birth rate is 10.6%, with regional variations between 8.7% and 13.4%.1 Southern Asia has the highest preterm birth rate (13.2%).2 It is in an increasing trend (9.9% of all births in 2020, 9.8% of all births in 2010).3 Its incidence is reported as 9.3%–14% in Nepal.4 5 Preterm infants (PTIs) are vulnerable to mortality, morbidity and disability. Globally, prematurity and its related complications remain the leading cause of neonatal and under-5 mortality.6

PTIs require special care in the neonatal care units (NCUs) to manage prematurity-related complications for a few days to several weeks or months.2 Evidence shows a higher level of stress among parents of PTIs in the NCUs.7 Studies also reported substantial psychological and emotional burden among parents of PTIs.8 9 For instance, a study among 260 mothers in the NCU showed significantly higher anxiety (66.2% vs 46.9%) and depression (45.4% vs 23.1%) among parents of PTIs compared with those of term infants.8 A previous study showed a significant association of stress with psychological problems in the postpartum period.10 Stress and psychological problems interrupt mother–infant bonding in the postpartum period.9 11 Inadequate infant–parent bonding and psychological problems might result in ineffective parental role with an effect on the development of the PTI.9 11

Identifying stressors and stress levels of parents in NCUs might be useful in planning an intervention to minimise the substantial effects of parental stress and negative feelings.9 12 13 Their stress originates from the premature birth, health status of the mother and infant, infant–parent separation, altered parenting role, NCU environment and resources needed. Evidence from India and the global context reported sight and sound in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) as the most common stressor, followed by behaviour of the infant and parental role alteration.714,16 A hospital-based study conducted among mothers of NICU-admitted infants in Nepal identified similar infants’ appearance and behaviour as the main stressor.17 Whereas other studies indicated parental role alteration as the most common stressor, followed by the infant’s looks and behaviour.18,20

Existing studies in Nepal reported divergent findings about parental stress among parents of infants in NCUs, whereas a paucity of evidence is available about parental stress and stressors among parents of PTIs in the Nepali context. Therefore, the study aimed to assess stressors, stress levels and their associated factors among parents of PTIs, including a larger sample size from multiple settings.

Methods

Design and settings

A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted in NCUs (both NICUs and sick newborn care units) of public hospitals in Nepal. NCUs having >8 neonatal beds with neonatal intensive care services were selected for the study. Among 12 eligible hospitals, five hospitals were selected randomly from two clusters: Hospitals within the Kathmandu Valley (capital city) (3/4) and Hospitals outside the valley (2/8). The study settings were Tribhuvan University, Teaching Hospital (TUTH), Paropakar Maternity and Women’s Hospital (PMWH), Kanti Children Hospital (KCH) in Kathmandu; Bharatpur Hospital, Bharatpur Chitwan and Lumbini Provincial Hospital (LPH), Butwal Rupendehi. Three hospitals were selected from outside the Kathmandu valley. One setting selected from outside the valley was cancelled due to an issue with administrative approval. More hospitals were selected from Kathmandu because of the availability of referral cases from across the country and the feasibility of data collection.

Among the study sites, KCH is a specialised children’s hospital with 27 neonatal beds (12 NICU beds) serving outborn infants from Kathmandu and beyond. PMWH, a maternity referral hospital with 60–80 daily deliveries, has an NCU with 40 beds (10 NICU beds) primarily serving inborn infants. TUTH in Kathmandu and other hospitals outside Kathmandu are general hospitals, including maternity and paediatric healthcare services. These hospitals provide services to both inborn and outborn infants. Hospitals included from Kathmandu are referral tertiary-level hospitals, so they receive referred cases from all over the country. TUTH, a university hospital, and other government academic hospitals provide care to a significant number of PTIs. Three hospitals (PMWH, Bharatpur Hospital and LPH) have the provision of free newborn care services. All the government hospitals have heavy case flow in their NCUs and high nurse-to-infant ratio.21

Population and sampling

The study population was the parents of the NCU-admitted PTIs. All parents (either mother or father) of PTIs admitted to NCUs of the study settings for ≥4 days and who were willing to participate were included in the study. Parents were recruited in the study until the fulfilment of sample size (consecutive sample). Sample size (295) was calculated using the Cochrane formula (n=Z α2 σ2/e2) at a 95% level of significance, 5.5% allowable error, and considering the mean stress score (3.14±0.48) among parents of infants admitted in NICU, Dharan, Nepal19

(1.96)2×(0.48)2/(0.055)2=295.

Parents whose PTIs were transferred from NCU to the mother–baby unit or kangaroo mother care unit after recovery were recruited for the study. Either the mother or father of a PTI was included in the study voluntarily.

Data collection and instrumentation

Eligible respondents were approached for participation in the study. After obtaining consent, in-person interviews were conducted by the first, fourth and fifth authors in colloquial Nepali language from October 2020 to February 2021. Interviews were conducted after stability in the condition of their PTIs in NCUs. Mothers were the main attendees in the hospital as they were provided a bed in the postnatal ward or in the mother’s room. Fathers were present during the early phase, then they were present on and off to attend their jobs and for the overall management. Therefore, more mothers were recruited for the data collection compared with fathers. If both mothers and fathers were present at the time of data collection, one of them participated voluntarily. In-person interviews were conducted in colloquial Nepali language by the first author in Kathmandu and by two coauthors in study sites outside Kathmandu. Co-authors were provided with orientation about taking informed consent, questionnaire and interviewing technique, and then involved in data collection. The first author also monitored the data collection procedure on those sites. Interviews were conducted according to a convenient time for the parents. Parents were interviewed in the counselling room in the unit after completion of the routine counselling session. It took around 20 minutes to complete the interview. Data were collected using a parental stress questionnaire as well as socio-demographic and obstetric information-related questionnaires.

An instrument to measure parental stress was developed based on in-depth interview findings among the parents of NCU-admitted PTIs22 and the Parental Stress Scale: NICU.23 The instrument had four domains and 17 items: NCU environment (6 items), appearance, condition and treatment need of the baby (6 items), roles and responsibilities of the baby (3 items) and management of the situation (2 items). Parents’ responses to each item were rated as ‘1’: not at all stressful, ‘2’: a little stressful (to some extent), ‘3’: moderately stressful, ‘4’: very stressful, ‘5’: extremely stressful. The total score on the scale was 85.

The developed instrument was translated into the Nepali language and back-translated into the English language by a translator blind to the original instrument. The instrument was pretested among respondents who attended two academic hospitals in Kathmandu. The internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha coefficient) of the scale was 0.82.

Data analysis

The responses for the 5-point rating scale were dichotomised as having stress (responses for 3–5) and not having stress (responses for 1 and 2), and reported as numbers and percentages. The median and IQR were used to report the subscale and total scale findings, as the data were non-normal (p=0.022, Shapiro-Wilk Test). Obtained scores were reported as high and low-level stress based on the median score as the cut-off point. The Chi-square test was used to assess the association of stress levels with selected parents’ and infants’ variables. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to determine the difference between subscale and scale stress levels among parents. Logistic regression was used to identify the contributing factors of parental stress. The logistic regression model was constructed, including independent variables that had a significant association with the dependent variables (p<0.050). The adjusted OR (aOR) was calculated to identify the determinants of parental stress (table 1).

Regarding their educational status, 181 (59.3) had school-level education, 122 (40.0%) were homemakers (unpaid household work), 168 (55.2%) belonged to a joint family. More than half, 162 (53.1%) of the parents were first-time parents and 206 (67.5%) had attended the antenatal clinic four or more times (table 2).

The stress level of the parents was significantly associated with age, educational status, family income and antenatal clinic visit status (p<0.05) (table 1). Furthermore, an association was also found with the type of delivery, birth weight and hospitalisation duration of the infants (p<0.05) (table 1).

The logistic regression analysis showed that parents whose infants were hospitalised for ≥15 days were 2.4 times more likely to experience higher stress compared with those with a hospital stay of ≤14 days (aOR 2.415, 95% CI 1.420 to 4.081, p=0.001). Similarly, parents who had assisted or operative deliveries were 1.8 times more likely to report higher stress than those with normal deliveries (aOR 1.825, 95% CI 1.093 to 3.048, p=0.022). In addition, parents with education up to the secondary level were 1.6 times more likely to experience higher stress compared with those with higher secondary or above education (aOR 1.665, 95% CI 1.040 to 3.023, p=0.025) (table 1).

Discussion

Study findings showed that admission of PTIs in NCU was moderately stressful to parents with a median score (IQR) of 3.0 (2.6–3.4). Some prior studies reported higher, moderate and lower stress with mean/median scores: >4.0,15 18 24 (3.0–4.0),7 13 18 and 2.8±0.6 of stress.25

In this study, the hospitalisation duration, as well as separation and altered parental roles, was common stressors for parents. Similar higher stress regarding separation and altered parental roles, like being unable to breastfeed, is reported by previous studies from Nepal, India and other countries.7 13 18 24 26 The situation is significantly common among the parents of PTIs due to uncertainty of outcomes, physical separation and altered/inadequate parental role.7 8 Their stress is related to prolonged separation and inadequate involvement of parents in parenting roles. Those parents also suffer from infant–parent attachment and psychological problems.20 Literature reported that parents who spent adequate time with their infants, were involved in caring for their infants like kangaroo mother care, and experienced good communication with NCU staff, had a positive experience in NCUs.20

Inconsistent with prior studies,7 13 27 parents in the present study were less stressed regarding appearance, pain and suffering, and use of tubes and equipment for infants. Furthermore, findings from India and Iran revealed that the NCU environment, including sight and sound, is related to higher parental stress,7 13 15 27 whereas the NCU environment was the least stressful in the present study. Parents in a prior study in Nepal28 reported limited access to parents in NCUs in the initial period of admission, scheduled access after starting feeding to the infant for providing expressed breastmilk, and unit-wise variation in visiting policies. Less stress related to the NCU environment, appearance, pain and suffering of infants might be related to less exposure of parents during the critical condition of their PTIs.

Previous studies reported significantly higher stress among mothers compared with fathers for the overall scale and different subscales (condition of PTIs, altered parental roles, NCU environment).7 15 27 The present study also identified higher stress among mothers for the subscales and overall scale. However, the difference was significant only for the altered parental roles, indicating higher stress among mothers compared with fathers. Fathers had higher stress related to the management of finances and resources, though it was statistically insignificant.

The finding of significantly higher stress among a lower age group (<25 years) parents and a low educational status (secondary or lower) in the present study was supported by studies in India and other countries.15 Similar to the present evidence, prior studies reported a significant association of stress level with birth weight, type of birth and the number of ANC visits.15 18 26

The present and previous studies identified an association of stress level with family income status and hospitalisation duration.15 18 In this study, duration of hospitalisation was the main determinant of parental stress, followed by mode of delivery and educational status. A previous study17 including 814 family caregivers who attended 11 hospitals in Nepal reported 40% out-of-pocket expenditure of families for treatment of sick newborns regardless of socioeconomic status. The highest expenditure was among families of PTIs, with significantly higher expenditure among PTIs who had a hospitalisation duration of >10 days. Their significant expenditure was on accommodation and travel to the hospital. Another study by Shrestha et al22 identified expenses (treatment and accommodation related) and lack of income generation activities due to engagement in the hospital as an important reason for parental stress during the longer duration of hospitalisation. Parents from hospitals that have free newborn care services reported less stress related to expenses. It indicates the necessity of enhancement of inpatient-free-newborn-care service provision to minimise stress related to unavoidable longer hospitalisation among parents of PTIs.

The strength of this study is that the study was conducted involving parents of PTIs from randomly selected hospitals in Nepal. Involvement of both mothers and fathers also identified broader perspectives. However, the involvement of parents with PTIs having a positive outcome might limit the generalisation among similar parents. A cross-sectional design with data collection at a single point in time might not represent parental stress in various situations and aspects of the phenomenon.

Conclusions

Admission of PTIs in NCUs is moderately stressful for parents. Separation and altered parental roles are identified as the most common stressors for parents, especially among mothers. Higher stress levels were observed among parents of lower age groups, lower educational and income status and those with inadequate ANC visits. Infant-related factors associated with increased stress were operative birth, lower birth weight (<1500 g) and longer hospitalisation of the PTIs. Among these, hospitalisation duration emerged as a key determinant of stress level. Study findings indicate the importance of strategies to enhance infant–parent attachment and parental involvement in NCUs to minimise parental stress in NCUs.
