Sagittal-oriented articular processes and intervertebral disc degeneration phenotypes can concurrently lead to an increased presence of annulus tears in the cranial adjacent motion segment
Authors: Zhipeng Xi, Yimin Xie, Shenglu Sun, Fei Hu, Jingchi Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimsIntervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) and sagittal-oriented articular processes can restrict motility and increase stiffness of the motion segment, potentially causing compensatory stress and higher motility in adjacent segments. It is unclear if these factors trigger IDD progression in adjacent segments. This study aimed to elucidate this using functional MRI, and identify biomechanical mechanisms with a validated numerical model.MethodsClinical data from 95 patients were retrospectively collected from January 2022 to April 2023. Disc collapse and fibrosis were assessed by disc height ratio and fractional anisotropy (FA) values in the L4-L5 segment. The orientation of articular facet processes in L4-L5 was examined. Annulus fibrosus integrity was investigated using diffusion tensor fibre tractography in cranial (L3-L4) and caudal (L5-S1) segments. Statistical analyses determined differences between patients with and without annulus tears, and regression analyses identified predictors of annulus tears. Numerical models of L3-S1 motion segment were developed, incorporating variations in disc collapse, fibrosis, and facet orientation angles in L4-L5. Stress distribution on cranial and caudal discs was calculated under various loading conditions.ResultsCompared to patients with intact annulus at the cranial segment (L3-L4), those with annulus tears show reduced facet orientation angles and disc height ratios, and elevated FA values. These parameters are independent risk factors for cranial annulus tears, not observed on the caudal side. Models with sagittal-oriented articular processes (facet orientation = 35°), disc collapse, and fibrosis show higher stress on the cranial disc, particularly within the annulus, compared to models with coronal-oriented processes (facet orientation = 65°) and healthy discs.ConclusionSagittal orientation of articular processes and IDD phenotypes may increase the risk of annulus tears in the cranial adjacent segment by compromising the biomechanical environment. This offers a novel perspective for understanding biomechanical interactions in adjacent segments during IDD progression.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2025;14(9):777–790.

Introduction

Lumbar degenerative disease (LDD) is a prevalent condition among the elderly population.1-4 Deterioration of the local biomechanical environment is a main aetiology of this disease.3,4 Intervertebral disc (IVD) degeneration (IDD) represents one of the primary aetiological factors contributing to local biomechanical deterioration.5,6 Disc fibrosis and collapse are two main phenotypes of IDD, which can trigger stiffness increase in the motion segment, causing abnormal stress distribution in both IVD and facet cartilages.7,8 Meanwhile, alterations in the morphological parameters of articular processes can influence the local biomechanical environment. Specifically, changes in facet orientation can impact the ability of motion restriction. Compared to coronal-oriented articular processes, sagittal orientation can provide a better restriction of spinal motion, which can also increase stiffness in its corresponding segment.9,10

Biomechanical interactions between adjacent motion segments are widely recognized as significant contributors to the progression of IDD.11,12 The pathological mechanism underlying adjacent segment disease (ASD) following lumbar interbody fusion (LIF) surgery serves as a pertinent example of this phenomenon.13,14 Studies indicate that increased stiffness within a specific segment is a fundamental mechanism driving the progression of ASD.13,14 Since IDD phenotypes can similarly elevate stiffness in particular motion segments, these alterations may provoke compensatory biomechanical changes in adjacent segments.4,15 Furthermore, sagittally oriented articular processes, as previously mentioned, can enhance motion restriction.9,16 Theoretically, this may also increase the stiffness of motion segments under corresponding loading conditions and induce higher stress values on adjacent segments through similar mechanisms. However, clinical follow-up of the current patient cohort remains essential to further validate our research conclusions. Additionally, the standard calibrated model may overlook the potential significance of additional factors.

This study primarily aimed to investigate the potential biomechanical impact of increased L4-L5 stiffness, resulting from both DD phenotype and the sagittal orientation of articular processes, on the acceleration of IDD in adjacent segments. A biomechanical analysis grounded in clinical evidence was employed to explore this topic. These findings may offer a novel perspective on the mechanisms of LDD through the lens of biomechanical interactions in adjacent motion segments.

Methods

Retrospective review of demographic and imaging data

We retrospectively reviewed the demographic and radiological data of 95 patients. The study included patients experiencing chronic low back pain (LBP) with symptoms persisting for more than three months, with a mean symptom duration of 5.3 months (SD 0.8). These patients underwent imaging examinations between January 2022 and April 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) patients experiencing LBP; 2) patients with complete lumbar MRI, including T1-weighted (T1), T2-weighted (T2), short time inversion recovery (STIR), DTI sequences, and CT scans conducted in the imaging department of our hospital; 3) IDD grades assessed according to the Pfirrmann grading system.17,18 A comparative analysis of IDD grades was performed for the L3-L4, L4-L5, and L5-S1 segments. It was observed that different motion segments exhibited varying degrees of IDD, with patients exhibiting the most severe IDD in the L4-L5 segment being included in this study. According to the Pfirrmann grading system – a classification scheme for IDD based on T2-weighted MRI, which primarily relies on the reduction in signal intensity of the nucleus and the loss of disc height as the main criteria for grading – the primary indicators for IDD were reductions in signal intensity on T2-weighted MRI and disc height.17,19 Consequently, patients demonstrating lower signal intensity and reduced disc height in the L4-L5 IVD compared to other segments were selected for inclusion in this study.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) patients with a history of pedicle screw fixation (with artifacts); 2) patients with alcohol addiction or chronic use of the hormone cortisol; 3) patients with grade 2, 3, or grade 4 degenerative spondylolisthesis or any grade of spondylolysis (i.e. isthmic spondylolisthesis); 4) patients who underwent imaging examinations at other hospitals. Patient demographic data, including age, sex, and BMI, were also recorded according to their medical records (Figure 1).

Disc fibrosis and collapse status of the L4-L5 IVD

The fractional anisotropy (FA) values, as measured by functional MRI techniques, have been selected to accurately assess disc fibrosis status.20,21 Additionally, the disc height ratio of the L4-L5 segment has been measured to mitigate the confounding effects of patient body size when evaluating disc collapse status. These imaging-based parameters have been previously reported in our published studies.22,23 A detailed description of these methods is presented herein. FA value of the L4-L5 segmental nucleus was measured to represent the disc fibrosis status by using the following methods: MRI was performed using a multitransmit scanner system with a dedicated six-channel spine coil (Discovery MR750 3.0 T; GE Healthcare, USA). T2-weighted sequence (repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE): 2,500 ms/105 ms; field of view (FOV): 512 × 512; receiver bandwidth: variable; 4.5 mm slice with a gap of 0.9 mm; number of excitations: 2) and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) sequences (TR, 6,320 msec; TE, 73.1 msec; b-values, 0 and 600 mm2/sec; slice thickness, 3 mm; matrix, 256 × 256; FOV, 260 mm; diffusion direction, 15) were obtained for these patients. The raw DTI data were processed using qMRLAB to generate FA images.24,25 Values of nucleus FA were measured in the central transverse plane of the IVD to judge disc fibrosis status. The border of the region of interest (ROI) of FA was identified as follows: on the first echo image T2WI, a length from the anterior to the posterior of the IVD was drawn, and the length was divided by 1:3:1. At the anterior and posterior sides of the annulus, hand tools were used to draw a rectangle (length is 1 cm, width accounts for the whole length 1/5); the remaining part of the NP draws a circle with a diameter of 60% of the whole length. All ROIs were transferred via ‘copy and paste’ into the DTI (Figure 2).26,27 Additionally, disc height ratios have been judged on the central sagittal plane of lumbar CT scan. When measuring this parameter, the heights of the anterior, middle, and posterior disc spaces were recorded, and the mean value of these heights was calculated as the disc height. The height of the vertebral bodies was also measured by the same method, and the ratio of the disc to the vertebral body was calculated to determine the degree of disc collapse (Figure 2).22,28

Measurement of facet orientation angle of the L4-L5 facet joint

Facet orientation was measured on the central transverse plane of IVD in the lumbar CT scan. In this process, two different lines, including a line from the posterior edge of the spinous process to the central position of IVD, and a line through the anterior to posterior edges of the articular process were drawn, and the angle between these lines was measured to judge the facet orientation.9,10 Left and right side facet orientation values were separately measured; the mean facet orientation value of left and right facet orientations was calculated to represent the facet orientation of the L4-L5 motion segment (Figure 2).

Annulus integration status of the L3-L4 IVD

According to published studies on the same topic,23 the DTI-based tractography (DTT) technique offers a precise and noninvasive assessment of annulus integration status, both in the presence and absence of annulus tears. Considering that annulus tears substantially contribute to the progression of IDD, the integration status of the annulus in both cranial and caudal adjacent motion segments has been independently evaluated. This evaluation aims to investigate the potential incidence of IDD progression associated with DD phenotype and the sagittal orientation of the L4-L5 facet joints. In this process, tractograms were generated from the DTI data using 3D Slicer29 and DSI Studio30 software. The radiologist (SS) manually delineated the discs on the DTI images using 3D Slicer software and saved them as ROI masks. Subsequently, the masks were imported into DSI Studio to create tractograms of the discs.31,32 Fibre tracking was initiated from manually selected seeds in the region corresponding to the annulus, using all orientations within a voxel until 1,000,000 tracts were detected. The detection of the annulus was optimized by consistently maintaining specific parameters across all patients and longitudinal scans: a threshold of 0.1 to obtain minimum FA for tracking termination, maximum tract turning angle of 30°, step size of 0.625 mm, and tract length constraint of 4 to 10 mm. Finally, each annulus’s status in the tractography rendering was scored by the same radiologist; a score of 0 indicated an intact annulus, while a score of 1 demonstrated annular tears (Figure 1).21,23

Numerical mechanical simulations

The alterations in stress distribution on the IVD of the L3-L4 and L5-S1 motion segments were calculated using a rigorously validated numerical model of the L3-S1 region. The strategies for model construction, calibration, and validation have been consistently employed in similar studies.33,34 The osseous structures, including cortical bone, cancellous bone, and bony endplates (BEPs), were modelled using fitted curves, with the cortical thickness standardized at 0.8 mm. The morphological parameters of the BEPs, such as thickness, concave angle, and depth on both coronal and sagittal planes, were delineated based on anatomical measurements and imaging data.35,36

The nonosseous components comprised the IVD and facet cartilage. The IVD is composed of the nucleus pulposus, the surrounding annulus fibrosus, and the cartilage endplates (CEPs). The nucleus occupied 38% of the IVD’s cross-sectional area, with its central point positioned slightly posterior to the IVD’s centre (the ratio between the anterior and posterior portions of the annulus in the central sagittal plane was 1.62), and the average radius of the annulus was set at 95.5% of the corresponding superior vertebral body.35,36 The outline of BEP covers the entire IVD with specific placement on cranial and caudal sides as well as inner part of annulus.37,38 In defining material properties, cortical and cancellous bone were defined with anisotropic laws, while other components were defined as isotropic materials.39,40 The annulus was assumed to be a hypoelastic material with stiffness defined separately for different layers and parts. The nucleus was set as a semifluid incompressible material. Ligaments and facet joint capsules were set as cable elements with different cross-sectional areas.33,34

The current numerical model was validated by calculating the range of motion (ROM), intradiscal pressure (IDP), disc compression (DC), and facet contact force (FCF) under various loading conditions and comparing these calculated values with experimental data from published studies.41,42 In the context of numerical mechanical simulation, ‘well validated’ is a specific concept that must satisfy two criteria. First, the discrepancy between the calculated values and the mean values from mechanical tests should be less than one SD from the mechanical test results.8,38 Second, model validation should be performed under diverse loading conditions and should include comparisons of various parameters such as stress and motility values. Research has shown that computational outcomes from a well-validated numerical model—defined as one that meets both of the aforementioned criteria—can accurately simulate real biomechanical environments, particularly under loading conditions that align with the model’s validation parameters (Figure 3).22,33

Construction for facet orientation difference and IDD phenotype in the L4-L5 motion segment

The following modifications have been implemented in the model, building upon the well-validated framework previously mentioned. Models with varying facet orientations were developed based on this validated model. The quartiles of facet orientation values were calculated, revealing values of 37.52° and 64.73° at the 25th and 75th percentiles, respectively. As a result, two different models with FO values of 35° and 65° were created in this process. To optimize computational efficiency, facet orientation was kept consistent on both the left and right sides within each model. This construction strategy ensures symmetry along the sagittal plane, permitting unilateral calculation of bending and axial rotation loading conditions (i.e. only right bending and rotation loading were applied in the current models). Furthermore, disc collapse and fibrosis were simulated separately to delineate their biomechanical significance. In simulating disc collapse, the disc height was reduced by 33%, while the rotation centre of the articular processes remained unchanged.22,43 When simulating disc fibrosis, the elastic modulus of the annulus and nucleus has been increased, and the cross-sectional area ratio between the annulus and nucleus has been increased by 40% (simulating dehydration, atrophy, and fibrosis of the nucleus), while maintaining unchanged IVD area and height.22,43 The model characterized by coronal-oriented articular processes and healthy IVD was designated as the healthy model (Model 1). Models exhibiting sagittal-oriented articular processes, disc collapse, and fibrosis were classified as Model 2, Model 3, and Model 4, respectively (Figure 3).

All degrees of freedom were fixed under the inferior surfaces of S1.33,44 Various directional loading conditions, encompassing flexion, extension, left and right lateral bending, and rotational moments, were applied to the superior surface of the L3 vertebra. During the mesh generation process, hybrid elements of varying sizes, including tetrahedral and hexahedral meshes, were employed across different components. Smaller mesh sizes were specifically chosen for thin structures and those undergoing significant deformations, such as facet cartilage, cortical bone, and the posterior regions of the annulus.45,46 Consistent mesh density was maintained across the different models to ensure computational reliability. The average mesh quality exceeded 0.8 in the current finite element analysis (FEA) models. A mesh convergence test was conducted on the calibrated intact model by assessing the variation in intradiscal pressure (IDP) with different mesh sizes.45,46 The model was deemed to have achieved convergence when the change in calculated IDP was less than 3%. The interaction between facet cartilages was modeled as frictionless. Variations in stress distribution on the cranial and caudal IVDs were calculated under different directional moments, with the moment values consistent with those used for the validation of ROMs (Figure 2).

Statistical analysis

To assess the interobserver and intraobserver reliability, a total of 20 patients were randomly selected. One week after the initial measurement of the imaging parameters mentioned above, the values for these patients were remeasured by both a radiologist and a well-trained spine surgeon (YX, SS) (with three years of experience in musculoskeletal imaging measurements). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was separately calculated to determine the repeatability of facet orientation, disc height ratio, and FA values.47,48 Kappa values were also calculated to assess interobserver and intraobserver repeatability in judging and annulus integrity status.23,49

Statistical analyses for annulus tears in cranial (L3-L4) and caudal (L5-S1) motion segments were performed independently. When comparing differences between patients with different annulus integrity statuses (with and without annulus tears), independent-samples t-test was used for continuous variables, while the chi-squared test was used for categorical variables; significance was set at p < 0.05.50,51 Logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify independent predictors for annulus tears. Univariate analyses of demographic and imaging-based parameters were performed separately, with variables achieving a significance level of p < 0.1 being entered into multivariate analyses.33,50 Variables with p < 0.05 were considered independent predictors in the multivariate analyses. Finally, ROC curve analyses were performed to evaluate the predictive value of different parameters; the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated as an indicator of predictive performance.33,50

Results

Clinical review

A total of 95 patients (51 male and 44 female) with LBP and the most severe intervertebral disc degeneration IDD at the L4-L5 motion segment, compared to other lumbar segments, were enrolled in this study. The mean age of the participants was 53.83 years (SD 14.94). Excellent inter- and intraobserver reliability was demonstrated, with all calculated Kappa and ICC values exceeding 0.8 (Table I). Consistent with published studies, the L5-S1 segment exhibited a higher incidence of annulus tears compared to the L3-L4 segment. The prevalence of annulus tears in the cranial adjacent segment was 23.16%, while in the caudal segment it was 46.31%.

Numerical mechanical simulations

A consistent trend of variation is evident across different numerical models, offering a plausible explanation for clinically observed phenomena. To evaluate the potential risk of annulus tears, maximum annulus stress and IDP were calculated under various physiological loading conditions. In the cranial motion segment, when compared to Model 1—which features coronal-oriented articular processes with a facet orientation of 65° and a healthy intervertebral disc without collapse or fibrosis—elevated annulus stress values were observed in the disc collapse model across all physiological loading conditions. Furthermore, under extension loading conditions, the annulus stress values in the sagittal-oriented articular processes model (Model 2) and the model with disc fibrosis (Model 4) were lower than those in Model 1. However, during flexion, bending, and rotation loading conditions, these models exhibited higher stress values than Model 1. The most pronounced difference was noted under bending loading conditions, where annulus stress values increased by 26.06%, 27.27%, and 17.69% for Models 2, 3, and 4, respectively, compared to Model 1. Similarly, the three models exhibited varying degrees of increases in IDP values compared to Model 1 across all loading conditions. Conversely, no definitive trends in variation were observed for either annulus stress or IDP values on the caudal side, with differences among the models generally remaining below 5% under most loading scenarios (Figure 6 and Figure 7).

Discussion

As a common degenerative condition, the prevalence of lumbar degenerative disease has progressively increased in conjunction with the ageing population trend in China, resulting in substantial social and economic burdens. The aetiologies of IDD, recognized as the primary initial trigger for lumbar degenerative disease, have been extensively documented.5,52 Among these aetiologies, the deterioration of the local mechanical environment has been identified as a critical factor contributing to IDD. Consequently, identifying potential risk factors for IDD and elucidating its biomechanical mechanisms can offer new insights into the underlying pathological processes. Biomechanical interactions occur not only among different components within a specific motion segment, but also between adjacent segments.4,15

This phenomenon offers a plausible explanation for various diseases. As previously discussed, research into the biomechanical mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of ASD has identified that an increase in stiffness within a specific motion segment can induce compensatory stress concentration and heightened mobility in adjacent segments.53,54 Similar studies suggest that the progression of IDD can result in increased stiffness in the affected motion segment. Furthermore, sagittally oriented articular processes may exacerbate motion restriction, thereby contributing to increased stiffness under certain physiological loading conditions. Building on this theory, the present study hypothesizes that these pathological and morphological changes may also initiate biomechanical deterioration and contribute to the progression of IDD in adjacent motion segments.

To validate this hypothesis and offer a novel perspective on the biomechanical interactions in adjacent segments during IDD progression, this study conducted a comprehensive investigation comprising an imaging data-based clinical review and corresponding numerical mechanical simulations. In the clinical review based on imaging data, the study demonstrates that the sagittal orientation of articular processes and the progression of IDD are associated with a higher incidence of annulus tears in the cranial adjacent motion segment. Correspondingly, numerical models exhibit elevated stress values in scenarios involving sagittally oriented articular processes, disc collapse, and fibrosis. Consequently, both clinically observed and calculated results corroborate the primary hypothesis of this study: the increase in stiffness due to IDD progression and variations in facet orientation not only impact the biomechanical environment of the corresponding motion segment, but also influence adjacent segments, potentially triggering further IDD progression.

From an alternative perspective, the impact of changes in facet orientation and IDD phenotype was predominantly confined to the cranial adjacent segment. Notably, there were no significant differences in these parameters between patients with and without annulus tears on the caudal side, and the biomechanical influence of these parameters on disc stress distribution in the caudal region was also minimal. This observation aligns with existing literature on ASD following lateral LIF procedures. Nevertheless, it is commonly posited by surgeons that the heightened risk of cranial ASD in LIF patients stems from a diminished motion arm of the cranial disc subsequent to pedicle screw fixation.53,55 Anatomically speaking, since the pedicle is located close to the superior aspect of the vertebral body, this configuration results in a smaller motion arm for the cranial disc compared to that of its caudal counterpart post-fixation.56,57

Nevertheless, although this study excluded any effects of pedicle screw fixation on adjacent segmental motion arms, changes in stiffness within specific motion segments primarily influence the biomechanical environment of the cranial segment.56,57 Therefore, the findings suggest that the increased incidence of cranial ASD following LIF procedures may not be exclusively attributed to the shortened motion arm resulting from pedicle screw fixation. Instead, it may also be influenced by the inherent motility characteristics associated with lumbar movement. Specifically, the caudal adjacent segment is restricted by its proximity to the sacrum, which limits compensatory stress concentration and increases the range of motion.3,58

In this study, the status of annulus integration, rather than disc degeneration, has been assessed and reported as an outcome variable. Anatomical and histological research indicates that annulus tears constitute the primary phenotype of IDD, particularly in the lower lumbar region. A subsequent autoinflammatory response following annulus tears plays a significant role in the progression of IDD.59,60 Although previous studies have recognized this phenotype, they have encountered difficulties due to the absence of effective non-invasive observational parameters for accurately evaluating annulus integration status. Traditional T2-weighted MRI techniques, which measure high-intensity zones, lack sufficient sensitivity and specificity for assessing annulus tears.61,62 Conversely, DTT offers a precise assessment of annulus integration status. This technology shows promise for broader applications in identifying potential risk factors associated with annulus tears.21,23

Furthermore, the current loading scenario, which involves the application of a pure moment rather than a combination of moment and compressive load, was selected for two primary reasons. First, pure moment was employed during the model validation process,40,63 thereby ensuring computational credibility. Additionally, similar studies in the field have demonstrated that while additional compressive loads can increase stress values across different models, they do not alter the overall trend of computational outcomes.33,34 Consequently, pure moments were chosen for the computation of stress values. Moreover, clinical and biomechanical research play a crucial role in identifying risk factors for musculoskeletal diseases. Unlike simple clinical reviews and numerical mechanical studies, comprehensive biomechanical research that integrates both components can significantly enhance the credibility of similar investigations.22,34 The integration of clinical and biomechanical research within a single study effectively overcomes the limitations associated with traditional clinical and biomechanical research. Traditional clinical research often lacks detailed discussions on mechanical mechanisms, while basic biomechanical studies may be devoid of clinical evidence. This methodological approach constitutes a significant contribution of the present study.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. As a cross-sectional comparative study, while regression analysis can identify potential risk factors for IDD progression, ongoing clinical follow-up of the current patient cohort is crucial to further validate our research findings. Furthermore, as previously noted, numerical simulations have been conducted using a well-validated standard lumbar model. This approach mitigates confounding effects from extraneous variables, however it may also overlook the potential significance of additional factors. Addressing these limitations will be essential in future research endeavours.

In conclusion, through the execution of a clinical evidence-based biomechanical investigation, this study demonstrates that the sagittal orientation of articular processes, alongside IDD phenotypes, may jointly contribute to a heightened incidence of annulus tears in the cranial adjacent motion segment by altering the local biomechanical environment. This finding provides a novel perspective for comprehending the biomechanical interactions in adjacent segments during the progression of IDD.
