Reprogrammable sequencing for physically intelligent underactuated robots
Authors: Leon M. Kamp, Mohamed Zanaty, Ahmad Zareei, Benjamin Gorissen, Robert J. Wood, Katia Bertoldi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceAutonomous interaction with unstructured environments remains a major challenge for robots, often requiring complex control systems and multiple sensors. Here, we present a design strategy for physically intelligent, underactuated mechanisms with energy landscape that can be passively reprogrammed through mechanical contact with their surroundings. To demonstrate the concept, we develop a mechanism that passively sorts objects by mass and a four-degree-of-freedom robot that autonomously avoids obstacles. As such, this work highlights a pathway for building adaptive, low-complexity robotic systems capable of programmable intelligent behavior through mechanical design alone.

Autonomous interactions with unstructured environments pose significant challenges for robots, often necessitating complex controls systems, and a multitude of sensors and actuators. However, there have been investigations in recent years that demonstrated how the incorporation of “physical intelligence” directly into the body of a robot can result in autonomous responses to environmental cues while utilizing fewer sensors, actuators, and controllers (1–3). It has been shown that underactuated mechanisms—mechanisms with fewer actuators than degrees of freedom—offer a promising framework for realizing physically intelligent systems, as the unactuated degrees of freedom can be leveraged to adapt to changing boundary conditions. For example, underactuated mechanisms with specific regions of tuned compliance have resulted in cockroach-inspired robots capable of robust locomotion through uneven terrains (4) and robotic grippers that can efficiently grasp objects of different sizes, shapes, and stiffness levels using a single actuator (5–7). Furthermore, soft tubes have been used to create self-oscillating limbs with autonomous locomotion strategies (8) and programmable compliance in underactuated mechanisms has been shown to simplify control in limbless robots (9). However, this passive compliance does not allow for the control necessary for coordinated actuation between joints. For example, although studies have demonstrated that underactuated mechanisms with selective distributed compliance can yield self-stabilizing and energy-efficient walking gaits, these mechanisms still depend on two actuators to synchronize swing and lift degrees of freedom (10, 11). A few examples have been reported of robots capable of various gaits with just one actuator, (12–15), but they still depend on traditional computational frameworks for sensing and control.

Recently, there have been advances in harnessing multistability to transition between states with distinct programmed behaviors (16–22). This opened new avenues for locomotion without traditional controllers. For instance, the snap through instability of a curved strip has been utilized to create self-rolling robots capable of autonomously navigating mazes (23), origami-inspired multiplexed switches have been implemented to realize an untethered crawler that avoids obstacles (24), and bistable mechanical valves have played a crucial role in the development of pneumatic circuits that control the locomotion of soft-legged robots (25). Extending beyond locomotion, arrays of multiple bistable units have facilitated the encoding of mechanical logic and metamaterials with reprogrammable properties (26–29). Furthermore, by manipulating the energy landscapes of individual degrees of freedom, predetermined transitions between states have been demonstrated (30–33). The theoretical framework describing these transitions is well established (34–38), enabling the design and analysis of serially coupled multistable systems capable of navigating complex transition pathways and performing simple information-processing tasks (38–41). Crucially, these pathways can be finely tuned by modulating the boundary conditions of the system (42). Despite the promising potential to harness control over transition pathways for developing robotic systems that adapt their motion to environmental inputs, practical functional applications of this capability have yet to be realized.

Building on these advances, we leverage control over transition pathways to create physically intelligent underactuated mechanisms with reprogrammable behaviors. To demonstrate this concept, we focus on mechanisms composed of serially connected rigid linkages coupled by elastic springs, which are particularly well suited for robotic applications, as their motion is precisely controlled (41). While multistability in such mechanisms has previously been employed to generate transition waves (43) and achieve jumping motions (44), here we combine geometric nonlinearities with elasticity and contact to realize reprogrammable mechanisms featuring multiwelled energy landscapes. These landscapes facilitate a variety of minimum-energy pathways, all actuated by a single input. Furthermore, we demonstrate the tunability of the energy landscape using mechanical inputs to autonomously reprogram the motion sequence of the mechanism. As a practical demonstration of this framework, we create a mechanism that passively sorts objects based on their mass and a four degrees of freedom robot capable of navigating mazes and avoiding obstacles—all without the need for computational intelligence and utilizing only a single actuator.

Results

Characterization of the Unit Cell.

Our unit cell with one degree of freedom consists of a parallelogram four-bar mechanism comprising two rectangular blocks connected by a pair of identical levers of length r and a spring (rubber band). As shown in Fig. 1A, the two ends of the rubber band are anchored at points defined by the vectors p and q, which originate from the midpoints between the joints of the levers. The range of rotation of the levers is constrained by the contact between the blocks on the interval [−θc, θc]. We define the configurations where θ=−θc and θc as state (0) and state (1), respectively (with θ representing the angle between the levers and the horizontal direction). To explore how the location of the anchor points of the rubber band affects the mechanical response, we consider six rubber band configurations with varying q, but constant θc=π/4, r=2 cm, p=[−10,0] mm, k=28.5 N/m and l0=10 mm (Fig. 1C). All units are placed in state 0 and moved to state 1 by applying a vertical displacement, u, with magnitude umax=2rsinθc to their outer (right) block, while keeping its horizontal displacement unconstrained to allow for unit rotation (Fig. 1A). In Fig. 1D and E we report the evolution of the vertical reaction force on the outer block, F, and the difference in energy between the current configuration and state 0, ΔE=E(θ)−E(−θc), as a function of θ. Three key features emerge from these plots. First, for all units the reaction force decreases monotonically for θ within the interval (−θc,θc), resulting in a concave-down energy profile. This force–displacement behavior and corresponding energy landscape closely resemble those of flexible bistable elements, such as tilted or curved beams (38, 45, 46). However, a distinguishing feature of our system is the pronounced stiffness contrast between the acrylic blocks and the rubber bands, which gives rise to a steep energy well on one side of the minima. This allows us to focus only on rotations away from contact in our analysis and ensures that the location of the energy minima is kinematically well-defined. Second, the location of the anchoring points for the rubber band strongly affect the energy costs for switching from state 0 to state 1. This influence opens up possibilities for creating reprogrammable sequences within arrays of coupled units. Third, all of the units, except for q=[0,−15] mm (purple), are bistable. These cases display two energy minima at θ=±θc separated by an energy barrier. Note that, since E is characterized by a local maximum at[4]θmax= arctanpy−qypx−qx,

any units with |θmax|<θc will display bistability and be characterized by two local energy minima located at state 0 and state 1. Conversely, when p and q are selected so that |θmax|>θc, we expect the units to have only one stable configuration (either at θ=−θc or at θ=θc).

To validate these predictions, we build a prototype comprising laser cut acrylic blocks and levers connected by pin joints with ball bearings. In our experiments, we clamp the left block and pull the right from state 0 to state 1, while recording the reaction force along the vertical direction using an Instron 5969 equipped with a 500 N load cell. In Fig. 1 D and E, we compare the experimentally measured forces and elastic energy (obtained by numerically integrating the measured reaction force) with numerical predictions for the six rubber band configurations shown in Fig. 1C. The agreement is sufficient to validate our simple analytical model.

Serial Coupling of Two Unit Cells.

Next, with the goal to encode deformation sequences, we turn our attention to a mechanism composed of two unit cells connected in series: an inner unit on the left and an outer unit on the right (Fig. 2A). The state of this system is characterized by the unit states (αinαout)∈{0,1} of the inner and outer unit, respectively, where a transition from state 0 to state 1 for a unit corresponds to its rotation from −θc to +θc. This implies that in configurations where the angle is in between these extrema, the system is in an intermediate phase that cannot be characterized by binary variables. Starting from state (00), we apply an upward vertical displacement of magnitude 2umax to the outermost block, causing the mechanism to transition to state (11). We then reverse the displacement by the same magnitude to return the system to state (00). As in previous tests, the horizontal movement of the outermost block remains unconstrained to allow unit rotation (Fig. 2A). During this loading, the mechanism follows a minimum-energy pathway. In particular, unit i rotates while unit j remains stationary if Fi(ui)<Fj(uj) for the transition from (00) to (11), and Fi(ui)>Fj(uj) for the reverse transition from (11) to (00), where ui and uj are the individual displacements of units i and j, respectively. By carefully selecting the rubber band anchoring points (defined by the vectors pin, qin, pout, and qout) we can manipulate the energy landscapes of the units and thereby control the sequence of transitions between states (00) and (11). In particular, we can engineer both undirected pathways, in which the states are accessed in the same order during upward and downward displacements (green and orange arrows in Fig. 2B), and directed pathways, where the system cyclically visits all four possible states under a single loading cycle (blue and red arrows in Fig. 2B). Directed pathways are enabled by a snapping instability that switches the system between two distinct minimum-energy trajectories during the transitions from (00) to (11) and back. These snapping events occur when the energy cost of rotating a unit backward becomes smaller than the energy gained by rotating the other coupled unit forward. To determine the exact pathway followed by the system, we first define its elastic energy, which is given by the sum of the elastic energy of the individual units[6]Etot=E(θin,pin,qin)+E(θout,pout,qout),

where θin and θout represent the angles between the horizontal direction and the levers of the inner and outer unit, respectively. When the displacement of the outer unit is controlled, the response of the system is characterized by one independent variable and the constraint[7]u=r(sinθin+sinθout−2sinθc).

To identify the path followed by the structure, we incrementally increase u starting from the initial configuration (defined by θin=θout=−θc) and locally minimize the elastic energy, Etot, using a quasi-Newton method (see SI Appendix for details).

In Fig. 2C we focus on a mechanism composed of two unit cells identical to those considered in Fig. 1. We choose pin=−qin=pout=[−10,0] mm, and systematically investigate the effect of qout on the sequence of transitions. We find that large values of qyout tend to promote undirected transition sequences (orange and green shaded areas in Fig. 2C), whereas large values of qxout tend to lead to directed transition sequences (red and blue shaded areas in Fig. 2C). In Fig. 2D–G we examine four configurations, with qout= [0,0], [20,0], [10,−15], [20,15] mm, that display the four supported sequences of transitions (corresponding to the diamond markers in Fig. 2C). For each of them, we report the energy landscape as a function of θin and θout, alongside the measured vertical reaction force at the outer unit as a function of the applied displacement. For all configurations, we observe that the minimum energy path mostly results in sequential motions, where the units transition between state 0 and state 1 one after the other, rather than simultaneously. This is due to the convex down nature of the energy landscape of the individual units, resulting in energy minima predominantly along the boundary. For the rubber band anchoring points considered here that produce directed pathways, we find that the snapping transitions occur near the end of the transitions from state (00) to state (11) and from state (11) back to (00) (see dashed colored lines in Fig. 2D and F, center). However, by expanding the range of anchoring point configurations, more pronounced snapping events can be achieved (see SI Appendix, section S1.3 for details).

Since for the rubber band configurations considered here snapping event have a minimal effect, the order of sequential transitions can be predicted in a simplified way by examining the reaction force of the two individual units at state 0, F0=F(−θc), and at state 1, F1=F(θc). More specifically, undirected pathways that traverse through state (01) are realized when F0in>F0out and F1in>F1out, while those transitioning through state (10) require F0in<F0out and F1in<F1out. Conversely, clock-wise directed sequences necessitate that F0in<F0out and F1in>F1out, while the counterclockwise sequences require F0in>F0out and F1in<F1out. Across all rubber band configurations examined in this manuscript, the proposed conditions successfully predict over 90% of the motion of the mechanism. That said, by broadening the set of anchoring point arrangements, it becomes possible to induce more complex sequential behaviors (see SI Appendix, section S1.3 for additional details). Finally, it is noteworthy that the combined energy landscape of all the two-unit mechanisms examined here reveals directed pathways (red area in Fig. 2C), even in situations where the outer units are monostable (represented by lightly shaded regions in Fig. 2C).

Fig. 2 demonstrates that the tunable energy landscape of the mechanism can be exploited to modify the pathway followed upon actuation. Up to this point, we have adjusted the anchor points of the rubber bands to control the transition sequence, but external forces arising from interactions with the surrounding environment can also influence the sequence. This unlocks opportunities for designing physically intelligent underactuated mechanisms.

To illustrate this, we present two applications: a mechanism that passively sorts objects based on their mass, and a robot with a single actuator that passively senses and avoids obstacles without conventional computational systems.

Physically Intelligent Mass Sorting Mechanism.

We begin by examining how attaching weights to the mechanism influences its transitions, enabling passive sorting of objects based on their mass. As shown in Fig. 3A, the proposed sorting mechanism consists of three units coupled by rubber bands with a rest length of l0=10 mm and stiffness k=28.5 N/m (identical to those used in Figs. 1 and 2) and a V-shaped leg mechanism connected to the two blocks of the outermost unit. The innermost block of the mechanism is anchored to the rigid body, while the outermost block is driven back and forth to transition the system between its (000) and (111) states. Switching the outermost unit between state 0 and state 1 raises or lowers the V-shaped mechanism, allowing it to lift a weight off the ground using a hook (Fig. 3A). Additionally, shifting the two inner units from state 0 to state 1 moves the entire V-shaped mechanism forward or backward, enabling the system to pick up and drop off objects at three distinct locations, spaced 2 cm apart, as indicated by the green, blue, and red dots in Fig. 3A.

The three-unit mechanism under consideration supports eight stable states. It has been shown that serially coupled systems comprising three units can support up to 6×44 transition graphs (38). However, in our system—operated within a parameter regime where snapping plays a limited role—this space simplifies to 36 transition pathways for cycling between states (000), (111), and back to (000). All possible transitions supported by the sorting mechanism are illustrated in Fig. 3B, with colored arrows highlighting those enabling pick-up and drop-off, and black arrows indicating transitions associated with translation.

Similar to the two-unit mechanism shown in Fig. 2, the sequence of transitions can be determined by analyzing the reaction forces of the three individual units at state 0 and state 1. In Fig. 3C, we consider a multistable mechanism with anchor points defined by pin=pmid=pout=[−10,0] mm, and qin=[0,0] mm, qmid=[10,0] mm, and qout=[20,0] mm, where the subscript mid denotes the middle unit cell. For this configuration, in the absence of external forces, F0in<F0mid<F0out and F1out<F1mid<F1in for which the model predicts the following sequence: (000)→(100)→(110)→(111)→(011)→(001)→(000). Therefore, a sufficiently small weight that does not affect the energy landscape can be picked up at the green dot and dropped at the red dot (Fig. 3C).

However, the energy landscape, and therefore the sequence of the mechanism, is affected for larger weights. The mass of the weight influences the reaction force at the outermost units, Fout, as the switching of the outer block involves raising and lowering the weight. In Fig. 3D we show how Fout varies with increasing mass m for the considered configuration of rubber bands. We find that for θout=−π/4, Fout is always greater than Fin and Fmid, since lifting the weight increases the reaction force in Fout. However, at θout=π/4, Fout exceeds Fmid for m>31 g. Note that the pickup location is independent of m and is solely determined by the rubber band configuration. Moreover, by adjusting the rubber band configurations, we can control the mass thresholds for release at various positions. In SI Appendix, section S2, we demonstrate that the analytical model not only predicts the transition sequences as a function of m but also provides guidance for placing the bands to achieve specific sorting categories (SI Appendix, Fig. S20).

Physically Intelligent Underactuated Robot.

Beyond sorting, we show that the proposed multistable mechanisms can be integrated into an underactuated robot to sequentially control four degrees of freedom, enabling multiple distinct locomotion modes with a single actuator. As shown in Fig. 4A, the robot consists of a pair of two-unit mechanisms with attached V-shaped legs similar to those used in the mass-sorting system, a large T-shaped foot for stability and a main body housing a stepper motor, battery, and controller (see SI Appendix, section S3.1 for details). The innermost block of the two-unit mechanism is anchored to the rigid body, while the stepper motor drives the outermost block back and forth, simultaneously driving the two mechanisms back and forth between their (00) and (11) states. Shifting the outer unit from state 0 to state 1 increases the distance between the connection points of the V-shaped leg, raising the foot. Conversely, a transition from state 0 to state 1 lowers the foot. Moving the inner unit from state 0 to state 1 propels the entire mechanism forward, while transitioning from state 0 to state 1 pushes the mechanism backward (Fig. 4C). Therefore, the sequence of transitions (00) → (10) → (11) → (01) →(00) leads to a forward step, whereas (00) → (01) → (11) → (10) →(00) results in a backward step (Fig. 4C).

We carefully select the anchor points of the rubber bands to ensure the motion of the leg follows the desired sequence of transitions. It is important to recognize that the robot’s response is also influenced by external factors, unlike a standalone mechanism where the energy landscape is solely determined by the springs (i.e., their stiffness, initial length, and anchoring points). Specifically, we need to account for the effects of friction with the substrate on the inner unit and gravity and friction in the leg mechanism on the outer unit. It follows that the reaction force on the inner unit of the robot is given by[11]Fin=Frin+Ff,

where Frin represents the reaction force along the y-direction caused by the rubber bands in the inner unit. Further, Ff is the frictional force due to the interaction with the ground, which can be expressed as[12]Ff=μmrobotg2,

where μ denotes the coefficient of friction between the feet of the robot and the substrate and mrobot = 0.177 kg is the mass of the robot (note that the factor of two is introduced because we assume that the mass is equally distributed between the two legs). Additionally, the reaction force on the outer block in the robot can be expressed as[13]Fout=Frout+Fg,y+Fint,

where Fg,y is given by Eq. 9 with m=6.6 g being the mass of the leg and Fint=0.115 N is a constant force caused by the friction between the rubber feet and the leg mechanism (SI Appendix, Fig. S24).

In our robot, the contribution of Ff, Fint and Fg,y is significant enough to affect the transition map of Fig. 2C. For instance, while a standalone mechanism with anchor points defined by pin=[−10,0] mm, qin=[10,0] mm, and pout=[−10,0] mm and qout=[qxout,0] mm achieves a sequence leading to a forward step for qxout>10 mm (dashed lines in Fig. 2C), for the robot moving on a medium density fiberboard (MDF) with μMDF=0.32±0.02, this sequence is only achieved when qxout>16 mm (continuous lines in Fig. 2C). Therefore, we select qxout=20 mm to ensure a robust forward gait (Movie S4). Conversely, the sequence leading to a backward step is not affected by friction as contact with the ground is only made in the second part of the sequence [i.e., (10)→(00)]. Therefore, a backward step can be realized by selecting qxout=5 mm for both the case of the mechanism itself and the robot (Movie S4). In Fig. 4D, we show the trajectory followed by the robot for different choices of qxout. As expected, we see that the robot moves forward for qxout=20 mm (Left), and moves backward for qxout=5 mm (center). Further, a turning motion is achieved by programming one leg to execute a forward step while the other performs a backward step.

In Fig. 4 E and F, we present the evolution of the displacement, d, and rotation, ϕ, of the robot for the three considered configurations of the rubber bands. We observe that for the robot programmed to move backward, d/umax=0.96, indicating that the displacement provided by the linear actuator is almost entirely converted into movement of the robot. However, for the forward motion, d/umax=0.71. We attribute this reduction to the snapping of the mechanism as it approaches the (11) state, exerting a force that pushes the robot in the opposite direction. Finally, we find that, for turning motion, d is negligible and ϕ≈π/12 rad per cycle.

The model enables us to program the robot to move on surfaces with higher coefficients of friction. As demonstrated in Fig. 4G and Movie S4, both the model and experiments reveal that with the rubber band configuration considered in Fig. 4D the robot is unable to move forward on a rubber surface with a coefficient of friction of μrubber=0.69±0.01. However, the model predicts that adding a second rubber band to the outer unit allows the robot to take steps forward on surfaces with a friction coefficient μ<1.15 (dark blue line in Fig. 4G). As shown in Fig. 4H, with two rubber bands on the outer unit, the robot successfully crawls on both rubber and MDF surfaces (additional results are provided in SI Appendix, section S2.3).

Gait Adaptation in Response to Mechanical Interactions with the Environment.

Fig. 4 demonstrates that shifting the anchor point position of the rubber bands can reverse the gait of the leg. This insight opens the door to designing robots that autonomously adapt their gait and navigate obstacles through mechanical interactions with their environment. To achieve this, we attach a module with two antennas at the front of the robot (Fig. 5A). Each antenna comprises two levers connected to the inner rubber band of the two-unit mechanism on the opposite side of the robot (Fig. 5B). When an antenna contacts an obstacle, the lever rotates by an angle β, altering pin and increasing Fin. Specifically, movement of the Left (Right) lever changes pin for the Right (Left) mechanism. Using our model, we guide the selection of the rubber band configuration that allows the robot to leverage changes in pin to adjust its gait and turn away from obstacles. Achieving this gait switch requires an increase of Fin large enough to change F0in<F0out and F1in>F1out to F0in>F0out and F1in<F1out. This presents a trade-off between the sensitivity of the system and the robustness of the gait. A robust gait in the absence of contact requires a significant force difference. However, overcoming this force difference would require a substantial rotation of the antennas to switch the gait.

Therefore, the tuning of the rubber band configuration is guided by the following considerations: i) the antennas should rotate with low contact forces to enhance the robot’s sensitivity and prevent slipping of the feet; ii) the robot must maintain a robust forward-walking gait when the antennas are not in contact with an external object; and iii) the robot should initiate a turn within one cycle after contact (corresponding to an antenna tip displacement of one forward step ≈1.9 cm achieved with a rotation β≈14°). Similar to the robot without antennas, we set qin=−pout=[10,0] mm and qout=[qxout,0], while pin is determined by the rotation β of the lever connected to the antennas. Moreover, to balance the trade-off between robustness and sensitivity, we tune the length and thickness of the rubber bands for the inner unit, while using the same rubber bands as the robot without antennas for the outer unit.

Guided by the model, we select the following parameters to meet the three criteria (details on the selection process are provided in SI Appendix, section S3.2.2): i) a rest length l0=25 mm for the inner rubber bands, as the model predicts Fin≈0 in the absence of contact for this extended rest length, ensuring low contact forces; ii) qxout=5 mm, which leads to F0in<F0out and F1in>F1out in the absence of contact, ensuring a stable forward-walking gait; and iii) a thickness tr=1.4 mm for the inner rubber bands, to enable a gait switch within one cycle after contact, while maintaining relatively low forces. In Fig. 5 C and D, we report the predicted evolution of Fin (blue) and Fout (red) at state 0 and state 1 as a function of the rotation β. We find that F0in=F0out at β=2.4° and F1in=F1out at β=11.1°. As such, we expect the robot to make a turn forward for β≥2.4° and a turn backward for β≥11.1°.

In Movie S5 and Fig. 5E, the robot is shown approaching a wooden cylinder with a diameter of 50 mm, making contact with its right antenna. This contact triggers the left lever to rotate, initiating a forward left turn at β≈9°, consistent with model predictions. The left turn increases the rotation further to β≈13°, causing the robot to execute a backward left turn until the antenna disengages from the obstacle. Once contact is lost, β resets to 0°, allowing the robot to resume forward motion.

The robot can effectively turn away from cylindrical columns, flat walls, and soft objects from a wide range of approach angles, and with contact at different points along the antennas, and can be programmed to have a similar response on rubber substrates (Movie S5 and SI Appendix, Figs. S30–S33). This makes it possible to navigate through arbitrary assemblies of obstacles as long as the spacing between them exceeds the robot’s width. To demonstrate the robustness of this behavior, in Fig. 5 G and H, we show the trajectory of the robot navigating environments obstructed by walls and columns. The robot successfully traverses these environments without electronic sensors, relying solely on a single actuator (Movie S5). These experiments in general show agreement with the behavior predicted by the analytical model; however, variation in surface roughness, contact forces, unavoidable asymmetries introduced during fabrication, and internal friction can lead to deviations from the predicted values of β that result in the gait switch. As a result, the robot may occasionally turn earlier than anticipated or execute only a backward turn (Movie S5). Another limitation of the current configuration occurs when both antennas simultaneously contact an obstacle, as this prevents the robot from turning away from an obstacle (SI Appendix, section S3.3.3). Since our approach allows passive encoding of specific target behaviors, this issue can be addressed by introducing an asymmetry in the rubber band configuration to favor turning in a specific direction. To this end, the placement of the outer rubber band on the right leg of the robot is adjusted to qout = [10, 0] mm. As demonstrated in Fig. 5 C and D, this increases the rotation angle β required for the right leg to switch gait and creates a bias toward turning left. Therefore, the robot can escape a confined V-shaped obstacle with internal angle of 92° (Fig. 5I and Movie S5). Initially, the left antenna contacts one wall, causing the robot to turn right toward the opposite wall. When the left antenna also contacts the second wall, the robot reverses direction and escapes the confined space. Finally, it is important to highlight that the capability of the robot to avoid obstacles depends on the coupling between the antennas and the rubber band. If the antenna is disconnected from the anchor point of the inner rubber band, the robot cannot switch gait and gets stuck after encountering an obstacle (Fig. 5F and SI Appendix, section S3).

Conclusions.

To summarize, we have demonstrated that multistability can be leveraged to sequence the motion of different degrees of freedom in a predetermined order. Additionally, the tunability of the energy landscape allows these sequences to be passively reprogrammed through mechanical stimuli arising from environmental interactions.

We showcase this approach by creating a mechanism that passively sorts objects based on their mass and a robot capable of forward and backward locomotion gaits using a single quasi-static linear input. The walking robot also adapts its gait in response to mechanical interactions with the environment, eliminating the need for electronic feedback and control. Although our robot currently supports only a walking gait, we see potential for future investigation in leveraging the snapping instabilities observed in certain configurations of the multistable mechanism (SI Appendix, Figs. S10–S17) for rapid movements, such as jumping over obstacles (47, 48). Finally, while our study focused on a platform of rigid blocks connected by levers and elastic springs, multistable energy landscapes can also be realized using beams and shells that can be monolithically fabricated (18, 47, 49, 50). Altogether, our work could be a starting point for designing physically intelligent autonomous machines that operate with minimal dependence on electronic controllers, sensors, and actuators.

Appendix 01 (PDF)

The unit cell: The unit cell is comprised of two block connected by a pair of levers to form a parallel four-bar linkage. We introduce a rubber band between the blocks to assign an energy landscape to the rotation of the mechanism. This energy landscape can be tuned by moving the anchor points of the rubber band. We are able to switch between a monostable and bistable landscape. The stable states of the system are defined as state 0 and 1.

The two-unit mechanism: We couple two unit cells in series. This mechanism supports a sequential transition between states. The placement of the rubber bands can be used to program the sequence. We show four configurations with different transition paths. The behavior of the system can be characterized by measuring the vertical reaction force for a transition (00)→(11). At the end of the first transition the blocks make contact, causing a sharp increase in the recorded force. When approaching state (11) the system can become monostable triggering a snapping instability.

Sorting mechanism: We create a sorting mechanism by coupling three units in series. A V-shaped mechanism is connected to the outer and middle unit, so that switching the outermost unit can lift/drop a weight, whereas moving the inner two units can translate the weight back and forth. We show that the pick up and drop off location can be changed by reprogramming the sequence with a different rubber bands configuration. Furthermore, the energy landscape and therefore sequence of transition are influenced by the weight hanging from the mechanism. We show that we can drop weights with three different masses (60, 40 and 20 g) at different locations using the same rubber bands configuration. By changing the rubber bands configuration we can adapt the sorting categories.

The robot: We build a robot containing a pair of two-unit mechanisms. A leg is attached to each mechanism, so that moving the outer unit lifts the leg, while moving the inner unit translates the leg back and forth. Therefore, we can program the gait of the robot through the placement of the rubber band. The sequence of the mechanism on the robot can be reprogrammed to walk forwards, backwards and turn by moving the two outer anchor points. Friction influences this sequence. We show that it is possible to tune the rubber band configuration on the robot for walking on a variety of substrates.

Mechanical sensing: We attach two antennas to the front of the robot. Moving the antenna moves the inner rubber band on the opposite side, causing a change in pin. This results in a change in the gait of the robot to turn away from the obstacle. We show that it is possible to passively sense and avoid obstacles from a wide range of approach angles and contact points with the antenna. For example the robot is able to escape a simple maze with three walls, a maze with three columns and a wall and a confined V-shaped obstacle, only using a single actuator and no electronic sensing.
