Infiltrative Basal Cell Carcinoma Masked by Extensive Posttraumatic Facial Scarring Involving the Bilateral Lacrimal System: A Case Report
Authors: SeungMo Kim, Jisang Han
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) is the most common malignant eyelid tumor, with the infiltrative subtype being particularly aggressive due to its poorly defined borders and high risk of local recurrence [1]. While BCC typically presents as a slow-growing nodule in sun-exposed areas, its coexistence with extensive traumatic wounds and the resultant scarring can mask its presence and substantially delay diagnosis. We present a rare case of an infiltrative BCC that was hidden within extensive posttraumatic facial scars, with the diagnostic delay allowing the tumor to extend deeply, ultimately necessitating wide excision of the cranio-facial area including the bilateral lacrimal system. Written informed consent for publication of the research details and clinical images was obtained from the patient.

A 60-year-old male patient with no prior ocular history was referred to our multidisciplinary team by the Department of Plastic Surgery. One year prior, the patient had been involved in a severe motor vehicle accident resulting in extensive soft-tissue wounds over the nasal and glabellar regions, accompanied by a complete loss of the nasal bone. The wound margins showed persistent induration and an irregular border atypical for a maturing scar. During a scheduled debridement and reconstructive surgery for these chronic traumatic wounds, an intraoperative frozen section biopsy of the wound margin unexpectedly confirmed the presence of an infiltrative BCC, prompting an immediate alteration of the surgical plan.

Instead of the initially planned reconstruction using a paramedian forehead flap and nasolabial flap, a wide local excision of the lesion was performed, followed by temporary closure using a split-thickness skin graft. Subsequent permanent histopathological analysis revealed positive tumor margins at the nose and both cheek skins. Postoperative orbital and facial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated findings highly suspicious for residual tumor infiltration involving both foreheads, the medial canthi, and the nasal sidewall. A whole-body fusion positron emission tomography scan ruled out distant metastasis. To precisely map the margins of the tumor, multiple map biopsies were performed at the ophthalmology outpatient clinic, which confirmed extensive BCC involvement in the left lower eyelid and both lacrimal sacs [2]. Given this extensive infiltration, a second radical surgery was planned via multidisciplinary cooperation involving plastic surgery, otolaryngology, and ophthalmology. The resection involved both medial canthi, the lacrimal puncta, sacs, and ducts, a portion of the anterior-medial orbital periorbita, and the semilunar folds. Intraoperative frozen section biopsies of the resected lacrimal sacs yielded positive margins, necessitating additional resection by the otolaryngology team. They performed endoscopic sinus surgery including uncinectomy, ethmoidectomy, lacrimal duct excision, resection of the upper lateral nasal cartilage, and surrounding mucosa, after which clear negative margins were confirmed. The plastic surgery team then performed a paramedian forehead flap and a split-thickness skin graft for reconstruction. Cryotherapy was applied to the conjunctiva of both eyes to prevent microscopic tumor seeding, and the resected tarsus was securely anchored to the periosteum posterior to the posterior lacrimal crest. Given the ambiguity of postoperative MRI findings at the right preseptal-medial canthal area, the team concluded that further surgery would carry disproportionate risk. Adjuvant radiation therapy was therefore administered at a dose of 200 cGy per fraction, given once daily for 5 consecutive days per week with a 2-day rest, for a total of 30 fractions, with a plan for close surveillance thereafter. A subsequent computed tomography scan demonstrated complete resolution, and surveillance at 9 months confirmed no recurrence.

This case primarily highlights a diagnostic pitfall: posttraumatic scar tissue severely compromises clinical detection of an underlying malignancy. The hyperplastic scars, induration, and irregular contours of the patient’s healing wounds completely masked the classic clinical features of BCC, delaying the oncological diagnosis [3]. It has been proposed that severe trauma may disrupt natural anatomical barriers—such as the periosteum, fascial planes, and subcutaneous fat layers—that normally help contain tumor growth [4], potentially creating a path of least resistance for the underlying tumor. In nonhealing or atypical posttraumatic wounds, clinicians must keep in mind the possibility of an underlying malignancy [5]. When extensive facial trauma and scarring obscure the diagnosis of BCC, a prompt multidisciplinary approach combining radical margin-controlled resection, extensive mapping, and tailored adjuvant therapy is vital to achieve complete oncological clearance.
