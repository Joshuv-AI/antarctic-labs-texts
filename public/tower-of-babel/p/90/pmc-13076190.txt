Beyond the neuron: Exosomes as intercellular modulators of mitochondrial networks in the pathogenesis and treatment of Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Liping Xing, Annan Liu, Wei Gao, Jianhui Li, Mingyuan Yao, Jing Song, Peihan Duan, Honglin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer's disease (AD) is a devastating neurodegenerative disorder characterized by β‐amyloid deposition, hyperphosphorylated tau protein, and progressive neuronal loss. Mitochondria form a dynamic interconnected network within the central nervous system, and their dysfunction plays a central role in AD, involving oxidative stress, kinetic dysregulation, and impaired mitochondrial autophagy. As key mediators of intercellular communication, exosomes carry bioactive components that regulate mitochondrial function in recipient cells. This review summarizes advances in research on exosomes as coordinators of the mitochondrial network in the central nervous system, regulating mitochondrial quality control across different neuronal cell types. It systematically outlines the molecular mechanisms by which exosomes modulate mitochondrial function in AD through regulating mitochondrial biogenesis, fusion‐fission dynamics, mitochondrial autophagy, and related signaling pathways. Furthermore, it explores the potential of engineered exosome‐based targeted therapies for AD intervention, aiming to provide a theoretical foundation and research direction for developing novel therapeutic strategies targeting mitochondrial dysfunction.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, exosomes, mitochondria, neuroprotection, targeted therapy

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) represents the most prevalent cause of dementia, accounting for 60%–80% of cases in the elderly population. Its defining neuropathological hallmarks include the extracellular deposition of amyloid‐β (Aβ) plaques, the intracellular accumulation of hyperphosphorylated tau protein forming neurofibrillary tangles, and the progressive degeneration of neurons and synapses, ultimately leading to debilitating cognitive decline. 1 With over 55 million individuals affected globally—a number projected to triple by 2050—AD poses an immense and growing societal burden. 2 The intricate pathophysiology of AD remains only partially deciphered, and the development of therapies capable of preventing or reversing its course has proven exceptionally challenging.

Within this context, mitochondria have garnered significant attention beyond their canonical role as cellular powerhouses. In energy‐demanding neurons, mitochondria are indispensable not only for ATP production via oxidative phosphorylation but also for calcium buffering, reactive oxygen species (ROS) regulation, and apoptotic signaling. 3 At the same time, mitochondria do not function as isolated entities; they form dynamic, interconnected networks that maintain cellular homeostasis through continuous fusion, fission, and mitochondrial autophagy. 4 A compelling body of evidence now indicates that mitochondrial dysfunction is not merely a secondary consequence but a primary pathogenic driver in AD, manifesting early in the disease course. This dysfunction manifests as impaired electron transport chain activity, diminished ATP synthesis, elevated ROS production, disrupted calcium homeostasis, and aberrant mitochondrial dynamics, collectively contributing to synaptic failure and neuronal demise. 5

Concurrently, the field of extracellular vesicles, particularly exosomes (30–150 nm in diameter), has revolutionized our understanding of intercellular signaling. These lipid‐bilayer nanoparticles are secreted by virtually all cell types and carry a molecular cargo (proteins, lipids, RNAs) reflective of their cell of origin and its physiological state. 6 By facilitating the horizontal transfer of bioactive molecules, exosomes mediate diverse biological processes, including immune modulation, tumor progression, and, notably, central nervous system (CNS) communication. 7 Recent pioneering work suggests that exosomes can directly or indirectly influence mitochondrial physiology in recipient cells, positioning them as potential key modulators in diseases with prominent mitochondrial involvement, such as AD. 8 , 9

This review aims to explore in depth the emerging link between exosome biology and mitochondrial network homeostasis in AD. We will first delineate the spectrum of mitochondrial impairments in AD. Subsequently, we will dissect the dualistic nature of exosomes in AD pathology, serving as both vectors for toxic protein dissemination and vehicles for neuroprotective signals. Building upon this foundation, we expand our perspective from mitochondrial function within individual cells to intercellular cooperative regulation, proposing the concept of a mitochondrial network in the CNS. This network coordinates mitochondrial function through intercellular communication among diverse neuronal cell types, including neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes. The core of this article will be dedicated to a systematic analysis of how exosomes regulate fundamental aspects of mitochondrial biology—biogenesis, fusion, fission, and mitophagy—in the AD brain, with an emphasis on underlying molecular mechanisms. Finally, we will discuss the translational potential of harnessing engineered exosomes as smart delivery systems to rectify mitochondrial deficits, offering a promising and innovative therapeutic paradigm for this relentless disorder.

To ensure the comprehensiveness and systematic nature of the literature review, we searched relevant publications published up to December 2025 in the PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar databases. Search keywords included “Alzheimer's disease,” “exosomes,” “mitochondria,” “mitochondrial biogenesis,” “mitochondrial dynamics,” “mitochondrial autophagy,” and related signaling pathways. Additionally, we manually searched the references of included studies to supplement relevant research.

MITOCHONDRIA AND AD

Mitochondrial dysfunction

Mitochondria, as highly dynamic and multifunctional organelles within cells, serve not only as the hub of energy metabolism responsible for generating adenosine triphosphate (ATP) through oxidative phosphorylation, but also play an irreplaceable central role in critical cellular processes such as maintaining Ca2+ homeostasis, regulating apoptotic signaling, and participating in ROS metabolism. 10 Within the CNS, neurons—particularly their synaptic terminals—exhibit extremely high energy demands to sustain advanced functions such as action potential conduction, neurotransmitter release, and synaptic plasticity. This renders them highly dependent on mitochondrial functional integrity. 11 Consequently, early mitochondrial dysfunction severely compromises neuronal vitality and functional integrity, diminishes synaptic efficacy, and ultimately triggers neuronal degeneration and death. This process is recognized as the direct cellular basis for the emergence of AD‐related cognitive symptoms. 12 Extensive pathological and imaging studies 13 confirm that mitochondrial structural and functional abnormalities are detectable in the brains of AD patients prior to the appearance of characteristic Aβ plaque deposition and tau protein neurofibrillary tangles. This establishes mitochondrial dysfunction as an early and critical driver in the pathological cascade of AD.

Mitochondrial dysfunction in AD manifests as a complex network of multifactorial, multilevel interactions, primarily involving metabolic disorders, exacerbated oxidative stress, disrupted calcium homeostasis, and comprehensive decline of the mitochondrial quality control system. These processes intertwine to form a vicious cycle. 14 For instance, impaired mitochondrial electron transport chain function reduces ATP production, triggering cellular energy crises, while simultaneously promoting excessive ROS production—such as superoxide—leading to oxidative stress. 15 Research indicates that pharmacologically or genetically reducing mitochondrial ROS levels can effectively reverse multiple pathological phenotypes in AD models, including inhibiting abnormal Aβ accumulation, reducing mitochondrial‐mediated apoptosis, and correcting hyperactive mitochondrial fission. 16 Concurrently, impaired energy metabolism directly impacts neuronal survival in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus—regions whose neuronal loss correlates directly with AD‐related cognitive decline. This process is accompanied by abnormal hyperphosphorylation of tau protein and the formation of insoluble intracellular aggregates. 17

As AD pathology progresses, the “mitochondrial autophagy” mechanism responsible for clearing damaged mitochondria gradually becomes dysfunctional, manifesting as reduced lysosomal function and impaired autophagic flux. This leads to the massive accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria within cells. 18 This further exacerbates cellular bioenergetic deficits and oxidative stress levels, accelerating neuronal apoptosis and establishing a vicious cycle closely linked to the progression of neurodegenerative diseases like AD. 19 Notably, energy metabolism disruption itself exerts a retroactive effect on AD's core pathological proteins: it promotes Aβ production and deposition by influencing the processing of amyloid precursor protein (APP); simultaneously, it activates multiple kinases—including glycogen synthase kinase‐3β (GSK‐3β)—directly leading to pathological hyperphosphorylation of tau protein. 20

The complete failure of the mitochondrial quality control system is a key factor in amplifying damage. First, impaired autophagy–lysosomal clearance pathways prevent the timely degradation of damaged mitochondria, leading to their accumulation. Second, the dynamic equilibrium of mitochondrial fusion and fission is disrupted, causing fragmentation of the mitochondrial network and impairing its normal transport and functional distribution. Third, the accumulation of mitochondrial DNA mutations activates innate immune signaling pathways such as cGAS‐STING, triggering persistent neuroinflammation that further deteriorates the microenvironment. 21 , 22 Ultimately, under the regulation of the Bcl‐2 protein family, mitochondrial membrane permeability alters upon apoptosis signal initiation. This leads to the release of pro‐apoptotic factors like cytochrome c into the cytoplasm, activating the caspase cascade and irreversibly executing neuronal death. 23 In summary, mitochondrial dysfunction pervades the entire AD disease course—from early synaptic plasticity impairment to late‐stage widespread neuronal loss and cognitive impairment—and exhibits a strong correlation with neuronal death. This profoundly reveals that mitochondria are not only hallmarks of AD pathology but also highly promising key therapeutic targets, providing a robust theoretical basis for developing novel therapies aimed at improving mitochondrial function.

Common mitochondrial drug delivery approaches

In AD treatment research, the development of mitochondrial‐targeted drug delivery systems offers new hope for enhancing therapeutic efficacy. The following are several promising mitochondrial drug delivery strategies. (1) Nanoparticles. After surface modification with mitochondrial targeting signals, nanoparticles can cross the blood–brain barrier and deliver drugs specifically to mitochondria, increasing intracellular drug concentrations and enhancing therapeutic efficacy. For example, cellular experiments by Gao et al. 24 demonstrated that curcumin encapsulated in nanoparticles exhibited significantly increased uptake within mitochondria. This delivery method alleviates mitochondrial oxidative stress, inhibits neuronal death in vitro and in vivo, and alleviates AD symptoms. (2) Mitochondria‐targeted peptides. Taking the Szeto–Schiller peptide as an example, its antioxidant properties stem from dimethyltyrosine residues. This positively charged, hydrophobic peptide segment interacts with mitochondria via their negative membrane potential and hydrophobic membrane structure, enabling internalization and subsequent mitigation of oxidative damage. 25 (3) Prodrug strategies. Prodrugs convert into active drug forms after metabolism in vivo. Constructing mitochondria‐targeted prodrugs activated by the mitochondrial microenvironment (e.g., enzymes on the inner/outer membranes or matrix, low pH environment) enables targeted drug delivery and enrichment. In AD therapy, the tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB) receptor agonist R13 is a mitochondrial‐targeted prodrug of 7,8‐dihydroxyflavone that converts to its active form within mitochondria. R13 modulates phosphorylation of AMP‐activated protein kinase alpha subunit (pAMPKα), peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor gamma coactivator 1 alpha (PGC‐1α), nuclear respiratory factor 1 (NRF1), and mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) to enhance mitochondrial biogenesis, significantly reducing tau protein phosphorylation and Aβ levels in AD model mice. 26 (4) Carrier‐based drug delivery. Liposomes are vesicles composed of phospholipid bilayers that can encapsulate hydrophilic or hydrophobic drugs. For example, modifying the liposome surface with positively charged mitochondrial‐targeting signals, such as polyarginine short peptides, enables specific binding to mitochondria and drug release. 27 The nanocapsule Mito‐Porter, a lipid‐based carrier coated with high‐density octa‐arginine, enters cells via endocytosis, fuses with the mitochondrial membrane, releases drugs into the matrix, and thereby improves cellular mitochondrial function. 28 Furthermore, exosomes—nanoscale membrane vesicles secreted by cells—share similarities with cellular components, enabling them to cross the blood–brain barrier. Their excellent biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and high stability make them ideal carriers for mitochondrial‐targeted drug delivery. 29 Targeted molecules on the exosome surface can actively interact with mitochondria or passively enter via mitochondrial physiological properties, enabling precise drug delivery to diseased neuronal mitochondria. 30 Compared to traditional methods, exosome‐based delivery offers significant advantages: it can cross the blood–brain barrier, exhibits superior biocompatibility, reduces adverse drug reactions, and enables the co‐delivery of multiple drugs for synergistic therapy. 31 This approach holds great promise for AD treatment and may emerge as a safe and effective novel therapeutic strategy.

EXOSOMES AND AD

Biological characteristics of exosomes

Exosomes are a type of extracellular vesicle with a lipid bilayer structure that can be secreted by living cells under both physiological and pathological conditions. 7 Extracellular vesicles also include microvesicles, which have a diameter of 150–1000 nm and form by direct shedding from the cell membrane. Microvesicles are primarily identified by marker proteins such as Selectins, Integrins, CD40, and Annexin A1. 32 Another class of extracellular vesicles is exosomes, with diameters ranging from 30 to 150 nm. They form when the outer membrane of a multivesicular body fuses with the plasma membrane and is released into the extracellular space. 33 Exosomes are characterized by marker proteins including ALIX, TSG101, CD81, CD82, CD63, and CD37. 34 During multivesicular body formation, exosomes progressively accumulate diverse proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and other substances. Consequently, exosomes released into the extracellular space are rich in bioactive molecules such as lipids, proteins, coding RNAs, and non‐coding RNAs (ncRNAs), with ncRNAs encompassing structural and regulatory ncRNAs. 35 Interestingly, both coding and non‐coding RNAs within exosomes participate in AD progression, including mRNAs, lncRNAs, miRNAs, and circRNAs. 36 Exosomes are present in various bodily fluids, including cerebrospinal fluid, urine, milk, tears, blood, sweat, and saliva. They play roles in numerous physiological and pathological processes, mediating intercellular communication by transporting molecular cargo to recipient cells via endocytosis, direct membrane fusion, or receptor‐ligand binding 37 (Figure 1).

Internal structure of exosomes. Exosomes are nanoscale lipid bilayer‐enclosed vesicles that carry a diverse cargo of bioactive molecules, including proteins, lipids, mRNAs, miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs. These components play a crucial role in intercellular communication, delivered to recipient cells via endocytosis, membrane fusion, or receptor‐ligand binding, and participate in various physiological and pathological processes such as Alzheimer's disease. circRNA, circular RNA; HSP70, heat shock protein 70; lncRNA, long non‐coding RNA; miRNA, microRNA; mRNA, messenger RNA; MVB, multivesicular body; TSG101, tumor susceptibility gene 101.

According to the Minimum Information Requirements for Extracellular Vesicle Studies (MISEV) guidelines, 38 the term “exosome” should be reserved exclusively for vesicles confirmed to have an endosomal origin, typically demonstrated by the presence of intraluminal vesicles within multivesicular bodies or the expression of specific endosomal markers. Most studies cited in this review employ separation methods such as differential ultracentrifugation, density gradient centrifugation, or precipitation kits. While these techniques can enrich small extracellular vesicles (<200 nm), they cannot definitively distinguish exosomes from other small extracellular vesicle subtypes with similar biophysical properties (e.g., microvesicles budding directly from the plasma membrane). Therefore, the term “exosome” is still used herein to denote preparations enriched for small extracellular vesicles, rather than strictly purified exosomes. Future advances in EV separation technologies will aid in definitively attributing specific mitochondrial regulatory functions to true exosomes versus other small extracellular vesicle subtypes.

Role of exosomes in AD treatment

Exosomes act as carriers for toxic proteins and inflammatory factors, thereby accelerating disease progression in AD. Amyloid‐β oligomers (AβO) and phosphorylated tau protein (p‐tau) are currently recognized as toxic proteins. In AD, the accumulation of metabolically abnormal Aβ leading to AβO aggregation constitutes a key factor causing neuronal toxicity. 39 Abnormal phosphorylation of the tau protein leads to the formation of fibrillar tangles, disrupting the internal structure of neurons. 40 .Sardar Sinha et al. 41 found that exosomes secreted by neurons or microglia in AD patients can serve as carriers for AβO. Through intercellular transmission, they cause neuronal axonal damage, triggering pathological cascades in AD. Inhibiting exosome production alleviates AD neurotoxicity. Wang et al. 42 demonstrated that neuronal exosomes mediate trans‐synaptic transmission of tau protein, exacerbating neurofibrillary tangle formation. Additional studies indicate that inhibiting macrophage exosome synthesis significantly reduces tau protein accumulation both in vitro and in vivo. 43 Exosomes also amplify AD inflammation by delivering proinflammatory factors (e.g., interleukin [IL] ‐1β, tumor necrosis factor‐alpha [TNF‐α]) to activate microglia and astrocytes. Furthermore, specific miRNAs carried by exosomes (e.g., miR‐146a‐5p) promote oxidative stress and neuronal death by regulating pathways such as NF‐κB. 44 For example, miR‐146a‐5p in microglial exosomes targets HIF1α to regulate mitochondrial ROS production, thereby influencing NLRP3 inflammasome activation and inflammatory cytokine secretion. 45

Additionally, some studies have revealed that glial cell‐derived exosomes exert protective functions in AD by clearing toxic substances and activating neuroprotective pathways. Microglia‐derived exosomes participate in Aβ clearance by transporting Aβ to lysosomes for degradation. This process relies on the transmembrane receptor TREM2 expressed on the exosome membrane, whose specific binding to Aβ not only promotes clearance but also modulates the surrounding inflammatory environment, enhancing microglial phagocytic function. 46 Studies utilizing ultrasound stimulation of astrocytes to promote exosome secretion have demonstrated effective reduction of Aβ neurotoxicity and decreased amyloid plaque accumulation on neurons in both in vitro and in vivo experiments. 47 Another study revealed that PrPC, a surface protein on human cerebrospinal fluid‐derived exosomes, can sequester and neutralize diffusible ligands derived from Aβ. 48 Furthermore, specific miRNAs carried by exosomes can regulate neuroprotective pathways. For instance, microglial exosomes carrying miR‐124‐3p target the Rela/apolipoprotein E (ApoE) signaling pathway to inhibit Aβ deposition and exert neuroprotective effects. 49 Circulating exosomal miR‐342‐5p reduces Aβ production by downregulating BACE1 expression, thereby improving AD pathology. 50

Therefore, in the progression of AD, exosomes serve as key mediators that promote the spread of critical toxic proteins such as AβO and p‐tau between neurons, exacerbate neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, and drive the spread of AD pathology. Simultaneously, the bioactive molecules they carry (such as protective miRNAs) exert protective effects against AD pathology through multiple mechanisms, including promoting Aβ clearance, mitigating its neurotoxicity, inhibiting plaque deposition, and activating neuroprotective pathways (Figure 2). This dual functionality primarily depends on the parent cell secreting the exosomes. Neuronal exosomes often exhibit pro‐pathogenic effects, while glial‐derived exosomes typically participate in clearance and repair processes during early stages. However, when overloaded in later stages, they predominantly promote the spread of AD lesions. Consequently, different brain‐derived exosomes can serve as biomarkers for detecting and diagnosing AD at distinct stages.

Dual roles and molecular mechanisms of exosomes in AD. Exosomes exert bidirectional regulatory effects in Alzheimer's disease: On one hand, they transport Aβ oligomers, phosphorylated tau, and pro‐inflammatory factors (e.g., IL‐1β, TNF‐α, miR‐146a‐5p), promoting neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and pathological protein propagation to accelerate disease progression; On the other hand, glial cell‐derived exosomes can mediate Aβ clearance via the TREM2 receptor or carry protective miRNAs (e.g., miR‐124‐3p, miR‐342‐5p) to activate neuroprotective pathways, thereby inhibiting Aβ production and deposition and exerting neuroprotective effects. REGULATION OF MITOCHONDRIAL FUNCTION BY EXOSOMES IN AD

Mitochondrial dysfunction in AD is not confined to a single cell type but represents a network collapse affecting neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes throughout the CNS. Against this backdrop, exosomes emerge as key coordinators within this multicellular mitochondrial network, regulating mitochondrial quality control across distinct neuronal cell types through intercellular communication. Exosome‐mediated mitochondrial regulation in Alzheimer's disease: A summary of in vivo and in vitro evidence

Mitochondrial biogenesis

Mitochondrial biogenesis refers to the process of mitochondrial formation, encompassing mitochondrial DNA replication, transcription, translation, and mitochondrial protein synthesis. 51 Mitochondrial biogenesis is crucial for maintaining mitochondrial function and numbers, playing a central role in numerous cellular physiological activities such as energy metabolism, cellular signaling transduction, and apoptosis. 52 In AD, mitochondrial dysfunction frequently leads to abnormal energy metabolism, increased ROS production, and disrupted calcium homeostasis, ultimately triggering neuronal injury and death. 53 Impaired biogenesis may be a key factor contributing to mitochondrial dysfunction, regulated by multiple factors including p‐AMPKα, PGC‐1α, NRF1, and TFAM. 54 In AD patient brain tissues and cellular models, reduced expression of mitogen‐induced biogenesis‐related factors (e.g., PGC‐1α, NRF1, TFAM) may lead to diminished mitochondrial numbers and impaired function, further accelerating AD pathology. 55 , 56 Researchers are currently investigating how exosomes derived from different cell types influence mitochondrial biogenesis by transferring these key regulatory factors. For instance, studies in a liver ischemia–reperfusion injury model demonstrate that adipose‐derived MSC‐derived exosomes significantly elevate the expression levels of PGC‐1α, NRF1, TFAM, and other mitochondrial biogenesis‐related proteins, promoting mitochondrial function recovery. 57 While this provides valuable mechanistic insights into exosome‐mediated regulation of mitochondrial biogenesis, direct validation in AD models is needed to confirm the relevance of these findings to AD pathology. Chiang et al. 58 discovered that human neural stem cell‐derived small extracellular vesicle (sEV) preparations regulate the expression of essential genes for mitochondrial biogenesis in a 1‐methyl‐4‐phenylpyridinium (MPP+) induced SH‐SY5Y human neuroblastoma cell model. Further analysis revealed that resveratrol treatment significantly improved mitochondrial function, with its protective effects primarily mediated through activation of the AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) and nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor 2 (Nrf2) signaling pathways. This not only promotes mitochondrial health but also reduces oxidative stress. To investigate whether sirtuin 1 (SIRT1)‐related pathways mediate the regulation of mitochondrial function by neural stem cell‐derived exosomes, Li et al. 59 found in a conditional SIRT1 knockout APP/PS1 mouse model of neurodegeneration, that neural stem cell‐derived exosomes activate the SIRT1‐PGC‐1α pathway, promoting synthesis of NRF1 and complex IV in the respiratory chain, thereby improving mitochondrial biogenesis. This process reverses the disrupted spatial distribution of mitochondrial biogenesis‐related factors in AD and suppresses astrocyte activation, indicating that neural stem cell‐derived exosomes significantly promote mitochondrial biogenesis and hold promise as a potential therapeutic strategy for AD. 60 In summary, exosomes have garnered significant attention for their ability to transfer key regulatory factors and influence mitochondrial biogenesis. Exosomes derived from adipose‐derived MSCs and human neural stem cells can improve mitochondrial function and alleviate oxidative stress, with mechanisms involving activation of AMPK/Nrf2 and SIRT1–PGC1α signaling pathways. These findings highlight the potential of exosomes in AD therapy, providing important directions for developing novel therapeutic strategies against AD (Figure 3).

Mitochondrial fusion

Mitochondrial fusion is the process by which mitochondria form larger network structures through membrane fusion, playing a crucial role in maintaining mitochondrial function and cellular health. 61 Insufficient mitochondrial fusion leads to mitochondrial fragmentation, affecting their distribution and abundance within synapses, which in turn impairs synaptic transmission. 62 Furthermore, dysregulation of mitochondrial dynamics is associated with microtubule instability, potentially inhibiting axonal transport and exacerbating neuronal loss in AD. 63 Therefore, maintaining the equilibrium between mitochondrial fusion and fission by regulating fusion‐related mechanisms is crucial for delaying the progression of AD. Mitochondrial fusion primarily involves two steps: outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) fusion and inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) fusion. 64 . OMM fusion is mainly mediated by the transmembrane GTPase family proteins Mitofusin 1 (Mfn1) and Mitofusin 2 (Mfn2); while IMM fusion is facilitated by a dynein‐associated protein, optic atrophy protein 1 (OPA1). 65 Therefore, regulating the expression of mitochondrial fusion‐related proteins through gene therapy or drug intervention can help restore mitochondrial dynamics equilibrium and improve mitochondrial function. 66 For instance, Wang et al. 67 investigated OPA1's role in AD using the OPA1 activator BGP‐15, the OPA1 inhibitor myls22, and an OPA1 overexpression virus. They observed reduced OPA1 expression and mitochondrial morphological defects in microglia during AD progression. Upregulating OPA1 expression promoted ATP production and Aβ clearance in microglia, thereby improving cognitive function in APP/PS1 mice. Exosomes have been reported to regulate mitochondrial fusion mechanisms by influencing the expression of fusion‐related proteins. For instance, in the doxorubicin‐induced cardiotoxicity model, trophoblast stem cell‐derived sEV preparations were shown to upregulate Mfn2 expression in rat cardiomyocytes and heart failure mouse models, promoting mitochondrial fusion and mitigating cardiac damage. 68 This observation in a cardiovascular context suggests a potential mechanism by which exosomes might regulate mitochondrial fusion, a hypothesis that warrants investigation in AD models where mitochondrial dynamics are disrupted. Furthermore, plasma exosomes can regulate mitochondrial fusion by delivering specific fusion modulators. Exosome‐carried miR‐195 functions as a negative regulator of Mfn2, a key protein mediating mitochondrial fusion. In AD mouse models, plasma exosome‐mediated delivery of miR‐195 to hippocampal neurons suppresses Mfn2 expression, leading to reduced mitochondrial fusion and increased mitochondrial fragmentation. 69 , 70 , 71 While this appears counterintuitive given the established role of mitochondrial fusion in neuronal health, these findings suggest that precise spatiotemporal regulation of mitochondrial dynamics—rather than simply promoting fusion—may be critical for synaptic function. The observed enhancement of memory following miR‐195 delivery indicates that modulating mitochondrial fusion‐fission balance, even toward increased fission, could be beneficial in specific pathological contexts where excessive fusion or abnormal mitochondrial morphology contributes to dysfunction. Further studies are needed to elucidate the complex relationship between Mfn2 expression levels, mitochondrial network dynamics, and neuronal function in AD. These findings highlight the significant potential of targeting mitochondrial fusion proteins in AD therapy and suggest exosomes as promising delivery vehicles for such interventions. However, current research focusing on improving mitochondrial dynamics via engineered exosomes for AD treatment remains limited, and the potential application of exosomes in neurodegenerative diseases warrants further exploration (Figure 3).

Mitochondrial fission

Mitochondrial fission is the process by which mitochondria divide from larger network structures into smaller individuals, primarily regulated by dynamin‐related protein 1 (Drp1). 72 Drp1 assembles into helical structures on the mitochondrial outer membrane, generating mechanical force through its GTPase activity to constrict mitochondria into two independent mitochondria. 73 In AD brains, elevated Drp1 expression occurs, and Aβ and tau proteins interact with Drp1, leading to excessive mitochondrial fission. This results in smaller, more numerous mitochondria with impaired function, ultimately causing synaptic dysfunction, neuronal damage, and cognitive decline. 74 Research indicates that certain therapeutic approaches can restore mitochondrial kinetic equilibrium by inhibiting the activity of fission‐related proteins like Drp1, thereby improving mitochondrial function, reducing neuronal death, and demonstrating potential therapeutic efficacy for AD. 75 Several pharmacological strategies targeting Drp1 to inhibit excessive mitochondrial fission have been developed, primarily including: P110, a peptide inhibitor that specifically disrupts the Drp1/Fis1 interaction; mdivi‐1, a small‐molecule compound that blocks mitochondrial fission by inhibiting Drp1 GTPase activity; and other compounds that regulate Drp1 expression or modification. By reducing pathological mitochondrial fission, these drugs enhance mitochondrial function, alleviate oxidative stress, and decrease protein aggregation, demonstrating potential neuroprotective effects in preclinical models. 76 Exosomes, as transport carriers for intercellular communication, are being explored in studies investigating exosome‐based therapies to regulate mitochondrial fission. For instance, in septic cardiomyopathy models, macrophage‐derived exosomes carrying P110 peptides were shown to inhibit Drp1/Fis1 interactions, reducing mitochondrial fission and improving cellular respiration. 77 , 78 While these findings in non‐CNS contexts provide proof‐of‐concept for targeting Drp1‐mediated fission via exosomes, their relevance to AD requires direct experimental validation. Similarly, studies in osteoarthritis models demonstrated that bone marrow MSC‐derived exosomes carrying Nrf2 suppressed Drp1 phosphorylation, inhibiting excessive mitochondrial fission and dysfunction. 79 These observations suggest a potential role for Nrf2‐containing exosomes in modulating mitochondrial dynamics, a hypothesis that could be explored in AD research. These advances highlight the significant therapeutic potential of targeting mitochondrial fission, particularly through exosome‐mediated strategies, in treating AD (Figure 3).

Mitochondrial autophagy

Mitochondrial autophagy is a physiological process whereby cells selectively eliminate damaged or dysfunctional mitochondria. 80 By precisely removing impaired mitochondria, this mechanism effectively prevents the accumulation of mitochondrial DNA mutations, suppresses excessive ROS production, and halts further deterioration of mitochondrial function, thereby maintaining normal mitochondrial physiology and cellular homeostasis. 81 Studies indicate that mitochondrial autophagy dysfunction is prevalent in brain tissue and derived cells from AD patients. Multiple AD‐associated pathological factors, such as tau protein, Aβ, ApoE4, and PSEN1 mutations, impair the degradation of dysfunctional mitochondria, thereby triggering mitochondrial autophagy impairment. 82 Taking tau protein as an example, its abnormal phosphorylation interferes with normal mitochondrial transport and function, thereby obstructing the smooth progression of mitochondrial autophagy. 83 Meanwhile, abnormal accumulation of Aβ leads to mitochondrial damage, elevating oxidative stress levels within mitochondria and subsequently disrupting the normal mitochondrial autophagy process. 84 These pathologically induced mitochondrial autophagy dysfunctions further exacerbate the pathological progression of AD, severely impairing neuronal function and survival. Research indicates that exosomes regulate mitochondrial autophagy through multiple mechanisms. For instance, Zhang et al. 85 reported that engineered activated neutrophil‐derived exosomes fuse with neuronal cell membranes, releasing their contents into the cytoplasm. This process modulates intracellular physiological parameters such as Ca2 + concentration, membrane potential, and ROS levels. A moderate increase in intracellular calcium ions activates calcium‐dependent protein kinase (e.g., calmodulin‐dependent protein kinase II), thereby activating the AMPK signaling pathway and initiating mitochondrial autophagy. Furthermore, substances released upon fusion of engineered activated neutrophil‐derived exosomes with the cell membrane can directly act on mitochondria, regulating mitochondrial permeability and function to promote mitochondrial autophagy, thereby exerting neuroprotective effects in AD. 86 Li et al. 87 discovered that in an AD cellular model constructed using Aβ, abnormal upregulation of Aβ1‐42 led to massive neuronal death, accompanied by mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired autophagy mechanisms. Building on this, the researchers further revealed that M2 microglia‐derived sEVs, characterized by CD63 and CD81 positivity could be effectively internalized by HT‐22 cells and microtubule‐associated protein 2‐positive neuronal cells from APP/PS1 transgenic mice, thereby exerting their neuroprotective effects. Data indicate that, in AD cellular models, M2‐EXOs significantly enhance cell viability, restore disrupted mitochondrial membrane potential, and dose‐dependently reduce mitochondrial and intracellular ROS accumulation. Further molecular mechanism studies revealed that M2‐EXOs' effects are closely associated with the PINK1/Parkin‐mediated mitochondrial autophagy pathway. Incubation with M2‐EXOs reverses the expression levels of autophagy‐related proteins Beclin1, LC3II, PINK1, and Parkin in AD model mice. These findings suggest that M2‐EXOs optimally regulate PINK1/Parkin‐mediated mitochondrial autophagy, exerting protective effects in AD pathogenesis and providing crucial insights for developing novel AD therapeutic strategies. 87 Furthermore, reports indicate that curcumin modulates mitochondrial bioenergetics, enhances mitochondrial function, and activates key proteins in the mitochondrial autophagy pathway (such as Unc‐51‐like kinase 1), thereby promoting the clearance of damaged mitochondria and maintaining mitochondrial health. 88 Ding et al. 89 discovered that engineered activated neutrophil‐derived exosomes can carry curcumin, effectively delivering it to target cells to enhance their regulation of mitochondrial autophagy. Exosomes protect curcumin from degradation and facilitate its crossing of the blood–brain barrier, increasing its concentration and efficacy within the brain. This combined strategy holds promise for novel therapeutic approaches in AD. In summary, mitochondrial autophagy is crucial for maintaining cellular homeostasis, and its impairment exacerbates AD pathology. Engineered activated exosomes can regulate mitochondrial autophagy indirectly by fusing with cell membranes to release cargo or by modulating pathways such as PINK1/Parkin. Curcumin delivered via exosomes enhances mitochondrial function, activates key autophagy pathway proteins, and promotes the clearance of damaged mitochondria, offering novel therapeutic strategies for AD (Figure 3).

THERAPEUTIC ROLE OF EXOSOMES IN REGULATING MITOCHONDRIA‐RELATED SIGNALING PATHWAYS IN AD

Nrf2 signaling pathway

The Nrf2 signaling pathway plays a crucial role in neuronal antioxidant defense and in preventing cellular damage in AD. 90 Under normal physiological conditions, Nrf2 is primarily negatively regulated by Kelch‐like ECH‐associated protein 1 (Keap1) and regulates the expression of a series of cell‐protective enzymes, such as superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2), catalase (CAT), and heme oxygenase 1 (HO‐1). 91 Multiple reports indicate that interactions between exosomes and Nrf2 promote the production of protective enzymes and regulate mitochondrial dynamics, thereby enhancing cellular antioxidant capacity and mitochondrial function. Exosomes also serve as delivery vehicles for Nrf2 activators, altering Nrf2 signaling in receptor neurons by directly transferring Nrf2‐related proteins or mRNA and transporting Keap1 inhibitors, thereby enhancing mitochondrial antioxidant capacity. 92 , 93 , 94 In traumatic brain injury (TBI) rat models, Zhang et al. 95 demonstrated that astrocyte‐derived exosome (ADE) therapy alleviated neurobehavioral deficits, cognitive impairments, and cerebral edema. Mechanistically, this protective effect was attributed to activation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway in hippocampal neurons, leading to suppressed mitochondrial oxidative stress and reduced neuronal apoptosis. 96 While TBI and AD share pathological features such as neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, the direct applicability of these findings to AD requires validation in AD‐specific models. In contrast, studies in APP/PS1 mice by Wang et al. 97 provide direct AD‐relevant evidence, demonstrating that umbilical cord blood MSC‐derived exosomes modulate the Nrf2/Keap1 pathway, mitigate mitochondrial damage, increase hippocampal neuron numbers, and reduce Aβ burden. This suggests that exosomes, through their interaction with Nrf2, offer a promising new therapeutic strategy for AD.

PINK1/Parkin pathway

The PINK1/Parkin pathway is a key regulator of mitochondrial autophagy, enabling the clearance of damaged mitochondria and alleviating mitochondrial dysfunction. 98 When mitochondria are damaged, their membrane potential undergoes changes, which serve as a critical signal triggering mitochondrial autophagy. 99 In this process, proteins such as PINK1 and the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin play essential roles. 100 Upon loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, PINK1 stably accumulates on the outer mitochondrial membrane, where it is phosphorylated and activates Parkin. Activated Parkin then ubiquitinates outer mitochondrial membrane proteins, labeling damaged mitochondria as autophagic substrates. Ubiquitinated mitochondria are then recognized by autophagy‐associated proteins and enclosed within double‐membrane phagosomes to form autophagosomes, ultimately completing the degradation and clearance of damaged mitochondria. 101 , 102 Studies indicate that exosomes can promote mitochondrial autophagy by regulating the PINK1/Parkin pathway, thereby exerting neuroprotective effects in AD. Human neural stem cell‐derived exosomes (hNSC‐Exos) activate the PINK1/Parkin pathway to induce mitochondrial autophagy, thereby improving mitochondrial function, reducing neuronal apoptosis, and protecting against oxidative stress‐induced neuronal damage. 103 Additional studies indicate that M2‐type macrophage‐derived exosomes can be internalized by neurons in AD models, partially restoring cellular viability, repairing mitochondrial membrane potential, reducing ROS accumulation, and exerting neuroprotective effects through the PINK1/Parkin pathway‐mediated mitochondrial autophagy. 87 , 104 In summary, exosomes precisely regulate the PINK1/Parkin pathway through their capacity to transport biomolecules, offering novel therapeutic insights and potential intervention targets for AD treatment.

SIRT1/PGC‐1α signaling pathway

The SIRT1/PGC‐1α signaling pathway is a core regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and cellular energy homeostasis, playing a crucial role in maintaining the health, number, and function of neuronal mitochondrial networks. 105 As an NAD+‐dependent deacetylase, SIRT1 promotes mitochondrial biogenesis‐related gene expression (e.g., NRF1, TFAM) by activating PGC‐1α, thereby enhancing mitochondrial function, improving energy metabolism, and reducing oxidative stress. 106 . During AD pathogenesis, SIRT1/PGC‐1α pathway activity is frequently suppressed, leading to impaired mitochondrial biogenesis and disrupted energy metabolism. 107 Research 108 indicates that exosomes can activate this pathway by delivering key factors such as SIRT1, PGC‐1α, or related regulatory molecules, thereby restoring mitochondrial biogenesis and improving mitochondrial function. For instance, exosomals derived from neural stem cells activate the SIRT1/PGC‐1α signaling pathway, upregulate NRF1 and mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV expression, promote mitochondrial biogenesis, and alleviate mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal damage in AD models. 69 Furthermore, mesenchymal stem cell‐derived exosomes enhance mitochondrial function through similar mechanisms, suppress ROS production, and exert neuroprotective effects. 109 Thus, exosome‐mediated regulation of the SIRT1/PGC‐1α pathway offers novel therapeutic strategies and potential targets for improving mitochondrial dysfunction in AD.

Other pathways

Hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1 (HIF‐1) is a heterodimeric transcription factor composed of an α subunit (HIF‐1α) and a β subunit (HIF‐1β), playing a crucial role in cellular adaptation to hypoxic environments. 110 Under hypoxic conditions, HIF‐1‐dependent metabolic reprogramming influences glucose metabolism, mitochondrial function, antioxidant capacity, and neuronal survival. 111 The role of HIF‐1 in AD is complex. On one hand, HIF‐1α exhibits neuroprotective properties by counteracting the harmful effects of Aβ and preventing excessive tau phosphorylation, providing a rationale for its potential as a therapeutic target in AD. 112 On the other hand, the interaction between HIF‐1 signaling and exosome‐mediated cellular communication is also considered a key factor driving the progression of neurodegenerative diseases. 113 Hypoxia‐treated adipose‐derived stem cell exosomes can specifically deliver HIF‐1 and other molecules to the brain, releasing regulatory components such as mRNAs, proteins, and miRNAs. These components then modulate HIF‐1‐dependent metabolic pathways, exerting direct or indirect effects on energy metabolism stability and mitochondrial function. 9 , 114 However, research on HIF‐1–exosome interactions in the AD field remains relatively scarce. This suggests that further exploration of the connection between HIF‐1 and exosomes and their role in disease progression holds significant importance for future AD research.

In summary, exosomes offer a promising new therapeutic strategy for AD by interacting with mitochondrial‐related signaling pathways. Exosomes not only deliver key antioxidant signaling molecules but also precisely regulate antioxidant defense systems in recipient neurons. This approach mitigates oxidative stress damage, improves mitochondrial function, and reduces neuronal apoptosis. Leveraging their unique advantages and mechanisms, exosome‐based therapies hold promise as a significant breakthrough in treating neurodegenerative diseases, offering new hope for improving patient outcomes.

CONCLUSION AND OUTLOOK

As a common neurodegenerative disease with complex and incompletely understood pathogenesis, AD imposes a heavy burden on healthcare systems worldwide and on affected families. In recent years, mitochondrial dysfunction has been widely recognized as one of the earliest core alterations in the AD pathological process, encompassing multiple aspects such as impaired mitochondrial energy metabolism, excessive ROS production, disrupted mitochondrial homeostasis, and increased susceptibility to apoptosis. Concurrently, exosomes—as crucial mediators of intercellular communication—have drawn increasing attention for their capacity to regulate mitochondrial function within the nervous system. Existing studies 115 indicate that exosomes derived from diverse cellular sources exhibit distinct advantages in AD therapy. Among these, MSC‐derived exosomes (including those from bone marrow, umbilical cord, adipose tissue, etc.) have been most extensively studied. By delivering key factors such as SIRT1, PGC‐1α, and NRF1 to promote mitochondrial biogenesis, suppress ROS production, and improve energy metabolism. Surface modifications (e.g., specific rabies virus glycoprotein) enhance neuronal targeting, significantly reducing Aβ deposition and tau phosphorylation. Glial cell‐derived exosomes, particularly those exhibiting the M2 phenotype, promote mitochondrial autophagy by activating the PINK1/Parkin pathway, repair mitochondrial membrane potential, reduce ROS accumulation, and exert anti‐inflammatory effects within the pathological microenvironment. 116 Exosomes derived from neural stem cells improve mitochondrial function by activating the SIRT1‐PGC1α signaling pathway, promoting mitochondrial biogenesis and respiratory chain complex synthesis. 69 Additionally, astrocyte‐derived sEV preparations enriched by differential centrifugation alleviate oxidative stress and protect neurons by activating the Nrf2/HO‐1 pathway. 117 With advances in engineering techniques, engineered exosomes (e.g., curcumin‐loaded neutrophil‐derived exosomes, MSC‐derived exosomes modified with specific rabies virus glycoproteins) demonstrate greater potential in enhancing targeting, stability, and therapeutic efficacy, providing novel tools for precisely regulating mitochondrial function. MSC‐derived exosomes are highly favored, partly because they can target mitochondrial dysfunction by carrying multiple mitochondrial protective factors such as SIRT1, PGC‐1α, and NRF1. These factors promote mitochondrial biogenesis, inhibit ROS production, and improve energy metabolism. Second, their small size (30‐150 nm) enables strong blood–brain barrier penetration, allowing direct action on neurons. Third, they possess immunomodulatory and anti‐inflammatory effects, mitigating neuroinflammation by regulating microglial activation, thereby indirectly protecting mitochondrial function. Regarding clinical translation, MSC‐derived exosomes from sources such as bone marrow (BMSC), adipose tissue (ADSC), and umbilical cord (HUCSC) have demonstrated efficacy in AD animal models, significantly reducing Aβ deposition and tau phosphorylation. They also exhibit substantial potential for engineering modifications—for instance, rabies virus glycoprotein modification enables neuronal targeting and enhances delivery efficiency. In summary, these findings reposition the restoration of mitochondrial network homeostasis in the CNS as a key therapeutic target for AD. By understanding how exosomes coordinate mitochondrial function across different neuronal cell types, we can develop more effective strategies aimed at repairing dysfunction at the network level rather than targeting individual cells in isolation. Future efforts should integrate engineering techniques to optimize delivery efficiency and explore synergistic mechanisms among exosomes from different sources, thereby advancing the clinical translation of mitochondrial‐targeted therapies for AD.

Although extensive in vitro and animal model studies have revealed diverse mechanisms by which exosomes regulate mitochondrial function, their specific role in the pathological state of human AD remains largely enigmatic. The diversity of exosome‐producing cells, the complexity of their action mechanisms, and the challenges of efficient delivery across the blood–brain barrier continue to limit their in‐depth application in AD. Future research should focus on elucidating the dynamic changes of exosomes from different sources during AD progression and their mitochondrial regulatory mechanisms; developing engineered exosomes with targeted and controllable properties for AD intervention. Integrating multi‐omics and imaging technologies could enable visual tracking and precise modulation of exosome‐mediated signaling pathways. By bridging fundamental research with clinical needs, exosomes hold promise as novel therapeutic strategies to regulate mitochondrial function and slow AD progression, offering fresh avenues for intervention.
