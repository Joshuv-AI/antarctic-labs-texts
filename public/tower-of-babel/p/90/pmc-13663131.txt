Early identification of cirrhosis risk in type 2 diabetes using the Fibrosis-4 index: evidence from a large U.S. cohort
Authors: Mark W McGiffin, Patrick J Kiel, David R Foster, Kevin J Mumford, Gregory H Palmrose, Michael A Preston
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsCirrhosis remains a leading cause of liver-related morbidity and mortality and is often diagnosed only at advanced stages. Individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are at increased risk, yet systematic screening for early fibrosis is rarely implemented. We evaluated whether the Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index, a widely used non-invasive biomarker, can identify elevated cirrhosis risk near the time of T2DM diagnosis.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using the NIH All of Us Research Program database. Adults with newly diagnosed T2DM and laboratory data for FIB-4 calculation within ± 365 days of diagnosis were included. Cases were participants who subsequently developed cirrhosis; controls were those who did not. FIB-4 distributions were compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Cox proportional hazards models assessed the association between FIB-4 and time to cirrhosis. Higher FIB-4 was associated with increased cirrhosis risk in Cox regression analyses (adjusted HR = 1.16 per 1‑unit increase; 95% CI 1.08–1.24; p < 0.001). ROC analysis yielded an AUROC of 0.66, indicating moderate discriminative ability, with an optimal cutoff of 1.23 derived for internal stratification. Introduction

By 2035, Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) will affect over 600 million people globally and remains a major driver of cardiovascular, renal, and hepatic complications [1]. Among these, Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is particularly prevalent, affecting up to 65% of individuals with T2DM [2–4]. MASLD encompasses a spectrum of liver pathology, ranging from simple steatosis to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), which can progress to advanced hepatic fibrosis, cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and ultimately liver failure [5]. This high prevalence in T2DM populations underscores the need for early detection and treatment to prevent future complications.

The asymptomatic nature of MASLD in its early stages poses a significant challenge to timely diagnosis and intervention. Liver biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing and staging hepatic fibrosis [6], however, its invasive nature, high cost, and potential for sampling error limit its utility in routine clinical practice, particularly for large-scale screening. Non-invasive alternatives, such as the Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index, offer a practical solution. Because MASLD-related fibrosis often coexists with metabolic risk factors, FIB-4 based on age and routine labs offers a scalable approach for early risk stratification. FIB-4 is calculated using readily available laboratory parameters which include the following: age, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and platelet count, and has been validated in various liver diseases, including hepatitis C, Human Immunodeficiency Virus, and MASLD [7–9].

Despite guideline recommendations from major liver and diabetes societies, FIB-4 remains underutilized in routine diabetes care [2]. This underuse may stem from limited awareness among primary care providers, lack of training on interpreting fibrosis scores, and uncertainty about appropriate thresholds in diverse populations [2, 10–14]. Additionally, the absence of standardized workflows for fibrosis screening in diabetes management and concerns about false positives or over-referral contribute to clinical inertia. These barriers highlight the need for implementation strategies that integrate FIB-4 into existing diabetes care pathways [2, 10–14].

Social determinants of health (SDOH) also play a critical role in the progression and detection of MASLD [15]. Individuals from disadvantaged backgrounds may experience delayed diagnosis due to limited access to screening, lower health literacy, and systemic barriers within healthcare delivery. These disparities can lead to more advanced disease at presentation and worse outcomes [15]. Understanding the influence of SDOH on fibrosis risk and screening practices is essential for developing equitable strategies for early detection in T2DM populations.

This study aimed to evaluate whether FIB-4 scores measured within one year of a new T2DM diagnosis differ between individuals who later develop cirrhosis and those who do not, and assess whether these associations vary by SDOH. The goal of this study is not diagnostic classification but early risk signaling at the time of diabetes diagnosis. Detecting risk in the first 12 months establishes a baseline for prognosis, triggers timely referral when FIB-4 is high, and enables earlier initiation/intensification of cardiometabolic therapies (e.g., structured weight-loss programs, incretin-based agents when appropriate) that guidelines endorse for MASLD [2]. By leveraging retrospective data from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) All of Us (AoU) database, we seek to determine the utility of FIB-4 as an early screening tool in diabetic patients.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of adults with newly diagnosed T2DM, with a case–control analytic comparison of baseline FIB‑4 values and longitudinal time‑to‑event analyses for incident cirrhosis using data from the NIH AoU Research Program Curated Data Repository (CDR v8) [16]. The objective was to compare FIB-4 scores obtained within 365 days of the index date (the earliest recorded T2DM diagnosis) between participants who subsequently developed cirrhosis (cases) and those who did not (controls). In all cases, FIB-4 measurements preceded cirrhosis diagnosis. We included all eligible controls without matching to maximize statistical power and maintain population diversity. Potential confounding was addressed through stratified bootstrapping and multivariable modeling which preserved the joint distribution of key covariates. Follow-up extended from the index date until cirrhosis diagnosis, last available record, or October 1, 2023 (administrative censoring date).

Data source

The NIH AoU Curated Data Repository (CDR v8) includes 623,464 participants from across the United States, with intentional oversampling of groups historically underrepresented in biomedical research [16, 17]. Approximately 84% of participants identify as underrepresented in research, and 43% as racial or ethnic minorities [17]. The database integrates electronic health record (EHR) data, including laboratory values, diagnoses, and physical measurements, with participant-reported survey data on health behaviors, socioeconomic factors, and lifestyle. All data are de-identified and linked using generic participant identifiers.

Eligibility criteria

Adults (≥ 18 years) from the NIH AoU CDR v8 were eligible if they met case or control criteria. Condition concepts were identified using Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) standard concept IDs, which map to the Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine – Clinical Terms (SNOMED-CT) clinical terminology. Because OMOP standardizes all conditions to SNOMED rather than ICD-9/10, SNOMED concept codes were used for validation and reproducibility (Supplemental materials, Table A.1–4). The index date was the earliest recorded T2DM diagnosis in the electronic health record.

Cases were participants with a subsequent diagnosis of cirrhosis (OMOP concepts for cirrhosis and non-alcoholic cirrhosis). Controls were participants with T2DM who never had a cirrhosis diagnosis during follow-up. Laboratory values for FIB-4 calculation were required within ± 365 days of the index date; the closest measurement was used.

Follow-up began on the index date and continued until the earliest of: (1) cirrhosis diagnosis, (2) last available record, or (3) October 1, 2023 (administrative censoring date). The exclusion criteria were adapted from a previously published study by Anstee et al. [18]. Participants with alcohol-related liver disease, viral hepatitis, autoimmune hepatitis, Wilson’s disease, or hepatotoxic medication use were excluded (supplemental materials, Tables A.1–4 for a complete list of inclusion/exclusion criteria).

The final analytic cohort included 9,253 participants: 136 cases and 9,117 controls. All eligible participants without cirrhosis were included to maximize sample size and diversity. Confounding was addressed through stratified bootstrapping and multivariable modeling.

Missingness

Missing data were handled using complete-case principles. Participants missing any required component of the FIB-4 calculation (AST, ALT, or platelet count within ± 365 days of the index date) were excluded from the analytic cohort. Implausible laboratory values (AST or ALT > 10,000 U/L; platelet count < 0 or > 2,500 × 10³/µL) were also excluded to minimize the influence of data-entry errors and acute illness. For covariates, including social determinants of health variables, missing survey or EHR values were retained as missing and excluded listwise in multivariable models and bootstrap stratification. We did not perform statistical imputation because imputing components of a non-linear composite biomarker (FIB-4) risks misclassification and distortion of scale [19], and laboratory testing patterns in EHRs are unlikely to satisfy missing-at-random assumptions [20].

Social determinants of health

SDOH variables were extracted from AoU survey instruments and harmonized following the official All of Us Survey Codebooks and the All of Us SDOH User Guide, which define the underlying constructs and provide guidance on variable coding [21, 22]. These surveys incorporate items from validated instruments including the Social Cohesion Scale, Perceived Stress Scale, Everyday Discrimination Scale, and the MOS Social Support Scale.

Consistent with scoring recommendations in the SDOH User Guide, variables were transformed into binary indicators for regression modeling. Disability status was coded “yes” if participants endorsed any functional limitation item (e.g., difficulty dressing or bathing, walking/climbing, concentrating, blindness, deafness), reflecting the AoU functional limitation construct. Educational attainment was dichotomized as “college” (any college or higher degree) versus “no college,” following standard AoU categorical groupings. Employment status was coded “employed” for individuals reporting current wage or self-employment. Annual income was dichotomized based on the AoU income brackets, using less than $75,000 vs. greater than or equal to $75,000 as recommended for analytic grouping. Smoking status was classified using the AoU measure of lifetime cigarette use, with greater than or equal to 100 cigarettes indicating ever-smoking, consistent with CDC standard definitions adopted in the AoU surveys. Marital status was coded “married” or “not married” based on current relationship status. Insurance status was coded as a binary indicator of any reported health insurance coverage.

Data analysis

Descriptive and comparative analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using R statistical software (v4.4.0) available in the NIH AoU researcher workbench cloud space. Descriptive statistics were generated for demographic and clinical characteristics of both patients with cirrhosis and those without. Continuous variables were summarized as medians (range) and categorical variables as frequencies (%). FIB-4 distributions were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test due to non-normality. Non-parametric bootstrapping (1,000 iterations) generated 95% CIs for medians.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the difference in FIB-4 scores between cirrhosis and control groups while accounting for potential confounding variables, we performed a stratified bootstrap analysis. Stratification was based on a combination of demographic and clinical covariates, including race, sex at birth, hypertension (HTN), cholesterol status (CHOL), triglyceride status (TG), body mass index (BMI), and age. Stratification variables were selected based on their significant association with cirrhosis in univariate analyses and their known clinical relevance to liver disease progression [23, 24]. A stratum variable was created using the interaction of these covariates. For each of the 1,000 bootstrap iterations, we resampled with replacement within each stratum to preserve the joint distribution of confounders. In each iteration, we calculated the median FIB-4 score for cases and controls and recorded the difference. We also performed a Wilcoxon rank-sum test to assess the statistical significance of the difference in FIB-4 scores between groups. A 95% confidence interval for the median difference was computed using the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution.

Predictive performance of the Fibrosis-4 index

We assessed the discriminative ability of FIB-4 for predicting cirrhosis using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis [25]. ROC curves were generated by plotting sensitivity versus 1-specificity across a range of FIB-4 thresholds. The area under the ROC curve (AUROC) was calculated using the non-parametric DeLong method to quantify overall performance [26].

The optimal cutoff was determined using Youden’s J statistic (J = sensitivity + specificity – 1) [27], which identifies the threshold that maximizes the balance between true positive and false positive rates. For our cohort, this cutoff was 1.228. We reported AUROC, 95% confidence intervals, sensitivity, specificity, and Youden’s J for the optimal threshold.

All ROC analyses were performed in R (v4.4.0) using the pROC package. Confidence intervals for AUROC were computed via 1,000 stratified bootstrap replicates to account for sampling variability.

Survival analysis

We conducted a survival analysis to evaluate whether FIB-4 scores measured at the time of type 2 diabetes diagnosis were associated with time to cirrhosis. Time-to-event was defined as the number of days from diabetes diagnosis to cirrhosis diagnosis or censoring at the last available follow-up (October 1, 2023). Cox proportional hazards models were fit with FIB-4 as a continuous predictor, and additional models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and hypertriglyceridemia. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals.

To assess the robustness of the hazard ratio estimate, we performed a non-parametric bootstrap with 1,000 resamples. The bootstrap procedure estimated the hazard ratio and 95% confidence interval for FIB-4. We repeated the bootstrap analysis within each racial subgroup (White, Black or African American, and Asian/Other) to evaluate the consistency of FIB-4’s predictive value across populations. Hazard ratios and percentile-based 95% confidence intervals were calculated for each group.

To visualize time-to-event differences, we generated Kaplan-Meier survival curves stratified by FIB-4 score using the optimal cutoff identified from ROC analysis. Survival time was defined as days from T2DM diagnosis to cirrhosis diagnosis or censoring at last follow-up. Differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test.

Results

Patient characteristics

The analytic cohort included 9,253 participants (136 cases and 9,117 controls). Median age at T2DM diagnosis was similar between groups (56.6 vs. 56.4 years). Sex distribution and most socioeconomic indicators were comparable (Table 1). Race distribution differed between groups (p = 0.003), with a higher proportion of White participants among cases (56.6% vs. 44.0%) and fewer Black or African American participants (16.2% vs. 28.1%). Cases had higher mean BMI (35.1 vs. 33.6 kg/m², p = 0.048) and greater prevalence of hypercholesterolemia (39.7% vs. 29.3%, p = 0.010), hypertension (91.2% vs. 84.5%, p = 0.042), and hypertriglyceridemia (percentages censored, p = 0.037).

Social determinants of health

The cirrhosis group had a higher percentage of patients identify as disabled compared to the control group (28.9% vs. 16.5%; p = 0.004; Table 1). No other differences in SDOH factors were observed between cirrhosis and non-cirrhosis groups.

Fibrosis-4 index scores

Median FIB-4 was higher among cases compared to controls (1.379 [95% CI: 1.252–1.484] vs. 0.998 [95% CI: 0.985–1.012]; Wilcoxon W = 818,003, p < 0.001; Fig. 1). The median difference was 0.38 (95% CI: 0.25–0.49). Bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) confirmed the robustness of this difference. FIB-4 scores were also higher in cases across most racial groups, except among Black or African American participants, where medians were similar (0.91 vs. 0.90) (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Statistical analysis

Hazard modeling

In Cox proportional hazards analyses, higher FIB‑4 scores measured near the time of type 2 diabetes diagnosis were associated with increased cirrhosis risk. In the primary adjusted model including age, sex, body mass index, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and hypertriglyceridemia, FIB‑4 remained independently associated with cirrhosis (HR = 1.16 per 1‑unit increase; 95% CI 1.08–1.24; p < 0.001), and proportional hazards assumptions were satisfied for all covariates (global p = 0.53). Results were consistent with the unadjusted Cox model (HR = 1.18; 95% CI 1.11–1.25) and robust to bootstrap resampling. Race‑stratified analyses demonstrated similar associations in White and Asian/Other participants, but not among Black or African American participants, where estimates were imprecise and not statistically significant.

Predictive performance of FIB-4

ROC analysis produced an AUROC of 0.659 (95% CI: 0.61–0.71). The optimal cutoff by Youden’s J was 1.228, yielding sensitivity of 0.61 and specificity of 0.66 (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Key findings

This study demonstrates that FIB-4 scores obtained within one year of T2DM diagnosis are higher among individuals who later develop cirrhosis compared to those who do not. Survival analysis showed that higher FIB-4 scores were associated with shorter time to cirrhosis, and ROC analysis indicated moderate discriminative ability (AUROC = 0.659) with an optimal cutoff of 1.228. This level of discrimination is consistent with prior population-based evaluations of FIB-4 in metabolically complex cohorts [28–31]. These findings suggest that hepatic injury may be present at or near the time of diabetes diagnosis, highlighting the potential of FIB-4 as an early risk indicator in this high-risk population.

Notably, although age, sex, ethnicity, and insurance status were comparable between groups, individuals who later developed cirrhosis had higher disability rates and greater metabolic comorbidity burden including higher BMI, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and hypertriglyceridemia. These baseline differences reflect real-world inequities in metabolic and functional health that may influence liver disease progression. Although our stratified bootstrap analysis adjusted for BMI, race, and cardiometabolic comorbidities to control confounding in the association between FIB-4 and cirrhosis, these underlying population differences remain clinically important when considering implementation and equity.

Clinical implications

FIB-4 is inexpensive, uses routinely available laboratory values, and can be automated within electronic health records. Incorporating FIB-4 into diabetes care workflows could enable early identification of patients at elevated risk for liver disease progression, prompting timely referral for imaging or hepatology evaluation. While FIB-4 alone is not diagnostic, its use as a first-line screening tool could reduce reliance on invasive procedures and improve care efficiency. Embedding automated FIB-4 alerts in EHR systems may facilitate scalable implementation in primary care and endocrinology settings.

Recent studies in patients with type 2 diabetes highlight the broader challenge of non‑invasive fibrosis risk stratification in metabolically complex populations. For example, the LIVERSTAT algorithm, an artificial intelligence‑based composite score, demonstrated similar overall accuracy to FIB‑4, both as a stand‑alone test and as a first‑line tool to identify candidates for liver stiffness measurement, with AUROC values in the moderate range and comparable false‑negative rates [32]. Notably, increased model complexity did not substantially improve discrimination for compensated advanced chronic liver disease. Together with our findings, these data suggest that the modest predictive performance observed for FIB‑4 reflects inherent limitations of current non‑invasive tests in T2DM, rather than shortcomings of any single tool, reinforcing the role of FIB‑4 as an accessible first‑line risk signal rather than a definitive diagnostic test.

Equity and SDOH considerations

Our findings indicate that SDOH-related factors may influence both liver disease progression and the utility of FIB-4 in T2DM populations. Although most SDOH measures (income, education, employment, marital status, smoking, and insurance coverage) did not differ significantly, disability status was nearly twice as prevalent among individuals who developed cirrhosis (28.9% vs. 16.5%), suggesting that functional limitations may reduce access to preventive care, liver disease monitoring, or diabetes management.

Differences in race distribution were also notable. The cirrhosis group included a higher proportion of White participants and fewer Black or African American participants compared with controls. While this pattern may reflect true epidemiologic differences, it may also reflect differential healthcare access, underdiagnosis, or unmeasured SDOH-related barriers. Indeed, the reduced predictive ability of FIB-4 among Black participants observed in our subgroup analysis may stem from structural inequities, differences in care pathways, or variation in baseline laboratory values rather than biological differences alone.

These findings reinforce that improving equity in liver disease detection requires addressing upstream factors such as disability-related barriers, uneven access to laboratory testing, and differential follow-up after abnormal results. Embedding automated FIB-4-based alerts within EHR systems may help standardize early detection, but implementation strategies must account for the disparities highlighted above.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. Cirrhosis diagnoses were identified using EHR diagnostic codes without systematic imaging or histologic confirmation, which may have introduced outcome misclassification typical of large real-world cohorts. Participants lacking complete FIB-4 components were excluded, which likely enriched the analytic cohort for individuals with more regular healthcare engagement and may have systematically excluded those with lower access to preventive services. This is consistent with the SDOH patterns we observed, including a significantly higher prevalence of disability among cirrhosis cases, which may reflect both increased health burden and differential care utilization.

Second, several clinical and metabolic characteristics differed significantly between groups, including BMI, hypercholesterolemia, hypertension, and hypertriglyceridemia. These conditions are common in T2DM but also directly influence liver disease progression, potentially confounding both the association between FIB-4 and cirrhosis and the performance of FIB-4 as a predictor. Age-related inflation of FIB-4 and lower ALT levels frequently observed in metabolic liver disease may further limit discriminatory ability and contribute to both false-positive and false-negative classification in this population.

Third, although race differences were significant in baseline characteristics, subgroup analyses were underpowered, limiting conclusions about population-specific performance. Given the known variability in non-invasive test performance across racial and metabolic subgroups [33], FIB-4’s moderate performance in our study is consistent with prior reports [34, 35]. Other non-invasive scores such as the NAFLD Fibrosis Score and AST-to-Platelet Ratio Index also show reduced specificity in older adults and individuals with metabolic comorbidities, suggesting limitations common across these markers in T2DM [36].

Lastly, the optimal cutoff of 1.228 was derived using Youden’s J statistic within this dataset and may not generalize to clinical thresholds validated in non-diabetic populations. Prospective studies and population-specific calibration will be required to establish clinically meaningful cut-points for T2DM.

Future research directions

Prospective studies are needed to validate the predictive utility of FIB-4 at the time of T2DM diagnosis and to determine its role in clinical decision-making. Future work should explore integration of FIB-4 into electronic health record systems with automated alerts, evaluate cost-effectiveness of early screening strategies, and assess whether combining FIB-4 with other non-invasive markers or imaging improves accuracy. Research should also examine how SDOH influence both biomarker performance and access to follow-up care, and develop implementation strategies that address these disparities. Finally, population-specific calibration of FIB-4 thresholds may be necessary to ensure equitable risk stratification across diverse demographic groups.

Conclusion

FIB-4 scores measured near the time of T2DM diagnosis may serve as an early signal for cirrhosis risk. Integrating FIB-4 into routine diabetes care could facilitate earlier intervention and improve long-term liver outcomes. Future research should prioritize prospective validation, cost-effectiveness analyses, and strategies to address racial variability and SDOH-related barriers to equitable implementation.
