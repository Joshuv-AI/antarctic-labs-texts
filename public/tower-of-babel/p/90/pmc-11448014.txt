Survival and functional outcomes after hemiarthroplasty in children with proximal tibial osteosarcoma
Authors: Yuan Li, Hairong Xu, Yongkun Yang, Huachao Shan, Zhen Huang, Ke Ma, Weifeng Liu, Xiaohui Niu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTreatment options for correcting limb-length discrepancy after limb-salvage reconstruction for proximal tibial osteosarcoma in children have several limitations. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the feasibility, complications, prognosis, and clinical outcomes of reconstruction using hemiarthroplasty after tumor resection in pediatric patients with proximal tibial osteosarcoma.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive retrospective analysis of the data of pediatric patients with osteosarcoma of the proximal tibia who underwent surgery between December 2008 and November 2018 at our center. We enrolled 49 consecutive patients who underwent hemiarthroplasty. The cruciate ligaments of all patients were reconstructed using special spacers, and the medial and lateral collateral ligaments of the knee and joint capsule were reconstructed using a mesh. Postoperatively, if the unequal length of both lower limbs exceeded 4 cm or knee instability occurred, a second-stage surgery was performed for limb lengthening and replacing the distal femoral prosthesis. We analyzed the oncological prognosis, complications of hemiarthroplasty, postoperative stability, and postoperative function.ResultsThe follow-up period ranged between 11 and 159 months, with a median of 84 (62, 129) months. The overall 5-year survival rate was 83.2%. Thirty-nine patients survived at the end of the follow-up period with 34 prostheses (87.2%). The overall prosthesis survival rate was 87.4% after 5 years, indicating the long-term benefits of the procedure. Limb length was measured in 28 adult patients. The average limb-length discrepancy was 33 ± 15 mm with a median of 33 mm (21, 47); the femur and tibia caused a discrepancy of 8.5 ± 9.9 mm and 24.8 ± 15.5 mm, respectively. The patients had 30–135° of knee motion, with a mean of 82 ± 24°. The femoral tibial angle was greater on the affected side than on the healthy side, with a mean difference of 4.5°±3.6°. The Musculoskeletal Tumor Society (MSTS) score was 25 ± 3. Five patients underwent second-stage distal femoral prosthesis replacement, with mean MSTS scores of 24 ± 2 and 28 ± 1 before and after second-stage surgery, respectively.ConclusionsHemiarthroplasty in children reduces limb-length discrepancy in adulthood by rebuilding cruciate ligaments, lateral collateral ligaments, and the joint capsule, thereby improving knee stability.

Background

Osteosarcoma is a common primary bone tumor in pediatric and adolescent patients. The proximal tibia is the second most common site of osteosarcomas. According to the Bone and Soft Tissue Tumor Database of Beijing Jishuitan Hospital, the proximal tibia accounts for 24% of cases of osteosarcoma onset [1]. As complementary therapies, surgical techniques, prosthetic designs, and material science have been developed, limb-salvage surgery, a procedure that removes the tumor while preserving the limb, has become the main treatment method for osteosarcoma of the limb [2, 3]. Reconstruction after the resection of osteosarcoma of the limb is mainly performed using an endoprosthesis, and its benefits include reliability in the early stages, quick postoperative recovery, good appearance and psychological acceptance by the patient, and satisfactory limb function. However, surgical resection of the proximal tibial osteosarcoma leads to resection of the epiphysis and epiphyseal plate. This impedes proximal tibial growth in pediatric patients and causes unequal limb lengths. Simultaneously, the femoral side prosthesis damages the femoral epiphysis and epiphyseal plate and affects distal femoral growth. Therefore, limb-length discrepancy is an important issue in the limb-salvage reconstruction technique for proximal tibial osteosarcoma in children. Although some methods compensate for the limb-length discrepancy, they have deficiencies and shortcomings [4, 5].

Our hospital uses the following treatment strategy for pediatric patients with proximal tibial osteosarcoma. Hemiarthroplasty of the proximal tibia is performed in the first stage instead of conventional joint replacement surgery to reduce the damage to the distal femoral epiphysis and epiphyseal plate and to reduce the degree of limb-length discrepancy in patients. When the children reach adulthood, the tibial-side prosthesis is retained in the second stage, and the femoral-side prosthesis is replaced separately to improve the stability of the knee joint. The proximal tibial hemiarticular prosthesis has no metal connection to the femur prosthesis and is poorly stabilized, making it highly susceptible to joint instability, pain, deformity, and even dislocation [6]. To increase joint stability, we use an ETHIBOND suture to reconstruct the cruciate ligament during surgery. A polypropylene mesh has also been used in some patients to reconstruct the medial and lateral collateral ligaments and joint capsules. This is a retrospective study of pediatric patients who underwent proximal tibial hemiarthroplasty at our hospital. The surgical approach to proximal tibial hemiarthroplasty with reconstruction of joint stability, oncologic prognosis, complications of hemiarthroplasty, postoperative stability, and postoperative function are described in detail.

Methods

This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Jishuitan Hospital[ Approval No. of Ethics Committee: 积伦[K2023]第[248]号-00]. We retrospectively studied the data of pediatric patients with proximal tibial osteosarcoma who received limb salvage therapy between December 2008 and November 2018. Medical records (medical history and surgical details), histological slides, image files, and follow-up information were collected. Patients who met the following criteria were included in the study: (1) stage IIb classic osteosarcoma of the proximal tibia; (2) male patients aged ≤ 14 years and female patients aged ≤ 13 years; (3) resection of the tumor of the proximal tibia followed by hemiarthroplasty; and (4) concomitant reconstruction of the cruciate ligaments and the use of a patch to reconstruct the soft tissues. The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) multiple lesions in the ipsilateral limb at the time of surgery and (2) previous prosthetic replacement.

Based on the selection criteria, 49 patients were enrolled in this study, including 31 males and 18 females. The average age of the patients was 11 ± 1.7 years old (range, 7–14 years). All patients were routinely examined using radiography, local computed tomography (CT), local magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), emission computed tomography (ECT), and chest CT after admission to the hospital and were treated with a combination of high-dose methotrexate, ifosfamide, cisplatin, and doxorubicin chemotherapy after the pathology tests confirmed the diagnosis of classic osteosarcoma. After chemotherapy, we reviewed the radiography, local CT, local MRI, ECT, and chest CT findings to assess the efficacy of chemotherapy.

The surgical plan was confirmed based on imaging findings after chemotherapy. The tibial osteotomy plane was 3–5 cm from the imaging tumor borders. The prosthesis system was manufactured by LDK Co., Ltd., Beijing, China. The proximal tibial inlay was customized to accommodate the femoral condyles on the right and left sides of the depression. Two or three pairs of reconstruction holes were arranged in the middle of the inlay to reconstruct the cruciate ligament. The holes were located on the prosthesis side, and 4-mm notches were made to accommodate the suture knots used for reconstruction of the cruciate ligament (Fig. 1).

During the surgery, the proximal tibial tumor was resected based on the preoperative osteotomy position. The medullary cavity of the resected end was biopsied, and border safety was ensured. The meniscus was removed from the tibia. The cruciate ligaments were cut 1 cm from the tibia, and the femoral side cruciate ligaments were reconstructed. Two or three No. 5 ETHIBOND EXCEL MB66G sutures (Ethicon, USA) were threaded through the reconstruction hole at the bottom of the proximal tibial inlay to join the remaining cruciate ligaments. A knot was tied at the base of the inlay to secure it to the distal femur, and the cruciate ligaments were reconstructed. The knee was fixed such that the inlay could slide anteroposteriorly on the femoral surface by 0–90° and with < 5° of varus and valgus. A double-layer mesh was sutured to the posterior lateral joint capsule and the medial and lateral collateral ligaments for future use. A tibial prosthesis was installed and affixed using bone cement. An inlay was affixed to the prosthesis. After fixing the prosthesis, the mesh was used to wrap the anterior prosthesis, and the suture was fixed using nonabsorbable sutures. The front was wrapped to the level of the tibial plateau. The kneecap was sutured onto the patch using nonabsorbable sutures to reconstruct the endpoint of the patellar tendon. The medial head of the gastrocnemius muscle was rotated to cover the anterior aspect of the prosthesis and close the wound (Fig. 2).

After the procedure, the cast immobilized the affected limb in the knee-extension position. The drainage tubes were maintained until the drain volume was < 50 mL/day. The surgical drainage tube was removed. Two weeks after the operation, the stitches were removed from the wound. After the surgery, the patient underwent 12–15 cycles of chemotherapy. The postoperative knee joint was fixed in a straight position for 8–12 weeks, during which isometric exercises of the affected limb and straight leg raises were performed. Eight to twelve weeks after the operation, the cast was removed to assess knee flexion and extension. Patients began to move around the floor with the help of crutches and gradually walked without them. After the patients began walking, they had to wear supportive braces that limited the lateral movement of the knee to ensure that the knee joints could only flex and extend.

Postoperative follow-up was performed every three months and included evaluation of survival, recurrence, and metastasis and functional assessments, such as complications, limb length, force lines, functional scores, range of motion, and extensor lag. A whole-leg radiographic examination was scheduled for patients at six-month intervals. The femoral length was defined as the distance between the center of the femoral head and the midpoint of the knee. The tibial length was defined as the distance between the midpoint of the knee and the midpoint of the ankle. The lower limb length was defined as the distance between the center of the femoral head and the midpoint of the ankle. The reasons for failure were recorded using the Henderson classification, which categorizes prosthesis failure into five categories. Functional outcomes were measured using the Musculoskeletal Tumor Society (MSTS) scoring system(Fig. 3).

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS software package(version 22.0; SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Measurements were described using mean, range, and median values, and t-tests were used for the comparison of measurements. The Kaplan–Meier curve was used to calculate the survival rate of the patients. Prosthesis survival rates were calculated using a competing risk regression model, implemented with the cmprsk package in R. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

All 49 patients completed the surgery, and the tibial tumor length was 118 ± 29 mm. The mean resection length was 153 ± 27 mm. Negative surgical margins were noted postoperatively.

The follow-up period lasted between 11 and 159 months, with a median of 84 (62, 129) months. Ten patients died during the follow-up period. Thirty-eight patients survived without tumors, and one patient survived with tumors. The overall 5-year survival rate was 83.2% (Fig. 4). Ten patients died owing to pulmonary or multiple metastases. Six patients underwent second-stage prosthetic revision, and three patients expressed interest in undergoing second-stage prosthetic revision (Fig. 5).

Prosthesis complications

One patient experienced dislocation at 90° flexion of the knee joint (Type 1 A failure). Three cases of Type 1B failure occurred, all of which exhibited postoperative necrosis of incision margins. Two patients recovered after undergoing local debridement and receiving third-generation cephalosporin antibiotic therapy for 2 weeks. One patient healed after cleaning and transfer of flap coverage. This was followed by recurrent infections, with amputation due to infection 41 months postoperatively.

Type 2 failures were not observed during the follow-up period. One case of Type 3 A failure was observed. The tibial prosthesis stem was fractured with a concomitant 56 mm lower limb-length discrepancy at 147 months after the initial surgery. The fractured portion of the tibial prosthesis was lengthened using an Ilizarov apparatus(Fig. 6).

There were two cases of Type 4 failure (infection). One case of deep infection occurred 19 months postoperatively and was treated with amputation. In the other case, a deep infection developed 73 months postoperatively, and the prosthesis was removed, implanted with a bone cement spacer, and lengthened with an Ilizarov apparatus. The prosthesis was replaced with a regular prosthesis 88 months after the first operation, with a final follow-up of 94 months.

Six cases of Type 5 failure occurred in 49 patients (12.2%; four recurrences and two ipsilateral limb metastases). There were three cases of soft tissue recurrence: two survived with no tumors after amputation, and one underwent partial resection of the recurrent lesion and chemotherapy, showed recurrence, and refused to continue treatment. The patient survived with the tumor, and the follow-up period lasted up to 65 months. The other three patients had combined pulmonary metastases: one had ipsilateral femoral metastasis and underwent amputation, one had soft tissue recurrence and underwent amputation, and one had ipsilateral femoral metastasis but did not undergo surgery again.

Prosthesis survival

Four patients underwent amputations due to tumor recurrence and transferring, two patients underwent amputations due to infection, one prosthesis was removed after infection in one patient, and one prosthesis was removed due to Type 3 failure. Thirty-nine patients survived at the end of the follow-up period and 34 prostheses survived (87.2%). The overall prosthesis survival rate was 87.4% at 5 years (Fig. 7).

Limb length

During the follow-up period, 38 patients grew from children to adults (two had amputations and eight refused whole-leg radiographs; therefore, their limb lengths were unknown). The limb lengths of the remaining 28 patients were measured. The mean increases in the length of healthy and diseased femurs were 53 ± 33 mm and 44 ± 33 mm(t = 4.602, p < 0.001), respectively. The mean lengths of the healthy and diseased lower limbs were 788 ± 60 mm and 755 ± 56 mm(t = 11.578, p < 0.001), respectively. The mean limb length discrepancy was 33 ± 15 mm with a median of 33 mm (21, 47). The femur was responsible for a discrepancy of 8.5 ± 9.9 mm, while the tibia was responsible for a discrepancy of 24.8 ± 15.5 mm. Seven, 12, and nine patients had length discrepancies ≤ 20 mm, 20–40 mm, and > 40 mm, respectively.

Five patients underwent a planned second-stage surgery with replacement of the distal femoral prosthesis. In one case, the tibial prosthesis was removed because of infection. A bone cement spacer was implanted, along with temporary fixation and lengthening of the tibial defect site with an Ilizarov apparatus, followed by replacement with a modular tumor endoprosthesis. The two lower limbs had equal lengths after revision. These five patients had a limb discrepancy of 19–40 mm and a mean of 29.2 ± 8.9 mm before second-stage surgery and a lower limb discrepancy of 0–32 mm and a mean of 16.4 ± 12.3 mm after second-stage surgery. These five patients had mean MSTS scores of 24 ± 2 and 28 ± 1 before and after second-stage surgery, respectively (Table 1). One patient had a fractured prosthetic stem, a lower-limb discrepancy of 56 mm and is currently undergoing lengthening using the Ilizarov apparatus. The remaining patients with a limb discrepancy > 20 mm maintained a normal gait using shoe lifts.

Function

Apart from the six patients with amputations, five patients used supportive braces that limited the lateral movement of the knee. The other patients removed their braces 3–6 months after wearing them and walked independently. The patients exhibited maximal knee flexion values between 30° and 135°, with a mean of 82°±24°. The femoral tibial angle was greater on the affected side than on the healthy side(t = 3.237, p = 0.003), with a mean difference of 4.5° ± 3.6°. One patient experienced dislocation at 90° knee joint flexion. However, he was able to walk with the aid of supportive braces, without the need for crutches. The mean MSTS score for all patients was 25 ± 3.

Discussion

Oncological results

Understanding the biology of osteosarcoma, the introduction of new effective chemotherapeutic agents, the development of new surgical techniques, improved diagnostic methods, and improved reconstructive surgical procedures have all contributed significantly to the success of limb-salvage surgery. Currently, limb-salvage surgery, rather than amputation, is the first-line treatment for osteosarcoma of the limb [2, 3, 5, 7]. After limb-salvage surgery for malignant tumors of the limb, the 5-year survival rate can reach 80% [8]. In this study, we applied four courses of neoadjuvant chemotherapy and performed proximal tibial osteosarcoma resection, and hemiprosthetic reconstruction. Approximately 12–15 cycles of adjuvant chemotherapy were administered postoperatively. The 5-year survival rate of the patients was 83%, with only four(8.2%) cases of recurrence, and the limb preservation rate of the surviving patients was 93.8%, which is a good oncological outcome for this group of patients.

Length problem

After proximal tibial osteosarcoma resection, limb reconstruction is challenging in pediatric patients. In adolescent patients, the distal femur grows at 1.3 cm/year, and the proximal femur grows at 0.9 cm/year [9, 10]. The surgical removal of the proximal tibial epiphyseal plate and epiphysis destroys the ability of the proximal tibia to grow. The installation of a distal femoral prosthesis destroys the distal femoral epiphyseal plate and epiphysis, which affects the growth of the distal femur. Both factors result in a long-term limb-length discrepancy, which is an essential factor affecting the long-term function of pediatric patients. Several authors have attempted to solve this problem using different methods, and use of expandable prostheses has often been reported [11, 12]. Lex et al. [12] reviewed 396 cases of periprosthetic knee expandable prostheses, with a mean lengthening of 38.1 mm (range: 6–88 mm) in all patients and persistent clinically significant (> 20 mm) limb-length discrepancy in 19.2% of patients at the final follow-up. Common complications of expandable prostheses include infection, loosening, and damage to prosthetic components, with an overall revision rate of 46.2%. For proximal tibial expandable prostheses, the expandable part of the expandable prostheses is located at the tibia and shortening of the femur and discrepancy in the knee joint line due to destruction of the distal growth of the femur also affect the patient’s gait.

We designed a care plan with staged surgical procedures to decrease limb-length discrepancy in pediatric patients. Stage one resection of the proximal tibial tumor was accompanied by proximal tibial hemiarticular prosthesis replacement. This procedure preserves the distal femoral growth plate, reduces limb length inequality, and maintains the joint plateau level. It maintains limb function until the stage-two procedure. Stage two of surgery is performed when the patient reaches adulthood. If the limb-length discrepancy is < 4 cm, the joint is directly revised into a regular joint to resolve knee instability. If the limb length discrepancy is > 4 cm, distal femoral limb lengthening can be performed using the Ilizarov apparatus to equalize the lengths of both lower limbs [13], and the joint can be revised to a regular prosthesis. At a median follow-up of 84 months after surgery, the length of the femur on the affected side was reduced by an average of only 8.5 mm compared with that on the healthy side, and the limb on the affected side was reduced by only 33 mm compared with that on the healthy side. This shows that this method can significantly improve the disruption of the growth capacity of the distal femur and reduce the degree of limb length discrepancy.

Stability

The stability of a normal knee depends on various physiological structures. First, the meniscus, located between the femur and tibia, helps distribute body weight, reduces impact and friction, and provides stability and cushioning. Second, the knee has four major ligaments that connect the femur to the tibia, offering stability and limiting excessive joint movement. These ligaments include the anterior cruciate ligament and posterior cruciate ligament, which mainly maintain the anterior and posterior stability of the knee joint, and the medial collateral ligament and lateral collateral ligament, which provide lateral stability. Third, the joint capsule around the knee is a firm layer of tissue that covers the surface of the joint and fixes and protects soft tissues within the joint. The shapes of the femoral condyles and tibial plateau complement each other to form the articulating surfaces of the knee, which fit well when the knee is flexed and straightened. This structural fit also contributes to the joint stability.

In patients with osteosarcoma of the proximal tibia, surgical removal of the tumor simultaneously removes the meniscus, resects all the joint capsule and ligaments, and destroys all the stabilizing structures of the knee, leading to knee instability and dyskinesia. Proximal tibial hemiarthroplasty can reduce limb length discrepancy in patients. However, no prosthesis manufacturers have produced prostheses specifically designed for this procedure to restore knee stability. The most common surgical approach is to use an existing proximal tibial rotating-hinge knee prosthesis with only a proximal tibial prosthesis and no distal femoral prosthesis. With such a prosthesis, the tibial side inlay has a shallow concave surface, which cannot stably support the femoral condyles. The prosthesis has no metal-mechanical connection on the femoral side, resulting in poor knee stability and severely compromised function [14]. The most common problems are anterior and posterior dislocations of the knee, valgus deformity, and walking pain.

Lozano-Calderón reported the cases of three patients with successful reconstruction using multiple nonabsorbable sutures around the entire knee capsule to fix and stabilize the prosthesis to the femoral side and additional multiple nonabsorbable sutures for the medial and lateral collateral ligaments [15].

In the 13 cases of proximal tibial hemiarthroplasty reported by Chung et al. [6], the authors wrapped the Trevira tube (Implantcast, Buxtehude, Germany) around the hemiarticular joint and then sutured it to the femoral side joint capsule for reconstruction, with three cases experiencing anterior dislocations with an MSTS score of 24.4. Patients with tibial hemiarthroplasties had a reduced range of motion (mean, 95°; range, 60–130°) and extensor lag (mean, 20°; range, 10–30°).

Zan et al. reconstructed the cruciate ligament and medial and lateral collateral ligaments with a Ligament Advanced Reinforcement System (LARS) in the distal femoral hemiarticular joint [16]. However, this method of reconstructing the cruciate ligament requires tunneling through the epiphyseal area, which may affect epiphyseal growth.

The medial and lateral collateral ligaments, the joint capsule, and the cruciate ligaments were simultaneously reconstructed in this study. Intraoperatively, we reconstructed the medial and lateral collateral ligaments and the joint capsule using artificial patches. The patch material was a polypropylene mesh (Bard Mesh) that facilitated the growth of fibrous connective tissue, thus forming a soft tissue patch. Ichikawa and Liu reported that mesh reconstruction of a knee extension device after tumor resection resulted in a sufficiently strong mechanical structure [17, 18]. In the patient cohort, the degree of increased valgus angle was significantly reduced after reconstruction with artificial patches, and knee stability increased with the use of patches. Vavken and Mahar’s study showed that reconstruction of the cruciate ligament with ETHIBOND nonabsorbable sutures provides adequate biomechanical strength [19, 20]. This approach was used in this study. The cruciate ligament was reconstructed using multiple strands of the No. 5 ETHIBOND sutures.

After two methods of reconstructing the knee joint for stability, the patients in this study were followed up for more than 5 years, and only one patient (2.5%) developed anterior dislocation at 90° of knee flexion; 88% (38/43) of the patients discontinued using the braces on their own during the follow-up period. The mean MSTS score of patients who did not use braces was 25 ± 3. This suggests that the above reconstruction also ensures knee stability.

Limitations

This study has a few limitations. First, owing to the small number of patients, only a single reconstruction method for bone defects could be studied without a control group treated with expandable prostheses. Second, the initial design of this study was a staged procedure; however, of the 38 adult patients included in the study, only six underwent a second-stage prosthesis revision followed by replacement mainly because most of the patients and their families refused second-stage surgery. The reasons for refusal included the following: (1) the ability to grow the distal femur was preserved during the surgery, the lower limb length gap was not large, and the limb-length discrepancy could be improved by shoe lifts; (2) the hemiarthroplasty functioned well enough for long-term use; and (3) unwillingness to take the risk of a second-stage surgery. This suggests that proximal tibial hemiarthroplasty in pediatric patients can be used long-term in pediatric patients if it provides adequate stability.

We will continue to follow up whit this group of patients to clarify the functional impact and complications of performing a second-stage revision after hemiarthroplasty in adult patients.

Conclusions

Malignant tumors of the proximal tibia in children present significant challenges during reconstruction, and proximal tibial hemiarthroplasty is an excellent treatment option. Normal distal femoral epiphyses can be preserved to maximize limb length and reduce the degree of limb length discrepancies in adulthood. Hemiarthroplasty simultaneously rebuilds the cruciate ligament, lateral collateral ligaments, and the joint capsule. It can also provide good knee stability. If joint instability or limb inequality occurs, the function can be further improved with second-stage limb-lengthening surgery or total joint replacement.
