Pediatric fracture classification in plain radiographs using EfficientNetV2 with proximal policy optimization fine-tuning
Authors: Malek Barhoush, Ruba Khasawneh, Salem Alhatamleh, Rabia Emhamed Al Mamlook, Donya M. Mistareehi, Hala Nawashi, Amer Hussein Obeidat, Mahmoud A. Obeidat, Ali Saeed Almuflih, Ameera Jaradat, Amal Alishwait, Mohammad Amin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pediatric fracture detection in plain radiographs presents distinct clinical challenges due to the presence of growth plates, incomplete ossification, and subtle cortical irregularities that can closely mimic normal developmental bone appearances particularly in young children. These features contribute to documented inter-reader variability and missed fracture rates in busy emergency settings, underscoring the need for reliable automated decision support. This paper presents a binary classification framework for pediatric radiograph analysis, combining EfficientNetV2-Small as a visual feature extraction backbone with a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) based actor–critic refinement module trained using a two-stage optimization framework. In the first stage, the model undergoes supervised warm-up using cross-entropy loss to establish a stable fracture-versus-normal classification boundary. In the second stage, PPO fine-tuning refines the diagnostic policy through a confidence-aware asymmetric reward function that imposes stronger penalties on high-confidence misclassifications directly targeting the clinical risk of overconfident missed fractures. The framework is developed and evaluated on the KAUH Pediatric Fracture X-ray Dataset, comprising 1221 real-world clinical radiographs collected from King Abdullah University Hospital, Jordan, spanning multiple anatomical sites of the appendicular skeleton and annotated by a certified pediatric radiologist. Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization is applied as a preprocessing step to enhance local bone contrast and improve the visibility of subtle fracture-related features. The proposed model achieves a test accuracy of 91.86%, an AUC of 95.90%, and a sensitivity of 98.38%, achieving higher performance than the evaluated CNN baselines under the reported experimental setting MobileNetV2, ResNet50, Xception, DenseNet121, and base EfficientNetV2-Small. The current framework should be regarded as a research prototype, and prospective multi-center validation is necessary before clinical deployment can be considered.

Introduction

Pediatric fractures represent one of the most frequent causes of emergency department visits, constituting about 10–25% of all pediatric injuries1. The types and patterns of fractures seen in children vary depending on both the severity of trauma and the age of the patient. In addition to accidental trauma, fractures are also common in child abuse, with reported prevalence reaching approximately 34%, of which around 18% present with multiple fractures2. In some cases, fractures may be occult on the initial radiographs with splinting and follow-up imaging after 10–14 days being a common practice to confirm the diagnosis. In some institutions, imaging of the contralateral limb is occasionally performed for comparison, which exposes children to additional radiation and inconvenience. Moreover, in commonly encountered pediatric fractures such as elbow fractures, clinicians frequently rely on indirect radiographic signs, such as the posterior fat pad sign, to suggest the presence of an occult fracture3,4.

Radiographic interpretation of pediatric fractures can therefore be challenging. The presence of growth plates, secondary ossification centers, and age-dependent variations in bone maturity can make fracture detection difficult particularly in busy and underserved emergency settings. Previous studies have reported notable discrepancies between initial and specialist readings of pediatric fracture radiographs. For example, Eakins et al. reported a 30% discrepancy rate in fracture detection between emergency physicians and radiology specialists5, while Taves et al. found missed fracture rates of 26.2% in the upper extremity and 17.5% in the lower extremity compared to the final interpretation6. Similarly, Al-Sani et al. documented an 7.8% error rate in emergency department interpretations of pediatric fractures7.

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a promising tool in medical imaging. Currently, most of the available AI-assisted tools in pediatric imaging are for bone age assessment, and such tools have been commercially available for more than a decade now8. Research is also ongoing to develop tools for assessment of developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) and skeletal dysplasias. However, despite rapid advancements in AI technologies, dedicated AI tools specifically designed for detecting pediatric fractures on radiographs remain limited. Pauling et al. reported that among 21 available AI tools for fracture detection, only three were intended for use in both adults and children9. Limited studies have evaluated their performance in detecting pediatric fractures with reported sensitivities in the range from 91.3 to 95.3%, while specificities between 90 and 91.2%10,11. Other reports have investigated the performance of AI tools among different expertise levels, demonstrating that AI tools provide greater diagnostic support for less experienced readers, such as residents or emergency physicians, compared with expert radiologists12–14. When directly compared with human readers, AI algorithms have demonstrated diagnostic performance comparable to radiologists in some studies15,16, while AI assistance has been shown to improve the diagnostic accuracy of radiologists in another study17.

A systematic review by Shelmerdine et al.18 showed that most published studies evaluating AI for acute pediatric appendicular fracture detection are based on radiographic interpretation, with upper limb fractures, particularly elbow fractures, being the most commonly studied region15,17,19. Additionally, nearly all studies utilized training, validation, and testing datasets derived from a single institution, and only a small number included external validation cohorts. This makes current evidence remains insufficient to establish performance consistently exceeding expert radiologist interpretation across diverse clinical settings. In very few studies that investigated AI-based fracture detection performance among different sites of appendicular skeleton in pediatric patients, Franco et al. reported high levels of diagnosis performance across different fracture sites20 and in other study Altmann-Schneider et al. showed higher rates of forearm fractures detection in comparison to lower limb fractures by using AI-based method21. There are still several barriers in the way of successful implementation of AI-assisted diagnosis of fractures in children, especially considering different sites of appendicular skeleton.

This study employs a real-world dataset of X-rays of children collected during routine medical practice at King Abdullah University Hospital, Jordan and related to different sites of the appendicular skeleton. This kind of dataset can provide the possibility to assess automatic X-ray classification algorithms based on distinguishing between children fractures and normal X-rays.

Literature review

The study has used standard convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures together with transfer learning techniques to build deep learning (DL) systems that detect bone fractures. The methods have remained important to the field since they introduce effective techniques for learning data representations. Recent studies have shown effective results with standard CNN-based frameworks according to numerous recent studies.

A study by Haque et al.22 showed that the modified VGG-19 model achieves 99.78% classification accuracy and an AUC of 1.00. In another study23, the DensenNet-201 model was trained on X-ray images of 1370 pediatric patients with elbow fractures from multiple healthcare centers. It achieved an accuracy of 94.1% and an AUC of 98.7%. Another study24 found a novel approach to bone fracture diagnosis using MobileNetV2; the model achieves a precision, recall, and F1-score of 95% for both fractured and non-fractured classes.

A framework was proposed in this study25, by training three advanced CNNs on a dataset of 2474 images of radial bone fractures in children. CNN predictions reached an AUC of up to 98%. In another study26, a new method for detecting fractures in arm bone X-rays was proposed, using an algorithm that combines YOLACT + + for image segmentation and finite adaptive contrast enhancement, achieving a high accuracy rate of 81.91%. According to Vasker et al.27, a new method for identifying and classifying femur fractures using a deep neural network (DNN) model. The accuracy rate reached 92.44%.

In another study28, a modified transfer learning network, based on the Xception-V3 architecture with additional convolutional inference layers, was trained to detect distal tibial fractures in children and achieve 95.9% accuracy. Another study29 found that using the EfficientNet-B4 network with the attention mechanism gives high results in detecting bone fractures in radiographs, achieving a sensitivity of 96.2%, a specificity of 94.8%, and an AUC of 98.3%. A framework based on a pre-training strategy using a self-learning model with the Vision Transformer core architecture30 was proposed for the diagnosis of forearm fractures. If the methodology was implemented, it achieved the highest classification performance with a True Positive Ratio (TPR) of 97% and a True Negative Ratio (TNR) of 74%.

According to Chaclas et al.31, they tuned a set of convolutional neural networks (EfficientNet) to a dataset that included 2103 X-ray images. The best model was EfficientNet-B2, which achieved an accuracy of 84% and a test accuracy of 84% in detecting physeal fractures, single anatomical regions, or standard CNN architectures without adaptive training mechanisms. A substantial proportion of prior studies rely on adult datasets. To the best of our knowledge, no published study has evaluated the effectiveness of PPO-based fine-tuning for mitigating uncertain or unsafe predictions during medical image classification. This study aims to address this research gap by proposing a novel training paradigm that leverages a combination of EfficientNetV2-Small and an actor-critic PPO agent, which is trained and validated on the real-world, multi-site pediatric radiograph dataset known as the KAUH-PFX dataset. This data includes several parts of the anatomy of the upper and lower limbs. The reward functionality uses confidence to narrowly treat the risk of fracture elusions.

Beyond fracture detection, recent advances in deep learning for medical image analysis have introduced several methodological directions that are broadly relevant to the field. DA-TransUNet integrates spatial and channel dual attention mechanisms within a Transformer U-Net architecture to improve medical image segmentation, demonstrating the effectiveness of combining attention-based feature selection with encoder-decoder designs32. Gradient-guided boundary-aware selective scanning with multi-scale context aggregation has been proposed for plant lesion segmentation, introducing feature extraction strategies that are transferable to other fine-grained visual recognition tasks in medical imaging33. FKD-Med addresses privacy and communication constraints in medical image segmentation through federated learning combined with knowledge distillation-based model lightweighting, highlighting the importance of deployment-aware model design in clinical settings34. A mutual inclusion mechanism for precise boundary segmentation in medical images further advances the accurate delineation of subtle structural boundaries a challenge closely related to the detection of cortical discontinuities in pediatric fracture radiographs35. Collectively, these works reflect the growing diversity of architectural and training innovations being applied to medical imaging tasks, and provide broader methodological context for the classification framework proposed in this study.

Recent work has also highlighted important practical and methodological challenges in deploying deep learning systems for medical image analysis that are directly relevant to the limitations of the current study. A comprehensive study on the practical challenges of applying YOLO models to medical imaging demonstrated that performance gaps between controlled research settings and real-world clinical deployment are common, driven by factors including data quality variability, annotation inconsistency, and domain shift across institutions36 challenges that are consistent with the sensitivity drop observed in our external FracAtlas evaluation. Additionally, MergeSlide introduced a continual model merging framework with task-to-class prompt-aligned inference for lifelong learning on whole slide images, demonstrating that model generalizability across evolving clinical data distributions can be improved through principled merging strategies37. This direction is particularly relevant to the single-center limitation of the KAUH-PFX framework, where exposure to a single institutional data distribution constrains cross-domain generalizability. Together, these works underscore that robust clinical deployment of AI-assisted diagnostic systems requires not only strong in-distribution performance but also principled strategies for domain adaptation and continual learning both of which are identified as priorities for future development of the proposed framework.

Dataset overview

KAUH pediatric fracture X-ray dataset (KAUH-PFX)

The King Abdullah University Hospital Pediatric Fracture X-ray Dataset (KAUH-PFX) is a curated set of clinical x-ray images of actual cases collected from children diagnosed and treated in King Abdullah University Hospital, which is a tertiary care medical facility based in Northern Jordan. The purpose of the creation of KAUH-PFX is to facilitate studies related to fracture detection and classification using machine learning models. All the x-rays contained in the KAUH-PFX are authentic depictions of clinical cases that have been collected and diagnosed by a pediatric radiologist from the King Abdullah University Hospital. The present retrospective study was carried out following the appropriate guidelines and regulations and obtained ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Jordan University of Science and Technology (IRB number: Sep2025/186-29). Informed consent from the participants was waived by the IRB because the research was retrospective in nature and only utilized anonymous patient information. All the methodologies employed in this study followed appropriate ethical guidelines and regulations including the principles laid out in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Pediatric plain radiographs were retrospectively collected from clinical cases spanning ten years, from September 1, 2015 to January 8, 2026, representing the full archive of eligible pediatric imaging studies available at King Abdullah University Hospital. The extraction and curation of images from the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) Fujifilm Medical Systems platform (version 7.1, Lexington, MA, USA) was carried out between October 1, 2025 and February 1, 2026, during which all images were reviewed, anonymized, and assessed for inclusion based on diagnostic quality and completeness criteria.

Dataset composition and gender distribution

The KAUH-PFX dataset includes 1221 images of children in a near-balanced class distribution: 612 fracture (50.1) and 609 normal (49.9) images. A male predominance was observed in both classes in both groups. Male cases in the fracture group were 68.1% (417 out of 612), and female cases were 31.9% (195 out of 612). There were 61.1% of males (372 out of 609) and 38.9% (237 out of 609) females in the normal group. This trend is in line with known epidemiological trends that have higher prevalence rates of traumatic injury among male children38. Anatomical coverage and data collection

Radiographs at KAUH-PFX dataset are all through the appendicular skeleton including upper limbs and lower limbs. Images of upper limbs involve thumb, 5th finger, index finger, hand, wrist, forearm (radius/ulna), elbow, humerus and shoulder, and clavicle. Images of lower limbs include the foot, ankle, fibula/tibia, knee, femur, hip, and pelvis. This wide anatomical spread is such that the dataset represents the large dissimilarity of skeletal locations that are usually impacted by traumatic injury in the pediatric group, appropriate in constructing the generalizable fracture extraction models over various anatomical areas. To have a balanced binary data, a corresponding normal group, i.e. patients who had no radiographic signs of fracture at that period of time, was included. Images were eliminated when of poor diagnostic quality owing to technical difficulties (e.g. patient movement, improper positioning etc.) or when external objects (e.g. clothing or metallic accessories that overlap the region of interest) were present and severely affected image quality. Data preprocessing

The dataset used in this study consists of 1221 pediatric X-ray images collected from King Abdullah University Hospital, Jordan. The images were marked down to binary classification of two diagnostic categories: fracture and normal. The class distribution shows almost equal distribution between two groups which includes 612 fracture images and 609 normal images. The dataset was divided into three subsets which included training, validation and test subsets to establish reliable model development and unbiased evaluation. The split results in 976 training images, 122 validation images and 123 test images.

The preprocessing pipeline was developed to maintain delicate bone characteristics which are essential for preserving diagnostically relevant fracture-related features. The first step of image preprocessing begins with spatial standardization, which resizes all radiographs to a specific input size. The preservation of high image resolution needs to be maintained because aggressive downsampling will cause the loss of cortical discontinuities, faint fracture lines and metaphyseal abnormalities. The image undergoes contrast enhancement through Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) after spatial standardization has been completed39. CLAHE differs from global histogram equalization because it splits the image into small tiles which it equalizes for intensity distribution before it redistributes excessive histogram peaks. The weak local contrast changes in pediatric radiographs make CLAHE is well suited for enhancing local contrast in pediatric radiographs for detecting diagnostically important abnormalities which show up as weak local contrast changes. The system uses CLAHE to enhance the detection of tiny fracture-related details while it also protects against excessive noise amplification40. EfficientNetV2-small

The proposed framework uses EfficientNetV2-Small as its main visual foundation which extracts features from pediatric radiographs. The backbone was chosen because it enables detection of small local defects which are essential for identifying pediatric fractures while maintaining low resource requirements. Fracture detection in pediatric X-ray images requires extraction of faint cortical interruptions, mild metaphyseal irregularities and weak structural distortions which become visible through appropriate discrimination methods43. The backbone should produce outstanding feature extraction abilities which need to maintain low processing demands. EfficientNetV2-Small is well suited to this setting because it offers a favorable balance between representational power, training speed, and parameter efficiency.

The development of EfficientNetV2 introduced an architectural design which optimizes training processes together with enhanced model performance through its specialized training-based design and optimized convolutional components. EfficientNetV2-Small uses a lightweight design which enables it to learn detailed visual features that are essential for pediatric fracture analysis because diagnosticians need to identify hidden and tiny details. The proposed functions well with hospital radiographs because it needs to maintain enough detail to identify fractures while remaining suitable for both training and implementation activities. EfficientNetV2 implements its architectural design through the use of MBConv and Fused-MBConv blocks44. The blocks function to boost feature extraction capabilities while maintaining their ability to represent information45. The initial stages of convolutional networks in pediatric radiographs need to capture both local boundary details and cortical edge information together with fine texture changes. Proximal policy optimization (PPO)

Although fracture labels are fully available, the PPO stage is not intended to replace supervised learning but to refine the diagnostic decision policy after supervised convergence. Unlike conventional loss-based approaches, the proposed formulation incorporates confidence-aware rewards that explicitly account for both prediction correctness and decision confidence, enabling risk-sensitive policy refinement.

After supervised initialization, the model is refined using PPO within an actor–critic framework. In this stage, pediatric fracture classification is reformulated as a policy-based decision problem. While several supervised alternatives exist for addressing class imbalance and overconfidence including focal loss, cost-sensitive learning, label smoothing, uncertainty-aware calibration, and confidence penalty methods none of these approaches offer a principled mechanism to dynamically penalize high-confidence misclassifications at the decision level. Focal loss re-weights training examples based on difficulty but does not distinguish between confident and uncertain wrong predictions. Cost-sensitive learning applies static class penalties that do not adapt to the model’s confidence at inference time. Label smoothing reduces overconfidence globally rather than selectively targeting dangerous high-certainty errors. Confidence penalty methods and uncertainty-aware calibration share conceptual overlap with our approach; however, they remain loss-level interventions and do not reformulate the classification as a sequential decision problem with explicit safety-aware rewards. In contrast, the PPO-based reward function defined in Eq. (11) directly encodes clinical risk by assigning asymmetric penalties: correct predictions are rewarded, while incorrect predictions particularly those made with high confidence, incur stronger negative returns. This formulation operationalizes the clinical principle that high-confidence false-negative predictions may carry greater clinical risk than uncertain predictions, and provides an alternative optimization mechanism distinct from conventional loss-based approaches.

Discussion

Generalization across different datasets

The proposed model needs testing to determine its ability to scale and handle all necessary components. The model was tested on the FracAtlas dataset53. The model demonstrated strong performance on the FracAtlas data according to Table 7 which showed an overall precision rating of 86.56%. The system achieved excellent performance in detecting healthy bones, which resulted in 96.44% specificity and 93.12% precision for non-fractured cases. The model played it very safe because the PPO agent used a highly demanding reward system. The actual fracture sensitivity dropped to 66.67% because the system applied excessive penalties for showing uncertainty. The agent requires vast visual evidence from the EfficientNet backbone before it can identify fractures on new imaging data which it has not previously encountered. While the proposed model demonstrated strong performance on the KAUH-PFX test set, its fracture sensitivity dropped substantially to 66.67% on the FracAtlas dataset. This result reveals a meaningful domain gap between the two datasets, likely attributable to differences in imaging protocols, anatomical coverage, and patient demographics. The conservative reward structure of the PPO agent which penalizes overconfident predictions appears to increase caution on out-of-distribution data, resulting in elevated false negatives when applied to imaging patterns not well-represented in the training distribution. This finding highlights a critical limitation: strong in-distribution performance does not guarantee cross-institutional robustness, and the current model should not be considered ready for deployment in clinical settings outside of the conditions represented by KAUH-PFX.

Mechanism for integrating the model into the hospital’s medical system

To implement the proposed model in a healthcare setting, it must first be integrated with the electronic medical record (EMR) or medical image management system (PACS). When a patient’s X-Ray image is entered, the system automatically analyzes the image using the trained model, which classifies the case as “Normal” or “Fracture”. The result is immediately presented to the physician along with the image, a confidence score, and potential error rate. For clinical application, the model must be integrated into the clinical workflow to avoid additional burden on physicians and provide automated support for their decisions, especially in complex or difficult-to-interpret visual cases. The system can also be used as a screening tool (second reader) before a final decision is made. To ensure safety and effectiveness, the model must be subject to internal validation by hospital staff, with performance monitored and periodically reviewed to be updated based on local data, in accordance with approved medical and legislative standards.

Limitations of the study

In this study, some limitations need to be taken into account while understanding the results. Firstly, the retrospective nature of the experiment, along with the fact that it was carried out in only one hospital in Jordan, might lead to difficulties in generalizing the results due to the specific features of clinical practice, patient selection, and imaging techniques in one tertiary care center. The performance of the model could differ if it was used in other healthcare facilities with a different range of patients and other imaging technology. Secondly, the images used in the research were obtained over ten years; therefore, different conditions could have been present.

Third, the inclusion of fractures from all appendicular skeletal sites across a broad pediatric age range introduced considerable heterogeneity in both image quality and fracture morphology. This diversity, although an accurate reflection of clinical reality, might be responsible for differences in the predictive capabilities of the models in various parts of the body and among different age groups. Fourth, the lack of validation is a major drawback. When the proposed model was evaluated on the FracAtlas dataset, fracture sensitivity dropped to 66.67%. This drop is attributable to three compounding factors: (1) domain shift arising from differences in imaging equipment, acquisition protocols, and patient demographics; (2) the conservative behavior of the PPO reward structure on out-of-distribution data, where the asymmetric penalty increases false negatives when visual evidence does not match training distribution patterns; and (3) the limited visual diversity of a single-center training dataset of 1221 images, which constrains the model’s ability to learn domain-invariant fracture representations. Domain adaptation strategies, larger and more diverse training datasets, and prospective multi-center validation are necessary steps before this framework can be considered for real-world clinical triage applications. Fifth, the binary classification framework employed in this study does not capture fracture subtypes, severity grades, or anatomical localization, which are clinically relevant factors that would need to be addressed before the model could be considered for integration into clinical decision-support workflows. Sixth, while the EfficientNetV2-Small WarmUp ablation variant was added to isolate the contribution of the PPO stage, direct ablation experiments comparing PPO against simpler confidence-aware supervised alternatives such as focal loss, cost-sensitive weighting, label smoothing, and confidence penalty regularization were not conducted in this study. The empirical improvements observed (accuracy: 78.05% → 91.86%; sensitivity: 82.26% → 98.38%) are consistent with the theoretical advantages of policy-based confidence-aware training; however, future work should include controlled comparisons against these alternatives to more rigorously isolate the specific contribution of the reinforcement learning stage.

Conclusion and future works

The presented study proposes an exploratory framework for pediatric bone fracture classification based on the use of EfficientNetV2-Small as a feature extractor network in conjunction with PPO-based actor-critic head training using two-stage supervised warm-up and confidence-aware policy refinement. On the KAUH-PFX test set, our model was able to achieve 91.86% accuracy and 98.38% sensitivity compared to five standard CNN baselines, proving that confidence-aware asymmetric reward shaping is capable of significantly improving sensitivity on a safety–critical binary classification problem. Nevertheless, there are several important limitations to consider when evaluating the results obtained. First, although performance improvements achieved by the proposed model compared to the base EfficientNetV2-Small backbone are in line with the expected benefits from PPO-based confidence shaping, it cannot be conclusively stated that PPO is indispensable as other types of confidence-aware supervised losses might prove just as effective and require further investigation in the future. Moreover, the significant decline in fracture sensitivity on FracAtlas dataset to 66.67% suggests that our model still requires significant improvement in terms of generalizability to different imaging domains.

This paper’s most important contribution lies in the proof-of-concept showing that confidence refinement based on a policy is a compelling and sound approach to incorporating safety awareness into medical image classification when there are asymmetric risks associated with overconfidence and classification errors. Bringing this to fruition will need prospective multi-center evaluation, reader studies, calibration studies, methods of domain adaptation, and subgroup analyses among others that have been outlined as future steps.
