Injectable pH-responsive carboxymethyl cellulose hydrogel for sustained delivery of IL-22 in the treatment of alcoholic liver disease
Authors: Huan Zhou, Dashuai Yang, Rongrong Gu, Yang Song, Zeyuan Wei, Cheng Qian, Nengcong Yang, Jian Liu, Sheng Ye, Tao Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlcoholic liver disease (ALD) is a major chronic liver disorder that poses a significant global health burden due to its severe health risks and limited therapeutic options. Although recombinant interleukin-22 (IL-22) is an available therapeutic agent with potent anti-inflammatory and anti-lipid accumulation effects, its application in ALD treatment has been limited by its short half-life. In this study, we developed a carboxymethyl cellulose hydrogel (CMC)-encapsulated system to prolong the half-life of recombinant IL-22, aiming to achieve effective and sustained inhibition of ALD. Recombinant IL-22 was incorporated into CMC (IL-22@CMC) through hydrogen bonding interactions. This system exhibited pH-responsive drug delivery properties and good biocompatibility. IL-22@CMC reduced alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels from 250 IU/L to 100 IU/L and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels from 700 IU/L to 200 IU/L. These levels were significantly lower than those observed with IL-22 alone (160 IU/L and 400 IU/L, respectively), indicating superior therapeutic efficacy in alcoholic liver injury. RNA sequencing analysis indicated that the enhanced inhibitory effect of IL-22@CMC was achieved through suppression of endoplasmic reticulum stress mediated by the AMP-activated protein kinase/sirtuin 1 signaling pathway in ethanol-induced AML-12 cells. By designing a pH-responsive hydrogel, this study integrates therapeutic agents with a hydrogel carrier via hydrogen bonding, thereby providing an effective strategy for developing drug delivery composites for inflammatory diseases.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04345-x.Keywords: IL-22, Carboxymethyl cellulose, PH-responsive, Drug delivery, Alcoholic liver disease

Introduction

Alcoholic liver disease (ALD) is a chronic liver disorder caused by prolonged and excessive alcohol consumption [1, 2]. In 2022, approximately one-fifth of global liver cancer deaths was associated with ALD [3]. The primary features of ALD include liver inflammation, lipid accumulation, and hepatocyte injury [4, 5]. Current treatments, such as alcohol cessation, nutritional support, and hepatoprotective medications, often fail to prevent disease progression in advanced stages, leaving liver transplantation as the only curative option [6–8]. Despite these therapies, liver transplantation remains the treatment of choice for end-stage ALD [9, 10]. However, this procedure is limited by high costs and organ scarcity. Therefore, there is an urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies that effectively slow or reverse ALD progression.

Interleukin-22 (IL-22), a member of the IL-10 cytokine family, has emerged as a promising therapeutic candidate for ALD because of its ability to inhibit the secretion of inflammatory cytokines and lipid accumulation [9–11]. However, the clinical translation of recombinant IL-22 is hampered by its short in vivo half-life, which necessitates frequent administration and limits therapeutic efficacy [12]. Although various drug delivery systems—such as Fc fusion, PEGylation, and nanoparticle encapsulation—have been explored for sustained protein release, they often encounter challenges, including protein denaturation, premature burst release, and inadequate tissue retention [13, 14]. In contrast, injectable hydrogels offer distinct advantages for protein delivery. Their higher water content helps maintain protein conformation, their tunable mesh size enables controlled release, and their excellent biocompatibility minimizes adverse reactions [15–17]. Moreover, pH-responsive hydrogels can release drugs in a stimulus-triggered manner, which is particularly advantageous in ALD, where the inflammatory liver microenvironment is often slightly acidic [18].

In this study, we developed an injectable, pH-responsive carboxymethyl cellulose hydrogel (CMC) for the sustained intraperitoneal delivery of recombinant IL-22. The intraperitoneal route was selected to facilitate sustained systemic exposure and hepatic accumulation. This hydrogel is formed by the hydrogen bond interaction between CMC and IL-22, thereby preserving bioactivity and enabling pH-dependent release kinetics. We demonstrated that IL-22@CMC significantly prolonged the half-life of IL-22, enhanced its stability, and outperformed free IL-22 in mitigating alcohol-induced liver injury, inflammation, and lipid accumulation in both cellular and murine models. Mechanistically, these protective effects were mediated through suppression of endoplasmic reticulum stress via the AMP-activated protein kinase/sirtuin 1 (AMPK/SIRT1) pathway. These findings not only present IL-22@CMC as a potent therapeutic candidate for ALD but also provide a versatile hydrogel-based platform for the delivery of protein therapeutics in inflammatory liver diseases.

Materials and methods

Materials

Carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC, C8H16O8, CM-52, MW: 240, Macklin, China). (±)-Epichlorohydrin (C3H5ClO, AR, Macklin, abbreviated as ECH, China). NaOH (95%, Macklin, China). Pure water (H2O, 18.25 MΩ·cm). Recombinant IL-22 was purchased from GenScript (Nanjing, China; purity > 98%, as analyzed by HPLC). Male C57BL/6J mice were acquired from Cavins Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Changzhou, China). Cell culture reagents, including F-12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 10 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin, were procured from Gibco (Carlsbad, CA, USA). RIPA lysis buffer and DAPI staining solution were provided by Beyotime (Shanghai, China). TRIzol reagent and SYBR Green were obtained from Cwbio (Suzhou, China). PBS-T washing buffer was acquired from Thermo Fisher (Shanghai, China). Commercial assay kits for determining alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). The IL-22 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit was obtained from R&D Systems (Tianjin, China). All remaining analytical-grade chemicals were sourced from Solarbio (Beijing, China).

Preparation of the IL-22@CMC

Carboxymethyl cellulose and 3% (w/v) NaOH were stirred for 30 min in a 250 mL three-neck round-bottom flask. Epichlorohydrin (ECH), which served as the crosslinker, was added dropwise and stirred for 2 h to obtain a homogeneous viscous material. The material was heated to 80 °C for 2 h to undergo prepolymerization in a glass Petri dish. NaOH and ECH were removed from the prepolymer using distilled water until the pH approached 7, after which the prepolymer was dried at 50 °C to obtain a dried CMC gel. To synthesize IL-22@CMC, 5, 25, 50, 100, 200, 400, 800, and 1600 ng of IL-22 were separately added to CMC systems at concentrations of 10, 20, and 30 mg/mL, respectively, yielding IL-22@CMC [19].

Rheology test

CMC was positioned between parallel plates with a diameter of 8 mm and a gap of 0.5 mm. During the experiment, excess gel was removed, and the outer edge of the sample was sealed with a thin layer of Vaseline to minimize water evaporation. The storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″), along with the sol–gel transition point, were measured under a strain range of 1%–100% at a frequency of 1 Hz.

Solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy test

The hydrogel was first lyophilized and subsequently ground into a fine powder, which was sieved through a 100-mesh sieve to ensure a particle size smaller than the sieve pore size. The resulting powder was then subjected to 13C solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy analysis.

FT-IR spectroscopy analysis

KBr was mixed with dry gel sample powder to prepare a film, which was scanned between 4000 and 400 cm− 1 to record the Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectrum.

Swelling behavior

To evaluate the swelling properties of IL-22@CMC in different environments, PBS) solutions with different pH values (2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 6.5, 7, 7.4, 8, and 9) were prepared in advance. A 5 mg dry gel sample was immersed in 10 mL of aqueous solution at the designated pH at room temperature and allowed to swell for 24 h. Characterization

Field emission Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi S4800, Japan), transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Tecnai G2 F20, USA) were used to characterize the morphology and structure of hydrogels. Solid-state 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra of hydrogels were recorded using a BRUKER 600 M spectrometer (Germany). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Nicolet iS 10, Thermo Scientific, USA) was used to analyze the characteristic absorption peaks in the infrared spectrum. An ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer (SHIMADZU, UV-2600i, Japan) was used to analyze the absorption of UV-visible light. The mechanical properties of the hydrogels were evaluated using a SUNS Model electronic universal testing machine (SUNS-Model, China). The rheological properties of the hydrogel were measured using the TA Instruments Discovery Hybrid Rheometer (TA Discovery HR-1, USA). Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) analysis was performed using a Waters 2695 separation module (USA) equipped with a 2410 refractive index detector and Empower workstation, and an Ultrahydrogel Linear column (300 mm ×7.8 mm, Waters, USA).

Animals and treatment

Male C57BL/6J mice aged 6–8 weeks and weighing approximately 20–22 g were procured from the Experimental Animal Center at Anhui Medical University. The mice were provided with a standard diet and water ad libitum and were acclimatized to a controlled environment (50 ± 5% relative humidity, 20–25 °C, and a 12/12 dark–light cycle) for 7 days before the commencement of the experiments. The Gao–Binge model process included a liquid diet adaptation phase for 5 days, a model induction period for 10 days, a single episode of EtOH gavage, and specimen collection over 1 day, spanning a total of 16 days [20]. The 40 mice were randomly allocated into five groups (n = 8 per group). Control group: mice in this group received a control liquid diet during the induction period, followed by a single gavage of isocaloric dextrin maltose (5 g/kg) on the last day. EtOH group: mice in this group were provided with an EtOH (5% v/v) liquid diet during the induction period and received a single binge dose of EtOH (20% alcohol, 5 g/kg) via gavage on the last day. CMC group: the modeling procedure was consistent with that of the EtOH group, and CMC was administered via intraperitoneal injection simultaneously. IL-22 group: the modeling procedure was consistent with that of the EtOH group, and IL-22 (1 µg/g) was administered via intraperitoneal injection simultaneously. IL-22@CMC group: the modeling procedure was consistent with that of the EtOH group, and IL-22@CMC (1 µg/g) was administered via intraperitoneal injection simultaneously. Nine hours after the final EtOH gavage, liver tissues and blood were harvested following anesthesia. All experiments were conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines for the use of laboratory animals at Anhui Medical University. The study was approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (LLSC20230876).

Evaluation of CMC degradation in vivo

CMC–Rhodamine B (Rhodamine B concentration: 30 µg/mL) was administered subcutaneously at a dose of 0.1 mL per mouse into the dorsal region. To minimize interference with the fluorescence signal, hair surrounding the injection site was shaved. At designated time points, mice were imaged using a small animal imaging system (In Vivo Xtreme, Bruker). Imaging parameters were as follows: excitation wavelength 520 nm, emission wavelength 650 nm, exposure time 1 s, binning low (2), F-stop 2.0, field of view 25, and object height 1.5 cm [21].

In vitro drug release studies

To assess the ability of the CMC to sustain the release of recombinant IL-22 and to investigate the release kinetics, recombinant IL-22 was integrated into the CMC combinations through noncovalent interactions. Briefly, 200 µL of IL-22@CMC (containing 800 ng IL-22) and 2 mL of F-12 medium were added to a 6-well plate. At each time point (6, 12, 24, 36, 48, and 72 h), 1 mL of medium was collected from each well and replaced with 1 mL of fresh medium. The collected medium was stored at −80 °C for further analysis. IL-22 release at each time point was quantified using an IL-22 ELISA kit. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. A standard curve was generated using reference samples with known IL-22 concentrations, and the IL-22 concentration in the medium was calculated based on this curve [22].

ELISA

AML-12 cells (2 × 105 cells per well in a 6-well plate) were pretreated with the designated treatments for 48 h and then stimulated with EtOH (200 mM). After an additional 24 h, supernatants and cells were collected. The concentrations of interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and IL-22 in the culture medium or cell lysates were quantified using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions (R&D Systems).

Cell culture

The mice hepatocyte cell line AML-12 was maintained at the School of Pharmaceutical Sciences, Anhui Medical University. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum and penicillin–streptomycin (10 U/mL). The cell suspension was adjusted to 1 × 10⁶ cells per well and seeded into six-well culture plates. The ALD cell model was established by exposing AML-12 cells to 200 mM EtOH for 24 h [23].

Cell viability measurement

AML-12 cell viability was assessed using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8). Cells were uniformly seeded in 96-well plates. After 24 h of adhesion, AML-12 cells were treated with IL-22, IL-22@CMC, or CMC for 24–48 h. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for an additional 1 h before measuring absorbance at 450 nm.

Measurement of phase transition behavior of sol-gel

The phase transition behavior of the sol-gel system was evaluated using the tube inversion method, with a temperature increment of 10 °C at each step. The sol-gel transition temperature was recorded when no flow was observed within 30 s after inversion of the vial.

Biochemical analysis

Serum levels of IL-22, ALT, AST, triglycerides (TG), and total cholesterol (TC), as well as hepatic concentrations of TG and TC, were measured using commercially available assay kits.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Group differences were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance with GraphPad Prism version 9.0. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Morphology and gelation properties of IL-22@CMC

In this study, carboxymethyl cellulose was used as the precursor for CMC preparation. As shown in Fig. 1a, the interaction mechanism between CMC and IL-22 is illustrated. The carboxyl and hydroxyl groups of CMC form hydrogen bonds with the amino groups of IL-22, enabling IL-22 to bind to CMC and thereby enhancing the crosslinking degree and stability of IL-22@CMC.

(e) Fourier transform infrared spectrum of CMC and IL-22@CMC. (f) 13C NMR spectra of CMC and IL-22@CMC. Compressive stress-strain curve of IL-22@CMC. (g) Frequency sweep test of the CMC. Scanning electron microscopy images revealed that both CMC and IL-22@CMC hydrogels possessed porous internal structures (1b-c, Fig. S1). Transmission electron microscopy images (Fig. 1d, Fig. S2) further elucidated the microtopography and fine structure of the hydrogels [24]. Elemental mapping of IL-22@CMC demonstrated a uniform distribution of C, O, and N elements, indicating successful preparation of IL-22@CMC and homogeneous incorporation of IL-22 (Fig. S3). Fig. S4 shows the gelation behavior of CMC and IL-22@CMC; CMC appeared clear and transparent after gelation. No obvious difference in appearance was observed between CMC and IL-22@CMC, indicating that IL-22 exerted minimal influence on the structural and physicochemical properties of CMC (Fig. S5). In addition, the UV–Vis absorption spectra of lyophilized CMC and IL-22 were mainly distributed below 400 nm (Fig. S6). FT-IR spectra were obtained for blank CMC and IL-22@CMC (Fig. 1e). The spectrum of pure CMC exhibited characteristic absorption peaks corresponding to hydroxyl (–OH) stretching vibration at approximately 3340 cm− 1, methylene (–CH2) asymmetric stretching vibration at approximately 2920 cm− 1, carbonyl (C = O) stretching vibration at 1590 cm− 1, and carbon–oxygen (C–O) stretching vibration at 1057 cm− 1. After IL-22 incorporation, the –OH groups participated in composite formation, and overlapping infrared signals of –OH (CMC) and –NH (IL-22) resulted in a peak shift from 3340 to 3410 cm− 1 [19]. Meanwhile, the peak at 1130 cm− 1 was attributed to intermolecular hydrogen bonding [25]. Compared with CMC alone, IL-22 loading increased the full width at half maximum and induced slight shifts in characteristic peak wavenumbers, confirming significant intermolecular interactions between CMC and IL-22. FT-IR spectral data indicated that IL-22 was successfully incorporated into CMC.

Solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy was conducted to further assess the chemical structure of CMC. Compared with CMC, the 13C NMR spectra of IL-22@CMC exhibited slightly positive shift near 177 and 103 ppm, corresponding to the –COOH and –OH groups of CMC, indicating the formation of hydrogen bonds (Fig. 1f). In addition, 1H NMR analysis was performed. Due to strong intermolecular proton dipolar coupling, the 1H NMR spectra of both CMC and IL-22@CMC displayed a single asymmetric broad peak. Notably, the peak of IL-22@CMC shifted toward a higher chemical shift compared with that of CMC. This shift may be attributed to proton deshielding induced by hydrogen bond formation between IL-22 and CMC (Fig.S7). This variation further supports the formation of hydrogen bonds between CMC and IL-22. The stress–strain curve reflects the deformation behavior of materials under external forces, including elasticity, plasticity, yielding, and fracture. IL-22@CMC fractured at 90.70% strain with a corresponding stress of 15.21 MPa, whereas blank CMC fractured at 88.24% strain with a fracture stress of 9.45 MPa. The enhanced mechanical strength of IL-22@CMC was attributed to hydrogen bonding between IL-22 and CMC. These interactions introduced additional reversible physical crosslinking points within the polymer network, thereby improving the hydrogel’s resistance to deformation and fracture (Fig.S8) [19, 26].

In addition, the viscoelastic properties of CMC were investigated through rheological experiments. Rheological analysis showed that when CMC was subjected to a strain range of 1.88%–62.63%, G′ remained consistently higher than G″ (Fig. 1g). Results from the dynamic angular frequency sweep experiment demonstrated that G′ remained above G″ across the angular frequency range of 0.1–100 rad/s (Fig. 1h). These findings indicate that the hydrogel retained its original three-dimensional network structure over a broad range of strains and frequencies. This property facilitates maintenance of structural integrity during syringe extrusion and prevents rapid dispersion in normal tissues after injection, thereby reducing premature drug leakage.

Figure S9 illustrates the relationship among temperature, concentration, and phase transition behavior of the hydrogel and defines the concentration range at which the hydrogel remained in the sol state at different temperatures. To further investigate the pH dependence of CMC, the swelling behaviors of IL-22@CMC and blank CMC were evaluated separately. The swelling ratios of the two freeze-dried CMC samples in solutions with pH values of 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 6.5 initially increased and reached a maximum at pH = 6.5. Notably, this pH response could function within the acidic environment of ALD. As the pH of the soaking solution further increased, the swelling ratio gradually decreased (Fig. 1i, Fig. S10). These results indicate that CMC undergoes expansion and contraction in response to changes in environmental pH. Under strongly acidic conditions, H+ combines with –COO− to form –COOH, thereby inhibiting ionization of the carboxyl groups. Consequently, intermolecular interactions within the polymer network become stronger than interactions between polymer chains and water molecules, leading to network contraction and a reduced swelling ratio. As the pH increases, –COO− becomes moderately ionized, hydrophilicity reaches an optimal level, and the network structure becomes more relaxed, resulting in an increased swelling ratio. However, when the pH continues to increase, excessive ionic strength within the gel network shields residual charges on the polymer backbone with counterions, causing contraction of the molecular chains. Figure 1j shows the cumulative release of IL-22 from IL-22@CMC at pH 6.5 and 7.4. At 24 h, the cumulative release of IL-22 was 57.33% at pH 6.5 and 40.91% at pH 7.4. This observation is attributed to the higher swelling ratio of IL-22@CMC at pH 6.5, which allows the hydrogel network to fully expand, thereby facilitating diffusion-mediated release of IL-22 from the network. These results demonstrate that IL-22@CMC exhibits pH-responsive drug release properties. Gel permeation chromatography analysis revealed that after storage at 25 °C for two months, the retention time of CMC showed no significant change compared with that before storage, indicating good storage stability of CMC (Fig. S11).

IL-22@CMC exhibits sustained release, biosafety, degradability, and injectability

To evaluate the application potential of IL-22@CMC, its key biological properties, including release kinetics, biocompatibility, in vivo degradability, and injectability, were systematically investigated. The in vitro release kinetics of IL-22@CMC were first assessed. IL-22 release exhibited a biphasic profile, characterized by an initial rapid release phase within 24 h followed by a sustained release phase over the subsequent 72 h (Fig. 2a). The release rate was influenced by CMC concentration, with lower CMC concentrations promoting faster and more complete release. This behavior is consistent with the diffusion-controlled release mechanism typical of swellable polymer hydrogels [27]. First-order kinetic analysis indicated that 10 wt% CMC exhibited the fastest release rate (Fig. S12). Therefore, this formulation was selected for subsequent studies to ensure adequate drug release. The sustained-release properties of IL-22@CMC were further validated at the cellular level. ELISA results showed that cells treated with IL-22@CMC exhibited consistently higher IL-22 concentrations than those treated with an equivalent amount of IL-22 solution (Fig. 2b). This finding was attributed to continuous IL-22 release from CMC, which effectively prolonged its cellular retention and activity. Importantly, this sustained-release capability translated into improved pharmacokinetic properties in vivo. Specifically, IL-22@CMC achieved an area under the concentration–time curve from time zero to the last measurable time point (AUC0–t) of 140 ng/mL·d, which was 1.6-fold higher than that of the IL-22 solution (86.5 ng/mL·d). The half-life of IL-22@CMC was 3 days, twice that of the IL-22 solution (1.5 days), and the mean residence time was 4.8 days, 1.5-fold longer than that of the IL-22 solution (3.2 days) (Fig. 2c, Table S1).

Biocompatibility is critical for translational applications. CCK-8 was used to assess the effects of CMC on AML-12 cell viability. In vitro experiments demonstrated that CMC at concentrations of 10–30 wt% exhibited no significant cytotoxicity toward AML-12 cells (Fig. 2d). Importantly, neither the IL-22 solution nor IL-22@CMC exhibited cytotoxic effects in AML-12 cells. Furthermore, IL-22 effectively attenuated ethanol-induced cytotoxicity in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2e, f). In vivo studies revealed no significant pathological alterations in major organs of CMC-treated mice compared with the PBS control group upon histological examination (Fig. 2g), confirming its favorable biocompatibility. To enable in vivo tracking of CMC degradation via fluorescence imaging, CMC–Rhodamine B was synthesized as a fluorescent probe. Following subcutaneous injection of CMC–Rhodamine B, degradation was monitored by fluorescence imaging. The gradual decrease in fluorescence intensity in the dorsal region of mice indicated progressive in vivo degradation of the hydrogel (Fig. 2h, i). To assess the biological stability of CMC, its viscoelastic properties under acidic or oxidative conditions were examined. As shown in Fig. S13, CMC gradually disintegrated within 6 h after adding hydrochloric acid solution (pH 6.5). Furthermore, a similar response was observed upon addition of 1 mM H2O2. In addition, IL-22@CMC was smoothly injected using a standard syringe needle (Fig. 2j).

In summary, IL-22@CMC fulfills the essential criteria for a sustained-release drug delivery system. It enables continuous drug release both in vitro and in vivo while improving key pharmacokinetic parameters. Moreover, IL-22@CMC exhibits favorable biocompatibility, controlled biodegradability, and injectability. These advantages support its potential as an effective therapeutic strategy for ALD.

The expression level of IL-22 correlates with the progression of ALD

To assess the clinical relevance of IL-22 in ALD, the relationship between IL-22 expression levels and disease severity in patients with ALD was analyzed. Serum analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between circulating IL-22 levels and the liver injury markers ALT and AST in patients with ALD (Fig. 3a, b). Given that elevated ALT and AST are established indicators of hepatocyte injury and disease progression in ALD, this negative correlation suggests that reduced IL-22 expression levels are associated with aggravated liver damage [28].

Subsequently, this association was validated in ALD mice. Compared with normal mice, both protein and mRNA levels of IL-22 were significantly downregulated in the livers of alcohol-induced ALD mice (Fig. 3c, e, g). In vitro experiments reproduced this suppression, as ethanol exposure markedly reduced IL-22 expression in AML-12 hepatocytes (Fig. 3d, f, h). Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence analyses further confirmed significantly decreased IL-22 protein levels in the livers of ALD mice (Fig. 3i, j).

The consistent downregulation of IL-22 expression levels across clinical, in vivo, and in vitro models suggests that IL-22 deficiency may represent a characteristic feature of ALD pathology. This finding is mechanistically relevant because IL-22 is a recognized hepatoprotective cytokine. Loss of this protective signaling may promote persistent inflammation and hepatocellular injury characteristic of ALD [29]. Therefore, the observed reduction in IL-22 expression levels supports the therapeutic rationale of this study, namely that restoration of physiological IL-22 levels via IL-22@CMC may ameliorate key pathophysiological abnormalities in ALD.

IL-22@CMC inhibits inflammation and lipid accumulation in AML-12 cells

To investigate the inhibitory effects of IL-22@CMC on inflammatory cytokine secretion and lipid accumulation in vitro, an ALD cell model was established. A prolonged treatment period (48 h) was applied to better evaluate the sustained-release characteristics of IL-22@CMC. Ethanol stimulation activates proinflammatory signaling pathways in hepatocytes, which represents a key driver of ALD progression [30]. The data indicate that both IL-22 solution and IL-22@CMC attenuated EtOH-induced upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. Notably, IL-22@CMC exhibited a more pronounced inhibitory effect. Immunofluorescence and Western blot analyses revealed significantly reduced protein levels of these cytokines in IL-22@CMC-treated cells compared with those treated with an equivalent dose of IL-22 solution (Fig. 4a–d, f–k). This enhanced efficacy was further confirmed at the transcriptional level by reverse transcription–quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and in cell culture supernatants by ELISA (Fig. S14, S15). The superior performance of IL-22@CMC is attributed to its ability to maintain effective IL-22 concentrations over extended treatment periods, thereby providing sustained inhibition of inflammatory signaling cascades.

Dysregulated lipid metabolism leading to intracellular lipid accumulation is a hallmark of ALD [31]. The effects of IL-22@CMC on regulators of lipid synthesis and fatty acid oxidation were subsequently evaluated. Ethanol treatment significantly increased the expression levels of the lipid synthesis regulators sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c) and fatty acid synthase (FASN), while suppressing the expression of the fatty acid oxidation regulator peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-α (PPAR-α). Although IL-22 solution partially ameliorated these alterations, IL-22@CMC exhibited superior regulatory effects. Specifically, it more markedly downregulated SREBP-1c and FASN expression while more strongly upregulated PPAR-α expression (Fig. 4e, l–n; Fig. S16). Furthermore, Oil Red O staining confirmed that IL-22@CMC was more effective than IL-22 solution in reducing ethanol-induced lipid droplet accumulation (Fig. S17). These findings are consistent with the established role of IL-22 in promoting lipid oxidation and inhibiting de novo lipogenesis in hepatocytes [29, 32]. The sustained delivery provided by IL-22@CMC appeared to ameliorate alcohol-induced lipid accumulation more effectively than single-dose treatment.

Collectively, these in vitro findings provide strong evidence that the IL-22@CMC hydrogel more effectively and persistently suppresses the two core pathological processes in ALD—inflammation and steatosis—by enabling sustained cytokine delivery. These cellular-level therapeutic effects support further in vivo evaluation.

IL-22@CMC inhibits inflammation and lipid accumulation in mice

To validate the therapeutic potential of IL-22@CMC in vivo, a Gao–Binge model was established using C57BL/6J mice (Fig.S18). The objective of this study was to determine whether IL-22@CMC demonstrated superior efficacy compared with IL-22 solution in mitigating the hallmark pathological features of ALD. Ethanol-fed mice exhibited significant liver injury, as evidenced by elevated serum ALT and AST levels, along with marked fatty vacuolation and inflammatory cell infiltration observed by hematoxylin and eosin and Oil Red O staining (Fig. 5a–e). IL-22@CMC more effectively alleviated liver injury and lipid accumulation than IL-22 solution in ALD mice. Specifically, IL-22@CMC more markedly reduced lipid accumulation and inflammatory cell infiltration compared with IL-22 solution (Fig. 5a, b). Similarly, IL-22@CMC more effectively decreased serum levels of ALT, AST, and TC than IL-22 solution (Fig. 5c–e). In addition, IL-22@CMC and IL-22 solution exhibited comparable therapeutic effects in reducing the liver-to-body weight ratio, hepatic TG, and hepatic TC (Fig.S19a–c). Notably, serum TG levels were not reduced by IL-22 solution but were significantly decreased by IL-22@CMC (Fig.S19d).

The therapeutic effects of IL-22@CMC were further investigated at the molecular level in vivo. Western blotting and RT-qPCR analyses revealed that ethanol feeding upregulated the expression of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) and lipogenic proteins (SREBP-1c and FASN), while downregulating the expression of the fatty acid oxidation regulator PPAR-α. Compared with IL-22 solution, IL-22@CMC treatment more effectively suppressed inflammatory and lipogenic protein expression while more strongly inducing PPAR-α expression (Fig. 5f-m; Fig. S20, S21). Immunohistochemistry further confirmed the superior efficacy of IL-22@CMC in suppressing inflammatory cytokine expression in liver tissue (Fig. S22).

In summary, the in vivo experimental results demonstrated that IL-22@CMC exhibited superior therapeutic efficacy compared with IL-22 solution in the ALD mice model. This enhanced therapeutic effect was attributed to the improved pharmacokinetic profile of IL-22@CMC, which enabled sustained therapeutic levels of IL-22 and continuous hepatoprotective signaling.

IL-22@CMC inhibits inflammation and lipid accumulation through ERS

To elucidate the intrinsic mechanism by which IL-22@CMC alleviates ALD, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on AML-12 cells treated with or without IL-22@CMC under EtOH exposure. Differential gene expression analysis revealed that IL-22@CMC treatment significantly altered the transcriptional profile of EtOH-treated hepatocytes (Fig. 6a). Subsequent Gene Ontology enrichment analysis indicated that endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) was among the most significantly modulated biological processes (Fig. 6b), suggesting a potential role for ERS in mediating the therapeutic effects of IL-22@CMC.

ERS is a well-established contributor to hepatocyte injury, inflammation, and lipid metabolic dysregulation in ALD [33, 34]. The experimental data confirmed that EtOH treatment markedly upregulated the protein expression of key ERS markers, including glucose-regulated protein 78 and C/EBP homologous protein, in AML-12 cells. Notably, IL-22@CMC treatment potently suppressed the EtOH-induced elevation of these markers, as demonstrated by both immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 6c–e) and Western blot analysis (Fig. 6f–i). To directly determine whether ERS inhibition is functionally required for the protective effects of IL-22@CMC, tunicamycin (TM), a pharmacological inducer of ERS, was administered in the in vivo ALD model. TM co-treatment abolished the therapeutic benefits of IL-22@CMC. Specifically, it reversed the improvements in liver histology (Fig. 6n) and lipid accumulation (Fig. 6o) conferred by IL-22@CMC and exacerbated serum ALT, AST, and TC levels (Fig. S23). At the molecular level, TM co-treatment counteracted the ability of IL-22@CMC to downregulate proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) and lipogenic proteins (SREBP-1c and FASN), while preventing the upregulation of the fatty acid oxidation regulator PPAR-α (Fig. 6j–m).

Collectively, these findings indicate that ERS suppression represents a central mechanism underlying the anti-inflammatory and anti–lipid accumulation effects of IL-22@CMC. This observation is consistent with accumulating evidence that alleviation of ERS is a promising therapeutic strategy for metabolic liver diseases [35]. Further investigation will clarify how IL-22@CMC modulates this critical pathological process in ALD.

IL-22@CMC regulates ERS in ALD through the AMPK/SIRT1 signaling pathway

To elucidate the mechanism by which IL-22@CMC suppresses ERS, additional analysis of RNA-seq data was conducted. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway enrichment analysis indicated that the AMPK signaling pathway may be involved in IL-22@CMC-mediated regulation of ERS (Fig. 7a). The AMPK pathway functions as a central regulator of cellular energy homeostasis and stress responses, and its activation has been shown to attenuate ERS and inflammation in multiple liver injury models [36, 37]. Furthermore, the experimental data indicated that ethanol stimulation inhibited AMPK phosphorylation and reduced the expression of its downstream target SIRT1 in AML-12 cells. Importantly, compared with IL-22 solution, IL-22@CMC treatment more effectively restored activation of the AMPK/SIRT1 signaling pathway (Fig. 7b–d), as supported by Western blot analysis (Fig. S24).

To investigate the functional interaction between AMPK and SIRT1, pharmacological inhibitors targeting each enzyme were applied. The AMPK inhibitor Compound C abolished the ability of IL-22@CMC to activate SIRT1 (Fig. S25), whereas the SIRT1 inhibitor EX-527 blocked IL-22@CMC-induced AMPK phosphorylation (Fig. S26). This reciprocal inhibition indicates the presence of a positive feedback loop between AMPK and SIRT1, consistent with previously reported mechanisms in which these proteins interact to amplify metabolic and stress-adaptive signaling [38]. Importantly, inhibition of this pathway with Compound C or EX-527 significantly reversed the beneficial effects of IL-22@CMC, including suppression of inflammatory cytokine secretion and lipid accumulation. Combined inhibition nearly completely abolished the therapeutic effects of IL-22@CMC (Fig. 7e–p, Figs. S27–S29).

In conclusion, these findings indicate that IL-22@CMC inhibits inflammatory cytokine secretion and lipid accumulation in AML-12 cells by suppressing ERS through the AMPK/SIRT1 signaling pathway. Although the present mechanistic investigation focused on the AMPK/SIRT1/ERS axis, the sustained release of IL-22@CMC may also promote activation of the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling pathway. Furthermore, crosstalk between the AMPK/SIRT1 and STAT3 signaling pathways may contribute to the enhanced efficacy of IL-22@CMC [39]. Therefore, future studies should investigate whether IL-22@CMC exerts its hepatoprotective effects through synergistic activation of both STAT3 and AMPK/SIRT1 signaling pathways.

Conclusion

In this study, an injectable, pH-responsive CMC was developed for the sustained delivery of recombinant IL-22 to treat ALD. The CMC efficiently encapsulated IL-22 via hydrogen-bonding interactions, exhibited pH-dependent swelling and release behavior, and demonstrated favorable biocompatibility and degradability. Compared with free IL-22 solution, a single intraperitoneal injection of IL-22@CMC provided prolonged systemic exposure and significantly enhanced therapeutic efficacy in both ethanol-induced AML-12 cells and a murine ALD model, as evidenced by reduced hepatic steatosis, decreased inflammatory cytokine expression, and attenuated hepatocellular injury. Mechanistically, RNA-seq and subsequent validation experiments revealed that the superior hepatoprotective effects conferred by IL-22@CMC were mediated through sustained activation of the AMPK/SIRT1 signaling pathway, leading to suppression of ERS. Collectively, these findings establish IL-22@CMC as a simple, effective, and mechanistically distinct therapeutic platform for ALD. This hydrogel-based delivery strategy may also be adaptable to other protein therapeutics for inflammatory liver diseases in which sustained and microenvironment-responsive drug release is desirable.
