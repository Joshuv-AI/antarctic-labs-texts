Prognostic Implications and Efficacy of Catheter Ablation by Atrial Fibrillation Type
Authors: Hiroshi Miyama, Seiji Takatsuki, Nobuhiro Ikemura, Takehiro Kimura, Yoshinori Katsumata, Shuhei Yamashita, Koki Yamaoka, Susumu Ibe, Yuta Seki, Terumasa Yamashita, Kenji Hashimoto, Ikuko Ueda, Koji Ueno, Takahiro Ohki, Keiichi Fukuda, Shun Kohsaka
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCatheter ablation (CA) for atrial fibrillation (AF) is preferred for paroxysmal AF (PAF) but selectively performed in patients with persistent AF (PersAF). This study aimed to investigate the prognostic differences and consequences of CA based on the AF type.Methods and ResultsData from a multicenter AF cohort study were analyzed, categorizing patients as PAF or PersAF according to AF duration (≤7 or >7 days, respectively). A composite of all‐cause death, heart failure hospitalization, stroke, and bleeding events during 2‐year follow‐up and changes in the Atrial Fibrillation Effect on Quality‐of‐life score were compared. Additionally, propensity score matching was performed to compare clinical outcomes of patients with and without CA in both AF types. Among 2788 patients, 51.6% and 48.4% had PAF and PersAF, respectively. Patients with PersAF had a higher incidence of the composite outcome (12.8% versus 7.2%; P<0.001) and smaller improvements in Atrial Fibrillation Effect on Quality‐of‐life scores than those with PAF. After adjusting for baseline characteristics, PersAF was an independent predictor of adverse outcomes (adjusted hazard ratio, 1.35 [95% CI, 1.30–1.78], P=0.031) and was associated with poor improvements in Atrial Fibrillation Effect on Quality‐of‐life scores. Propensity score matching analysis showed that the CA group had significantly fewer adverse events than the medication group among patients with PAF (odds ratio, 0.31 [95% CI, 0.18–0.68]; P=0.002). Patients with PersAF showed a similar but nonsignificant trend.ConclusionsPersAF is a risk factor for worse clinical outcomes, including patients' health status. CA is associated with fewer adverse events, although careful consideration is required based on the AF type.Keywords: atrial fibrillation, catheter ablation, health‐related quality of life, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, persistent atrial fibrillationSubject Categories: Atrial Fibrillation

Atrial fibrillation (AF), the most prevalent arrhythmia, is associated with a high risk of heart failure (HF), thromboembolism, death, and poor health‐related quality of life (HR‐QoL). 1 , 2 , 3 Catheter ablation (CA) for AF reportedly improves not only patients' HR‐QoL but also cardiovascular prognosis in subgroups such as patients with HF. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 From the perspective of the efficacy of CA, paroxysmal AF (PAF) has conventionally been a preferred indication over persistent AF (PersAF), 8 , 9 with a recent increase in reports supporting rhythm control emphasizing this trend, especially for those with early AF (ie, first AF episode, recent onset AF, and PAF). 7 , 10 , 11

However, CA is selectively performed for patients with PersAF in daily clinical practice, whereas the subanalysis of randomized controlled trials for direct oral anticoagulants has revealed the poor cardiovascular prognosis of patients with PersAF compared with that of patients with PAF over the past decade. 12 , 13 , 14 At this point, there is still a lack of insight into prognostic differences, including patients' health status (ie, HR‐QoL) and the consequences of undergoing CA by the type of AF.

Therefore, this study aimed to identify how cardiovascular prognosis and patient‐reported HR‐QoL differ by AF type and to analyze whether there are differences in outcomes after CA using a multicenter registry‐based cohort of patients with AF. We sought to identify the subgroup of patients with AF at high risk of serious complications and HR‐QoL deterioration or who benefited from CA.

METHODS

Data Sources

We analyzed patient‐level data from the KiCS‐AF (Keio Interhospital Studies‐Atrial Fibrillation) registry. The rationale and design of the KiCS‐AF registry have been previously described. 15 The registry included patients with prevalent or incident AF who were newly diagnosed at or referred to the 11 participating hospitals in the Tokyo metropolitan area in the previous 6 months and assessed 150 variables, including disease history; symptoms; prior and current medication use; and ECG, echocardiography, and laboratory results. All patients underwent follow‐up examinations, and data on major adverse cardiovascular events, laboratory test results, procedures, and medications were updated yearly. On‐site auditing was performed to ensure proper consecutive patient enrollment. Data collection from the registry was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, and institutional review board/ethics committee approval was obtained from all involved institutions. All participants provided written informed consent. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population

The data available until December 2019 were retrieved. Overall, 3304 consecutive patients with AF were included in the registry, of whom 220 (6.7%) with a history of CA or maze procedure, 206 (6.2%) with initial AF, 55 (1.7%) lacking data on AF type, and 35 (1.1%) lacking 2‐year follow‐up data were excluded, leaving 2788 (84.4%) patients for assessment. The mean follow‐up period was 671 (SD, 150) days (Figure S1).

In the analysis, we first compared the risk of adverse clinical outcomes, including poor HR‐QoL, between the types of AF. Second, we conducted a propensity score (PS) matching to assess the effect of undergoing CA on the clinical outcomes and compared the 2‐year event rates between those who either underwent CA or received medication alone at 1 year after registration. For the CA procedure, all patients with PAF underwent pulmonary vein isolation, the cornerstone of AF ablation, by radiofrequency or cryoballoon ablation. Patients with PersAF underwent additional nonpulmonary vein trigger ablation (eg, left posterior wall, superior vena cava, or coronary sinus), linear ablation (eg, cavotricuspid isthmus ablation, or lateral mitral isthmus ablation), continuous fractionated atrial electrogram ablation, or focal premature atrial contraction ablation at the discretion of the physician.

AF Type and Study Outcomes

AF type was defined as either PAF or PersAF (including long‐standing AF), depending on whether termination occurred within 7 days, which is consistent with the major guidelines for AF management. 16 , 17 The primary outcome measure was a composite of all‐cause death, hospitalization for HF, stroke, and bleeding events within 2 years after registration; these outcomes were assessed independently. The occurrence of events was determined by an end point adjudication committee that included 3 cardiologists who reviewed health records and interviewed the responsible clinical research coordinators at each site.

Assessment of Quality of Life

Patients answered the Atrial Fibrillation Effect on Quality‐of‐life (AFEQT) questionnaire at registration and at a mean of 414 (SD, 71) days after enrollment. The AFEQT quantifies the following 4 domains of AF‐related QoL using a 7‐point Likert‐type response scale: symptoms, daily activities, treatment concerns, and treatment satisfaction. 18 , 19 An AFEQT overall summary (AFEQT‐OS) score ranging from 0 (worst health status) to 100 (best possible health status [no impairment]) was calculated from the first 3 domains. AFEQT data were missing in 0.4% (12/2788) and 14.5% (404/2788) of the patients at baseline and follow‐up, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as median (interquartile range) or frequency (percentage), depending on whether it was continuous or categorical, respectively. Comparisons of baseline characteristics were performed using the Mann–Whitney U or Pearson's chi‐square tests, as appropriate. The rate of missing data for patient‐level factors was <2%, except that of estimated glomerular filtration rate (5.2%, n=144). Patients with missing AFEQT data were excluded from HR‐QoL analyses.

The time to the composite outcome was summarized using the Kaplan–Meier method, and the difference in the cumulative survival rate between PAF and PersAF was compared using the log‐rank test. The stratified Cox proportional hazards model was used to investigate the association between AF type and the composite outcome after adjusting for clinically relevant variables, and the results are presented as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs. The model was adjusted for age; sex; body mass index; history of hypertension, diabetes, HF, stroke or transient ischemic attack, or coronary artery disease; baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (analyzed as a continuous variable); use of oral anticoagulants, beta ‐blockers, renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone blockers, diuretics, or antiarrhythmic drugs; and undergoing CA within the 2‐year follow‐up. The Schoenfeld residuals test was used to verify the validity of proportional hazards assumption.

Additionally, we performed subgroup analysis and interaction tests using the following variables: sex, age (<75 or ≥75 years), history of HF, left ventricular ejection fraction (<50% or ≥50%), left atrial dimension (LAD; <40 or ≥40 mm), use of beta blockers, and presence or absence of CA for AF during the 2‐year follow‐up. Furthermore, sensitivity analysis was performed, excluding patients with a history of HF. Among those with PersAF, subanalyses were performed on the following variables: persistent AF or permanent AF (persistent AF with a decision not to pursue a rhythm control strategy), LAD; <40 or ≥40 mm, presence of moderate or greater valvular disease, and sinus rhythm or atrial arrhythmia (ie, AF or atrial tachycardia) at the 2‐year follow‐up visit.

To highlight the variability in health status, AFEQT scores were calculated as median (interquartile range). Changes in AFEQT scores at 1 year were evaluated using paired t tests; positive and negative changes represented improved and worsened HR‐QoL, respectively. To investigate the association between AF type and changes in AFEQT‐OS scores, we constructed a linear regression model that was adjusted for the baseline AFEQT‐OS score, CA within a 1‐year follow‐up, and the aforementioned variables used in the stratified Cox proportional hazards model.

To evaluate the consequences after CA, we compared the incidence of composite outcomes after 2‐year follow‐up between the CA group, which underwent CA for AF within 1 year after registration, and the medication group. The effect of CA was assessed using (1) Cox proportional hazards model adjusted for the aforementioned baseline characteristics, and (2) PS matching analysis for comparison of those with and without CA for both PAF and PersAF patients (Figure S1). PS matching analysis was performed using the model from a previous study that evaluated the effect of CA in patients with AF based on the KiCS‐AF registry. 6 PS was calculated for each patient using a logistic regression model predicting selection into the CA group using 46 variables (Data S1). The nearest neighbor method was used as the matching method, and covariate balance within the matched cohorts was assessed using standardized mean differences, with <0.1 being considered adequate balance. 20 Using conventional sensitivity analysis to reduce the immortal time bias, 81 days (median value of the time from registration to ablation) were reduced from the medical therapy group, and the respective time from registration to ablation was reduced from the CA group. The outcome was compared between the CA and medication groups using univariate logistic regression analysis for each type of AF (ie, PAF and PersAF).

SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) was used to analyze the type of AF, primary outcome, and HR‐QoL. Analysis using PS matching was performed using R version 3.6.3 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). All reported P values were 2‐sided, and values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

The majority (68.4%; n=1907) of patients were male. The median age was 69 (interquartile range 61–76) years, and the mean CHA2DS2‐VASc score was 2.5 (SD 1.7). Overall, 1439 (51.6%) and 1349 (48.4%) patients had PAF and PersAF, respectively. The AF diagnosis date was documented for 87.5% of patients (n=2439), of whom 67.5% (n=1647) were diagnosed with AF within 1 year before enrollment.

AF Type and Clinical Outcomes

During the 1‐year follow‐up, patients with PAF were more likely to undergo CA for AF than those with PersAF (46.4% versus 31.9%; P<0.001), and the likelihood increased to 49.3% and 33.6% after 2‐year follow‐up, respectively (P<0.001). Antiarrhythmic drugs were continued in 14.0% and 8.6% of patients with PAF and PersAF, respectively (data available for 79.7% of the cohort), and anticoagulants were continued in 46.7% and 67.2%, respectively (data available for 79.6%). Patients with PersAF had a significantly higher composite outcome incidence at 2 years than those with PAF (Figure 1 and Table 2).

Effect of CA and AF Type

When comparing the outcomes in the CA and medication groups during the 2‐year follow‐up, the incidence of composite outcomes was significantly lower in the CA group in the total population and in patients with PAF and PersAF (Table S4). After adjusting for relevant variables, CA was associated with a lower risk of adverse outcomes in the total population (adjusted HR, 0.44 [95% CI, 0.30–0.64]; P<0.001). The results were comparable between those with PAF (adjusted HR, 0.34 [95% CI, 0.20–0.61]; P<0.001) and PersAF (adjusted HR, 0.61 [95% CI, 0.37–1.03]; P=0.063).

In PS matching, 36/448 (8.1%) and 11/336 (3.3%) patients with PAF and PersAF, respectively, in the medication therapy group underwent CA for AF 1 year after registration (ie, crossover). The frequency of achieving sinus rhythms 2 years after registration was significantly higher in the CA group than in the medication group for both patients with PAF and PersAF (CA versus medication group, PAF: 95.4% versus 80.7%; P<0.001 and PersAF: 88.5% versus 28.3%; P<0.001). Undergoing CA was associated with a lower incidence of composite outcomes in those with PAF (odds ratio [OR], 0.31 [95% CI, 0.18–0.68]; P=0.002), and a similar trend was observed in those with PersAF, although it did not reach statistical significance (OR, 0.57 [95% CI, 0.30–1.12]; P=0.11) (Figure 5, Table S5). For patients' HR‐QoL, there was no significant difference in baseline AFEQT‐OS scores between the groups. However, when focusing on changes in HR‐QoL over 1 year, the CA group showed a significantly greater improvement, resulting in better health status at 1 year. The result was more evident in patients with PAF but was also significant in those with PersAF (Table S6).

DISCUSSION

In this prospective, multicenter study, patients with PersAF showed significantly worse clinical outcomes at 2 years compared with those with PAF and were associated with less improvement in HR‐QoL. Multivariable analysis and PS matching showed a lower risk of adverse outcomes in the CA group than in the medication group among patients with PAF, and the trend, which did not reach statistical significance, was consistent in those with PersAF. The present results suggest the pathogenicity of long‐standing AF, which can be a rationale for intervention, including CA, to prevent adverse clinical events and disease progression in the early stages of AF (ie, PAF), while the indications for CA in those with PersAF require deliberate consideration.

Conventionally, the cardiovascular risk in patients with AF is often assessed without considering the type of AF (ie, PAF or PersAF). For embolic events, only patient‐level factors are focused on (eg, CHA2DS2‐VASc score), without accounting for the duration or burden of the AF. 21 , 22 Guidelines for the management of AF have also clearly stated that anticoagulant therapy should be considered, regardless of the AF type. 16 , 17 A paradigm change has occurred over the past decade, and clinical investigations have compared the risk of the duration of AF in the context of the increasing use of direct oral anticoagulants. The results of post hoc analyses of the Apixaban for Reduction in Stroke and Other Thromboembolic Events in Atrial Fibrillation trial, the Effective Anticoagulation with Factor Xa Next Generation in Atrial Fibrillation trial, and the Rivaroxaban Once Daily Oral Direct Factor Xa Inhibition Compared with Vitamin K Antagonism for Prevention of Stroke and Embolism Trial in Atrial Fibrillation have reported a higher risk of stroke in patients with PersAF than in those with PAF. 12 , 23 , 24 , 25 Similarly, reports of increased risks of death and HF in those with PersAF have been published, suggesting the encompassing role of AF type in cardiovascular prognosis. 13 , 14 The present study is in line with these studies and is the first report to confirm the clinical implication of AF type among Asian populations. The results were robust and reproducible for several subgroups or in sensitivity analysis excluding those with a history of HF. It is also noteworthy that in a subgroup analysis of patients with PersAF, cardiovascular events were more frequent in those with permanent AF, left atrial dilatation, valvular heart disease, and atrial arrhythmias at 2‐year follow‐up ECG, suggesting that early‐stage AF, even in those with PersAF, perhaps a subgroup with a particularly preserved prognosis that should be considered for intervention. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, our prospective study is the first to identify the relationship between the type of AF and the prognosis of the HR‐QoL in patients with AF. Although the previous studies by Witassek et al. and Peinado et al. did not find a significant association between the type of AF and patients' HR‐QoL, it is noteworthy that both studies included patients who were enrolled relatively long after their AF diagnosis (3–5 years), and a considerable proportion of patients were already receiving rhythm control treatment at enrollment, including CA. 25 , 26 It is also important to note that these were cross‐sectional studies, and thus, the potential effects of treatment on HR‐QoL should be taken into consideration. The patients' health status tends to be preserved in those with PersAF compared with that in patients with PAF, 26 which was also observed in our study for baseline AFEQT‐OS scores. However, our study revealed that PersAF was independently associated with less improvement in the AFEQT‐OS score 1 year after registration, suggesting a worse prognosis not only for adverse clinical outcomes but also for HR‐QoL. These results might cause physicians to pay more attention to HR‐QoL among those with PersAF, even if patients seem to be well tolerated, and to recommend aggressive interventions (ie, CA), if necessary.

Considering the prognostic implications of CA, recent studies have shown its efficacy in reducing cardiovascular risks among patients with AF. 4 , 6 , 7 The present results are in line with those of studies and support the findings suggesting the benefits of CA in patients with early‐stage AF. 7 , 11 , 27 , 28 , 29 The EAST‐AFNET 4 (Early Treatment of Atrial Fibrillation for Stroke Prevention) trial study showed that rhythm control improved the clinical outcomes of patients with early AF who were diagnosed within 1 year. 7 Of the patients included in the trial, 36% were PAF and 20% from the rhythm control arm (treated with antiarrhythmic drugs or CA) underwent CA. 7 The present registry included a high proportion of patients undergoing CA, partly because the majority of the population was young and newly diagnosed with AF (67.5% were diagnosed within 1 year before enrollment). Most patients undergoing CA underwent the procedure within 1 year after registration, and this early intervention may have contributed to the improvement of prognosis, especially in those with PAF. Meanwhile, the present study could not confirm the significance of the efficacy of CA among patients with PersAF who demonstrated worse clinical outcomes and seemed to benefit more from the intervention. The lack of statistical power or the less successful maintenance of a sinus rhythm compared with that in the PAF population might limit the efficacy of CA and its contribution to the outcomes. Although the indications for CA in those with PersAF require careful consideration at this point, further analysis could improve the understanding of the efficacy of CA for different subgroups of patients with PersAF.

Our study has several limitations. First, the number of patients was relatively small in PS matching to be able to detect the efficacy of undergoing CA compared with receiving only medication. Second, the protocol for CA was not standardized, as the KiCS‐AF registry was a prospective cohort study, and the details of each procedure were not available across facilities. Although all patients underwent pulmonary vein isolation by radiofrequency or cryoballoon ablation, additional nonpulmonary vein trigger ablation, linear ablation, complex fractionated atrial electrogram ablation, or focal premature atrial contraction ablation was at the discretion of the physician. This may have introduced some degree of heterogeneity in the treatment approach and outcomes, which should be considered when interpreting our study's results. Third, we could not determine the effect of the AF burden on clinical outcomes. AF burden (ie, the proportion of the AF duration) is the most accurate indicator of how AF affects individuals. However, only those treated with implanted cardiac electronic devices can know the actual amount, and AF duration based on AF type (ie, PAF or PersAF) is the most feasible classification that could be used in daily clinical practice. Fourth, the registry did not collect data on the recurrence of atrial arrhythmias after CA. However, the ECG at 2 years showed a significantly higher frequency of sinus rhythm in the CA group compared with the medication group for both PAF and PersAF, which may indicate the durability of CA. Fifth, the follow‐up HR‐QoL survey data from 404 patients (14.5%) were missing. However, the completion rate was higher than that in other registries. 30 Furthermore, as patients with missing HR‐QoL data were largely comparable to those of the analytic cohort, we believe that our results are sufficiently generalizable. Sixth, the registry had a relatively high amount of data defects for prescription drugs (antiarrhythmic drugs and oral anticoagulants) at 2‐year follow‐up and the echocardiogram data, such as left ventricular ejection fraction and LAD. For oral anticoagulants, although there was no significant difference in the incidence of stroke or bleeding events between AF types, the effect of drug continuation or discontinuation is not considered. Finally, given the observational nature of this study, unknown confounders may have influenced our results.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, PersAF was an independent risk factor for adverse clinical outcomes and a strong, independent indicator of a poor improvement in HR‐QoL. Undergoing CA was associated with a lower risk of adverse outcomes in patients with PAF. The indications for CA in patients with PersAF require careful consideration and further investigation.
