Relationship Between Time‐Varying Achieved High‐Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol and Risk of Coronary Events Depends on Haptoglobin Phenotype Within the ACCORD Lipid Study
Authors: Rachel A Warren, Allie S Carew, Pantelis Andreou, Andrew P Levy, John Sapp, Orit Lache, Henry N Ginsberg, Eric B Rimm, Christine Herman, Susan Kirkland, Leah E Cahill
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Hp (haptoglobin)2‐2 phenotype (~40% of people) is associated with dysfunctional high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) that is heavily oxidized in hyperglycemia, which may explain why raising HDL‐cholesterol (HDL‐C) does not reliably prevent coronary artery disease (CAD) in diabetes.Methods and ResultsIn this observational study using longitudinal data from the ACCORD (Action to Control Cardiovascular Risk in Diabetes) lipid trial, time‐varying (achieved) HDL‐C updated at 4, 8, and 12 months, and annually thereafter over a mean of 4.7 years, was analyzed in relation to risk of CAD and secondary outcomes using Cox proportional hazards regression with time‐varying covariables among participants with (n=1781) and without (n=3191) the Hp2‐2 phenotype. HDL‐C did not differ between the phenotypes throughout the study. Having low HDL‐C (<40 mg/dL for male participants and <50 mg/dL for female participants) was associated with a greater risk of CAD compared with non‐low HDL‐C among participants with the non‐Hp2‐2 phenotype (hazard ratio [HR], 1.48 [95% CI, 1.18–1.87]) but not among the Hp2‐2 phenotype (HR, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.70–1.35]; P interaction=0.03). Similarly, an inverse relationship was observed between HDL‐C quintiles and CAD risk among participants without the Hp2‐2 phenotype, whereas no significant inverse relationship was observed among participants with the Hp2‐2 phenotype (P interaction=0.38). Among the Hp2‐2 phenotype group, having low HDL‐C was associated with higher risk of CVD mortality (HR, 2.09 [95% CI, 1.05–4.13]), and compared with the lowest HDL‐C quintile, higher quintiles were associated with lower risk of CVD mortality and congestive heart failure.ConclusionsHp phenotype modified the association between HDL‐C and risk of CAD in the ACCORD lipid study, suggesting that HDL dysfunction in the Hp2‐2 phenotype may hinder CAD‐protective properties of HDL‐C.Keywords: coronary artery disease, epidemiology, genetic association, HDL‐cholesterol, type 2 diabetesSubject Categories: Coronary Artery Disease; Diabetes, Type 2; Atherosclerosis; Epidemiology; Precision Medicine

Despite the fact that a low serum concentration of high‐density lipoprotein‐cholesterol (HDL‐C) is a known risk factor for coronary artery disease (CAD), large clinical trials that aimed to raise HDL‐C in people with diabetes have not provided consistent evidence to support a cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk reduction from raising HDL‐C in people with diabetes. 1 , 2 , 3 Further, recent epidemiological studies have found that very high levels of HDL‐C are associated with a greater risk of CVD and mortality. 4 , 5 , 6 Potentially, HDL‐C raising is beneficial only to some people and not others. It is now recognized that the quantity of HDL‐C does not necessarily reflect the quality or antiatherogenic function of HDL and that although 2 individuals may have the same concentration of serum HDL‐C, the quality of their HDL may differ, thereby resulting in differing CAD risk profiles. 7 Unmeasured differences between individuals, such as genetics, that affect HDL function could help to provide an explanation for inconsistent results previously reported in trials that aimed to pharmacologically raise HDL‐C.

A common variation in the gene that codes for the abundant plasma protein Hp (haptoglobin) identifies individuals who may be at increased risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease from hyperglycemia and altered HDL function. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 In patients with the Hp2‐2 genotype (who thus produce the Hp2‐2 protein and have the Hp2‐2 phenotype) and hyperglycemia, HDL has been shown to be dysfunctional and proatherogenic with the potential to increase susceptibility to atherosclerosis and ultimately CAD. 9 , 13 , 14 As a result, higher HDL‐C may not be beneficial for CAD prevention among people with the Hp2‐2 phenotype (approximately 40% of people worldwide) and hyperglycemia due to the dysfunctional nature of HDL in these individuals. However, higher HDL‐C may be protective against CAD among people without the Hp2‐2 phenotype (who thus have the Hp1‐1 or Hp2‐1 phenotype) in whom the functions of Hp and HDL are better preserved.

In our reanalysis of the ACCORD (Action to Control Cardiovascular Risk in Diabetes) lipid study where we incorporated Hp phenotype, we observed that fenofibrate (HDL‐C raising and triglyceride lowering drug) compared with placebo significantly reduced the risk of CAD events among participants with the non‐Hp2‐2 phenotype but not among those with the Hp2‐2 phenotype in a significant interaction. 15 Our analysis determined that the effect of fenofibrate against CAD events among the non‐Hp2‐2 phenotype group was most effective in participants with severe dyslipidemia at baseline (triglycerides ≥204 mg/dL and HDL‐C ≤34 mg/dL, P interaction=0.01), and in male participants (P interaction=0.02) with an increased CAD risk from fenofibrate treatment observed in female participants with the Hp2‐2 phenotype (P interaction=0.002). 15 However, as in the original ACCORD lipid study, 1 it was an intention‐to‐treat analysis that did not consider actual achieved HDL‐C values over the study duration, and it is uncertain whether the findings could be owing to HDL dysfunction among people with the Hp2‐2 phenotype. 15

The aim of the present study was to determine whether the relationship between time‐varying (longitudinally updated) achieved HDL‐C over time and risk of incident CAD and other secondary CVD outcomes is modified by Hp phenotype. We conducted an analysis of time‐varying HDL‐C data from the ACCORD lipid study to determine the relationship between low HDL‐C (clinically defined as <40 mg/dL for male participants and <50 mg/dL for female participants 16 ) and risk of incident CAD events in each of the Hp phenotype groups separately. To further understand the relationship across the range of HDL‐C in our study, we also analyzed quintiles of HDL‐C in relation to all outcomes in each phenotype group separately.

METHODS

Study Design and Participants

A reanalysis of data from the ACCORD lipid study with the addition of Hp phenotype was undertaken to determine the relationship between time‐varying “achieved” HDL‐C and CAD and secondary CVD outcomes among each of the Hp phenotype groups. The design, methods and major findings of the ACCORD study (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT00000620) have been reported previously. 1 , 17 , 18 , 19 The ACCORD study protocol previously published was approved by the institutional review board or ethics committee at each center, as well as by a review panel at the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute. 18 Briefly, the ACCORD study was a large‐scale multicenter (77 clinical sites in Canada and the United States) double‐blind 2×2 factorial design randomized control trial designed to examine the effect of strict glycemic control and either intensive blood pressure control or lipid therapy on cardiovascular outcomes in 10 251 high‐risk male participants and female participants with type 2 diabetes. There were no data on other sexes or on gender. Randomization occurred between January 11, 2001, and October 29, 2005, and was performed centrally on the study website using permutated blocks to conceal study‐group assignment. All participants in the study had to have a glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level of ≤7.5% and were aged between 40 and 79 years if there was evidence of clinical CVD or between 55 and 79 years if there was anatomical evidence of significant atherosclerosis, albuminuria, left ventricular hypertrophy, or at least 2 additional risk factors for CVD at baseline. In the lipid arm of the ACCORD study, 5518 patients were randomized to receive either fenofibrate or placebo in addition to open‐label background simvastatin over a mean follow‐up of 4.7 years. Participants had to have a baseline low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol level of 60 to 180 mg/dL, HDL‐C of <55 mg/dL for female and Black participants or <50 mg/dL for all others, and a serum triglyceride level <750 mg/dL if not on a lipid medication or <400 mg/dL if on a lipid medication. Lipid and HbA1c determinations were performed at the ACCORD central laboratory. A fasting plasma lipid profile was measured at baseline; 4, 8, and 12 months postrandomization; annually thereafter; and at study end. HbA1c was measured at baseline, every 4 months thereafter, and at study end. 1 , 17 , 18

All participants in the ACCORD study provided written informed consent for future research. The ACCORD study was completed in 2009, and the data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author and the National Institutes of Health's Open Biologic Specimen and Data Repository Information Coordinating Center.

Haptoglobin Phenotyping

The Hp phenotype of ACCORD participants was determined using a validated high‐throughput enzyme linked immunosorbent assay with a sensitivity and specificity of 99% and 98.1%, respectively. 20 Hp phenotype does not change over time; therefore, a blood sample from either baseline or a follow‐up visit was used. Of the 5518 ACCORD lipid participants, Hp phenotype was determined for 4996 (90.5%). The exclusion of the other 522 participants occurred because serum samples from these participants had previously been depleted by the measurement of other biomarkers.

Outcome Events

We report our primary outcome of CAD events according to the original ACCORD lipid study prespecified diagnosis criteria and definition of “major CAD events,” which is defined as the first occurrence of a fatal coronary event, a nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI), or unstable angina. Although the mechanism is not well understood, stroke is an end point that has been associated with the Hp1‐1 phenotype rather than the Hp2‐2 phenotype, 21 , 22 suggesting that CAD and stroke should be separated from a composite CVD outcome for analyses by Hp phenotype. For this reason, the present analysis studied the primary outcome of CAD events rather than the original ACCORD lipid study primary outcome of major CVD events.

Secondary outcomes were major CVD events (nonfatal MI, nonfatal stroke, or CVD mortality), nonfatal MI, CVD mortality, total mortality, stroke, and congestive heart failure (CHF) events. All reported cardiovascular outcomes in the ACCORD study were adjudicated by a panel blinded to treatment allocation using predefined adjudication criteria, as described previously in the ACCORD study protocol. 1

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata/SE software version 17.0 (College Station, TX) at a 2‐tailed alpha level of 0.05. Participants with the Hp2‐1 or Hp1‐1 phenotypes (those without the Hp2‐2 phenotype) were combined to form a group, which is a common approach when studying the Hp phenotype because of the low frequency of the Hp1‐1 phenotype (~15%) and because the structure and function of the Hp2‐1 and Hp1‐1 proteins are similar in relation to the Hp2‐2 protein. 11 , 12 , 23 , 24

Participants were grouped based on Hp phenotype and presence of low baseline HDL‐C (<40 mg/dL for male participants and <50 mg/dL for female participants 16 ), and characteristics were compared using t tests and Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables and chi‐square tests for categorical variables. There were <3% of data missing for baseline variables. For time‐dependent HDL‐C and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, 4.8% of data were missing, 13.2% for HbA1c, 8.3% for systolic blood pressure, and 6.0% for body mass index. The last observation carried forward method was used for missing time‐dependent variables. Twenty‐four (0.5%) participants had missing baseline HDL‐C measurements and thus were excluded from the analysis. Mean HDL‐C at baseline, 4, 8 months, and annually thereafter was compared between Hp phenotype groups using t tests.

Multivariable adjusted hazard ratios with 95% CIs were determined using Cox proportional hazards regression models with time‐varying covariables to quantify the relationship between low HDL‐C (<40 mg/dL for male participants and <50 mg/dL for female participants 16 ) and each outcome in the 2 phenotype groups separately. To understand the relationship across the range of HDL‐C in our study, an analysis of HDL‐C quintiles (determined for male participants and female participants separately) was also conducted. Time‐varying covariables were used to relate the most recent measure for each of those variables to incident outcomes at the time of an event to avoid potential bias from using a single baseline measurement. Time‐independent covariables recorded at baseline included age (continuous), sex (female/male), race or ethnicity (White/Black/Hispanic/unspecified), diabetes duration (≤10/>10 years), a history of CVD (present/absent), a history of CHF (present/absent), baseline cigarette smoking (yes/no), baseline alcohol consumption (drinker/nondrinker), family history of CVD (absent/premature heart disease or stroke/heart disease or stroke at an unknown age/unknown), education (high school/less than high school/some college/college degree or higher), glycemic control group assignment (standard/intensive), lipid treatment assignment (placebo/fenofibrate), clinical network, baseline thiazolidinedione use (present/absent), baseline antihypertensive agent use (present/absent), and baseline lipid‐lowering agent use (present/absent). Time‐varying covariables included HDL‐C (mg/dL) (predictor), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (mg/dL), HbA1c (%), systolic blood pressure (mm Hg), and body mass index (kg/m2). Cluster variance estimates adjusting for within‐subject correlation of repeated measures were used.

Analyses stratified by sex, previous CVD at baseline, race (White or Black), glycemic control group (standard or intensive), and baseline triglycerides in the highest tertile (≥204 or <204 mg/dL, as per the original ACCORD study 1 ) were performed separately for the primary CAD outcome in each phenotype group. Interactions were tested between HDL‐C and Hp phenotype, and then when stratified by Hp phenotype, between HDL‐C and sex, CVD history at baseline, race, glycemic treatment group, and baseline triglycerides. Follow‐up time was defined as the time from randomization to date of documented outcome or until they were censored at 7 years after randomization if no event occurred.

RESULTS

The distribution of Hp phenotype frequencies was 17.9% Hp1‐1, 46.2% Hp2‐1, and 35.9% Hp2‐2. The median follow‐up was 4.7 years in each of the Hp phenotype groups. Baseline characteristics that differed either between the phenotype groups or between HDL‐C categories (low versus not low HDL‐C) included age, sex, race profiles, family history of premature CVD, smoking status, blood pressure, alcohol consumption, education, history of CHF, body mass index, diabetes duration, baseline thiazolidinedione use, baseline antihypertensive medication use, baseline lipid‐lowering medication use, and baseline triglycerides (Table 1). HDL‐C levels did not differ between phenotype groups at any time point (Figure). HDL‐C (mg/dL) quintile 1 was ≤31 for male participants and ≤35 for female participants, quintile 2 was 32 to 36 for male participants and 36 to 41 for female participants, quintile 3 was 37 to 40 for male participants and 42 to 45 for female participants, quintile 4 was 41 to 45 for male participants and 46 to 51 for female participants, and quintile 5 was >45 for male participants and >51 for female participants.

In multivariable adjusted Cox models, low HDL‐C (<40 mg/dL for male participants and <50 mg/dL for female participants) was associated with a 48% higher risk of CAD compared with those without low HDL‐C among participants with the non‐Hp2‐2 phenotype (hazard ratio [HR], 1.48 [95% CI, 1.18–1.87]) but not among participants with the Hp2‐2 phenotype (HR, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.70–1.35]; P interaction=0.03) (Table 2). The results did not differ when Hp phenotype was stratified by history of CVD at baseline, glycemic treatment, and baseline triglycerides and among male and White participants. Among female participants with the non‐Hp2‐2 phenotype, low HDL‐C was associated with a 154% (HR, 2.54 [95% CI, 1.34–4.84]) greater risk of CAD when compared with participants without low HDL‐C. Among female participants with the Hp2‐2 phenotype, having low HDL‐C compared with non‐low HDL‐C was associated with a nonsignificant trend toward a lower CAD risk (HR, 0.54 [95% CI, 0.28–1.07], P interaction for sex=0.03). Among Black participants, having low‐HDL‐C compared with non‐low HDL‐C was associated with a nonsignificant trend toward a higher risk of CAD among participants with the non‐Hp2‐2 phenotype (HR, 1.60 [95% CI, 0.89–2.88) and a lower risk of CAD among participants with the Hp2‐2 phenotype (HR, 0.41 [95% CI, 0.10–1.65]). Low HDL‐C was also associated with a higher risk of major CVD (HR, 1.38 [95% CI, 1.07–1.79]) and nonfatal MI (HR, 1.53 [95% CI, 1.09–2.14]) events among participants with the non‐Hp2‐2 phenotypes and a higher risk of CVD mortality among participants with the Hp2‐2 phenotype (HR, 2.09 [95% CI, 1.05–4.13]) (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

Using longitudinal HDL‐C data from the ACCORD lipid study analyzed as achieved low HDL‐C (yes/no) concentrations over time, we found that low HDL‐C compared with non‐low HDL‐C was associated with a higher risk of CAD among participants with the non‐Hp2‐2 phenotype but not among participants with the Hp2‐2 phenotype. Accordingly, when HDL‐C was categorized into quintiles, a significant inverse relationship was observed between HDL‐C quintiles and incident CAD risk among participants without the Hp2‐2 phenotype, whereas there was no significant inverse relationship among participants with the Hp2‐2 phenotype. Similar results were observed for CVD and nonfatal MI events. Among the Hp2‐2 phenotype group, having low HDL‐C was associated with a greater risk of CVD mortality among participants with the Hp2‐2 phenotype, and compared with the lowest HDL‐C quintile, higher quintiles were associated with lower risk of CVD mortality and CHF.

Early epidemiological studies have consistently demonstrated an inverse relationship between HDL‐C levels and CVD, and having low HDL‐C is considered to be a marker of increased CVD risk. 25 , 26 Paradoxically, there is a lack of evidence to support HDL‐C raising for CVD prevention, and more recent studies have reported that both CVD and mortality risk are increased at extremely high levels of HDL‐C. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 The results of the present study suggest there may be effect modification by Hp phenotype in the relationship between HDL‐C and incident CAD. Thus, raising HDL‐C may be more important for CAD prevention in people without the Hp2‐2 phenotype than among people with the Hp2‐2 phenotype.

Our findings for the outcome of CAD overall are supported by 2 existing studies in the literature. In an intention‐to‐treat analysis, we observed that compared with statin monotherapy, adding fenofibrate (HDL‐C raising and triglyceride lowering drug) to statin therapy reduced the risk of CAD events among ACCORD lipid study participants with the non‐Hp2‐2 phenotype (HR, 0.74 [95% CI, 0.60–0.90]) but not among those with the Hp2‐2 phenotype (HR, 1.16 [95% CI, 0.87–1.56]; P interaction=0.009). 15 Further, in a subset of participants with diabetes in the AIM‐HIGH (Atherothrombosis Intervention in Metabolic Syndrome With Low HDL/High Triglycerides: Impact on Global Health Outcomes) study, Asleh et al found a higher proportion of participants with dysfunctional pro‐oxidant HDL in the Hp2‐2 phenotype compared with the Hp1‐1 phenotype at baseline, and niacin improved HDL antioxidant function in individuals with Hp1‐1 but worsened HDL antioxidant function in individuals with the Hp2‐2 phenotype despite the fact that HDL‐C levels did not change drastically throughout the study. 27

Our CAD findings from the present study are also supported by the body of literature investigating the biological mechanism for the relationship between Hp phenotype and risk of CAD in diabetes. 9 , 13 , 14 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 In brief, it is well documented that people with the Hp2‐2 phenotype produce an Hp protein that is larger and less functional than the Hp2‐1 and Hp1‐1 proteins at removing hemoglobin (Hb) from blood (a primary function of Hp alongside Hp's function as an antioxidant). Hp2‐2 function is further impaired when Hb is glycated (hyperglycemia), resulting in many glycated Hb‐Hp complexes that are dysfunctional as antioxidants circulating in people with the Hp2‐2 phenotype and hyperglycemia. 9 , 13 , 29 , 31 , 32 These Hp2:HbA1c complexes become pro‐oxidant when they bind to and oxidize HDL, thereby decreasing the functions of HDL, paradoxically turning the HDL into a proatherogenic molecule and thus increasing susceptibility to atherosclerosis, deterioration of cardiac function, and ultimately CAD. 13 , 14 , 28 , 29 , 30 Therefore, the biology of Hp and HDL suggests that higher serum HDL‐C would not be beneficial for CAD prevention among individuals with the Hp2‐2 phenotype and hyperglycemia, which is what we observed in the present study in which low HDL‐C compared with non‐low HDL‐C was associated with a greater CAD risk among those without the Hp2‐2 phenotype (those with the Hp1‐1 or Hp2‐1 phenotypes) only, in whom Hp and HDL function are better preserved. Further, our analysis of the HDL‐C quintiles revealed that increasing HDL‐C may be beneficial for CAD prevention in the Hp2‐2 phenotype only at very low concentrations of HDL‐C (as in quintile 2). Raising HDL‐C above a certain point (such as the concentrations in HDL‐C quintiles 3–5) may not have an added CAD benefit for people with the Hp2‐2 phenotype.

The sex differences we observed are in accordance with our previous finding in the ACCORD clinical trial that when compared with statin monotherapy, fenofibrate in combination with statin therapy increased the risk of CAD events among female participants with the Hp2‐2 phenotype. 15 The role of endogenous sex hormones and gender on lipid metabolism and cardiometabolic disease is not yet well understood 33 , 34 ; however, a potential explanation for our findings in female participants could be that postmenopausal metabolic changes may trigger chronic inflammation over time that could further alter the quality of HDL. 35 , 36 In MESA (Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis), Khoudary et al found a positive association between HDL‐C and plaque formation in postmenopausal female participants. 35 Given the older age of the ACCORD lipid cohort, HDL‐dysfunction due to menopausal metabolic changes may have had a synergistic effect with HDL‐dysfunction because of having the Hp2‐2 phenotype in hyperglycemia. However, the negative relationship between low HDL‐C and CAD was pronounced in female participants without the Hp2‐2 phenotype, and specific menopausal status information was not collected in the ACCORD lipid study. Another potential explanation is that female participants have naturally higher levels of HDL‐C than male participants, 37 and thus female participants with the Hp2‐2 phenotype in this study would have theoretically had the highest serum concentration of oxidated HDL‐C. Race is a social construct and reflects inequities in access to and quality of care that are a result of systemic racism. 38 , 39 Thus, social rather than biological factors are likely contributors to our findings of a trend toward greater harm among Black participants.

We did not expect to find that having higher HDL‐C would be associated with a lower risk of CVD mortality and CHF among people with the Hp2‐2 phenotype. A possible explanation for these findings is survivor bias, because the Hp2‐2 phenotype has been associated with CVD mortality and morbidity in people with diabetes. 40 , 41 However, in a sensitivity analysis where we stratified our CVD mortality and CHF results by age (<65‐year cut‐point) and history of CVD at baseline, we did not see evidence of survivor bias (Tables S1 and S2). We also unexpectantly observed that the relationship between time‐varying achieved HDL‐C and CAD did not differ by glycemic control group within each phenotype. However, the frequency of the Hp2‐2 phenotype differs among race‐based and geographic populations, 10 and based on previous findings in the entire ACCORD cohort, 42 the results would ideally have been stratified into race‐based groups had sample size allowed to avoid potential population structure influences.

Limitations

A major limitation of this study was that the participants in the ACCORD lipid arm had to have relatively low levels of HDL‐C at baseline, and HDL‐C levels increased by only a few mg/dL on average during the study (Figure 1), so it is unknown if the current results apply to wider or higher serum HDL‐C concentration ranges. Another limitation of this study was that data on HDL function, HDL‐associated proteins (ie, apolipoprotein A1, lecithin cholesterol acyltransferase, etc) or other markers of inflammation that would help to further investigate the proposed biological mechanism in future studies, were not measured. Furthermore, our study is observational in nature, and there may be unmeasured confounders that could influence the association between HDL‐C and cardiovascular events, such as diet and physical activity. We were unable to measure Hp phenotype for 522 (9.5%) participants due to lack of available serum sample; these participants had a higher percentage of previous CVD baseline compared with the rest of the participants (data not shown). Finally, participants were middle‐aged and elderly individuals at a high risk for CVD who were mostly non‐Hispanic White male participants, who do not adequately represent the greater population. Data on gender were not available. It remains unknown whether these results are generalizable across different race‐based, age, and gender groups.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, this study reports that low HDL‐C was inversely associated with risk of CAD among participants with the non‐Hp2‐2 phenotype only (and not among participants with the Hp2‐2 phenotype) in the ACCORD lipid study. Participants with and without the Hp2‐2 phenotype had similar HDL‐C concentrations throughout the present study, suggesting that the level of HDL function or dysfunction (rather than HDL concentration) is the likely mechanism linking HDL‐C to risk of CAD. These results suggest that Hp phenotype may modify the relationship between HDL‐C and incident CAD, thereby providing a potential explanation for the inconclusive results of randomized controlled trials of HDL‐C raising therapies previously reported that did not account for Hp phenotype. Further research that investigates this relationship across a broader range of HDL‐C is warranted to see whether the relationship between higher levels of HDL‐C and CAD is dependent on Hp phenotype. The current clinical consensus is that HDL‐raising therapy improves lipid profiles, but there is not yet conclusive evidence about how changes in lipid profiles from HDL‐raising therapy relate to CVD prevention in diabetes, and Hp phenotype may be important to incorporate into such future research.
