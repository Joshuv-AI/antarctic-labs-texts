Pharmacokinetics and safety of nafamostat and its metabolites following single- and multiple-dose oral administration of nafamostat mesylate in healthy male participants
Authors: Jun Ki Hong, Hyun Chul Kim, In-Jin Jang, SeungHwan Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNafamostat mesylate, a serine protease inhibitor, is a potential antiviral agent that inhibits the intracellular entry of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2. An oral formulation is under development to improve patient compliance over the existing intravenous formulation. This study evaluated the pharmacokinetics (PKs) and safety of oral nafamostat mesylate following single and multiple doses in healthy male participants. An open-label, single-dose and multiple ascending dose (MAD) study was conducted. In the single-dose study, 8 participants were administered 200 mg of nafamostat mesylate. In the MAD study, 8 participants in each dose group received nafamostat mesylate 100, 200, or 400 mg every 6 hours (QID) for 7 days. PKs were assessed for nafamostat in the single-dose study, and for nafamostat, 6-amidino-2-naphthol (AN) and 4-guanidinobenzoic acid (GBA) in the MAD study. The safety and tolerability were evaluated throughout the study. The median time to maximum concentration at steady state was 0.50–0.88 hours for nafamostat, 1.75–2.00 hours for AN, and 2.50–3.50 hours for GBA. Terminal elimination half-life ranged 0.61–3.84, 21.24–31.44, and 8.28–14.70 hours respectively. The accumulation ratio of nafamostat was 23.86 at 400 mg QID, with no accumulation at 100 and 200 mg QID. The mean accumulation ratios were 7.43–13.73 for AN and 1.84–2.11 for GBA. Nafamostat exposure showed a more than dose-proportional increase, whereas metabolites exposures were dose-proportional. No serious adverse events were reported. Multiple doses of oral nafamostat mesylate were well-tolerated up to 400 mg QID in healthy male participants.Trial RegistrationClinical Research Information Service Identifier: KCT0005647Keywords: Nafamostat, Oral Administration, Pharmacokinetics, Safety

INTRODUCTION

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), resulted in a global public health crisis and the death of millions of people [1,2]. Although vaccines have been developed and widely distributed, COVID-19 remains a global threat. It continues to cause fatalities across the world. SARS-CoV-2 is cleaved by the respiratory epithelial cellular enzyme, transmembrane protease serine subtype 2 (TMPRSS2), facilitating membrane fusion and the entry of the virion into the host respiratory epithelial cell. TMPRSS2 plays a crucial role in viral entry and is considered a promising drug target for coronaviruses, making it a key focus for antiviral therapeutic development [3,4,5,6].

Nafamostat mesylate is a synthetic serine protease inhibitor known for its anticoagulant, anti-inflammatory, mucolytic, and antiviral effects. Since the 1980s, intravenous (IV) formulation of nafamostat mesylate has been widely used in Japan and South Korea for the treatment of acute pancreatitis and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) as an anticoagulant in extracorporeal hemofiltration and dialysis [7].

Beyond its established clinical applications, nafamostat has also attracted attention as a potential therapeutic agent against SARS-CoV-2. In vitro studies have shown that nafamostat inhibits TMPRSS2, which prevents coronaviruses from attaching to the host cell membrane, thereby inhibiting their intracellular entry, including SARS-CoV, MERS-CoV, and SARS-CoV-2 [8]. Given that nafamostat is rapidly cleared from systemic circulation, understanding its metabolic fate is clinically relevant when considering its antiviral potential [9,10,11,12]. Nafamostat mesylate undergoes rapid hydrolysis mediated by carboxylesterase (CES1 and CES2) and arylesterase (PON1), yielding the metabolites 6-amidino-2-naphthol (AN) and 4-guanidinobenzoic acid (GBA) [9,10,11,13]. In vitro evidence indicates that both nafamostat and its metabolites bind to the S1 substrate-binding pocket of serine proteases and inhibit enzymatic activity, suggesting that the metabolites may also contribute to TMPRSS2 inhibition observed for the parent compound [3,14].

Transitioning from IV to oral formulation has been shown to improve treatment convenience and reduce healthcare burden across multiple therapeutic classes [15]. Nafamostat has an extremely short elimination half-life (t1/2) of approximately 8–10 minutes, which has necessitated continuous IV infusion and resulted in its clinical availability being limited to an IV formulation [12,16]. To address these limitations and improve dosing convenience, the development of an oral formulation is being explored. A recent clinical investigation by prior investigators (NCT04406415) has been initiated to evaluate multiple oral liquid formulations of nafamostat mesylate across different dosing regimens. Differed to previous study, the present study investigated a solid tablet formulation of nafamostat mesylate after a single dose as well as multiple doses with four times a day, characterizing the PK of nafamostat and its metabolites to further assess the feasibility of oral administration and enhance its clinical applicability.

This study aimed to evaluate the safety, tolerability, and PK characteristics of nafamostat and its metabolites, AN and GBA, following a single dose and multiple doses of oral nafamostat mesylate in healthy male participants.

METHODS

Subjects and study design

This study was approved by Ministry of Food and Drug Safety and the Institutional Review Board at Seoul National University Hospital (H-2011-031-1169) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Korean Good Clinical Practice (Clinical Research Information Service, CRIS No. KCT0005647). The objective and contents of the study were explained to each participant, and written informed consent was obtained prior to any study-related procedures.

The study was an open-label clinical trial consisting of a single-dose study followed by a multiple ascending dose (MAD) study. In the single-dose study, 8 participants were orally administered 200 mg nafamostat mesylate once. In the MAD study, each group of 8 participants were orally administered 100 mg, 200 mg, or 400 mg of nafamostat mesylate every 6 hours daily (QID; at 9 AM, 3 PM, 9 PM, and 3 AM) for 7 days. Participants fasted for at least 10 hours prior to the first dose, and nafamostat mesylate was administered under fasted conditions. Participants remained fasted for 4 hours after dosing in the single-dose study and after the first and the last dose in the MAD study, while a 2-hour post-dose fast was required for all other dosing occasions. Consistent with this restriction, water intake was allowed starting 2 hours after the dose.

Healthy male participants aged 19 to 50 years old with a body mass index (BMI) ranging from 19 to 30 kg/m2 were enrolled. Their eligibility for the study was evaluated based on health status including medical history, vital signs, physical examinations and clinical laboratory tests. Participants with hypersensitivity to nafamostat mesylate or other drugs, such as aspirin or antibiotics, a history of clinically significant allergic reactions, or a history of gastrointestinal disease or surgery were excluded. Participants were excluded if screening tests demonstrated any of the following: serum aspartate aminotransferase or alanine aminotransferase levels greater than 1.5 times the upper limit of normal (ULN); total bilirubin greater than 1.5 × ULN; serum creatine phosphokinase greater than 1.5 × ULN; or an estimated glomerular filtration rate less than 90 mL/min/1.73 m2 as calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease equation.

Plasma drug concentration analysis

Serial blood samples were obtained for PK analysis of nafamostat and its metabolites, AN and GBA at 0 (pre-dose), 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.25, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, and 24 hours after dosing in the single-dose study. In the MAD study, blood samples were obtained for PK analysis of nafamostat and its metabolites at 0 (pre-dose), 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.25, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, and 6 hours after the first dose on day 1, and 0 (pre-dose), 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.25, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, and 24 hours on day 7 after the last dose at steady state. In addition, blood samples were obtained at 12 hours (pre-dose) and 18 hours (pre-dose) on day 6.

In single-dose study, about 7 mL of blood was collected into tubes containing the anticoagulant ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-K2 for each sampling point. The plasma was obtained by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4°C, then aliquoted into the Eppendorf tubes and stored at −70°C until analysis. In the MAD study, procedures differed in that sample stabilization was implemented because plasma nafamostat concentrations in the single-dose study were predominantly below the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) or not detected at most time points, which limited pharmacokinetic characterization. Specifically, 350 μL of 0.5 M citric acid (5% of the anticipated blood volume) was added to EDTA-K2 tubes prior to blood collection. After plasma separation, an additional 35 μL of 0.5 M citric acid was added to each plasma aliquot before storage at −70°C. The stabilizer solution was prepared by dissolving 9.606 g of citric acid in 100 mL of purified water to obtain a 0.5 M concentration.

Plasma concentrations of nafamostat and its metabolites were determined by a liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) using an AB SCIEX API 5000 mass spectrometer (Applied Biosystems/MDS Sciex, Foster City, CA, USA). In the single-dose study, nafamostat was quantified, whereas in the MAD study, nafamostat and its metabolites, AN and GBA, were quantified. The LLOQ of nafamostat was 2 μg/L in single-dose study and 50 ng/L in the MAD study, while the LLOQs of its metabolites, AN and GBA, were 0.2 μg/L and 0.5 μg/L, respectively, in the MAD study.

Pharmacokinetic assessment

PK assessments were conducted respectively following the completion of the single-dose study and the MAD study. Plasma PK parameters were estimated by non-compartmental methods using Phoenix WinNonlin® (Version 8.3; Pharsight, Mountain View, CA, USA). The assessed PK parameters for nafamostat and its metabolites on day 1 included the time to maximum plasma concentration (Tmax), maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), and area under the concentration–time curve during the dosing interval (AUCτ). On day 7, the evaluated parameters included the time to maximum plasma concentration at steady state (Tmax,ss), maximum plasma concentration at steady state (Cmax,ss), area under the concentration–time curve during the dosing interval at steady state (AUCτ,ss) calculated using the linear-up/log-down trapezoidal method, elimination half-life at steady state (t1/2,ss), apparent oral clearance at steady state (CLss/F), apparent volume of distribution at steady state (Vd,ss/F), the accumulation ratio (AUCτ,ss/AUCτ), and metabolic ratios calculated as the ratio of AUCτ,ss for each metabolite to AUCτ,ss for nafamostat.

Safety and tolerability assessments

Safety and tolerability were evaluated throughout the study based on adverse events (AEs), physical examinations, vital signs, 12-lead electrocardiograms (ECGs) and clinical laboratory tests.

Statistical analysis

SAS® (Version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) was used for statistical analysis. In the MAD study, dose-normalized Cmax,ss and AUCτ,ss (Cmax,ss/Dose and AUCτ,ss/Dose) were compared among the three dose groups using the Kruskal-Wallis test. In addition, dose-proportionality of nafamostat and its metabolites were evaluated using a power model based on linear regression between logarithm-transformed Cmax,ss and AUCτ,ss and logarithm-transformed dose, expressed as lnY = α + β · ln(Dose), where Y is the PK parameter, α is the intercept, and β is the dose-proportionality coefficient. If the 95% confidence interval (CI) of the estimation included 1, dose-proportionality was concluded.

RESULTS

Study population

In the single-dose study, a total of 8 participants completed the study. In the MAD study, 8 participants in each dose group completed the study, for a total of 24 participants (Fig. 1). The values of age, height, weight, and BMI of the 8 participants in the single-dose study were 29.0 ± 5.9 years, 175.4 ± 5.4 cm, 71.2 ± 8.9 kg, and 23.1 ± 2.4 kg/m2 (mean ± standard deviation), respectively. For 24 participants in MAD study, these values were 31.3 ± 6.6 years, 172.6 ± 5.9 cm, 70.3 ± 9.4 kg, and 23.5 ± 2.2 kg/m2, respectively. There were no differences in demographic characteristics between dose groups in the MAD study (Table 1).

Pharmacokinetics parameters of nafamostat and its metabolites

In the single-dose study, plasma concentrations of nafamostat in the 200 mg dose group were detectable at only one sampling time point in 2 out of 8 participants, and PK analyses were not performed.

In the MAD study, nafamostat showed median Tmax,ss values of 0.50–0.88 hours at steady state. The t1/2,ss ranged from 0.61 to 3.84 hours, and mean accumulation ratios were 0.69, 1.12, and 23.86 at 100 mg, 200 mg, and 400 mg QID, respectively following multiple dosing (Fig. 2A, Table 2). For the metabolite AN, the median Tmax,ss was 1.75–2.00 hours at steady state (Fig. 2B, Table 2). The t1/2,ss values ranged from 21.24 to 31.44 hours, with mean accumulation ratios between 7.43 and 13.73 across dose levels at steady state. The mean metabolic ratios for AN were 2,592, 3,800, and 294 at 100 mg, 200 mg, and 400 mg QID, respectively. At steady state, GBA exhibited median Tmax,ss values of 2.50–3.50 hours, t1/2,ss values ranging from 8.28 to 14.70 hours and mean accumulation ratios ranged from 1.84 to 2.11 with the metabolic ratios of 11,796, 9,332, and 1,026 at 100 mg, 200 mg, and 400 mg QID, respectively (Fig. 2C, Table 2).

Following multiple dosing, statistically significant differences in dose-normalized Cmax,ss and AUCτ,ss across dose groups were observed for nafamostat and GBA, while AN showed no significant differences (Fig. 3). The steady state systemic exposures (Cmax,ss and AUCτ,ss) of nafamostat showed more than dose-proportional increases over the nafamostat mesylate 100–400 mg QID dose range. Based on the power model analysis, the 95% CIs of the slopes were 1.23–4.54 for Cmax,ss and 1.73–4.02 for AUCτ,ss. In contrast, the steady state systemic exposures of the metabolites, AN and GBA, showed dose-proportional increases over the nafamostat mesylate 100–400 mg QID dose range. For AN, the 95% CIs of the slope in the power model for Cmax,ss and AUCτ,ss were 0.89–1.37 and 0.94–1.28, respectively, and the corresponding values for GBA were 0.96–1.41 and 0.99–1.37, respectively (Supplementary Table 1).

Safety and tolerability

In the single-dose study, no treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were reported (Table 3). In the MAD study, overall, 23 TEAEs were reported in 7 participants (29.2%, 7/24), whereas no TEAEs were reported in the single-dose study (0.0%, 0/8). No TEAEs occurred in the 100 mg QID group, whereas TEAEs were reported in the 200 mg QID group (4 participants, 50.0%, 13 events) and in the 400 mg QID group (3 participants, 37.5%, 10 events). The most frequently observed events were diarrhea (18.3%), pyrexia (12.5%), and headache (12.5%) (Table 3). No serious AEs occurred during the study. In other safety assessments including physical examinations, vital signs, ECGs, and clinical laboratory tests, there were no clinically significant changes from baseline throughout the study.

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the PKs and safety of nafamostat and its metabolites, AN and GBA, following single- and multiple-doses of oral nafamostat mesylate in healthy participants. Previous clinical investigations have provided limited PK characterization of nafamostat metabolites. Notably, the DEFINE trial, a phase Ib/IIa, open-label, randomized, controlled study in patients with COVID-19 pneumonia, represents the only prior clinical study in which nafamostat and GBA concentrations were measured [17]. In contrast, this study simultaneously characterized the PKs of nafamostat, AN, and GBA, all of which exhibit antiviral potential, following oral administration of nafamostat mesylate. The pharmacokinetic findings obtained in this study are expected to support investigations of exposure response relationships and to inform the development of pharmacokinetically guided dose optimization strategies for nafamostat mesylate and its metabolites in the treatment of COVID-19.

In the MAD study, systemic exposure to nafamostat increased more than proportionally with dose, whereas the metabolites, AN and GBA, showed dose-proportional increases in systemic exposure over the nafamostat mesylate 100–400 mg QID dose range. The nonlinear accumulation observed for nafamostat may be related to its inherently low oral bioavailability and rapid metabolic clearance, which result in low systemic concentrations. Under such conditions, plasma concentrations frequently may fall near or below the LLOQ, leading to systematic underestimation of the actual concentrations. Consequently, the apparent exposure may deviate from dose-proportional expectations as the dose increases.

Previous literature has described hyperkalemia, hyponatremia, thrombocytopenia, and hepatic dysfunction as clinically significant AEs following IV administration of nafamostat mesylate [18,19,20]. In contrast, in this study of oral administration of nafamostat mesylate, TEAEs observed were diarrhea, pyrexia, and headache, all of which were generally mild in intensity. Specifically, the occurrence of diarrhea in this study is considered to be attributable to the excipient microcrystalline cellulose contained in the investigational product [21]. The absence of AEs previously reported with IV administration, along with the distinct pattern of TEAEs observed with the oral formulation in this study, may support the safety profile of oral nafamostat across the 100–400 mg QID dose range.

The therapeutic target concentration of nafamostat for SARS-CoV-2 inhibition has not been established. In vitro, the half maximal effective concentration (EC50) of nafamostat for inhibition of SARS-CoV-2 infection in human lung epithelial cells has been reported to range from 2.7 to 5.4 μg/L [8,14]. In clinical practice, continuous IV infusion of nafamostat mesylate at 0.1–0.2 mg/kg/hour for the treatment of DIC has resulted in steady state plasma concentrations of 14–130 μg/L [14]. At steady state under the nafamostat mesylate 400 mg QID dosing regimen in this study, the trough, mean, and peak plasma concentrations were 0.14, 4.72, and 52.34 μg/L, respectively. The trough concentration remained below the EC50, whereas the mean and peak concentrations were above this value, suggesting that effective antiviral exposure could be achieved. Notably, the peak concentration achieved under the nafamostat mesylate 400 mg QID dosing regimen was within the range of concentrations observed during continuous IV infusion of nafamostat mesylate for the treatment of DIC, indicating that this oral dosing regimen can achieve plasma exposures comparable to those previously attainable in clinical settings. However, direct comparison of the intermittent peak concentrations after oral administration to the steady-state concentrations achieved during continuous infusion should be interpreted with caution, as the PK profiles differ. Considering the PK properties of nafamostat, AN, and GBA together with their inhibitory activity against serine proteases, a more comprehensive investigation into the relationship between metabolite exposure and antiviral efficacy will be important to further refine its therapeutic potential [17,22].

The frequent occurrence of nafamostat concentrations below the LLOQ in the single-dose study is primarily attributed to its inherent instability and rapid ex vivo hydrolysis by esterases. To mitigate this issue, a citric acid stabilization procedure was implemented in the MAD study, which significantly improved the quantification of nafamostat and its metabolites.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the enrollment was restricted to healthy male participants to minimize physiological variability and ensure safety of participant in the first clinical study of the new oral formulation. But it may limit the generalizability of these findings to whole population. Second, the relatively small sample size in each dosing cohort may have limited the statistical power for a more robust characterization of the PK. Third, the lack of stabilization procedures in the single-dose study resulted in plasma concentrations frequently below the LLOQ, precluding a comprehensive PK characterization.

Furthermore, a significant limitation remains that this study was conducted in healthy adults. Patients with COVID-19 often present with pronounced systemic inflammation, altered organ function, and reduced metabolic capacity, which can lead to PK profiles that differ substantially from those observed in healthy individuals [23]. In particular, severe COVID-19 has been associated with hepatic and renal dysfunction, hemodynamic changes, altered plasma protein binding, and downregulation of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters, all of which may affect the systemic exposure of nafamostat and its metabolites [23,24,25]. Consequently, the PK profile observed in this study may not fully reflect the drug’s behavior under the complex physiological conditions present in COVID-19. Therefore, additional PK studies in patient populations are considered to confirm whether these findings translate to clinical settings and to inform appropriate dose selection and safety evaluation.

A new oral formulation of nafamostat was well tolerated up to 400 mg QID. Nafamostat showed supra-proportional PK characteristics at 400 mg QID, whereas its metabolites, AN and GBA, showed dose-proportional PK characteristics across the 100–400 mg QID. These results support the further clinical development of oral nafamostat mesylate as a new treatment option for SARS-CoV-2.
