NSUN2 promotes leukemogenesis by ferritin heavy chain 1-mediated anti-ferroptosis and enhancing global protein synthesis in an m5C-dependent manner in B-acute lymphoblastic leukemia
Authors: Yigang Yuan, Min Li, Xuanyu Yu, Chen Meng, Shirui Yu, Xinyao Chen, Shixin Zhang, Liuzhi Shi, Xiaoyi Qin, Ziyang Huang, Chongyun Xing, Shanhu Qian, Bin Zhou, Shenmeng Gao

AbstractBackgroundNOP2/Sun RNA methyltransferase family member 2 (NSUN2) catalyzes 5-methylcytosine (m5C) modifications in tRNA, rRNA, and mRNA, playing critical roles in various malignancies. However, its expression and functional relevance in B-acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) remain largely undefined.MethodsLineage-negative progenitor cells from Nsun2 knockout (Δ/Δ) and wild-type (fl/fl) mice were used to generate BCR-ABL (P190)-driven murine B-ALL model. Furthermore, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed to identify NSUN2 targets. Knockdown of NSUN2 reduced m5C amounts and induced ferroptosis and apoptosis in B-ALL cells. Moreover, Nsun2 knockout significantly suppressed the leukemic burden and extended the OS in the murine B-ALL model in vivo. In addition, NSUN2 deficiency inhibited global protein synthesis in both human and murine B-ALL cells. In contrast, Nsun2 depletion had a minimal impact on ferroptosis and protein synthesis in murine pre-B (B220+) cells, suggesting selective leukemic dependency.ConclusionsOur results demonstrate that NSUN2 facilitates leukemogenesis by increasing FTH1 expression and enhancing global protein synthesis in an m5C-dependent manner. Background

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is an aggressive hematologic malignancy originating from transformed immature B or T cell progenitors and is associated with a substantial risk of relapse and poor prognosis [1, 2]. Although intensified multi-agent chemotherapy achieves complete remission in over 80% of adult ALL patients, approximately 50% of ALL patients eventually experience relapse. Over the past 20 years, advances in molecular diagnostics, disease biology, immunotherapy, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and supportive care have considerably improved clinical outcomes [3–5]. Nevertheless, the high relapse rate and severe long-term side effects limit further improvements in patient prognosis and quality of life [6, 7]. Consequently, there is an urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies [8].

RNA 5-methylcytosine (m5C) modifications have been detected in tRNA [9], mRNA [10], and ribosomal RNA (rRNA) [11]. Recent studies have shown that RNA m5C modifications influence mRNA stability, export, and splicing [12, 13]. Thus, dysregulated m5C modulations have been implicated in the pathogenesis of various human diseases, including hematologic malignancies [14]. As reported, RNA m5C modification is mainly catalyzed by the NSUN family. NSUN2, a conserved RNA methyltransferase, was initially characterized as a canonical tRNA and rRNA methyltransferase [15]. However, NSUN2 also induces m5C modifications on mRNA, stabilizing its expression [10, 16–18]. For example, NSUN2 enhances m5C modifications on growth factor receptor-bound protein 2 (GRB2) mRNA, promoting its stability in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma cells [17]. Recently, NSUN2 has been reported to facilitate the development of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) by enhancing the expression of phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH) and serine hydroxymethyltransferase 2 (SHMT2), two key enzymes in the serine/glycine biosynthesis pathway [19]. Despite these findings, the functional role of NSUN2 in B-ALL remains largely unexplored.

Ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1), the major ferritin subunit, is critical for iron homeostasis [20]. It facilitates intracellular iron storage by sequestering ferrous iron within ferritin nanocages and regulating its trafficking [21]. Loss of FTH1 elevates intracellular ferrous iron levels and triggers ferroptosis [22, 23], a new type of regulated cell death driven by lipid reactive oxygen species (lipid ROS) accumulation and membrane lipid peroxidation. Thus, FTH1 is a key modulator of ferroptotic sensitivity. Notably, FTH1 is highly expressed in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) patient samples and AML patients with high FTH1 expression correlate with poor response to cytarabine treatment [24]. High FTH1 expression is also associated with adverse outcomes in AML patients, suggesting that FTH1 may serve as a potential therapeutic target in leukemia [25]. Although FTH1 possesses anti-ferroptotic properties in AML and solid tumor cells, the function of FTH1 in B-ALL cells is unclear. Accordingly, whether NSUN2-mediated m5C modifications modulate ferroptosis by regulation of FTH1 in B-ALL cells remains unknown.

Here, our study reveals that NSUN2 is markedly overexpressed in B-ALL patient samples compared to NC samples. Functionally, NSUN2 facilitates leukemogenesis and anti-ferroptotic activity by catalyzing m5C modifications at the 3'UTR of FTH1 mRNA in an m5C-dependent manner, thereby enhancing its stability and expression. Furthermore, NSUN2 enhances global protein synthesis. Together, our results demonstrate that NSUN2 is critical for B-ALL cell survival, and targeting NSUN2 might provide a promising therapeutic strategy for B-ALL.

Material and methods

B-ALL cell lines, B-ALL patient samples, and normal hematopoietic cells

Human leukemic cell lines, including REH and NALM-6 (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) in a humidified 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. Human bone marrow (BM) mononuclear cells were obtained from newly diagnosed B-ALL patients using Ficoll-Paque density gradient (Invitrogen) and cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen until use. Human BM CD19+ cells were sorted by flow cytometry Aria II (Becton Dickinson, Mountainview, CA, USA) from B-ALL patients, and healthy donor-derived CD19+ cells were sorted as normal controls (NCs). B-ALL patient samples were cultured in RPMI 1640 or Stemspan medium with 10% FBS, SCF (50 ng/mL), IL-7 (10 ng/mL), and FLT3-L (50 ng/mL). All patient samples were collected according to the protocol from the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University, with informed consent obtained under an approved Institutional Review Board (KY2024-R207). The clinical characteristics of B-ALL patients are listed in Table S1.

Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from human and murine cells using TRIzol reagent (TaKaRa Bio, Tokyo, Japan) according to standard protocol [26]. All cells were lysed in TRIzol and shaken vigorously. Cells were incubated at room temperature for 15 min to confirm the complete cell lysis. Chloroform was added and thoroughly mixed with the TRIzol solution. After centrifugation at 15,000 × rpm for 15 min, the aqueous phase was carefully extracted and mixed with an equal volume of isopropanol. Total RNA was precipitated and washed with 75% ethanol after centrifugation at 12,000 × rpm for 10 min. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a DS-11 spectrophotometer (DeNovix, Wilmington, DE, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa) and amplified using TB Green Premix Ex Taq (TaKaRa) on a Q5 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Gene expression was quantified using the 2−ΔΔCT method. GAPDH (human) and β-actin (mouse) were used as endogenous controls. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Plasmid production

The wild-type coding sequences (CDS) of NSUN2 and FTH1 were amplified from BM mononuclear cells of a healthy donor and subcloned into the MSCV-puro and LVX-puro (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), respectively. Point mutations (C271A and C321A) in NSUN2 were introduced by site-directed mutagenesis using a commercial kit (Vazyme biotech, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) based on the MSCV-NSUN2 wild-type construct. Gene-specific short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting NSUN2 or FTH1 were synthesized and cloned into the pLKO.1 (Sigma-Aldrich). A scrambled shRNA sequence served as a negative control. All primer sequences are listed in Table S2. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing.

Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation was performed using an anti-m5C antibody. Protein G-Sepharose beads (ab193259, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) were incubated overnight at 4 °C with either anti-m5C antibody (ab10805, Abcam) or IgG isotype control (ab190475, Abcam) under rotation. B-ALL cells were cross-linked with UVC irradiation (400 mJ/cm2) and lysed in IP buffer (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 0.1% NP-40, 100 U/mL RNase inhibitor). Lysates were incubated with antibody-conjugated beads for 2 h at 4 °C under rotation. After immunoprecipitation, RNA was extracted and purified using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, USA). One-tenth of each sample was reserved as input. qRT-PCR was performed using both input and m5C-enriched RNA. Enrichment was calculated by normalizing the Cq value of the IP sample to that of the input. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Surface sensing of translation (SUnSET) assay [27]

Global protein synthesis was assessed using the SUnSET method. Cells were incubated with puromycin (10 μg/mL) for 10 min at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. Cells were then washed twice with RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS and re-cultured for an additional 50 min. Cells were harvested and subjected to Western blot analysis using an anti-puromycin antibody to detect nascent peptides.

Data availability

All RNA-seq data are publicly available on the Gene Expression Omnibus under accession number GSE299607.

Other procedures

CCK8, apoptosis assay, m5C blot, and other experiments are described in the Supplemental Methods.

Statistical analysis

All results from at least three independent experiments in vitro are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance between two groups was determined by Student’s t test. One two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test was performed to compare multiple groups of data. OS was analyzed by Kaplan–Meier analysis, and differences between survival curves were assessed by the log-rank test. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed by GraphPad Prism version 8.0.

Results

NSUN2 expression is higher in B-ALL cells compared with normal controls (NCs)

To investigate NSUN2 transcript levels in B-ALL, we initially analyzed publicly available datasets. In the Leukemia MILE study from BloodSpot [28], NSUN2 expression was significantly higher in bone marrow (BM) cells from untreated B-ALL patients than in healthy volunteer controls (NCs) (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, B-ALL cells harboring chromosomal translocations t(9;22) and t(12;21) exhibited higher NSUN2 expression compared with NC samples (Fig. 1B). Consistently, analysis of the GSE13159 dataset confirmed increased NSUN2 levels in B-ALL patient samples compared with NCs (Fig. 1C). We further validated these findings in our in-house cohort by qRT-PCR, analyzing 56 B-ALL patients and 12 NCs. NSUN2 expression was significantly upregulated in B-ALL patient samples than NCs (Fig. 1D). Similarly, NSUN2 levels were higher in 10 CD19+ B-ALL cells than in 6 normal CD19+ B cells (Fig. 1E). In the murine model dataset GSE56345, Nsun2 transcript abundance was also higher in pro-B-ALL cells than controls [29] (Fig. 1F). In addition, immunofluorescence (IF) staining showed stronger NSUN2 signals in CD19+ cells from two B-ALL patients relative to two CD19+ NC samples (Fig. 1G–H). Accordingly, Western blot analysis confirmed higher NSUN2 protein levels in BM cells from five CD19+ B-ALL patients compared to two CD19+ NC samples (Fig. 1I).

Furthermore, we employed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) using GSE154109 [30] to investigate NSUN2 expression in B-ALL-related cell subsets. The cell populations were grouped into six different cell types (Figure S1A), which were annotated using established markers of hematopoietic populations via an unsupervised clustering method [31]. CD8+ T cells, hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), B cells, and CD34+ pro-B cells had higher levels of NSUN2 in BM from seven B-ALL patients compared with four healthy volunteers (Figs. 1J-K and S1B). CD8+ T cells exhibited higher expression of NSUN2 compared with HSCs, B cells, and CD34+ pro-B cells (Figure S1B).

NSUN2 promotes B-ALL cell growth and survival

To elucidate the functional role of NSUN2 in human B-ALL, we knocked down NSUN2 expression using specific shRNAs in REH and NALM-6 cell lines (Fig. 2A-B). Knockdown of NSUN2 significantly inhibited proliferation as measured by CCK8 assays (Fig. 2C-D) and induced cell cycle arrest at the G0/G1 phase (Figs. 2E-F and S2A). Furthermore, knockdown of NSUN2 induced apoptosis (Figs. 2G-H and S2B) and activated cleavage of PARP (Fig. 2I), a caspase-3 substrate and a marker for apoptosis [32]. In addition, NALM-6 cells transduced with shNC or shNSUN2 were transplanted into NOD/SCID‑IL2Rγ (NSG) mice. NSUN2 knockdown substantially extended the OS in recipient NSG mice (Fig. 2J). Considering that NSUN2 catalyzes RNA m5C methylation [33], we measured m5C levels in B-ALL cells and found markedly reduced m5C content following NSUN2 knockdown compared to shNC controls (Fig. 2K).

Furthermore, primary CD19+ cells were isolated from two B-ALL patients and transduced with shNSUN2 or shNC. qRT-PCR confirmed efficient NSUN2 knockdown (Fig. 2L), substantially reducing cell viability in both B-ALL patient samples (Fig. 2M-N). Upon xenotransplantation into NSG mice, B-ALL patient samples with NSUN2 knockdown demonstrated significantly prolonged OS compared to control cells (Fig. 2O-P).

NSUN2 facilitates anti-ferroptotic activity by increasing FTH1 expression

To identify the potential targets of NSUN2, RNA-seq was performed in NALM-6 cells transduced with either shNC or shNSUN2. Ferroptosis and transcriptional misregulation in cancer were among the top 20 enriched pathways identified by Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (Fig. 3A). Heatmap and Fragments Per Kilobase of exon model per Million mapped fragment (FPKM) analysis highlighted dysregulation of ferroptosis-associated genes, including FTH1, ACSL4, CYBB, and ELOVL6 (Fig. 3B-C). Additionally, heatmap and FPKM analysis revealed dysregulation of transcriptional misregulation in cancer-associated genes, including PROM1, LMO2, RUNX2, and ATF5, in NSUN2-depleted NALM-6 cells compared with control cells (Fig. 3B and D). We confirmed that NSUN2 knockdown decreased the transcript expressions of FTH1, PROM1, LMO2, but not RUNX2 and ATF5, by qRT-PCR in NALM-6 cells (Fig. 3E). Given that FTH1, the major component of ferritin, exhibits anti-ferroptotic activity by regulating lipid peroxidation and Fe2+ level [22, 23], we focused further analyses on FTH1. Both transcript and protein levels of FTH1 were significantly reduced upon NSUN2 depletion compared to shNC controls (Fig. 3F-G). Notably, NSUN2 knockdown in REH cells did not alter transcript and protein levels of SLC7A11 and GPX4 (Figure S3A-B), two key anti-ferroptosis regulators [34, 35], indicating that NSUN2 regulates ferroptosis independent of SLC7A11-GPX4 signaling pathway in B-ALL cells.

Next, we assessed the potential induction of ferroptosis by NSUN2 knockdown in B-ALL cells. NSUN2 knockdown increased intracellular Fe2+ levels (Figure S4A). By contrast, NSUN2 knockdown modestly decreased glutathione (GSH) amounts (Figure S4B) in REH cells, but did not affect GSH in NALM-6 cells (Figure S4B). Also, NSUN2 knockdown did not affect malondialdehyde (MDA) contents in REH and NALM-6 cells (Figure S4C). These results suggest a moderate induction of ferroptosis by NSUN2 knockdown. Notably, combining NSUN2 knockdown with low-dose treatment of the ferroptosis-inducer RSL3 synergistically elevated lipid ROS level (Fig. 3H-I and Figure S4D), decreased GSH contents (Fig. 3J-K), and increased MDA accumulation (Fig. 3L-M) compared with NSUN2 knockdown or RSL3 treatment alone. These results indicate that NSUN2 depletion sensitizes B-ALL cells to ferroptosis induction.

Since GSH exhibits key anti-ferroptotic effects by safeguarding cells against oxidative damage, we subsequently explored whether GSH supplementation blocks ferroptosis induced by NSUN2 knockdown. Lipid ROS level was measured in B-ALL cells with NSUN2 knockdown or NSUN2 knockdown treated with GSH. GSH (1 mM) significantly blocked the increase in lipid ROS induced by NSUN2 knockdown (Figure S5A-B).

Overexpression of FTH1 rescues the phenotype induced by NSUN2 knockdown

To confirm whether FTH1 is a functional downstream target of NSUN2, NSUN2-depleted B-ALL cells were transduced with a vector overexpressing FTH1 or a blank control (Vec). Overexpression of FTH1 (Fig. 3N) partially restored cell proliferation (Fig. 3O-P) and apoptosis induced by NSUN2 knockdown (Fig. 3Q-R). In addition, overexpression of FTH1 partially blocked the increase in lipid ROS induced by NSUN2 knockdown (Figure S6A-B). Because NSUN2 knockdown enhances RSL3-induced ferroptosis, we explored whether overexpression of FTH1 rescues ferroptosis induced by NSUN2 knockdown combined with RSL3 treatment. As expected, FTH1 overexpression reversed NSUN2 knockdown combined with RSL3 treatment-induced decrease in GSH and increase in MDA levels (Figure S7A-B), suggesting that FTH1 mediates the anti-ferroptotic effects of NSUN2.

FTH1 knockdown facilitates ferroptosis and apoptosis in B-ALL cells

To investigate the role of FTH1 in cell survival, we knocked down FTH1 using specific shRNAs (Fig. 4A-B). FTH1 depletion substantially induced apoptosis in NALM-6 and modestly in REH cells (Fig. 4C-D). In addition, knockdown of FTH1 enhanced RSL3-induced cell death (Fig. 4E-F). While knockdown of FTH1 alone did not significantly elevate lipid ROS levels, it strongly potentiated the RSL3-induced increase in lipid ROS levels (Fig. 4G-H). Furthermore, FTH1 knockdown enhanced RSL3-induced decrease in GSH amounts (Fig. 4I-J). These findings demonstrate that FTH1 supports B-ALL cell survival by inhibiting both ferroptosis and apoptosis.

NSUN2 regulates FTH1 expression and affects leukemogenesis in an m5C-dependent manner

To determine whether NSUN2 regulates FTH1 expression via m5C methylation, NSUN2-depleted REH and NALM-6 cells were transduced with either blank control (Vec), wild-type (WT) NSUN2, or two catalytically inactive NSUN2 mutants (C271A and C321A) [36]. Western blot confirmed a successful WT and mutant NSUN2 protein overexpression (Fig. 5A). Only WT NSUN2, but not the mutants, blocked the downregulation of FTH1 protein (Fig. 5A) and transcript levels (Fig. 5B-C) induced by NSUN2 knockdown. Correspondingly, WT NSUN2, but not its two mutants, rescued the decreased cell proliferation (Fig. 5D-E) and the increased apoptosis (Fig. 5F) induced by NSUN2 knockdown. Furthermore, NSUN2 knockdown cells treated with RSL3 were transduced with WT or mutant NSUN2. WT NSUN2 restored GSH levels and reduced MDA accumulation, effects not observed with mutant NSUN2 (Fig. 5G-H), confirming that the m5C methyltransferase activity of NSUN2 is critical for its regulation of ferroptosis-related processes. In addition, NSUN2-depleted NALM-6 cells were transduced with either Vec, WT NSUN2, or its two mutants, followed by transplantation in NSG mice. Overexpression of WT, but not its mutants, blocked the extended OS induced by NSUN2 knockdown in recipient mice (Fig. 5I).

NSUN2 enhances the stability of FTH1 mRNA in an m5C-dependent manner

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which NSUN2 regulates FTH1 expression, we measured the stability of FTH1 mRNA in B-ALL cells following NSUN2 knockdown. Knockdown of NSUN2 significantly shortened the half-life of FTH1 mRNA in B-ALL cells (Fig. 6A-B). To explore the potential m5C-modified sites of FTH1 mRNA, luciferase (Luc) reporters containing the FTH1 5'UTR, coding sequence (CDS), or 3'UTR were constructed (Fig. 6C). NSUN2 knockdown significantly decreased the Luc activity of FTH1-3'UTR reporter, but not 5'UTR or CDS reporter (Fig. 6D). Conversely, overexpression of WT NSUN2, but not its two mutants, increased Luc activity of FTH1-3'UTR reporter (Fig. 6E). These results indicate that NSUN2 catalyzes m5C modifications on the 3'UTR of FTH1 mRNA. We further used RIP to confirm m5C modifications at FTH1 mRNA. The anti-m5C antibody significantly enriched FTH1 mRNA relative to anti-IgG antibody control, suggesting that m5C modifications occur in FTH1 mRNA (Fig. 6F). We used human β-actin (BACT) as negative control. However, anti-m5C antibody did not enrich BACT mRNA relative to anti-IgG antibody control (Fig. 6G). Furthermore, NSUN2 knockdown decreased, while NSUN2 overexpression increased m5C enrichment on FTH1 mRNA (Fig. 6H-I), supporting that NSUN2 directly catalyzes m5C methylation on FTH1 mRNA to enhance its stability.

NSUN2 promotes B-ALL maintenance and progression in vivo.

To investigate the role of NSUN2 in B-ALL in vivo, we utilized Nsun2 conditional knockout mice (Nsun2fl/fl CreERT2) to generate BCR-ABL (P190)-induced B-ALL model [37]. PCR assay confirmed the presence of Flox/Flox and CreERT2 alleles in mice #46, 47, and 48 (named as fl/fl) (Figure S8A). fl/fl mice were injected with TAM or corn oil, and BM cells were isolated for qRT-PCR. TAM treatment, but not corn oil, efficiently depleted Nsun2 expression in BM cells (Figure S8B), referred to as Nsun2 knockout mice (Δ/Δ). Lin− BM cells isolated from Nsun2fl/fl CreERT2 mice were transduced with BCR-ABL (P190) and transplanted into recipients (Figure S8C). TAM injection on days 15–20 after primary BMT induced genetic depletion of Nsun2 in murine GFP+ leukemic cells compared with corn oil treatment (Figure S8D and E). Flow cytometry revealed a lower percentage of GFP⁺ leukemic cells in the PB of Δ/Δ mice versus fl/fl controls (Figure S8F). Nsun2 depletion significantly prolonged overall OS in the primary BMT (Figure S8G).

BM GFP+ cells from primary recipients were isolated and retransplanted into secondary recipients (Figure S8H). Δ/Δ cells exhibited a markedly reduced proportion of GFP+ leukemic cells in PB and BM compared with fl/fl controls (Fig. 7A-B). Wright–Giemsa staining showed decreased leukemic infiltration in BM following Nsun2 depletion (Fig. 7C). Moreover, spleen and liver weights were reduced in Δ/Δ mice (Fig. 7D–F), accompanied by a decrease in leukemic infiltration in these organs (Fig. 7G). Colony formation assays revealed near-complete abolition of colonies by Nsun2 knockout (Fig. 7H). Consistent with these findings, Δ/Δ recipient mice exhibited significantly extended OS in secondary and tertiary BMT (Fig. 7I-J). To explore whether Nsun2 is required for the self-renewal of leukemia stem cells (LSC), the LDA assay was performed by transplanting various doses of fl/fl and Δ/Δ cells into recipients. Nsun2 depletion impaired LSC self-renewal capacity, with a functional stem cell frequency of 1/1475 in Δ/Δ cells versus 1/374 in fl/fl cells (Fig. 7K, Table S3). Also, Δ/Δ cells exhibited lower Fth1 levels than fl/fl cells (Fig. 7L-M).

NSUN2 facilitates global protein synthesis in human and murine B-ALL cells

Although FTH1 was identified as a key target, overexpression of FTH1 only partially rescued the cytotoxicity caused by NSUN2 depletion, suggesting additional mechanisms. The rapid tumor proliferation, including in leukemia, demands to synthesize multiple proteins [38]. NSUN family members have been implicated in regulating protein synthesis [39]. Using the SUnSET assay, NSUN2 knockdown markedly reduced global nascent protein synthesis compared with shNC controls in REH and NALM-6 cells (Figure S9A). Consistent with human B-ALL cells, Nsun2 knockout in murine B-ALL cells reduced global protein synthesis as measured by decreased puromycin incorporation (Figure S9B). These data suggest that NSUN2 deficiency disrupts protein synthesis in B-ALL cells.

Nsun2 knockout does not significantly affect ferroptosis and global protein synthesis in normal BM cells.

Next, we explored whether Nsun2 could regulate ferroptosis and global protein synthesis in normal BM cells. Depletion of Nsun2 did not significantly affect total BM cellularity (Fig. 8A). Levels of GSH and MDA were comparable between fl/fl and Δ/Δ BM cells (Fig. 8B-C), indicating minimal influence of Nsun2 knockout on ferroptosis under physiological conditions. Further, colony formation assays of BM B220⁺ pre-B cells showed no differences in colony numbers or cell number of all colonies between fl/fl and Δ/Δ cells (Fig. 8D–F). Assessment of global protein synthesis revealed equal protein synthesis in Δ/Δ versus fl/fl B220⁺ cells (Fig. 8G). These findings demonstrate that Nsun2 is dispensable for ferroptosis and global protein synthesis in physiological conditions.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that NSUN2 promotes the survival of B-ALL cells by catalyzing m5C modifications on FTH1 mRNA, thereby enhancing FTH1 mRNA stability in an m5C-dependent manner. The NSUN2-FTH1 signaling axis contributes to leukemogenesis by inhibiting ferroptosis in B-ALL cells (Fig. 8H). Moreover, NSUN2 facilitates survival by enhancing global protein synthesis (Fig. 8I). Therefore, NSUN2-FTH1 signaling is required for the survival of B-ALL cells. Targeting NSUN2 provides a potential therapeutic target for B-ALL.

Our results demonstrate that NSUN2 knockdown decreases m5C contents in B-ALL cells. Overexpression of NSUN2 (WT), but not its mutants, almost rescues NSUN2 knockdown-induced cytotoxic effects and downregulation of FTH1. These results confirm that NSUN2 regulates leukemogenesis and FTH1 expression in an m5C-dependent manner, consistent with previous studies [17, 33]. Notably, NSUN2 expression is elevated in B-ALL cells compared to NCs, underscoring that NSUN2-mediated m5C modifications are essential for B-ALL cell survival, which is consistent with the report that NSUN2 facilitates leukemogenesis in acute myeloid cells [19, 40].

Recent studies indicate that inducing ferroptosis can overcome acquired drug resistance in therapy-resistant cancer cells [41, 42]. For instance, adriamycin-resistant leukemia cells express higher FTH1 levels than parental drug-sensitive cells, and dihydroartemisinin sensitizes resistant cells by downregulating FTH1 levels [43]. Several reports also demonstrate that NSUN2 inhibits ferroptosis by catalyzing m5C modifications on GPX4 mRNA, thus stabilizing GPX4 mRNA in hepatocytes [44]. In addition, NSUN2 enhances ferroptosis resistance via m5C modifications on SLC7A11 mRNA in endometrial cancer [45]. However, NSUN2 knockdown does not alter SLC7A11 and GPX4 expressions in B-ALL cells, suggesting that these two canonical ferroptosis regulators are not NSUN2 targets in this context. Instead, our study identifies FTH1 as a key NSUN2 target in B-ALL, indicating that NSUN2-dependent m5C modifications are context-dependent and vary across cell types. Several reports demonstrate that B-ALL cells are extremely sensitive to ferroptosis [46, 47], suggesting the therapeutic vulnerability of ALL by interrupting NSUN2-FTH1 signaling.

Our results demonstrate that depletion of Nsun2 in normal murine BM cells does not significantly induces ferroptosis. However, knockdown of NSUN2 robustly induces ferroptosis in B-ALL cells. These results are consistent with the reports that B-ALL cells are extremely sensitive to ferroptosis[46, 47]. B-ALL cells exhibit high lipid ROS and polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) levels in steady-state conditions [48], suggesting that B-ALL cells are under constant oxidative stress and do not have the adequate buffering capacity protecting them against oxidative stress. Furthermore, the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) was inhibited by B cell-specific transcriptional factor Ikaros family zinc finger protein 1 (IKZF1) and paired box 5 (PAX5) [49], leading to low contents of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate and GSH, which finally restrict their ability to cope with oxidative stress. Therefore, metabolic stress and oxidative stress result in the high sensitivity of B-ALL cells to ferroptosis.

Downregulation of FTH1 promotes ferroptosis by increasing iron release, labile iron pool, and ROS production in esophageal adenocarcinoma [50], demonstrating that FTH1 is a potential target of ferroptosis in cancer cells. Consistent with these findings, FTH1 knockdown induces ROS accumulation and ferroptosis in B-ALL cells. Hence, genetic or pharmacological inhibition of FTH1 represents a promising strategy to induce ferroptosis and kill cancer cells [51, 52]. Although FTH1 is identified as a direct target of NSUN2 in B-ALL cells, overexpression of FTH1 partially rescues the phenotype induced by NSUN2 knockdown, indicating the complicated regulatory net by NSUN2.

Although knockdown of FTH1 induces ferroptosis in both REH and NALM-6 cell lines, NALM-6 exhibits more sensitivity to ferroptosis than REH cells (Fig. 4). FTH1 regulates ferroptosis through modulating iron accumulation and oxidative stress such as lipid ROS. Knockdown of FTH1 combined with RSL3 induces higher lipid ROS level in NALM-6 than REH cells, probably leading to more ferroptosis in NALM-6 than REH cells. We speculate that different intracellular concentrations of Fe2+ and lipid ROS in REH and NALM-6 cell lines result in different effects in NALM-6 versus REH cell lines.

Rapid proliferation in tumor cells requires accelerated protein production, a characteristic feature of cancer cells. Our results reveal that NSUN2 supports global protein synthesis in human and murine B-ALL cells, thus maintaining rapid proliferation in B-ALL cells. In contrast, Nsun2 depletion has little effects on global protein synthesis in murine normal pre-B cells, indicating that targeting NSUN2 may exploit a unique vulnerability in B-ALL cells. The higher expression of NSUN2 in B-ALL cells compared to NCs further supports the notion that elevated NSUN2 expression is critical for sustaining rapid proliferation. Additionally, the slower cycling rate of progenitor cells may reduce their demands for protein synthesis [53], further explaining their resistance to Nsun2 loss. Although several reports demonstrate that NSUN2 regulates protein synthesis by catalyzing m5C modifications on tRNA and rRNA [54–56], the underlying mechanism by which NSUN2 enhances global protein synthesis in B-ALL cells requires further study.

In this study, we have identified the 3'UTR of FTH1 mRNA as the site of NSUN2-mediated m5C modifications. However, the precise m5C sites could not be pinpointed due to potential random degradation of mRNA during bisulfite treatment, which may cause omission of m5C sites. This highlights the need for more accurate and reliable methods for m5C mapping to understand m5C sites in mRNA fully. Moreover, patient cohort is limited and more B-ALL patient samples should be used in this study. Although three different shRNAs were used to decrease NSUN2 expression, the potential off-target effects of shRNA knockdown may still exist. The assessment of normal hematopoietic toxicity by Nsun2 knockout is very limited, and further study will investigate the role of Nsun2 in normal hematopoiesis and lymphocyte development. Finally, metabolic profiling is absent and more study about it will be performed in the future research.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings establish a critical role for NSUN2 in maintaining survival during B-ALL pathogenesis and reveal the NSUN2-FTH1 signaling pathway as a key driver of B-ALL leukemogenesis.
