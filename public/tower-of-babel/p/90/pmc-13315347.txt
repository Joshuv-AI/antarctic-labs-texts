Impact of Secondary Antibiotic Prophylaxis for Latent Rheumatic Heart Disease Over 5 Years
Authors: Anneke C Grobler, Joselyn Rwebembera, Andrea Beaton, Emma Ndagire, Emmy Okello, Jafesi Pulle, Craig Sable, Rachel Sarnacki, Alison Spaziani, Andrew Steer
Published: 2026

Rheumatic heart disease (RHD) affects >55 million people with a high burden in Uganda, where most patients present too late for effective intervention. 1 GOAL (Gwoko Adunu pa Lutino; 2018–2020) was the first randomized trial to show that secondary antibiotic prophylaxis (SAP) halted progression of early RHD, reducing risk from 8.2% to 0.8% over 2 years. 2 Although this transformed screening recommendations, 3 the optimal duration of SAP remains unknown. Existing SAP guidelines are decades old and not tailored to children with early, screen‐detected disease.

An extended 3‐year follow‐up study (“GOAL‐Post” 4 ) assessed the long‐term outcome of children with screen‐detected RHD who had completed 2 years of SAP versus not, followed by targeted treatment based on echocardiographic findings. GOAL‐Post aimed to refine SAP duration to optimize outcomes while reducing treatment burden.

In GOAL, a parallel group, open‐label, superiority trial, 916 Ugandan children aged 5 to 17 years with screen‐detected early RHD were randomized 1:1 to receive either 2 years of SAP using intramuscular benzathine penicillin G (BPG) or no SAP. 2 In GOAL‐Post, participants were stratified by their 2‐year echocardiogram results. Those with persistent early RHD received BPG for an additional 3 years, and those with normal findings were observed without treatment. 4 Both studies were approved by the institutional review boards at Makerere University School of Medicine (REC 2018–048) and Children's National Hospital (P000010408). Participants gave informed consent to participate.

In this analysis we combine GOAL and GOAL‐Post into 1 randomized comparison. We reconstruct a longer trial, analyzing the data as if GOAL originally had 5‐year follow‐up planned. All originally randomized participants were included. The data and analysis methods are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

This reconstructed design retained the original randomization as the foundation, comparing 2 treatment strategies: (1) SAP+: 2 years BPG, then tailored continuation based on echocardiographic findings; (2) Control+: 2 years no treatment, then the same tailored continuation. Patients who still had screen‐detected RHD received treatment, and patients who normalized stopped SAP. The primary 5‐year outcome was echocardiographic progression of RHD, defined by World Heart Foundation 2012 criteria: worsening valve regurgitation, emergence of new pathological regurgitation, or increased valve morphological abnormalities, including category changes (eg, borderline to definite RHD 5 ).

Echocardiograms were performed using standardized 13‐view protocols and reviewed by a blinded panel of 4 cardiologists. The panel interpreted baseline and 5‐year studies without knowledge of treatment allocation or time point.

No new sample size calculation was done. The reconstructed trial included all 818 participants in the modified‐intention‐to‐treat population from GOAL. We estimated the 5‐year risk difference and risk ratio in RHD progression using binomial identity or log‐link generalized linear models, adjusting for baseline RHD category. Analysis used available case and multiple imputation (50 imputations via multiple imputation by chained equations using a Gibbs‐like algorithm) assuming missing‐at‐random. The imputation model included treatment arm, outcome, baseline RHD category, age, sex, maternal education, skin infection, and family history of RHD or rheumatic fever.

From July to December 2018, 916 children were randomized in GOAL. Following expert echocardiographic review, 98 were excluded due to reclassification, leaving 818 in the modified‐intention‐to‐treat population (409 per arm). Baseline characteristics were similar across arms, mean age 12.6 (SAP+) and 12.5 years (control+). 2 Adherence and retention were high (98%).

In all, 799 participants (97.7%) completed GOAL; 687 (86.0%) were enrolled in GOAL‐Post. Thirty‐six had progressed to RHD during GOAL (3 SAP+, 33 control+) and were referred for care but included in the 5‐year outcome analysis. Outcome data were available for 340 (83.2%) in the SAP+ arm and 332 (81.2%) in the control+ arm (Figure1).

At 5 years, we observed a significantly lower risk of echocardiographic progression in SAP+ (15/340, 4.4%) versus control+ (47/332, 14.2%), risk difference −9.8% (95% CI, −14.1% to −5.4%; P<0.001; risk ratio, 0.31 [95% CI, 0.18 to 0.55]). Multiple imputation yielded similar results, 4.9% versus 14.3%, risk difference −9.5% (95% CI, −14.0 to −5.0%; P<0.001; risk ratio, 0.34 [95% CI, 0.19 to 0.59]). After SAP cessation, progression occurred in 3.5% of the SAP+ arm and 4.2% of the control+ arm. Less than 1% of children in each arm progressed to moderate or severe RHD after 5 years, suggesting 3‐year risk of clinically significant disease after echocardiographic normalization is low.

This reconstructed trial provides real‐world evidence to inform duration of SAP. A strategy of 2 years of SAP followed by discontinuation in children with normal echocardiograms led to sustained reduction in RHD progression at 5 years compared with no initial prophylaxis. Among children who initially regressed to normal at completion of GOAL and ceased SAP, recurrence rates of latent RHD were similar between the SAP+ and control arms, suggesting no rebound effect after cessation.

The extended follow‐up and dynamic treatment strategy were not prespecified and were informed by the favorable 2‐year trial results. The analysis should be interpreted as an emulation of a dynamic treatment strategy rather than a prespecified multistage trial.

These findings support the effectiveness of time‐limited SAP in children with latent RHD and normal follow‐up echocardiograms and inform refining prophylaxis duration to balance clinical benefit with treatment burden. A SAP‐discontinuation trial would provide higher quality evidence to guide SAP discontinuation policy after echocardiographic normalization in early RHD.
