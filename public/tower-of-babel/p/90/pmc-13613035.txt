Reconstruction of a chronic gastrocnemius tendon rupture using ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) suture tape and flexor digitorum lateralis augmentation in a dog
Authors: Yebin RYU, Hwi-Yool KIM, Yongsun KIM
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 5-year-old Poodle presented with a plantigrade stance three weeks after traumatic gastrocnemius tendon rupture. Surgical exploration confirmed a complete rupture; following debridement and tenorrhaphy, a 20 mm defect remained. This gap was bridged using a double-layer ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) suture tape and augmented with flexor digitorum lateralis (FDL) transposition. At 2 months, ultrasonography confirmed maintained tendon continuity without foreign body reaction. By 3 months, posture normalized and gait analysis showed near-symmetric hindlimb loading. No major complications occurred during the 12-month follow-up. Combined UHMWPE bridging and FDL augmentation is an effective surgical option for chronic gastrocnemius ruptures with substantial defects when primary apposition is not feasible.Keywords: chronic tendon rupture, common calcaneal tendon, flexor digitorum lateralis, gastrocnemius tendon rupture, ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene

Common calcaneal tendon (CCT) rupture is a frequently encountered orthopedic condition in dogs, often resulting from acute trauma or excessive mechanical loading [4, 14]. The CCT is composed of the tendinous insertions of the gastrocnemius tendon, the superficial digital flexor tendon (SDFT), and a conjoined tendon formed by the biceps femoris, semitendinosus, and gracilis muscles; among these, the gastrocnemius tendon is a major functional component of the calcaneal mechanism [5]. Complete rupture of gastrocnemius tendon can result in a characteristic plantigrade stance, an inability to support weight-bearing, and profound functional impairment [4, 10].

Standard surgical management typically requires the debridement of disorganized fibrous scar tissue followed by the primary tenorrhaphy of the tendon stumps, with or without adjunctive augmentation depending on the chronicity and degree of tendon loss [11, 13]. Maintaining precise apposition of the tendon ends is critical. In biomechanical studies, a tenorrhaphy gap exceeding 3 mm is commonly used as a clinically relevant failure threshold because increased gapping is associated with reduced mechanical integrity of the repair [6]. In chronic or delayed-presenting cases, retraction of the gastrocnemius musculotendinous unit and dense fibrous scar tissue can make tension-free end-to-end apposition nearly impossible. Consequently, substantial tendon defects in chronic ruptures necessitate augmentation strategies beyond primary suture repair.

Several augmentation techniques have been described, including autogenous grafts such as fascia lata or semitendinosus muscle and local tendon transpositions such as the flexor digitorum lateralis (FDL) tendon [1, 7, 13]. After primary suture, the repair site may be augmented with a muscle or tendon flap, and small residual defects can be bridged while providing additional biological reinforcement. In addition, synthetic implants, specifically ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) tapes, are increasingly utilized to bridge these structural gaps and enhance mechanical stability [3, 10].

In this report, we describe a successful surgical management of a chronic complete rupture of the gastrocnemius tendon component of the CCT with a large defect because of severe musculotendinous retraction. Our objective was to describe the clinical applicability of a dual-augmentation technique combining a UHMWPE synthetic implant with FDL tendon transposition, assessed through long-term clinical follow-up.

A 5-year-old, 4.6 kg, castrated male Poodle was referred for suspected CCT injury. The injury had occurred 3 weeks earlier and was suspected to have resulted from penetrating trauma to the left distal hindlimb during an outdoor walk. Upon clinical evaluation, the patient exhibited a characteristic plantigrade stance on the left hindlimb and moderate lameness (Fig. 1A). Physical examination revealed hyperflexion of the tarsocrural joint accompanied by palpable swelling and thickening around the CCT. The estimated gap between the retracted tendon stumps was approximately 40 mm. Based on the orthopedic examination and radiographic findings, a calcaneal mechanism injury with presumed rupture of the gastrocnemius tendon component was diagnosed (Fig. 1B). Definitive characterization of the ruptured tendon components was performed intraoperatively.

Surgical repair was conducted under general anesthesia. The patient was medicated with an intravenous injection of 4 mg/kg tramadol (Samsung Pharm, Hwasung, Korea), 0.2 mg/kg meloxicam (Boehringer Ingelheim, Ingelheim am Rhein, Germany), 0.02 mg/kg midazolam (Bukwang Pharm, Seoul, Korea). Induction was performed with an intravenous injection of 6 mg/kg propofol (Daewon Pharm, Seoul, Korea). Following tracheal intubation, anesthesia was maintained with isoflurane (Hana Pharm, Seoul, Korea) at 1.5 minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) throughout the procedure.

The patient was prepared for aseptic surgery and positioned in right lateral recumbency and the affected tarsocrural joint maintained in full extension. A caudolateral skin incision was made from the popliteal region extending distally to the calcaneus. Surgical exploration confirmed complete rupture of the gastrocnemius tendon with surrounding fibrous scar tissue. The SDFT remained grossly continuous; however, its tension appeared reduced on intraoperative manipulation. Following careful dissection to mobilize the tendon stumps, the fibrous tissue at the ends was excised. The proximal stumps, including portions of the gastrocnemius muscle-tendon unit, were secured using a locking-loop suture pattern with 3–0 polypropylene (Prolene; Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA), while the distal stump was advanced using a stay suture (Fig. 2A). Despite these efforts, a 20 mm defect remained between the stumps.

To bridge this persistent defect, a UHMWPE synthetic implant (FiberTape®; Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA) was applied in a double-layer configuration and secured to the adjacent musculotendinous units using simple interrupted polypropylene sutures (Fig. 2B). For biologic augmentation, the FDL tendon was carefully isolated from the tibia (Fig. 2C). The FDL tendon was transposed caudally as a pedicled graft and sutured over the gastrocnemius tendon repair site and synthetic implant using 3–0 polydioxanone (PDS II; Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA) in a simple interrupted pattern (Fig. 2D). The subcutaneous tissues and skin were routinely closed.

Prophylactic broad-spectrum antibiotics were administered, and analgesic treatment (meloxicam 0.1 mg/kg, PO, once a day) was provided from the day of surgery until approximately 7 days after surgery. Postoperatively, the affected limb was immobilized using a modified Robert Jones bandage for three weeks, and the patient’s physical activity was strictly restricted for six weeks. The coaptation was removed at 3 weeks because a clinical examination revealed a >50% decrease in flexion despite normal extension. During the early postoperative period, mild weight-bearing lameness remained, and the range of motion (ROM) of the tarsal joint was mildly improved to 150°/70° (extension/flexion) at 6 weeks. At the two-month follow-up, ultrasonographic examination confirmed that the structural continuity of the tendon at the surgical site was well maintained, with no evidence of an implant-associated foreign body reaction (Fig. 3).

By the three-month postoperative recheck, the patient exhibited a normal weight-bearing standing posture (Fig. 4A, 4B). The ROM of the tarsocrural joint had significantly improved, achieving a flexion angle of 60 degrees and an extension angle of 170 degrees. Limb function during the walking gait was objectively quantified using a force plate gait analysis system (CanidGait®, zebris Medical GmbH, Isny, Germany) at 4 weeks, 6 weeks, and 3 months postoperatively (Table 1). All reported kinetic values represent the mean of 7 valid walking trials. The objective kinetic data demonstrated progressive functional recovery over time. The peak vertical force (PVF) of the operated left hindlimb improved steadily from 20.4 N at 4 weeks to 23.2 N at 6 weeks, and reached 27.4 N by the 3-month follow-up. Similarly, the vertical impulse (VI) increased from 1.8 ± 0.9 N·sec at 4 weeks to 2.9 ± 0.6 N·sec at 3 months. By the 3-month recheck, the body weight distribution, PVF, and VI values during walking returned to near-normal physiological levels, demonstrating excellent symmetry with the contralateral unaffected limb (PVF: 27.5 N; VI: 3.3 ± 0.7 N·sec) (Fig. 4C). The patient successfully returned to all normal activities, and no other complications were observed during the 12-month follow-up period. A subsequent telephone consultation with the owner at 24 months postoperatively confirmed that the patient maintained a normal gait without any observable localized swelling, abnormal tissue formation, or apparent reduction in tarsal range of motion at the surgical site.

The present case demonstrates the clinical applicability of combining a UHMWPE synthetic implant with FDL tendon transposition for management of a chronic complete rupture of the gastrocnemius tendon component with a substantial tendon defect. In this patient, severe musculotendinous retraction and fibrotic tissue prevented tension-free physiologic re-apposition, and a 20 mm defect remained after debridement. The UHMWPE implant functioned as a mechanical bridge across the residual defect and provided immediate structural support, while FDL tendon transposition served as biologic augmentation at the repair site.

Chronic tendon repair is inherently challenging because restoration of functional tendon length generally requires excision of nonfunctional scar tissue; in chronic cases, complete debridement often leaves a large residual gap due to contracture and retraction of the injured musculotendinous unit [5, 11]. Under these conditions, tension-free end-to-end apposition may be difficult or impossible, and repairs performed under excessive tension are more prone to gap formation and failure [10]. Similar challenges are also recognized in chronic Achilles reconstruction in human medicine, where chronic degeneration, tissue loss, and compromised local vascularity complicate surgical repair and healing [8]. In chronic injuries, the healing process is further hindered by scanty soft tissue vascularization and the progressive accumulation of mechanically inferior fibrotic tissue.

To overcome these large defects, synthetic materials such as UHMWPE are utilized in human orthopedics to bridge the gap and provide immediate structural integrity [9]. However, the application of synthetic implants carries specific risks. In human Achilles tendon repairs, complications such as postoperative infections and severe foreign body reactions have been reported. Furthermore, UHMWPE wear debris and synthetic suture materials can induce chronic foreign body granulomas or granulomatous pseudotumors, which may ultimately compromise the repair and necessitate implant excision alongside secondary salvage procedures [12, 15]. Despite these risks, the clinical application of UHMWPE synthetic implants in veterinary medicine has yielded promising results. The veterinary literature documents its successful and safe use in augmenting canine extra-articular cranial cruciate ligament stabilization, repairing patellar ligament avulsions, and bridging gastrocnemius tendon defects, thereby proving its viability when direct tissue apposition is impossible [2, 3].

This dual-augmentation approach addresses the inherent limitations of using either method alone. The UHMWPE implant compensates for the initial lack of mechanical integrity by providing immediate structural stability across the defect. Simultaneously, the transposed FDL pedicled graft offers the distinct advantage of providing a vascularized tissue source with viable tenocytes to support sustained biological remodeling at the repair site. Although harvesting the FDL may increase overall surgical time, biomechanical studies have demonstrated that augmented repairs provide superior strength and resistance to gap formation compared with primary suture repair alone [7]. Together, these complementary effects create a stable and biologically favorable environment for physiologic tendon healing, which reduces the risk of postoperative failure in complex reconstructions involving substantial tendon loss.

The calcaneal region is notoriously susceptible to wound complications and surgical site infections due to minimal soft tissue coverage. We acknowledge that the use of synthetic implants and potentially increased operative time are established risk factors for such complications. However, in this chronic case with a substantial defect, the application of UHMWPE was essential to provide the necessary tensile strength and immediate mechanical stability. To mitigate the risk of implant bulk, a low-profile, flat UHMWPE tape was utilized, and the pedicled FDL graft acted as a vascularized biological envelope over the synthetic material, potentially aiding in local infection resistance. Additionally, the total operative time was maintained at approximately 120 min, which is comparable to standard complex orthopedic procedures, further minimizing the risk of infection.

The success of this intervention was confirmed during the follow-up period. Ultrasonography at 2 months demonstrated maintained tendon continuity without evidence of an implant-associated foreign body reaction, and the patient showed progressive functional improvement. At 3 months, force plate gait analysis objectively supported the clinical impression of recovery, with near-symmetric peak vertical force (27.4 N vs. 27.5 N) and a small difference in vertical impulse (2.9 ± 0.6 N·sec vs. 3.3 ± 0.7 N·sec) between the operated and contralateral hindlimbs. The secondary laxity observed in the intact SDFT was not surgically shortened to avoid over-constraining the joint and minimizing surgical morbidity. The restoration of normal PVF and VI values suggests that re-establishing the primary gastrocnemius lever arm is sufficient for functional recovery, without needing to intervene on the secondarily elongated SDFT.

Several limitations should be considered. This is a single-case report, and the findings cannot be generalized without further clinical evaluation. Furthermore, although the 24-month telephonic follow-up is highly encouraging, extended multi-year clinical and ultrasonographic monitoring is ultimately necessary to definitively rule out late-onset delayed foreign body reactions, which have been reported in human medicine. Additional studies in larger cohorts, including dogs with different body sizes, chronicity, and tendon defect severity, are warranted to clarify the indications and reproducibility of this augmentation technique.

In conclusion, dual augmentation using a UHMWPE synthetic implant and FDL tendon transposition may be a useful surgical option for selected dogs with delayed-presenting gastrocnemius tendon rupture and substantial tendon defects when tension-free primary apposition is not achievable, providing a reliable and stable outcome for functional recovery.
