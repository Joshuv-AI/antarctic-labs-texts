Effects of zinc and probiotic co-supplementation on inflammation, gastrointestinal symptoms, quality of life, and mood in irritable bowel syndrome patients
Authors: Mahsa Rezazadegan, Maryam Soheilipour, Reza Amani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractZinc supports intestinal integrity, and probiotics restore microbial balance, thereby improving gut health. This study examined the effect of zinc and probiotic co-supplementation on inflammation, gastrointestinal symptoms, quality of life (QoL), and mood in IBS patients. The 84 participants were assigned to three groups and received: (1) a zinc gluconate tablet (20 mg elemental zinc) and a placebo; (2) a zinc gluconate and a probiotic capsule; (3) 2 placebos as a control for 90 days. The serum zinc and TNF-α, IBS severity, mood, and QoL were assessed by validated methods. Supplementation with zinc, without or with probiotics, improved serum zinc (6.36 ± 0.80 and 13.03 ± 3.82, vs. -1.76 ± 1.82 µg/dL; p = 0.008), and IBS severity (-182.85 ± 16.27 and − 195.71 ± 23.46, vs. -73.21 ± 17.69; p = 0.003) compared to placebo. Depression (− 3.62 ± 1.46 or − 0.78 ± 1.16 vs. 2.07 ± 1.53, p = 0.004) and stress scores (-2.88 ± 1.82 or -2.64 ± 1.49, vs. 1.85 ± 1.95, p = 0.013) decreased after zinc or placebo supplements compared to zinc plus probiotic. Zinc supplementation, with or without probiotics, improved serum zinc levels and GI symptoms in IBS, whereas zinc alone was more effective than zinc plus probiotics in reducing depression and stress scores. Further research is suggested to confirm our findings.Keywords: Zinc, Probiotic, Inflammation, IBS symptoms, Mood, Quality of lifeSubject terms: Diseases, Gastroenterology, Health care, Medical research, Microbiology

Introduction

The disorders of gut-brain axis interactions (DGBI) have a 40% globally prevalence1. Although DGBI is not considered life-threatening, it exerts a substantial detrimental impact on the quality of life (QoL) and imposes financial burdens on healthcare systems2. Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is among the most prevalent DGBIs3. The prevalence of IBS in women is more than in men (approximately 12% vs. 9%)4. The overlap between IBS and other conditions, such as functional dyspepsia, depressive disorders, somatization disorder, and gastroesophageal reflux disorder, is noteworthy5,6. The pathophysiology of IBS is complex and multifactorial, including gut motility abnormalities, visceral hypersensitivity, increased leaky gut, low-grade inflammation, dysregulation of gut-brain interactions, and psychosocial distress, but is not limited to them. These mechanisms are related to each other and can contribute to the heterogeneity of clinical presentation and symptom severity among patients7. Psychosocial distress and the numerous psychiatric comorbidities in IBS contribute to disorder development and symptom exacerbation8. Moreover, IBS is related to gut dysbiosis, typically characterized by reduced microbial diversity, altered composition, and depletion of beneficial taxa such as Bifidobacterium9. Genetic, antibiotic use, infections, stress, and poor diet are factors that can lead to dysbiosis and subsequently increased intestinal permeability (IP)10. Studies have shown that vitamin D, probiotics, and zinc are related to IP11–13.

Probiotics are live microorganisms that confer health benefits to the host when administered in adequate amounts14. Probiotics, through interference with potential pathogens, improvement of gut barrier function, and immunomodulation, exert lucrative effects on the host15. Studies support the use of probiotics, especially Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus genus, to modulate intestinal microbiota in IBS patients16,17. Based on guidelines, probiotics may be an effective treatment for global symptoms and abdominal pain among IBS patients, with the strongest effect for genus Lactobacillus16. Xie et al. revealed that the low FODMAP diet and probiotics (especially Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, and Bacillus) are two common interventions for patients with IBS before initiation of pharmacological treatments18. Diet restrictions among IBS patients can cause zinc deficiency, which is the main trace element for improving gut tight junction integrity and reducing inflammation and oxidative stress19. Beef, poultry, seafood, dairy products, legumes, and whole grains are food sources of zinc20. About 17.3% of the population in the world and approximately 25% of Iranian households are at risk of inadequate zinc intake21,22. Some studies demonstrated that there is a lower intake or serum zinc levels in patients with IBS; however, other studies did not find any association23–26. In our previous study, we showed that serum zinc was negatively correlated with serum zonulin (a marker of leaky gut) in diarrhea-predominant IBS patients11. Zinc and probiotics are two effective components in the reduction of IP, inflammation, and oxidative stress, and improving mood. Some studies have shown that concurrent consumption of zinc with probiotics can help increase zinc absorption and have a synergistic effect in improving antioxidant status and immune system function27,28. Probiotics, through the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and the reduction of gut pH, can increase the solubility of certain minerals, like zinc. Also, probiotics produce phytases that break down phytic acid, thereby releasing minerals such as calcium and zinc and making them available for absorption29. Considering that no study has been conducted so far on zinc supplementation and in combination with probiotics in IBS patients, the aim of the present study was to investigate the zinc and probiotic co-supplementation on inflammation, gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms, QoL, and mood states in patients with IBS.

Methods

Trial design

This study was a double-blind parallel randomized placebo-controlled clinical trial stratified based on sex. It was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee at the Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUI.PHANUT.REC.1402.079). It was also registered at the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (https://www.irct.ir) under the registration number IRCT20180818040827N5 on 29/01/2024.

Participants

From July 2024, IBS patients who were referred to the subspecialty Clinic of Khorshid Hospital and Al-Zahra Hospital Digestive Health Center and two gastroenterology clinics in Isfahan (Iran) were screened. Men and women aged 20–60 with a body mass index (BMI) between 18.5 and 40 kg/m2 and a confirmed diagnosis of IBS by a gastroenterologist based on Rome IV criteria were enrolled30. The exclusion criteria were patients with a history of GI surgery or infection, celiac, inflammatory diseases such as inflammatory bowel diseases, pregnancy, lactation, hospitalization, malignancy, allergic reactions to zinc, probiotic or placebo, supplementation with probiotic products or zinc, following a special diet, smoking and alcohol drinking habit, chronic anemia, being a professional athlete and taking medications including antibiotics, anti-diarrheal or laxative, penicillamine, affecting on Gl motility, as well as heparin and warfarin. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants before enrollment. Medical and demographic characteristics were also collected through interviews.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using the recommended formula for clinical trials, taking into account type one error of 0.05 and type two errors of 0.20 (power = 80%) based on a previous study31. Therefore, 25 IBS patients in each group were required. According to a ∼10% drop-out rate, we finally recruited 28 patients with IBS for each group, 84 in total.

Randomization and blinding

The participants were assigned to treatment (zinc or zinc plus probiotic) and control (placebo) groups using stratified (based on sex) permuted block randomization (allocation ratio = 1: 1: 1, block size = 6). We used the website (https://www.sealedenvelope.com/simple-randomiser/v1/lists) for the generation of a random allocation sequence. Zinc, probiotic, and placebo tablets were sealed in bottles that were numbered in a specific order. The sequence generation and allocation concealment were implemented by an independent medical expert. The patients, laboratory staff, and researchers were blinded to the randomization. Zinc, zinc placebo, probiotic, and its placebo were similar in size, weight, shape, color, and packaging.

Intervention

Patients were randomized into three parallel groups1, zinc group: zinc gluconate tablet (20 mg of elemental zinc, Amin Pharmaceutical Company, Isfahan, Iran) and FamiLact placebo capsule (Microcrystalline cellulose, Zist Takhmir, Tehran, Iran)2; zinc and probiotic group: zinc gluconate tablet (20 mg of elemental zinc, Amin Pharmaceutical Company, Isfahan, Iran) and FamiLact capsule (109 colony-forming units (CFUs) of Lactobacillus rhamnosus, Lactobacillus casei, Lactobacillus plantarum, Lactobacillus acidophilus, Bifidobacterium breve, Bifidobacterium longum, Bifidobacterium lactis, Bifidobacterium bifidum, and Streptococcus thermophilus, Zist Takhmir, Tehran, Iran)3; the placebo group: zinc placebo tablet (Microcrystalline cellulose, Amin Pharmaceutical Company, Isfahan, Iran) and FamiLact placebo capsule (Microcrystalline cellulose, Zist Takhmir, Tehran, Iran). The dose and duration of supplementation were chosen based on previous studies32–34. A zinc tablet (supplement or placebo) and a probiotic capsule (supplement or placebo) were prescribed once daily after breakfast with sufficient water for 90 days. All patients in the three groups were instructed with appropriate dietary advice for IBS based on the recommendations of the American Gastroenterological Association guideline35. These recommendations were: having regular meals and taking time to eat, avoiding missing meals or leaving long gaps between eating, drinking at least 8 cups of fluid per day, especially water, restricting caffeine, and reducing intake of alcohol and fizzy drinks. All groups were asked to record their dietary intakes and physical activity levels during the study. Follow-up phone weekly calls, clinic visits in the middle of the period, and monitoring the number of consumed supplements or placebos were conducted to assess treatment compliance.

General characteristics, anthropometric indices, dietary intake, and physical activity

In the first visit, participants were asked about their general characteristics, including age, sex, marital status, education level, history of diseases, medications, and supplements they consumed. A fixed, non-stretchable tape at 0.1 cm precision was used to measure height in a standing position. Weight was measured in minimal clothing to the nearest 0.1 kg by a digital scale, Omron BF511. Waist circumference was determined at the midpoint between the iliac crest and the last rib. Body mass index was calculated by dividing weight (kg) by height (m2). Food records were made three times during the study (at the beginning and in weeks 5 and 12). Nutritionist IV software customized for Iranian foods was used (First Databank, San Bruno, CA, USA) for the calculation of energy and nutrient intake36. Physical activity levels were estimated using a valid and reliable short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (SF-IPAQ) at the beginning and in weeks 5 and 1237. Also, possible side effects of supplements or placebos, particularly GI complications, were monitored during the trial period.

Outcomes assessment

Venous blood samples were obtained from the participants after 10–12 h of overnight fasting. The serum was separated by centrifugation and then stored at − 80 °C until final analysis. Serum zinc level was measured by Cobas integra 400 plus analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Rotkreuz, Switzerland). The enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) technique using a commercially available kit (Karmania Pars Gene Company, Iran) was used to assess serum TNF-α level.

A validated IBS severity score system (IBS-SSS) was used to assess the IBS severity38. The validated IBS-SSS included five clinical items1 severity of abdominal pain2, frequency of abdominal pain3, severity of abdominal distention4, dissatisfaction with bowel habits5, interference of IBS with daily activities in general during 10 recent days. Scores less than 75, 75–175, 175–300, and above 300 indicated remission, mild, moderate, and severe IBS, respectively. Participants were evaluated for depression, anxiety, and stress scores using a validated 21-item questionnaire (DASS-21)39. There were four response options, and based on the scores in each question, each parameter of depression, anxiety, and stress was calculated. A self-reported 34-item IBS quality of life (IBS-QoL) questionnaire was provided to IBS patients40. The Persian version of this questionnaire was reliable and validated, having high internal consistency41. The IBS-QoL questionnaire has 8 subscales, including “dysphoria”, “interference with activity”, “body image”, “health worry”, “food avoidance”, “social reaction”, “sexual worries” and “relationship”. A five-point response scale from always1 to never5 was used to measure patients’ QoL. Scores of all items were converted to scales between 0 and 100, in which higher scores showed better QoL.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed according to the intention-to-treat (ITT) approach, using the last-observation-carried-forward (LOCF) method by a statistical software package (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA), version 23.0. Statistical significance was considered at P < 0.05. Normal distribution of quantitative variables was checked using Q–Q plots and skewness. Data with normal distribution were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error (SE), and categorical data were reported as frequency (percentage). The non-normally distributed data were shown as median (interquartile range). The participants’ baseline characteristics between groups were assessed by one-way ANOVA and Chi-square tests. Within-group comparisons after a 90-day intervention were conducted by a paired-samples t-test. Differences between the three groups after intervention were detected using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), adjusted for the baseline value of each variable, as well as physical activity, supplementation, and medications (antidepressant, antispasmodic, and antacid) use in the final model. Also, changes in dietary intake components and physical activity over time were assessed using the repeated measure analysis of variance.

Results

The recruitment period and follow-up of participants were from July 2024 to February 2025. Of 84 IBS patients, seven participants were excluded (two patients due to personal reasons from the zinc group, two patients due to personal reasons (n = 1), and gastrointestinal complications (n = 1) from the zinc plus probiotic group, and three patients due to personal reasons (n = 2) and low compliance rate (n = 1) from the placebo group). Nevertheless, all 84 participants were included in the final analysis according to the ITT approach (Fig. 1).

Effect of intervention on serum zinc level, inflammation and gastrointestinal symptoms

Within- and between-group comparisons of serum zinc, inflammation and gastrointestinal symptoms at the baseline and 90 days are reported in Table 3. In the zinc supplementation and zinc plus probiotic supplementation groups, serum zinc level was significantly increased (mean change: 6.36 ± 0.80, p = 0.001, and 13.03 ± 3.82, p = 0.002, respectively), and serum TNF-α level (mean change: −116.22 ± 8.16, and − 113.13 ± 12.14, p = 0.001, respectively) and severity of IBS symptoms (mean change: −182.85 ± 16.27, and − 195.71 ± 23.46, p = 0.001, respectively) were decreased compared with the baseline. Also, in the placebo group, serum TNF-α level (mean change: −110.25 ± 10.51, p = 0.001) and IBS severity (mean change: −73.21 ± 17.69, p = 0.001) were decreased at the end of the study compared with that of the beginning.

Between-group comparison illustrated that zinc plus probiotic supplementation significantly increased serum zinc level compared to the control in both adjusted models (13.03 ± 3.82 vs. −1.76 ± 1.82; model 1: p = 0.004, final model: p = 0.008). In the ANCOVA model adjusted for baseline values and other confounders, this significant difference was also revealed in zinc supplementation compared with the placebo group (6.36 ± 0.80 vs. −1.76 ± 1.82; p = 0.008). However, there was no significant effect on TNF-α level between the intervention and the placebo groups.

A significant reduction for IBS severity score were observed in both zinc supplementation separately and plus probiotic in adjusted model for baseline values and only in zinc supplementation in model adjusted for baseline values and other confounders compared to the placebo (−182.85 ± 16.27 or −195.71 ± 23.46, respectively, vs. −73.21 ± 17.69; model 1: p = 0.003, final model: p = 0.034).

Effect of intervention on mood status

The effect of the 90-day intervention on depression, anxiety, and stress is shown in Table 3. The within-group comparison revealed a significant reduction of depression and anxiety scores in the zinc supplementation group at the end of the study, compared to the beginning (mean change: − 3.62 ± 1.46, p = 0.02, and − 2.37 ± 1.12, p = 0.046, respectively).

In the model adjusted for baseline values, depression score decreased significantly in the zinc group compared to the zinc plus probiotic, not the placebo, group (− 3.62 ± 1.46 vs. 2.07 ± 1.53, p = 0.014). After adjusting for other confounders and baseline values, in addition to the previously mentioned result, a significant reduction of depression score was found in the placebo group in comparison to the zinc plus probiotic supplementation group (− 0.78 ± 1.16 vs. 2.07 ± 1.53, p = 0.004). Furthermore, the stress score was diminished significantly in both zinc and placebo groups when compared to the zinc plus probiotic group (−2.88 ± 1.82 or −2.64 ± 1.49, respectively, vs. 1.85 ± 1.95, p = 0.013). We found no significant effect of interventions on anxiety score compared to placebo.

Effect of intervention on quality of life

Within- and between-group comparisons of quality of life and its subscales during the study are indicated in Table 4. After 90 days, social reaction in all three groups (zinc, mean change: 12.26 ± 5.54, p = 0.036; zinc plus probiotic, mean change: 14.73 ± 6.52, p = 0.032; placebo, mean change: 12.94 ± 4.86, p = 0.013) was significantly improved. Also, a significant improvement was observed for dysphoria (mean change: 15.49 ± 6.31, p = 0.021), interference with activity (mean change: 13.26 ± 5.74, p = 0.029), health worry (mean change: 15.77 ± 5.80, p = 0.011), and sexual worries (mean change: 18.30 ± 7.67, p = 0.024) in zinc plus probiotic group. Moreover, in the placebo group, the relationship (mean change: 9.34 ± 3.68, p = 0.017) and body image (mean change: 14.95 ± 4.58, p = 0.003) were significantly ameliorated.

Side effects

In this trial, just one patient in the zinc-probiotic co-supplementation group reported GI complications.

Discussion

Our study is the first study that investigates the co-supplementation of zinc and probiotics on inflammation, IBS severity symptoms, QoL, and mood in patients with diagnosed IBS. The present study revealed that 20 mg of elemental zinc, separately and in combination with 109 CFUs of probiotic, for 90 days, elevated serum zinc levels and improved the severity of IBS symptoms compared with the placebo. In our study, fiber intake, particularly after intervention with zinc supplement alone and with probiotics for 90 days, was slightly increased, which may indicate gut tolerance improvement following supplementation. However, this finding was not statistically significant. In the context of mood states, depression and stress scores reduced after zinc or placebo supplement use compared to zinc plus probiotic supplement. Also, zinc plus probiotic supplementation showed no better effect on body image, as one of the quality of life subscales, compared to placebo during the intervention time.

Because of dietary restrictions for the management of GI symptoms in most IBS patients, nutrient deficiencies such as zinc are probable among them11. In our study, co-supplementation of zinc and probiotics could increase serum zinc concentration twice that of zinc supplement alone. Furthermore, more relief of IBS symptoms was observed in the zinc plus probiotics group. There are several possible mechanisms, including improved zinc bioavailability, increased production of SCFAs, reduced leaky gut, anti-inflammatory synergy, and microbiota modulation through probiotics (especially multi-strain formulations)42,43. The review by Wong-Chew et al. highlighted the synergistic potential of zinc and Alkalihalobacillus clausii in gut health and immunity promotion. This combination could manage acute diarrhea and antibiotic-associated dysbiosis in children up to adolescence44. Moreover, Park et al. found that a blend of probiotics, rosavin, zinc, and prebiotics alleviated acute colitis in mice by modulating the production of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines45. Other studies have also examined the effect of co-supplementation of vitamins or other trace elements with probiotics on GI problems. A crossover randomized double-blind controlled trial by Bonfrate et al. on 25 IBS patients reported that the novel formulation of Bifidobacterium longum BB536 and Lactobacillus rhamnosus HN001 with B6 vitamin can improve symptoms and severity of disease, and restore IP, and gut microbiota46. In another study, Zhu et al. demonstrated that selenium-enriched Bifidobacterium longum DD98 restored gut microbiota in antibiotic-disrupted mice through boosting antioxidants, increasing Lactobacillus, and suppressing pathogens47.

In our study, reduction of TNF-α levels after 90 days in all three groups was observed, but the difference between groups was not significant. This can be due to the almost identical significant reduction in TNF-α levels in any three groups, even in the placebo group, but cannot be attributed to dietary modifications, as the comparison of the three groups did not show any significant differences in dietary intakes during the time. Zinc supplementation may reduce TNF-α levels by inhibiting nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) activation and reducing oxidative stress through cell membrane stabilization and antioxidant defense systems enhancement48. Hosseini et al., in a meta-analysis study on 21 clinical trials, showed that zinc supplementation was associated with a significant reduction in TNF-α levels in adults49. Also, Jafari et al. in a 90-day randomized trial study on young women with PMS who received 30 mg/day zinc gluconate revealed a reduction of CRP and IL-6 levels as inflammation biomarkers50. Probiotic supplementation may reduce TNF-α levels by several mechanisms. Probiotics may enhance the integrity of the intestinal barrier by increasing tight junction proteins expression and limiting LPS translocation. Thereby, it can attenuate Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR 4)-mediated NF-κB activation and subsequently decrease TNF-α production51. Moreover, some probiotics can promote anti-inflammatory cytokines and regulate dendritic cell and macrophage responses, leading to TNF-α suppression52. In a recent randomized, double-blind, controlled trial in severe traumatic brain injury patients, supplementation with seven-strain probiotic capsules (Zist Takhmir, Iran) a day containing Lactobacillus (rhamnosus, casei, acidophilus, bulgaricus) and Bifidobacterium (breve and longum) and Streptococcus thermophilus significantly decreased serum TNF-α levels compared with placebo after two weeks of treatment53. Another study reported that the combined in ovo (injection into the egg before hatching) use of a multi-strain probiotic and zinc-glycine chelate modulates the immune response and decreases pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α)54. A study by Mohammad Malyar. et al. in 2019 found that zinc-enriched probiotics improved growth, antioxidant capacity, and immune function in heat-stressed Wistar rats28. Psychological modulation, stress reduction through diminishing stress hormones, and awareness of being observed, which can lead to lifestyle change, may be underlying mechanisms of the placebo effect55.

Depression, stress, and anxiety are prevalent in IBS patients and linked through the gut-brain axis, where psychological distress exacerbates visceral hypersensitivity56. The present study showed that supplementation with zinc or a placebo could reduce depression and stress scores compared to zinc plus probiotics. This finding may be due to a conflict of neurotransmitter effects; Zinc boosts serotonin, while some probiotics divert tryptophan to non-mood pathways57,58. Also, it is possible that probiotics supplement in some participants, when introduced for the first time, activate mild mucosal immune responses, causing short-term GI discomfort such as bloating and changes in bowel habits, which may have caused increased stress about GI symptom exacerbation59. Although GI symptoms subsequently improved, this early discomfort might have prevented the zinc and probiotics co-supplementation from showing a significant stress-reducing effect compared to the zinc or placebo supplements. Zinc, as an antioxidant, can regulate neurotransmitters and prevent oxidative stress-driven neuronal damage in the enteric nervous system60. Also, zinc may reduce stress and depression symptoms by modulating glutamatergic neurotransmission through N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor inhibition and enhancement of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) signaling61. A recent meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials indicated that zinc supplementation can reduce the risk of and alleviate depression62. Moreover, this trace element had beneficial effects on physical and psychological symptoms in young women with premenstrual syndrome50. In a randomized clinical trial of adults undergoing methadone maintenance treatment, daily zinc supplementation for three months was associated with a significant reduction in depression, anxiety, and stress63. We used microcrystalline cellulose as a placebo, which is a chemically inert, non-digestible excipient that is not absorbed or systemically metabolized in humans and has no direct physiological effect64. Placebo administration may trigger dopamine and endogenous opioid release in the brain, improving mood and pain tolerance65. Also, placebo-induced relaxation activates the vagus nerve, which inhibits inflammation in the gut mucosa66. It can be hypothesized that the use of specific psychobiotic strains may exert more pronounced anti-stress and anti-depressant effects compared with non-specific multi-strain probiotics; however, this hypothesis warrants further investigation in future trials.

According to the studies, IBS patients have lower QoL than the general population67. The IBS-QoL questionnaire evaluates QoL across domains that reflect the main burdens of IBS, including emotional distress (dysphoria), limitation of daily activity, food avoidance, health worry, social and relationship impacts, body image, and sexual concerns40. Our study revealed that social reaction was improved at the end of the study compared to the baseline in all three groups. This improvement across all groups may partly reflect the well-documented placebo response in IBS, where enhanced patient-provider interaction and regular follow-up can positively affect psychosocial dimensions of QoL independent of intervention. Furthermore, a significant improvement for dysphoria, interference with activity, health worry, and sexual worries was observed after intervention with zinc plus probiotic supplementation. This finding may be partly explained by the role of zinc in reducing neuroinflammation, together with probiotics’ capacity to regulate the gut-brain axis through immune modulation, ultimately influencing psychological well-being56,68. We found that although co-supplementation of zinc and probiotics after 90 days could marginally improve body image (p = 0.063), relationship (p = 0.052), and food avoidance (p = 0.051), no better effect on body image was observed compared to the placebo. The zinc and zinc plus probiotic group had a higher number of people with severe IBS at baseline. In comparison, the placebo group included a higher number of IBS patients with moderate symptom severity. This difference was marginally significant (p = 0.063). This can be the reason for the lack of significant effect of the intervention groups on the quality of life and its subscales compared to the placebo group. Our previous case-control study showed that dietary zinc intake was positively correlated with psychological health in the healthy controls and with body image in the diarrhea-predominant IBS patients26. A recent study illustrated that three months of supplementation with probiotics (4.5 × 10 11 CFUs of a combination of Lactobacillus plantarum, Lactobacillus paracasei, Lactobacillus acidophilus, Lactobacillus bulgaricus, Bifidobacterium infantis, Bifidobacterium longum, Bifidobacterium breve, and Streptococcus thermophiles, 2 times/day), alone or in combination with 25 mg zinc, enhanced health-related quality of life in patients with cirrhosis69. Furthermore, zinc supplementation for three months had a positive effect on physical aspects of quality of life in women with premenstrual syndrome70.

As physical activity is a potentially important behavioral factor that can affect clinical symptoms, metabolic and gut-brain interactions in IBS, we measured physical activity at baseline, mid-intervention, and the end of the study71. In our study, physical activity levels were significantly different between groups. Therefore, physical activity was considered a potential confounder and adjusted in the ANCOVA model to control for its possible effect on outcomes. Controlling for this confounder strengthens the internal validity of the results.

To the best of our knowledge, the current study is the first randomized controlled trial investigating the efficacy of zinc supplement alone or in combination with probiotics on inflammation, IBS severity symptoms, QoL, and mood in IBS patients. Also, we used a safe and tolerable dose of zinc and probiotics72. However, some limitations of our study should be addressed. Although the sample size was determined a priori and considered sufficient to detect the primary outcome, its relatively modest size may limit the ability to detect smaller effect sizes and reduce the findings generalizability. Randomization based on age and BMI, in addition to sex, could have further balanced the groups, but it was not feasible given practical constraints and the modest sample size. Also, the lack of a probiotics-only intervention group may be considered a limitation; however, the study was specifically designed to evaluate the independent and combined effects of zinc supplementation. Moreover, there were funding limitations; therefore, the gut microbiota composition and IP were not assessed.

Conclusion

A 90-day zinc supplementation, alone or with a probiotic, could improve serum zinc levels and the severity of GI symptoms in IBS patients. Also, zinc supplement compared to the combination of zinc and probiotic, had a favorable effect on depression and stress symptoms. Therefore, supplementation with zinc or zinc plus probiotics may be an effective adjuvant therapy for IBS, whereas the role of probiotics in improving mood-related outcomes remains unclear. It seems that future studies with a higher dose of the aforementioned supplements with other probiotic strains that can be more focused on mental health and a larger sample size are needed to confirm the results of this study.
