Key Interventions in Friedreich's Ataxia and Their Impact on Patient Outcomes: A Systematic Review
Authors: Dorota Sarwinska, Maresa Buchholz, Audrey Iskandar, Marcus Grobe‐Einsler, Thomas Klockgether, Jennifer Faber, Bernhard Michalowsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFriedreich's ataxia (FA) is a rare neurodegenerative disease with multisystemic symptoms that requires multidisciplinary care. This systematic review summarizes available pharmacological and nonpharmacological interventions, their outcomes, and alignment with patient‐centered care domains, as well as their impact on these domains. The publication search was conducted in three databases (PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library) from 2010 to 2025 for human studies. Studies were included if they met predefined eligibility criteria (FA population/pharmacological or nonpharmacological interventions/use of comparator/any outcomes, especially patient reported). All study designs were included. Risk of bias assessment was performed by two reviewers using Cochrane RoB‐2 for randomized controlled trials and JBI Critical Appraisal Tools for case reports, case series, and quasi‐experimental studies. Results were synthesized narratively. Ninety studies (69 FA only, 21 mixed ataxia) were included. FA‐only studies included randomized controlled trials (32%), quasi‐experimental studies (35%), and case reports (33%), mostly with small samples (<100) and low‐to‐moderate bias. Pharmacological interventions (66.7%), mainly disease modifying (93.5%), were more common than nonpharmacological interventions, with the most effective interventions being omaveloxolone (pharmacological), as well as rehabilitation and heart interventions (nonpharmacological). Pharmacological studies often showed biological improvements without clear clinical benefit, whereas nonpharmacological therapies yielded more consistent patient‐relevant gains, although evidence was limited. Physical health was the most frequently addressed domain (42.3%), whereas mental health and caregiver aspects were rarely studied (1.8% and 1.4%). No study investigated caregiver burden or economic outcomes. More holistic studies are needed, integrating patient‐ and caregiver‐reported outcomes and combining multiple treatment approaches in larger cohorts. © 2026 The Author(s). Friedreich's ataxia (FA) is a rare neurodegenerative genetic disorder with a prevalence between 1:200,000 and 1:750,000 in Europe 1 ; it is the most common autosomal recessive ataxia, most frequently caused by a GAA trinucleotide repeat expansion in the frataxin (FXN) gene. The expanded GAA ultimately leads to decreased frataxin protein levels, and thus to iron accumulation in mitochondria, increased oxidative stress, and cell degeneration. 2 , 3 Depending on the length of GAA1 repetitions, the disease onset can occur either earlier in childhood (longer expansion) or later in adolescence (shorter expansion). 4 The age at onset is also inversely correlated with disease severity, where faster progression and earlier loss of ambulation are observed in patients with early onset. 4 , 5 , 6

FA is a multisystemic, progressive disease with neurological core symptoms, including ataxia, dysarthria, sensory loss, muscle weakness, and spasticity, as well as nonneurological symptoms such as cardiomyopathy, diabetes mellitus, scoliosis, and pes cavus. 2 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Mental disorders and decline in cognitive functions also occur in the course of the disease. 12 , 13 , 14 Caregivers of patients with FA also report worries and uncertainty about their loved ones, as well as managing their care, representing a substantial caregiver burden. 15 All mentioned symptoms, burdens, and challenges notably influence patients' and caregivers' quality of life. 15 , 16

FA requires complex treatment, care, and support. Therefore, several pharmacological and nonpharmacological approaches exist, 2 , 17 with a primary focus on physical health and clinical outcomes. Since 2024, omaveloxolone has been approved in both the United States and the European Union as the first disease‐modifying treatment for FA, 18 , 19 representing an important milestone. 20 , 21

Due to the variety of symptoms and available interventions, a summary of all available evidence about existing interventions in FA would help identify the possible range of treatment options. Despite this, little is known about how the interventions affected various health domains, including physical, mental, and psychosocial health, which is summarized as health‐related quality of life. Therefore, it is essential to comprehensively capture all existing evidence on interventional approaches in FA to identify key intervention categories and evaluate their impact on patient‐ and caregiver‐related outcomes. However, so far, no systematic review has synthesized the currently available interventions.

Consequently, this study aims to systematically review all available evidence on pharmacological and nonpharmacological interventions implemented in FA, with the objectives of (1) identifying key intervention categories employed in FA, (2) assessing their alignment with core patient and caregiver outcome domains, and (3) evaluating the intervention impact on these outcomes. Understanding these effects is crucial for developing holistic, patient‐ and caregiver‐centered treatment and care strategies in FA.

Methods

This systematic review was performed in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions. 22 The PRISMA 2020 checklist was submitted for editorial review. 23

Protocol and Registration

The protocol for this review was registered in PROSPERO (ID No. CRD420251075597). All predefined steps were followed throughout the whole process to ensure transparency and quality of this review. 24

Deviations from the Protocol

After registration, two methodological modifications were implemented: the risk of bias tool was changed to better match the study designs, and the planned certainty assessment was not conducted because of substantial heterogeneity of the evidence. All deviations were documented to maintain transparency.

Study Eligibility Criteria

Study eligibility criteria were determined based on the PICOS (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Study design) approach for systematic reviews. 22 Inclusion and exclusion criteria are presented in Table S1.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

Three databases were searched: PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library (CENTRAL). The pilot search was conducted before the study protocol registration. The search included publications released since 2010, with no language limitation applied, as long as an English abstract was available. Texts from foreign languages were translated into English. Animal, cell, or in silico studies were excluded.

The search strategy was based on the two dimensions: (1) “Friedreich ataxia” and (2) “interventions.” The key words used in the first dimension included “Friedreich ataxia” (MeSH), “cerebellar ataxia,” “hereditary ataxia,” and “progressive ataxia.” Because FA is a rare disease, the search also included other ataxias because studies often group several ataxia types together. In the second dimension, key words encompassed a variety of interventions that included both pharmacological and nonpharmacological aspects, such as “intervention,” “rehabilitation” [MeSH], “treatment” [MeSH], “training,” “exercise,” and “therapy.”

The search excluded reviews to limit the options for manual screening. Potential eligible publications identified in previous reviews, but not captured by the search strategy, were manually added. In addition, gray literature was searched through Google Scholar. The searching was finalized on June 3, 2025, for databases and on June 18, 2025, for manual search. The exact search strategy can be found in Table S2.

Study Selection

Publications from the databases search, manual search, and previous reviews were then added to the online tool Rayyan. Duplicates were removed, and both initial (Title/Abstract) and full‐text screenings were performed 25 by two reviewers (D.S. and M.B.) against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discrepancies were discussed and solved involving the third reviewer (B.M.).

Data Extraction

From the included publications, we extracted data for (1) study identification (author, year of publication, country), (2) characteristics (design), (3) population (sample size, age, FA characteristics), (4) intervention (type, dose, administration, duration), and (5) outcomes (types, domains, categories, outcome measure, result, observation period).

Risk of Bias Assessment

On the basis of the designs of the included studies, we used the Cochrane RoB‐2 for randomized controlled trials (RCTs), 26 the JBI Critical Appraisal Tool for quasi‐experimental studies, 27 and the JBI Critical Appraisal Tool for case reports and case series 28 for risk of bias assessment, which was performed independently by two reviewers (D.S. and M.B.). In case of discrepancies, the third reviewer (B.M.) was consulted. The results were standardized and presented in a scale in a color‐coded manner: ≥75% low risk of bias (green), 50% to 75% moderate (yellow), ≤50% high (red). 29 Because the study design directly influences the general bias of the study, the assessed risk of bias should be considered within a study design, rather than comparing studies with different designs.

Data Synthesis

Due to the heterogeneity of interventions, a narrative synthesis of the gathered outcomes was performed. The interventions were grouped into key categories, and their effects on the outcomes were compared descriptively. In addition, the alignment of the interventions with core outcome domains defined by the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement (ICHOM) (including physical functioning, symptom control, quality of life, mental well‐being, and caregiver burden) was assessed. 23 FA affects both children and adults and, like dementia, is a progressive neurodegenerative disease without a cure, requiring increasing medical and informal care over time. Therefore, the existing ICHOM outcome sets for general pediatric health, general adult health, and dementia were used to assess intervention outcome alignment with health domains.

Results

Study Selection

The search yielded 2227 studies, from which 114 were removed as duplicates. After title and abstract screening, 136 publications were eligible for full‐text screening. Finally, 90 publications (69 FA only and 21 mixed ataxia population; Fig. 1A) were included in the narrative syntheses. The study selection process is presented on the PRISMA chart in Figure S1. 30

Almost all included studies were written in English (n = 86; 96%), with only four publications written in languages other than English. Most studies with FA‐only patients (n = 69) were conducted in Europe (n = 29; 42.0%), North America (n = 15; 21.7%), or were international studies (n = 20; 29%). The majority of studies included pharmacological interventions (n = 69; 66.7%), with only 33.3% (n = 23) investigating nonpharmacological interventions (Fig. 1D).

Most pharmacological studies were quasi‐experimental studies (n = 20; 43.5%) or RCTs (n = 19; 41.3%), whereas nonpharmacological interventions were mostly case reports including case series (n = 16; 69.6%) and quasi‐experimental studies (n = 4; 17.4%) (Fig. 1F). Considering all studies, the prevalence of RCTs, quasi‐experimental studies, and case reports was similar (32%, 35%, and 33%, respectively) (Fig. 1F). Sample sizes were small, with only one pharmacological study having more than 100 patients.

Risk of Bias

Studies differed in levels of bias and strength of evidence, from RCTs with the highest reliability, through the quasi‐experimental studies, up to case reports with the highest bias. The summary of risk of bias assessment for specific study designs is presented in Figure 2. The details from the performed assessments are available in Tables S5 to S10 and Figure S3.

Risk of bias assessment performed for all included studies (n = 69 for Friedreich's ataxia [FA] only) using certain tools depending on the study design. *The answers from JBI Critical Appraisal Tool have been standardized 29 and presented in the color manner. RCT, randomized controlled trial. [Color figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary.com]

The majority of included studies had a low‐to‐moderate risk of bias. For case reports/series, the bias mostly considered the reporting of adverse events or the clear and full presentation of patients' disease histories. For quasi‐experimental studies, the most considerable bias was related to internal validity because most studies did not include a control group but instead employed a pre‐/post‐intervention design. For RCTs, the most concerning aspects were the randomization process and measurement of outcomes, specifically the blinding of assessors and the assessment of outcomes. In addition, RCTs had small sample sizes.

Study Population

FA study participants were children (from 9‐year‐old patient in case report), young adults with mean age most commonly around 30 years old, up to 65 years old (individual case report). Mostly, the study population had relatively early FA onset, with different ages and, therefore, different disease durations. In mixed ataxia studies, patients with FA represented only a small percentage of study populations and were generally older than in FA‐only studies. Other ataxias included in these studies were various types of, for example, autosomal dominant cerebellar ataxia such as spinocerebellar ataxias (eg, types 3, 6, 14, 28), idiopathic late‐onset cerebellar ataxia, and multiple system atrophy of cerebellar type, which accordingly varied in their clinical characteristics. Therefore, results cannot be generalized to patients with FA; however, they may provide evidence for potential interventions, which target the ataxia symptoms that are not specific for FA.

Interventions

Pharmacological Interventions

Most pharmacological interventions in FA were disease‐modifying therapies (93.5%), with idebenone (13.0%), interferon‐γ (10.9%), erythropoietin (8.7%), and omaveloxolone (8.7%) as the most commonly investigated medications (Fig. 1C,I). Symptomatic treatment was described in three case reports (twice intrathecal baclofen for spasms and memantine for optic nerve atrophy). For mixed ataxia studies, in pharmacological interventions, three disease‐modifying treatments (vatiquinone and twice riluzole) and one symptomatic treatment (baclofen) were investigated.

As demonstrated in Table 1, 42% of pharmacological RCTs (n = 8) did not show a significant positive effect of the pharmacotherapies, including idebenone (three studies), interferon‐γ, erythropoietin, carbamylated erythropoietin, liraglutide, and luvadaxistat, on the investigated outcome measures. 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 Similarly, no positive trend of the treatment on outcomes was demonstrated in 15% of quasi‐experimental studies on interferon‐γ, tocotrienol, and mixed therapy with darbepoetin alfa, idebenone, and riboflavin. 39 , 40 , 41 Other medications such as idebenone, 42 deferiprone in high doses 43 (RCTs), and calcitriol 44 (quasi‐experimental study) demonstrated significant increase of ataxia severity (measured with Friedreich's Ataxia Rating Scale [FARS] or International Cooperative Ataxia Rating Scale [ICARS], FARS, and FARS ADL [activities of daily living]) or other outcomes (walking speed).

Although erythropoietin reduced oxidative stress and improved brain structures (imaging and laboratory outcomes), it showed no clear benefit in ataxia severity (Scale for the Assessment and Rating of Ataxia [SARA]) in quasi‐experimental studies and no efficacy in an RCT. 36 Methylprednisolone improved patients' global impression in the overall population but showed no significant improvement in other clinical, laboratory, or patient‐reported outcomes. 45 In contrast, calcitriol treatment significantly increased frataxin levels but was associated with worsened walking speed. 44

When observing specific subgroups, pharmacological interventions tended to be more effective in younger, pediatric, and ambulant patients with a shorter disease duration but longer GAA1 repetitions, indicating accelerated disease progression. 34 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 Similarly, better effects were reported for patients with lower initial ataxia severity as measured with ICARS or SARA. 43 , 50

Nonpharmacological Interventions

Nonpharmacological interventions were rehabilitation, multimodal rehabilitation, occupational therapy, transcranial direct current stimulation, whole‐body vibration therapy, and neuromuscular electrical stimulation (52.2%). Specific target symptoms (47.8%) included cardiomyopathy, hearing, scoliosis, cognitive function, or dysarthria (Fig. 1C), with heart intervention (including heart transplantation, septal myectomy, and catheter ablation; 26.1% of all NP interventions) and rehabilitation (26.1%) being the most common treatments (Fig. 1J).

Nonpharmacological interventions showed more consistent positive outcome tendencies compared with pharmacological interventions, with at least one statistically significant or notable improvement in at least one outcome measure in each study. Conversely, some nonpharmacological interventions also demonstrated deterioration in some outcomes, for example, neurological function after heart transplantation, 51 , 52 , 53 physical performance after the occupational therapy, 54 or gait and endurance after scoliosis surgery. 55

When observing subgroups, nonpharmacological interventions tended to be more effective in ambulant, younger patients. 56

The most common nonpharmacological interventions in studies with mixed ataxia patients were transcranial direct current stimulation (29.4%) and any rehabilitation (23.5%, including multimodal rehabilitation of 5.9%). In addition, other approaches were presented for mixed populations, such as video games (ie, training including body‐controlled video games, often with the use of virtual reality) (17.7%) and noninvasive focal mechanical vibration‐proprioceptive stabilizer (11.8%).

Alignment of Outcome Measures and Evidence Synthesis According to the ICHOM Criteria

Outcome Measures Used

A summary of outcome measures used across all interventions for FA‐only studies is demonstrated in Fig 1G.

Mostly, clinical measures (CL) were used (60 studies in total; 87%), with SARA (24 studies) and FARS (19 studies; FARS Neuro [neurological examination from FARS] 2 studies, mFARS/mFARS Neuro [modified FARS] 9 studies) being the most prevalent measures for ataxia severity. Also, the 9‐HPT (9‐Hole Peg Test) was commonly used for manual dexterity and upper limb coordination (21 studies) and the T25FW (Timed 25‐foot Walk) for mobility and gait assessments (12 studies) (Fig. 1H).

Laboratory measures (L) (25 studies; 36%) mainly included frataxin levels (16 studies), oxidative stress markers (2 studies), iron metabolism (2 studies), and various assessments of cerebellar function (1 study) or muscle function (2 studies). Imaging outcomes (IMG) (25 studies, 36%) included echocardiography (23 studies) imaging of the brain (white or gray matter) or spinal cord (2 studies).

Patient‐reported outcome measures (PROMs) were included in only 37 studies (54%), whereas subjective patient perspectives were gathered in 7 studies (10%) and quality‐of‐life assessments in 18 studies (26%). The most commonly used PROM was the short form health survey with 36 questions (SF‐36; 12 times), modified Fatigue impact scale (MFIS; 6 times), and self‐reported assessment of ADL (5 times). Only four studies (6%) for FA‐only patients included subjective caregiver perspective, 35 , 55 , 57 , 58 but no caregiver burden or well‐being measures were used.

Administered outcome measures in mixed ataxia studies are demonstrated in Tables S3 and S4 and Figure S2.

Addressed Health Domains

Figure 3 categorizes the interventions for patients with FA into seven domains: physical health, overall health, social health, mental health, clinical status, caregiver support, and economic considerations. Each domain demonstrates the number of assessments in FA‐only studies (n = 69 studies, n = 280 assessments in total), in which at least one of the earlier‐mentioned outcomes was addressed. Identified studies most often addressed the physical health domain, with 118 assessments (42.3%) from 54 studies (78.3%). The predominant assessments were cardiac function (23 studies), upper limb and manual dexterity (21 studies), as well as mobility and gait (17 studies). The overall health domain encompassed aspects of health‐related quality of life, which was the most prevalent assessment in this domain (18 studies), as well as ADL (14 studies) and independence (7 studies). Overall health was addressed in 42 assessments (15.1%) from 30 studies (43.5%). Clinical status was the second most commonly addressed domain, with 54 studies (78.3%) and 103 assessments (36.8%). Generic and FA‐specific ataxia severity (35 and 27 studies, respectively) and molecular markers (17) were the most prevalent in this domain.

Patient‐centered outcome measures used in the included studies; n = 280 assessments from 69 studies for Friedreich's ataxia (FA)‐only patients and the division in domains and subdomains. [Color figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary.com]

None of the interventions focused on the social health domain in terms of interpersonal functioning. However, hearing (two studies) and speech (six studies) were addressed, which are physical symptoms that directly influence interactions with other people. In total, social health was investigated in only 11.6% of studies (n = 8) (and 2.9% of assessments, n = 8). Mental health was one of the least addressed domains, with four studies (5.8%) and five assessments (1.8%). Although caregivers were involved in four assessments (1.4%) from four studies (5.8%) as proxy ratings, caregiver burden and well‐being were never used. The economic domain—that is, healthcare service utilization, costs, and cost‐effectiveness—was neglected in all of the included studies.

Effectiveness of Intervention Across Health Domains

Most of the reported outcomes showed no differences, although several demonstrated positive effects, but they were rarely significant. Case reports for most subdomains reported positive effects. Details about domains and the effectiveness of interventions, including study designs and assessments, are available in Table S11.

Even though the physical health domain was most frequently addressed, just 10 significant positive results were reported for the following subdomains: upper extremity and manual dexterity, 59 mobility and gait (in the child population), 45 speech, 46 , 60 cardiac function, 43 , 61 exercise capacity, 50 , 62 hearing, 63 and neuromuscular function. 64

Included studies did not show a clear improvement in patients' quality of life (overall health domain). The improvement was obtained in specific subdomains, for example, vitality, 58 physical functioning, 49 health transition, 36 or social health. 65 Moreover, independence significantly improved in one study 54 and ADL in two studies. 48 , 66

Symptoms important for social interactions were effectively addressed. Speech improved significantly after pharmacotherapy 46 , 60 and hearing problems after the use of hearing device and cochlear implantation. 63 , 65 Speech therapy was more often investigated in mixed ataxia studies.

Several aspects of mental health were addressed in cognitive therapy, 67 but with no significant positive effect, except for improvements in short‐term memory, cognitive flexibility, and inhibition. There was no effect on depression. Conversely, significant improvements in cognitive function (measured by the Cerebellar Cognitive Affective Syndrome Scale [CCAS‐S]) have been reported, as well as subjective improvements in energy levels and significant improvements in the vitality. 50 , 68

Significant improvements in the generic ataxia scale SARA were reported in six studies, 39 , 50 , 56 , 61 , 69 , 70 in the FA‐specific scale FARS in four studies, 57 , 60 , 71 , 72 and in the mFARS in four studies. 47 , 48 , 73 , 74

The patient's impression of the disease reported two significant improvements. 45 , 66 Frataxin concentration was the most commonly investigated biomarker, with a significant positive effect reported four times. 44 , 57 , 58 , 75 In addition, reduced oxidative stress was reported once 84 or twice as a reduced ferritin level, leading also to a reduction in oxidative stress. 75 , 76 In neuroimaging, significant improvement in fractional anisotropy in white matter was reported, but with no correlation with SARA or FARS, 32 as well as a significant increase of gray matter volume in the pulvinar and posterior parietal cortex with a correlation with SARA. 77

Discussion

This systematic review identified 90 intervention studies in FA (69 FA only and 21 mixed ataxia) and analyzed key interventions, as well as their effects and alignment with standardized outcome sets of patient‐centered treatment and care. Most studies were small and varied in design and quality, ranging from case reports to RCTs. Overall, pharmacological interventions often showed mixed effects, with omaveloxolone demonstrating more consistent benefits. In contrast, nonpharmacological approaches, such as rehabilitation and particularly multidisciplinary programs, were more frequently reported to improve patient‐relevant outcomes. However, the evidence was based primarily on small case series and potential biases. Subgroup analyses also demonstrated a tendency for better intervention effects in a younger population, groups at earlier stages of FA, with a higher likelihood of faster disease progression.

Clinical Management Guidelines for FA indicated that most intervention recommendations are based on very low levels of evidence, with only a few achieving moderate strength but strong recommendation. 78 Multimodal rehabilitation has the strongest support for neurological components and fatigue, whereas cardiac interventions (eg, transplantation or heart devices), with limited evidence, may be considered on an individual basis in patients with impaired cardiac function. In our review, (multimodal) rehabilitation and heart interventions were the most prevalent nonpharmacological interventions, highlighting the importance of these approaches. Heart and muscle function are interdependent because the heart supplies oxygen and nutrients essential for muscle performance, while muscle activity supports cardiovascular health. Although movement‐related symptoms are often the most disruptive in everyday life in FA, 15 , 38 , 79 cardiac complications may be less apparent to patients yet represent a significant life‐threatening risk 38 ; therefore, it is crucial to address these issues. Presented findings highlight both the potential of specific therapies and the substantial gaps in the existing evidence base of interventional research in FA. Future research should focus on strengthening the evidence base for interventions in FA, with particular attention to their impact on patients' daily lives.

Nonpharmacological interventions were more patient and symptom oriented and more frequently reported positive outcomes because they typically target specific functional impairments. They may also involve more active engagement of patients; for example, physical activity can improve energy and motivation, 80 or social interaction may contribute to a better overall effect. 81 However, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the frequent use of case reports and quasi‐experimental designs, which are associated with a higher risk of bias. Pharmacological interventions often aim either to stop or at least to slow the disease progression; therefore, the positive effect may not be directly visible. Although placebo effects in neurodegenerative ataxias may reach up to 32% showing improvement in at least one ataxia rating scale, 82 the successful blinding of the RCT may minimize the expectation‐related bias. 54

Many medications proved ineffective; some improved only certain outcomes. Clear evidence for effective disease‐modifying therapies remains lacking. Current disease‐modifying therapeutic strategies in FA aim to increase frataxin expression or replacement, improve mitochondrial function, reduce oxidative stress, modulate downstream metabolic pathways, and provide neuroprotection. 83 Targeting multiple pathogenic mechanisms may enable complementary or combination approaches and support the development of more individualized treatments. Several mixed effects were reported for pharmacological interventions, where laboratory or imaging outcomes were improved, with no effect on clinical measures or PROMs. This mismatch between biological signals and clinical benefit underscores the complexity of FA and may indicate that an intervention influences underlying biological processes without yet producing detectable clinical or patient‐perceived benefits. Clinically meaningful changes in patient‐relevant outcomes may evolve gradually, requiring longer treatment and observation periods to capture clinical improvements. Understanding the mechanisms underlying therapeutic effects is crucial for optimizing interventions, establishing causal pathways, and translating promising findings into effective clinical treatments. Future research should therefore integrate the variety of outcomes, including laboratory, clinical, and patient‐reported outcomes, in a complementary manner, 84 , 85 to better capture the association between outcomes and the full spectrum of treatment effects, ensuring that advances translate into meaningful benefits for patients.

Idebenone was the most frequently investigated medication; however, the results were inconclusive. The clinical trial IONIA in the pediatric population showed that idebenone did not improve cardiac function, 33 whereas in the open‐label extension, potential therapeutic benefit to stabilize the disease progression and to improve specific symptoms, for example, fine motor skills and speech, was shown. 42 So far, omaveloxolone in the MOXIe trial demonstrated more consistent positive effects in terms of neurological progression assessed with mFARS. 47 , 48 Short‐term effects and the long‐term slowing of the progression in comparison with natural progression (almost 3 years) were demonstrated. 86 Greater improvement was reported for the patients without pes cavus and those who started the treatment earlier, suggesting its efficacy at the less severe FA stages. 73 Future research should therefore prioritize identifying responsive populations and specific symptoms to support more personalized and effective therapeutic strategies.

Outcome measures were heterogeneous across studies in this review, with clinical scales being most common and PROMs less prevalent. PROMs were reported in just more than half of the FA‐only studies, highlighting the need for possible greater patient involvement and the development of standardized, responsive measures. 87 , 88 , 89 Several recommendations have been made regarding outcome measures used in FA research (eg, core measure instruments, including clinical scales such as SARA, 9‐HPT, T25FW, and mFARS 113 , 114 ) to facilitate a comprehensive evaluation 119 and good comparability between studies. It is crucial to use standardized outcome measures and reporting, with consistent thresholds for clinical relevance. Although the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) is essential for determining whether observed changes are clinically meaningful, currently, no disease‐specific MCID values are available for FA. 90 Standard outcome measures in FA should capture clinically meaningful aspects of the disease, including neurological function and disease progression, health‐related quality of life, mental well‐being, and patient perspective. Selection should prioritize validated, responsive measures that are relevant to patients and aligned with the specific objectives of the intervention. This will enable consistent reporting and a clearer overview of interventional outcomes, ultimately leading to improved development of new therapies.

Physical health was the most frequently addressed domain, which demonstrates that FA is primarily considered a movement disorder. Holistic care of patients with FA requires recognizing it as a multidimensional disease and addressing more health domains, for example, psychosocial health. FA considerably affects mental health, with literature reporting the prevalence of depression in FA up to 40% 91 , 92 or other emotional issues (eg, decreased motivation, feeling fearful about the future, social isolation). 14 , 15 Deterioration in cognitive functions (eg, in emotional processing, social cognition) and CCAS has also been reported. 13 , 93 , 94 , 95 In addition, a loss of ambulation or transition between mobility devices as the disease progresses may directly influence mental health. 96 , 97 Patients with FA are often stigmatized in social life because of symptoms of movement and speech impairments resembling alcohol or drug use, which may further exacerbate the social isolation and mental health issues. 98 Although the gathered evidence level is low, the Clinical Management Guidelines strongly recommend counseling or therapy in case of depression and anxiety, as well as lifestyle changes (such as exercise, diet, social activities) or pharmacotherapy. 78 This indicates that psychosocial health is important and should be addressed more thoroughly to gather further evidence. Future interventional studies should therefore expand beyond physical function to systematically address psychosocial and cognitive domains, ensuring a holistic and patient‐centered approach.

In our review, we found no studies that considered caregivers' burden or the impact of the availability of a caregiver. The perspective of caregivers of patients with FA has been largely neglected but should be included in research and interventions. Caregivers play a central role in supporting individuals with FA, yet often experience substantial physical, emotional, social, and financial burden, which can negatively affect their own health and caregiving capacity. 137 Supporting caregivers may therefore not only improve caregiver well‐being but also indirectly enhance patient care.

FA is a tremendous economic and societal burden. 99 , 100 , 101 , 102 The economic and health resource outcomes in FA, including healthcare utilization, informal care, caregiver productivity losses, and adverse health events, constitute a gap in the current research that has only recently begun to be addressed. 103 , 104 Therefore, future interventional studies should also consider incorporating such outcomes to capture the full societal and economic impact of FA and to inform sustainable healthcare planning and policy. Given the complexity of FA, comprehensive evaluation, including clinical, patient‐reported, caregiver, health, economic, and psychosocial outcomes, may yield the best results.

Limitations

Although the searching strategy was broad and included several interventions, we cannot ensure that all existing interventions were included in this review. Studies with various designs and small sample sizes have been included, exhibiting a low‐to‐moderate bias. These aspects may significantly impact the level of evidence supporting the presented outcomes and limit their robustness and generalizability.

Implications for Future Research

Future interventional studies should use multicenter and robust study designs that incorporate a variety of clinical, patient‐reported, and health system–relevant end points. The literature has indicated that patients with FA are motivated to participate in studies, which could help facilitate the setup of larger studies. 105 However, common barriers include the ethical allocation to a placebo group, treatment side effects, and the logistics of travel and participation. 105 Innovative, remote, or app‐based interventional designs might overcome these barriers by reducing patient burden, increasing accessibility, and enabling more flexible participation in FA trials.

Interventions implemented in mixed ataxia populations can be further investigated in FA‐only populations to confirm efficacy and reduce heterogeneity across distinct disease entities. Several nonpharmacological interventions are currently ongoing that target various issues, for example, swallowing and respiratory function, 106 standing balance, 107 speech therapy involving behavioral intervention, 108 biofeedback therapy, 109 or NAD+ (Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (oxidized form)) and exercise. 110 Nonpharmacological interventions must be investigated using more robust trial designs because controlled designs are still underrepresented. Similarly, several ongoing clinical studies at various stages are investigating new medications for FA. 111 Omaveloxolone has been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA), since 2023 and 2024, respectively, 18 , 19 as the first FA disease‐modifying treatment for patients aged 16 years and older. Recently, vatiquinone, another candidate drug for FA treatment, was evaluated by the FDA; however, further evidence is still needed to prove its efficacy. 112 All presented studies and protocols show that there is constant development in the research on interventions for FA management, and new approaches are being tested, providing hope for patients with FA to gain access to another new and effective therapy.

Conclusion

This review summarizes current research on pharmacological and nonpharmacological interventions in FA and highlights symptoms and health domains that remain underexplored. Pharmacological interventions as disease‐modifying therapies targeting pathophysiology of FA have shown mostly limited benefits to date, with omaveloxolone exhibiting considerable effectiveness. Omaveloxolone, although not yet widely available, may potentially become part of standard care in the future. Nonpharmacological approaches showed promise in symptom‐driven research. Research has focused primarily on physical symptoms affecting movement, whereas many comorbidities, psychosocial health, caregiver burden, well‐being, and economic aspects remain poorly studied. These gaps underscore the urgent need for further research. Translating findings into effective therapies requires rigorous research and multidisciplinary approaches that integrate all relevant health domains and involve both patients and caregivers.
