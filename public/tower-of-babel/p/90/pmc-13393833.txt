Climate change impacts on European electricity demand and supply until 2100
Authors: Franziska Schöniger, Gustav Resch, Demet Suna, Peter Widhalm, Gerhard Totschnig, Nicolas Pardo-Garcia, Florian Hasengst, Herbert Formayer, Philipp Maier, David Leidinger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryRenewable and electrified energy systems are highly weather-dependent, making them vulnerable to climate change. Energy system modeling therefore requires high-quality data that captures the spatiotemporal complexity of climate conditions. We present SECURES-Energy, an open-access dataset providing hourly electricity demand and supply data for Europe at the national level from 1981 to 2100. Historical data are derived from ERA5 reanalysis, while future projections use two EURO-CORDEX scenarios (RCP 4.5/RCP 8.5). The dataset includes onshore and offshore wind, solar photovoltaic (PV), and hydropower generation, as well as all electricity demand components such as heating, cooling, and mobility. Results indicate no consistent trends for solar PV and hydropower across Europe. Offshore wind declines by up to −4%/−3% by 2035–2064 and −6%/−9% by 2071–2100 relative to 1981–2010. Cooling demand rises sharply (up to +80%/+149% by mid-century; +129%/+317% by end-century), while heating demand falls (−19%/−24% by mid-century; −25%/−40% by end-century). These findings highlight substantial climate-driven shifts in future electricity demand and supply.Keywords: renewable energy, climate change impact, RCP, GWL, hydropower, heating and cooling, extreme events, energy system modeling

Introduction

Renewable energy systems are highly linked to meteorological patterns and weather impacts. In the past, many energy system analyses in research or system planning were based on a single or a selection of historical weather years,1 representing average historical conditions from observations. With the ongoing decarbonization and electrification of energy systems, it has become increasingly important to take into account climate change, which has also been required by recent European regulations.2 This applies to adequate estimations of the future potential of variable renewable resources, such as wind, solar, and hydro, as well as the effect of temperature on electricity demand. Besides changing patterns of electricity generation from renewable sources and demand profiles, the frequency and intensity of extreme events are relevant for adequacy assessments in the electricity system and are impacted by climate change. This leads to “a pressing need to improve the representation of extreme weather events and long-term climate change”3 to represent climate risks for present and future electricity systems using datasets suitable for energy system modeing.4 Ignoring these effects risks underestimating infrastructure needs, flexibility requirements, and resilience measures in long-term planning.

Further, these datasets need to be “coherent, using homogenized and long-term climatic data sources and energy conversion models […] on a continental scale to study the possible (dis)balancing effects on a large area.”5 In recent years, there has been growing interest in climate change impact datasets for energy system modeling by research, policymakers, and the energy industry to consider the effects of climate variability and climate change. Several studies have been analyzing climate change’s impact on specific technologies or regions, or more comprehensive publications for the past and future. Some of them have also provided data, including time series of electricity demand and supply. Since a wide range of uncertainty comes from different climate projections6 and energy conversion models applied,7 previous studies came to the conclusion that a broad data basis8 and comparison of approaches and resulting datasets is necessary to capture these uncertainties.9

This paper addresses the research question of how climate change impacts electricity demand and supply in Europe. We provide the consistent open-access dataset SECURES-Energy containing electricity demand and supply profiles for 1981–2100 in a format suitable for electricity system modeling (hourly resolution). It can be used to analyze historical developments or long-term outlooks considering climate change based on the provided continuous hourly electricity demand and generation profiles. We apply robust meteorological and statistical methods from different research disciplines to analyze the reference period 1981–2010 from ERA5(-Land)10,11 and future climate impact projections derived from two emission scenarios (representative concentration pathway (RCP) 4.5 and RCP 8.512) 2011–2100. Almost all European countries are covered by the electricity supply profiles for wind (onshore and offshore), hydro (run-of-river (RoR) and reservoir), and solar generation. On the side of the demand profiles, we provide data for the EU countries (except for Cyprus), Switzerland, Great Britain, and Norway.

The novelty of this study lies in its harmonized integration of climate and energy system data for Europe and is 2-fold. First, we develop a long-term dataset of electricity demand and renewable energy supply that enables consistent country-level analyses across both historical and future climate conditions. Second, we apply this dataset within a unified analytical framework to quantify how climate change differentially affects individual renewable supply components as well as heating and cooling demand across European countries. Overall, the methodology provides a foundation for integrated climate-energy impact assessments at multiple scales—from incorporating various meteorological variables into energy demand and supply modeling, to supporting evaluations across different geographical levels, and enabling robust energy system planning under climate uncertainty.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section state of the art gives an overview of the existing literature in this field, clustered into the different demand- and supply-side components. Section methods and data describes the applied method of processing climate data for energy system information. Section results and discussion presents the resulting data and discusses the corresponding analyses, shedding light on the different renewable generation technologies and the demand side. Section key findings and implications concludes this paper, opening the space for future work.

State of the art

Energy system models play a crucial role in informing policy decisions and reflecting societal priorities. They are not merely technical instruments but essential tools for exploring uncertain futures and possible options13 and for guiding policy and public interest decisions.14 With growing concerns about climate change and the urgent need for effective political action, it is increasingly important to incorporate climate impacts into energy system modeling to design robust future energy systems capable of withstanding climate-related uncertainties.15” Therefore, there has been increasing interest in studying the impact of climate change on energy systems in recent years. This section provides an overview of the literature, divided into climate change impact on (1) energy systems in general, (2) energy demand, (3) hydro, wind, and solar generation, and (4) thermal generation. It identifies literature gaps and describes the presented dataset’s SECURES-Energy16 specific contributions.

Climate change impact on energy systems in general

Many studies analyze climate change’s impact, considering various temporal and spatial scopes. Generally, the effects of climate change on electricity demand or supply on a larger spatial scale are usually less distinct than for specific regions due to smoothing effects as soon as the considered spatial scale exceeds the spatial scale of relevant weather patterns, which stresses the need for highly interconnected systems and electricity trade between countries.8 Further, the choice of climate models and scenarios and impact model assumptions (e.g., crop models in the case of biomass or hydrological models in the case of hydropower) lead to diverging projections of the impact of climate change.17 While most studies include multiple demand-supply-side effects,18 evaluations of single components of electricity systems are more prevalent in the literature.17 Most studies dealing with a single technology focus on hydropower (e.g., in the USA19 or Portugal20), followed by wind power (as shown in the review by21 or the comprehensive climate model analysis by22), thermal power plants (e.g., focusing on specific geographic23 or technological conditions24), solar power (in global analyses like25 or for single countries26), and bioenergy.27

Bloomfield et al.7 quantified the sensitivity of power systems to different energy scenario assumptions and climate change impacts. They found that the uncertainty associated with climate change is smaller than the divergence from different power system assumptions. They found different directions of change for wind and solar generation in Europe impacted by climate change. In a comprehensive literature review of studies projecting climate change impacts on energy systems globally, Yalew et al.9 found differentiated impacts of climate change across regions. At the same time, there is a prevalence of projected decreases in hydropower potentials. They reported unremarkable or slightly positive climate change impacts on regional solar power potentials as a net result of changes in irradiation and temperature, while the results regarding wind power diverge across regions and between studies. They stated mixed results of climate change impacts on bioenergy potential, which is more complex due to the linked effect of CO2 fertilization (increasing the potential), temperature and precipitation impacts (decreasing the potential), and socioeconomic factors such as competition with other land use forms because of climate change. For Western Europe, averaged climate change impacts across studies showed the largest decreases in hydro generation, thermal generation, and heating demand (reduction by 5–10%). They found evidence for decreasing wind power for southern Europe and slightly increasing wind power for central and northern Europe. Gernaat et al.28 used climate and integrated assessment models to estimate the effect of climate change on eight renewable technologies across two warming scenarios worldwide. In the baseline warming scenario, they found the most significant impact to be an increased availability of bioenergy (depending on the strength of CO2 fertilization). They also found minor impacts on solar power and—depending on the region—increasing or decreasing hydro and wind power generation. In a future mitigation scenario, more minor climate impacts are offset by a more substantial reliance on renewables in the energy system. Simoes et al.8 studied the effect of climate variability until 2050 in six EURO-CORDEX (Coordinated Downscaling Experiment—European Domain) scenarios. They found changes of −3% to +3% for solar photovoltaics (PVs) and −4% to +2% for wind power for Europe as a whole, depending on the scenario.

Climate change impact on energy demand

The most significant impact on energy demand is the effect of increasing temperatures on heating and cooling demand, showing clear increasing trends for cooling and decreasing trends for heating across regions and climate projections in Europe,29 in the USA,30 and in China.31 To estimate the effect, integrated modeling is often used.32 Depending on whether a country is located in a relatively colder or warmer climate, for the overall energy demand, net negative (for colder countries) or net positive (for warmer countries) effects are expected.18 Dowling4 found demand-side impacts to be larger than supply-side impacts and larger in southern Europe than in northern Europe. This is due to a decrease in heating demand in the residential and services sectors, leading to reduced energy demand, which outweighs all other climate change impacts in the EU.

Spinoni et al.33 analyzed eleven high-resolution bias-adjusted EURO-CORDEX simulations (RCP 4.5 and RCP 8.5), all showing a significant decrease in heating demand, especially over Scandinavia and European Russia, and an increase in cooling demand, especially pronounced in the Mediterranean region and the Balkans. Viguié et al.34 presented an extensive literature review of the effects of climate change on energy demand. They found that studies almost exclusively focus on energy demand for heating and cooling, mainly on average demand (instead of peak demand). It is important to note that different climate change adaptation possibilities differ substantially in the resulting energy demand (e.g., air conditioning versus shading measures in building design). Rode et al.35 estimated the impacts of climate change on heating and cooling demand. They found a net decrease globally, with significant increases in cooling demand in the tropics and heating reductions in colder countries. Notably, the results of changed energy demand due to adaptation measures are highly dependent on the affordability of this increased demand. Although temperature is the dominant determinant of heating and cooling demand, recent studies indicate that humidity can substantially amplify cooling energy requirements beyond the effects of temperature alone.36,37 This influence is more pronounced in warmer regions than in those with moderate climates, where cooling demand can differ by 10–15% compared to temperature-induced demand alone.36

Climate change impact on hydropower, wind, and solar generation

Hydropower is the technology that has been analyzed the most in the context of climate change impacts17 due to the prevalence of this technology in the renewable electricity mix globally, long investment cycles, and the high dependency on precipitation patterns.18 Climate change effects affecting hydropower are—inter alia—changed precipitation and evaporation patterns, altered stream flows, increased variability, droughts and floods, depletion of permanent glacier and ice storage, reduction in seasonal snow storage and earlier snowmelt.38 There are also secondary effects of climate change and natural hazards relevant to hydropower, including glacier lake outbursts, landslides, and sediment load.39

Gøtske & Victoria40 found that the impact of climate change on hydro inflow is more significant than that of interannual and inter-model variability, although hydropower generation generally has a strong interannual variation. Van Vliet et al.41 expect global gross hydropower potential to increase between +2.4% (RCP 2.6) and +6.3% (RCP 8.5) for the 2080s compared to 1971–2000. They found a significant decline in hydropower potential and cooling water discharge potential for thermal power plants in Europe. According to their analysis, Southern Europe is among the regions with considerable (>20%) declines in hydropower potential, which is in line with other findings.20

In Europe, there is robust evidence indicating a seasonal shift in RoR hydropower generation from summer to winter, driven by altered snowmelt dynamics; however, this pattern exhibits notable regional variability.42 One important factor is the elevation of the hydropower plant.43 When analyzing changes in hydropower generation, it is essential to evaluate changing inflows in relation to dam structure characteristics, as increased inflows do not necessarily lead to increased hydropower generation in all situations.44 Another factor to consider regarding climate change impacts is the potential for increasing competition between hydropower and other forms of water usage, such as irrigation in the agricultural sector. For Portugal, a study showed a 17% increase in water needs for irrigation for average hydrological conditions by 2050, reducing the annual runoff available for hydropower.45

For Europe, there has been emerging consensus for increasing wind power potential in the north and a slight decrease over the south (including the Mediterranean area),21 with any change in regional wind resources over the current century being smaller than the current interannual variability of wind generation. However, studies based on more recent climate scenarios show a decrease in wind power potential for the whole of Europe until the end of the century (−10% to −20%),46 and other studies didn’t find clear trends for onshore wind in Europe impacted by climate change.7,8

Based on EURO-CORDEX ensemble data, change in solar PV generation was found to be in the range of −14% to +2% by the end of the century.47 There are regional differences, though, with the most significant decreases in northern countries due to cloudier conditions. However, according to this analysis, temporal stability shows a slight positive trend in southern countries despite increasing efficiency losses due to rising ambient temperature trends.

Climate change impact on thermal generation

The availability and efficiency of thermal power plants depend on the average temperature, humidity, pressure, and water availability.48 Thermal power generation is expected to be impacted by climate change, causing reduced streamflow and warming ambient and streamflow temperatures covering the cooling requirements of the power cycle. This field shows opposing trends: Thermal generation is expected to cover a smaller share of power demand in decarbonized electricity systems with high renewable penetration. Hence, impacts on thermal generation are less significant in those systems. However, these systems' remaining thermal power plants might be increasingly equipped with carbon capture and storage (CCS) technology, which is expected to have increased cooling water requirements.49 In addition, replacing fossil fuels in thermal power plants with alternative fuels such as bio-methane or hydrogen can also be anticipated. Studies analyzing the impact on thermal generation focus mainly on other continents such as Asia50 or South America,51 with projected thermal power plant capacity during peak demand being projected to decrease by 0.8–1.2 percentage points with each degree Celsius of additional warming, requiring an extra 18–27 GW of thermal capacity globally.52 For Germany and single heat wave days until 2070, a reduced power output of all considered once-through cooling (OTC) units down to 66% was found.53 Impacts are around three times higher for OTC than for closed-circuit cooling (CCC) water systems.

Besides the impact on thermal power plant availability and efficiency, there are interactions of the hydro-meteorological cycle with the hydropower sector, which is described above, and the availability of bioenergy and fossil fuel resources, as well as CCS applications,17 which are not covered by our analysis.

The presented dataset SECURES-Energy16 contributes to the thematic fields above by providing a consistent climate and electricity system dataset for two RCPs. The dataset is in a standard format used in energy system modeling. It provides high temporal resolution (hourly) of the electricity generation (wind, hydropower, solar PV) and demand (incl. weather-dependent heating, cooling, and e-mobility and non-weather-dependent components) data and aggregates it on the national NUTS0 level—without losing the information due to the high granularity of the underlying climate data. Additionally, it includes hydropower, which is often missing in comparable datasets containing several electricity system components due to the complex hydrological and energy conversion methods applied. The energy conversion methods are mainly based on state-of-the-art methods from the literature, which we harmonized regarding the temporal and spatial resolution and the underlying climate data, enabling a consistent and holistic analysis of future electricity systems. Finally, the dataset provides all relevant electricity generation and supply components until 2100, allowing a range of analyses to be conducted as short-term trend evaluations and long-term projections in the energy system.

The novelty of this study lies in its harmonized integration of climate and energy system data for Europe and in the comprehensive methodology applied to process meteorological data for use in energy system models. The paper outlines a consistent and reproducible framework that transforms high-resolution climate inputs into energy-relevant variables, ensuring temporal and spatial coherence across all components. By allowing for assessing scenario- and model-induced uncertainty of climate change impacts on energy systems, the presented dataset and paper add to the scientific discussion and comparison of recently published datasets for Europe such as C3S Energy (electricity demand and supply from wind, solar PV and hydropower for the past 40 years and the future based on ERA5 reanalysis data and ENTSO-E energy data),5 Antonini et al. (wind power generation, solar power generation, hydropower inflow, heating demand, and cooling demand based on ERA5 reanalysis (1940–2023) and CMIP5 EURO-CORDEX (2006–2100)),54 Pan-European Climate Database (PECD) (based on ERA5 reanalysis and selected CMIP6 global climate models for 1980–2021 and 2015–2065),55 or Bloomfield et al. (wind power capacity factor, solar power factor, and degree days for 1950–2020 and 2020–2050).56 SECURES-Energy enables comprehensive, reproducible, and uncertainty-aware analyses of future electricity systems. This combination of methodological transparency and dataset consistency advances the robustness and comparability of climate-energy assessments across Europe.

Methods and data

Using energy conversion methods, we convert meteorological variables to hourly electricity supply and demand profiles that can broadly be applied in energy system models. There are two datasets underlying the following analysis: (1) ERA5(-Land) reanalysis data covering the period 1981–2010, and (2) two bias-adjusted regionally downscaled climate model projections covering the period 2011–2100 (EURO-CORDEX ICHEC-EC-EARTH—KNMI-RACCMO22E RCP 4.5/RCP 8.5). The two underlying emission scenarios provide assumptions for a medium (RCP 4.5) and a strong (RCP 8.5) climate change scenario for Europe until 2100.

Underlying climate scenarios

The presented hourly demand and supply profile dataset SECURES-Energy16 is based on the meteorological dataset SECURES-Met,57,58 which was generated within the same project. SECURES-Met provides temperature (2 m), radiation, wind power potential, and hydropower potential and was further refined to retrieve all electricity generation and demand components needed for modeling the future electricity system. SECURES-Met covers the historical period based on ERA5(-Land) reanalysis data and two bias-adjusted possible emission pathways for the future using two climate scenarios: One representing a medium (RCP 4.5) and one a strong (RCP 8.5) climate change signal (EURO-CORDEX ICHEC-EC-EARTH—KNMI-RACCMO22E RCP 4.5/RC P8.5). The selection of possible regional climate projections was limited to the models for which hydrological river runoff data for the whole of Europe on a daily basis were available from the E-HYPE database.64 The comparison of the temperature development in Europe within the 21st century with the CMIP 6 ensemble indicates that the low emission scenario model “ICHEC-EC.EARTH-rcp45-rlilp1_KNMI-RACMO22E” shows good agreement with the ensemble mean of the CMIP6 models forced with the emission scenario SSP1-2.6 and the high emission scenario “ICHEC-EC.EARTH-rcp85-rlilp1_KNMI-RACMO22E” with SSP3-7.0 58 (see Figure 1). The difference in the temperature development in Europe between the low- and high-emission scenarios starts around 2030 and becomes more relevant in the second half of the century, leading to a temperature difference of more than 2° at the end of the century between the two scenarios. The global warming level (GWL) 2.0°C is reached in the year 2035 in the high emission scenario (RCP 8.5) and in 2044 in the low emission scenario (RCP 4.5). The GWL 4.0°C is only reached by the high emission scenario (in the year 2082).

The underlying climate scenario set SECURES-Met is an aggregated European-wide dataset for the historical period and climate change projections that fulfills all relevant criteria for energy modeling. It has an hourly temporal resolution, considers local effects up to a spatial resolution of 1 km, and has a practicable size, as all variables are aggregated to NUTS (national) regions using variable-specific aggregation methods. Additionally, meteorological information from wind speed and river runoff is directly converted into power production using state-of-the-art techniques and the current information on the potential location of power plants. It provides hydropower capacity factors for RoR and reservoirs, which are often missing in comparable datasets but are highly relevant when considering climate change impacts in countries with high shares of hydropower.

To estimate the wind speed at 150 m above ground, the 10 m wind speeds of ERA5-Land and Euro-CORDEX were mapped to the wind speeds of COSMO-REA665 at 150 m level using statistical methods. Then, the relative power curves for onshore and offshore wind turbines were applied to the wind speed. Full information concerning the methods used to produce the meteorological base information is described by Formayer et al.58

All meteorological variables were bias-adjusted using statistical methods66 and the ERA5-Land data for the reference period 1991–2020. This implies that during the reference period, the bias-adjusted models and ERA5-Land have the same climatology (see Figure S7, Methods S1). The bias adjustment method used does not impact the climate change signal of the individual climate models.

The scenario analysis uses a GWL approach. The definition of the GWLs follows the method of Becsi & Formayer.67 A GWL is reached when the average climate of 20 consecutive years first crosses a given global temperature threshold. The average temperature of the period 1850–1900 from the 'historical' general circulation model (GCM) runs served as a baseline, as defined in the IPCC AR6.68

Electricity demand patterns

The presented dataset SECURES-Energy contains all relevant electricity demand components projected for future decarbonized energy systems. We provide the weather-dependent and non-weather-dependent components so that the dataset enables the modeling of the complete electricity system.

Weather-dependent electricity demand

On the demand side of the system, the main weather-dependent electricity demand components are heating and cooling. Further, we generated weather-dependent e-mobility charging profiles to consider temperature-related efficiency losses during cold temperatures. The resulting profiles were normalized with respect to the reference year 2010.

Heating and cooling

The heating and cooling demand time series are based on temperature-dependent regressions combined with the temperature variables from the two climate scenarios.57 Population-weighted temperature data were used as population density is highly relevant for heating and cooling demand.33 The regressions cover the demand for sanitary hot water, space heating, and space cooling for the residential and tertiary sectors. We used regressions (day curves in hourly resolution) for all sectors in each EU country based on60,61,62 (details on the regression method in the Methods S1, Section 3). All data were processed in NUTS0 resolution (national profile).

E-mobility

The energy consumption of electric vehicles depends on ambient temperature. In winter, the car is heated, and in summer, the vehicle needs to be cooled; therefore, the electricity consumption is increased in addition to the consumption due to driving.

The profiles developed in the project Define,63 are based on a German survey69 in the residential sector, and consider different weekdays, seasons, and residential areas. Three different user groups were considered: Users 1) charging the electric vehicle at work, 2) using the electric vehicle to drive to work but not charging there, and 3) not driving to work. A temperature correction formula based on Liu et al.70 was used to capture the effect of ambient temperature on electricity load, showing a U-shape with a minimum load at around 20°C ambient temperature. In this paper, Figure 2 shows energy consumption per kilometer vs. ambient temperature (energy demand of about 500 electric vehicles depending on temperature). This polynomial was digitized71 into data points and was fitted to a third-order polynomial to determine its parameters. To obtain a unitless scaling function f(T) (Equation 2) for the vehicle consumption, the data points were normalized to the mean value of the observations (0.1497 kWh/km) before the regression. We limited the function for extreme temperatures to the value of 1.6 in order not to get unrealistic scaling.

Non-weather-dependent demand

The provided dataset SECURES-Energy also includes other relevant, non-weather-dependent demand components in a decarbonized electricity system. We differ between electricity demand for industry, electrolysis, and the rest.

First, the non-temperature-dependent industry demand profiles (paper, non-metallic minerals, iron and steel, food and tobacco, and chemicals and petrochemicals) directly used from60,61,62 were calculated, assuming that heat demand correlates with electricity demand in the industry. While we assume that the heating and cooling demand strongly depends on outdoor temperature (see Section weather-dependent electricity demand), the industry demand profile mainly depends on industrial processes. Each subsector has day profiles for each month of the year and type of day (working days, Saturdays or days before a holiday, Sundays or holidays) (see Table 5).

The annual sum of the demand component for overall industrial electricity demand was normalized, accounting for 1,000,000. This corresponds to a normalized value of 200,000 per single sector (non-metallic minerals, paper, iron and steel, food and tobacco, and chemicals and petrochemicals).

The electricity demand for hydrogen electrolysis was assumed to be constant over the year but can be flexibly optimized when using the demand profiles in energy system modeling.

In addition to the above-listed demand components, the rest electricity demand profile was generated, covering all remaining demand components in the electricity system. We retrieved the profile of the rest electricity demand by deducing the specific sectors above from the overall electricity demand profile provided by Resch et al.72 The detailed methodological steps are in the Methods S1, Section 1. The underlying assumption is that the normalized profile of the rest electricity demand will stay the same in the future since main changes are expected to happen in other components of the electricity demand, e.g., e-mobility and heating, which are provided separately. As a further step in energy system modeling, the demand profiles of all sectors can be scaled so that the annual demand of a specific (weather) year corresponds to an amount specified in the respective scenario.

Electricity generation patterns

For SECURES-Energy, we generated normalized hourly profiles for solar PV, onshore and offshore wind, and RoR hydropower generation. As a further step in energy system modeling, the hourly profiles can be scaled to the installed power or annual generation per technology according to the respective scenario.

Photovoltaic power

The generated PV power was calculated from the time series of population-weighted direct normal radiation (DNI) and global horizontal radiation (GHI) provided by the meteorological dataset SECURES-Met.57 For the detailed method of generating the radiation profiles, see Formayer et al.58,73 (in57,58 , DNI is called BNI). The PV potential is calculated as the yield per installed PV capacity.

Since the PV potential depends on latitude and longitude, it was computed at the NUTS2 (subnational) level using the centroid coordinates of each NUTS region and population-weighted DNI and GHI data. In the next step, the PV potential was aggregated on the national level (NUTS0). For the detailed method of PV potential calculation, see Section 4 in the Methods S1.

Onshore wind power

The meteorological dataset SECURES-Met,57 which was the basis for our electricity generation profiles, provides hourly time series for each country’s onshore and offshore wind potential as numbers in the interval [0, 1]. The calculation of these time series is described in detail by Formayer et al.58 It considers wind speed at 150 m combined with the power curves of specific turbine types and a geographic information system (GIS)-based analysis of suitable land for wind power in high spatial resolution. These climate model data are bias corrected for use in energy system modeling. For bias correction, we used a reference dataset59 containing wind potential calculations for the control period from 1995 to 2018. The time series of the climate models were adjusted to this reference dataset using Quantile Mapping. The mapping was calculated based on the quantile function Fref−1 of the reference dataset and the distribution function Fmodel of the climate model data in the control period (see Equation 3):

We applied a loss factor of 0.85 (15% loss) for wind park shading and efficiency losses.

Offshore wind power

Offshore wind profiles were taken directly from the meteorological dataset SECURES-Met,57 and the method is described in detail by Formayer et al.58 SECURES-Met provides an hourly time series for the wind potential for each country, as shown in the numbers in the interval [0, 1]. We applied a loss factor of 0.85 (15% loss) for wind park shading and efficiency losses. The dataset was already bias-adjusted, and we did not apply any additional quantile mapping.

Hydropower

RoR hydropower profiles were taken directly from the meteorological dataset SECURES-Met,57 and the method is described in detail by Formayer et al.58 The climate models provide the time series of each country’s daily energy generated by the RoR power plants (based on the current installed capacity). These time series were first converted into potential time series in the interval [0, 1] by dividing the values by the current installed capacity. The potential time series were resampled to 1-h intervals. SECURES-Met57 additionally provides hydro reservoir generation profiles, which are not part of this paper (see Table 2).

Results and discussion

This section describes the impacts of climate change on electricity generation and demand as derived from the dataset SECURES-Energy. We chose the two countries Spain and Germany as two of the largest energy systems in Europe and as examples for the result presentation of different climates—namely, southern and central Europe—to visualize differences in the impacts of climate change and give an overview of different regions and demand and supply components in Sections annual electricity generation, annual electricity demand, and seasonal patterns of hydropower generation. However, the dataset SECURES-Energy provides the basis for analyzing all EU and several additional European countries. In the Methods S1, we show the results for all countries (see Figures S3–S6). Section summary and discussion of changes in generation and demand for Europe presents an overview of relative changes in solar PV, wind, and RoR hydropower generation, as well as e-heating and e-cooling demand in all assessed countries by the end of the century. In the following results sections, we present the anomaly caused by climate change in the chosen climate models (RCP 4.5 and 8.5) comparing future periods in the climate models to the historical period of the ERA5(-Land) data. As described in Section underlying climate scenarios, all meteorological variables were bias-adjusted using statistical methods66 and the ERA5(-Land) data for a reference period ensuring the same climatology for the bias-adjusted models and ERA5-Land in this reference period. The observations and the bias-adjusted climate model results for the reference period 1981–2000 show a good agreement not only in terms of the mean climatological values but over the entire range of observed values (see Figure S7 in the Methods S1). This ensures that the shown annual capacity factor anomalies are correct. However, a limitation of this approach is that the models might not be able to fully cover all aspects of the reference data (see Section key findings and implications).

Relative increases in cooling are higher in colder countries, as shown for Germany; relative decreases in heating are higher in warmer countries (see also Figures S3 and S4 for all countries). Due to the very low cooling demand in the reference period in cold countries, the relative increase and the interannual variability are extremely high (see, e.g., Norway in Figure S4). This also leads to extreme spikes in these countries in some climate years due to the methodological approach. However, it is essential to note that in Germany (as in other colder countries), the absolute heating demand is much higher than the absolute cooling demand, resulting in a net decrease in energy demand for heating and cooling (as exemplified for Austria by Suna et al.74 In warmer countries such as Spain, climate change increases the overall energy demand for heating and cooling, whereas it decreases the overall energy demand in colder countries such as Germany (as also shown by Bloomfield et al.7). This seasonal shift of energy demand for heating and cooling also implies a higher temporal correlation and compatibility with solar PV generation and hydro RoR generation in some countries (see example of Germany in Figure S2B in the Data S1).

Seasonal patterns of hydropower generation

Besides trends in annual electricity generation changes, climate change impacts on seasonal patterns are also relevant for energy systems. Especially in electricity systems with high shares of hydropower, the seasonal shifts in hydropower generation have essential implications for flexibility and storage needs in the system. We show two EU countries with high amounts of hydropower generation as examples: France and Austria (see Figure 5). While climate change changes the seasonal pattern of hydropower in Austria (B), it intensifies the already existing pattern of lower generation during summer in France (A). In Austria, earlier runoff in spring, changed cover and snow melting periods, and altered precipitation patterns during winter (rain instead of snow) lead to a more evenly distributed hydropower generation over the year, implicating lower seasonal storage requirements.74 Still, lower generation during summer may lead to increased storage requirements during dry periods in summer. This pattern can also be observed in other countries, e.g., Germany (see Figure S2B in the Data S1). In France, the pattern of high generation during winter and low generation during summer is intensified with climate change impact, increasing the already existing seasonal storage need. A parallel pattern can be observed in Spain (see Figure S2A in the Data S1).

Summary and discussion of changes in generation and demand for Europe

The dataset provides information on relative changes in electricity generation and demand due to climate change impact in the two assessed emission scenarios (RCP 4.5 and RCP 8.5). As an overview, we provide results on relative changes in 30 years in the middle and at the end of the century compared to 1981–2010. The results for the end of the century (2071–2100) are displayed in Figure S1 in Data S1. The results for the impact of climate change at the mid-century (2035–2064) compared to the reference period (1981–2010) show no consistency in the sign of change for hydropower generation across Europe and a slightly positive trend for solar PV, with hydropower showing a higher variation and only minor impacts are visible for solar PV generation (see Figure 6). There is a slight negative trend for PV generation in some Northern European countries toward the end of the century. For wind onshore and hydro RoR generation, different signs of change can be observed in several countries in the middle versus the end of the century. This shows that interannual variability (especially of hydropower) is higher than climate change impact in some countries in the two considered emission scenarios. In the considered RCP 4.5 and RCP 8.5 scenarios, we observe a slight negative development of offshore wind capacity factors by the mid-century (up to −4.1%/-2.5%), which turns into a consistent negative trend in offshore wind generation across various countries by the end of the century (Except for Greece) (up to −6%/-9%).

Technical validation

The primary objective of SECURES-Energy is to provide electricity profiles that are consistent and coherent across all relevant demand and supply components in decarbonized energy systems, with high temporal resolution (hourly) and broad geographic coverage (Europe). This design enables flexible application in a wide range of energy system modeling contexts and research questions. While we acknowledge that the plausibility of these profiles is essential for building confidence in the models, a direct comparison with actual historical electricity supply and demand at hourly resolution is challenging for several reasons. For PV and wind energy, SECURES-Energy profiles are based on standardized reference installations (e.g., fixed PV tilt, hub height, turbine type; see Section electricity generation patterns)), which differ substantially from real-world configurations. Therefore, validation should primarily be understood as an assessment of meteorological consistency rather than a direct comparison of energy balances.

The meteorological variables—temperature, solar radiation, wind power potential, and hydropower potential (run-of-river and reservoir)—that constitute the SECURES-Met dataset used in this study were validated to ensure reliability and scientific robustness (see Methods S1, Section 1; Figure S7; originally described by Formayer et al.58). Solar PV generation was not explicitly validated; however, it exhibits a strong correlation with the validated solar radiation profile. Unlike electricity generation profiles, which are directly linked to meteorological variables (e.g., wind power generation to wind speed, solar PV generation to solar radiation), the relationship between electricity demand profiles and temperature is more complex, as demand depends on numerous weather-dependent and weather-independent factors (see Section electricity demand patterns). This complexity necessitates additional validation.

All SECURES-Energy generation and demand profiles used in this study are normalized to enable flexible scaling under any capacity assumptions in energy system models. For validation purposes, however, absolute values are required for all 14 demand components (see Section electricity demand patterns). Developing such a scenario lies beyond the scope of this paper; therefore, we adopt a 2030 scenario from,74,85 which represents the earliest available year and provides the most suitable basis for comparison with historical electricity demand. This scenario reflects measures aimed at achieving full decarbonization in Europe by 2050, including extensive sector coupling and electrification of industry and mobility. For validation, both emission pathways (RCP 4.5 and RCP 8.5) are considered, as they offer a more robust distribution while warming potential remains nearly identical for 2030 (see Figure 1).

We chose four countries representing northern (Finland), southern (Spain), and central Europe (Romania and France) to compare total load for the years 2015–2024 (without 2020 due to COVID effects) from ENTSO-E (see Figure 7).86

This discrepancy arises from the scenario assumptions required for validation, which include greater electrification of the 2030 energy system compared to 2015–2024. In-depth analysis shows that this is due to overestimation mainly in summer. Possible reasons for this, in addition to the greater electrification assumed for the 2030 energy system, include higher cooling demand due to elevated temperatures in the 2030 scenarios, increased air-conditioning penetration, and changes in user behavior.

Key findings and implications

The dataset provides important insights into the climate sensitivity of electricity demand and renewable energy supply across Europe. In summary, this work developed a dataset capturing the impacts of climate change on all relevant electricity demand and supply components for Europe in a suitable format for energy system modeling. This resulted in the open-access dataset SECURES-Energy16 containing two different climate change projections based on EURO-CORDEX models (RCP 4.5 and RCP 8.5) and ERA5(-Land) reanalysis data in hourly resolution covering the years 1981–2100. It is a transient dataset allowing for the quantification of interannual variability of electricity demand and supply, fostering to overcome the limitation of using one “reference year” in energy system modeling. SECURES-Energy, containing historical data and climate projections in high temporal and spatial resolution, is a ready dataset to be implemented in various kinds of modeling applications, e.g., scenario assessment in energy system modeling, heating and cooling strategies for specific regions, or the analysis of security of supply aspects in energy systems. Almost all European countries are covered by the electricity supply profiles for wind (onshore and offshore), hydro (RoR and reservoir - reservoir profiles are available under57), and solar PV generation. The demand profiles contain all relevant electricity demand components in future electricity systems. The provided weather-dependent demand components are residential and tertiary heating and cooling (space heating and sanitary hot water) and e-mobility charging demand. The non-weather-dependent demand profiles cover the industrial electricity demand, the electricity demand for electrolysis, and a rest electricity demand profile. On the side of the demand profiles, data for the EU countries excluding Cyprus and additionally including Switzerland, Great Britain, and Norway is provided.

The analysis of annual electricity supply across Europe shows the highest interannual variability in hydro run-of-river generation and the lowest in solar PV generation. No clear trend is observed under the two considered climate scenarios for run-of-river hydropower, onshore wind, and solar PV. In contrast, offshore wind capacity factors exhibit a negative trend, declining by up to 4.1%/-2.5% by mid-century and −6%/-9% by the end of the century under the RCP 4.5/8.5 scenarios. Climate change has a strong positive impact on cooling demand (up to 1.8 to 2.5 times higher) and a significant negative impact on heating demand (reduced by around −19% to −24%) across European countries by mid-century compared to the reference period 1981–2010. Relative changes in heating demand (cooling demand) are highest in warmer (colder) countries. Regarding climate change impacts on seasonal generation patterns of RoR hydropower, we show a shift from summer to winter for Austria and Germany, implying a more even profile over the year and reducing annual storage demand. In contrast, the pattern of low summer runoff is intensified by climate change in countries such as Spain and France. Most of these findings are in line with recent literature (see Section results and discussion), while the choice of meteorological variables and energy conversion models, regions, and climate models and scenarios in different studies lead to uncertainties and differences between studies, especially for the assessment of the climate change impact on electricity generation.

The limitation arising from the choice of climate projections highlights the need for inter-model comparisons across different regions and scenarios. In this paper, we focus on the impact of climate change on directly weather-dependent renewable generation sources. However, for a more integrated analysis, the effects of climate change on bioenergy and fossil fuel resources or heat pumps in connection with geothermal energy and the temperature impact on the power transmission grids can be included in further analyses by utilizing the hourly temperature profile. While the effect of climate change in terms of increasing or decreasing overall supply or demand is relatively straightforward, analyses of impacts on variability are more complex. While there are more studies on climate change impacts on overall electricity demand and renewable generation, there is a research gap for evaluating extreme events and more disruptive impacts on infrastructure. Possible ways forward are to use the dataset in energy system modeling and assess the impact on the security of supply and adequacy assessments.

This paper is a starting point, presenting the open-access dataset SECURES-Energy and providing a basis for further analyses. The dataset is open to the scientific community and other interested parties, and we encourage comparative studies with different datasets and more detailed evaluations of specific aspects.

Limitations of the study

The results presented in this study are subject to several limitations related to data availability, modeling assumptions, and methodological choices.

First, the analysis relies on a limited set of climate projections (EURO-CORDEX RCP 4.5 and RCP 8.5). While these scenarios capture a range of future climate conditions, the choice of models constrains the representation of climate uncertainty. Inter-model variability is known to be substantial, and a more comprehensive ensemble would be required to fully capture the range of possible climate impacts on electricity demand and supply.

Second, although bias adjustment was applied to ensure consistency between historical and projected climate data, this method cannot fully reproduce all characteristics of the observed climate. In particular, spatial and temporal variability, extreme events, or other statistical properties and higher moments may not be adequately represented. It is important that even if the individual variables are bias-adjusted, differences between the climate models and observations can still be caused by other spatial structures or distortion of the physical relationships due to the bias adjustment or the climate model specifics, and therefore, impact the anomaly of the presented annual heating and cooling demand.

Third, the modeling of electricity demand involves several simplifying assumptions. Heating and cooling demand are derived from temperature-based regressions and do not account for all relevant drivers. For example, humidity effects on cooling demand are excluded due to the lack of consistent hourly humidity data across the study domain; incorporating humidity effects would have introduced significant uncertainty and reduced comparability. Therefore, this study accounts only for temperature-dependent effects when projecting future cooling demand, potentially leading to underestimation in warmer regions. In addition, the analysis assumes constant technology adoption rates, such as air-conditioning and heat pumps, and therefore only captures temperature-driven changes in demand rather than structural shifts in energy use. However, previous studies have demonstrated that rising temperatures also drive increased air-conditioning market penetration.87

Finally, the dataset is normalized to a reference year to allow flexible scaling in energy system models. While this enhances applicability, it limits the direct interpretation of absolute values and requires external scenario assumptions for validation and application.

Data sources

The meteorological input data were obtained from the SECURES-Met57,58 dataset, which provides hourly climate variables for Europe at high spatial resolution. SECURES-Met combines (i) ERA5(-Land) reanalysis data covering the historical period (1981–2010) and (ii) bias-adjusted regional climate projections from EURO-CORDEX (ICHEC-EC-EARTH—KNMI-RACMO22E) for two emission scenarios (RCP 4.5 and RCP 8.5) covering 2011–2100. The meteorological variables used include temperature (2 m), solar radiation (GHI and DNI), wind speed, and hydrological runoff.

Data processing

Meteorological data from SECURES-Met57,58 were transformed into electricity demand and generation profiles using a set of technology-specific and sector-specific conversion models.

Climate data preprocessing

Hourly meteorological variables were spatially aggregated to the national level using variable-specific aggregation methods (see Section underlying climate scenarios). Climate projections were bias-adjusted using ERA5-Land data as a reference to ensure consistency between historical and future periods. Wind speeds were extrapolated to 150 m hub height using statistical mapping techniques. All datasets were harmonised to consistent temporal (hourly) and spatial (NUTS0) resolution.

Electricity demand modelling

Weather-dependent electricity demand (see Section weather-dependent electricity demand and Section 3 in the Methods S1) was derived using temperature-based regression models: Heating and cooling demand were computed using population-weighted temperature and sector-specific hourly load profiles. Separate regressions were applied for residential and tertiary sectors, incorporating behavioural parameters (hour of day, day type, season). E-mobility demand was adjusted using a temperature-dependent efficiency function based on a third-order polynomial relationship between temperature and vehicle energy consumption.

Non-weather-dependent demand components (industry, hydrogen electrolysis, rest demand) were derived from predefined load profiles and assumed to be independent of weather conditions (see Section non-weather-dependent demand).

All demand components were normalised to a reference value (2010) to allow scaling in energy system modelling applications.

Electricity generation modelling

Generation profiles were derived as follows:

•Solar PV: calculated from population-weighted solar radiation (GHI and DNI) using standard PV performance models (see Section 3.3.1 and Section 4 in the Methods S1).

Solar PV: calculated from population-weighted solar radiation (GHI and DNI) using standard PV performance models (see Section 3.3.1 and Section 4 in the Methods S1).

•Onshore wind: derived from wind speed time series combined with turbine power curves and corrected via quantile mapping (see Section onshore wind power).

Onshore wind: derived from wind speed time series combined with turbine power curves and corrected via quantile mapping (see Section onshore wind power).

•Offshore wind: directly obtained from SECURES-Met capacity factors with applied loss factors of 15% (see Section offshore wind power).

Offshore wind: directly obtained from SECURES-Met capacity factors with applied loss factors of 15% (see Section offshore wind power).

•Run-of-river hydropower: derived from hydrological runoff data and converted into hourly capacity factor time series (see Section hydropower).

Run-of-river hydropower: derived from hydrological runoff data and converted into hourly capacity factor time series (see Section hydropower).

Loss factors (e.g., 15% for wind generation) and bias adjustments were applied to ensure realistic generation profiles. All generation profiles are provided as normalised capacity factors, enabling flexible scaling to installed capacities.

Published dataset

Electricity demand and supply profiles were generated and resulted in the SECURES-Energy dataset.16 This dataset provides hourly country-level time series of electricity demand and renewable generation (wind, solar PV, and hydropower) for Europe over the period 1981–2100.

The dataset is deposited in Zenodo and can be accessed via DOI: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.14615500.

The underlying meteorological dataset SECURES-Met57 and associated data processing routines73 are also publicly available via Zenodo. All data are provided in formats suitable for direct use in energy system models and are accompanied by documentation enabling reproducibility.

Evaluation of interannual variability and extreme values

For each metric, the unit of analysis (n) corresponds to individual years or hourly observations within defined time windows. Central tendency is represented by mean and median values, while variability is assessed using quartiles and extrema. No formal significance testing was performed, as the focus lies on dataset generation and scenario-based comparison.

Comparisons between scenarios and time periods are based on relative deviations from a reference period (1981–2010) in ERA5-(Land) and aggregation across multiple climate scenarios where applicable.
