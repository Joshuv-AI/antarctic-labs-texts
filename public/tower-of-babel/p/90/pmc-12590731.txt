Stunting and its predictors among children aged 6–59 months in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mitku Mammo Taderegew, Alemayehu Wondie, Habtamu Geremew, Lemlemu Maru, Winta Tesfaye, Natnael Yikeber Hailesilassie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStunting is a major public health problem in many developing countries. Despite the availability of the studies conducted on stunting, most of the studies were single-centered and with small sample sizes and showed significant variation. Hence this review was conducted to assess the pooled prevalence of stunting and its predictors among 6–59 months of age children.MethodsThe relevant studies were identified by electronic database searching methods. Data were extracted by employing a data extraction format developed in Microsoft Excel. Inverse variance (I2) test was employed to evaluate the heterogeneity within the included studies. Begg’s and Egger’s regression tests were utilized to evaluate publication bias. Moreover, predictors of stunting were also assessed. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA™ Version-14 software.ResultsA total of 43 papers were combined in the review. The pooled prevalence of stunting was found to be 39.9% (95%CI:37.1–42.83) with a substantial level of heterogeneity (I2 = 97.5%; p < 0.001). The highest prevalence of stunting was found in Amhara region (48.14%) and the lowest prevalence was in Sidama (14.7%). Being 25–59 months of age (POR = 1.36, 95%CI: 1.12–1.64), being male (POR = 1.20, 95%CI:1.05, 1.37), rural residence (POR = 1.91, 95%CI: 1.58–2.31), lack of maternal formal education (POR = 1.55, 95%CI:1.33, 1.81), living with family size of ≥ 5 (POR = 1.32, 95%CI:1.07–1.64), having more than two under five children in the household (POR = 1.78, 95%CI:1.36–2.32), poor household wealth index (POR = 1.80, 95%CI: 1.51–2.13), absence of ANC follow-up (POR = 2.24, 95%CI:1.54–3.26), home delivery (POR = 1.66, 95%CI:1.29–2.13), diarrhea in the last two weeks (POR = 2.20, 95%CI:1.52–3.19), absence of PNC (POR = 2.08, 95%CI:1.63–2.67), non-exclusive breastfeeding (POR = 2.48, 95%CI:1.36–4.54), early or late complementary feeding initiation (POR = 1.53, 95%CI:1.04–2.27), household dietary diversity score of < 4 (POR = 1.75, 95%CI:1.28–2.41), birth order 2 or more) (POR = 1.44, 95%CI:1.06–1.97), and absence of immunization (POR = 2.62, 95%CI:1.61–4.28) were predictors of stunting among 6–59 months of children.ConclusionNearly two out of five children were stunted, Hence strategies to limit family size, to improve the living standard, delivery at health facilities, perinatal care, exclusive breastfeeding in the first six month, and timely initiation of complimentary feeding should be encouraged to combat stunting among 6–59 months of children.

Background

Malnutrition is a major public health problem in many developing countries. It is known to be one of the important causes of childhood illness, death, and disability [1]. Stunting is one of the most common indicators of chronic malnutrition, which is a linear growth failure or inability to attain potential height for a particular age [2].

The etiologies of stunting are multifactorial, and interrelated and comprise insufficient food consumption, poor nutritional diversification, improper food handling practice, improper parental guidance and nurturing, poor breastfeeding practice, infectious comorbidities, poor sanitation, inadequate healthcare accessibility and poor follow-up of antenatal services [3–8].

Worldwide, an estimated 149.2 million children below the age of five years suffer from stunting in 2020, which represents 21.3% of the children under 5 years among whom, 36% were residing in African countries [9]. Furthermore, an estimated 17% of the mortality burden in children under 5 years old is associated with stunting [10].

Despite the government designing different strategies to avert the magnitude of stunting in Ethiopia, available evidence showed that stunting is still one of the major public health issues in the country. Results from the 2019 Ethiopian Mini Demographic and Health Survey show that 37% of children under-5 years of age were stunted [11]. Other numerous primary studies conducted in various areas of the country displayed the prevalence of stunting ranging between 14.7 [12] and 64.5% [3].

Despite the availability of the studies conducted on the burden and determinants of stunting among children aged 6 to 59 months in Ethiopia, most of these were conducted at single centers, involved relatively small sample sizes, and produced findings that were notably varied and inconsistent. Previously, systematic review and meta-analysis were also conducted to estimate the pooled prevalence of stunting and/or its predictors. While the studies made important contribution by synthesizing available evidence, these were conducted without giving adequate attention to search optimization and the careful extraction of data for each identified predictor. In particular, there is a noticeable gap in the number of incorporated studies and predictors that were systematically identified.

Hence, this systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to evaluate the pooled prevalence of stunting and its predictors among children aged 6 to 59 months in Ethiopia by integrating findings from existing research.

The findings of this study would provide baseline data for policymakers and other relevant stakeholders for designing and implementing targeted intervention and prevention strategies for combating malnutrition, with a specific focus on reducing stunting. The results of this study will provide baseline information for future researchers interested in conducting further investigations in this field.

Materials and methods

The study was carried out to assess the pooled prevalence of stunting among 6–59 months of children in Ethiopia. The study was guided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) checklist [13]. The review was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) 2024 CRD42023447215 Available from: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42023447215.

The literature was searched through multiple databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar, African Journals Online, and the Cochrane Library. While exploring the scholar articles the following key exploration terms and Medical Subject Headings [MeSH] “prevalence”, “magnitude”, “stunting”, “growth disorders”, “malnutrition” “associated factors”, “predictors”, “determinant”, “children”, “6–59 months of age”, “under five years”, and “Ethiopia” were used alone or along with the Boolean operator’s terms “AND” and “OR” as follow: (((((((((((Prevalence) OR (Magnitude)) AND (stunting)) OR (growth disorders)) OR (malnutrition)) AND (associated factors)) OR (predictors)) OR (determinant)) AND (children)) OR (65–59 months of age)) OR (under five years)) AND (Ethiopia).

Additionally, the reference lists of all retrieved articles were examined to find more relevant studies. The search fused on studies available until 28th of February 2025 and it was limited to full-length paper, human studies, and published in peer-reviewed journals.

In the meantime, all the included studies were exported into the EndNote version X20 software, and then, the duplicate records were excluded. Subsequently, a thorough screening of the titles, abstracts, full texts, and quality of all retrieved articles was conducted according to the pre-established eligibility criteria.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All primary studies that revealed the magnitude of stunting and/or its predictors among 6–59 months of age children were illegible for the study. However, the following studies were excluded: those that were not fully available despite two attempts to contact the primary author via email, in circumstances where the outcome of interest was not explicitly reported or could not be estimated from the existing data, studies that did not address our outcome of interest, and those with poor quality scores based on the established criteria.

Data extraction and quality assessment

The selected studies were carefully examined, and the necessary information was separately extracted and summed-up by two authors using a standardized data extraction spreadsheet format created in Microsoft Excel. If any disagreements arose between the data extractors, a third author was consulted to resolve them. For each included study, information was extracted with regard to the corresponding author, publication year, region of the study, study design, sample size, stunting prevalence, and identified predictors. Two-by-two data were also extracted for each identified factors.

The methodological quality of each included study was independently assessed by two authors using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), adapted for assessing the quality of cross-sectional studies [14]. Any disagreements between reviewers regarding the quality assessment of individual articles were resolved through discussion among all authors, with the final decision reached by consensus. Ultimately, only studies that scored 5 or more out of 10 across the three domains of the modified NOS were included in the analysis. In addition, three authors autonomously assessed the risk of bias in the included studies using the risk of bias tool designed for prevalence studies developed by Hoy et al. [15]. The assessment tool included ten criteria, with each unmet criterion scored as 0 (no). The total score was calculated by summing the responses, yielding an overall quality score between 0 and 10. Any discrepancies at the time of data abstraction were fixed by discussion and consensus. When available data were insufficient to make a judgment on a specific criterion, the corresponding authors were contacted for clarification. If uncertainty remained, the item was scored as 0, indicating a high risk of bias. Eventually, studies receiving 8 or more 'yes' responses were categorized as with a low risk of bias, those with 5 to 7 'yes' responses as moderate risk, and those with 4 or fewer 'yes' responses as high risk.

Data processing and analysis

Heterogeneity among the included studies was assessed using the I2 statistic. The results indicated substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 97.6%; P < 0.001). Therefore, a random-effects model was used to estimate the pooled prevalence of stunting among children aged 6 to 59 months in Ethiopia. The pooled prevalence, along with its corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI), was calculated and displayed using a forest plot. Potential sources of heterogeneity were further explored with meta-regression and subgroup analysis. To evaluate the influence of each study on the overall assessment, a sensitivity analysis was performed by systematically excluding each study one at a time. Additionally, publication bias was judged using funnel plot symmetry, Egger’s regression test, and Begg’s test. Finally, the various predictors of stunting among children aged 6–59 months were expressed using pooled odds ratios (PORs) with corresponding 95% CI. STATA™ version 14 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA) was used for all statistical computations.

Results

Study selection

A total of 6092 studies concerning the prevalence of stunting and its predictors among 6–59 months of age children were identified using electronic database searches. Out of the total studies identified, 438 were omitted due to repetition. Following a review of titles and abstracts, an additional 5,567 studies were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria for this systematic review and meta-analysis. The remaining 87 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility based on the predefined criteria, leading to the exclusion of 44 more studies. Prevalence of stunting among 6–59 months of age children

The pooled prevalence of stunting among 6–59 months of age children was estimated to be 39.9% (95% CI: 37.1–42.8) with a significant level of heterogeneity as evidenced by I2 statistic (I2 = 97.4%; P < 0.001). Subgroup and meta-regression analysis

To detect the possible source of heterogeneity across the included studies, subgroup analysis based on the region where the study was conducted, study design, years of publication, and sample size were conducted. The subgroup analysis showed that the pooled prevalence of stunting among children aged 6 to 59 months was 44.2% in the Amhara region and 14.7% in the Sidama region. In terms of publication year, the prevalence was 39.4% in studies published from 2020 onwards and 40.4% in studies published before 2020. Additionally, the prevalence was 42.9% in large survey-based studies and 28.5% in institution-based cross-sectional studies. The subgroup analysis also showed that studies with a sample size greater than or equal to the median (≥ 615) reported a higher pooled prevalence of stunting (44.4%) compared to those with sample sizes below the median (34.6%) (Table 2). Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was conducted using the leave-one-out method to assess the impact of individual studies on the pooled prevalence estimate of stunting among children aged 6 to 59 months. The analysis indicated that no single study had a substantial impact on the overall prevalence assessment.

Publication bias

Visual examination of the funnel plot showed a symmetrical distribution, suggesting no evidence of publication bias among the included studies (Fig. 3). Factors associated with stunting among 6–59 months of age children

A total 27 predictors of stunting among 6–59 months of age children were taken out from the available studies. For each identified factor, sensitivity analysis was carried out by excluding each study one by one, but the result showed that there was no strong evidence for the effect of a single study on the overall results. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to estimate the prevalence of stunting and its predictors among 6–59 months of age children in Ethiopia. The reviewed results indicated that the pooled prevalence of stunting among 6–59 months of age children was found to be 39.9% (95% CI: 37.1–42.8). This is in accordance with the findings of the studies conducted in Uganda (41.1%) [55], Cameroon (42.2%) [56], Demographic Health Surveys in five South Asian countries (38.0%) [57], and results from 35 low- and middle-income countries (38.8%) [58]. On the other hand, this review found a greater magnitude of stunting compared to the result of the studies in Sub-Saharan African countries (35.0% [59], Nepal Demographic and Health Survey (26.3%) [60], and China (4.4%) [61]. These variations in the magnitude of stunting among 6–59 months of age may be due to variations in socio-demographic characteristics, income level of the country, the quality of healthcare services, and health-seeking behavior of the participants.

It was also found that the age and sex of the children, rural residence, educational status of the mothers, family size, poor household wealth index, household food insecurity status, absence of ANC follow-up, home delivery, absence of PNC, delayed breastfeeding initiation, non-exclusive breastfeeding, dietary diversity score, being not the first child, and immunization status were significantly associated with stunting among 6–59 months of children.

In comparable with the results of Demographic and Health Survey in Rwanda [62], Nigeria [63], Cambodia [64], Congo [65], and meta-analysis of 16 demographic and health surveys in Sub-Saharan Africa, our study revealed significant association between sex of the child and stunting in which being male was more likely to be stunted than female. This may be due to the combined effects of biological and social factors. Due to hormonal difference between boys and girls, larger body sizes and more rapid growth among boys even during fetal development, the energy demand among male is relatively high which makes boys more vulnerable for malnutrition than girls. Another explanation could be that in some cultures, males may be prioritized to initiate complementary foods earlier, reducing the duration of exclusive breastfeeding, which can impair their normal wellbeing and growth [66, 67]. In contrast to our finding, study conducted in Northern Ethiopia reported males are less likely to be stunted than female [68]. Another study also revealed non-significant association between sex of the child and stunting [69]. These variations in the association of sex and stunting may be due to the difference in socio-economic strata and cultural beliefs of the study participants.

Our finding also indicates significant association between the age of the children and stunting. Children aged 24–59 months were at higher risk of stunting than those in the younger age group 6–12 months, which has been supported by many scholars in Ethiopia and elsewhere in other parts of the globe [62–64, 70].

This is due to the fact that stunting is a cumulative result of chronic or recurrent malnutrition, infections, and is commonly manifested after long-term nutritional deprivation. Even if stunting is occurred during early age, it becomes more apparent as children get older. Another potential reason for the higher risk of stunning among older children could be due to the problems of hygiene while infants crawling and preparing additional food that exposes children to frequent infections and associated malnutrition.

In this study, residence had a statistical significantly association with stunting of children. Children from rural residences were found to be more stunted than children from urban residences. This study is in line with the study conducted in Indonesia [71]. This may be due to the fact that children from urban areas have great access to dietary diversity. Children from rural residences have also poor access to good healthcare delivery which make them vulnerable for many communicable diseases and associated malnutrition that leads them to have disturbed developmental milestones.

In this review results, wealth index, and dietary diversity score were significantly associated with stunting of children in which children from households with poor wealth index, and dietary diverse score of less than four were found to be more stunted than their counter parts. These were in line with the results of the Rwanda Demographic and Health Survey [72], Nepal Demographic and Health Survey [60], and a study result conducted in Bangladesh [73]. This is because in households with poor wealth index and low dietary diversity, health-seeking behavior may be low, access to quality of care and treatment can be limited, so that children may not get better healthcare service when they feel sick which may have a negative impact on their health as well as on their growth and development. Besides these households can’t afford divers and nutrient-rich diet, which increase the risk of stunting in the children.

In line with the studies conducted in Sub-Saharan Africa [59], and Bangladesh [73], a place of delivery had a significant association with stunting of children in which children born at home were more likely to be stunted than those born at health institution. This is due to the fact that while the children are born at a health facility proper care for the mother as well as for the children will be given and that will be helpful for the proper growth and development of the child. Moreover, absence of antenatal and postnatal care also significantly associated with stunting. These findings indicate the essentiality of antepartum, intrapartum and postpartum care for a child's healthy growth and development.

Conclusion and recommendation

Nearly two out of five children were stunted. Age of the children, being male, educational status of the mother, rural residence, family size, having more than two under five children in the household, poor household wealth index, absence of ANC follow-up, home delivery, absence of PNC, diarrhea in the last two weeks, early or late complementary feeding initiation, non-exclusive breastfeeding, household dietary diversity score of < 4, being birth order of the second or more, and absence of immunization were significantly associated with stunting among 6–59 months of children. Based on the findings, it is recommended that efforts should be made to improve ANC and PNC follow-up, immunization, home delivery, timely initiation of complimentary feeding, and exclusive breastfeeding. It is also recommended that governmental and non-governmental organizations and other stallholders should pay attention to limit family size, to improve the household wealth index, and dietary diversity score of the communities.

Limitations of the study

In this systematic review and meta-analysis there are some limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, the majority of the included studies were cross-sectional. Consequently, cause-and-effect relationships cannot be determined. Secondly, there was considerable heterogeneity among the included studies, and the underlying sources of this heterogeneity remain unclear.
