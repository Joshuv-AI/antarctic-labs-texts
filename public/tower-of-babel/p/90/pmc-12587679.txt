Modern contraceptive methods use behaviour among young married women in Urban Ethiopia: a multilevel mixed-effects binary logistic regression analysis
Authors: Tsion Habtu Tebeje, Habtamu Tamene Temesgen, Yihunie Lakew, Tigist Urgessa Wakene, Biruk Melaku Ayalew, Tsega Berhanu Zerihun, Betemariam Alemu Tiruneh, Simon Heliso Kuka, Chalachew Tiruneh Alemu, Wasihun Andualem Gobeze, Dereje Haddis Engida, Israel Lemma Hailu, Yimer Seid Adem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBased on the Ethiopia Demographic and Health Survey, low contraceptive utilization has been reported from young married women in Ethiopia, coupled with unmet reproductive health needs heightening the risk of unintended pregnancies and associated poor health outcomes. Consequently, this study was conducted to identify main determinants influencing contraceptive use among young married women in selected cities across Ethiopia.MethodsA dataset comprising 680 young married women aged between 18 and 29 years was obtained from a secondary source, and collected between December 12, 2021 and January 03, 2022, across 13 selected cities. The data was analyzed using STATA version 18. A multilevel mixed-effects binary logistic regression analysis was used to identify individual-level determinants of contraceptive use while accounting for variability attributable to differences among cities.ResultsAt the time of data collection, 401 (59% CI: 55%-63%) young married women reported current contraceptive use. The most common utilized contraceptive methods were injectables (42%) and implants (35%). Significant variation in contraceptive use was observed across cities, with lowest contraceptive prevalence in Jigjiga (10%, CI = 3%-24%) and the highest in Hawassa (81%, CI = 70%-89%). Multilevel analysis identified several individual-level factors significantly associated with contraceptive use: having more than two family members (AOR = 3.3, 95% CI = 2.04–5.27), knowledge of contraceptive methods (AOR = 2.6, 95% CI = 1.43–4.83) and exposure to radio broadcast (AOR = 1.6, 95% CI = 1.13–2.32) were significantly associated with contraceptive use.ConclusionThe study shows higher levels of contraceptive use among young married women in urban Ethiopia. Increasing radio listenership and improving contraceptive knowledge further enhance their contraceptive use behavior.Keywords: Contraceptive use, Media exposure, Family size, Logistic regression, Women

Background

The utilization of contraceptives among young women brings substantial advantages for their health and society. It plays a crucial role in postponing pregnancies in young girls who are at risk for health complications arising from early childbearing. By reducing the occurrence of unintended pregnancies, we can significantly reduce the demand for unsafe abortions, curb the transmission of sexually transmitted infections, and avert pregnancy-related fatalities. This reduction can further support the pursuit of education for girls and empowers young women to actively participate in society, including through paid employment [1, 2].

Modern contraceptive use among young married women aged 15–24 in low- and lower- middle-income countries of Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa remains alarmingly low, with considerable regional disparities and various barriers to access; approximately 37% utilize modern contraceptives, while 24% face unmet needs for these methods [3]. Young married women in urban Ethiopia are a key demographic for understanding contraceptive use due to their high reproductive potential and early childbearing risks. In Ethiopia, young married women (age 15 to 24 years) use of modern contraceptives increased from 6% in 2000 to 16% in 2005 and then to 36% in 2011 based on the three EDHS [4]. By 2016, prevalence reached 36.7% in this age group with substantial geographic variation, with higher in Amhara, Addis Ababa, while Afar, and Somali regions show lower prevalence [5]. Focusing on this group helps identify context-specific barriers and informs targeted interventions to improve reproductive health and support national family planning goals. Urban settings offer greater exposure to media and health information, yet disparities persist. Despite better access to health services in urban areas, contraceptive use remains inconsistent, influenced by individual, cultural, and structural factors. Key determinants of modern contraceptive use among married women in sub-Saharan Africa include women’s education level, wealth status, urban residency, media exposure, and decision-making autonomy [6].

Among the challenges in designing social behavior change interventions to improve contraceptive use among young married women in urban Ethiopia is lack of sufficient evidence regarding determinants for their contraceptive use. Previous research on family planning use among young married women has not explicitly elucidated the driving factors nor adequately addressed geographic heterogeneity [7]. This study, therefore, focused on the utilization of contraceptives among married women, with the objective of identifying key determinants such as radio listenership, family size, and contraceptive knowledge that influence contraceptive use. The analysis conducted in this study also accounted for city-level variability. The findings of this study are used by sexual health service implementers, partners and policymakers who are working to improve SRH among young women.

Methods

Data source and study population

This study used secondary data from the original formative assessment conducted as part of the Integrate Youth Activities Project (IYA), which was implemented in 18 cities throughout Ethiopia. Due to the prevailing security concerns, 13 out of 18 designated project implementation cities were selected. Then, the formative assessment using a cross-sectional design was conducted in major cities of Ethiopia identified as project targets, namely: Adama, Addis Ababa, Assosa, Bahir Dar, Bishoftu, Dilla, Dire Dewa, Gambella, Harar, Jigjiga, Jimma, Hawassa, and Shashemenie, and the data collection was carried out from December 12, 2021, to January 03, 2022. The orginal study included participants from both community and Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) settings, selected through systematic random sampling and proportional allocation methods. A total sample of 3,215 young people aged 18 to 29 years from both the community and HEIs participated in the original study. Then, the data was collected through face-to-face interviews using tablet computers with Kobo Toolbox, and data collector-facilitated online survey for the HEI students. For this study, data on 680 young married women were extracted from the original dataset using inclusion criteria restricted to young married women to understand contraceptive use behavior within this subpopulation.

Study variables

The dependent variable in this study is the use of modern contraceptive methods at the time of data collection. The “Yes” category comprises individuals themselves or their husbands who are currently using a range of modern contraceptive methods, including oral contraceptive pills (OCPs), implants, injectables, intrauterine devices (IUDs), condoms, emergency contraceptives, standard days method, the lactational amenorrhea method, and female sterilization. The category referred to as “No” encompasses individuals who employ contraceptive methods such as the rhythm method, withdrawal, other traditional methods, and no contraceptive method at all.

This study includes the following independent variables: respondent age (18–24 years, 25–29 years), exposure to radio media (yes, no), exposure to TV media (yes, no), and knowledge of contraceptives (labelled as ‘yes’ if the respondents know at least two contraceptive methods and no if respondents know only one contraceptive method). Furthermore, family size significantly influences contraceptive use among young married women. Those with fewer or no children often delay contraception due to the desire to build a family, while women with more children are more likely to use contraceptives to space or limit births. Family size is labelled as ‘yes’ if the respondents’ family size is less than three and ‘no’ if the respondents’ family size is greater than or equal to three. Additionally, the presence of partner communication norms was assessed using a composite of three variables: [1] a girl can suggest to her boyfriend that he can use a condom [2], it wouldn’t be too embarrassing for someone like me to buy or obtain condoms, and [3] if a girl suggested using condoms to her partner, it wouldn’t mean that she didn’t trust him. Partner communication norms labelled as ‘yes’ if the respondents’ selected any of the options and ‘no’ if none were selected.

Operational definition

In this study, the term “young women” refers to individuals aged 18 to 29. An urban setting was defined as a locality with a population of at least 2,000 people and basic infrastraucture and administration service [8]. “Media exposure” refers to those young married women who had listened to radio or watched television at least once a week. The study categorized modern contraceptive methods as oral contraceptive pills, implants, injectables, intrauterine devices, condoms, emergency contraceptives, the standard days method, the lactational amenorrhea method, and female sterilization. On the other hand, the rhythm method and withdrawal method were classified as traditional contraceptive methods [9]. Contraceptive use is defined as proportion of women of reproductive age who have their need for family planning satisfied with modern contraceptive methods [10]. Individuals themselves or their husbands who are currently using at least one modern method of contraception (OCPs, implants, injectables, IUDs, condoms, emergency contraceptives, the standard days method, the lactational amenorrhea method, and female sterilization) is included in the “Yes” category. “Current use” in this study means at the time of data collection. Knowledge was defined as awareness of at least two contraceptive methods. Participants were categorized as ‘Yes’ if they reported two or more methods, and ‘No’ if they reported fewer than two [11, 12].

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ characterstics. Continous variables, such as age, were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR) due to non-normal distribution, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. The contraceptive use among young women in selected cities of Ethiopia, the distribution of contraceptive use across cities and individual-level factors were examined. Sources for obtaining contraceptives and contraceptive information were summarized. Data were analyzed using STATA Version 18 software.

A multilevel binary logistic regression analysis was done to measure the association between contraceptive use and the variation across cities. Cities were considered as random effects specified at Level 2 to cater for the unexplained variability at the individual-level. Crude odds ratios (CORs) were first estimated using bivariate logistic regression. Variables that were statistically significant in the bivariate analysis (p < 0.05) were then selected for inclusion in the multilevel models to estimate adjusted odds ratios (AORs), controlling for potential confounders. Further, the statistical significance of the associations between each of the factors and current use of contraceptives was determined at a p-value of less than 0.05.

Two models, comprising the null model (model 0) and model 1 were fitted. Model 0 showed the variance in modern contraceptive use attributed to the clustering of the primary sampling units without the explanatory variables. Model 1 is progressive containing the individual factors: age, media exposure through radio, media exposure through TV, knowledge of contraceptives, family size & partner communication norms. Model comparison was done using the log likelihood and Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) tests. The lowest AIC and highest log likelihood were used to determine the best fit model. For model one, Odds ratio and associated 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Using the variance inflation factor (VIF), a test for multicollinearity was done and there was no evidence of high collinearity.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

Among the 680 young married women selected for this study, 653 (96%) were those selected from a community setting and 27 (4%) were from HEIs. Eleven (2%) were young women with physical disabilities. The median age of participants was 25 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 23–28; range: 18–29). Four hundred thirteen (61%) of the respondents were between ages of 25–29 years while the remaining 267 (39%) were in the age group of 18–24 years. Respondents from Addis Ababa were 81 (12%), Adama 78 (12%), Assosa 26 (4%), Bahir Dar 63 (9%), Bishoftu 36 (5%), Dilla 33 (5%), Dire Dewa 68 (10%), Gambella 56 (8%), Harar 24 (4%), Hawassa 74 (11%), Jigjiga 39 (6%), Jimma 55 (8%) and Shashemenie 47 (7%) were interviewed. High contraceptive use variability was observed across cities. The prevalence of contraceptive use among young married women in Jigjiga and Harar was found to be low in the range of 10% and 29%. Religious opposition, the desire for large families, and the limited cultural acceptability of modern contraceptive methods could be the main reasons for their lower utilization, as indicated by another study [13]. The highest use of contraceptives was observed among the target population in Adama and Hawassa in the range of 65% to 81% (Fig. 2). These variations may be explained by marked differences in population size across cities in the country. Population in largest city data was reported to have more than 4 million population [14]. Furthermore, differences in socioeconomic status, access to information and healthcare, and cultural norms are likely to contribute to the observed variations in contraceptive use. Therefore, family planning policies and strategies should take these differences into account when designing a targeted and impactful family planning initiative.

Knowledge of contraceptives

Most of the young married women respondents 643 (94.6%) have heard about family planning methods. Five hundred seventy nine (85.0%) young married women knew at least two types of contraceptive methods. The most known methods were injectables 575 (85.0%) and OCPs 531 (78.0%). The least known methods were withdrawal 62 (9.1%) and male sterilization 64 (9.4%).

Sexual and reproductive health information

Exposure to messages related to SRH was assessed among young married women and almost half of the young married women 334 (49.0%) had received SRH-related information from any source in the preceding three months. The most common SRH message sources for the young married women were TV 199 (29.0%), followed by health facilities 171 (25.0%) and social media 59 (9.0%).

Sources of contraceptive

Health centers were the most common sources of SRH services 181 (27.0%) for young married women, followed by government hospitals 61 (9%) and private health facilities 47 (7.0%).

Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence and associated factors with contraceptive use among young married women in urban settings and found 401 (59%) of young married women were current user of contraceptives. This suggested that urban young married women have better SRH access to services and information. The 2019 Ethiopian Mini Demographic and Health Survey data indicated that the prevalence of modern contraceptive use among young married women aged 15 to 19 was 36%, 51% among women aged 20–24, and 47% among women aged 25–29, which is similar to our estimates [15]. The finding also demonstrates that the utilization of contraceptives among urban young married women exceeds the targeted national contraceptive rate of 50% set for the years 2024–2025 [16]. This could be due to higher listenership of SRH-related messages among young married women residing in urban areas. Similarly, another study conducted in Malawi indicated that 31% of young women used contraceptives [17].

Our multilevel mixed-effects binary logistic regression analysis identified factors influencing contraceptive use. The study illustrated that young women who had more than two members in their family were more likely to use contraceptives. Women in larger families may opt for contraception to limit further births, especially if they feel their family size has reached their desired number. Conversely, women in smaller families might be more open to having more children or may not see immediate need for contraception. Larger families often face economic pressures, leading women to use contraceptives to prevent additional pregnancies that could strain family resources. Conversely, some women in smaller families might delay contraception if they desire more children or face cultural expectations to expand the family. In societies valuing larger families, women might be less inclined to use contraception until they reach a culturally acceptable number of children. Women in larger families might have more or less motivation to seek contraception depending on their experiences and perceptions of family planning services, which can be shaped by community norms and healthcare access. Decisions about contraception are often made within the context of partner and family expectations, which are influenced by family size and reproductive goals. Overall, family size influences contraceptive decisions among young married women by shaping their reproductive goals, economic considerations, cultural norms, and interactions with healthcare services. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for designing effective family planning programs tailored to the needs and preferences of women at different family size stages. Consistent with this finding, analysis of 2000 to 2016 Ethiopian Demographic and Health Surveys indicated that having two children has increased the likelihood of modern contraceptive use [18]. A study conducted on married women of reproductive age in rural Zambia indicated that compared to women with no children, women with one or two, three or four and five or more children were more likely to use contraceptives [19].

The result of this study also showed that young married women who had comprehensive knowledge of family planning were more likely to use contraceptives than those who did not. Similarly, a multilevel analysis of the household survey in Ethiopia indicated having knowledge of contraceptive methods increased young women’s use of contraception by more than twofold [20]. A community based cross-sectional study conducted in rural Ethiopia indicated that exposure to family planning information and having knowledge of contraception were significantly associated with young married women contraceptive use [21]. This means that access to SRH related information plays a significant role in increasing the use of contraception as it can raise an individual’s awareness on family planning and use of contraceptives. Furthermore, the original data indicated that the young women most commonly reported lack of privacy and the absence of user-friendly health facilities as barriers to utilizing SRH services.

Young married women who listened to the radio at least once a week were more likely to use contraceptives than those who did not. This is consistent with existing literature, which indicates access to information on contraceptive methods through different sources increases the use of contraceptive methods. According to a study conducted by Adu-Bonsaaoh K, et al. (2022), women in Sub-Saharan Africa who had media exposure were found to be more likely to make informed choices regarding contraceptive methods compared to women who had no media exposure [22]. According to High Impact Practices (HIPs), mass media programming is recognized by global experts as a high-impact intervention for influencing contraceptive use behavior [23]. A multilevel analysis of the DHS data indicated that women who had no exposure to radio had lower odds of using modern contraceptives, across 29 countries in Sub Saharan Africa [24]. Furthermore, women who recalled only one media source with a family planning message were even more likely to use a modern contraceptive method than women who recalled no media source [25]. Further, the findings of a mixed methods study conducted in four regions of Ethiopia indicated that a weekly exposure to the radio is a primary influencing factor for contraceptive use [26]. This could be due to exposure to radio content that can contribute to knowledge and attitudes about contraception influencing an individual’s decision to use contraceptives. Compared with previous studies, there is an improvement in the number of young married women using contraceptive methods in this study which could be due to relatively higher exposure to contraceptive use related messages.

Limitation of the study

This study solely relied on a quantitative research methodology to evaluate the contraceptive use among young women in urban Ethiopia. Employing a qualitative research method would have identified some of the socio-cultural factors that influence modern contraceptive use, thereby providing deeper insights into their behavior.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that young married women exhibit a relatively higher prevalence of contraceptive use. It has been observed that their contraceptive behavior is significantly influenced by factors such as family size, knowledge of contraceptive methods, and engagement with radio programs. Notably, radio listenership emerges as a strong predictor of contraceptive use among this subpopulation.
