Nutrient resorption rather than root acquisition offsets increased N limitation induced by the stand development of a secondary Pinus tabuliformis forest in North China
Authors: Songlan Duan, Yunxiang Zhang, Tianjin Chang, Wenhui Qiu, Yusong Li, Zhihao Zhang, Jinping Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlants allocate nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) to green leaves through a cost-optimal trade-off between root acquisition and leaf resorption, which affects plant nutrient limitation and cycling. However, our understanding of the nature of this positive trade-off under nutrient limitation in forests with stand development under N deposition is limited. We carried out experiments along age sequences of 42, 55, and 65 in a P. tabuliformis forest under five N treatment levels (0, 5, 10, 15 and 20 g N m− 2·yr− 1). We multiplied a multivariate functional variable, INPS (the integral N: P stoichiometry of plants), which reflects the overall nutrient limitation in plants, to explore its response strategies under N treatment during stand development. We found that the P. tabuliformis forest experienced progressive N limitation with stand development under N deposition, as evidenced by the decreased N: P ratios across needles, twigs, and fine roots, and the whole plant (INPS). Thus, to cope with this progressive N limitation and maintain stoichiometric homeostasis, P. tabuliformis forest adopted divergent nutrient utilization strategies for N and P. Regarding the N, the NRE-N (nutrient resorption efficiency of N) did not decrease significantly as a coordinated response to the declining NAC-N (nutrient acquisition capacity of N) along the chronosequence. Conversely, for P, the relative P limitation weakened relative to N along the age sequence in P. tabuliformis forest led to a compensatory trade-off between NRE-P (nutrient resorption efficiency of P) and NAC-P (nutrient acquisition capacity of P), consistent with nutrient-economy and adaptive strategies. N deposition generally alleviated N limitations in P. tabuliformis forest yet significantly decreased the NRE-N and NRE-P across all age classes. However, the ratio of NRE-N: NAC-N exhibited a substantial increase along the chronosequence and negatively linearly correlated with the INPS. These findings indicate that NRE-N acts as a compensatory mechanism to offset age-related progressive stand-level N limitation. Our results suggest that resorption relieves nutrient limitations, a strategy used broadly by woody plants, providing a comprehensive framework for understanding forest adaptation strategies in a changing environment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08463-4.Keywords: N limitations, Stand development, NRE, Pinus tabuliformis, N deposition

Introduction

The trade-off between the nutrient acquisition of feeder roots from the soil and the resorption of fresh leaves from senescing leaves has become a plant nutrient economy strategy in forest ecological processes [1–3]. Nutrient resorption provides approximately 36–76% of the N required for the growth and development of forests annually, effectively reducing reliance on the external soil environment [4, 5], which is becoming a key internal nutrient conservation mechanism for plants [6, 7]. Conversely, nutrient uptake, which is initially mediated by feeder roots, favours further translocation to the transport roots and upwards movement to the shoots and is becoming an important external nutrient acquisition strategy [1, 3, 5]. In general, an effective trade-off between these two nutrient utilization strategies occurs in plants [1, 8, 9] in the course of long-term natural selection to achieve the most economical cost–benefit equilibration and maintain stoichiometric homeostasis [10–12].

N and P in plants are essential nutritional elements that affect their physiological metabolism [13, 14]. The critical threshold values of leaf N: P ratios are typically employed for N or P limitation in terrestrial ecosystems [15–18]. The N: P stoichiometric characteristics of different tissue components of trees reflect the trade-offs between resource acquisition and nutritional utilization strategies involving acquisition, transportation, and resorption [5, 8, 19, 20]. However, the response patterns to nutrient limitation vary with growth stage, type and region and are divergent [21, 22]. For example, N limitation did not significantly differ with increasing stand age in Pinus tabuliformis forest ecosystems in North China [23], whereas P limitation in Pinus massoniana in South China increased with increasing stand age [24]. Moreover, an important shift from N to P limitation in trees younger than 36 years of age was observed in a mixed forest in North-eastern China [25]. In addition, organs have different responsibilities in the distribution process of N and P [26, 27] because of the “optimal allocation theory” and “functional balance” [28]. Therefore, to improve our understanding of the patterns of N: P stoichiometry along a chronosequence under N inputs, a comprehensive variable indicating the N: P stoichiometry of different tissues needs to be developed, and more experiments urgently need to be conducted.

Forests in temperate areas are always considered to be limited by soil N availability [23, 29–31] and experience a progressive increase due to anthropogenic N input [32]. N deposition increases the availability of soil N and other limited nutrients, such as P and potassium (K) [19, 33, 34]. These increases further stimulate forest growth [35] and ultimately improve annual net primary production [7, 36, 37]. However, the responses of the physiological metabolism and growth and development patterns of stands of different ages to N deposition exhibit different trends. For example, the growth of Metasequoia glyptostroboides plantations in middle-aged stands demonstrated P limitation, whereas N limitation occurred in young stands after exogenous N input [38]. There was a transformation from greater N deficiency in young plantations to greater N retention in mature plantations in larch ecosystems after long-term N inputs [39]. This transformation inevitably affects nutritional utilization strategies such as the acquisition and resorption of N and P in plants, as well as the final trade-off between them [40–42]. Nonetheless, the nature of this trade-off to alleviate the nutrient limitation induced by stand age under increasing N deposition remains unclear. Hence, further research should explore the final utilization trade-off of N and P when trees sustain nutrient deficiency owing to increased stand age for adaptation to forest growth and development. An improved understanding of this concept will contribute to the elucidation of the possible evolutionary mechanism due to the corresponding nutrient limitations for a given species [14, 29, 43].

P. tabuliformis is a major tree species in Shanxi, North China, because of its ability to adapt to unfavourable soil conditions and its sensitivity to increasing N deposition [44, 45]. Previous studies have demonstrated that soil N is the key factor affecting the growth of P. tabuliformis in this area [23, 46]. However, how N deposition affects utilization strategy trade-offs and nutrient limitations, especially their variation along the age sequence, is still unknown. Here, we synchronously integrated the nutritional utilization strategies of N and P to explore the simultaneous responses of nutrient trade-off preferences and limitations along a chronosequence of stands in age classes 42, 55, and 65 under an exogenous N input experiment (0, 5, 10, 15 and 20 g N m− 2·yr− 1) in a secondary P. tabuliformis forest in North China. Our aim was to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the utilization strategy trade-off response to nutrient limitation induced by increased stand age under N deposition. We hypothesized that (1) this P. tabuliformis forest would be N limited and that this limitation would be aggravated with increasing stand age due to long-term N accumulation in plant biomass and litter during stand development; (2) the nutritional utilization strategies of N and P would be affected by N deposition and stand development; and (3) although a trade-off between acquisition and resorption exists in regulating N and P limitations with increasing stand age under N deposition, nutrient resorption would exert a more pivotal influence on maintaining plant N: P stoichiometric homeostasis.

Methods

Site description

This research was carried out at the Xiaowenshan Forest Farm (111°24′ E − 112°37′ E, 37°41′ N − 37°54′ N), where the elevation ranges from 1,460 to 1,610 m. The farm, established in 1962, is located in the Guandishan Forest Region of Shanxi Province, northern China, and spans approximately 1.99 × 104 ha. The regional climate is categorized as a typical inner-continental mountain monsoon climate, featuring a relative humidity of 70.9%, an annual mean temperature of 4.2 °C, and an annual precipitation amounting to 822.6 mm. The soil was classified as an Alfisol with a humus layer 3–7 cm thick. The natural secondary forest is dominated by P. tabuliformis, with small amounts of Larix principis-rupprechtii, Picea wilsonii, Betula platyphylla, and Quercus mongolica. The understory vegetation of the P. tabuliformis forest is composed mainly of shrubs such as Spiraea salicifolia, Rosa xanthine, Corylus mandshurica, Rosa bella and Lespedeza bicolor [45].

Experimental design

An experimental site for exogenous chronic N application was established during the development of stands with age classes of 42, 55, and 65 years in P. tabuliformis forests at the forest farm in October 2020 (Fig. 1). A randomized complete block design with three replicates was used in this study. Five 201 m2 circular plots (radius = 8 m), each containing a buffer zone [47, 48], were assigned five levels of N application distributed randomly within each block at each stand age: N0 (0 g N m− 2·year− 1), N1 (5 g N m− 2·year− 1), N2 (10 g N m− 2·year− 1), N3 (15 g N m− 2·year− 1), and N4 (20 g N m− 2·year− 1). Each N application level required corresponding urea (CH4N2O) solutions, which were fully dissolved in 20 L of water. Finally, urea solutions of varying concentrations were sprayed evenly on the corresponding plots using a backpack sprayer on nonrainy days continuously during mid-May, July, and September annually [49].

Position of the sample area

Soil and plant sampling

In the last ten-day period of August (the peak growing season) in 2021, after continuous N application for one year, living plants and soil were sampled from established plots within each stand at the three developmental stages. We used a lopping shear to collect green needles and representative twigs from five healthy dominant trees on their upper, middle, and lower canopies in each plot. The fine roots (Ø ≤ 2 mm) were also collected via the digging method [50]. Moreover, five soil cores (8 cm inner diameter, 15 cm depth) were randomly collected around the dominant trees in each plot. Senesced needles were collected through five 1.0 m2 litter traps fixed 1.0 m above the ground near the dominant trees in late October 2021. The green needles, twigs, fine roots and senesced needles, as well as the soils collected at the five points from one plot, were then pooled into a mixed sample.

Laboratory analysis of the soil and plant samples

After the soil samples were air dried at room temperature (approximately 25 °C), they were passed through a 0.25 mm sieve prior to analysis of the contents of soil organic carbon (SOC), total N (TN), and total P (TP). The root samples were rinsed gently in a 0.5 mm wet sieve with deionized water. All the plant samples were ground to a fine powder after heat-killing at 105 °C for 15 min and drying at 75 °C for 48 h to a constant weight to estimate the concentrations of TN, TP, and total C (TC)).

The SOC content and plant tissue TC concentrations were determined by the dry combustion method via a TOC/TN analyser (multi N/C 3100, Analytik Jena, Germany). Soil TN and TP contents were determined by a discrete autoanalyzer (Smart Chem 450, AMS Alliance, Italy). TN was measured by the indophenol blue method after Kjeldahl digestion [51], and TP was quantified by the molybdenum blue colorimetry method after HClO₄-H₂SO₄ digestion [52]. For plant TN and TP, initial digestion with H2SO4-H2O2 was performed prior to quantification using the same discrete autoanalyzer by the indophenol blue and molybdenum blue methods, respectively [53].

We employed multi-group structural equation modelling to examine the potentially complex effects of the predictor variables on the integral N: P stoichiometry of plants (INPS). The analyses were based on a priori hypothesized models (Fig. 2). These models were specified according to N: P stoichiometric homeostasis in terrestrial plants and were used to test effects in N and P cycling. Across the two groups, all variables were operationalized and scaled identically, supporting measurement comparability. This allows meaningful between-group comparisons of structural path coefficients and reduces the likelihood that apparent group differences are attributable to measurement artifacts. For the variable INPS, we multiplied the N: P ratios of green needles, twigs, and fine roots via PCA (Table 1) prior to linear regression and SEM. PCA is a standard ordination-reduction approach for summarizing correlated variables into orthogonal components and has been widely used to derive integrated indices in ecological datasets [55–57]. In this study, we extracted the first two principal components [58, 59]. The first principal component clearly met the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalue > 1), with an explained variance of 40.43%. Although the second principal component had an eigenvalue of less than 1, it explained an additional 31.35% of the variance. This resulted in a more informative and representative composite measure that captured 72% of the total variance (> 70%), which adequately captured the majority of the shared variance among the variables. Indexing is generally performed in three steps: (1) PCA is performed with z score transformation on the three variables (N: PNeedles, N: PTwigs and N: PRoots); (2) the eigenvalues and eigenvectors are acquired; and (3) the score (S) is combined into an index. Because the dimensions of the three variables are consistent and to preserve the ecological significance of the original variables, we ultimately calculated the scores using the raw data rather than the standardized data. Furthermore, subsequent validation revealed a high correlation (r = 0.97) between the two approaches (Fig. S1). Therefore, using the raw data in our study is both scientifically sound and reasonable. Finally, we applied min–max normalization to the INPS to preserve the ecological significance of the original variables. Results

Variation in age-related N: P ratios in needles, twigs, and fine roots under increasing N deposition

The N: P ratios of the three different components, i.e., needles, twigs, and fine roots, for P. tabuliformis were 8.0521, 7.7387, and 6.9230; 2.6853, 2.3295, and 3.0064; and 2.0415, 1.3908, and 1.1155 across the three stand ages, respectively (Fig. 3). Specifically, the N: P ratios of the aboveground (needles and twigs) and belowground (fine roots) organs generally decreased with stand age under N deposition (P < 0.05; Fig. 3a, b, c). This result implies a shift towards relative N limitation for P. tabuliformis along the chronosequence. N deposition generally increased the N: P ratios in all the metabolically active organs of P. tabuliformis except for the twigs in the 55-year-old stands and the roots in the 65-year-old stands (P < 0.05; Fig. 3a, b, c). The interactions between stand age and N deposition level had a significant effect solely on twigs (P < 0.05; Fig. 3b).

Variation in age-related trade-off based on NRE: NAC of N and P under increasing N deposition

We derived the NRE: NAC of both N and P as an index of the trade-off between NRE and NAC, aiming to track shifts along the conservation (NRE) vs. acquisition (NAC) strategy axis associated with N deposition level and the age sequence. We report NRE: NAC on the original scale for interpretability, as treatment-induced directional patterns were qualitatively consistent for NRE: NAC and log (NRE: NAC). Specifically, higher NRE: NAC values indicate a greater relative contribution of conservation of N and P compared with acquisition, and vice versa. The average NRE-N: NAC-N ratios for the three stands of different ages were 0.8981, 1.2028, and 1.3685 (Fig. 4a), which significantly increased with the development of P. tabuliformis forests under increasing N deposition (P < 0.05; Figs. 4a, 5a and 6a). However, the average NRE-P: NAC-P ratios for the three stands of different ages were 0.0974, 0.0902, and 0.0894 (Fig. 4b), significantly decreasing with stand age (Figs. 4b, 5b and 6b). Thus, for N utilization, the dependence on nutrient resorption was stronger than that for P utilization across all stand ages of the P. tabuliformis forest, and this dependence increased with stand age. N deposition generally decreased both the NRE-N: NAC-N and NRE-P: NAC-P ratios in the 42- and 55-year-old stands but increased it in the 65-year-old stand (P < 0.05; Figs. 4a, b). The highest levels of N deposition at N4 in the 42- and 55-year-old stands were associated with a relative trade-off preference for the root foraging of N compared with the other N levels (Fig. 4a). The interactions between stand age and N deposition level had a significant effect solely on the NRE-P: NAC-P (P < 0.05; Fig. 4b).

Discussion

Age-related nutrient limitations of P. tabuliformis forests under increasing N deposition

The decrease in the N: P ratios of the three different components (needles, twigs, and fine roots) and INPS along the chronosequence provides evidence that the P. tabuliformis forests in our study area are experiencing progressive N limitation with stand development under N addition (Fig. 3; Tables 1 and 2) [15, 16, 21, 77, 78]. And this result indicated that the growth of both aboveground and underground parts was consistent along the chronosequence [46, 65, 71], demonstrating that there was a nutrient utilization trade-off between fine root acquisition and green needle resorption. Our results also revealed that the N: P ratios of resorption, acquisition, and transportation were > 1, < 1, and < 1, respectively, in the stands of the three different ages (Fig. 9a, b, c, d), demonstrating that more N than P was consistently needed but that less N than P was acquired (acquisition and transportation) at every developmental stage measured [31, 79, 80].

Earlier studies have proposed that a transition of soil nutrients from N to P limitation [81, 82] or exacerbated P limitation [72, 79] occurs with increasing stand age. However, our results demonstrated progressive N limitation during stand-level development under increasing N deposition, which was in line with some previous findings [14, 23]. This is attributed mainly to the high accumulation of labile C and N because of the rapid simultaneous accumulation of above- and belowground biomass in trees as they develop and grow [65, 72, 83]. This leads to the exhaustion of nutrients in the SOC and soil N pool (Fig. S2a, b). And the synchronous decline in TN of fine root and soil along the chronosequence reveals a progressive constraint of external N supply (Fig. S3a) [84]. In contrast, the ability of P to be obtained from the soil by P. tabuliformis increased due to the promoted phosphatase activity [85, 86] and its ectomycorrhizal (EM) properties under N deposition (Fig. S4a) [85, 87]. This ultimately results in relative N limitation rather than P limitation of growth in P. tabuliformis forests.

Overall, N deposition increases the TN concentration in the needles and fine roots (Fig. S3a, b), thereby reducing N limitation in the P. tabuliformis forest; however, high N inputs facilitated rapid N uptake in P. tabuliformis tissues and N retention in needle litter (Fig. S3) [7, 88, 89]. The relatively short duration was insufficient for the complete decomposition and mineralization of this N-rich needle litter, thereby limiting the return of N to the mineral soil pool (Figs. S2b, S3c, S5) [90–92].

Age-related limitation of P. tabuliformis drives trade-offs in N and P utilization strategies

Our findings demonstrated that the NRE-N: NAC-N and NRE-P: NAC-P ratios were positively and negatively correlated with stand age, respectively (Fig. 6). And the NRE-N: NAC-N was negatively correlated with the INPS (Fig. 7). These relationships indicated that preference for N resorption via needles and P acquisition via fine roots are key strategies for P. tabuliformis to maintain a constant N: P stoichiometric ratio with increasing stand age in this area (Fig. 6a, b). This is because trees have evolved to preferentially rely on nutrient acquisition strategies via the roots under nutrient-rich soil conditions and resort to nutrient resorption by green foliage in nutrient-deficient soil environments [1, 5, 93].

In addition, our SEM indicated that NRE–N was positively associated with NAC–N, whereas NRE–P was negatively associated with NAC-P (Fig. 8a, b). These findings indicated that P. tabuliformis engaged in active trade-offs in nutrient utilization strategies for N and P [1, 3, 94] in response to corresponding nutrient limitations. With respect to N limitation due to increasing stand age, the NRE-N not sufficiently significantly decreased as a coordinated response to decreasing NAC-N values with increasing stand age (Fig. S4b, c) [95–97]. In contrast, in terms of P utilization, P. tabuliformis forest were unlikely to be relatively P-limited compared with N as stands developed, given that both NRE-P and NAC-P increased with stand age (Fig. S4a, d) [34, 85]. However, the compensatory coupling between NRE-P and NAC-P may therefore represent an optimization of resource use, consistent with nutrient-economy and adaptive strategies [1, 3].

The trade-offs in N and P utilization strategies of P. tabuliformis are based on its N: P stoichiometric homeostasis

The NRE: NAC ratios of both N and P generally decreased in the 42- and 55-year-old stands but increased in the 65-year-old stand after N deposition (P < 0.05; Fig. 4a, b). These decreases occurred because the NRE-N vs. the NRE-P, and the NTC-N (NTCN-N and NTCT-N) vs. the NTC-P (NTCN-P and NTCT-P), in stands of different ages (Fig. 9a, c, d), the TN concentration vs. the TP concentration in all active organs, and the number of senesced needles were generally linearly correlated (Fig. 10). Taken together, these findings demonstrated that plants alter their N and P utilization strategies to maintain a persistent N: P stoichiometry with the external environment during forest development (Fig. 8) [74, 98, 99].

Simple linear regression analysis was conducted of N: P stoichiometry in needles (a), twigs (b), fine roots (c) and senescent needles (d) of P. tabuliformis forest

Based on the SEM results, in terms of N utilization, the NAC-N was moderately impacted by stand age through the variation in the soil TN pool (Fig. 8a, c). The NRE-N directly affected N limitation or indirectly affected it through changes in the NAC-N. With respect to P utilization, the INPS was moderately impacted by NAC-P, stand age, and soil TP through the variation in the NRE-P (Fig. 8b, d). This finding was consistent with our stoichiometric homeostasis results. These results revealed the “plastic” and “weak plastic” homeostasis of fine roots and twigs to soil TN, respectively, but the generally “homeostatic” or “strictly homeostatic” classifications to soil TP (Table 3), which is in line with some previous studies reporting that trees mainly absorb N from the soil but P from senescent leaves in P-deficient areas [47, 60, 61].

We developed a conceptual framework to elucidate the response characteristics of soil nutrient SOC, TN and TP, as well as the N adaptive strategies of P.tabuliformis forests with stand development under N addition (Fig. 11). This framework contributes to our understanding of the ecological significance of the forests’ self-regulation mechanisms, which is essential for maintaining or improving the health and sustainability of forests.

Conclusion

In this study, we found that P. tabuliformis forests experienced progressive N limitation along the age sequence under N addition. Consequently, P. tabuliformis forest adopted divergent nutrient utilization strategies for N and P. Regarding the N, the NRE-N did not decrease significantly as a coordinated response to the declining NAC-N along the chronosequence. Conversely, for P, relative P limitation weakened relative to N as stand age increased in P. tabuliformis forest led to a compensatory adjustment between NRE-P and NAC-P, consistent with nutrient-economy and adaptive strategies. Thus, the trade-off between NRE and NAC for both N and P in P. tabuliformis forests regulated the shifts in resource limitation with increasing stand age under N addition. We attribute these adaptive strategies to the N: P stoichiometric homeostasis of P. tabuliformis. Crucially, our results demonstrate that nutrient resorption of N plays a more pivotal role than nutrient acquisition in regulating plant N: P stoichiometry, acting as a compensatory mechanism to offset age-related progressive stand-level N limitation. This provides mechanistic insights into plant nutrient economy and elucidates how adaptive strategies shift along the chronosequence under N addition, although the generalizability of the conclusions may be constrained by the limited sample sizes. Further studies are needed to include more age classes, long-term N deposition, and litter production. This will help determine whether nutrient limitation and the corresponding utilization strategies change in P. tabuliformis ecosystems in this area.
