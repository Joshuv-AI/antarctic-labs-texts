SCAN: an automated phenotyping tool for real-time capture of leaf stomatal traits in canola
Authors: Lingtian Yao, Susanne von Caemmerer, Florence R Danila, Tracy Lawson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCanola is an important economic and agronomic crop globally, but its yield is under threat due to climate change. Stomata are a key breeding target because of their importance in carbon capture and water use efficiency. However, screening for elite stomatal traits could be laborious and time-consuming. We developed a new toolkit called Stomatal Comprehensive Automated Neural Network or SCAN that combines the use of high-resolution portable digital microscopy with machine learning to automate stomatal trait phenotyping in canola. We show that SCAN can rapidly measure stomatal density, size, and pore area in canola at 97–99% accuracy, and capture real-time stomatal pore status that strongly correlated with leaf porometer measurement in canola. Here we use SCAN to investigate how leaf stomatal traits vary through a canopy in different ecotypes of canola grown in the field and glasshouse conditions. SCAN revealed that stomatal density in canola decreases in more expanded leaves with the abaxial surface having up to 40% more stomata that are 2× more open than the adaxial surface. SCAN also showed that patterns of stomatal traits in canola vary between leaf position in the canopy and change with environment in an ecotype-dependent manner.Keywords: Canola, digital microscopy, high-throughput phenotyping, leaf development, machine learning, stomata

Introduction

Canola (Brassica napus L.) production has economic and agronomic advantages. It is grown as one of the most important oil seeds, being the world’s third most produced vegetable oil after palm and soybean (FAOSTAT: https://www.fao.org/statistics/en). The unique fatty acid profile with zero trans-fat and the lowest amount of saturated fat has made canola oil not only one of the healthiest oils (Lin et al., 2013) but also well suited as a biofuel feedstock linked to its excellent cold-flow properties (Sey et al., 2020). Canola oil enriched with omega-3 is also now being used in Norway as a salmon feed (Ruyter et al., 2022), expanding the impact of canola in the industry. Global canola production for 2023–2024 is projected at 87.4 Mt, with top canola producers including Canada, the European Union, China, India, and Australia (Reidy, 2023). Globally, the canola industry is expected to increase production annually to meet rising demand—not only for edible oil but also for renewable diesel. However, with climate change causing more frequent heat waves and severe drought, canola production and oil quality are under threat (Secchi et al., 2023). Hence, there is a need to selectively breed for heat-resilient and drought-tolerant canola varieties.

The most direct measure of drought tolerance is based on water-use efficiency (WUE) and yield under water-limiting conditions (Tuberosa, 2012). Biologically, stomata are important ‘gates’ for both gas exchange (CO2) of the leaf during photosynthesis and water vapour during transpiration (Lawson and Matthews, 2020). Hence, stomata play a fundamental role in regulating plant water use and carbon gain, presenting a key target for improving WUE and yield (Bertolino et al., 2019; Nguyen et al., 2023). Stomatal conductance—a measure of the degree of stomatal pore opening—and transpiration—the rate of water loss from the leaf through evaporation—are the typical parameters measured to estimate WUE. Stomatal conductance is most commonly measured using portable infrared gas analysers (IRGAs) or hand-held leaf porometers (Toro et al., 2019; Batke et al., 2020). IRGAs use the rate of water loss to calculate stomatal conductance and therefore provide a better account of whole-physiological diurnal plant responses (Batke et al., 2020). Capturing the differences in water in air streams that flow in and out of the leaf chamber in an IRGA could be time-consuming (Toro et al., 2019), but snapshots with an IRGA can now be made within 1–2 min per leaf (e.g. LI-COR LI-6800). However, given that IRGAs are expensive, bulky, and requires significant training to operate, their use is impractical for high-throughput screening of field-grown plants required for crop breeding (Toro et al., 2019). On the other hand, hand-held leaf porometers are lightweight, cheaper, faster, and easy to use compared with IRGAs, making them more suitable for field applications (Toro et al., 2019; Batke et al., 2020). The LI-COR LI-600 leaf porometer uses an algorithm to facilitate high-speed measurement of stomatal conductance—about 5–15 s per leaf depending on species and leaf conditions (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA; https://www.licor.com/products). Leaf porometers measure stomatal conductance by placing the conductance of a leaf in series with two known conductance elements and comparing the humidity measurements between them to estimate water vapour flux (Bell and Squire, 1981; Monteith et al., 1988; Batke et al., 2020). Consequently, leaf porometers give instantaneous stomatal conductance but are limited in capturing maximum operational stomatal conductance responses to different environmental conditions (Toro et al., 2019; Batke et al., 2020).

Stomatal conductance is a function of stomatal traits such as density, size, and aperture (Franks et al., 2009; Lawson and Blatt, 2014). Consequently, stomatal trait measurements are often made in tandem with stomatal conductance measurements from either IRGAs or leaf porometers. Multiple studies have shown that manipulating stomatal density or the number of stomata per leaf surface area directly affects stomatal conductance, WUE, and plant growth (Konrad et al., 2008; Xu and Zhou, 2008; Franks et al., 2015; Nguyen et al., 2023). Since each stoma is made up of two guard cells, which border the pore, stomatal traits such as size and pore dynamics are important factors in understanding stomatal responsiveness under different environmental conditions (Lawson and Matthews, 2020; Lunn et al., 2024). Therefore, knowledge on which stomatal trait combination will provide the most benefit for a particular crop and environmental conditions in the context of stomatal conductance could aid in identifying target genes for improving the WUE and carbon assimilation of a plant (Lawson and Blatt, 2014; Bertolino et al., 2019; Nguyen et al., 2023).

Image capture of stomata can be achieved by simply placing the leaf under a microscope. Conventionally, this could involve either cutting the leaf from the plant prior to imaging (Bourdais et al., 2019; Fetter et al., 2019; Aono et al., 2021; Sai et al., 2023) or via nail polish imprint as a less destructive alternative (Casado-García et al., 2020; Millstead et al., 2020; Kwong et al., 2021). These methods can produce clear images, but require tedious sample preparation and are often lab-based due to dependency on conventional microscopes. The advent of hand-held digital microscopes such as ProScope (Bodelin Technologies, USA) and Dino-Lite (Anmo Electronics Corporation, Taiwan) provided opportunities for a non-destructive method of stomatal capture (Liang et al., 2022; Pathoumthong et al., 2023; Chaplin et al., 2024, Preprint), which is particularly important for field phenotyping applications. However, with this alone, image processing and quantification of stomatal traits remain the bottleneck. As most crop science research heads in the direction of industry, developing high-throughput approaches on image capture but also trait measurement is imperative.

With the development of computer vision, image processing and trait quantification can now be automated. Machine learning-based models to automate stomatal trait measurements including stomatal density, size, and/or pore dynamics have been reported for industrially important crops such as rice (Pathoumthong et al., 2023), wheat (Pathoumthong et al., 2023; Chaplin et al., 2024, Preprint), tomato (Pathoumthong et al., 2023), barley (Casado-García et al., 2020; Sai et al., 2023), maize (Aono et al., 2021; Liang et al., 2022), oil palm (Kwong et al., 2021), soybean (Sakoda et al., 2019; Casado-García et al., 2020), and poplar (Fetter et al., 2019; Li et al., 2019). However, to date, none is available specifically for canola. While some of the existing models such as StomataScorer can be applied to multiple species including rapeseed, the accuracy of prediction is often subpar relative to the main species used for training the model (Liang et al., 2022). This is especially true for stomatal size and pore area measurements. Meanwhile, other models are not applicable simply because of the different image quality used to train the model subject to various methods of image capture such as magnification, lighting, and image background.

To address these, we developed a user-friendly toolkit for stomatal trait measurement specifically for canola based on an efficient and high-throughput computer vision inferencing method. This method involves four processes, namely: (i) microscopy image collection and file structuring; (ii) model training; (iii) statistical analysis after model prediction; and (iv) high-throughput system. We called this toolkit Stomatal Comprehensive Automated Neural Network or SCAN. SCAN employs portable, high-resolution, digital microscopy to give researchers a non-destructive and real-time approach to capture stomatal traits in high throughput, while the built-in models automate the subsequent inferences and post-processing analysis. SCAN was also packaged as an offline application, giving users more flexibility and immediate access to results. Our manual–machine correlation test showed that SCAN can predict stomatal density, size, and pore area at up to 99% accuracy. We also tested the suitability of SCAN in other species, and showed that it can effectively predict stomatal density in other species at a similar level of accuracy without additional training.

Since canola is comprised of three diverse ecotypes, namely spring, winter, and semi-winter (Wu et al., 2019), and stomatal conductance is strongly dependent on the environmental conditions and stomatal traits (Konrad et al., 2008; Fanourakis et al., 2016; Toro et al., 2019), it is imperative to understand if stomatal traits vary among canola ecotypes and whether growing conditions influence stomatal traits in canola. To facilitate high-throughput measurements, we use SCAN in combination with a LI-COR LI-600 porometer/fluorometer to build stomatal trait profiles during leaf development through the canopy in different canola ecotypes grown in the field and in the glasshouse. We found that stomatal trait variation exists in different ecotypes of canola and that these anatomical traits directly correlate with stomatal conductance measurements made by the porometer. SCAN also revealed that patterns of stomatal traits in canola differ between adaxial and abaxial surfaces through the canopy and change with growth environment in an ecotype-dependent manner.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growing conditions

Plants used in this study were grown in either glasshouse or field conditions. Plants in the glasshouse were grown in pots under ambient light and photoperiod with controlled temperature of 28 °C for canola (Brassica napus L. cv Oscar), rice (Oryza sativa cv Kitaake), maize (Zea mays cv B73), and proso millet (Panicum miliaceum), or 22 °C for wheat (Triticum aestivum), Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana cv Columbia), and tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L. cv Samsun). In the field (coordinates: −35.0537477038107, 147.34839065466318), Brassica carinata and Brassica oleracea were grown in pots, while spring (cv 43Y92CL), semi-winter (cv P3083_DH), and winter (cv JET-NEUF-7_DH) ecotypes of canola were grown directly in soil. Plants used were in the vegetative (at least 10-leaf stage) to early flowering stages. All plants used were well watered.

Image capture and pre-processing

We used a portable digital microscope with 5.0 megapixel sensor resolution and ×700–×900 magnification (Dino-Lite Edge 3.0, Cat no. AM73915MT8) to capture the stomata from the top (adaxial) and bottom (abaxial) surfaces of the canola leaves (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Images were obtained within the middle leaf region. During imaging, the Dino-Lite microscope was placed in gentle contact with the leaf surface, supported from behind by a finger. The Dino-Lite magnification was adjusted as needed for image resolution, and can be locked in place. Micrographs were captured at a focused magnification of approximately ×691, with a resolution of 2560×1920 pixels and an embedded scale bar of 0.05 mm via the compatible imaging software, DinoCapture 2.0 (https://www.dino-lite.com/download01.php). For better contrast, light intensity was adjusted by setting Dino-Lite Luma to 11 during imaging. Leaf micrographs from canola were manually annotated for stomatal traits such as stomatal density, stomatal size, and pore area using Roboflow (Dwyer et al., 2024). We used a polygon to outline each stoma (i.e. area occupied by the stomatal pore and corresponding guard cells) to generate our dataset for both stomatal detection and segmentation. We selected distinguishing stomata with different degrees of aperture status from stomatal segmentation prediction results, and applied the same annotating strategies to build the dataset for pore segmentation. These datasets were split into training and validation set ratios of 0.8/0.2 and 0.75/0.25, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S1B).

Model training

The entire detection and segmentation tasks were carried out using either the You Only Look Once (YOLO) algorithm modular templates or transferred learning from our pre-trained model. The workflow involved two tasks at each stage of model training: (i) initializing the YOLO architecture template from scratch to classify and localize each stoma region of interest (ROI), enabling transferred learning for stomata size and pore segmentation; and (ii) fine-tuning the hyperparameters to improve the performance of the model. YOLOv8 consisting of two modules was used (Supplementary Fig. S2). These modules are (i) CSPDarkNet53 (Wang et al., 2020) with a feature extractor in the backbone including Convolution (conv), Cross Stage Partial bottleneck with 2 convolutions—Fast (C2f), and Spatial Pyramid Pooling—Fast (SPPF) units, and (ii) the head equipped with Path Aggregation Feature Pyramid Networks (PAFPN), which fuses multi-level features followed by the final prediction layer to deliver detection or segmentation tasks. All original images in datasets with 2560×1920 resolution pixels were resized to 640×480 with RGB channels to avoid model overcomplexity and increase the training speed. The stomatal leaf surface dataset was initially used to train the stomata detection model with the default hyperparameters setting in YOLOv8 for 200 epochs. However, two major obstacles were encountered: (i) incomplete or cropped stomata at the edge of the microscope images and (ii) blurred or out-of-focus stomata. To address these issues, we employed the K-folder cross-validation strategy (Mosteller and Tukey, 1968) and a higher training image resolution of 1024×792 to enhance model learning without requiring an extended dataset and additional annotations. As a result, the detection model was able to classify the two types of stomata (Supplementary Fig. S3). The stomatal dataset was split into K partitions, with each partition used as a validation set to evaluate the model. Subsequently, the overall average performance across the K partitions was recorded for model evaluation.

After several training evaluations of the detection model, hyperparameter fine-tuning strategies were carried out to improve the model performance. Weight regularization was adjusted using the AdamW optimizer (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017, Preprint) with a learning rate range from 1E-3 to 1E-6, in addition to applying dropout (Srivastava et al., 2014) and mosaic data augmentation to enhance model generalization on unseen datasets. With the optimal results from fine-tuning for detecting and localizing each stoma in the microscope images, the weights from the detection model were transferred to train the stomatal segmentation model. Concurrently, regions of each representative stoma with either open or closed pores were cropped to establish a pore dataset. The pore segmentation model applied YOLOv8-nano structure templates with transferred weights from the stomatal segmentation model, resized to 108 pixels for training and fine-tuning based on the domain knowledge of the extracted pore dataset. The details of the three model training templates and transferred weights are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

The training tasks were carried out on an Ubuntu 20.04 Linux server using two Nvidia A30 (24G) Graphic Processing Units (GPUs). The full details of the weights, hyperparameters, training scripts, and datasets of the models can be found at https://github.com/William-Yao0993/FD_detection.

Scale bar detection

The results from model inferences, including both bounding boxes from detection and masks from instance segmentation, were at the pixel level. These measurements cannot directly correlate in the bioinformatic domain without standardization to real-world units. We applied an automated algorithm to detect the scale bar in the microscope images and return a pixel-to-unit ratio. A combination of Canny edge detection (Canny, 1986) and Hough line transformation (Duda and Hart, 1972) was used to identify line candidates in the image; in this case, the scale bar was embedded at the bottom left of the input image. Line candidates were filtered based on rules in a polar coordinate system, namely have angles in polar coordinates near 0° or 90°, with a tolerance of ±2°. The line with the maximum length was chosen as the result. The ratio was calculated between the scale bar unit and the detected line. In cases where no lines fit the rules-based algorithm, a default ratio was used. The pseudocode of the scale bar detection algorithm is provided in Supplementary Fig. S6. With the connection between pixel and real-scale units, the following three measurements were derived: stomatal density, stomatal size, and pore area. Stomatal density was determined by the numbers of bounding boxes in the stomata detection model, while stomatal size and pore area were calculated based on the masked area in pixels and converted to real scale using the scale bar ratio from stomatal segmentation and pore segmentation models, respectively. These processes comprised the pipeline of SCAN for a single microscopy image (Fig. 1).

SCAN measurement pipeline and system design. SCAN automates stomatal trait measurement from digital images collected via a Dino-lite portable microscope. The parallel system design of SCAN enables users to provide a main folder containing multiple subfolders of the micrograph dataset as an entrance. SCAN works through the structure from the entry point and enables parallel processing of each subfolder. Within each subfolder, SCAN sequentially processes each microscopy image through a single pipeline. The pipeline of SCAN involves several key steps to process and analyse microscopy images for stomatal traits. [1] The stomata detection model locates all possible stomata above a confidence threshold in the input image by drawing a bounding box around it. This step establishes the position and approximate size of the stomata but does not differentiate individual pixels belonging to the guard cells or pore. Meanwhile, the [2] scale bar detection algorithm is also running in parallel. The localized stomata from [1] are then used to initiate the stomata segmentation model [3]. The stomata segmentation model assigns a label to each pixel in the bounding box area, capturing the entire stoma (both guard cells and pore) with pixel-level precision. Each stoma within the bounding boxes is extracted [4] to form a dataset for pore analysis. The extracted stoma dataset from [4] enters the pore segmentation model [5]. The pore segmentation model isolates and labels only the pore region within a given stoma, providing measurements of the shape of the pore independently from the surrounding guard cells. This pipeline ensures a thorough and automated analysis of stomatal traits, from initial detection to detailed segmentation. All information from the parallel pipelines is then gathered for statistical analysis. Data output from SCAN includes an exportable predicted image with annotations, an Excel worksheet containing raw values and statistical tables, and plots. A full demonstration and download of SCAN can be found at: https://github.com/William-Yao0993/SCAN.

High-throughput system and application

Beyond the prediction on a single image, a parallel computing system using Central Processing Units (CPUs) in conjunction with the multiple model engines to significantly reduce model inference and statistical analysis time was integrated in SCAN to improve the system performance on standard laboratory workstations that lack a GPU and large random-access memory (RAM). This enables SCAN to perform inferences and computations at multiple folder levels, with each folder containing microscopy images. Here, the input folder was first searched and structured to target image type files (supported formats: jpg, jpeg, png, bmp, dng, mpo, tif, tiff, webp, and pfm). The system was designed to automatically enable multiple model engines based on the folder structure. For example, if the input folder contains x numbers of subfolders of images as shown in Fig. 1, the system enables x numbers of models to run in parallel and perform statistical analysis. As results, the system generates an Excel file that contain each piece of stoma-level information (stoma size and pore area in mm2), each image-level summary (stomata density per mm2, statistical mean and SEs for stoma size and pore area), and each subfolder-level summary (statistical mean and SEs for stomatal density, size, and pore area). Boxplots for these traits were also generated for distribution comparison among folders. SCAN was integrated into an interactive, user-friendly application that allows users to visualize and export predicted images (with annotations), statistical tables, and plots. Detailed application instruction and download information can be found at https://github.com/William-Yao0993/SCAN/blob/main/README.md.

Manual–machine correlation test in canola

Stomatal density, stomatal size, and pore area were manually recorded and measured from the micrographs using ImageJ software. Stomatal density was defined as the number of stomata per mm2 leaf area. Stomatal sizes are measured as the area occupied by the stomatal pore and corresponding guard cells in mm2. Pore area is captured as the area occupied by the stomatal pore in mm2. For stomatal density, 66 unseen microscopy images were counted. For stomatal size and pore area, the freehand drawing tool was used on 155 extracted image areas containing both open and closed stomatal pores. The SCAN results and manual ImageJ results were compared by plotting SCAN results on the x-axis and manual ImageJ results on the y-axis, fitting a linear regression line, and calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient.

SCAN processing time evaluation

The SCAN processing time across three different processing units: a mid-range CPU (Intel® Core™ i7-6700 CPU @ 3.40 GHz, 3.41 GHz), a newer CPU (13th Gen Intel® Core™ i7-1365U 1.80 GHz), and a server GPU (NVIDIA A30 Tensor Core GPU 24G) was compared. Processing time was measured for the entire SCAN system workflow using two test datasets. The 132 image dataset is an unseen dataset containing canola microscopy images from abaxial and adaxial leaf surfaces, while the 427 image dataset was generated by selecting images from the training dataset. Each microscopy image has a resolution of 2560×1980. Total images processed and the processing time in seconds for each configuration, with and without pore measurements, were tabulated in Supplementary Table S2.

Leaf stomatal trait profiling

We measured stomatal traits in glasshouse-grown canola (cv Oscar), maize, and tobacco (Supplementary Dataset S1). Additionally, we analysed three ecotypes of canola in the field (spring, semi-winter, and winter; Supplementary Dataset S1). All plants used were wild type, and profiling was performed from the second youngest leaf (i.e. leaf position 1) through to the succeeding 10th leaf below (Supplementary Fig. S7). Three biological replicates were included for each experiment. Using SCAN, stomatal density (mm−2 leaf area) and stomatal size (mm2) were quantified across all three species, while pore area (mm2) was only measured for canola. Images were collected between 09.00 h and 14.00 h consistently across all experiments. For each leaf, microscopy images were captured from both adaxial and abaxial surfaces, with ∼5–10 images taken per side within the middle region of the leaf excluding the midvein. In parallel, we performed stomatal conductance measurement in glasshouse-grown (cv Oscar) canola plants (Supplementary Dataset S1). Stomatal conductance (mol m⁻2 s⁻1) was measured using the LI-COR LI-600 porometer/fluorometer (LI-COR Environmental, USA). For glasshouse-grown plants, measurements were taken on consecutive days within the same time frame, while for field-grown plants, data were collected on the same day within the same time frame. Field-grown plants used were sown in early June and measured in late August within the same year.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using two-way ANOVA to determine the main and interaction effects of leaf position (leaf 1–10), ecotype (spring, semi-winter, and winter), or location (glasshouse versus field) on stomatal density, size, and pore area in canola.

SCAN application to other species

The suitability of SCAN to detect and measure stomatal density was tested in other species. These comprise four dicot species (B. carinata, B. oleracea, Arabidopsis, and tobacco) and four monocot species (rice, wheat, maize, and proso millet). Micrographs were captured at a magnification of approximately ×691 (Dino-Lite Edge 3.0, Cat no. AM73915MT8) or approximately ×414 (Dino-Lite Edge 3.0, Cat no. AM73915MZT4) depending on the species. The manual–machine correlation test for stomatal density was performed as described above.

Results

SCAN allows rapid and accurate measurement of stomatal traits in canola

The parameters in each model of SCAN were optimized to deliver the best prediction results for canola. To evaluate the performance of SCAN, we ran machine learning metrics and a manual–machine stomata measurement test on leaf images obtained from canola. When tested on the validation set, SCAN achieved the highest mAP at a 0.5 confidence level (mAP@50) of 0.967, 0.965, and 0.958 (Supplementary Fig. S4) and F1 scores of 0.94, 0.94, and 0.91 (Supplementary Fig. S5) for stomata detection, segmentation, and pore segmentation, respectively. Across the three models, the stomatal density and size models performed stably and reliably at the 0.83 confidence level, while the pore area model had slight decreases compared with the other two (Supplementary Fig. S5). The input images for the pore segmentation model were the extracted areas from the microscope images which had dimensions of ∼80–130 pixels, and hence contain much less information for accurate prediction. We conducted manual measurement using the ImageJ software to verify the correctness of SCAN for stomatal density, stomatal size, and pore area (Fig. 2). We observed that SCAN accurately measured stomatal density when compared with manual measurements (Fig. 2A), while there was an overestimation for stomatal size measurements from SCAN compared with manual measurements (Fig. 2B). As expected, there is an underestimation for pore area measurements from SCAN compared with manual measurements (Fig. 2C). Nevertheless, we found that the data were highly aligned along the fitted line in all stomatal trait plots, achieving correlation coefficients of 0.983, 0.978, and 0.990 for stomatal density, size, and pore area measurements, respectively (Fig. 2).

Correlation test between manual and SCAN measurements in canola. SCAN can effectively predict and measure stomatal density (A), stomatal size (B), and pore area (C) in canola as shown by the high correlation between manual and automated measurements. Each graph plots manual measurements on the y-axis and automated measurements on the x-axis, with a black line representing the best fit line for the manual–machine results and the dashed line as the 1:1 line. The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) is indicated in each graph with sample size (n).

In terms of processing time, SCAN offers significant advantages compared with manual methods or other stomatal detection systems. On average, the manual measurement time for one microscopy image takes ∼3–6 min, depending on the total number of stomata, image quality, and pore aperture status. SCAN can automate the measurement of each image within 2 s. This was tested across three different processing units using two canola testing datasets with varying numbers of images, as summarized in Supplementary Table S2. The high-throughput performance of SCAN was demonstrated, showing that it can handle hundreds of images within minutes, even with minimal processing units. In addition, SCAN can process and produce results for >400 images including all measurements, within 157.7 s when using a GPU. This automation significantly reduces the workload for researchers, as it would take days to manually measure stomatal density, size, and pore area in hundreds of images, where each individual image contains 15–60 stomata.

SCAN captures real-time stomatal trait relationship in canola

To determine the relationship between stomatal traits in canola, we built stomatal trait profiles for both adaxial and abaxial surfaces using cv Oscar grown in glasshouse conditions (Fig. 3A). The Oscar plants were at the rosette stage during the time of measurement (Supplementary Fig. S7A). Stomatal density showed a decreasing trend as the leaf expands, and was consistently higher on the abaxial leaf surface compared with the adaxial surface at all leaf positions (Fig. 3A). Similarly, pore area showed a decreasing trend with leaf expansion, indicating a positive relationship with stomatal density (Fig. 3A). However, pore areas were greater on the abaxial leaf surface compared with the adaxial leaf surface (Fig. 3A). In contrast, stomatal size showed an increasing trend with leaf expansion, suggesting a negative relationship with stomatal density and pore area (Fig. 3A).

Stomatal density, stomatal size, and pore area on both adaxial and abaxial surfaces from leaf positions 1 to 10 (see Supplementary Fig. S7A) were measured using SCAN to build leaf stomatal trait profiles in glasshouse-grown canola cv Oscar (A). In parallel, stomatal conductance was measured using the LI-600 porometer (B). The opening and closing of the stomatal pore define stomatal conductance, which could influence the WUE and yield of a plant. To further verify the accuracy of the stomatal aperture status derived from SCAN, we measured stomatal conductance using the LI-COR LI-600 porometer/fluorometer. Results displayed a similar decreasing trend to that observed in pore area measurements, with conductance declining from leaf position 1 to leaf position 10 (Fig. 3B). However, measured stomatal conductance was much higher in the adaxial leaf surface compared with the abaxial leaf surface (Fig. 3B). When plotted against each other, positive correlation coefficients of 0.87 and 0.83 between SCAN-measured total pore area and LI-600-measured stomatal conductance were achieved for the adaxial leaf surface and abaxial leaf surface, respectively (Fig. 3C). These findings suggest that SCAN can capture the real-time stomatal pore status of canola and can be used in tandem with an LI-600 porometer for high-throughput stomatal trait measurement.

SCAN reveals influence of leaf position, location, and ecotype on stomatal traits in canola

To demonstrate whether the leaf stomatal trait relationships described above are conserved in canola, we used SCAN and performed similar leaf profiling measurements on three canola cultivars of different ecotypes growing in the field (Fig. 4). All three ecotypes were grown at the same time during the mid-winter–early spring season in Australia (coordinates: −35.0537477038107, 147.34839065466318). At the time of measurement, the spring ecotype was flowering (Fig. 4A), the semi-winter ecotype was budding (Fig. 4B), and the winter ecotype was starting elongation of the stem (Fig. 4C). Measurements from SCAN revealed a consistent decreasing trend in stomatal density from leaf position 1 to leaf position 10 on both adaxial and abaxial surfaces in all three ecotypes (Fig. 4). Meanwhile, stomatal size showed an increasing trend from leaf position 1 to leaf position 10 on both adaxial and abaxial surfaces (Fig. 4), consistent with the negative relationship observed between stomatal density and size in glasshouse-grown canola (Fig. 3A). While there is a general trend observed for stomatal density and size, the spring ecotype (Fig. 4A) showed greater oscillations in measured values compared with semi-winter (Fig. 4B) and winter (Fig. 4C) ecotypes. In terms of pore area, the spring ecotype showed a decreasing trend (Fig. 4A) while semi-winter (Fig. 4B) and winter (Fig. 4C) ecotypes had increasing trends. Despite a difference in developmental stages, the decreasing trend in the pore area of the spring ecotype (Fig. 4A) was consistent with that of glasshouse-grown cv Oscar (Fig. 3A), which is also a spring ecotype.

The detailed leaf profiling study conducted using SCAN in canola identified stomatal trait relationships and demonstrated stomatal trait variations among canola cultivars of varying ecotypes (Figs 3, 4). Statistical analysis revealed significant effects of leaf position, ecotype, and location on stomatal density and size, but not pore area (Table 1). For canola cultivars belonging to the same ecotype, stomatal density was significantly influenced by location, while stomatal size was significantly influenced by leaf position and location; however, no significant interaction effects were found (Table 1). Notably, the significant effect of location (P-value <0.001) on stomatal size was stronger than that of leaf position (P-value <0.05). For canola grown in the field, stomatal density and stomatal size were both significantly influenced by leaf position and ecotype, but no significant interaction effects were found (Table 1). In this case, ecotype (P-value <0.001) had a stronger influence on stomatal traits than leaf position (P-value <0.05). Expanding from these results, we calculated the ratios of stomatal density and pore area between adaxial and abaxial surfaces across leaf position 1 to leaf position 10 to quantify the stomatal trait difference (Fig. 5). We found that, in general, young, non-fully expanded canola leaves (i.e. leaf position 1) have a similar number of stomata per leaf surface area on the adaxial and abaxial surfaces (Fig. 5A). However, in more expanded leaves, stomatal density decreases at a lower rate on the abaxial surface compared with the adaxial surface, leading to up to 40% more stomata per leaf surface area on the abaxial surface that are 2× more open compared with those of the adaxial surface (Fig. 5B).

SCAN can be used for other species

To test if the applicability of SCAN can be extended to other species, we collected images (Fig. 6A) and conducted the manual–machine test on stomatal density in eight other species (Fig. 6B), namely B. carinata, B. oleracea, Arabidopsis, tobacco, rice, wheat, maize, and proso millet, with the same devices and analyses used for canola. The results of the manual–machine test on stomatal density for all eight species tested indicate a high correlation between SCAN and manual measurements. Correlation coefficients obtained were 0.9871, 0.9847, 0.8123, 0.9582, 0.9582, 0.9106, 0.8998, and 0.8786 for B. carinata, B. oleracea, Arabidopsis, tobacco, rice, wheat, maize, and proso millet, respectively. These high correlation coefficients demonstrate the capability of SCAN to accurately predict stomatal density even in species not included in the training set. To test this further, we performed leaf profiling measurement in tobacco (Fig. 6C; Supplementary Fig. S7B) and maize (Fig. 6D). We showed that SCAN was able to replicate the leaf stomatal trait profile generated from our previous work in tobacco (Clarke et al., 2021). SCAN also revealed that, in contrast to what we observed in canola (Fig. 5), stomatal density between the adaxial and abaxial leaf surfaces of tobacco were different in younger leaves and became more similar as the leaf expands (Fig. 6C). While SCAN was able to predict stomatal density and size in other species (Fig. 6B–D), SCAN cannot be used for pore area measurement without additional training.

Discussion

SCAN automates stomatal trait measurement in canola

It is known that anatomical and functional stomatal traits can vary intra-specifically (McAusland et al., 2020). While this natural variation provides an opportunity to identify cultivars with sought-after stomatal traits for breeding elite varieties (Faralli et al., 2019b), screening for these differences is both challenging and time-consuming. Several pioneering approaches have emerged for automated stomatal detection and counting, but none exists yet specifically for canola, which is an important crop globally. Consequently, we developed a tool that provides rapid and reliable phenotyping measurement of leaf stomatal traits in canola by combining the use of high-resolution digital microscopy and machine learning. We call this tool SCAN. SCAN contains three models, stomatal detection, stomatal segmentation, and pore segmentation to predict stomatal density, stomatal size, and stomatal pore area, respectively (Fig. 1). Our model testing revealed that SCAN can produce consistent and reproducible predictions each time (Supplementary Figs S4, S5), eliminating variations in measurements introduced by human error during manual counting. In addition, we demonstrated that SCAN could carry out the workflow of hundreds of images in minutes (Supplementary Table S2) and at very high accuracy in canola (Fig. 2). We also showed that SCAN can be used in tandem with a LI-COR LI-600 porometer/fluorometer for stomatal trait phenotyping. The results from the LI-600 porometer (Fig. 3B) validated the accuracy of the pore area measurements obtained from SCAN (Fig. 3A). The strong positive correlation between pore area and stomatal conductance (Fig. 3C) confirms that SCAN reliably reflects stomatal traits that directly influence plant physiological processes (Kaiser, 2009; Lawson and Blatt, 2014). This underscores the applicability of SCAN in capturing stomatal traits under varying environmental conditions. Overall, these findings demonstrate the robustness of SCAN for accurately profiling stomatal traits across different growth environments in canola.

SCAN offers advantages over existing models

In contrast to other existing models that employ Mask Region-based Convolutional Neural Networks (R-CNN) to segment the guard cells followed by estimation of the pore area via an ellipse approximation (Fetter et al., 2019; Li et al., 2019; Kwong et al., 2021; Pathoumthong et al., 2023; Sai et al., 2023), SCAN involves a purely neural network-based segmentation for measuring pore area. This allows SCAN to capture subtle morphological differences that might be lost with an ellipse approximation, which is particularly beneficial for detection of incomplete stomata in image edges (Supplementary Fig. S3). The use of a high-resolution digital microscope in capturing leaf stomatal density and aperture status (i.e. open or closed) in SCAN provided a non-destructive and automated analysis of stomatal features in real-time during phenotyping. This allows parallel physiological measurements and/or molecular screening to be performed, improving accuracy in correlation studies. The lightweight design of the digital microscope, straightforward pairing between the digital microscope and a laptop, and the offline accessibility of SCAN offer a portable and user-friendly tool for field application. Dino-Lite microscopes are also commercially available (https://www.dino-lite.com/), making the setup easily reproducible. The desktop application system of SCAN eliminates the technical burden for the users by packaging the three models (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table S1) in a user-friendly interface that enables processing of multiple images by just clicking the appropriate buttons in the display window—another major advantage of SCAN over other existing stomatal trait machine learning models (Kwong et al., 2021; Pathoumthong et al., 2023; Sai et al., 2023). SCAN can also process the collected data into plots that can be exported along with the processed images and a raw data file that includes statistical analysis, allowing immediate data comparison and quality assessment. These make SCAN a better option as a phenotyping tool for stomatal trait measurement for laboratory and field work, particularly in canola.

SCAN identifies leaf development, ecotype, and environment as pivotal drivers of stomatal trait variation in canola

The distributional pattern of stomata and the stomatal area could strongly influence the ecological and evolutionary success of a species in a particular habitat as these traits are important factors determining rates of CO2 assimilation and water and heat loss (Mott et al., 1982; Drake et al., 2019; Wall et al., 2022). Notably, in this study, when different canola ecotypes were sown and grown at the same time in the field, we observed that the rate of plant development varies (Fig. 4). Hence, there is expectation that canola belonging to different ecotypes will have differences in stomatal traits. Here, we used SCAN to build a leaf developmental atlas of stomatal traits in canola of various ecotypes. Stomatal patterning is reported to be differentially regulated between adaxial and abaxial leaf surfaces, with stomatal traits such as stomatal density, size, and pore aperture greatly influenced by plant developmental variation and/or environmental cues (Aston, 1978; Soares et al., 2008; Richardson et al., 2017; Faralli et al., 2019a; Jalakas et al., 2024). Consistent with this, our results revealed the influence of leaf position, ecotype, and location on stomatal traits in canola, particularly on stomatal density and size (Figs 3A, 4; Table 1). Similarly, we also showed that patterns of stomatal density and pore area in canola leaf vary between adaxial and abaxial surfaces through the canopy (Figs 3–5; Table 1). In Arabidopsis, natural variation in stomatal trait responses to environmental conditions such as light, CO2, and humidity in different ecotypes has been attributed to the differences in intrinsic response mechanisms to environmental signals among ecotypes (Takahashi et al., 2015). Given that Arabidopsis and canola are closely related species belonging to the large Brassicaceae family (Yang et al., 1999), it is likely that different intrinsic response mechanisms are also present among canola ecotypes. These findings provide valuable insights to support canola breeding programmes.

SCAN application can be extended to other species

We observed that the SCAN predictions hold at different magnifications (Figs 2, 6A, B), even though only ×691 magnification images of canola were used in our training set (Supplementary Fig. S1). We showed that SCAN can detect stomata in different species of dicots and monocots and predict stomatal density at 81–99% accuracy (Fig. 6B). Consequently, we use SCAN to perform leaf stomatal density profiling in tobacco (Fig. 6C) and maize (Fig. 6D). Consistent with our previous findings (Clarke et al., 2021), our results revealed that stomatal density is higher in younger leaves compared with older leaves in tobacco (Fig. 6C). These results highlight the ability of SCAN to detect the stomatal density pattern consistently even in species other than canola, re-emphasizing its reliability as a tool for high-throughput phenotyping.

However, it is also important to note that the limitation of SCAN for application in other species lies in stomatal size and pore area measurements. In this study, SCAN only managed to predict stomatal size in tobacco (Fig. 6C) and maize (Fig. 6D), but not in other species tested. The distinct guard cell shapes in dicots (kidney-shaped) and monocots (dumbbell-shaped) (Hetherington and Woodward, 2003) and different complex types of stomata (Lawson and Matthews, 2020) made it difficult for SCAN, which is specifically trained for canola, to accurately predict stomatal size and/or pore area in other species. SCAN implements a pixel-by-pixel area calculation based on the kidney-shaped guard cell morphology in canola (Fig. 1); hence, when another species with very distinct guard cell morphology is analysed, there will be a decreased accuracy in pixel-level segmentation. We also observed that differences in leaf structural features can have an influence on the quality of images obtained and hence model predictions. For instance, in Fig. 6B, Arabidopsis showed the lowest accuracy in stomatal density predictions (81%) because abundant trichomes present on the Arabidopsis leaf surface create noise and blurring in the background, affecting the focus of the microscope images. This is on top of the small size and inherent curvature in the leaf of the Arabidopsis plant, which made non-destructive imaging challenging. Prediction accuracy will improve for Arabidopsis with higher quality images or by retraining the model to tailor for features of Arabidopsis, which can also be initiated using SCAN.

The potential of extending SCAN to other species offers two main benefits. First, SCAN’s weights can be used to train a new fine-tuned network for other species, which can reduce training time by transferring the pre-trained network from SCAN. For instance, researchers can use SCAN as a starting point for measuring stomatal traits in Arabidopsis based on the similar feature found in stomata. SCAN can then learn and conduct accurate prediction once a new dataset of Arabidopsis is annotated and provided. This brings us to the second benefit, because SCAN can also serve as an annotation tool when preparing a new training dataset for other species, reducing the trivial and repetitive work in stomata and its corresponding pore labelling. In this case, researchers only need to annotate the remaining undetected instances in establishing the new dataset before retraining a new model for other species for measuring stomatal leaf traits.

Conclusion

In this study, we describe the development and application of the first automated leaf stomatal trait measurement tool for canola called SCAN. We demonstrate that SCAN provides accurate measurements and automated analysis of stomatal traits in a high-throughput, real-time, and non-destructive manner, allowing for more detailed measurements to be made in plant canopies. We show that stomatal traits in canola are influenced by leaf development, ecotype and environment, and that stomatal patterning and stomatal response vary between the upper and the lower side of leaves throughout the canopy. We show that SCAN can be used to measure stomatal density in other species and can be utilized in tandem with a LI-COR LI-600 porometer/fluorometer to study stomatal dynamics in canola. This makes SCAN a valuable field tool for studying real-time stomatal traits. This is important as it offers an opportunity for faster and more accurate trait screening towards breeding climate-resilient crops.
