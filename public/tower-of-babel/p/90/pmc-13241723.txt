Protocol for generating endogenous degron tags in essential transcription factors in human iPSCs via CRISPR-Cas9
Authors: Ziwei Li, Arthur Roberts, Utkal Nimse, Lucas Vu, Jia Fei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPrecise genome engineering in human pluripotent stem cells remains inefficient, limiting endogenous fluorescent tagging needed to study phase-separated membraneless nuclear compartments. Here, we present a protocol for generating precise knockin alleles of essential genes in human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) via CRISPR-Cas9 editing. We describe steps for combining transient p53 inhibition, optimized transfection conditions, and fluorescence-activated cell sorting. This protocol enables recovery of viable edited clones that would otherwise be lost due to editing-induced stress or essential gene perturbation.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Li et al.1

Before you begin

Efficient CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing in human pluripotent stem cells depends critically on careful guide RNA (gRNA) and donor template design. In this protocol, gRNA sequences are cloned into Cas9 and gRNA co-expression plasmids to enable transient but robust Cas9 activity during the editing window.

Donor plasmids are constructed using NEBuilder HiFi Assembly or an equivalent Gibson-style cloning strategy.2 For degron-based protein degradation systems, the E3 ligase auxin receptor F-box protein (AtAFB2) is commonly introduced by viral transduction or safe harbor locus knockin.3 However, viral methods can cause variable expression and promoter silencing, leading to cellular heterogeneity. We therefore attempted knockin at the safe harbor Adeno-associated virus integration site 1 (AAVS1) locus, but this did not yield robust expression in our system. gRNA target sites are selected such that the Cas9 cleavage site lies within 50 bp of the intended insertion site, with optimal efficiency typically observed when the cut site is within approximately ±25 bp.4 Based on repeated empirical observations, knockin efficiency is highest for insertions smaller than 3 kb, although larger insertions can still be recovered using fluorescence-activated cell sorting-based enrichment.

For studies involving phase-separated condensates or live-cell imaging, careful selection of fluorescent tags is critical. GFP variants are bright and suitable for low-abundance proteins, whereas commonly used red fluorescent proteins, such as mCherry, are dimmer and often limit resolution. For single-molecule or ligand-based imaging, self-labeling tags such as HaloTag may be preferable. Tag choice should also consider endogenous expression levels and spectral separation when labeling multiple proteins.

When incorporating degron-based perturbations, the choice of degradation system should be carefully matched to protein abundance and experimental requirements. The auxin-inducible degron (AID) system provides efficient and reversible protein depletion and is particularly well suited for essential genes, as degradation can be titrated by adjusting auxin concentration.5,6 However, for highly abundant proteins, AID-mediated depletion may be incomplete or slow. In such cases, alternative systems such as dTAG (degradation tag, an FKBP12_F36V-based degron) can achieve more rapid and robust degradation.7 These advantages must be balanced against potential limitations, including higher cost, prolonged effects after ligand removal, and reliance on endogenous E3 ligases, which may perturb native ubiquitin-proteasome pathways.

An additional key consideration in degron design is the choice of N- or C-terminal tagging. Tag placement can significantly influence protein stability, localization, and function (For example,8,9), and should therefore be evaluated in the context of the protein’s structure and biological role. Careful assessment of tagging strategy is essential to ensure effective degradation while preserving native protein behavior prior to induction.

Innovation

Mechanistic studies of transcription and chromatin regulation have often relied on mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs), as precise genome engineering remains inefficient in human pluripotent stem cells due to low homology-directed repair (HDR) efficiency and poor survival after single-cell dissociation.10 As a result, endogenous fluorescent tagging of transcription factors in native human chromatin, which is required to study phase-separated nuclear condensates, has been difficult to achieve.

Targeting essential genes such as SUPT16H or Nucleosome Destabilizing Factor (NDF, also called Glyr1) further exacerbates these challenges, as the combined stress of Cas9-induced double-strand breaks and single-cell isolation frequently triggers p53-dependent apoptosis before edited clones can be recovered.1,11,12,13,14 This protocol mitigates these barriers through three key modifications. First, transient suppression of the p53 response is achieved by co-transfection of the dominant-negative p53 construct pCE-mp53DD, reducing apoptosis during the critical editing window without permanently altering checkpoint control.15 Second, DNA repair outcomes are biased toward homology-directed repair through the use of the non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) inhibitor SCR7, thereby increasing the likelihood of precise knockin events.16 Finally, survival during single-cell isolation is supported by the use of CloneR2 or RevitaCell Supplement, which is essential for maintaining viability during FACS-based clonal selection. Together, these features enable reliable recovery of precise knockin clones at essential loci in human iPSCs.

Maintain human iPSCs under optimal culture conditions

Timing: 2–3 days

Timing: 2–3 days

Before initiating genome editing experiments, it is important to establish appropriate culture conditions for human iPSCs. Cells should be maintained under feeder-free conditions using a defined medium such as Essential 8 (E8 or E8 Plus; Thermo Fisher Scientific) or an equivalent formulation (e.g., TeSR; STEMCELL Technologies).17,18 Alternative approaches, including homemade E8 formulations supplemented with defined growth factors, may also be used if properly optimized.19,20

Culture conditions should be selected to support stable pluripotency, minimize spontaneous differentiation, and ensure high cell viability, particularly prior to transfection. Maintaining cells at appropriate density and avoiding excessive mechanical stress are critical, as both overconfluency and harsh dissociation can compromise cell health and reduce editing efficiency.

For genome editing applications, cells should be in a robust proliferative state with consistent morphology and minimal differentiation. Optimization of culture conditions, including medium choice, coating substrate, and passaging strategy, may be necessary depending on the cell line and experimental design.

Detailed step-by-step procedures for iPSC culture and maintenance are provided in the following section.

Genomic DNA extraction

Column-based genomic DNA extraction using DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit.

This section describes two alternative approaches for genomic DNA extraction from human iPSCs. Column-based purification yields high-quality genomic DNA suitable for sequencing, cloning, and detection of low-frequency genome editing events, whereas the HotSHOT method provides a rapid and low-cost preparation compatible with PCR-based genotyping of clonal cell lines.23 However, due to its lower sensitivity, the HotSHOT method is not suitable for detecting low-frequency editing events in bulk cell populations after transfection.

Expected outcomes

This protocol enables recovery of viable human iPSC clones carrying precise knockin alleles at essential transcription factor loci. By combining transient p53 inhibition using p53DD and suppression of non-homologous end joining with the SCR7 inhibitor, approximately 0.1–1% of parental cells can be successfully edited and sort out by fluorescence-activated single-cell sorting (Figure 3).

Recovered clones maintain characteristic iPSC morphology, normal growth behavior, and expression of pluripotency markers after expansion. Endogenously fluorescently tagged proteins are expressed at physiological levels and display subcellular localization patterns consistent with known biology, with signal intensity varying according to endogenous protein abundance and fluorophore choice (for example,1). For degron-tagged alleles, ligand addition induces robust and reversible protein depletion within 1-4 h, with depletion kinetics depending on protein abundance and the degradation system used. In Western blot analysis, this is reflected by a progressive decrease in target protein levels with increasing ligand concentration or time (for example, Fig. 6e of the ref.1).

Limitations

Insertion size strongly influences editing efficiency; insert larger than ∼3 kb significantly reduces the frequency of edited cells as assessed by FACS. Targeting essential genes can compromise cell viability, further decreasing overall editing efficiency.

Knockin efficiency in human iPSCs remains substantially lower than in mouse embryonic stem cells. For point-mutation knockins, hundreds of Cas9-mCherry-positive cells typically must be isolated and expanded to identify correctly edited clones, which requires substantial hands-on time and effort.

When the E3 ligase is expressed as a fusion with an endogenous gene, its expression level depends on that of the host gene. As a result, the efficiency and kinetics of degron-mediated protein degradation may vary between targets.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Extensive cell death after transfection or nucleofection (Steps 92-123).

Potential solution

High mortality typically results from poor DNA quality, excessive nucleic acid dosage, or inadequate post-transfection survival support. Human iPSCs are uniquely sensitive to impurities and the physical stress of membrane disruption. To mitigate toxicity, ensure all plasmids are prepared using endotoxin-free kits with an A260/A280 ≈ 1.8–2.0 and A260/A230 > 2.0. High concentrations of DNA or transfection reagents often drive apoptosis. Therefore, titrating the total DNA amount by 25%–50% while maintaining component ratios can significantly improve viability. Ensure iPSCs are healthy, undifferentiated, and seeded at 60–70% confluency before transfection. Avoid over-dissociation and include ROCK inhibitor, CloneR2, or RevitaCell during the post-transfection recovery period.

Problem 2

Low frequency of edited or fluorescent cells detected by FACS (Step 146).

Potential solution

Low editing efficiency is typically driven by poor gRNA design, sub-optimal donor template architecture, or inherent biological constraints such as low target gene expression and protein intolerance to terminal tagging. Select gRNAs with high predicted on-target activity and position the Cas9 cut site within ±25 bp of the intended insertion site. Ensure donor templates include sufficiently long homology arms and incorporate PAM- or gRNA-disrupting mutations to prevent re-cutting. Reduce insertion size if feasible. Seed cells at the recommended density (60–70% confluency) and ensure the cells are evenly distributed prior to transfection to optimize DNA uptake and editing efficiency. Prior to FACS, validate editing via microscopy or PCR. However, note that low-abundance proteins may not yield detectable fluorescence at this stage. If the signal remains undetectable, reconsider the tagging terminus (N- vs. C-terminal) to avoid disrupting protein stability or folding. While antibiotic selection can enrich for edited populations, iPSCs are highly sensitive to selection pressure. Thus, fluorescent-based enrichment is preferred. For low-expression genes, ensure imaging equipment is optimized for high signal-to-noise ratios to distinguish true positives from autofluorescent background.

Problem 3

Poor survival or few colonies formation after single-cell sorting (Steps 147-151).

Potential solution

Low clonal recovery is usually caused by physical damage during cell preparation, the high pressure of the sorting machine, or poor growth conditions after sorting. Human iPSCs are large and fragile; additionally, cells that are still stressed from the initial transfection are often unable to survive the added pressure of being dissociated and sorted into single wells.

To improve survival, minimize the time cells spend in dissociation reagents and perform FACS immediately after preparation. Add DNase I to the buffer to prevent clumping and handle the cells gently by pipetting slowly. Avoid using tip-mounted strainers, as these create high pressure that can kill fragile cells. We highly recommend passaging the cells at least once after transfection but before sorting. This “recovery passage” allows the most stressed cells to be cleared out naturally, ensuring you only sort the healthiest, most robust cells. During sorting, use a large nozzle (e.g., 100 μm) to reduce shear stress. While specialized recovery media is expensive, we found that using 100 μL per well in a 96-well plate increases colony formation by 20% compared to using 50 μL. Under these conditions, you should expect to recover 20–30 colonies per 96-well plate. For the best recovery, ensure plates are freshly and evenly coated with Geltrex. Supplement the recovery media with RevitaCell or CloneR2 during and after the sorting process. It is important to continue including these supplements in the media to support the developing colonies.

Problem 4

Incomplete or slow degron-mediated protein depletion (Step 199).

Potential solution

Confirm that the degron tag is correctly integrated. For example, sequencing the junction to rule out frame-shifts that might reduce AID function. Confirm AFB2/E3 ligase expression by immunoblotting or RT-PCR if possible. If AFB2 expression is low, the host gene context might not produce enough ligase; consider alternate strategies such as a stronger promoter or using a different degron system, such as dTAG, if appropriate. Extending the auxin treatment time or increasing the auxin concentration within non-toxic ranges may increase the rate of degradation. Note that degradation kinetics may vary between targets.
