Novel insight into mechanisms of ROS1 catalytic activation via loss of the extracellular domain
Authors: Kristen Jones, Clare Keddy, Chelsea Jenkins, Katelyn Nicholson, Ujwal Shinde, Monika A. Davare
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The ROS1 receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) possesses the largest extracellular amino-terminal domain (ECD) among the human RTK family, yet the mechanisms regulating its activation are not fully understood. While chimeric ROS1 fusion proteins, resulting from chromosomal rearrangements, are well-known oncogenic drivers, their activation mechanisms also remain underexplored. To elucidate the role of the ROS1 ECD in catalytic regulation, we engineered a series of amino-terminal deletion mutants. Our functional studies compared the full-length ROS1 receptor, the CD74-ROS1 oncogenic fusion, and ECD-deleted ROS1 constructs, identifying the ECD regions that inhibit ROS1 tyrosine kinase activity. Notably, we found that deletion of the ROS1 ECD alone significantly increases constitutive catalytic activation and neoplastic transformation in the absence of an amino-terminal fusion partner, challenging the presumed necessity for a dimerization domain in the activation mechanism of kinase fusions in cancer. Our data suggest that inter-genic deletions resulting in the loss of the ECD may be underappreciated oncogenic drivers in cancer. Furthermore, our studies demonstrate that RNASE7 is not a ligand for the ROS1 receptor as previously reported, confirming that ROS1 remains an orphan receptor. Thus, the discovery of a ROS1 ligand remains an important future priority. These findings highlight the potential for disease-associated somatic aberrations or splice variants that modify the ROS1 ECD to promote constitutive receptor activation, warranting further investigation.

Introduction

ROS1, a receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK), is recognized for its role as a proto-oncogene. Chromosomal rearrangements of ROS1 generate constitutively activated ROS1 kinase-fusions that are oncogenic drivers in a diverse subset of human malignancies1–6. These ROS1 fusions are therapeutic targets, and pre-clinical and clinical research led to the development of ROS1 tyrosine kinase inhibitors, including the FDA-approved ROS1 kinase inhibitors crizotinib and entrectinib6–14. While ROS1 fusions as oncogenic drivers are well-characterized, the regulation of ROS1 as a receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) remains largely unexplored.

ROS1 possesses the largest extracellular amino terminal domain (ECD) among proteins in the human RTK family. The ECD is predicted to consist of nine fibronectin type III (FNIII) repeats, interspersed with three ß-propeller domains 15. FNIII domains, comprised of ß-sandwich structures, are involved in binding to various proteins and extracellular matrix components16. ß-propeller domains, approximately 43 residues long, exhibit a characteristic Tyr (Y)-Trp (W)-Thr (T)-Asp (D) sequence. These domains form symmetrical, propeller-like shapes in three dimensions and are found in a wide range of functionally diverse proteins17,18. The organization of the ROS1 ECD domain is relatively unique among the broader RTK family and the insulin receptor family, of which ROS1 is a member. In these families, the immunoglobulin (Ig) domain is more common, followed by the leucine- or cysteine-rich domains and L domains19.

Investigating ROS1 activation and subsequent signaling events has been challenging due to the absence of a confirmed ligand. However, two recent studies proposed RNASE7 and NELL2 as potential ligands for ROS120,21. To date much of what is known about ROS1 signaling has been determined using chimeric ligand-activated ROS1 receptors and ROS1 kinase fusions 9,15,22–32. These studies show that ROS1 autophosphorylation of several tyrosine residues serves as docking sites for various adaptor proteins. Studies examining downstream signaling activation have shown that ligand-activated chimeric ROS1 receptor and constitutively activated ROS1 fusions recruit SH2-domain containing proteins and other canonical adaptor proteins stimulating signaling via RAS-MEK-ERK, PI3K-Akt-mTOR, and JAK-STAT3 pathway 6,22,24–26,28,30–40. These pathways induce cell growth, survival, and proliferation and are involved in oncogenic ROS1-induced tumorigenesis9,15,22–29.

More than 50 diverse amino-terminal fusion partners (5ʹ fusion partner genes) have been identified in ROS1 fusions6, with many lacking dimerization motifs. Traditionally, it has been believed that the acquisition of a new amino-terminal fusion partner containing a dimerization motif, such as the coiled-coil domain, along with the loss of an inhibitory ECD domain, leads to constitutive dimerization and catalytic activation of the kinase fusions (e.g., ETV6-NRK3 or EML4-ALK. However, because the majority of oncogenic ROS1 fusion proteins lack dimerization motifs, we hypothesized that the loss of the ROS1 ECD, which is presumed to impose an inhibitory constraint, may be sufficient to activate ROS1 catalytic activity and drive ROS1-mediated oncogenesis.

To begin unraveling the structure–function relationship of the ROS1 extracellular domain, we generated a series of single domain deletions and larger amino-terminally truncated ROS1 constructs. Using engineered cell models, our objective was to identify critical regions within the ROS1 extracellular domain that govern ROS1 activation, as determined by ROS1 auto-phosphorylation, activation of downstream signaling pathways, and functional transformation assays. In course of these studies, employing ECD deleted construct, we also interrogated the role of RNASE7 and NELL2 as ROS1 ligands as was recently reported20,21,41.

Results

Structural features of the ROS1 extracellular domain

Since structural studies of the ROS1 receptor are lacking, we utilized a homology modeling approach (Fig. S1a), to generate molecular architecture of the ROS1 receptor using existing crystal structures of individual fibronectin type III (FNIII) and WD40 (β-propeller) domains; the composite ROS1 extracellular (ECD) model was built in a modular manner with flanking overlaps. The in-house homology model and AlphaFold42,43 prediction of the ROS1 amino-terminus (Fig. S1b,c) are relatively concordant and offer a preliminary glimpse into the ROS1 ECD domains that may be involved in ligand binding and/or impose catalytic inhibition.

ROS1 ECD deletions reveal key regulatory regions

More than fifty 5ʹ fusion partner genes are known to form oncogenic ROS1 fusion proteins6. Given the promiscuity of these amino-terminal fusion partners, the majority of which lack canonical dimerization domains, we hypothesize that the loss of the extracellular domain alone results in constitutive catalytic activation of ROS1. To determine the extent of catalytic activation in the ECD deletions as compared to known oncogenic ROS1 fusions, we transfected ΔFN1-6, ΔFN1-6, ΔFN1-7, and ΔFN1-8, alongside the CD74::ROS1, EZR::ROS1, and SLC342A2::ROS1 fusions (Fig. 1d). These data show that the ECD truncations exhibit similar auto-phosphorylation and activation of pERK1/2 (MAPK) as the known ROS1 fusion proteins.

We tested whether single FNIII domain deletions could impact catalytic activation and found that single FNIII domain deletions do not significantly or consistently affect ROS1 activation (Fig. S2a–c).

Activated ROS1 recruits SHP2 and self-associates independent of activation state

SHP2, encoded by the PTPN11 gene, is a signaling nexus downstream of RTK signaling46 and regulates many cellular pathways, including RAS/MAPK, JAK/STAT, and PI3K/AKT signaling. SHP2 is a noteworthy effector of activated ROS125. Here, we tested whether ROS1 ECD deletions recruit SHP2 proportional to their activated state. Flag-tagged ROS1 immunoprecipitates showed increased auto-phosphorylation (ROS1 Y2274) and enhanced interaction with SHP2 in ΔFN1-6, ΔFN1-7, and ΔFN1-8 compared to ΔFN1-5, wildtype ROS1, or Empty Vector (Fig. 3a).Fig. 3Interaction of ROS1 ECD deletion constructs with self and effector protein SHP2. We hypothesized that the intramolecular protein–protein interaction of the ROS1 would be enhanced due to structural changes induced by ECD deletion. We refer to this as self-association since we cannot resolve the potential for dimerization or oligomerization with the bulk biochemical methods used. To test this hypothesis, we generated Myc-epitope tagged ROS1 variants, including the CD74::ROS1 fusion. HEK293 T17 cells were transiently transfected with Flag-ROS1 and Myc-ROS1 constructs as indicated in Fig. 3b, or with Myc-ROS1 constructs co-expressed with an empty vector to measure the specificity of immunoprecipitation and the extent of non-specific binding. To our surprise, all co-transfected constructs co-immunoprecipitated to an equivalent extent, including ΔFN1-5 and CD74::ROS1. Unexpectedly, full-length wildtype ROS1 associated with the ECD deletions and with the CD74::ROS1 fusion. This was not due to non-specific binding of Myc-tagged proteins to Flag agarose, as seen in the control lanes where Myc-ROS1 variants were co-expressed with GFP. Protein expression of Myc-ROS1 variants was equivalent, as shown in the input blot.

Interrogation of the RCSB PDB Protein Data Bank revealed a computed structure model of lorlatinib-bound ROS1 (PDB: 4uxl) with a biological assembly suggestive of a ROS1 kinase dimer. Data adapted from this predicted biological assembly are shown in Fig. S4. Each ROS1 monomer was lorlatinib-bound, and the ROS1 kinase dimer was reported to be a homodimer with cyclic-C2 symmetry, composed of two identical subunits related by dual rotational axes. The dimeric interface suggests that the monomers may interact via hydrogen bonds or salt bridges. Given this predictive data, suggestive of a ROS1 kinase domain dimer, we generated ROS1 K1996A and E1993A kinase domain mutants with the hypothesis that the loss of these hydrogen bonding pairs may weaken dimerization.

An alternate hypothesis explored was that ROS1 engages in protein–protein interactions via its carboxy-terminal domain. To test this, we generated a carboxy-terminal truncated ROS1 construct (ROS1 N2224*). In addition to the ER-resident CD74::ROS1 fusion, we also included the cytoplasmic GOPC-ROS1-Short (S) fusion (GOPC Exon 3::ROS1 Exon 35 variant) to explore interactions with the full-length ROS1 receptor. Overexpression of proteins can result in non-specific aggregation in trafficking or quality control compartments. Thus, it is possible that the co-immunoprecipitation observed in Fig. 3b is a result of non-selective or non-specific protein overexpression and aggregation. To test this, the ETV6-NTRK3 kinase fusion was transfected as a specificity control for the observed interactions. All wells were transfected with wildtype ROS1-MYC, and MYC-agarose was used for co-immunoprecipitation of ROS1-MYC with Flag-tagged test constructs as described above. The structural features of these proteins are shown in the illustration (Fig. 3c).

These data (Fig. 3c) show that full-length ROS1 (WT), ROS1 N2224* (carboxy-terminal truncation), the kinase domain mutants K1996A and E1993A, the ECD deletion ΔFN1-8, as well as GOPC-ROS1, all co-immunoprecipitated with full-length ROS1. In contrast, CD74-ROS1 and ETV6-NTRK3, while exhibiting equivalent expression levels to full-length ROS1, ECD deletion variants, and GOPC-ROS1 in the input samples, showed weaker interaction with the full-length ROS1 protein.

To further determine the specificity of these protein–protein interactions, we titrated the co-transfected plasmid amounts for Myc-tagged ΔFN1-8 (range 100–2000 ng) with one microgram of Flag-tagged full-length, wildtype ROS1 in HEK293A cells (Fig. 3d). The lowest detectable limit of ROS1 ΔFN1-8-MYC was with the transfection of 250 nanograms of plasmid DNA. We observed co-immunoprecipitation of ΔFN1-8-MYC with stably expressed ROS1 WT, even at low level of expression.

ROS1 ECD deletion and CD74::ROS1 broadly activate the same effector signaling pathways

We used a commercially available semi-quantitative membrane-based sandwich immunoassay (Proteome Profiler™ Human Phospho-Kinase Kit) to assess the signaling pathways governed by ΔFN1-8 compared to CD74::ROS1. Unprocessed data from one representative experiment is shown in Fig. S5a. ΔFN1-8 and CD74::ROS1 intensities were normalized to an internal reference and then to the WT signal as described in the Methods section. The most robust upregulated targets observed in lysates expressing CD74::ROS1 fusion and the ΔFN1-8 ECD mutant are involved the JNK/Jun and JAK/STAT pathways (Fig. S5b). Broadly, few differences were noted between the ΔFN1-8 fusion and CD74::ROS1. We were concerned about the relative sensitivity of this assay given that known effector pathways that are activated by CD74::ROS1 oncofusion, particularly MAPK (pERK1/2) were not detected. Given these limitations, we tested a subset of known targets, and validated JNK and Jun pathways activation via immunoblotting (Fig. S5c). ΔFN1-8 robustly upregulated the phosphorylation of SHP2, STAT3, and MAPK (ERK1/2), all previously known targets of activated ROS1. Phospho-c-Jun were also elevated in ΔFN1-8 lysates compared to wildtype. GAPDH served as an internal loading control. These data collectively demonstrate that ECD deletion alone is sufficient to activate ROS1 catalytic activity and invoke its oncogenic potential, equivalent to ROS1 fusions.

Subcellular localization and aggregation of ROS1 variants

We conducted immunocytochemistry on HEK293 cells expressing Flag-tagged ROS1 full length, ΔFN1-6, ΔFN1-7, and ΔFN1-8 variants using a Flag monoclonal antibody. HEK parental cells were stained as controls and showed no Flag signal. Our observations showed that as more of the ECD domain was deleted, ROS1 protein formed larger aggregates or puncta, with ΔFN1-8 displaying the largest aggregates. In contrast, the wildtype exhibited smaller puncta or a more diffuse expression pattern (Fig. S6a). Intriguingly low levels of wildtype ROS1 was present at the cell surface, given lack of substantial co-localization of Flag (ROS1) and the cell membrane marker dye, CellBrite™ as shown in Fig. S6b. The ΔFN1-8 ROS1 consistently formed large aggregates or puncta, in contrast to the wildtype (Fig. S6b).

RNASE7 is an unlikely ligand for ROS1

Canonical RTK activation models involve ligand binding-induced receptor dimerization, triggering conformational shifts that permit kinase domain activation. Recently, neural-epidermal growth factor-like 2 (NELL2) and RNASE7 were proposed as ligands for ROS120,21. Kiyozumi et al. demonstrated that recombinant NELL2 and ROS1 interacted in vitro, and NELL2 knockout mice exhibit male-specific infertility, phenocopying ROS1 knockout mice20. Liu et al. reported activation of ROS1 in hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines after exposure to either recombinant RNASE7 or conditioned medium from cells expressing RNASE721. While Kiyozumi et al. did not show catalytic activation of ROS1 after NELL2 exposure, Liu et al. demonstrated robust ROS1 autophosphorylation following RNASE7 treatment.

Given the possibility that ROS1 ECD deletions might mimic ligand-induced structural changes, we investigated these reported ROS1 ligands using an engineered HEK293A ROS1 wildtype cell line. Multiple independent experiments (Fig. S7) failed to replicate Liu et al.’s findings. Treatment with recombinant RNASE7 at concentrations ranging from 250 to 1000 ng as used by Liu et al., and treatment durations of 5 or 10 min, did not activate ROS1 catalytic activity (Fig. S7a). Similarly, recombinant NELL2 did not activate ROS1 autophosphorylation or the effector protein SHP2 (Fig. S7b). The ROS1 kinase domain mutation, D2113N, served as a positive control, robustly activating ROS1 catalytic activity (Fig. S7).

We tested the possibility that endogenous ROS1 might respond differently than ectopically expressed ROS1 in HEK293 cells. To this end, we used the HEP3B and TONG hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines from Liu et al.’s study, obtained from the Hung laboratory, as these cell lines were reported in their publication to harbor easily detectable endogenous ROS1 protein levels. Despite repeated attempts, we could not detect ROS1 protein in these cell lines using the reliable commercially available ROS1 D4D6 antibody (Fig. 4a). To investigate further, we examined ROS1 mRNA levels in these cell lines using data from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE)48 accessed via cbioportal tools52,53. Hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines HUH7, HEPB3B217 (same as HEP3B), and SKHEP1, which Liu et al. reported to express abundant ROS1 protein, had negative ROS1 mRNA Z-scores (Fig. 4b). In contrast, cell lines like LN-428 and SW-1353, which we confirmed to express ROS1 protein, showed positive ROS1 mRNA Z-scores. These transcriptomic data support our negative findings in regard to lack of detectable ROS1 protein in HEP3B and HUH7 hepatocellular cancer cell lines.

We validated transcriptomic data by immunoblotting using two ROS1 antibodies: D4D6 and OTI1A1 (TA805804). Immunoblotting with the D4D6 antibody correlated with ROS1 transcript Z-scores from CCLE, showing abundant ROS1 protein in SAOS2, SW-1353 cell lines with positive ROS1 mRNA Z-score and undetectable levels in Hs578 and BT-549, breast cancer cell lines with negative ROS1 mRNA Z-scores (Fig. 4c). Endogenous ROS1 in SAOS2 and SW-1353 cells exhibited Y2774 phosphorylation under basal conditions, with the detection of phosphorylated ROS1 correlating to the abundance of total protein (Fig. 4d).

The OTI1A1 antibody robustly detected a non-specific band at approximately 190–200 kDa in all cell lines, including those with negative ROS1 mRNA Z-scores, suggesting non-specific protein binding (Fig. S8b) (Fig. 4c). To test this hypothesis, we ectopically expressed full-length ROS1, the ROS1 fusion GOPC::ROS1, and carboxy-terminal truncated ROS1 variants in HEK293T17 cells. The D4D6 ROS1 antibody epitope resides within the last 50 amino acids of the ROS1 carboxy terminus, while the OTI1A1 antibody reportedly binds to the kinase domain (amino acids 1882–2125) (Fig. S8a). Immunoblot analysis comparing these two antibodies unequivocally demonstrates a non-specific band with a molecular mass of approximately 190–200 kDa, detected by the OTI1A1 antibody (Fig. S8b). This non-specific reactivity persists even in cells expressing the empty vector alone that do not exhibit ROS1 expression. The carboxy-terminal truncations of full-length ROS1 or GOPC::ROS1 (E2270* (stop) and N2224* (stop)) are not recognized by the D4D6 ROS1 antibody, but they are detected by the OTI1A1 antibody.

Given the lack of ROS1 protein in the hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines from Liu et al.’s study, we tested effects of recombinant RNASE7 and NELL2 treatments in SW-1353 chondrosarcoma cells, which express high levels of endogenous ROS1 as demonstrated. Neither RNASE7 nor NELL2 activated ROS1 catalytic function, via phospho-ROS1 Y2274 immunoblotting readout, in these cells (Fig. 4e–g).

Collectively, these results support the conclusion that RNASE7 is not a canonical acting ligand for ROS1. We also establish that the previously published data21 were based on hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines that lack ROS1 mRNA or protein expression.

Discussion

Despite the well-established role of ROS1 fusion proteins as druggable oncogenic drivers in cancer, ROS1, this largest type I RTK in the human kinome remains understudied. ROS1 fusions arise from a cluster of chromosomal breakpoints of the ROS1 gene residing adjacent to the exons coding for transmembrane domain, and the diversity of 5ʹ fusion partner genes (amino-terminal fusion partners with ROS1) in cancer is expanding, with over 55 different 5ʹ fusion genes identified to date. Traditionally, constitutive catalytic activation in oncogenic chimeric kinase fusions has been attributed to the combinatorial loss of the RTK extracellular/ligand-binding domain coupled with the acquisition of a constitutive dimerization domain from the new amino-terminal fusion protein49. This mechanism has indeed been observed in recurrent fusions involving the NTRK3 and ALK kinases50–53. Based on the findings presented in this study, we conclude that the loss of the extracellular domain alone is sufficient to induce constitutive kinase activation of ROS1 that is equivalent to that of ROS1 fusions.

Additionally, canonical models of RTK activation involve ligand-induced dimerization that induces structural rearrangements to enable conformational changes in the kinase domain19,54,55 that are permissive to trans-autophosphorylation and substrate phosphorylation54,56. However, newer studies reveal that RTK activation is more nuanced. For instance, studies on PDGFRA, EGFR, and c-KIT demonstrate activation of RTK activity in the absence of ligand-induced dimerization or through alternate mechanisms57,58.

Our findings suggest that ROS1 can self-associate independently of ligand or even the extracellular domain (ECD). This observation raises intriguing questions about the mechanisms of ROS1 activation and self-association or oligomerization, indicating that ROS1 may have inherent structural or biophysical properties enabling its oligomerization in absence of its putative ligand.

Intriguingly, our immunocytochemistry data support the self-association theory, showing that increasing deletion of the ROS1 ECD promoted the formation of large protein aggregates or granules. This phenomenon aligns with the concept of biomolecular condensates, as described by Tulpule et al., who reported that EML4-ALK and CCDC6-RET oncofusions forms membraneless granules necessary for activating the RAS/RAF/MEK pathway59. However, our study had a fundamental difference: the lack of an amino-terminal fusion partner. Tulpule et al. demonstrated that EML4 contributes a trimerization domain, and deletion of this domain disrupts granule formation; similarly, CCDC6 contains a coiled-coil dimerization domain. Importantly, our data suggest that even in the absence of a fusion partner, the ECD-deleted ROS1 can form similar aggregates, condensates, or granules, indicating that ECD deletion alone is sufficient to drive this process. These granules could represent biomolecular condensates that facilitate aberrant signaling60, potentially leading to constitutive activation of ROS1 in a manner similar to that observed for EML4-ALK.

However, these results must be interpreted with caution due to the study’s limitations. The ectopic overexpression of proteins in HEK293 cells could force oligomerization in the endoplasmic reticulum or other protein processing and trafficking compartments due to protein crowding. Previous studies have documented similar instances of macromolecular crowding-induced protein self-association61. Therefore, further research involving biochemical and structural studies on ROS1 self-association is necessary to validate these findings under physiological conditions, preferably involving native ROS1 expressing cells.

Recent studies have proposed NELL2 and RNASE7 as potential ligands for the ROS1 receptor. However, our attempts to replicate these findings were unsuccessful. Kiyozumi et al. demonstrated binding of NELL2 to ROS1, but they did not show an increase in catalytic activity20, leaving it unclear whether NELL2 is an interacting protein, a cooperative ligand, or simply exhibits non-specific binding. Although our findings do not support NELL2's direct role as a functional ligand that activates ROS1 catalytic activity, the involvement of NELL2 and the recently identified putative co-ligand, Nicol41, in ROS1 regulation remains an open question requiring further investigation.

The previously reported finding of RNASE7 as a ligand raises perplexing questions as we were unable to detect ROS1 protein in the cell lines that Liu et al.21 reported to express endogenous ROS1. Independent data from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia repository also supports the absence of ROS1 mRNA expression in these cell lines. Moreover, the ROS1 antibodies used in Liu et al.'s study are either unavailable or discontinued from commercial distribution, making it presently not possible to confirm their findings. In our tests, we compared the D4D6 anti-ROS1 antibody with another ROS1 antibody, OTI1A1, and found that the latter detects an unknown protein of similar molecular weight (~ 190 kDa) as ROS1, with high sensitivity. Therefore, it is possible that the ROS1 protein detected in Liu et al.’s paper, using the now-discontinued ROS1 antibodies, represents a non-specific band migrating at the same molecular weight, leading to data misinterpretation.

To date, there have been no reports of validated ROS1 ECD deletion events in cancer samples. However, Kalla et al. documented the expression of ROS1 splice variants in a subset of lung adenocarcinoma patient samples using quantitative RT-PCR62. These variants were found to lack portions of the ROS1 ECD, suggesting the existence of novel ROS1 variants resulting from alternate transcription initiation start sites and exon skipping during splicing. Whether these ECD-lacking ROS1 proteins are expressed in lung cancer remains uncertain and requires further investigation. There is a precedent for an oncogenic ECD deletion similar to ROS1 ΔFN1-8 in ALK. In ALK, a cryptic alternative transcript initiation (ATI) site located in intron 19, known as ALK ATI, leads to the generation of an oncogenic ALK variant that lacks the majority of its extracellular domain63. Tumorigenic effects of ALK TKI have been demonstrated even without any fusion partners, similar to the engineered ROS1 deletions we showed here. The ALK ATI genomic locus was shown to contain several epigenetic modifier sites that are indicative of transcriptional regulatory regions. Whether similar regulatory sites or ATI sites exist in the ROS1 gene remains to be investigated.

Our findings suggest that ROS1 deletions lacking the majority of the ECD domain can act as oncogenes independently, without the presence of an amino-terminal fusion partner. This implies that the ECD plays a crucial role in maintaining a kinase conformation that favors catalytic inhibition. From a clinical perspective, these types of ROS1 ECD deletions may be present in a small subset of non-small cell lung cancer tumors that currently lack a defined oncogenic driver. However, given their design, many RNA-based clinical diagnostic tests would not detect these ECD deletions, and many DNA-based tests might misinterpret intergenic ROS1 breakpoints as ROS1-negative tumors. Consequently, the subset of patients harboring potential ROS1 ECD deletions who might benefit from treatment with ROS1 TKIs, would not be identified using many standard molecular pathology workups. The data presented herein compel specific interrogation of aberrations in exons coding for the ROS1 amino terminal domain in order to rigorously determine the potential occurrence of TKI-targetable ROS1 ECD deletions in cancer patients.

Our research findings indicate that ROS1 deletions lacking the majority of the ECD domain can act as oncogenes independently, without the presence of an amino-terminal fusion partner. This suggests that the ECD plays a role in maintaining a kinase conformation that favors catalytic inhibition. The identity of the ligand for ROS1 remains elusive, and it is currently unknown whether a mechanism similar to ALK's ATI can generate shorter variants of ROS1 that retain the kinase domain. Further studies focused on elucidating the structure of the ROS1 receptor will contribute to a deeper understanding of this enigmatic protein.
