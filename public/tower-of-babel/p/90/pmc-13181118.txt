ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvent-based surveillance (EBS) is a complementary method to traditional indicator-based surveillance (IBS) as it provides early warnings of emerging health threats. This paper explores Oman’s experience in planning and implementing an EBS system alongside its existing IBS framework. A multisectoral technical working group (TWG) designed and integrated an EBS reporting module on the Oman’s electronic disease reporting system, Tarassud, and developed corresponding training materials. Background

Event-Based Surveillance (EBS) is the organised approach to the detection and reporting of ‘signals,’ defined as information that may represent events of public health importance, often through channels outside of routine surveillance systems [1]. It has demonstrated significant value not only as a routine surveillance tool but also in enhancing existing systems during outbreaks and epidemics. In resource-limited settings, prioritizing EBS in outbreak-prone areas or where traditional surveillance is weak can significantly improve early detection and response. For example, during the Ebola epidemics in Sierra Leone, Guinea (2015), and Uganda (2022), EBS enabled public reporting of signals through call centers, facilitating timely alerts [2]. In Haiti, the Alert and Response system supported cholera response efforts by complementing indicator-based surveillance and identifying unexpected events [2]. Similarly, South Korea’s EBS system was crucial in the early recognition and containment of MERS-CoV [3, 4]. Likewise, pilot implementation of community-based EBS in selected provinces in Vietnam demonstrated its effectiveness as an early warning system, enabling rapid detection and immediate notification of emerging public health threats prior to nationwide scale-up [5].

. In Oman, the IBS framework comprises nationally notifiable disease reporting, laboratory-based surveillance, sentinel surveillance for selected priority conditions, syndromic surveillance, and routine analysis of hospitalization and mortality data. Prior to the introduction of EBS, early warning functions were primarily driven by mandatory notifiable disease reporting and laboratory confirmation systems [6]. Over the last ten years, Oman’s surveillance system has undergone significant advancements, particularly through digitalization. The launch of the electronic reporting platform Tarassud in 2017, integrated with the national hospital information system Shifa, marked a significant milestone [7]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, access to Tarassud was expanded to include governmental, academic, military, and private sectors, ensuring comprehensive disease reporting across the country.

Recognizing the ongoing public health threats and the need to strengthen early warning and response capacities, Oman implemented Event-Based Surveillance (EBS) to complement its existing surveillance systems. In recent years, the country has faced complex epidemiological challenges, including healthcare-associated infections and the emergence of multidrug-resistant pathogens such as Candida auris [8], imported and emerging infections such as mpox [9], and the ongoing need to strengthen tuberculosis elimination efforts through migrant latent TB screening programs [10]. Zoonotic diseases, meanwhile, remain a top priority, including Middle East Respiratory Syndrome (MERS-CoV), Brucellosis, and Crimean-Congo Haemorrhagic Fever (CCHF) [11]. Additionally, Oman has reported a human infection with avian influenza A (H9N2) virus [12]. Sustaining elimination gains for vaccine-preventable diseases, including measles, has also required vigilant surveillance to rapidly detect immunity gaps and outbreak clusters [13]. In parallel, vector-borne diseases such as dengue have re-emerged in the region, with predictive modelling highlighting increasing transmission risk under ecological and climatic shifts [14]. These interconnected threats underscored the need for a more agile, multisectoral, and real-time surveillance mechanism capable of detecting atypical signals beyond routine indicator-based reporting. The EBS initiative was motivated by successful international models and aligned with national efforts to operationalize the One Health approach. Oman’s EBS framework aims to bridge surveillance gaps, improve responsiveness, and enhance epidemic intelligence through real-time detection of public health threats.

This paper presents an overview of the planning and implementation stages that led to the practical application of EBS in Oman, outlining the key challenges, opportunities, and lessons learned during its first year of implementation.

Preparatory Discussions and Conceptualization

In September 2021, Oman’s Ministry of Health, in collaboration with the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (US CDC), initiated discussions to plan and implement EBS in Oman. The proposed model was to collect signals from both health facilities and community-based sources, including animal and environmental health signals. The system emphasized decentralization across all 11 governorates with central level oversight. Designed to be fully electronic, the implementation was delayed by the COVID-19 pandemic but resumed in early 2022.

Needs Assessment

A formal needs assessment was conducted in October 2022 by a joint team from the MoH’s Disease Surveillance Department and the US CDC. The assessment aimed to evaluate institutional readiness, stakeholder alignment, and technical feasibility of the EBS framework. Consultative meetings were held with senior public health officials to capture strategic insights, assess alignment with national surveillance priorities, and identify integration pathways within the existing organizational structure.

Field visits were undertaken to two governorates Dakhliyah and North Sharqiyah which were identified as potential pilot sites, due to their proximity, representativeness and community health experience. During these visits, semi-structured interviews were conducted with personnel from departments of disease surveillance and control, primary health care departments as well as from governmental and private healthcare facilities. The interviews were guided by broad, open-ended questions focusing on assessing the feasibility, relevance, and operational practicality of EBS in both facility and community settings. Additionally, the team engaged with the Directorate General of Agriculture and Animal Wealth to discuss opportunities for intersectoral collaboration and to assess the integration of animal health signals that may affect human health into the EBS platform under the One Health framework.

The assessment also evaluated the potential use of the MOH’s existing call center as a channel for capturing and managing community-based health signals [15]. Overall, the assessment’s findings confirmed both the strategic need for and the institutional readiness to operationalize EBS in Oman, paving the way for phased national implementation.

Technical Working Group Committee

Following the national needs assessment, a Technical Working Group (TWG) was established, and comprised of 10 multidisciplinary experts in epidemiology, public health, microbiology, toxicology, environmental health, and disease surveillance mainly from Ministry of Health, along with two public health experts from US CDC. The primary responsibilities of the TWG included the development of national technical guidelines for EBS, defining standard operating procedures (SOPs) for signal detection, triage, verification, risk assessment, and response coordination. The group also spearheaded the creation of training curricula, supervision tools, and workshop materials. Initially, the TWG convened on a weekly basis to expedite progress, with meetings later shifting to a biweekly schedule as technical documents neared completion. Based on the readiness of the national framework, it was agreed that a pilot phase would be launched in December 2023.

Stakeholder Engagement

Given the inherently multisectoral nature of EBS and its alignment with the One Health approach, stakeholder engagement was identified as a critical component for the successful implementation of the system. The TWG adopted a phased strategy to gradually onboard key sectors and ensure cohesive collaboration, data sharing, and signal validation across disciplines. Signals and EBS Process

The development of EBS signals in Oman was guided by epidemiological priorities, national diseases burden, and practical feasibility of signal’s detection and response. TWG members drafted the initial signals list based on their respective areas of expertise, the country’s communicable diseases profile, and known public health threats. Drafted signals were reviewed iteratively during TWG meetings and subsequently refined following feedback from relevant departments and intersectoral stakeholders.

Signals were defined as any data or information suggestive of an unusual or unexpected event that could pose a threat to public health. A final consensus list of signals (Supplementary Table 1), categorized by sector and source, was compiled and endorsed by the TWG. Examples include clusters of febrile illnesses in schools, unexpected die-offs among livestock, environmental exposure to a chemical, radiological or biological threat, or laboratory reports of emerging or re-emerging pathogens.

To facilitate structured signal processing, Oman’s EBS system utilizes a standardized five-step protocol to guide the transition from detection to response. Signals can be reported by all stakeholders, both MoH and non-MoH. The remaining steps are primarily carried out by regional disease surveillance and control departments, while the national level monitors the entire process, to maintain oversight and provide support as needed. EBS Electronic Module Development

Tarassud, Oman’s electronic public health surveillance system, was first launched by the Ministry of Health (MoH) in 2017 as a digital platform for communicable disease notification [7]. Since its inception, Tarassud has been progressively enhanced to meet evolving public health priorities.

Recognizing the utility of building upon an established infrastructure, the MoH opted to integrate the EBS module into the existing Tarassud system rather than developing independent platform. Development began in mid-2023 and was finalized within six months. Tarrasud homepage, a sample description of EBS form and EBS dashboard is illustrated in Fig. 3.

The TWG advised on the design of the modules’ structure and content, including the design of standardized signal reporting forms that reflect the full five-steps protocol. Stakeholders’ engagement ensured accurate definition of users and their titles, alongside submission of role-specific access requests for system login. The module’s interface was purposefully designed to ensure simplicity and ease of use, incorporating dropdown menus and pre-set selection fields to standardize reporting formats and reduce both data entry time and errors.

Signals were categorized by source such as community, laboratories, animal health, and environmental, enabling users to select from a tailored list during the notification process. Upon submission, the system automatically alerts the relevant governorate-level Disease Surveillance and Control team, based on the geographic location provided. Role-based access permissions support secure system uses and allow for the phased expansion of users and institutions over time.

Importantly, the EBS module functions as a centralized and secure repository for all signal records, enabling retrospective analysis, operational auditing, and performance monitoring In addition, the platform serves as a comprehensive EBS management system, where all signal-related data - from initial detection and reporting through verification, risk assessment, response, and control measures - are systematically recorded, tracked, and archived to ensure full traceability and institutional memory. The platform is accessible via a secure web-based interface, facilitating collaboration between health and non-health sectors in alignment with the One Health framework.

Each end user across all stakeholder groups was provided with confidential credentials to log into the system and report signals. Account privileges were restricted according to functional roles. For example, end users were only permitted to report signals, whereas regional epidemiologists were granted additional privileges for triaging, verification, and related activities.

Training Materials

As part of the EBS implementation process, the TWG developed a comprehensive bilingual training package – available in Arabic and English - to strengthen capacity across all levels of Oman’s health system. Drawing from global technical experience, scientific literature, and resources provided by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the curriculum consisted of five core modules. The first module introduced the foundational concept of EBS, emphasizing its complementary relationship with IBS and its collective contribution to epidemic intelligence and early warning systems. The second module outlined the structured five-step EBS workflow: reporting, triage, verification, risk assessment, and response. In the third, participants explored signal identification and classification tailored to Oman’s national epidemiological profile and priorities. The fourth module provided practical guidance on navigating the EBS electronic interface within the Tarassud platform. Finally, the fifth module featured scenario-based exercises using locally contextualized examples, designed to deepen participants’ understanding and promote effective application of EBS processes in real-world settings.

The main training was primarily designed using a training-of-trainers (ToT) approach and was conducted in Muscat. The initial training included around 50 technical leaders from regional disease surveillance and control units, primary health care, and headquarters-level technical staff from non-health sectors and was followed by additional refresher training. Subsequently, disease surveillance and control staff conducted cascade trainings in their respective regions for end users across both health and non-health sectors (Supplementary Table 2). In addition, members of selected technical committees supported the training of their colleagues within their respective departments, mainly central public health laboratories, surveillance units, and related services.

To complement the in-person training, a preparatory e-learning program was developed in collaboration with the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF) [16]. Tailored to Oman’s public health context, the e-module served as a foundational learning tool for EBS. The link was shared with participants via email prior to the in-person workshop. This approach facilitated baseline knowledge alignment, allowing candidates to engage more during the hands-on training sessions.

Monitoring and Evaluation Tools

To ensure the effectiveness, sustainability, and quality assurance of the EBS system, the TWG developed a comprehensive suite of monitoring and evaluation (M&E) tools. These tools assess key performance indicators such as signal detection volume, triage timeliness, verification accuracy, response initiation intervals, and intersectoral coordination. The toolkit includes standardized supervisory checklists, pre- and post-training assessments, stakeholder feedback forms, and dynamic performance dashboards, all integrated within the Tarassud platform to enable real-time monitoring across national and subnational levels. Data collected through these tools is periodically analyzed to identify operational gaps and inform targeted improvements to enhance the overall functioning of the EBS system.

The Pilot Phase

Oman, with a population of approximately 4.47 million, is administratively divided into 11 governorates. For the pilot phase of EBS, the Ministry of Health selected Dakhliyah and North Sharqiyah governorates, for their proximity to Muscat, the capital and hub for national-level technical support, and for their established track record in community-based public health intervention. With populations of 478,501 and 271,822 respectively, and ranking 3rd and 7th nationally, these regions offered practical and logistical representativeness [17].

Their departments of Disease Surveillance and Control,, spearhead initial activities through stakeholder meetings involving hospitals, primary healthcare institutions, and public health partners. Specialized sessions targeted staff from the MAFWR, the Food Safety and Quality Centre, and the Environment Authority. EBS signal reporting began alongside training, scaled progressively as staff competencies improved, was bolstered by periodic field visits conducted by the TWG to address challenges, reinforce guidance, and foster continues learning by providing refresher training as needed.

The six-month pilot, launched in December 2023, culminated in a formal evaluation of the entire experience. Initial activities began with introductory meetings led by the governorates’ Departments of Disease Surveillance and Control, targeting regional hospitals, primary healthcare institutions, and key public health stakeholders. During these sessions, focal points were designated for each participating institution. The evaluation phase ensured the practicability of the experience, the smooth flow of information, and productivity based on the reported signals. Additionally, the key lessons were documented to inform national roll-out and strengthen EBS integration into Oman’s public health surveillance system.

Signals Reported Through 2024

During the pilot implementation, a total of 35 signals were reported through the EBS platform. Of the total signals received, 18 were considered effective and impactful in terms of public health significance and surveillance performance. The effectiveness of each signal was evaluated based on whether it was reported and acted upon prior to detection through the routine IBS system, as well as the epidemic potential or public health threat posed by the event or pathogen.

The validated signals included the early detection of several notifiable and high-priority pathogens and diseases, namely: Legionella spp., Parvovirus B19, Shigella dysenteriae, Shigella flexneri, hand-foot-and-mouth disease (HFMD), rabies, brucellosis, and Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever (CCHF). These events are summarized in Table 1. Promptly identifying these conditions, several of which pose moderate to high transmission risks or zoonotic potential, facilitated timely public health investigations and responses, thereby decreasing the likelihood of outbreak escalation.

Importantly, the system facilitated the first instances of formalized, real-time notification of zoonotic disease events from the animal health sector. These notifications triggered prompt multisectoral responses, reflecting improved alignment with the One Health approach and enhanced cross-sector collaboration.

Conversely, 11 signals were assessed as ineffective. Among these, nine signals of predominantly foodborne illness events were already detected and investigated under the IBS system. The remaining two signals pertained to low-priority conditions that fell outside the defined EBS scope. These findings informed subsequent revisions of the signal list and triaging protocols, aimed at refining signal specificity and reducing redundancy. In addition, six invalid reports were identified as duplicate signals resulting from simultaneous notifications from multiple sources; system modifications are underway to improve automated de-duplication and reporting consistency.

Discussion

The COVID-19 pandemic, along with other recent outbreaks and global threats, has exposed critical weaknesses in public health surveillance systems, highlighting the urgent need for rapid, integrated, and adaptable mechanisms [18]. Traditional IBS, while valuable for tracking predefined diseases, often lacks the flexibility to detect emerging or unexpected threats in real time [19]. In contrast, EBS has gained global prominence as a vital strategy for early warning and detection, leveraging informal sources such as community reports, animal health data, media monitoring, and environmental observations. EBS is therefore essential to advancing the One Health approach, facilitating intersectoral collaboration and enabling early identification of zoonotic diseases and environmental health threats [1, 19]. Its adaptability makes it particularly effective in areas where IBS faces challenges related to limited resources, infrastructure, or accessibility.

Oman’s experience with EBS implementation highlights both the opportunities and complexities of integrating event-driven systems into existing surveillance architecture within a high-income country setting. Unlike some lower-income contexts, where EBS often evolved primarily through community-based reporting or media scanning, Oman’s model emphasized institutional capacity, electronic integration, and formal cross-sectoral coordination from the outset.

Pilot testing has played a critical role in successful implementation of EBS systems [2, 3]. The phased, strategic approach adopted in Oman, beginning with two representative governorates and followed by structured monitoring and feedback, proved effective in optimizing system performance. This iterative process, ultimately culminated in the deployment of a robust and efficient national EBS framework.

As Oman advances toward national scale-up, sustained investment in workforce development, digital alert mechanisms, and intersectoral governance will be critical to maximizing the impact of EBS. These efforts not only strengthen national epidemic intelligence capabilities but also contribute meaningfully to the global public health surveillance architecture.

Challenges Encountered

While Oman’s implementation of EBS demonstrated several early successes, it also revealed operational and structural challenges that warrant attention prior to nationwide expansion. A primary concern was the integration of EBS data streams with the existing IBS framework. Achieving full interoperability between the two systems requires sophisticated data architecture and robust information technology infrastructure. Although the development of the EBS electronic module within the Tarassud platform significantly improved real-time reporting, further enhancements are needed to support automated data synthesis, cross-referencing, and decision support functionalities. One commonly reported gap by field staff was the absence of a real-time alert mechanism, whether through in-platform notifications, email, or SMS, to flag new signals or confirm escalated events.

Sustaining cross-sectoral engagement emerged as another key challenge. While initial collaboration with non-health sectors such as environmental agencies, food safety authorities, and the veterinary department was strong, long-term engagement proved more difficult in the absence of formal governance frameworks, shared protocols, and institutional accountability. The lack of dedicated financial and human resources further constrained efforts to scale up training programs, implement advanced analytics, and address staff turnover, particularly at the subnational level.

In addition, public awareness and community involvement in EBS signal reporting remain limited. The effectiveness of EBS relies heavily on diverse and inclusive sources of signal detection, including community observations. Strengthening community-level engagement will require targeted awareness campaigns and investment in accessible reporting mechanisms, such as mobile applications and enhanced call center services [20].

Enabling Factors

Despite these challenges, several enabling factors contributed to the successful execution of the pilot and laid the foundation for national scale-up. Oman’s longstanding experience with IBS has provided a solid epidemiological and operational foundation on which EBS could be built. The presence of a functional digital health infrastructure, particularly the Tarassud platform, facilitated the efficient integration of EBS functionalities and reduced the technological learning curve among surveillance staff. The availability of a skilled workforce, including trained epidemiologists and public health practitioners, further supported implementation.

Multisectoral collaboration, though in need of further formalization, benefited from existing cross-cutting engagements between the health sector and other ministries. Joint initiatives in animal health, environmental monitoring, and food safety offered practical entry point for EBS integration. Crucially, high-level administrative support from both central ministries and governorate-level leadership helped foster political commitment, operational continuity, and staff motivation. Within the pilot governorates, program promotion through hospitals, public institutions, and community platforms was actively pursued, supported by regular sensitization efforts and refresher training sessions.

Way Forward

The successful implementation of the EBS in Oman marks a significant milestone in strengthening the country’s epidemic intelligence framework. This initiative has demonstrated the feasibility, utility, and added value of integrating non-traditional surveillance streams into our existing public health architecture, reinforcing our national capacity to detect and respond to emerging threats in real time.

As we move into the next phase of EBS toward full national scale-up, the Ministry of Health remains committed to a model of continuous evaluation, guided by process, outcome and impact indicators. This approach will ensure that implementation fidelity is maintained, feedback from end users is systematically captured, and course corrections are made based on measurable performance metrics. Evaluation will not be limited to outcomes but will encompass system inputs, operational dynamics, and the responsiveness of intersectoral coordination.

In parallel, Oman is actively exploring the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and advanced analytics into EBS and the broader national surveillance ecosystem. AI offers the potential to enhance signal classification, detect anomalies in signals, prioritize risks, and support predictive modelling, thereby enabling smarter and faster decision-making. Such innovation is aligned with Oman’s digital health strategy and vision for a future-ready public health system.

The next steps will also involve streamlining response mechanisms, ensuring effective coordination across departments within the centre for disease prevention and control and with multisectoral partners. This includes clarifying response protocols, establishing real-time feedback loops, and enhancing operational readiness at national and subnational levels.

Conclusion

The implementation of EBS in Oman has proven to be a critical step in modernizing the country’s public health surveillance infrastructure. By integrating EBS with the existing IBS system, Oman has established a more dynamic and responsive mechanism for early detection and risk assessment of public health threats. The foundation laid by the pilot phase, combined with the lessons learned and the collective commitment of our partners, positions Oman well for advancing toward a more agile, integrated, and intelligent surveillance system.
