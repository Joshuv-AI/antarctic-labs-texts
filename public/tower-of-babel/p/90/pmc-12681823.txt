Convergent systemic therapy for liver metastases: Bridging primary tumor heterogeneity through shared pathways
Authors: Wenjing Wang, Caiyun Peng, Hongli Yu, Jiamin Cheng, Zhipeng Liang, Yinying Lu, Zhixian Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Liver metastases represent a major clinical challenge in oncology, often indicating advanced disease with complex management requirements. Despite originating from diverse primary tumors such as colorectal, pancreatic, and breast cancers, liver metastases exhibit shared biological behaviors influenced by the unique tumor microenvironment and immunosuppressive conditions of the liver. This review synthesizes advances in systemic, locoregional, and surgical therapies, demonstrating how shared mechanisms enable unified strategies such as pathway-targeted agents and immunotherapy–radiotherapy combinations, even amid primary tumor heterogeneity. Multidisciplinary team collaboration is critical for optimizing individualized treatment plans, balancing locoregional and systemic treatments to convert unresectable cases and improve the survival rate. We propose a paradigm of precision convergence “harmonizing tumor therapies with lineage-specific adjustments” guided by the principle of “commonalities in biology, divergences in context”.

Introduction

The liver is one of the most frequent sites for metastasis from malignant tumors, and the presence of liver metastases (LM) often signifies a poor prognosis. The most common primary tumor that metastasizes to the liver is colorectal cancer (CRC), followed by pancreatic cancer, breast cancer, melanoma, and lung cancer.1, 2, 3 The heterogeneous nature of liver metastatic carcinoma (LMC) has long impeded progress in clinical treatment. Currently, common clinical approaches for liver metastasis encompass a variety of modalities, including surgical treatment, locoregional therapy and systemic treatment.4 Recent advancements in surgical techniques, systematic therapy, and the development of multidisciplinary team (MDT)-based diagnostic and treatment strategies, have significantly improved both the survival rate and quality of life for patients with LMC. The combination of chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy has laid the foundation for comprehensive treatment of LMC, and the value of surgical treatment has been demonstrated in selected patients with oligometastatic disease.5 Furthermore, the evolution of locoregional therapy has introduced new treatment options for patients with LMC. In addition, the integration of real-time imaging guidance and minimally invasive robotic platforms has further refined the precision of hepatic interventions, thereby reducing peri-operative morbidity and expanding eligibility to patients who were previously deemed unsuitable for surgery. These technological refinements also enable more accurate assessment of tumor margins, which is critical for achieving complete resections while preserving functional liver tissue.

The highly heterogeneous nature of primary tumors in patients with LMC presents challenges, including unclear clinical therapeutic objectives and inconsistent treatment strategies. However, multiple studies have demonstrated that the tumor microenvironment6 and specific immune conditions of the liver,7 can result in LM from different primary tumors sharing similar biological characteristics and therapeutic responses. This similarity allows these tumors to be treated with similar therapeutic strategies.8,9 Patient survival and quality of life can be significantly improved with individualized precision medicine and input from physicians of multiple specialties.

The liver's unique immunotolerant microenvironment, rich in Kupffer cells, hepatic stellate cells, and immunosuppressive cytokines, creates a fertile soil for metastatic seeding from various primary tumors.10 Common mechanisms include angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, immune evasion, and metabolic reprogramming. These shared pathways—such as VEGF, EGFR, JAK-STAT, and DDR signaling—enable convergent therapeutic strategies despite primary tumor heterogeneity. Recognizing these convergent pathways has motivated the design of clinical trials that enroll patients based on molecular alterations rather than tissue of origin.

In this review, we discuss surgical, local, and systemic treatments for LMC, and focus on exploring the potential for systemic therapies.

Treatment of metastatic tumors in the liver

In clinical practice, treatment decisions for patients with LMC are discussed in MDT meetings, and the specific regimen is usually selected based on tumor origin, which dictates molecular targets and treatment sensitivity; tumor burden assessed via imaging to quantify lesion number, size, and distribution; and the patient's performance status evaluated using the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group scale.4

Surgical treatment and ablation

For LMC, particularly in patients with oligometastatic disease, surgery remains the optimal curative treatment, including hepatectomy and liver transplantation.11,12 Liver transplantation is restricted to select patients with unresectable LM, no extrahepatic disease, and preserved liver function. Portal vein embolization is a safe and effective procedure for redirecting blood flow toward the portion of the liver that will remain after surgery to increase future liver remnant volume prior to major hepatectomy. Recent advances include the use of mesenchymal stem cells to augment liver regeneration. These cells, typically isolated from bone marrow or adipose tissue, secrete hepatocyte growth factor and VEGF, which stimulate hepatocyte proliferation and angiogenesis, thereby accelerating liver recovery after embolization or resection. This approach represents a novel strategy to improve surgical outcomes.13 Moreover, pre-operative functional imaging is increasingly employed to predict postoperative liver function, helping to select candidates who will tolerate extensive resections.

Ablation techniques, such as radiofrequency ablation,14 microwave ablation,15 cryoablation, and irreversible electroporation serve as alternatives to resection for curative or palliative intent. These techniques can preserve the liver parenchyma critical for patients with underlying liver disease or limited liver reserve. Several studies have demonstrated that ablation in patients with LMC yields promising local control in patients with oligometastatic disease.16,17 The safety and efficacy of irreversible electroporation have been established in preclinical studies; however, no randomized controlled clinical trial has yet been conducted.18 Additionally, several cohort studies have shown that combining radiofrequency ablation with systemic chemotherapy or hepatectomy in patients with LMC results in improved overall and progression-free survival.19,20,21 Emerging image-guided technologies, such as fusion-guided microwave ablation and intraoperative ultrasound navigation, have further increased the precision of ablative therapies, both reducing the risk of residual disease and enabling treatment of lesions located near major vascular structures. Moreover, the adoption of pre-procedural contrast-enhanced computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging allows real-time assessment of ablation margins, ensuring complete tumor necrosis while sparing adjacent healthy tissue. These advances also facilitate percutaneous approaches in patients who are poor surgical candidates, expanding the therapeutic accesses.

Other locoregional therapies

Non-surgical locoregional therapies, including transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), transarterial embolization, transarterial radioembolization, hepatic artery infusion (HAI), and radiotherapy, offer the advantages of being minimally invasive and effectively controlling local lesions.22 Emerging techniques such as radiosensitizers are under preclinical development to enhance the efficacy of radiotherapy.23 For patients with a poor response to first-line chemotherapy, local treatment, such as HAI and TACE, are recommended in several guidelines as alternative options to second-line or third line treatment.24, 25, 26 Clinical trials demonstrated that, for patients with colorectal cancer liver metastases (CRLM),27, 28, 29 gastric cancer (GC) liver metastases,30 pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasia liver metastases,31 breast cancer liver metastases,32 and neuroendocrine tumor liver metastases, the incorporation of TACE extends first-line progression-free survival in cases of disease progression despite systemic therapy.16,33 A retrospective study revealed that combining HAI with first-line systemic therapy serves as an effective translational therapy for chemotherapy-naïve patients, achieving a conversion surgery rate of up to 57% for LM.34 In addition, selective internal radiation therapy (SIRT) using Yttrium-90 resin microspheres has shown promising disease control rates in patients with refractory liver metastases, particularly when combined with systemic immunotherapy, suggesting a synergistic effect that warrants further investigation. The dosimetric precision of SIRT can now be individualized using voxel-based planning, which improves tumor to normal tissue ratios and may further enhance outcomes.

Systemic therapy

Most patients with LMC are in the advanced stage of cancer, and systemic therapy has become the mainstay of prolonging patient survival. In addition, effective systemic therapy is an integral part of the comprehensive treatment strategies for LMC that seeks cure. Meanwhile, the application of systemic therapy in the perioperative period can reduce recurrence and thus enhance the survival rate of patients.

Chemotherapy

For the vast majority of LMC, combination chemotherapy regimens remain the cornerstone of systemic therapy. The combination chemotherapy regimens are used not only for palliative treatment of unresectable LMC, alleviating symptoms such as abdominal pain and jaundice, while prolonging survival, but also for neoadjuvant therapy of initially resectable LM, conversion therapy for potentially resectable LM, bridging therapy prior to liver transplantation, and postoperative adjuvant therapy of high-risk recurrent LM.35 A randomized controlled trial showed that mFOLFOX6 adjuvant chemotherapy can improve disease-free survival in patients undergoing liver resection for CRLM.36 While mFOLFOX6 has become a standard adjuvant regimen for CRLM, the choice of chemotherapy for LM from other primary tumors remains tailored to the biological behavior of the original cancer, because different malignancies exhibit distinct sensitivities to cytotoxic agents. In the treatment of patients with LM from gastric cancer, the most common chemotherapeutic regimens include platinum (oxaliplatin or cisplatin), fluorouracil (5-fluorouracil, S-1, or capecitabine), and paclitaxel (docetaxel or albumin-bound paclitaxel) agents.37 In a retrospective study, first-line treatment with floxuridine via HAI pump in combination with standard systemic chemotherapy resulted in higher intrahepatic responses and better overall survival in patients with pancreatic cancer LM.38

With the development of targeted immunotherapy, the combination of chemotherapy with targeted and/or immunotherapy has become a new trend. In a multicenter study, FOLFOXIRI bevacizumab was the preferred treatment for CLRM patients with a right-sided or RAS/BRAF-mutated tumor who were initially unresectable.39 A randomized controlled trial showed an improvement in R0/R1 hepatic resection rate from 48.4% to 56.9% in patients with initially unresectable LM from CRC using 3-CTx, a intensive chemotherapy regimen in combination with bevacizumab or cetuximab, based on RAS status.40 Recent phase II trials have explored the addition of oxaliplatin-based triplet regimens together with immune checkpoint blockade, reporting both higher objective response rates and increased conversion to resectability, especially in patients with microsatellite-stable CRC liver metastases. Notably, dose-dense scheduling of FOLFOXIRI has been shown to reduce treatment-related neutropenia while maintaining efficacy, offering a more tolerable option for elderly patients with limited hepatic reserve. Pharmacogenomic profiling is increasingly used to tailor dose intensity, minimizing toxicity while preserving antitumor activity.

Different primary tumors that have metastasized to the liver may have similar gene mutations or aberrantly expressed signaling pathways.44,45 This shared molecular landscape is a key driver of the convergent therapy hypothesis, as it suggests that targeting common pathways could streamline treatment decisions and improve outcomes for patients with diverse LMC subtypes. Mph_SPP1, a pro-angiogenetic macrophage subset derived from resident tissue, may interact with endothelial cells to promote pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma metastasis to the liver.46 Mph_SPP1 secretes SPP1, a protein that binds to integrin receptors on endothelial cells, enhancing angiogenesis and vascular permeability; this creates a “pro-metastatic” vascular environment that facilitates the colonization of pancreatic cancer cells in the liver. Doheny et al. showed that combined inhibition of the JAK2-STAT3 and SMO-GLI1/tGLI1 pathways suppressed breast cancer stem cells, tumor growth and metastasis.47 Romayor et al. showed that silencing DDR1 significantly reduced tumor migration and metastatic growth in the liver, especially in patients with CRLM.48 For patients with the same genetic mutations, the same targeted therapy can be used for treatment, realizing similar treatment strategies guided by common pathways despite primary tumor heterogeneity.

Immunotherapy

In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway have made breakthroughs in the field of immunotherapy.49, 50, 51, 52, 53 However, several studies have shown that the efficacy of immunotherapy is generally poor for patients with LM,54 which may be attributed to the unique immunosuppressive microenvironment of the liver,3 where aggregation of immunosuppressive cells and depletion of effector T-lymphocytes55,56 are involved in shaping the inhibitory immune tumor microenvironment and resisting immunotherapy.57 Despite this, combination strategies such as immunotherapy with radiotherapy or targeted agents have shown promise in overcoming resistance. A clinical trial demonstrated that VB-111, a novel antiangiogenic therapy with immunomodulating effects, combined with nabulizumab demonstrated CD8 T cell activation and proliferation after treatment in patients with microsatellite-stabilized CRLM.58 Marie et al. demonstrated that anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy can activate the immune response of T and B cells in metastatic tumor therapy.59 However, LM can lead to acquired immunotherapy resistance through peripheral tolerance mechanisms mediated by CD8+ T cell deficiency.60 Acquired resistance occurs when CD8+ T cells become “exhausted” after prolonged exposure to tumor antigens, losing their cytotoxic function and expressing additional checkpoint molecules that further suppress their activity. At this time, the stereotactic radiation therapy can not only overcome resistance, but also enhance systemic anti-tumor immunity. CART cell therapy works by genetically modifying the patient's own T cells to enable them to specifically recognize and attack tumor cells. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses have shown that different metastatic entities from different primary sites differentially shape the tumor microenvironment of LMC, inducing specific changes in Kupffer cells (KCs) and natural killer cells.46 Tumor expression levels of KC characteristic genes, particularly TIMD4 and CLEC1B, are positively correlated with survival in hepatocellular carcinoma patients.62 An animal experiment found that specific depletion of KCs using Clec4f-iDTR mice before or after the development of LM indicated that KC dysfunction was a key determinant of LMC progression.63 This animal study highlights the dual role of KCs. While they can phagocytose tumor cells in early stages, they become dysfunctional in established metastases, secreting pro-metastatic cytokines that promote tumor growth. Consequently, targeting KC function could be a novel strategy to prevent LMC progression. As CART cells have strong anti-tumor activity and durability, they can break through the inhibition of the tumor immune microenvironment, and have potential therapeutic effects on LMC of different origins, which can achieve the effect of “similar treatment strategies guided by common pathways despite primary tumor heterogeneity”.

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) for LMC treatment

The gut microbiota, through the “gut–liver axis”, is able to deliver bacteria and their metabolites to the liver via the hepatic portal system, thus regulating the progression of LMC.64 Intratumoral E. coli C17 disassembles the gut vascular barrier (GVB) associated with increased PV-1, allowing gut microbes to translocate to the liver. Disseminated microbes colonize the liver and establish a “pre-metastatic niche” (PMN) microenvironment characterized by increased expression of chemokines and deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM), which ultimately promotes liver metastasis.65 Similarly, Desulfovibrio (DSV) in the intestine induces barrier dysfunction as well as inflammation in the colorectum and liver, suggesting that it contributes to the formation of a microenvironment conducive to tumorigenesis and metastasis, hence promoting liver metastasis.66 Fusobacterium nucleatum infection also increases the rate of colorectal carcinoma cell transfer to the liver.67

Beyond gut microbiota themselves, microbial toxins and cancer-associated cytokines mediated by microbiota-related pathways also contribute to LM. Campylobacter jejuni-derived cytolethal distending toxin (CDT) in primary colorectal cancer promotes liver metastasis through JAK2-STAT3-MMP9 signaling.68 Cancer cells can also release exosomes enriched with miR-106a-5p that promote macrophage M2 polarization and consequently liver metastasis through inhibition of SOCS6 and activation of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway.69 Complex biological systems play an important role in promoting the development of LM (Fig. 1). In a previous study, Kaplan et al. reported that tumor cells generate a “pre-metastatic niche”70 that facilitates metastasis before they reach the metastatic site. In practical studies, researchers have found that it is possible to influence the development of LMC or alter the efficacy of immunotherapy by interfering with the community structure, taxonomic composition, and molecular function of microbiota.71 Wang et al. reported an increase in efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors with fecal microbiota transplantation.67 By altering the gut microecology, the microbiome can contribute to immunotherapy in the treatment of LM from different primary tumors, which means that the microbiome and immunotherapy have a synergistic effect. Recent pilot studies have combined FMT with anti-PD-1 therapy in patients with refractory colorectal liver metastases, observing both enhanced T cell infiltration in hepatic lesions and a modest improvement in progression-free survival, suggesting a viable translational pathway for larger randomized trials. Ongoing trials are now evaluating oral microbiota-derived metabolites as adjuvants to standard systemic regimens, to standardize the microbiome modulation across tumor types.

Conclusion

The presence of LM carries a conservative prognosis despite a therapeutic approach combining locoregional, systemic and surgical treatments, and therefore clinical and scientific research is urgently needed to help develop preventive and therapeutic management. LMC, with its complex biology and high degree of heterogeneity, is still far from a truly standardized clinical practice. However, we found that LM from different primary tumors may have similar biological characteristics and therapeutic responses, and that the tumor microenvironment and special immune conditions of the liver allow for similar therapeutic strategies. In the management of patients with LM, it is important to preserve biological commonalities while accounting for diverse primary tumor backgrounds, and to determine the primary tumor's gene mutations or abnormally expressed signaling pathways by means of gene sequencing and other means, which enables formulation of the appropriate therapeutic strategy.44,45

The MDT model is essential when treating patients with LM from various primary tumors. Through collaboration among surgeons, radiologists, oncologists, pathologists, and nurse specialists, MDT meetings enable personalized and efficient treatment planning.4 Hepatic resection remains the most curative option. For initially unresectable cases, combined systemic and locoregional therapies can downstage tumors to enable resection. Treatment must also be tailored according to the primary tumor type and individual patient factors, such as age, comorbidities, and functional status.

To clinically validate the convergent therapy hypothesis, future research should focus on the following: (1) developing preclinical models that recapitulate the liver metastatic niche from multiple primary tumors; (2) designing basket trials that enroll patients with LM based on shared biomarkers rather than primary tumor origin; (3) integrating multi-omics data to identify convergent pathways across tumor types; (4) exploring novel combinations of immunotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted agents to overcome liver-mediated immunosuppression; and (5) validating the role of gut microbiota modulation in enhancing treatment response across different LM subtypes. Success in these research areas could transform LMC treatment from a primary tumor-centric model to a pathway-based approach, by reducing variability in care and improving access to effective therapies for patients with rare or understudied primary tumors.

Looking ahead to the future, the treatment pathway for LMC will achieve a high degree of applicability through unified strategies based on shared pathways, while at the same time taking into account the precision of treatment for each patient, by adjusting regimens according to individual molecular features and clinical status.
