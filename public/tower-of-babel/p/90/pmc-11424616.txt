Women in chemistry: Q&A with Dr Ritika Gautam-Singh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Dr Ritika Gautam-Singh is an Assistant Professor at the Indian Institute of Technology Kanpur, India, where she leads a research group focused on medicinal inorganic chemistry.

Ritika Gautam-Singh

Ritika Gautam-Singh

Ritika was born and raised in Azamgarh, a town in the eastern Uttar Pradesh, India. She received her B.Sc (Hons) and M.Sc degrees in Chemistry from Banaras Hindu University and the Indian Institute of Technology Delhi, respectively. In 2012, she relocated to the United States to pursue doctoral studies at the University of Arizona. She joined Prof. Elisa Tomat’s research group in the Department of Chemistry and Biochemistry and worked in bioinorganic chemistry. After receiving her Ph.D. in Chemistry in August 2017, Ritika joined the research group of Professor Kim D. Janda as a research associate at The Scripps Research Institute (TSRI), La Jolla, California. Her work at TSRI centered on the design and development of active vaccines to mitigate the psychoactive effects of synthetic psychoactive drugs (SPDs).

In the fall of 2019, Ritika returned to India to establish her independent research group at IIT Kanpur. Her group focuses on engineering metal-based diagnostics and therapeutics at the interface of biological inorganic chemistry, synthetic immunotherapy, and medicinal chemistry. Ritika has received notable early-career recognitions, including being named a ChemComm Royal Society of Chemistry Emerging Investigator and an American Chemical Society Organic & Inorganic Au Rising Star in 2023. Her dedication to teaching is evidenced by regular Excellence in Teaching recognitions at IIT Kanpur.

She has successfully secured initial grants, including the DST INSPIRE Faculty Fellowship, SERB-SRG, CSIR ASPIRE, DST-AMR-TDP, and ICMR grants. In 2024, Ritika was inducted as an associate of the Indian Academy of Sciences, Bengaluru, marking another significant milestone in her growing career. When not in the lab, Ritika enjoys traveling and spending quality time with her 3.5-year-old son and husband.

Why did you choose to be a scientist?

My deep-rooted passion for medical healthcare was the driving force behind my decision to pursue a career in science. From a young age, I was captivated by the intricate mechanisms of the human body and the immense potential of science to tackle complex health challenges. I aspired to be at the forefront of groundbreaking discoveries, pioneering new ways to prevent, diagnose, and treat diseases, all with the goal of improving countless lives. This unwavering commitment to making a meaningful impact on human health is what inspired me to embrace scientific research, where I can contribute to transformative medical advancements.

What scientific development are you currently most excited about?

The discovery of cisplatin (cis-diamminodichloridoplatinum(II)) in the late 1960s marked the inception of an exciting new frontier: metals in medicine. However, despite this groundbreaking discovery, it took over a decade for cisplatin to gain FDA approval. The transformative potential of metals is being reimagined, driving revolutionary advancements in the realm of medicine. The development of metallodrugs that exploit non-covalent interactions with diverse pharmacological targets is once again shifting the paradigm. These advancements are poised to complete the full cycle from discovery to clinical application. Our research group is particularly excited about the latest metal-based agents currently in clinical trials, which hold promise for treating various diseases and enhancing imaging techniques.

What direction do you think your research field should go in?

Our current research focuses on the burgeoning field of medicinal inorganic chemistry, specifically within the realm of metals in medicine. This exciting subfield of medicinal chemistry emphasizes the development of metal-based diagnostic and therapeutic agents. The discovery of the therapeutic potential of metallodrugs has catalyzed the design and synthesis of a new class of these agents, endowed with unique modes of action (MoA) to combat infectious diseases, malignancies, and neurodegenerative metabolic disorders. To fully understand how these mechanisms influence therapy, we must venture beyond conventional methodologies and explore innovative approaches for mode-of-action discovery and target identification.

On the project front, we are engaged in a range of ongoing investigations. These projects explore the non-covalent interactions of metal complexes with various pharmacological targets, while also leveraging the distinctive properties of metal complexes to address complex health challenges, such as targeting metastatic malignancy, antimicrobial photodynamic inactivation (aPDI), and Lafora neurodegenerative disease models. Looking to the future, we are advancing towards the development of self-assembled metallodrug-based nanoaggregates, which hold promise for enhancing drug uptake and functioning as immune response modulators.

How would you describe your research philosophy?

Learning is a lifelong journey, and one should never cease to pursue knowledge. In today’s world, the expertise needed to solve complex problems is incredibly diverse, making it impossible for any one person to possess all the necessary skills. Therefore, it’s essential to cultivate a constant curiosity and a willingness to learn—not only through self-study but also from colleagues, seniors, and most importantly, from our students.

What aspects of your research do you find most exciting or most rewarding?

The most exhilarating aspect of my research is the thrill of navigating its unpredictable highs and lows—the quest to tackle a challenging problem, troubleshooting, encountering setbacks, and yet rising each time to try again until we achieve the perfect solution. This iterative process of discovery is deeply fulfilling, and it’s inspiring to witness how my young, dynamic group of students navigates these challenges with unwavering determination and positivity. The vibrant environment, especially at institutions like IIT Kanpur, provides an exceptional platform for young faculty, fostering a culture of innovation and resilience that propels us all forward.

What topics do you most enjoy teaching?

At IIT Kanpur, we have a vibrant community of undergraduate, masters, and graduate students, and each semester brings the opportunity to teach various courses in inorganic chemistry. One topic I particularly enjoy teaching is “Metals in Medicine,” which is part of the Frontiers of Inorganic Chemistry course curriculum. When it comes to medicinal inorganic chemistry, many students initially see the field as limited to platinum-based metallodrugs like cisplatin against tumour growth. However, it is incredibly rewarding to expand their vision and demonstrate that the paradigm of metallodrugs has evolved far beyond this. The field now encompasses an array of innovative applications, from the development of novel therapeutics to the exploration of metal complexes with unique properties. These complexes are proving to be exceptionally effective, from photochemotherapy and catalytic metallodrugs to advancements in medical diagnostics like magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and cutting-edge bioanalytical techniques. Teaching this expanded landscape of metallodrugs is not only intellectually stimulating but also offers students a broader understanding of the field’s potential.

What do you most (and least) enjoy about being a scientific researcher?

What I most enjoy about being a scientific researcher is the freedom it affords—the freedom to pursue knowledge for the sheer joy of discovery, rather than to prove something to someone else. There is a unique fulfilment in mentoring young, dynamic students and working collaboratively on problems that have never been solved before.

However, one of the most challenging aspects of this journey is the prolonged timeline required to see tangible results. The process of taking a new drug/diagnostic tool from discovery through to approval, including the inevitable setbacks along the way, demands significant investment in both time and resources. This extended timeline can be disheartening at times, but it is also a testament to the rigor and dedication required to make a lasting impact in science.

Are you or have you been supported by a mentor? What was the best advice you received?

I have been incredibly fortunate to have been mentored by Prof. Elisa Tomat, a remarkable woman scientist, during my Ph.D. at the University of Arizona. She has been and continues to be a true inspiration to me, as well as to many other women aspiring to build a career in science and academia. One of the most valuable pieces of advice she imparted was the importance of bringing competency to the forefront, rather than focusing on being likable. This shift in mindset allowed me to prioritise my vision, my dreams, my ideas, and ultimately, my destiny.

In addition to being mentored by women scientists, I am proud to say that I have received tremendous support from my postdoctoral advisor, Prof. Kim D. Janda (The Scripps Research Institute, La Jolla, California), and from my male colleagues at IIT Kanpur. The Department of Chemistry at IIT Kanpur was founded in 1959, and though I was the first female faculty member to join at the entry level and continue in the department, I never felt out of place. One particular incident that stands out is when I was attending a CRSI conference with my six-month-old son. As I waited outside my hotel, preparing to head to the banquet dinner, a car arrived with four current and past directors of Indian Institutes. Without hesitation, they all stepped out and insisted that I take the car to my destination. While this may seem like a small gesture, it is moments like these that make me feel supported and grateful to be part of such a system where seniors as well as your contemporaries extend support to make your journey enjoyable and memorable.
