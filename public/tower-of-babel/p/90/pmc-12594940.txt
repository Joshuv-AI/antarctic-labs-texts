Melatonin microbe interactions in plant rhizosphere
Authors: Imad Aijaz, Eun-Hae Kwon, In-Jung Lee, Abdul Latif Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryMelatonin has emerged as a pivotal molecule in agriculture due to its ability to promote plant growth and alleviate abiotic stresses. Beyond endogenous biosynthesis, plants recruit microbial symbionts that also produce melatonin, which regulates redox balance, systemic resistance, and root development. Although plants and microbes employ distinct yet partially overlapping enzymatic pathways, both contribute to melatonin production under diverse conditions. A major limitation of direct melatonin application is its light sensitivity and instability across soil pH, restricting field-level use. Alternatively, melatonin-producing microbes can enrich soils, enhancing melatonin availability, uptake, and transport within plants to improve stress tolerance and growth. This review synthesizes current understanding of melatonin biosynthesis in plants and microbes, its ecological and physiological roles, and the promise of microbial consortia as delivery systems. We highlight melatonin-based strategies as sustainable tools for crop management and outline future directions for agricultural applications.

Melatonin biosynthesis in plants

Plant synthesizes melatonin through multifaceted pathways starting from the aromatic amino acid tryptophan via the shikimic acid pathway.1 This pathway involves at least six enzymes. The specific subcellular locations where melatonin and its intermediate compounds are produced are dependent on the sequence of the enzymatic reactions.2 Melatonin is synthesized from tryptophan through four key enzymatic reactions, each catalyzed by a different enzyme. Initially, tryptophan decarboxylase (TDC) converts tryptophan to tryptamine. After this step, tryptamine 5-hydroxylase (T5H) catalyzes the conversion of tryptamine to serotonin, a reaction that occurs in the endoplasmic reticulum.3 For the synthesis of serotonin, plants can employ another different pathway where tryptophan is first converted to 5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP) through the action of tryptophan 5-hydroxylase (TPH). Then, L-tryptophan decarboxylase (TDC) converts 5-HTP into serotonin. This accumulation of serotonin in the cytoplasm is a result of TDC’s enzymatic activity, which facilitates the decarboxylation of tryptophan, producing tryptamine.4

The subsequent steps in synthesis pathway lead to serotonin undergoing further modification. It is either acetylated by serotonin N-acetyltransferase (SNAT) to form N-acetyl-serotonin or methylated by N-acetylserotonin methyltransferase (ASMT) to form 5-methoxytryptamine.5,6 In the chloroplast, the enzyme arylalkylamine N-acetyltransferase (AANAT) or N-acetyltransferase (SNAT) performs two conversions: AANAT and SNAT transforms serotonin into N-acetyl-serotonin in the chloroplast and also, SNAT can facilitate the transformation of tryptamine to N-acetyl-tryptamine. However, T5H is unable to convert N-acetyl-tryptamine into N-acetyl-serotonin.5 At the last stage, N-acetyl-serotonin is methylated by ASMT or caffeic acid O-methyltransferase (COMT) into melatonin in the cytoplasm. Interestingly, alternative biosynthetic routes, such as the direct acetylation of tryptamine, enable plants to bypass specific intermediates such as serotonin and N-acetyl-serotonin. Melatonin is endogenously synthesized in a diverse array of plant species, predominantly in reproductive tissues, including flowers and seeds, and at the cellular level, biosynthesis takes place in subcellular compartments including the cytoplasm, chloroplasts, and endoplasmic reticulum.8 Melatonin concentrations in plants vary not only across different species but also among cultivars within the same species.9 While there was melatonin detected in tomato (5.9 ng/g fresh weight [FW]),10 rice (80 ng/g FW),11 and pepper (111.6 ng/g FW),12 the highest melatonin levels (>2300 ng/g dry weight [DW]) have been reported in medicinal plants and herbs, including thyme, Chinese licorice, sage, and St. John’s wort, which are widely utilized for their potential anti-aging properties and for managing free radical-associated diseases such as neurological disorders.13,14,15 Moreover, melatonin levels vary across different organs or tissues within a single plant, with the highest concentrations typically observed in reproductive structures such as seeds, fruits, and flowers.16 Melatonin has also been observed to accumulate highly in mature seeds. Its concentrations can vary in leaves, fruits, and roots based on the developmental stage, strongly suggesting that melatonin controls these developmental processes.12

Melatonin biosynthesis has been genetically characterized in several plant species. In Oryza sativa (rice), genes encoding enzymes such as TDC, T5H, SNAT, and ASMT have been identified and functionally linked to melatonin production.17 Similarly, Solanum lycopersicum (tomato) exhibits the presence of biosynthetic genes for melatonin.18 Additional studies in species such as Prunus avium (sweet cherry) and Hypericum perforatum have contributed to the broader understanding of melatonin synthesis at the molecular level.19 In genetically edited rice, overexpression of the TDC3 gene significantly elevates the levels of tryptamine and its downstream metabolites, culminating in enhanced melatonin synthesis.20 These findings collectively demonstrate that while the core enzymatic machinery for melatonin biosynthesis is conserved, there is also species-specific variation in gene expression and regulation, underscoring the importance of further comparative studies in model plants (Arabidopsis thaliana) and non-model species such as Prunus avium. These comparative analyses can uncover conserved and divergent enzymatic functions, gene expression patterns, and regulatory networks. Such studies are essential not only for elucidating how melatonin biosynthesis contributes to plant reproduction, stress adaptation, and crop improvement in both staple crops and horticultural species but also for providing deeper insights into the diverse physiological roles of melatonin in plants.

Melatonin biosynthesis in microbes

Melatonin synthesis in microbes shares foundational similarities with its synthesis in plants and animals, but also exhibits distinct characteristics reflective of the diversity among microorganisms.21 In microbial systems, melatonin biosynthesis starts with tryptophan.22 The process involves the hydroxylation of tryptophan into 5-HTP, catalyzed by tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH), which serves as the initial and critical enzyme.7 This reaction is followed by hydroxylation of tryptamine to serotonin by 5-hydroxy-L-tryptophan decarboxylase (DDC). Subsequently, serotonin is acetylated by arylalkylamine-N-acetyltransferase (AANAT), forming N-acetyl serotonin, and the pathway culminates with the methylation of N-acetyl serotonin to produce melatonin, catalyzed by N-acetyl serotonin-O-methyltransferase (ASMT) or similar methyltransferases.23,24,25

To support these enzymatic transformations, cofactor-dependent systems are pivotal in facilitating melatonin synthesis in microbes. One such cofactor is tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4), which is a molecule that acts as a vital electron donor for hydroxylation reactions catalyzed by monooxygenases such as TPH; it is synthesized through the BH4 pathway.21,26 BH4 in the BH4 pathway and the S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) cycle are critical supporting systems in melatonin biosynthesis, especially in microorganisms,21,25,27 because L-tryptophan serves as the primary precursor, which is converted to HTP by TPH and this step requires both oxygen and the cofactor tetrahydropterin.25 During the last step in melatonin synthesis, SAM serves as a methyl group donor in the final step of melatonin biosynthesis; N-acetylserotonin is O-methylated to melatonin by ASMT, and during this O-methylation, SAM is used as a cofactor, producing adenosylhomocysteine (SAH). SAH can then be recycled back to SAM via the SAM cycle. In microbial organisms, this pathway exhibits a high degree of flexibility and adaptation, which allows the organisms to produce melatonin in response to specific environmental and physiological conditions.21 For example, the expression levels of key enzymes such as TDC and AANAT can vary significantly depending on external factors such as oxidative stress, nutrient availability, or light conditions.29 This adaptability enables melatonin to perform diverse roles in microbial cells, including acting as a potent antioxidant, a signaling molecule, and a regulator of metabolic pathways under stress conditions.30

Melatonin as a biological rhythm regulator in plants

The regulatory function of melatonin in modulating light-dark cycles has been well established in mammals.31 Endogenous circadian rhythms in melatonin levels have been identified in certain species, and although data remain limited, they indicate a central role for this molecule in regulating day-night cycles in plants. In plants, the circadian oscillator can adjust the phase of various biological processes, such as gene expression, metabolic regulation, and protein stability, to coincide with daily and/or seasonal cycles.8 Thus, circadian regulation enhances photosynthesis and growth rates and may influence crop flowering, seed yield, and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses.32,33 This regulatory function in circadian rhythms and photoperiodicity served as the foundation for research on plant melatonin.

The first study suggested that plant-based melatonin is involved in regulating the circadian rhythm,34 showing that melatonin levels were low during the light period and significantly higher in darkness (maximum levels of 250 pg/g FW). This is in synergy to that observed in mammals.35 Plants exposed to different photoperiodic cycles did not exhibit a relationship between the duration of melatonin increase and the photoperiod applied.36 It was concluded that, although melatonin levels were always highest after dark, the biosynthesis of melatonin was not directly influenced by light but instead displayed a circadian rhythm similar to that observed in mammals. Similar experiments with St. John’s wort (Hypericum perforatum L.) exhibited rhythmic variations in melatonin concentration during light-dark conditions. Photoperiods (wavelength and intensity of applied light) significantly impacted melatonin concentration. Under long-day conditions, melatonin was approximately six times greater than under short-day conditions and was highest under blue light treatment.36

Moreover, the circadian clock controls plants’ daily stomatal rhythms.37 This was correlated with abscisic acid (ABA) production and darkness-induced stomatal closure by activating NADPH oxidase-dependent reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and a phytomelatonin receptor (PMTR1, formerly known as CAND2) in Arabidopsis regulates stomatal closure through an ROS-dependent signaling pathway dependent on NADPH oxidase activity.38 Therefore, the circadian rhythm of melatonin appears to be present in plants. Melatonin crosstalks with plant hormones

Numerous studies have reported that melatonin mitigates various individual and combined abiotic stresses over the last two decades. Melatonin has been studied to defend the plants and their associated microbiomes from the impacts induced by (1) salinity, (2) cold, (3) heat, (4) flooding, (5) drought, (6) metal-induced toxicity, and (7) waterlogging stress (Table S1).39,40 Melatonin plays an important role not only as a protective agent against environmental stress but also as a versatile signaling molecule that modulates plant hormone networks to modify physiological responses.41 Melatonin does not react in isolation; it interacts with endogenous phytohormones to co-regulate developmental programs and stress adaptation mechanisms.42 These interactions can occur at multiple levels, including hormone biosynthesis, transport, signaling, and gene expression.43,44 For example, melatonin has been observed to influence the hormonal balance by enhancing ABA accumulation during drought while attenuating ethylene and gibberellic acid (GA) signaling to delay senescence and conserve energy under stress.45,46 In addition, melatonin can act upstream or downstream of salicylic acid (SA) and jasmonic acid (JA) pathways, strengthening plant immune responses against pathogens.47 Some studies show that melatonin can stabilize cytokinin (CK) and brassinosteroid homeostasis, thereby maintaining cellular division and elongation even under challenging growth conditions.48

As a multifunctional molecule, melatonin’s ability to coordinate with classical and newly discovered plant hormones underscores its central role in maintaining plant vitality under normal and stressful environments.49 Building on this hormonal interplay, recent research has explored specific interactions between melatonin and individual phytohormones in greater detail (Figure 3). Over the last decades, several investigations have highlighted melatonin as a critical chemical in root development. Its role is similar to auxin, in hypocotyls of Lupinus albus L. and monocots such as wheat, canary grass, barley, and oat, and dicots such as Arabidopsis thaliana (L.).1,50 Similarly, promoting the root branching and elongation is crucial for nutrient uptake and plant stability.51 In synergy with other plant growth regulators, there is a strong dose effect observed, such as low concentrations stimulate root growth, whereas high concentrations inhibit it52,53,54 (Figure 3).

Phytohormones participate in numerous processes throughout the plant life cycle, serving as essential regulatory molecules that influence development and economic yield under various conditions, including stress.55 Researchers have found that phytomelatonin interacts with other plant hormones.56 Initial studies focused on auxin due to its similar molecular structure.43 Using a strategy of supplementing melatonin and its interaction with other phytohormones is a more effective approach. Their combined action helps to reduce the adverse effects of abiotic stresses, thereby ensuring a strong and consistent yield.56 The versatility of phytohormones in a plant’s response to abiotic stresses has led to a significant increase in attention from plant researchers.57 Melatonin has been shown in various studies to have a close association with almost all plant hormones. This includes classical hormones such as auxin, GA, CK, and ABA, as well as newly identified ones such as JA, SA, brassinosteroids, polyamines, and strigolactones43.

Auxin

Melatonin plays a role in activating growth processes in plants, which has been shown in recent studies to regulate the function of signaling elements. Specifically, it works on auxin receptors, regulators, and tiny auxin upregulated RNA genes.58 It often acts as a signaling molecule that regulates IAA transport and signaling auxin activity to optimize growth under stress.58,59 Studies have shown that melatonin can regulate the expression of auxin-related genes, such as AUX/IAA, auxin response factors, and genes encoding PIN proteins, which control auxin polar transport. This regulation affects auxin biosynthesis, transport, and signaling. Also, research on lupin plants has highlighted the regulatory function of melatonin and IAA in root formation.41,50 Melatonin-induced root growth takes place via an auxin signaling pathway-dependent mechanism. In Arabidopsis thaliana, melatonin functions synergistically with auxin to regulate auxin transport, thereby promoting lateral root development. However, these synergistic effects were not observed in knockout mutants of individual auxin transport-related genes, including pin-formed 5, with-no-lysine kinase 1, transparent testa 4, and transparent testa 5, examined in Arabidopsis thaliana (L.).59 In rice, melatonin also influenced the expression of key genes in the root meristem and enhanced auxin signaling, leading to improved root system development.60 The findings indicate that melatonin interacts with IAA during root formation via activation/repression of regulatory factors, thereby promoting root development. Moreover, melatonin has a significant influence on the regulation of auxin transporters (especially PINs, which are auxin efflux transporters) and initiation of root primordia, which promote root growth (Figure 3).

Gibberellic acid

Melatonin has been reported to stimulate GA biosynthesis under stressful conditions, thereby promoting plant growth and development. In cucumber seedlings subjected to salt stress, melatonin application upregulated key GA biosynthetic genes, GA20ox and GA3ox, resulting in elevated levels of active GAs (GA3 and GA4) and mitigating the inhibitory effects of salinity on seed germination.61 Similarly, in Pyrus (Pear), melatonin induced parthenocarpic fruit development by modulating GA20ox and GA2ox, resulting in elevated GA levels that triggered cell division and mesocarp expansion.62 Interestingly, the relationship between melatonin and GAs is bidirectional. Exogenous GA application has been found to stimulate melatonin production in rice, resulting in melatonin-enriched seeds. Conversely, transgenic rice plants with downregulated GA3ox2—a gene involved in converting inactive GA9/GA20 into active GA4/GA1—exhibited reduced melatonin levels.11 This suggests that endogenous GAs may act as positive regulators of melatonin biosynthesis (Figure 3).

Abscisic acid

Melatonin also interacts closely with ABA, particularly by reducing its levels during stress responses. Under salt stress, melatonin treatment downregulated 9-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase (NCED), a key gene in ABA biosynthesis, and upregulated ABA catabolism genes cytochrome P450 family 707 subfamily A (two CYP707 monooxygenases). This led to a rapid decline in ABA content during seed germination.61 A similar trend was observed in drought-stressed Malus pumila (apple) leaves, where melatonin pretreatment reduced ABA levels by regulating the same set of biosynthesis and degradation genes.63 Moreover, ABA and melatonin influence each other’s levels and signaling pathways. In Arabidopsis thaliana, mutants impaired in ABA biosynthesis or signaling displayed higher basal melatonin levels than wild-type plants. Additionally, melatonin treatment downregulated genes involved in ABA biosynthesis and signaling.64 The combined action of melatonin and ABA enhances plant tolerance to a range of abiotic stresses.45 In the past five years, numerous studies have investigated the interactions between melatonin and ABA in plants, with particular emphasis on their roles in stress responses and developmental processes. Despite this antagonistic pattern, melatonin can also enhance ABA function in certain stress responses. For example, it supports ABA-regulated ion channels and transporters under salinity, contributing to ion homeostasis.61 Melatonin also modulates ABA-responsive gene expression, pointing to a complex and sometimes synergistic role in abiotic stress tolerance and development.65

Ethylene

Melatonin treatment in tomatoes activates carotenoid biosynthesis genes, enhances the transcription of ethylene-related genes, including 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid oxidase, 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid synthase (ACS), ethylene insensitive3-like 1 (EIL1), ethylene insensitive3-like 3 (EIL3), and ethylene response factor 2, and influences proteins linked to ripening.66,67 It improves fruit quality by increasing levels of antioxidants, lycopene, and citric acid while enhancing ripening and flavor, though levels of certain minerals such as nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), magnesium (Mg), and others decrease.68 In Fragaria × ananassa (strawberries), melatonin application extends shelf life, boosts bioactive compounds, and enhances fruit quality and postharvest attributes.69 Also, melatonin enhances ethylene biosynthesis and stress resistance against several abiotic stresses. These results were shown in Vitis (grapevines) and Nicotiana tabacum (tobacco plants), especially under salinity stress.70 Melatonin treatment markedly upregulated the expression of Myb domain protein 108A (MYB108A) and 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid synthase 1 (ACS1), functioning as a transcription factor and a key gene in ethylene biosynthesis, respectively, thereby enhancing salt tolerance through MYB108A-mediated regulation of ACS1 (Figure 3). Similarly, exogenous melatonin enhanced waterlogging stress tolerance in tomatoes by downregulating the expression of respiratory burst oxidase homolog (RBOH)-regulating genes while upregulating the transcription of ethylene biosynthesis genes.71

Cytokinin

Cytokinins are vital phytohormones that stimulate cell division and retard leaf senescence. Recent studies have highlighted a synergistic relationship between melatonin and CKs, particularly under abiotic stress conditions such as drought or high salinity. Exogenous melatonin application has been shown to increase CK levels, maintain chlorophyll content, and delay leaf senescence, thereby enhancing stress tolerance.72 Interestingly, CKs also stimulate melatonin biosynthesis by upregulating melatonin synthesis genes, leading to increased endogenous melatonin levels. This mutual regulation appears to be mediated by CK signaling components, including type-A and type-B arabidopsis response regulator transcription factors, which are upregulated upon melatonin treatment. In drought-stressed creeping bent grass, both wild-type and transgenic lines overexpressing the CK biosynthetic gene isopentenyl transferase exhibited improved drought tolerance when treated with melatonin. The combined action of CKs and melatonin improved multiple physiological parameters, including photochemical efficiency, chlorophyll content, and relative water content.72 In Arabidopsis thaliana, melatonin was found to mitigate high-light-induced stress by restoring the expression of CK synthesis and signaling genes that were otherwise suppressed under stress conditions.73 However, this protective effect of melatonin was absent in CK signaling mutants, indicating that a functional CK signaling pathway is essential for melatonin-mediated regulation. Moreover, exogenous CK treatment upregulated melatonin biosynthesis genes under high light, further supporting a bidirectional and synergistic interaction between CKs and melatonin in stress mitigation (Figure 3).

Salicylic acid

The activation of SA is responsible for regulating a plant’s defense mechanisms against biotic stress, such as attacks from pathogens or insects.74,75 Increased production of SA, JA and ethylene is generally observed after the application of melatonin. The crucial role of melatonin in a plant’s defense against biotic stress has been revealed in previous studies.43 Melatonin plays a crucial role in boosting plant immunity by upregulating pathogenesis-related genes, especially those dependent on SA and ethylene signaling pathways.43 However, this effect is significantly reduced in mutants with impaired SA or ethylene signaling, suggesting that melatonin’s defense-enhancing effects require functional hormone signaling networks.76 Additionally, melatonin activates other SA-related genes, resulting in increased resistance to pathogens. Evidence from SNAT knockout mutants, which are deficient in melatonin biosynthesis, shows reduced levels of both melatonin and SA, along with increased susceptibility to pathogen attack.76 This supports a model where melatonin positively regulates SA-mediated immunity (Figure 3).

Jasmonic acid

Among the various hormones involved in plant stress responses, JA stands out for its dynamic role in regulating defense and developmental processes.77 It acts as a key integrator between external stress signals and internal physiological responses, enabling plants to adjust and survive under conditions such as drought, salinity, mechanical injury, and pathogen invasion.78 JA works through a controlled system that can turn responses on or off, depending on plant stress. This adaptability renders JA especially important in orchestrating cross-talk with other signaling molecules, including melatonin, which has been identified as a modulator of JA-mediated responses under diverse stress conditions78 (Figure 3).

The defense system of plants is strengthened by jasmonates or JA, which upregulate the genes, proteins, and metabolites involved in this process.79 The interaction between melatonin and JA is complex and context dependent. In some cases, melatonin appears to support JA-mediated defense responses. For example, in wheat plants under heat stress, melatonin enhanced MeJA-induced protection of photosynthetic activity by regulating ethylene biosynthesis genes and promoting antioxidant metabolism. Similar protective effects of melatonin were observed in tomato plants under drought stress.80 However, melatonin suppresses the salt stress-induced increase in JA biosynthesis and accumulation in rapeseed seedlings, downregulating it to normal levels and thereby alleviating JA-dependent stress inhibition mechanisms while promoting seedling growth. The production of JAZ proteins is triggered by melatonin. These proteins are repressors in the JA signaling pathway, and by suppressing the JA-mediated response, they enhance plant’s salt tolerance and development.81 It seems that melatonin induced the expression of JAZ proteins, which are repressors in the JA signaling pathway, and this suppression of JA-mediated responses contributed to improved salt tolerance and plant development. Thus, while melatonin may enhance JA responses in certain contexts, it can also downregulate JA signaling through JAZ protein induction, particularly when suppression of JA-related stress pathways confers adaptive benefits.

Melatonin-producing microbes and their application

Melatonin plays a multifaceted role in microbial fermentation and metabolite production, serving as both a bioactive compound and a modulator of microbial physiology. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, melatonin is produced intracellularly during the lag phase and exported to the extracellular medium during the stationary phase.82 During this process, melatonin interacts with glycolytic proteins, suggesting a regulatory role in energy metabolism that is critical for efficient fermentation.82 The biosynthesis of melatonin in microorganisms is closely linked to their ability to cope with environmental challenges. For instance, in fungi and certain bacteria, melatonin has been implicated in enhancing oxidative stress resistance by scavenging ROS.83 This function is particularly crucial in high-stress environments where microbes encounter oxidative damage from UV radiation, heavy metals, or toxic metabolites.

Moreover, melatonin contributes to maintaining cellular homeostasis and regulating metabolic fluxes, which are essential for microbial survival and growth under adverse conditions.84 Also, melatonin’s antioxidant properties enhance stress tolerance in microorganisms, maintaining cellular integrity during industrial fermentation by scavenging ROS. This protective role supports consistent metabolite production and improves the robustness of microbial cell factories. Its roles as a target product, metabolic modulator, and antioxidant highlight its importance in advancing fermentation technologies and optimizing metabolite production.85 In Lactobacillus species commonly used in probiotic fermentation, melatonin enhances metabolic processes, resulting in increased production of lactic acid and other key metabolites essential for food preservation, flavor improvement, and therapeutic uses.86 Moreover, melatonin has been shown to affect microbial quorum sensing, which helps regulate collective behaviors within microbial populations, indirectly promoting metabolite production.87 These insights suggest that melatonin could be utilized to optimize fermentation processes and increase the microbial production of high-value metabolites (Table S2).

Melatonin has garnered increasing interest due to its wide-ranging applications in both medicine and agriculture. While traditionally associated with animal and plant biosynthesis, recent studies have identified numerous microorganisms capable of producing melatonin, presenting a safer and more sustainable alternative to chemical synthesis.27 Several yeast and bacterial species, including Pichia kluyveri, Variovorax, Bacillus, Oenococcus, and photosynthetic bacteria such as Rhodospirillum rubrum and Erythrobacter longus, have been reported to produce melatonin.88,89,90,91,92,93 Additionally, melatonin production has been detected in various lactic acid bacteria (e.g., Lactobacillus spp., Bifidobacterium spp., Enterococcus faecalis TH10, and Streptococcus thermophilus), further expanding the diversity of melatonin-producing microbial species (Table S2).

Industrial-scale melatonin production via microbial91 fermentation has also been reported.94 Microorganisms such as Pseudomonas fluorescens and yeast generate substantial melatonin levels during fermentation, with yeast-derived melatonin shown to enhance fermentative metabolism by interacting with glycolytic proteins and improving oxidative stress resistance.21 During alcoholic fermentation, Saccharomyces cerevisiae QA23 produced 1.35 ng/mL of melatonin, while Levucell SC20 generated 93.14 ng/mL.95 These fermentation-derived products have been commercialized, such as melatonin drops (Quantum Nutrition Labs, Texas, USA), which contain 8.0 mg of yeast-derived melatonin and are marketed as dietary supplements supporting sleep, immune function, and antioxidant activity.

Microbial melatonin production also plays a role in interspecies interactions and symbiotic relationships with plants, influencing stress responses and growth. Several plant growth-promoting bacteria have demonstrated melatonin biosynthetic ability, thereby enhancing plant resilience under abiotic stress conditions. For example, Pseudomonas 42P4 and Enterobacter 64S1 produced 402.9 ± 18 and 99.0 ± 36 ng/mL of melatonin, respectively, in tryptophan-supplemented cultures and increased endogenous melatonin levels in Arabidopsis thaliana under drought stress.96 These strains also improved photosynthetic pigment content under both drought and well-watered conditions, mirroring the effects of exogenous melatonin application. Similarly, Bacillus safensis EH143, can mitigate salt and cadmium stress in soybeans. This microbe, which produces 35 ng/mL of melatonin in tryptophan-enriched media, achieves this by enhancing antioxidant defenses and regulating stress-responsive pathways.97 Another study identified an endophytic strain of Pseudomonas fluorescens isolated from grapevine roots that produced 30,000–50,000 ng/mL of melatonin in vitro.88 Under salinity stress, this bacterium promoted endogenous melatonin biosynthesis in grapevines, improving antioxidant enzyme activity, nutrient uptake, and root architecture, thereby enhancing plant resilience. Collectively, these findings underscore the potential of melatonin-producing microorganisms for biotechnological applications in agriculture and industry (Table S2). Their ability to promote plant growth and stress tolerance, alongside the development of microbial fermentation systems for melatonin production, highlights promising avenues for sustainable crop improvement and natural melatonin supplementation.

Current commercial production is predominantly reliant on chemical synthesis, a process that is associated with significant environmental burdens and the utilization of hazardous reagents.21 Alternative sources, such as animal- or plant-based extraction, are constrained by the limited availability of raw materials and inherently low extraction efficiency. Recent progress in synthetic biology and metabolic engineering has facilitated the development of microbial cell factories capable of de novo melatonin biosynthesis. These approaches typically involve the augmentation of precursor supply, fine-tuning of heterologous gene expression, and enhancement of pathway flux through the optimization of cofactor and coenzyme utilization. For instance, Wang engineered Escherichia coli strain BL7992 to biosynthesize melatonin from low-cost L-tryptophan, which was produced by incorporating a heterologous biosynthetic pathway comprising monooxygenase XcP4H (mXcP4H) from Xanthomonas campestris, aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase (HsAADC), arylalkylamine N-acetyltransferase (HsAANAT), and hydroxyindole-O-methyltransferase (HIOMT) from Homo sapiens.22 Despite achieving a relatively low titer (1.13 mg/L) compared to other engineered microbial systems (up to 136 mg/L in shake flasks and 0.65 g/L in bioreactors), the study identified HsAANAT as the rate-limiting enzymatic step. Metabolomic profiling further revealed diversion of L-tryptophan to competing indole pathways, indicating that targeted deletion of the tnaA (tryptophanase) gene could mitigate substrate loss. Strategic genome editing, such as tnaA deletion, prevents L-tryptophan catabolism and directs flux toward serotonin and melatonin synthesis. While the yield remains inferior to chemically synthesized melatonin, the strategy’s reliance on inexpensive precursors and its avoidance of toxic solvents underscores its potential for eco-friendly production (Table S3).

Similarly, Germann reconstituted the mammalian melatonin biosynthetic pathway in Saccharomyces cerevisiae by expressing heterologous enzymes that sequentially convert L-tryptophan into 5-HTP, serotonin, N-acetylserotonin, and finally melatonin.25 A major bottleneck in this system was the rate-limiting nature of TPH, which depends on the cofactor BH4. To address this, the authors co-expressed genes involved in BH4 biosynthesis and recycling, alongside multi-copy integration of the TPH gene. BH4 is a necessary cofactor for TPH. Pathways for BH4 biosynthesis (e.g., SPR, DHPR, and PCBD) and BH4 regeneration (PTS and OCH1) are engineered or boosted to stabilize cofactor supply. These modifications substantially enhanced pathway flux and resulted in melatonin titers of approximately 14.5 mg/L following 76 h of fermentation.

In addition to the incorporation of animal-derived biosynthetic genes, recent studies have demonstrated the efficacy of utilizing plant- and microbe-derived genes to construct melatonin pathways. Zhang et al. established a biosynthetic route in E. coli by integrating genes from Streptomyces albulus, Xanthomonas campestris, and Oryza sativa.27 Optimization of heterologous protein expression and the supplementation of glycerol (3%) led to a 3-fold increase in melatonin production. Further metabolic enhancements, including overexpression of caffeic acid 3-O-methyltransferase (COMT) and deletion of the speD gene to elevate intracellular SAM, collectively yielded an 11-fold improvement in production. SAM is the methyl donor for melatonin synthesis, and genes related to SAM regeneration (e.g., SAMS, SAH1, and MS) are overexpressed to ensure enough methyl groups are available. The engineered strain, designated EcMEL8, achieved titers of 136 mg/L in shake-flask cultures and 0.65 g/L in a 2-L bioreactor supplemented with L-tryptophan (Figure 4).

Collectively, these studies highlight the promise of engineered microbial platforms as sustainable, scalable, and cost-effective alternatives to traditional melatonin production. Bioengineered melatonin is particularly well suited for applications requiring high purity, such as in pharmaceuticals and nutraceuticals, while also serving as a functional additive in agriculture for enhancing plant resilience and crop quality. Ongoing advances in strain engineering, pathway modularization, and bioprocess optimization are anticipated to further increase production efficiency, thereby facilitating the integration of microbial melatonin biosynthesis into industrial-scale food, agricultural, and healthcare systems.

Melatonin effects on rhizosphere and soil microbiome

The plant-associated microbiome, often referred to as the “second genome,” serves a crucial role in maintaining plant health and enhancing productivity. Its composition and function are shaped by abiotic factors such as temperature, precipitation, soil pH, and nutrient status, as well as plant-specific traits including species, developmental stage, and root exudate profiles.98,99 These elements collectively influence the recruitment and activity of beneficial or pathogenic microbes. However, intensive and continuous cropping systems disrupt this balance, leading to microbial community shifts, reductions in beneficial microbes, enrichment of pathogenic fungi, and a transition from bacterial to fungal dominance, which increases plant susceptibility to soil-borne diseases.100,101

Recent studies suggest that melatonin, beyond its role as a multifunctional phytohormone, also acts as a regulatory molecule in the rhizosphere, influencing microbial diversity, composition, and function. Exogenous melatonin application enhances rhizosphere conditions by promoting soil enzyme activity, improving nutrient cycling, and increasing beneficial microbial populations, while reducing several pathogenic fungi.102 It also exerts inhibitory effects on microorganisms detrimental to plant health and rhizosphere stability (Table S4).

In bacterial communities, melatonin promotes the growth of Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria, especially under low-temperature conditions.103,104 These groups are integral to nitrogen fixation, phosphorus solubilization, and enhancing resilience to environmental stressors, including drought, heavy metals, microplastics, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons.105 Proteobacteria are particularly important in carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling.106 Meanwhile, changes in Acidobacteria abundance suggest melatonin’s involvement in nitrogen metabolism and exopolysaccharide production.104 Also, the melatonin-driven enrichment of Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria should be linked to their established functions in nutrient cycling and stress tolerance, particularly evident in waterlogged soils where microbial community restructuring enhances oxygen availability and maintains ion balance.107

Melatonin treatment also recruits stress-associated genera such as Azovibrio, especially under cadmium stress, with positive correlations to plant growth.108 Bacillus spp., known for disease suppression via antibiotics and bacteriocins, are enriched by melatonin, potentially improving replant soil environments and microbial community stability.109 He, X. et al. reported a significant increase in Bacillus abundance in replant soils, suggesting melatonin-induced enhancement of apple replant disease resistance.110 Furthermore, melatonin reduces nitrosating bacteria such as Halomonas and certain Bacillus strains, indicating its role in modulating nitrogen cycling to improve nitrogen use efficiency in Malus hupehensis.110

In fungal communities, melatonin reduces Ascomycota, a phylum involved in organic matter decomposition but containing many pathogens,111 while promoting beneficial fungi from Glomeromycota and Basidiomycota.112 At the genus level, melatonin increases the abundance of Mortierella and Penicillium, which enhances urease activity and contributes to nitrogen and phosphorus cycling.113 It also supports beneficial fungi such as Trichoderma spp. and Saccharomyces cerevisiae while suppressing pathogenic Fusarium spp.114 Crucially, melatonin enhances colonization by arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi [AMF]), which form symbiotic relationships with nearly 80% of land plants.115 AMF exchange photosynthates for water and mineral nutrients, improving drought tolerance and plant resilience.116

In summary, melatonin exerts multifaceted effects on rhizosphere microbial communities by enriching beneficial taxa, suppressing pathogens, enhancing enzymatic activity, and stabilizing microbial networks. These effects collectively enhance soil health, nutrient dynamics, and plant stress tolerance. Further research is warranted to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying these shifts and to assess the long-term ecological impacts in diverse agroecosystems. Moreover, this result raises the question of whether melatonin-producing microorganisms are introduced into the rhizosphere and how they interact with native soil microbial communities. It can be hypothesized that (1) these microbes may exert localized effects through continuous melatonin biosynthesis, potentially mimicking or enhancing the effects of exogenous melatonin; (2) they could influence host plant physiology—such as root architecture or exudate composition—which in turn reshapes microbial assembly; and (3) the microbial shifts observed may partially overlap with those induced by exogenous melatonin, but could also exhibit unique signatures depending on microbial strain, colonization efficiency, and soil type. Investigating these dynamics will be critical to understanding whether microbial inoculants can serve as functionally equivalent bio-inputs to direct melatonin application.

Rhizosphere microbiome engineering toward sustainable agriculture

The integration of engineered microbiomes into agricultural systems is gaining traction for enhancing plant health, stress tolerance, and nutrient uptake.117 Advances in molecular tools and next-generation sequencing now allow for the precise identification of plant-associated microbial taxa. Inspired by synthetic microbiome applications in human health, such as fecal microbiota transplantation,9 soil microbiome manipulation offers a promising route to induce systemic resistance against pathogens and pests in crops.118 Microbial inoculation strategies span from single strains to complex microbial consortia and microbiome transplants.119 Although single-strain inoculants are widely used, they often perform inconsistently due to limited functionality and poor persistence in natural soils.120 In contrast, multispecies consortia provide greater functional redundancy, ecological resilience, and improved plant-microbe interactions under field conditions.121 Synthetic communities (SynComs), rationally assembled from defined taxa, offer a controlled approach to stabilize beneficial functions.122 While high-complexity SynComs exhibit better adaptability, low-complexity SynComs may lack robustness in variable environments123 (Figure 5).Figure 5Strategies for applying Synthetic biology enables the construction of strains with enhanced traits such as nutrient solubilization, biocontrol, and stress adaptation. For example, phosphate-solubilizing bacterial consortia improved maize growth under phosphorus deficiency,124 while halotolerant and drought-adaptive microbes enhanced plant performance in saline and dry soils.125 Tailoring microbial consortia to root exudate profiles further increases their effectiveness and ecological compatibility. Despite laboratory-scale successes,126 field-scale implementation of SynComs remains limited by ecological variability and the complexity of soil environments. Field validation, cost-effectiveness, and scalability remain key challenges to realizing the full potential of microbiome engineering in agriculture.

Genetically engineered microbes also enable precise control over synthetic pathways by incorporating diverse genetic regulatory modules responsive to plant-derived signals. It allows dynamic regulation of plant growth-promoting (PGP) traits via biosensors responsive to plant signals such as sugars, auxin, and quorum-sensing molecules. For instance, a modified E. coli Trp repressor functions as an FRET-based biosensor that selectively detects auxin derivatives, enabling auxin-triggered gene expression. Similar systems have enabled quorum-sensing-dependent IAA production in Cupriavidus pinatubonensis, enhancing colonization and hormone signaling. Additionally, co-expression of ACC deaminase and siderophore biosynthesis genes in PGPRs has synergistically improved sunflower growth.127 Engineered Bacillus strains expressing auxin biosynthetic genes, and Enterobacter and Kosakonia endophytes carrying ACC deaminase, have increased both growth and disease resistance in banana by mitigating ethylene stress. These examples underscore the potential of synthetic biology to create programmable microbial inoculants with coordinated, multi-trait expression, thereby enabling climate-resilient and sustainable crop production systems (Figure 5).

Application of melatonin-producing engineered microbiome in agriculture: Its potential and limitations

Engineered melatonin-producing microbiomes, developed through synthetic community design or genetic modification, present a promising approach to mitigate plant dysbiosis induced by biotic factors (e.g., pathogen infection, insect herbivory, and parasitic weeds) and abiotic stresses (e.g., salinity, drought, and heavy metal toxicity), thereby enhancing crop productivity and stress resilience. Genetically engineered strains can be tailored to biosynthesize melatonin in response to specific environmental cues, potentially mitigating drought and salinity stress, as demonstrated by other PGP-engineered microbes.104 Although exogenous melatonin application has shown agricultural benefits, its high-cost limits large-scale use. For example, melatonin powder (>98% purity) costs approximately US$101 per gram (Sigma-Aldrich), rendering it economically impractical even for efficient delivery methods such as seed coating. Lower-cost alternatives lack verified purity and provenance, raising concerns over consistency and safety.128 Thus, utilizing melatonin-producing microbes offers a cost-effective and sustainable alternative that enables dynamic and in situ melatonin production aligned with plant stress responses. When integrated into synthetic microbial consortia, these strains may also synergize with other PGP traits such as nutrient mobilization and biocontrol, forming multifunctional bioinoculants.

The ecological behavior of engineered strains—particularly survival, colonization, and potential impacts on native microbial communities—requires further study. Risks such as horizontal gene transfer and unintended ecosystem effects must be carefully assessed. Moreover, regulatory frameworks in many countries restrict the field deployment of genetically modified (GM) microbes, and public acceptance remains a significant barrier. Moreover, integrating engineered melatonin-producing microbes with well-established agronomic practices can help address their current challenges and provide more effective solutions under field conditions. For instance, strategies such as ridge intertillage, which reshapes rhizosphere microbiomes to alleviate waterlogging damage, have been demonstrated to synergize with melatonin pathways under real-world conditions.129 Similarly, agronomic approaches such as the removal of early fruits in crops have proven effective in mitigating waterlogging-induced yield loss through both physiological and adaptive mechanisms.130 These examples highlight the potential of combining biotechnological innovations with traditional agronomic strategies to enhance crop resilience and productivity.

Future efforts should therefore prioritize not only the design of tightly regulated gene circuits for melatonin biosynthesis but also the establishment of a dedicated subsection in research and reviews comparing lab-based mechanisms with field-level outcomes. This comparative perspective is essential to emphasize the need for stressor-specific synthetic microbial consortia that can complement agronomic practices and provide reliable resilience across diverse agroecosystems. A responsible and well-regulated approach is indispensable for realizing the full potential of melatonin-producing microbiomes as next-generation tools for sustainable and climate-resilient agriculture.

Future prospectives

In the future, plant melatonin research needs to move beyond just studying popular food and medicinal crops (see more in Box 1). So far, most work has focused on species that are important for agriculture or health, but we know very little about how melatonin works in wild plants or those with cultural value. Studying these overlooked plants could help us understand how melatonin helps them survive harsh environments or adapt to changes in nature. Another area that needs more attention is how plants make melatonin. The process is complicated and varies between species, but with better tools such as CRISPR and omics technologies, we can start to figure out the functions of enzymes more precisely. Melatonin also breaks down into other forms, such as hydroxy melatonin, which might have its role in helping plants handle stress. However, researchers haven’t studied these much yet. Using advanced techniques to track these molecules could tell us a lot more. Finally, one of the biggest missing pieces is how melatonin moves inside plants, and researchers are still finding what proteins help transport it, or where it goes after being made.
