A study of the successful preparation and tribological self-regulation of a novel hybrid lamination of the SiCf/Ti–Ti2AlNb-based gears
Authors: Kang Yang, Yulong Shi, Guoqing Zhang, Jufeng Qiao, Fang Qian, Yanyan Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
For enhancing the tribological behavior of gear-driven systems, TA, AO, GNS, SiCf and Ti are used to prepare Ti-based composite foils that are subsequently combined with Ti2AlNb foils to prepare laminated samples for use in aviation gearing systems. Friction and wear tests are executed to demonstrate the optimum tribological behavior of TAGSTT, coupled with ideal reductions in the friction coefficient (57.89%) and wear rate (60.32%) relative to that of TT. These improved metrics are probably because the SiCf, GNS, TA and AO induce smaller nanocrystals in the lubrication film on the transition layer, which improves the self-healing ability of wear scars, prevents the wear interface from damage, and imparts excellent tribological behavior to the TAGSTT. These results provide a propagable method for the tribological design of drive-gear systems.

Introduction

Potential applications of aviation gears composed of Ti and Ti2AlNb alloys have attracted extensive attention. The power transmission of gear-driven systems of aero-engines is observably improved because of their low densities,1 high melting points,2 excellent oxidation resistance,3 good thermal conductivity,4 and superior process ability,5etc. Higher ductility (∼20%) and smaller yield strength (∼373 MPa) of Ti alloys have been reported6,7 compared with those of Ti2AlNb (3.5–8% and ∼775 MPa, respectively). However, poor combinations of their respective ductility and strength have hindered their potential use, especially in the meshing gears of aircraft engine systems, which experience dry sliding wear, and undermined the friction-reducing and wear-resistant behavior, thus restraining the power transmission of gear systems.8 For instance, Ti2AlNb-based gears are prone to brittle fracture with a sustained increase in friction and wear circles, due to their low ductility.9 This results in aeronautical safety risks. On the other hand, Ti alloy-based gears deliver poor power transmission because of premature plastic yielding owing to their low strength, crippling their use in powered aero-engines.10 Hence, combinations of Ti and Ti2AlNb are potentially beneficial for achieving excellent ductility and strength, which can be used to realize enhanced friction and wear behavior of gear systems, thus expanding the power transmission of aircraft engines.

In dry-friction processes, Ti-based alloys can easily undergo plastic deformation under an applied load, with a sliding friction coefficient and wear rate of 0.3–0.5 and 2.95–7.52 × 10−4 mm3 N−1 m−1, respectively.11,12 Furthermore, these dry-friction interfaces of Ti-based alloys produce surface damage under high loads, resulting in friction and wear properties that do not satisfy the industrial need for aero-gear lubrication. In order to solve the above-mentioned problem of Ti alloys, tribologists prepare cemented carbide coatings on the Ti alloys, which significantly reduce the friction coefficients and wear rates, thus achieving good lubrication.13,14 However, when larger or sudden loads are applied to the friction coatings, brittle fractures can form, leading to further damage. Particularly, the bonding interfaces between the coatings and their substrates are severely damaged, resulting in lubrication failure of the surface coatings. Simultaneously, it has been reported that a soft metal film on a high-hardness layer can easily produce the desired deformation and achieve a self-healing interface during wear;15 the high-hardness layer prevents the rapid propagation of cracks into the friction interface, protecting it from being destroyed, thus maintaining the excellent lubrication.16 However, under larger loads, damage to the soft metal film can easily occur, and effective self-repair is difficult, resulting in reduced lubrication. As can be seen from the above discussions, it is clearly important to ensure that the soft Ti alloys (3.2–4.7 GPa) are prepared on the high-hardness Ti2AlNb (approximately 7.5 GPa), as this is favorable for enhancing the lubrication behavior. Consequently, this represents an effective avenue for improving the tribological behavior of power-driven gears of aircraft engines. Nevertheless, the Ti surface can be damaged by sudden load increases or excessive loads, which impose restrictions on further improvements in lubrication behavior, thus restraining application expansion.

In order to enhance the damage resistance of Ti-based interfaces and maintain good lubrication, silicon carbide fibers (SiCf),17 graphene nanosheets (GNS),18 Al2O3 (AO) particles19 and Ti2AlC (TA)20 lubricants are introduced into the Ti alloy-based interface. The combination of higher hardness and strength is utilized for significant enhancement of damage resistance. For instance, the SiCf are used to construct a ceramic reinforced skeleton in the Ti-alloy (SiCf/Ti), thus supporting the damage resistance of the Ti based layer.21 GNS are applied to produce interlayer slipping, which effectively enhances the low-friction interface to protect it from wear damage, thus improving the lubrication behavior.22 Micro-/nano-particles of AO allow rolling friction; because of their near-spherical structures and higher hardness and strength, they effectively reduce friction resistance and surface damage, and increase interface lubrication.23 The Mn+1AXn (MAX) phases (TA) are added to the Ti-based alloy for interface healing; this is because the formed oxides, with their high relative volume expansion, can enhance the self-healing ability of a frictional interface,24 thereby imparting excellent lubrication. In addition to the inherent advantages of SiCf, GNS, AO and TA lubricants, their dimensions and morphologies also play important roles in regulating the lubrication performance. The SiCf, with a diameter of 60–80 µm and a length–diameter ratio of 30–120, can form continuous reinforcement frameworks that not only improve the mechanical properties of wear scars, but also provide a stable support for the lubrication film. Few-layer GNS (3–5 layers) with a lateral size of more than 500 nm, due to its ultra-thin and flexible structure, can easily spread on wear scars to form a uniform and low-shear lubrication film, thus reducing the friction and wear. Nano-scale AO particles (more than 50 nm) with spherical morphologies act as micro-rollers between the friction pairs, reducing adhesive wear and improving wear resistance. TA, with its layered structure and particle sizes of more than 3 µm, can decompose and release lubrication phases under friction heat, and its lamellar morphologies are conducive to the continuous formation and stability of the lubrication film.

Compared with the enhanced tribological properties of Ti-based composites, which are accomplished by traditional random particle dispersion strengthening technology, very few reports have explored the good synergistic effects of bionic hard–soft laminate samples that combine a SiCf skeleton, GNS low-shear lubrication, AO micro-rolling effects and TA self-healing lubrication. This multi-scale and multi-component collaborative design achieves adaptive lubrication regulation, which differs significantly from single-phase reinforced methods.

In this study, according to the biomimetic principle of shell micronano-structures with soft–hard interbedding lamination, the friction and wear behaviors of laminated samples of Ti–Ti2AlNb (TT), composed of Ti and Ti2AlNb layers, are mainly explored. Subsequently, SiCf, GNS, AO and TA are added into the TT Ti-layers to construct samples such as SiCf/Ti–Ti2AlNb (STT), GNS/SiCf/Ti–Ti2AlNb (GSTT), AO/GNS/SiCf/Ti–Ti2AlNb (AGSTT), and TA/AO/GNS/SiCf/Ti–Ti2AlNb (TAGSTT). The first step is to prepare the Ti-based composite sheets using raw powders of Ti, AO and GNS combined with SiCf. In the second step, the laminated sample with an alternating structure is accurately prepared with hot-pressing equipment from Ti-based composite sheets and commercial Ti2AlNb foil. Thereafter, the friction and wear behaviors of the Ti composite interfaces of the laminated samples are examined. Finally, the sliding friction and wear behavior of the Ti alloy-based interface is analyzed based on the respective lubrication mechanisms of TT, STT, GSTT, AGSTT and TAGSTT. By employing transmission electron microscopy (TEM), electron probe microanalysis (EPMA), and a surface profiler (model no. ST400), the formation mechanisms and surface morphologies of the lubrication films are analyzed in detail to study the self-regulated lubrication functions. The influence of the lubrication film on the tribological properties of the laminate sample is explored. The results obtained in this study provide useful data for increasing the application precision and service life of the aero-gear systems of aircraft engines.

To address the issue of the tribological behavior of Ti and Si3N4 gears, laminated samples of Ti–Ti2AlNb (TT), SiCf/Ti–Ti2AlNb (STT), GNS/SiCf/Ti–Ti2AlNb (GSTT), AO/GNS/SiCf/Ti–Ti2AlNb (AGSTT), and TA/AO/GNS/SiCf/Ti–Ti2AlNb (TAGSTT), were prepared using hot-pressed sintering. The main preparation procedures for the laminated samples are presented as follows.

Synthesis of Ti-based foils

In previous research,25 we observe that the main components of commercial foils are difficult to optimize, especially considering the difficulty of adding solid lubricants. Thus, the tribological properties are not ideal for industrial applications. In this study, Ti-alloy foils were prepared using raw powders of Fe, C, N, H, and O, and the balance of Ti (purity > 95.00%; size 100 nm to 600 µm), with nominal mass fractions (wt%) of 0.30, 0.10, 0.10, 0.10, 0.30 and 99.10. These powders were loaded into a vacuum mixer and vibrationally mixed at 200 rpm velocity and 45 Hz frequency for 24 h, to obtain homogeneous powders for the Ti-alloy foils. The aforementioned powders were respectively combined with GNS, AO/GNS, or TA/AO/GNS to prepare the Ti-based composite foils with a layer thickness of 120 µm; for example, GNS/Ti (GT), AO/GNS/Ti (AGT), and TA/AO/GNS/Ti (TAGT). The commercial powders of Ti, GNS, and AO, and the TA were purchased from Nanjing XFNANO Materials Tech Co., Ltd, and the SiCf was provided by Baoji Hongding Metal Material Co., Ltd.26,27

The contents of SiCf, GNS, TA, and AO were determined according to their functional characteristics, lubrication mechanisms, and possible side effects, such as agglomeration and interface deterioration. The SiCf were added to the Ti layers at a relatively high content to construct a load-bearing skeleton. The highly lubricating GNS was used at a low content to avoid interface weakening. TA and AO were added at moderate levels to achieve self-lubrication and micro-rolling effects, respectively. More than 30 tests were also carried out in the earlier studies, and the dosage of each component was optimized to realize a synergistic improvement of friction-reduction and wear-resistant properties. The main compositions of the Ti-based composite foils with respect to Ti, GT, AGT and TAGT are listed in Table 1. In accordance with the nominal mass fractions in Table 1, the powder mixture was respectively combined with preselected peptizing agents of pre-configured polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA). The nominal volume fractions of 70.00 vol% for the powder mixtures were matched with 30.00 vol% PMMA, and then stirred for 30 min to obtain a homogeneous mixture. Afterwards, the mixtures of PMMA and Ti-based powders were placed on a glass pane and repeatedly pushed and pressed into the flat sheets with approximately 120 µm thickness. In the next step, the flat sheets were loaded in a vacuum drying chamber (DZF-6020MBE) for 50 min, then the dried sheets were segregated into a large number of square slices with 25 mm in the side length, thus obtaining the GT, AGT and TAGT foils. Further, the SiC fiber cloths were adopted to combine two foils (Ti, GT, AGT, TAGT) for the effective synthesis of SiCf/Ti-based foils, such as ST, GST, AGST, TAGST, as shown in ST1, ST3 and ST5 of Fig. 2.

Preparation of the laminated samples

For illustrating the preparation process for the laminate samples, a typical synthesis schematic of STT is indicated in Fig. 2. As shown in Fig. 2, the Ti2AlNb foil (almost 100 µm thickness and more than 95.00% purity; Baoji Hongding Metal Material Co., Ltd) is mainly composed of Ti, Al and Nb elements, with a nominal atomic ratio (at%) of 53 : 22 : 25. A dried square sheet with 25 mm length was used as the ST-based foil. The morphologies before/after cleaning of the ST and Ti2AlNb (T) metal foils are shown in Fig. 2a–d. As can be seen for ST1 and ST2 in Fig. 2a–d, the Ti and Ti2AlNb foils were segmented into square samples with 25 mm in side length (see Fig. 2a and c), and then cleaned for 10 min using an ultrasound instrument that was sequentially loaded with alcohol, HF + HNO3 mixture and acetone. This cleaning process removed harmful oxides and oil impurities from the foils. The post-cleaning foils of Ti and Ti2AlNb were well-dried in an electrical vacuum oven, as shown in Fig. 2b and d, respectively.

Subsequently, SiC fiber cloth was used to combine two Ti-based foils for a good synthesis of SiCf/Ti foils, as shown in ST3 and ST5 of Fig. 2. After ST6, two SiCf/Ti foils are combined with a cleaned Ti2AlNb (T) slice (Fig. 2d) to construct a laminated unit of SiCf/Ti–Ti2AlNb (STT), as shown in Fig. 2h. Herein, the SiCf in this study was provided by Baimtec Material Co., Ltd. The SiCf cloth was braided in series with a braiding width of approximately 40 mm and an axial distance of approximately 200 µm to ensure uniform distribution on numerically controlled filament winding equipment, as shown in Fig. 2g. In the SiCf cloth fabrication process, one end of the SiCf is fixed on a circular roller with the diameter of more than 1000 mm, while the other end is attached to a movable trolley that can translate along the roller's axial direction. By coordinating the rotational speed of the roller and the moving speed of the trolley, the axial spacing between adjacent SiCf wound on the roller is precisely set to 200 µm. Subsequently, the peptizing agent of pre-configured polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) is sprayed onto the winding SiC fibers to secure their arrangement on the roller. After the PMMA adhesive is fully cured, the SiCf assembly encapsulated by the PMMA is cut using a blade to obtain SiCf cloth with a length of 600 mm and a width of 400 mm, as shown for ST4 in Fig. 2. The laminated units of STT are repeatedly overlaid, as shown in ST6, and shaped in the compact multilayer structures of the STT, as shown in Fig. 2h. From these discussions (Fig. 2), it can be summarized that the SiCf are well coated into the Ti foils to form SiCf/Ti layers, which are then combined with the Ti2AlNb foils to render a laminated unit of STT. The laminated units are then overlaid to shape the in-compact structure of a laminated sample (STT), as shown in Fig. 2h.

The preparation of the STT is shown in ST7 of Fig. 2. The in-compact structures (Fig. 2) were then sintered at high temperature, in a vacuum hot-pressing furnace (ZT-50-20Y), as shown in Fig. 3a. The sintering process of the in-compact STT is described in Fig. 3b–e. As shown in these figures, the loose laminate STT is loaded in a graphite mould, as noted in Fig. 3b, and then enclosed in a vacuum furnace for hot pressing (Fig. 3c). Finally, the sintering temperatures and pressures were applied for developing a sintering atmosphere for the STT, as shown in Fig. 3d. The temperatures and pressures are detailed in Fig. 3e. As shown in Fig. 3e, a low sintering rate of 3 °C min−1 was selected to control the temperature rise, which is beneficial for preventing PMMA carbonization. After 133 min, the temperature reaches 400 °C, and a continuous sintering occurs for 120 min, finally resulting in PMMA removal from the STT. The temperature is then increased to 935 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1, and the pressure is maintained at 35 MPa for 30 min; this accomplishes the in situ sintering of the STT. Following this, the sintering temperature is reduced to 700 °C after approximately 78 min at a cooling rate of 3 °C min−1. The cooling rate of 3 °C min−1 is adopted to facilitate the interface combination of the SiC fibers, Ti, and Ti2AlNb layers. Finally, the successful preparation of the STT is accomplished when the temperatures are reduced to room temperature, under natural cooling.

In a similar way, with references to important parameters, such as the heating and cooling rates, sintering pressures, and applied temperatures, the other samples used in the study, namely, TT, GSTT, AGSTT, and TAGSTT, were also prepared. The successful synthesis of these samples provides an important foundation for subsequent analysis of the micro–nano/structures and examination of their tribological behavior.

With reference to the XRD and EDS results, the TiC phase is identified as the main interfacial product that forms in the transition layer. It is mainly derived from the C and Ti reactions between the C coating and the Ti layer. This reaction is mainly because the quick combination of Ti and C atoms during the element inter-diffusion at 920 °C satisfies the dynamic and thermodynamic conditions for the chemical reaction of Ti and C atoms, leading to the TiC synthesis; the reaction can be expressed as Ti + C → TiC; ΔG° = −184.80 + 15.55 × 1373/1000 = −163.45 < 0. Herein, the Gibbs free energy ΔG0 has a value (−163.45) that is less than zero, which leads to the spontaneous reactions for the TiC synthesis. However, this reaction restricts the continuous diffusion of the C atoms further into the Ti layer, producing a narrow TiC layer between the C coating and the Ti layer.

As discussed above, the SiCf/Ti and Ti2AlNb foils are alternately overlaid to develop the 10-lamination units, which are placed in a vacuum furnace to successfully prepare the multi-layer samples. This is likely facilitated by the transformation of the hexagonal close-packed structures of α-Ti into the body-centered cubic lattice of β-Ti, resulting in the beneficial formation of the SiCf/Ti layer under high temperatures. Furthermore, a load of 35 MPa is used vertically on the SiCf/Ti–Ti2AlNb to induce a unidirectional motion, which allows the β-Ti to produce good layered combinations of SiCf/Ti and Ti2AlNb. High-temperature sintering causes atomic diffusion of the layers to form good combinations, thus realizing the successful preparation of the STT.

To explore the interface bonding of the SiCf/Ti and Ti2AlNb layers, typical FESEM morphologies showing the interface bonding abilities of the STT are shown in Fig. 5d–f. The magnified interface morphologies of the regions indicated by rectangles A and B in Fig. 5d are shown in Fig. 5e and f, respectively. As shown in Fig. 5d–f, during sintering, Ti atoms that are stored in the Ti layer migrate continuously to the Ti2AlNb layer, while the Al and Nb atoms migrate in the reverse direction, resulting in the formation of a good transition layer in the bonding interface. This transition layer is mainly composed of Ti, α + β biphasic structure, B2-rich and Ti2AlNb phases. The needle-like or lath-shaped α + β biphasic structure is first formed in the Ti/Ti2AlNb interface; this is due to the atomic diffusion of Al and Nb into the stable α and β phases of Ti, respectively. During the thermal fabrication, elements such as Al and Nb keep diffusing towards the Ti layer, and the strip-shaped O phases are distributed in the B2 phases, which are mainly dominated by Ti2AlNb, indicating a consistent orientation. Namely, the element diffusion of SiCf/Ti and Ti2AlNb prompts a solid-state reaction, leading to a good transition layer. In this layer, no micropores or surface gaps are detected, which ensures excellent bonding and provides a favorable connection of the SiCf/Ti and Ti2AlNb layers.

To explain the atom diffusion and the bonding interface, formation mechanisms that describe the diffusion interfaces of the SiCf/Ti and Ti2AlNb layers are indicated in Fig. 7. As shown in Fig. 7a, the SiCf/Ti layers are well overlaid onto the Ti2AlNb layers, making close contacts that lead to plastic deformation under large load. In a high-temperature sintering process, the atoms absorb a quantity of dynamic energy that leads to entering into a critical activation state in the SiCf/Ti and Ti2AlNb layers, due to their deformation energies, thus resulting in the inter-diffusion of atoms. With increased diffusion time, the Ti atoms show the well-observed inter-layer migration from the SiCf/Ti to Ti2AlNb, while the Al and Nb atoms undergo the opposite movements. Thus, more atoms undergoing interface inter-diffusion produce more contact areas of the bonding interface, resulting in needle-like or lath-shaped α + β biphasic structures between the SiCf/Ti and Ti2AlNb layers, as shown in Fig. 7b. A schematic of the atom diffusion mechanism forming the B2 phase in the bonding interface is shown in Fig. 7c. As shown in Fig. 7c, during the diffusion, due to the large diffusion coefficients, low activation energy and small relative atomic mass, the beneficial movement of Al atoms is more prone to a high energy-driven interstitial and vacancy diffusion, thus forming the B2-rich phase with abundant Nb and deficient Al near the Ti2AlNb layers, as shown in Fig. 7c. Afterwards, further diffusion occurs and the metallurgical bonding between the SiCf/Ti and Ti2AlNb layers is well-observed, because of continuous diffusion of Ti, Al, and Nb. This results in the growing thickness being dominated by a B2-rich phase, as noted in Fig. 7d, thus increasing the bonding abilities of the SiCf/Ti and Ti2AlNb interface.

Friction and wear tests of the laminated samples

According to an ASTM Standard G99-95,28 a reciprocating instrument (model no. MXW-1; Jinan Yihua Tribology Testing Technology Co., Ltd) was used to measure the sliding wear. Under a load of 20 N, the tribological behavior of the laminate samples was measured on a rectilinear tribo-system with the ball-on-flat instrument, at room temperature. In order to analyze the tribological properties of the frictional interaction between Si3N4-based and multi-layer-based gear samples, Si3N4 balls with a high hardness of 15.0 GPa were chosen as the friction pair for the laminate samples. The use of such a hard counterpart allows the friction and wear performance of the gear meshing pair to be evaluated under severe service conditions, which is beneficial for obtaining reliable and comparable experimental results. The friction and wear tests were performed at 50–55% humidity and 0.20 m s−1 speed. Sliding friction coefficients are continuously recorded for 80 min in the computer-controlled system. The equation W = (A × T)/(F × L) = V/(F × L), is applied for solving the wear rate W (mm3 N−1 m−1) of the as-prepared samples;29,30 wherein, main parameters of L, F, A, T and V are defined as the sliding distance (m), applied load (N), cross-sectional area (mm2), wear-scar perimeter (mm) and the wear-scar volume (mm3), respectively.

Results and discussions

A reciprocating and linear wear tester was used to evaluate the tribological behaviors of the laminated samples, when they are slid against fixed Si3N4 balls; typical friction coefficients and wear rates for 80 min of the laminated samples were recorded, and the results are shown in Fig. 9a and b, respectively. The SiCf were added into the Ti layers to prepare the STT, and these exhibited better friction and wear behaviors than those of the non-SiCf enhanced TT sample. Notably, the best tribological behavior among the above-mentioned samples was observed for TAGSTT. It was found that, after 5–10 min of sliding, the friction and wear behavior are stable, and this transition occurs earlier for the TAGSTT compared with the other samples. Meanwhile, the mean wear rates, as shown in Fig. 9c, of the Si3N4 balls sliding against the TAGSTT are observed to be smaller than those of the other samples. This is probably because the Ti2AlC (TA) consolidants are added to the AGSTT to prepare the TAGSTT, leading to the self-regulating lubrication functions of the wear scar, thereby achieving significant reductions in friction coefficients (57.89%) and wear rates (60.32%) relative to that of the TT sample, thus exhibiting the best friction and wear behavior.

Compared to the tribological behavior (friction coefficients of 0.3–0.5 and wear rates of 2.95–7.52 × 10−4 mm3 N−1 m−1) of homogeneous Ti-alloy based gears, the application advantages of the TAGSTT composites are significant, with the mean friction coefficient reduced to 0.13, and the wear rate showing a significant decrease to 1.00 × 10−4 mm3 N−1 m−1. This shows that TAGSTT has significant potential to replace the traditional Ti-alloy-based materials to enhance the service life and reliability of aviation gears.

This deformation effectively reduces the friction resistance and material loss, and allows the TAGSTT to achieve excellent lubrication self-regulation for a pairing wear scar. The results reveal the intrinsic microstructure–property relationship of the laminated composite.

Conclusions

This paper describes the successful preparation and tribological self-regulation of laminated SiCf/Ti–Ti2AlNb-based hybrids for reduced sliding friction and improved wear behavior of novel SiCf/Ti–Ti2AlNb-based gears, drawing the following conclusions.

(1) Ti powders combined with the TA, AO, GNS are used to prepare the Ti-based foils. Subsequently, two Ti-based foils are combined with the SiCf cloths to fabricate the SiCf/Ti foils. Further, the SiCf/Ti and Ti2AlNb foils are used to build the laminated units, which are overlaid to successfully prepare the laminated samples.

(2) Chemical reaction between Ti and C atoms creates TiC in a bonding interface between the Ti-based layer and the C coating. The solid-phase bonding interfaces between the Ti and Ti2AlNb layers consist of an α + β biphasic structure and a B2-rich phase, thus ensuring excellent connection between their layers.

(3) The tribological behaviors of the laminate samples are evaluated, and the results show that the best tribological behavior is achieved with TAGSTT. This is probably because the TA consolidants cooperate with the AO, GNS and SiCf to improve a lubricating self-regulation of the wear interface, thereby exhibiting the best tribological behavior.

(4) SiCf, GNS, TA and Ti induce smaller nanocrystalline dimensions, helping the lubrication film on the transition layer to achieve good self-regulation of friction and wear behavior, with the smallest friction coefficient and wear rate exhibited by TAGSTT.

(5) SiC, TA, GNS and AO improve the self-healing abilities of the wear scars and prevent them from being damaged; this is beneficial for the self-regulating tribological behavior of the TAGSTT, which exhibits the lowest friction coefficients and wear rates.
