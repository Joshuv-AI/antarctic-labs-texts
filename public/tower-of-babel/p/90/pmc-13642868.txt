Prevalence and Predictors of Anxiety among Tuberculosis Patients in India: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Janmejaya Samal, Manas Ranjan Behera, Ranjit Kumar Dehury, Nithya Shree, Benson Thomas, Magnus Michael Sichalwe, Hari Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAnxiety among TB patients can influence quality of life, treatment adherence, and outcomes. Understanding the prevalence and contributing factors of anxiety in TB patients can assist programme managers in improving patient outcomes.ObjectivesThis systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to identify factors associated with anxiety and to estimate the pooled prevalence of anxiety among Indian TB patients.MethodsData extraction and screening were conducted between February and March 2025 using four databases: Scopus, PUBMED, DOAJ, and EMBASE, along with cross-references; a total of 13 articles were included. These articles underwent quality assessment using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), the Modified-NOS, and the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool. Due to potential heterogeneity, a random effects model was applied to estimate the pooled prevalence of anxiety among Indian TB patients at a 95% confidence interval.ResultsFrom 160 identified articles, 13 were included for review, representing six Indian states. The combined sample in these 13 studies was 9368 (mean(SD) = 721(1910), median = 120, IQR = 100–272). The study estimated a pooled prevalence of anxiety of 34% (95% CI: 22%-47%), with substantial heterogeneity with I²=98.67%. Several factors contributing to anxiety in TB patients within the Indian population were identified and categorised under demographic, disease-related, social, psychological, and literacy factors.ConclusionsThe review outlined anxiety-associated factors and estimated the pooled prevalence among Indian TB patients; however, given the high heterogeneity, the pooled estimate should be interpreted cautiously. Considering the influence of anxiety at various stages of the treatment process, timely detection and management are crucial to improve TB outcomes.Keywords: Anxiety, India, Mental health, National TB Elimination Program, Tuberculosis

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is the leading cause of death attributable to a single infectious disease globally. It has resulted in nearly double the deaths compared to HIV/AIDS. Over 10 million individuals are anticipated to fall ill every year, with an upward trend observed since 2021 [1]. According to the World Health Organisation’s estimates, the incidence of TB is 134 (95% UI: 125–145) cases per 100,000 population worldwide [1] and 195 (95% UI: 164–228) cases per 100,000 population in India in 2023 [1, 2]. People with TB also have mental disorders, and this significantly impacts the treatment outcome of the patients [3]. Approximately 30% of the world’s adult population is documented to have experienced a common mental disorder during their lifetime, including anxiety and depression, which are the leading causes of morbidity in the world [4]. Reports suggest that the association of mental disorders are neither well-researched nor fully understood [3]. Moreover, TB and mental disorders disproportionately affect the vulnerable population [5–7]. Simultaneously, individuals with mental disorders exhibit a reduced likelihood of completing treatment due to low compliance or non-adherence to anti-TB drug regimens [8].

Furthermore, persons with TB can also acquire a mental disorder during the cascade of TB care through the adverse effects of TB drugs or simply through the response to the disease itself [8]. This can lead to social isolation and stigma. The prevalence of stigma can further lead to the occurrence of mental health disorders among these patients, causing a vicious cycle [9]. Nonetheless, approximately one in two people experience one or the other form of mental disorder after being diagnosed with TB, as evident from a systematic review [10]. These problems are more pronounced during the early phases of TB diagnosis and treatment [11, 12]. The fear of being a contagion, stigma, shame, and apprehension of the protracted duration of treatment, possible adverse effects, fear of being hospitalised time and again, causes anxiety symptoms to get pronounced during the early phase of the TB care cascade [13, 14]. Anxiety and related mental health disorders, if left untreated, can influence the treatment outcome of the TB patients in achieving remission [15]. Several observational studies have documented the prevalence of anxiety and related mental health disorders in the recent past and found that depression and anxiety disorders in TB patients are the leading causes of years lived with disability worldwide [16, 17]. However, systematic attempts to estimate the prevalence of anxiety are scarce [18]. Two recent systematic reviews [9, 19] have estimated the prevalence and factors related to anxiety in TB patients; however, the studies have not primarily focused on the Indian population and included limited Indian observational studies in the review. One of these two studies focused on the anxiety, stress and quality of life of TB patients and included only three studies from India [9], and the other study included seven studies; however, only two studies have reported the prevalence of anxiety in Indian TB patients and focused on both depression and anxiety [19]. Both of these studies focused on the global population. Given this background, a systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted to estimate the pooled prevalence of anxiety in Indian TB patients and assess the factors associated with the same among them.

Objective

The primary objectives of this systematic review and meta-analysis were to determine the pooled prevalence of anxiety among TB patients in India and to assess the determinants of anxiety in this population.

Methods

The reporting of this review adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [20], and the protocol has been registered with the PROSPERO database (CRD420251154885). This section outlines the data sources, search details utilised, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and the article selection process. The design and implementation of this systematic review and meta-analysis followed standardised methods established in the authors’ previous work, adhering to the PRISMA guidelines [21, 22].

Study Selection

In the first step, the duplicates were removed by screening titles and abstracts of the articles using Zotero 7.0.7 (Corporation for Digital Scholarship) [23]. After that, the first three authors (JS, MRB, RKD) independently reviewed the articles. During this independent review process, each of the three authors read the titles and abstracts, then the full texts of the articles, and the final inclusion of the articles was based on mutual consensus among the authors.

Eligibility Criteria

The studies were included based on the PICOS [Table 2] framework. Additionally, English language articles reporting the prevalence of anxiety in TB patients, primarily conducted in an Indian setting and published between 2004 and 2024, were incorporated into the review. The language was restricted to English, as scientific literature in India is mostly in English, with no use of regional languages.

Quality Assessment of Included Studies

The selected articles comprised observational and mixed-methods studies, including cross-sectional and cohort designs. The risk of bias in cohort and cross-sectional studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) and the Modified Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), respectively [24, 25]. The mixed-methods study was appraised using the Mixed Method Appraisal Tool (MMAT) [26]. Studies with a NOS score of 7–9 stars, 5–6 stars, or less than 5 stars were classified as having a low, moderate, or high risk of bias. Similarly, studies with a modified-NOS score of 6–7 stars, 4–5 stars, or less than 4 stars were classified as having a low, moderate, or high risk of bias. For the MMAT tool, studies were classified as having high, moderate, or low risk of bias if the score was 40% or less (0 to 2 criteria met), 60% (3 criteria met), or 80% to 100% (4 or 5 criteria met), respectively.

Statistical Analysis

Stata 18 (Stata Corp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) was utilized for analysis. A random effects model estimated the pooled prevalence of anxiety in Indian TB patients at a 95% confidence interval, assuming heterogeneity. The random effects model was adopted since demographics, contexts, timeframes, and study designs varied, indicating that anxiety prevalence would vary. The model accounts for within-study and between-study variability. Forest plots showed the pooled estimate, while funnel plots assessed publication bias. A leave-one-out meta-analysis was performed for sensitivity analysis because the Galbraith plot demonstrated heterogeneity. Small study effects were assessed using regression-based Egger’s and Begg’s rank correlation tests. The included studies were assessed for heterogeneity using the I² statistic and τ² (DerSimonian–Laird estimator, Freeman–Tukey-transformed proportion). Meta-regression and subgroup analysis were used to explain heterogeneity. The Q statistic and τ² values were used to assess model fit and residual heterogeneity after stratification.

Results

Study Selection

Out of the 160 articles obtained, 25 were detected as duplicates, and the automation tool retracted two articles. A total of 133 titles were examined, and 99 were eliminated. Thirty-four full-text articles were sought, three studies were found to be untraceable, and 18 studies were excluded for failing to meet the eligibility criteria. This resulted in 13 articles for the review and meta-analysis. Study Characteristics

All 13 studies were distributed across six Indian states, and the studies have used seven different study tools for the assessment of Anxiety in TB patients in the Indian population [Figure 2]. The commonly used tools were the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HARS), the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) and the General Anxiety Disorder (GAD) scales. Most of the studies are cross-sectional studies, with one mixed-method study and two longitudinal studies. All the studies have been published over the last decade (the earliest in 2015 and the most recent in 2025), with the highest and lowest sample sizes of 7056 and 41, respectively, both conducted in Gujarat. Of the 13 studies, five have not reported the determinants of anxiety in Indian TB patients. Table 4 provides the results of individual studies.

Result Synthesis

Characteristics of Studies

With the 13 included studies, the total sample size was 9368 (mean(SD) = 721(1910), Median = 120, IQR = 100–272). The minimum sample was 41, and the maximum sample was 7056, with both studies conducted in the state of Gujarat.

Quality Appraisal of the Included Studies

Table 4 present the scores for each study included and the corresponding risk of bias assessed. Eight studies were identified as having a low risk of bias, whereas five exhibited a moderate risk of bias. Among the cross-sectional studies, four and six studies were identified as having low and moderate risk of bias, respectively, and in the Cohort design, both studies were identified as having low risk of bias. The MMAT assessed the mixed-method study as having a low risk of bias.

Pooled Prevalence of Anxiety

The pooled prevalence of anxiety in Indian TB patients was 34% (95% CI: 22%−47%) [Figure 3]. Considerable heterogeneity (I²=98.67%) was found in the random-effects model. The studies had effect sizes ranging from 0.11 by Chauhan A et al. to 0.56 by Srinivasan G. The random effects model’s effect size (0.34) is significant (z = 8.63, p < 0.01), falling between lower and higher estimates from individual studies.

Forest plot showing the pooled prevalence of anxiety in TB patients in India

Subgroup Analysis

Three subgroup analyses were conducted. The first examined TB case categories, where the pooled estimate for DR-TB cases was 40% (95% CI: 26%−55%) compared to 31% (95% CI: 19%−45%) for all categories of TB patients [Figure 4]. In the second subgroup analysis, anxiety assessment tools were employed. Studies that used the HARS tool indicated the highest prevalence of 43% (95% CI: 32%−53%), while those that used the MINI instrument reported the lowest [Figure 5]. According to the third subgroup analysis that examined study designs, mixed method studies had the highest prevalence [45% (95% CI: 40%−50%)] and cohort studies had the lowest prevalence [21% (95% CI: 17%−27%)] [Figure 6].

Publication Bias

The funnel diagram [Figure 7], Doi plot [Figure 8], Egger’s test, Begg’s test, and trim-and-fill method [Table 5] examined publication bias. The asymmetric funnel diagram shows more studies on the right side of the pooled estimate. The Doi plot showed LFK = 4.68, indicating a major asymmetry. To validate this bias, Egger’s test yielded non-significant results (beta1 = 2.17, SE = 2.836, z = 0.77, p = 0.4437). Begg’s test was also not significant (Kendall’s tau = −19.00, SE = 16.279, z = −1.23, p = 0.2688). The funnel plot’s asymmetry may not be due to small study effects, but to heterogeneity. Trim-and-fill method results using a random-effects model and the DerSimonian-Laird approach corroborated the findings of Egger’s and Begg’s tests. The method recommended no additional study imputation, indicating publication bias is unlikely to alter the pooled estimate.

Heterogeneity

Figure 9 shows the Galbraith Plot for heterogeneity assessment. Chauhan et al., a high-precision outlier, was the only study outside the 95% confidence interval (CI). The included studies showed significant heterogeneity, with an I² statistics ≈ 99%. According to the Cochrane Handbook for systematic reviews of interventions, I2 values of 0–40% may not be significant, 30–60% may indicate moderate heterogeneity, 50–90% large, and 75–100% considerable heterogeneity. The tests for subgroup differences showed statistically significant results for two subgroup analyses based on study tools (Qb = 48.06, p < 0.001) and study designs (Qb = 41.24, p < 0.001), confirming that study designs and measurement tools contributed to heterogeneity. Additional unmeasured variables like sampling variability and respondent characteristics may also have contributed to the observed heterogeneity.

Galbraith Plot showing the heterogeneity

Meta Regression

A meta-regression analysis was carried out using the total sample, prevalence, types of TB cases, study tools and study design as moderators [Table 6]. Due to the limited number of included studies (n = 13) and to avoid the risk of overfitting, the multivariate meta-regression was avoided; instead, a univariate meta-regression for each of the moderators was conducted to find out the sources of substantial heterogeneity. For all the meta-regressions, the random effects model with the DerSimonian–Laird estimator was used. Of the five moderators, the total sample was found to be statistically significant; however, it was negatively associated with the effect size, indicating that a larger sample size is associated with a smaller effect size. The association of the total sample size with that of the effect size is also graphically represented through a bubble plot [Figure 10].

The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis [Figure 11] elucidated the impact of each study on the pooled estimate by systematically excluding one study at a time. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis estimated that 34% (95% CI: 22%−47%) of Indian TB patients had anxiety. Other global systematic reviews have approximated a similar range. A systematic review of 116 studies with 36,340 participants from 40 countries found that anxiety related to respiratory and sleep problems was 30.3% (95% CI: 27.9–32.9%, 10,679/36,340). The same study’s subgroup analysis found the highest anxiety prevalence among pulmonary TB patients (43.1% (95% CI: 32.6–54.3, 643/1489; n = 8)) [40]. Another global review of mental health issues, social stresses, and health-related quality of life in MDR-TB patients from 20 countries and 40 studies found 24% (95% CI: 2%−57%) of them had anxiety [41]. In a global sample of 8086 participants from 29 studies, anxiety prevalence was 32.54% [95% CI: 24.95, 41.18], similar to this review [9]. A systematic review of 40 studies on depression and anxiety in TB patients globally found a pooled prevalence of 28% (95% CI: 26–29) [19]. Similar to this review, various systematic reviews and meta-analyses estimated TB patients’ anxiety prevalence at 24% to 43.1%. Observational studies in diverse settings have found a similar prevalence. The prevalence was 39% (95% CI: 34.6%−44.6%) in 390 individuals in Gondar city health facilities, North West Ethiopia [42]. Another study in Wolita Sodo, Ethiopia, found 41.5% of 417 TB patients had anxiety disorders [11]. These variations in the prevalence of anxiety could be due to differences in the study population, which could range from all types of TB cases to drug-sensitive, drug-resistant and extrapulmonary TB cases. The variations are also attributed to the different tools being used, which the review found to be seven in number across the included studies. In addition, study design, sampling, cultural and regional factors also play a crucial role in the variation of prevalence.

Nonetheless, the range varied across different groups of TB patients as estimated in this review. The pooled estimate varied from 31% in all types of TB cases (both DR and DS TB cases) to 40% in DR-TB cases. Findings of other studies in different settings also support the higher prevalence among the drug-resistant TB cases. An Indonesian study among 69 MDR-TB cases, obtained through consecutive sampling, reported that 80% of them had anxiety [43]. Among 219 multidrug-resistant/Rifampicin-resistant TB cases in Guangzhou Chest Hospital in Southern China, 57.53% (n = 126) reported having anxiety [44]. Similarly, another Indonesian study among 102 MDR-TB cases in two hospitals in Banten province reported minimal to moderate anxiety in 46.1%, marked to severe anxiety in 33.3%, and the most extreme anxiety in 6.9% of cases [45]. Research indicates that patients with drug-resistant tuberculosis are threefold more likely to encounter adverse emotions, including anxiety and depression, largely attributable to extended treatment duration and significant treatment expenses [46]. The extent of depression and anxiety in patients undergoing therapy is anticipated to exhibit an “iceberg phenomenon,” in light of the rising incidence of multidrug-resistant/Rifampicin-resistant TB cases [44]. Regrettably, in clinical practice, the psychological alterations and needs of patients are often overlooked, potentially leading to adverse treatment outcomes, such as treatment cessation or attrition [47]. Consequently, healthcare professionals must promptly address the emotional well-being of drug-resistant TB patients to avert the worsening of adverse psychiatric outcomes [44]. The second subgroup analysis based on the study tools also found a range of estimations from 14% by MINI to 43% using HARS. The possible explanation for this could be due to the psychometric properties and the cut-off criteria used to define anxiety by these tools [21]. The tools have their inherent scope and limitations in assessing anxiety among a variety of study participants and settings. For instance, MINI is a gold standard structured tool with high reliability; however, it requires trained manpower to use the tool. One of the major limitations of this tool is that it only helps to meet the diagnostic criteria; however, it doesn’t help in quantifying the intensity of the illness, contrary to GAD-7, which helps to produce a score on a scale of 0–21 [48]. At the same time, GAD-7 only focus on general anxiety disorders and might miss the other anxiety disorders like phobias and panic attacks [49]. The review found a higher score in studies that used HARS, which is most likely due to the inclusion of somatic items that might inflate the score in medical illness, as in this case, TB [50]. Furthermore, the review also found that the studies that used cross-sectional studies estimated a higher prevalence (35%) compared to the studies that used the cohort study (21%); however, one study that used the mixed method has estimated the highest (45%). The variations are most likely due to the study designs and the potential biases [51].

The review further assessed the associated factors of anxiety in Indian TB patients. These were primarily related to the disease, psychological factors, and demographic and literacy-related factors. Studies in different settings have also reported similar factors. A facility-based study among 410 pulmonary TB cases in Southwest Ethiopia reported that being female, perceived stress, and family history of mental illness were found to have significant associations with anxiety in TB patients [52]. The study in North West Ethiopia in Gondar city health facilities, among 390 patients, reported that coexisting chronic diseases and perceived stigma were found to have significant associations with anxiety in TB patients [42]. A study in Thailand among 210 TB patients found that mental health problems were significantly associated with low family support and high perceived stigma [53]. Female gender is a strong predictor of anxiety in TB patients, as reported in the reviewed studies. Studies report that female patients have a 2.4 times higher likelihood of having anxiety compared to their male counterparts [11]. Both biological factors, such as pregnancy and social factors, such as household-level responsibilities, were associated with a higher prevalence of anxiety in women compared to men [54, 55]. The reviewed studies reported that the phases of treatment, especially the intensive phase of treatment, significantly contribute to the prevalence of anxiety. This is predominantly related to the learning of recent diagnoses and prominent signs and symptoms during the early phases, which wane after 1–2 months of treatment initiation [56, 57]. Social isolation and perceived stigma have a strong association with anxiety in TB patients. Studies report that there is a 3.58 times higher likelihood of anxiety in patients with perceived stigma [11]. This review estimated the pooled AOR of [2.94, 95% CI: 2.25–3.85], suggesting a 2.94 times higher odds of anxiety in patients with perceived stigma. Patients with high perceived stigma tend to socially isolate themselves with the fear of spreading the disease and may also have low self-image, which may predispose them to anxiety [11, 58]. Social support is paramount in anxiety in TB patients, and is lower among patients with strong social support. Inadequate social support leads patients to perceive themselves as neglected, isolated, and worthless, in stark contrast to robust social support [37]. Medication side effects are also a significant predictor of anxiety in TB patients, as reported by reviewed studies. Among 135 Nepali MDR-TB patients, the anxiety score was 1.59 points higher (95% CI: 0.45–2.73) in those who exhibited physical side effects of treatment than those who did not [59].

Given the understanding of the anxiety factors associated with TB patients in India, specific interventions need to be implemented to address anxiety for better treatment outcomes. Female patients require greater emotional support, which is often neglected in Indian societies. Disease factors, such as side effects, should be addressed promptly to improve adherence and compliance. Most importantly, psychological assessments should be conducted for TB patients undergoing treatment, which are frequently overlooked by primary care physicians. Anxiety in TB patients can be alleviated with self-efficacy and self-esteem, as both appear to significantly influence anxiety levels in these patients. Self-efficacy refers to the conviction that one can successfully plan and execute activities effectively [60]. High self-efficacy among patients fosters a more optimistic view of treatment, thereby aiding recovery and enhancing their quality of life, which directly encourages treatment completion [61]. Conversely, self-esteem can mitigate the adverse impacts of stigma on anxiety. Consequently, bolstering self-esteem as a strategy to alleviate the impacts of stigma on anxiety can enhance mental health in TB patients and diminish the harmful effects of anxiety within this population [62].

The study encountered a power-sensitivity discrepancy coupled with high heterogeneity, with I² ≈ 99%. Most importantly, the pooled estimate of 0.34 remained stable, as shown by the leave-one-out meta-analysis, confirming its robustness since removing any single study did not significantly change the pooled estimate. However, significant between-study heterogeneity and publication bias were detected. Although Egger’s and Begg’s tests were not statistically significant, this is likely due to low statistical power resulting from the inclusion of only 13 studies. The Doi plot and the LFK index of 4.68 indicated substantial asymmetry supported by the funnel plot. Additionally, the Galbraith plot flagged one study, Chauhan et al., as a high-precision outlier. The meta-regression demonstrated that sample size contributed to significant variability. As a result, although the overall effect appears robust, the high heterogeneity suggests that the findings must be interpreted with caution.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

The strengths of the study lie in its systematic approach, adhering to the PRISMA guidelines and including studies conducted in the Indian population, encompassing the most recent observational studies. The review estimated the pooled prevalence of anxiety associated with TB and also assessed the factors predicting it. However, several limitations exist in this review as well. High heterogeneity was observed, which may limit the reliability of the pooled estimate. Additionally, the reported sample sizes across the individual studies ranged from 41 to 7056, which could introduce bias and reduce comparability. Moreover, the incorporation of grey literature could have enhanced the study’s credibility; however, this was impracticable as grey material is typically not listed in the databases utilised for this systematic review. Furthermore, the search technique is diverse and labour-intensive; thus, only published literature was employed for the review. Additionally, although there are specialised databases such as PsycINFO that are commonly used for mental health research, this research did not include these databases, as original research on anxiety among TB patients is predominantly indexed in biomedical databases. Furthermore, there is a considerable overlap in the indexing of articles among EMBASE, Scopus, and PsycINFO, minimising the likelihood of missing relevant studies.

Implications of the Study

The review has the following implications: (1) Anxiety-specific interventions: The review estimated a pooled prevalence of anxiety, which can guide policymakers and health administrators in designing integrated services for timely management of anxiety in TB patients. (2) Creating awareness about TB disease and its services: Raising awareness about the disease and its services at different stages of the TB care cascade can help patients seek help promptly, which would improve TB outcomes. (3) Focus on social determinants of health: The review identified numerous social determinants contributing to anxiety, which need to be addressed through multisectoral collaboration and institutional support that aligns with efforts to eliminate TB in India.

Conclusions

The review estimated the prevalence of anxiety in TB patients and identified the factors associated with it. It found that social and demographic determinants, psychosocial factors, and literacy levels are critical in the occurrence of anxiety among TB patients. The findings underscore that anxiety can significantly influence outcomes in TB patients, emphasising the need for a multi-faceted approach and institutional support. The review recommends designing tailored interventions to detect anxiety promptly, raising awareness about TB and its services, and enhancing psychosocial support for TB patients. Furthermore, the social determinants contributing to anxiety should be addressed through policy-level and institutional support to aid in TB elimination. Since a high heterogeneity is observed, it is recommended to interpret the pooled estimate with caution.
