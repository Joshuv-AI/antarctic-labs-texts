Tetanus risk and prevention after Venezuela's earthquake doublet: protecting survivors and responders
Authors: Alfonso J. Rodríguez-Morales, Alexandra E. Sierra-Pelaez, Claudia Beltrán-Arroyave, Alejandro Risquez, Marbelys Hernández
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

On June 24, Venezuela was struck by two consecutive high-magnitude earthquakes (7.2 and 7.5), leading to devastating consequences affecting La Guaira, Caracas, and multiple states of the country (https://www.unicef.org/documents/venezuela-humanitarian-situation-report-no2-earthquake-27-june-2026). The public health response to Venezuela's recent earthquake doublet must extend beyond trauma care, shelter, water and sanitation, and surveillance of communicable diseases. Tetanus prevention deserves specific and immediate attention. Although tetanus is not transmitted from person to person and should not be framed as an inevitable “post-earthquake epidemic,” earthquakes create precisely the circumstances in which preventable cases may occur: crush injuries, puncture wounds, burns, contaminated lacerations, delayed wound care, disrupted vaccination records, and limited access to tetanus-containing vaccines and tetanus immune globulin. The potential severity of tetanus is particularly concerning when critical-care capacity is disrupted or overwhelmed [1].

The doublet earthquake also offers an opportunity to strengthen routine protection beyond immediate post-exposure prophylaxis. WHO/UNICEF estimates indicate a marked decline in Venezuela's routine diphtheria–tetanus–pertussis (DTP) vaccination coverage over the past decade: DTP3 coverage decreased from 81% in 2012 to 54% in 2023, after reaching a low of 43% in 2022 (Fig. 1) (https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/country-profiles/immunization/2024-country-profiles/immunization-2024-ven.pdf) [2]. Although DTP1 and DTP3 coverage are not direct measures of tetanus immunity among adults, rescue personnel, or health-care workers, they indicate persistent gaps in the delivery of tetanus-containing vaccines and reinforce the importance of risk-based wound prophylaxis and catch-up vaccination in the aftermath of the earthquake. Displaced families may have lost vaccination cards, and many adults may be uncertain about previous doses. Outreach services should use this moment to reconstruct records when feasible and to provide catch-up vaccination, particularly for children, adolescents, pregnant women, and adults with incomplete schedules. Risk-based tetanus prophylaxis after an earthquake: practical scenarios for affected populations and responders.

For the purposes of wound prophylaxis, a complete primary series consists of at least three documented doses of a tetanus toxoid-containing vaccine; fewer than three doses constitute an incomplete primary series. An unknown vaccination history should be managed as incomplete.

Dirty or major wounds include contaminated wounds, puncture wounds, crush injuries, burns, compound fractures, wounds with devitalized tissue, and wounds containing soil, dust, debris, feces, or foreign material. Tetanus-containing vaccine refers to an age-appropriate formulation, such as DTaP, Tdap, or Td. Antibiotics should not be used solely to prevent tetanus. An interactive, downloadable file that does not require an internet connection has been developed to help with immunization decisions during the earthquake aftermath in Venezuela-affected areas (Decision Tree: Post-Earthquake Immunization [in Spanish, Árbol de Decisión: Inmunización Post-Terremoto], https://osf.io/q35z2/overview). According to medical volunteers deployed in the field, neither tetanus vaccination certification nor certification for other vaccines was requested by Venezuelan authorities upon arrival during the first two weeks of the disaster.

The tetanus risk is driven by exposure of injured tissue to environmental spores of Clostridium tetani, not by rust itself. Soil, dust, debris, animal waste, contaminated water, devitalized tissue, and foreign material may all contribute to tetanus-prone wounds. This distinction is important for risk communication. Public messages should encourage all persons with wounds caused by rubble, glass, wood, metal, or contaminated materials to seek early clinical evaluation, rather than promoting indiscriminate vaccination based solely on exposure to “rusty metal.” They should also clarify that a previous episode of tetanus does not confer protective immunity and that vaccination remains necessary when indicated [1,3].

For affected populations, tetanus prevention should be integrated into emergency wound management. Immediate irrigation, cleaning, removal of foreign material, and debridement of devitalized tissue are essential first steps. Vaccination decisions should then be based on wound characteristics and documented immunization history. Persons with an unknown vaccination history or an incomplete primary series (<3 documented doses) should receive a tetanus-containing vaccine (Table 1). At the same time, those with completed schedules may require a booster depending on the type of wound and the interval since their last dose. In general, clean and minor wounds warrant a booster when the last dose was received 10 or more years earlier, whereas contaminated, puncture, crush, burn, or other major wounds require a booster after a shorter interval, commonly five years [1,3,4].

Tetanus immune globulin must also be available for people at highest risk. It should be considered for contaminated or major wounds in persons with an unknown vaccination history or an incomplete primary series (<3 documented doses), and should be administered to persons with HIV infection or severe immunodeficiency regardless of their prior tetanus immunization history. Vaccines and immune globulin serve different functions: vaccines induce active, durable protection, whereas immune globulin provides immediate passive protection. In overwhelmed health services, access to the latter may be overlooked, yet its timely use can be lifesaving [4].

Antimicrobial therapy should not be used as a substitute for appropriate wound care, vaccination, or immune globulin. Antibiotics may be clinically indicated for established wound infection, extensive tissue injury, or other bacterial complications, but they do not replace tetanus prophylaxis. This point is particularly relevant during disasters, when empiric antibiotic use can become common despite scarce supplies and the risk of antimicrobial resistance [5].

Rescue personnel, health-care workers, volunteers, military personnel, and humanitarian responders also require explicit protection. Their risk does not arise simply from working in an earthquake-affected setting, but from the possibility of penetrating injury, contact with debris, and delayed access to care during prolonged operations. Before deployment, responders should have their tetanus vaccination history reviewed and complete any missing doses in accordance with national recommendations. Those with documented, up-to-date protection do not require automatic revaccination merely for entering an affected area. However, pre-deployment assessment provides a critical opportunity to identify incomplete schedules, unknown histories, or overdue boosters. Personal protective measures, including closed footwear, gloves, durable clothing, and appropriate wound-reporting procedures, should complement immunization [1,5].

At the operational level, tetanus prevention should be incorporated into emergency medical posts, mobile clinics, hospitals, and shelter-based health services. A simple triage algorithm, based on wound classification, verification of tetanus immunization history, administration of a tetanus-containing vaccine when indicated, selective use of tetanus immune globulin for eligible high-risk wounds, documentation, and arrangements for completion of the primary series or future boosters, can facilitate consistent decisions across affected populations and responders (Table 1). Maintaining vaccine cold chains, sufficient supplies of tetanus-containing vaccines and immune globulin, safe injection materials, and referral pathways should therefore be part of early disaster logistics [1,4,5].

Clinical teams should remain alert to compatible symptoms, including trismus, dysphagia, painful muscle rigidity, spasms, or autonomic instability following a recent wound. A suspected case of tetanus should prompt urgent clinical management and a public health investigation into missed opportunities for prevention. Each case represents not only a severe individual event but also a warning of gaps in wound care, access to immunizations, or emergency preparedness [4,5].

Finally, in Venezuela, tetanus prevention should be regarded as a core component of earthquake response [1,2]. The most effective approach is neither complacency nor indiscriminate vaccination, but timely wound care, targeted active and passive immunization, protected supplies, trained health personnel, and pre-deployment preparedness for those assisting affected communities. These actions can prevent a rare but potentially fatal complication from becoming an avoidable second tragedy.
