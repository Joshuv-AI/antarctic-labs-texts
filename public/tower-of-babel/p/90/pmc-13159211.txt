Fungal–bacterial interaction networks in lignocellulose degradation: an integrated ecological and mechanistic perspective
Authors: Yun-Yeong Lee, Jeonghee Yun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lignocellulosic biomass (LCB) degradation is the process by which fungi and bacteria contribute complementarily at multiple organizational levels. This review integrates their enzymatic, ecological, and metabolic roles, and suggests the linkage structure between the microbial community dynamics and the design of synthetic microbial consortia. Fungi and bacteria form functionally coordinated interactions involving structural modification of substrates, microenvironmental regulation, and the continuity of metabolic pathways. These interactions enable degradation efficiencies that are not achievable under monoculture conditions. Recent omics-based studies conducted in LCB-rich environments show that this cooperative relationship is commonly observed in natural ecosystems. Furthermore, analyses of enzyme gene distributions and functional roles further indicate that key microbial members can be selected to construct simplified consortia without reproducing the full complexity of natural communities. Therefore, this review highlights a shift from viewing LCB degradation as a set of isolated enzymatic steps or single-strain activities to understanding it as a network-level biological process shaped by fungal–bacterial interactions. This perspective can provide a theoretical foundation for the rational design and optimization of microbial consortia in future bioconversion applications.

Introduction

Global energy demand continues to rise with industrialization and population growth, and fossil fuels remain the dominant energy source, raising major sustainability concerns [1, 2]. In South Korea, more than 70% of total energy consumption still depends on fossil fuels as of 2022 [3]. Accordingly, renewable bioresources such as plant-derived biomass are being actively explored as sustainable alternatives [4]. Among them, lignocellulosic biomass (LCB) is receiving great attention due to its abundance, renewability, and potential as a feedstock for bio-based fuels and chemicals [4].

LCB can be derived from a variety of plant-based materials, including agricultural residues, forestry by-products, and industrial lignocellulosic waste [4]. The utilizationn of LCB not only supports sustainable energy production, but also mitigates the environmental impacts associated with fossil fuel use [1, 5]. Structurally, LCB is composed of three major biopolymers—cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, which together form a highly recalcitrant matrix resistant to microbial degradation [4]. Typically, plant-based materials are composed of 35–50% cellulose, 20–35% hemicellulose, and 5–30% lignin, along with minor constituents such as acetyl groups, phenolics, and minerals [1, 5]. This intrinsic recalcitrance limits enzymatic accessibility and efficiency, often necessitating pretreatment steps to enhance subsequent microbial and enzymatic conversions [1, 5].

After partial deconstruction of the lignocellulosic matrix, microbial hydrolysis converts polysaccharides into fermentable sugars, which are essential intermediates in bioenergy and bioproduct generation [6]. This conversion process is schematically illustrated in Fig. 1. Fungi and bacteria constitute the primary biological agents responsible for LCB degradation. Filamentous fungi, including Trichoderma, Aspergillus, Penicillium, and Fusarium, secrete complex cellulolytic and hemicellulolytic enzyme systems, often accompanied by oxidative enzymes involved in lignin modification [7, 8]. Bacterial taxa, such as Streptomyces, Bacillus, Pseudomonas, Clostridium, and Ruminococcus, contribute through free extracellular enzymes or cell-surface-associated multi-enzyme complexes, including cellulosomes [6, 9]. Recent publication trends have further emphasized the need for an integrated perspective that combines microbial ecological interactions with mechanistic enzymic analysis to explain functional complementarity during lignocellulose degradation. As shown in Fig. 3a, the overall number of studies on LCB degradation has increased steadily over the past 20 years, reflecting ongoing scientific and industrial interest. Both fungal and bacterial degraders have been actively investigated. Fungal systems have maintained a steady and foundational presence in the field. In comparison, bacterial studies have expanded significantly since the early 2010s, highlighting their diverse and specialized metabolic roles within complex communities. On the other hand, studies that explicitly address fungal–bacterial co-cultures and omics-based systems analysis are relatively limited, despite their importance in understanding functional complementarity and designing optimized microbial consortia. Consistent with this trend, the keyword-based research landscape indicates that the current work mainly focuses on substrate pretreatment and enzymatic depolymerization processes (Fig. 3b). In contrast, terms associated with microbial community interactions, co-culture systems, and synergistic degradation mechanisms appear much less frequently. These patterns indicate that while the enzymatic mechanisms of individual microbial taxa are relatively well-characterized, the cooperative interactions that enable efficient LCB degradation in natural and engineered ecosystems are still insufficiently explored. Therefore, the present review brings together current knowledge of the fungal and bacterial mechanisms of LCB degradation, with a particular focus on: (1) enzymatic strategies employed by fungi and bacteria; (2) synergistic interactions within fungal–bacterial consortia; (3) ecological and functional dynamics revealed by metagenomic and multi-omics approaches; and (4) the rational design of an optimized synthetic consortium for industrial bioconversion. By organizing lignocellulose degradation into an integrated microbial interaction network, this review highlights system-level principles that guide both basic research and next-generation co-culture platform development.

Methodology

This review was conducted based on a systematic literature search using the Web of Science as the primary database, with Scopus and PubMed used as supplementary sources. The search strategy employed Web of Science Topic Search (TS), which retrieves records based on terms appearing in the title, abstract, and keywords. A common baseline query was first applied to capture studies related to lignocellulosic biomass degradation and conversion, using combinations of lignocellulose-related terms and degradation- or conversion-related terms connected by Boolean operators (AND/OR). For example, TS = (“lignocellulose” OR “cellulose” OR “hemicellulose” OR “lignin”) AND TS = (“degrad*” OR “depolymeri*” OR “bioconversion” OR “biodegrad*” OR “saccharif*” OR “hydrolys*”). This baseline dataset was used to generate the total publication trend shown in Fig. 3a.

For subgroup analyses, publications were subsequently categorized by applying additional topic-based filters to the same baseline dataset. These filters included microbial descriptors such as “fungi” or “fungal”, “bacteria” or “bacterial”, as well as descriptors of mixed or community-based systems such as “co-culture”, “consortium”, “consortia”, “mixed culture”, and related terms. Omics-related studies were identified using topic filters such as “metagenomics”, “multi-omics”, or “sequencing”. These keyword combinations were used to categorize publications into thematic subgroups for trend analysis (Fig. 3a), rather than to define independent or title-based search pools.

In addition, a keyword-based trend analysis was conducted to examine recent research directions. For the wordcloud visualization (Fig. 3b), publications published between 2023 and 2025 were selected to capture recent trends in fungal–bacterial lignocellulose degradation research. Author-provided keywords were collected from 1000 publications, yielding 4498 keyword occurrences for analysis.

All keywords were manually curated to ensure consistency, including unification of spelling, capitalization, abbreviations, and synonymous expressions. Generic terms shared across all publications, such as “lignocellulose”, were excluded to avoid distortion of the wordcloud. The curated keywords were subsequently categorized into five thematic groups: pretreatment (blue), bioprocess/bioenergy (light green), microbial systems (pink), lignocellulosic components (green), and enzymes/depolymerization (orange). All analyses and visualizations were performed in R (v. 4.4.2). The packages “writexl” and “readxl” were used for data import and export, while “tidyverse” packages were used for data structuring and preprocessing. Wordcloud visualization was generated using “ggplot2” combination with “ggwordcloud”.

Overview of lignocellulose structure

LCB forms the major structural framework of plant cell walls and is composed of three major components: cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin [1, 5]. These polymers are strongly interconnected, forming a rigid, biologically recalcitrant matrix. Among them, lignin acts as a protective layer that surrounds cellulose and hemicellulose, restricting enzymatic access to polysaccharides. These structural barriers are a major cause of poor biodegradability without pretreatment [10]. Despite these complexities, microorganisms in natural ecosystems gradually depolymerize LCB, contributing to the global carbon cycle. Fungi and bacteria are the dominant biological degraders, each utilizing distinct enzymatic systems to attack cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin [10, 11]. Cellulose

Cellulose is a linear polysaccharide composed of β-1,4-linked glucose units, and it forms the most abundant carbohydrate in plant biomass [12]. Cellulose includes highly aligned crystalline regions and less aligned amorphous regions, the latter of which is more susceptible to enzymatic hydrolysis [10]. The degradation of cellulose is catalyzed mainly by the cellulase complex, consisting of three enzyme classes: endoglucanase (EG), cellobiohydrolase (CBH), and β-glucosidase (BGL) [10, 11]. EG cleaves internal β-1,4-glycosidic bonds, resulting in new chain ends (Fig. 4a). CBH acts on these ends, releasing cellobiose, and BGL subsequently hydrolyzes cellobiose to glucose as the final step [13, 14].

Hemicellulose

Hemicellulose is a branched polysaccharide composed of diverse sugar monomers, and its structure varies among plant taxa [10]. Xylan is predominant in hardwood and herbaceous plants, whereas softwood plants contain large amounts of galactoglucomannan [15]. Xylan consists of a β-1,4-linked xylose backbone with side chains of arabinose, glucuronic acid, or acetic acid (Fig. 4a). Galactoglucomannan is a β-1,4-linked copolymer of glucose and mannose with galactose substitutions [15]. Because of this heterogeneity, hemicellulose hydrolysis requires multiple enzymes acting cooperatively. Key enzymes include endo-β-1,4-xylanase, β-xylosidase, β-mannosidase, and α-L-arabinanase [15, 16].

Lignin

Lignin is a complex, three-dimensional aromatic polymer produced by oxidative polymerization of monolignols such as coniferyl, sinapyl, and p-coumaryl alcohols, which are derived from phenylpropanoids [8]. Ether linkages among these subunits generate dense and heterogeneous networks. Lignin is the most recalcitrant component of LCB and binds cellulose and hemicellulose firmly, thus reducing enzyme accessibility [10]. The degradation of lignin proceeds through high-redox-potential oxidases rather than glycoside hydrolases. The major ligninolytic enzymes include laccase, manganese peroxidases (MnP), and lignin peroxidase (LiP) [10, 17].

Fungal strategies for LCB degradation

Fungi play a central role in the biological degradation of LCB. They recycle woody materials in natural ecosystems and provide valuable enzymatic resources for industry [10]. LCB-degrading fungi are typically classified as soft-rot, brown-rot, or white-rot fungi, depending on their degradation mechanisms and substrate specificities. These taxa mainly belong to the Ascomycota and Basidiomycota [11, 17]. Soft-rot fungi, mainly Ascomycetes, degrade cellulose and hemicellulose efficiently but have limited lignin degradation capacity. Genera such as Aspergillus, Trichoderma, and Neurospora are representative examples widely used in laboratories and industries due to their rapid growth and high enzyme productivity [17, 18]. Brown-rot fungi, consisting of Basidiomycetes, rapidly depolymerize cellulose and hemicellulose but only slightly modify lignin. Representative species include Gloeophyllum trabeum, Coniophora puteana, and Postia placenta [19]. White-rot fungi, such as Phanerochaete chrysosporium, Pleurotus ostreatus, Botrytis cinerea, and Trametes versicolor, degrade all LCB components and are capable of complete delignification [17, 18].

Cellulose degradation

Among fungal degraders, filamentous fungi have been studied most intensively because of their strong protein secretion ability and affinity for solid substrates [20]. The core cellulase system consists of EG (EC 3.2.1.4), CBH (EC 3.2.1.91), and BGL (EC 3.2.1.21) [11]. However, recent studies have highlighted the importance of accessory enzymes, including oxidative and non-catalytic proteins. In particular, lytic polysaccharide monooxygenases (LPMOs) have emerged as important oxidative enzymes for cleaving crystalline cellulose via copper-oxyl intermediates [37]. Genomic analyses have revealed that the LPMO genes are widely distributed across filamentous fungi [38]. In addition, supplementation of LPMOs to commercial cellulase cocktails has been reported to significantly increase the saccharification yields [39, 40]. For instance, LPMOs from Chaetomium thermophilum and Penicillium funiculosum exhibited enhanced activity under high-temperature and high-solids conditions [41, 42]. Similarly, LPMOs of Thermothielavioides and Thermoascus enhanced hydrolysis when combined with cellulases derived from Trichoderma reesei [43, 44]. Recently, a novel LPMO from Talaromyces rugulosus was found to oxidize both cellulose and xylan, indicating bifunctional activity [45].

In addition, non-catalytic proteins, such as expansins and swollenins, exhibit synergistic effects with cellulases. Expansins, originally found in plants, disrupt hydrogen bonding within cell-wall polymers to increase enzyme accessibility [46]. Likewise, fungal expansins can enhance cellulose hydrolysis when combined with commercial cellulase preparations [47]. Swollenin from T. reesei increased corn-stover saccharification by more than 70% in combination with CBH [48], whereas swollenins from Penicillium species improved crystalline cellulose hydrolysis by almost 50% [49]. Furthermore, the cerato-platanin protein (CPP) from Fusarium graminearum specifically bound to cellulose, exhibiting a promoting effect similar to that of expansin [50]. These results indicate that fungal cellulose degradation extends beyond the conventional EG–CBH–BGL system. Accessory oxidative and structural-modifying proteins can operate cooperatively to maximize hydrolytic efficiency in industrial processes.

Hemicellulose degradation

Hemicellulose is structurally heterogeneous and consists of branched polysaccharides, such as xylan, mannan, and arabinan. Therefore, the degradation of hemicellulose requires multiple enzymes acting simultaneously [15]. The major hemicellulases include endo-β-1,4-xylanase (EC 3.2.1.8), β-xylosidase (EC 3.2.1.37), β-mannosidase (EC 3.2.1.25), α-L-arabinanase (EC 3.2.1.99), and α-galactosidase (EC 3.2.1.22) (Table 3). Among them, endo-β-1,4-xylanase hydrolyzes β-1,4-xylosidic linkages in arabinoxylan, while β-xylosidase converts the resulting oligosaccharides into xylose [51]. Several fungi exhibit remarkable hemicellulolytic activity. For example, Chrysosporium lucknowense C1 expressed multiple xylanase and arabinofuranosidase genes, whose purified enzymes exhibited high activity toward arabinoxylan [52]. Similarly, Thermomyces lanuginosus produced thermostable xylanases in the absence of cellulase activity [53]. Moreover, endophytic fungi such as Aspergillus niger and Trichoderma atroviride showed strong xylanase and arabinanase activities, and Annulohypoxylon and Talaromyces species showed high mannosidase activity [54].

However, in the natural LCB matrix, cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin are tightly intertwined, so hemicellulases alone are not sufficient. Therefore, many studies have emphasized the synergistic interaction between cellulases and hemicellulases. Filamentous fungi secrete both enzyme groups, and their coordinated secretion is essential for lignocellulose conversion. For instance, T. reesei is known to include enzymes acting on both cellulose and hemicellulose backbones [55]. Genome analyses of T. reesei have identified 39 genes encoding cellulases and hemicellulases, including novel β-glucosidases, xylosidases, and mannosidases [55]. It was reported that isolates of Aspergillus and Penicillium from forest soils secreted both enzyme types when cultured on rice straw or wheat bran [51]. Furthermore, co-cultivation of A. niger and Trichoderma viride on agricultural residues revealed high enzyme titers [56]. Transcriptional regulators, such as XlnR (Xyr1) of A. niger and T. reesei, regulate both xylanase and cellulase expression, suggesting a close genetic relationship between the two systems [57]. These findings indicate that the integrative regulation of cellulolytic and hemicellulolytic enzymes in filamentous fungi enhances efficient hemicellulose hydrolysis.

Lignin degradation

Lignin degradation is predominantly performed by Basidiomycetes, especially white-rot fungi, via high-redox oxidative enzymes. Among them, laccase oxidizes phenolic lignin units, and numerous white-rot species are strong laccase producers [17, 58]. For example, co-cultivation of Ceriporiopsis, Physisporinus, Phanerochaete and Pleurotus significantly enhanced laccase activity and wood degradation compared to monocultures [59]. Moreover, Trametes trogii maintained stable laccase production even at 50 °C [60].

In addition to laccase, lignin peroxidase (LiP) and manganese peroxidase (MnP) play important roles in lignin oxidation. LiP cleaves non-phenolic lignin structures, whereas MnP oxidizes Mn2+ to Mn3+, which then oxidizes both phenolic and non-phenolic units via lipid peroxidation [58]. Phanerochaete chrysosporium is a model organism for these enzymes. In particular, enzyme expression is greatly influenced by the cultivation mode, and solid-state fermentation is known to promote high LiP and MnP production [61–63]. Similarly, Lenzites betulinus and Fomitopsis pinicola exhibited high MnP activity [64].

Interestingly, several exceptional cases have also been reported. Certain brown-rot fungi were reported to produce laccase in liquid culture [65], and the marine fungus Trematosphaeria mangrovei showed high laccase yields under nitrogen limitation [66]. Furthermore, versatile peroxidase (VP), identified in Pleurotus species, combined the catalytic features of LiP and MnP and oxidized a wide range of lignin substructures [58]. Overall, these various oxidative enzyme systems demonstrated the metabolic versatility of fungi in lignin mineralization and highlight their biotechnological potential for LCB bioconversion.

Bacterial strategies for LCB degradation

Historically, early research on LCB degradation focused predominantly on fungi. However, since the early 2010s, bacterial studies have expanded rapidly, reflecting their increasing recognition as important degraders with distinctive ecological and biotechnological roles [9]. Bacteria offer several advantages, including short generation times, simple cultivation, and high adaptability to variations in temperature, salinity, pH, and oxygen [9, 67]. They also tolerate environmental stress and often proliferate during later stages of biomass degradation. Importantly, bacteria can convert oligomeric and monomeric aromatic compounds released from lignin depolymerized by fungi [68]. Bacterial lignocellulose degradation strategies can be broadly categorized in relation to oxygen availability [11]. Under aerobic conditions, bacteria predominantly employ free extracellular enzyme secretion systems, which provide flexible access to diverse polysaccharide substrates [11]. In contrast, under anaerobic conditions, bacteria commonly utilize highly organized cell-bound multi-enzyme complexes, such as cellulosomes, which can enhance catalytic efficiency by maintaining close spatial proximity between enzymes, substrates, and the cell surface [16]. The functional implications of these strategies are discussed below in the context of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin degradation. Representative bacterial genera involved in LCB degradation include Bacillus, Pseudomonas, Paenibacillus, Rhodococcus, Clostridium, and Actinomycetes [67, 69, 70]. Cellulose degradation

Bacteria and fungi employ comparable enzyme groups to hydrolyze cellulose and hemicellulose. However, fungi have a greater diversity of glycoside hydrolases (GHs), which provide superior efficiency in degrading crystalline cellulose [11, 67]. Bacteria, on the other hand, generally produce less active cellulases and hence preferentially degrade amorphous cellulose. Their contribution to crystalline cellulose degradation remains limited [11].

Bacterial cellulose degradation strategies can be divided into two principal types. The first is the free-enzyme secretion system, in which extracellular enzymes are released individually to hydrolyze polymers, similar to aerobic fungi (Fig. 4b) [11, 71]. Bacillus species produce enzymes that retain their activity under diverse physicochemical conditions, and some strains can even attack crystalline cellulose [72–76]. Previous studies have shown that Geobacillus maintains cellulase activity above 55 °C [77–79], whereas Pseudomonas co-produces cellulases and xylanases over a wide pH range [76, 80]. Paenibacillus is mostly mesophilic but contains thermostable members [81–83]. This aerobic strategy is simple and enzymatically diverse, but its overall efficiency compared to fungi is limited by their relatively low secretion levels [11]. Nevertheless, actinomycetes such as Streptomyces, Cellulomonas, and Nocardiopsis exhibit strong protein secretion and wide substrate applicability [84–93]. In addition, cellulase activity has been reported in Arthrobacter [94], Acinetobacter [95], Neobacillus [96], and Staphylococcus [76]. Representative findings for each genus are summarized in Table 4.

The second strategy is to utilize the cellulosome, a surface-bound multiprotein complex that is typical of anaerobic bacteria. A cellulosome contains central scaffolding equipped with cohesion domains that anchor cellulases carrying complementary dockerin domains (Fig. 4c). The carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs) in scaffoldins attach the complex to cellulose, thereby maintaining close proximity between enzymes and substrates and increasing stability [71]. While fungi rarely employ this mechanism, bacteria utilize it extensively and efficiently. Representative bacterial genera that utilize cellulosome systems for cellulose degradation are summarized in Table 4. For example, Clostridium is one of the most well-studied producers, possessing highly organized scaffoldin–dockerin networks capable of degrading crystalline cellulose [97–99]. Acetivibrio and Ruminococcus encode multiple scaffoldin and dockerin genes, enabling the assembly of various cellulosomes, some of which have been reported to be similar to those of Clostridium [100, 101]. Ruminiclostridium has been reported to possess relatively simple cellulosomes [101]. Bacteroides forms multi-subunit cellulosomes that have been demonstrated to degrade not only crystalline cellulose but also agricultural residues such as corn stover or rice straw [102, 103]. Collectively, bacterial cellulose degradation reflects a wide range of ecological adaptations by combining simple aerobic free enzyme release with anaerobic cellulosome-based mechanisms.

Hemicellulose degradation

Compared with cellulases, the regulatory mechanisms of bacterial hemicellulases are less understood [104]. In general, both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria are known to produce enzymes with hemicellulolytic activity [105]. Most bacterial hemicellulases belong to GH families that cleave glycosidic bonds. GH10 and GH11 encode xylanases, GH26 mannanases, and GH43 xylosidases and arabinofuranosidases [71, 105].

Similar to cellulose hydrolysis, bacteria break down hemicellulose using either a free-enzyme system or a cellulosome-based system (Table 4). Aerobic bacteria, such as Bacillus and Cellvibrio, are known to secrete extracellular enzymes that cleave hemicellulose backbones [106–110]. This strategy has the advantage of minimizing carbon loss to competing microorganisms in the environment [16]. In addition, thermophilic genera, such as Thermobacillus and Geobacillus, have been reported to maintain stable xylanase activity at high temperatures [77, 111–114]. Meanwhile, it has been investigated that actinomycetes, such as Streptomyces, produce both cellulase and hemicellulases, supporting synergistic LCB degradation [86, 87]. Furthermore, some bacteria secrete multi-enzyme complexes that are distinct from conventional cellulosomes and can simultaneously degrade cellulose and hemicellulose [108, 110].

In contrast, anaerobic bacteria, such as Clostridium and Ruminococcus, use complex cellulosomes containing both cellulolytic and hemicellulolytic enzymes. These complexes enable efficient biomass depolymerization through multiple scaffoldins and dockerins [16, 71]. In Clostridium, multiple studies have shown that the cpbA scaffolding gene and catalytic genes, such as xynA, manA, and engH, are co-localized within a single gene cluster [99, 115, 116]. Consequently, cellulase and hemicellulase expression is regulated in a substrate-dependent manner [117]. Ruminococcus species, particularly R. flavefaciens, are known to assemble the most complex cellulosomes reported to date [118], in which an expanded repertoire of CBMs has been reported to enhance substrate affinity and catalytic efficiency [119, 120]. These results suggest that bacteria employ both free-enzyme and cellulosome-based strategies for hemicellulose degradation, and that their genetic and structural diversity is optimized for specific environmental conditions.

Lignin degradation

Although bacterial lignin-degrading enzymes have been identified at the genetic level, their efficiency remains low compared to fungal systems, and their underlying mechanisms are still poorly understood [11, 70]. Nevertheless, bacteria represent promising resources as lignin-degrading enzymes due to their short generation time, metabolic diversity, and environmental resilience [70]. The major bacterial oxidative enzymes include dye-decolorizing peroxidases (DyPs) and laccase-like multicopper oxidases (LMCOs), which oxidize lignin polymers and aromatic derivatives [9]. Although their redox potential is generally lower than that of fungal peroxidases, many bacterial enzymes exhibit superior thermal stability and higher optimal pH values, which are favorable properties for industrial use [11].

Representative bacteria involved in lignin degradation are shown in Table 4. The bacteria capable of lignin modification are mainly soil-dwelling Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria [9]. Because soils contain abundant aromatic compounds, microorganisms utilizing these compounds have evolved various degradation pathways for aromatic metabolism [70]. Current knowledge of bacterial lignin degradation is mainly derived from studies of aromatic compound catabolism [70]. For example, previous studies have reported that Streptomyces species possess laccase genes, and some strains also encode MnP and LiP genes [121, 122]. In other studies, enzymes derived from Streptomyces have been found to modify functional groups of lignin, such as carbonyl and methoxyl residues [123], and to participate in several aromatic catabolic pathways, including the β-ketoadipate and anthranilate pathways [122]. Rhodococcus and Pseudomonas primarily express DyP-type peroxidases [124–126], and some strains also contain LMCO genes [127]. These enzymes oxidize both lignin polymers and soluble aromatic intermediates [128], and the presence of metal ions enhances their activity or promotes C–C bond cleavage [124]. In addition, rather than attacking the lignin polymer itself, Sphingomonas has been demonstrated to metabolize lignin-derived aromatic compounds, such as 5,5′-dehydrodivanillate, through extensive catabolic pathways [129, 130]. In general, bacterial ligninolytic activity is weaker than that of fungi, but their metabolic flexibility and enzyme stability enable them to act as essential partners in natural lignocellulose turnover.

Fungal–bacterial interactions in lignocellulose degradation

Limitations of fungal and bacterial monocultures

Fungi have long been recognized as the primary decomposers of LCB; however, intrinsic limitations appear when they are cultivated in monoculture [9]. Fungal enzymes often exhibit relatively low substrate specificity and reduced stability under fluctuating physicochemical conditions [9]. Furthermore, fungal biomass typically dominates in nutrient-rich and oxygen-sufficient environments. On the other hand, under oxygen-limited or nutrient-depleted conditions, bacterial taxa play a more prominent role, particularly in the degradation of lignin-derived aromatic compounds [131]. In addition, the enzyme composition secreted by fungi is often unbalanced, with a cellulolytic dominance (≈70% CBH, 20% EG, < 1% hemicellulase) [71]. These biased enzyme profiles restrict efficient degradation of heterogeneous LCB. Moreover, genetic manipulation in fungi remains challenging compared to bacteria, and the high production cost of large-scale enzyme synthesis further limits their industrial applicability [11].

Bacteria also display clear constraints when maintained in monoculture. Although they generally exhibit high tolerance to environmental stresses and can occupy diverse ecological niches, their intrinsic cellulolytic capacity is comparatively lower [9, 67]. In particular, many bacteria show limited activity against crystalline cellulose, and their lignin-degrading enzymes possess lower oxidative potential than those of white-rot fungi [71]. Furthermore, the underlying mechanism of lignin depolymerization in bacteria has not yet been fully elucidated. Current knowledge is mainly derived from studies focusing on the catabolism of lignin-derived aromatic intermediates rather than on the initial oxidative cleavage of lignin polymers [70].

These technical and ecological limitations explain why fungi and bacteria frequently struggle to completely degrade structurally complex LCB under monoculture conditions, despite their independent degradation mechanisms. In particular, under suboptimal conditions, monoculture systems often suffer from a restricted enzyme repertoire or feedback inhibition due to metabolite accumulation, which reduces overall efficiency. In cellulolytic monocultures, this feedback inhibition is frequently associated with the accumulation of soluble hydrolysis products, particularly cellobiose. Such accumulation inhibits CBH activity when BGL is insufficiently represented in the enzyme system [71]. This limitation is more pronounced in closed monoculture settings, where the absence of rapid sugar assimilation further reinforces end-product inhibition and constrains sustained hydrolysis [9]. Consequently, co-culture systems that integrate the complementary metabolic functions of fungi and bacteria can offset these individual limitations and enhance degradation performance, thereby representing a strategic alternative for effectively controlling complex lignocellulose deconstruction processes.

Synergistic mechanisms of fungal–bacterial interactions

Although fungi and bacteria each have intrinsic limitations under monoculture conditions, their coexistence enables complementary functions that substantially enhance lignocellulose degradation (Fig. 5). These complementary interactions can be broadly synthesized into three interconnected mechanisms: (1) spatial and physicochemical regulation within the hyphosphere; (2) fungal-driven structural modification of lignocellulose that improves bacterial accessibility; and (3) bidirectional metabolic and enzymatic complementarity between fungi and bacteria. (1) Spatial and physicochemical regulation within the hyphosphere represents a primary mechanism through which fungi facilitate bacterial activity during lignocellulose degradation. These interactions occur predominantly within the hyphosphere, where fungal hyphae form a structural matrix that promotes bacterial attachment, dispersal, and persistence [132]. This spatial organization enables close physical proximity between fungi and bacteria, thereby facilitating metabolite exchange and coordinated decomposition processes. Importantly, the hyphosphere functions as a dynamic microenvironment in which local physicochemical conditions, particularly pH and oxygen availability, are actively regulated by fungal activity [132].

Through the secretion of organic acids, basidiomycete fungi locally reduce pH at the hyphal interface, typically to values ranging from 4 to 6 [133]. This acidification aligns with the optimal activity ranges of many lignocellulolytic enzymes, including cellulases, pectinases, phenol oxidases, LPMOs, and peroxidases [133]. In addition to enhancing enzymatic efficiency, localized pH regulation influences bacterial motility, adhesion, and spatial distribution along fungal hyphae, thereby indirectly modulating bacterial functional activity [133].

Oxygen gradients generated by fungal respiration further contribute to spatial complementarity between fungi and bacteria. Although lignocellulose degradation by fungi has traditionally been regarded as a strictly aerobic process, several wood-decaying fungi are capable of shifting toward fermentative metabolism under oxygen-limited conditions, producing ethanol from lignocellulosic substrates [134]. Moreover, oxygen consumption along actively growing hyphae generates microscale gradients, creating hypoxic or anoxic niches within the lignocellulosic matrix [135]. These microsites can support the fermentative metabolism of anaerobic bacteria or facultative anaerobic bacteria, enabling a division of labor along oxygen gradients and allowing aerobic fungi and anaerobic bacteria to coexist within the same microhabitat.

(2) Fungal-driven structural modification of lignocellulose constitutes a second key mechanism underlying fungal–bacterial complementarity. Fungal enzymatic activity alters the physical architecture of lignocellulosic substrates, thereby facilitating subsequent bacterial colonization and degradation. Zhang et al. [136] demonstrated that pretreatment of Manila grass, ryegrass, plane tree, and camphor tree with Pleurotus species resulted in roughened and porous biomass surfaces, accompanied by partial removal of lignin and hemicellulose. These structural changes increased cellulose exposure and elevated the availability of nutrients such as phosphate and ammonium nitrogen, collectively improving conditions for bacterial growth and activity [136]. By restructuring recalcitrant biomass components, fungi effectively expand the substrate spectrum accessible to bacteria, thereby alleviating limitations associated with bacterial monocultures.

(3) Bidirectional metabolic and enzymatic complementarity further reinforces fungal–bacterial cooperation through reciprocal stimulation of metabolic activity. In such systems, metabolites produced by one group directly enhance the physiological performance of the other. For example, Zhang et al. [137] reported that fungal metabolites, including betanidin and ergosterol, and bacterial metabolites, such as L-phenylalanine and taurine, mutually stimulated metabolic activity within a co-culture consortium, leading to enhanced lignin depolymerization. Similarly, Detain et al. [138] showed that co-cultures of A. niger and Streptomyces species exhibited increased production of fungal secondary metabolites (e.g., naphthopyrone and pyranonigrin) and bacterial metabolites (e.g., albaflavenone and terpenes), which were implicated in the degradation of lignin-derived aromatic intermediates.

Consistent with these mechanistic interactions, co-culture systems frequently exhibit synergistic enhancements in lignocellulolytic enzyme activity. Detain and Besaury [139] observed elevated xylanase and peroxidase activities in co-cultures of T. reesei, Streptomyces coelicolor, Rhizobium sp., and Sphingobacterium prati. Likewise, Cortés-Tolalpa et al. [140] reported that a consortium of Coniochaeta ligniaria, Citrobacter freundii, and Sphingobacterium multivorum achieved an 18.2-fold increase in biomass relative to monocultures, accompanied by enhanced activities of CBH, mannosidase, and xylosidase. Similar trends were observed in co-cultures of A. niger with Streptomyces avermitilis and S. coelicolor, which exhibited increased xylanase, glycosidase, and mannosidase activities [138].

In addition, such synergistic fungal–bacterial interactions can be further reinforced during the enrichment of natural microbial communities. Through enrichment of lignocellulosic substrates, fungal–bacterial assemblages selected from natural communities have repeatedly been shown to exhibit enhanced lignocellulose degradation [141, 142]. Sequential aerobic enrichment of forest soil on plant biomass yielded stable fungal–bacterial consortia (e.g., Sphingobacterium/Pseudomonas spp. with Coniochaeta ligniaria), which showed substrate-dependent improvements in lignocellulose degradation [141]. Similarly, consecutive batch subculturing of rumen-derived inocula enriched a stable anaerobic consortium dominated by fungi (Neocallimastigales, Anaeromyces, Orpinomyces) coexisting with bacteria such as Proteiniphilum acetatigenes, resulting in enhanced rice straw degradation and methane production [142].

In summary, fungal–bacterial synergism in lignocellulose degradation arises from the integration of spatial and physicochemical regulation, fungal-mediated structural modification of biomass, and bidirectional metabolic and enzymatic complementarity. As schematically summarized in Fig. 5, these interactions enable fungi and bacteria to overcome the functional constraints of monoculture systems and establish coordinated degradation networks, ultimately improving the efficiency and robustness of lignocellulosic bioconversion.

Such synergistic outcomes are driven by complex metabolic division of labor and interdependencies that extend beyond simple pairwise interactions. Consequently, mechanistic descriptions alone are often insufficient to predict consortium behavior across different environmental and compositional contexts. To address this limitation, genome-based and metagenomics-informed approaches have been proposed to interpret, predict, and rationally design fungal–bacterial consortia. These genome-resolved strategies, which link functional potential with community-level interactions, are discussed in detail in “Omics-based exploration of fungal–bacterial consortia” section.

Omics-based exploration of fungal–bacterial consortia

Traditionally, microorganisms have been isolated and cultivated to obtain biocatalysts for enzyme production. However, culture-dependent approaches capture only a very limited portion of the microbial diversity present in natural environments. It is estimated that less than 1% of environmental microorganisms can be cultivated under standard laboratory conditions [12]. In addition, commercially applied lignocellulose-degrading enzymes are mainly derived from a few fungal genera, especially Aspergillus and Trichoderma. This limited origin restricts the diversity of available enzymes and consequently increases the operational costs of industrial lignocellulose conversion.

To overcome these limitations, omics-based approaches, including metagenomics, metatranscriptomics, and metaproteomics, have emerged as powerful tools to characterize microbial communities without cultivation [143]. These methods provide integrated information about community composition, functional genes, enzyme families, and metabolic networks, enabling the discovery of novel biocatalysts. In addition, they have facilitated the identification of previously unrecognized fungal and bacterial lineages involved in lignocellulose degradation [12, 143].

Community structure and ecological dynamics

Natural environments such as soil, compost, and decaying wood serve as representative ecosystems to study fungal–bacterial coexistence during the lignocellulose degradation process. These habitats are characterized by the continuous accumulation and decomposition of plant residues and woody organic matter. This process generates complex microenvironments with diverse physicochemical conditions, including variations in temperature, moisture, pH, and carbon sources [143, 144]. Such heterogeneity promotes diverse ecological successions within microbial communities and facilitates interactions between fungi and bacteria with distinct degradative capabilities. Therefore, community analyses of lignocellulose-rich environments provide a crucial foundation for understanding the ecological patterns and functional interactions in the LCB degradation process. Soil serves as a major reservoir of microbial diversity and a key resource for studying lignocellulose-degrading microorganisms. Forest and agricultural soils are rich in plant residues and organic matter, which promote active carbon conversion [143]. Miao et al. [145] utilized RNA-based stable isotope probing (SIP) to compare cellulose-degrading communities in soils amended with compost, nitrogen–phosphorus–potassium (NPK) fertilizer, or left unfertilized. In compost-amended soils, members of Dothideomycetes (particularly Cryptococcus) dominated cellulose degradation, whereas Sordariomycetes were more abundant in NPK- and unfertilized soils. These results indicate that organic amendments enhance both fungal diversity and cellulose-degrading efficiency. Similarly, Wilhelm et al. [131] combined pyrosequencing with DNA-SIP to identify microorganisms involved in lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose degradation in coniferous forest soils. Families such as Burkholderiaceae, Comamonadaceae, and Caulobacteraceae were associated with lignin degradation, while most fungi were specialized in cellulose degradation. Interestingly, Caulobacter exhibited metabolic versatility, being capable of degrading all three substrates. Weiss et al. [146] enriched microbial consortia from sugarcane-cultivated soils using sugarcane bagasse as the substrate and observed stable coexistence among nine bacterial classes, including Actinobacteria, Alphaproteobacteria, Bacilli, Bacteroidia, and Cytophagia. These bacterial groups collectively contributed to cellulose and hemicellulose decomposition. These studies consistently demonstrate that lignocellulose degradation in soil environments is regulated by the interplay between environmental factors and microbial community structure.

Decaying wood provides a dense, solid substrate in which cellulose and lignin are tightly bound, making it an ideal system for investigating cooperative degradation by fungi and bacteria [143]. Qi et al. [147] analyzed the degradation processes of coniferous (Pinus tabulaeformis) and broadleaf (Quercus aliena) wood to investigate microbial community structure and successional patterns across degradation stages. Bacterial abundance exceeded that of fungi, with dominant bacterial genera including Bradyrhizobium, Paraburkholderia, Pseudomonas, and Sphingomonas. Fungal communities shifted from Ascomycota to Basidiomycota and Mucoromycetes as decay progressed, and wood density and moisture content were key determinants of community composition. Ceballos et al. [148] tracked temporal changes in microbial communities during high-solids thermophilic fermentation using Douglas fir as a lignocellulosic substrate. Bacterial taxa associated with lignin degradation in the early stages included members of the families Burkholderiaceae and Alcaligenaceae, as well as the genus Erwinia. However, as decomposition progressed, these groups were gradually replaced by members of Beijerinckiaceae, Xanthomonadaceae, and by the genera Rubrobacter, and Mycobacterium. Talaromyces dominated the fungal community throughout the process. These studies indicate that lignocellulose degradation involves a clear metabolic division of labor between fungi and bacteria, dynamically shaped by substrate characteristics and decomposition stage.

Compost represents a dynamic ecosystem characterized by high temperatures, aeration, and steep nutrient gradients, serving as a rich reservoir of lignocellulose-degrading microorganisms. During the composting process, thermophilic microorganisms become particularly active as the core temperature rises and play an important role in lignocellulose degradation [143, 144]. Wang et al. [144] reported that Actinobacteria dominated the microbial community during rice straw composting, with thermophilic bacteria such as Thermobispora bispora, Thermomonospora curvata, and Thermobifida fusca functioning as major degraders of plant biomass. Similarly, Ma et al. [149] compared composts derived from bagasse pith and manioc waste. In the early phases, Firmicutes were predominant, whereas Actinobacteria and Bacteroidetes gradually increased over time. During the thermophilic stage, Bacillus, Thermobifida, and Luteimonas were the key bacterial degraders. These community shifts indicate that composting promotes ecological adaptation and diversification among lignocellulose-degrading microorganisms.

Extreme environments such as hot springs may also serve as valuable reservoirs of novel lignocellulose-degrading enzymes. These habitats are characterized by temperatures exceeding 55 °C, diverse ionic compositions, and high salinity, which support the growth of thermophilic and acidophilic microorganisms. Such extremophiles can be important sources of highly stable extremozymes with substantial industrial potential [143]. For example, Knapik et al. [150] analyzed metagenomes from sediment samples collected at the Lobios hot springs in Spain and found Acidobacteria and Chloroflexi to be dominant phyla. In addition, they identified a new xylanase (XynA3) that retained strong activity at elevated temperatures, highlighting the potential of hot spring microbiomes for discovering thermostable enzymes.

In addition to free-living environments, such as soil, compost, and decaying wood, the rumen of ruminant animals represents a host-associated ecosystem that is continuously exposed to lignocellulosic substrates and harbors distinct microbial communities involved in plant biomass degradation. Accordingly, recent studies have applied omics-based approaches to characterize the composition and variability of lignocellulose-degrading microbial communities in the rumen. For example, a study analyzed the community structure of a stabilized anaerobic fungal consortium enriched from water buffalo rumen inoculum using shotgun metagenomic sequencing [142]. The consortium was dominated by anaerobic fungi belonging to Neocallimastigales, Anaeromyces, Orpinomyces, and Feramyces. These fungi coexisted with bacteria such as Proteiniphilum acetatigenes, as well as methanogens, forming a distinct and stable community structure [142]. In another study, rumen fluid from Angus steers was used to examine the effects of initial pH on microbial community composition and diversity [151]. Acidic conditions reduced bacterial diversity, whereas fungal diversity increased. Under neutral pH conditions, the relative abundances of key genera associated with lignocellulose degradation, including Succiniclasticum, Treponema, and Neocallimastix, were highest. Environmental factors such as pH, acetic acid, and propionic acid were identified as major drivers shaping both bacterial and fungal community structures [151]. Gharechahi et al. [152] investigated microbial communities attached to six forages with different lignocellulosic compositions in the rumen of Taleshi steers using Illumina MiSeq sequencing. During the early stages of incubation, forage-specific differences in community composition were clearly observed. Forages with high neutral detergent fiber (NDF) contents were preferentially colonized by members of the phylum Fibrobacteres, whereas Ruminococcus predominated on substrates with higher acid detergent fiber (ADF) contents. As incubation progressed, microbial community compositions gradually converged across the different forages [152].

Community-based studies conducted across diverse lignocellulose-rich natural habitats demonstrate that the ecological roles of fungi and bacteria vary depending on environmental conditions. Despite their inherent biological differences, the two microbial groups contribute complementarily to the process of lignocellulose degradation. Omics-based approaches enable the exploration of the metabolic potential of uncultivable or rare taxa. In doing so, they overcome the limitations of traditional culture-dependent methods that rely on only a small subset of cultivable microorganisms. Indeed, metagenomic analyses of complex ecosystems, such as soil, compost, and decaying wood, have revealed numerous previously uncharacterized fungal and bacterial lineages involved in lignocellulose degradation. These findings provide a foundation for more realistically reconstructing the diversity and complexity of microorganisms contributing to lignocellulose degradation. They also establish the basis for elucidating the functional integration of fungal–bacterial interactions.

Functional insights from metagenomic and bioinformatic integration

Community analyses in lignocellulose-rich environments are useful approaches for elucidating the coexistence patterns and ecological succession of fungi and bacteria. However, community-level analysis alone provides limited insight into the specific functional roles of community members. To overcome these limitations, recent studies have shifted from purely taxonomic descriptions to functional analyses that investigate the distribution of functional genes and metabolic pathways. This function-oriented strategy enables detailed characterization of microbial consortia at the metabolic network level. This paradigm shift marks a transition in lignocellulose degradation research from identifying who is present to understanding what they are doing. Representative studies on metabolic pathway analysis are summarized in Table 6.

Qi et al. [147] analyzed metagenomic data obtained from decaying hardwood and softwood. They found that microorganisms regulate carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism and perform complementary functions during wood decomposition. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Carbohydrate-Active enZymes (CAZy) database analyses showed that the major enzyme families, including glycosyltransferases (GTs), GHs, and CBMs, were primarily derived from Proteobacteria and Firmicutes. The distribution of these functional genes changed dynamically in response to community succession across different decomposition stages. This study demonstrated that the enzymatic network within wood-degrading ecosystems reorganizes over time to maintain functional coordination. Wang et al. [144] identified approximately 5000 CAZyme (Carbohydrate-Active enzymes) genes from metagenomic data of rice straw compost. The dominant enzyme families included GHs, CBMs, and polysaccharide lyases (PLs), with Actinobacteria showing a broad enzyme profile. Many of these enzymes participated in the hydrolysis of lignocellulose-derived intermediates, indicating that thermophilic microbial communities play a central role in metabolism during compost degradation. These findings suggest that compost-associated microbes adjust their enzymatic functions in response to changes in temperature and substrate concentration, thereby optimizing metabolic efficiency. Kaushal et al. [153] analyzed four hot spring metagenomes and identified roughly 4000 CAZyme genes, most of which encoded glycoside hydrolases and oxidoreductases. These enzymes displayed high structural stability under elevated temperatures, confirming their potential to degrade lignocellulosic polymers in thermophilic environments. Similarly, metagenomic functional analyses have elucidated lineage-specific roles in lignocellulose degradation within host-associated ecosystems [154]. A study analyzing 2223 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) recovered from termite gut metagenomes demonstrated that cellulolytic and hemicellulolytic functions are partitioned among different bacterial lineages in lower- and higher-termite systems. In particular, in higher termites, members of Fibrobacterota and Spirochaetota were shown to occupy cellulolytic niches that emerged following the loss of flagellates, whereas certain lineages of Pseudomonadota and Actinomycetota were implicated in oxygen-dependent modification of lignin and cellulose at the gut periphery [154].

Functional analyses of natural microbial communities reveal the inherent enzymatic diversity and spontaneous interactions among microorganisms. However, due to the environmental complexity of these systems, it is often difficult to determine the precise contribution of individual community members. To overcome this limitation, recent studies have adopted selective enrichment approaches that enhance specific microbial functions by selectively adjusting substrate type, temperature, or nutritional conditions. These strategies enable detailed characterization of enzyme families and key metabolic pathways specific to lignocellulose degradation. For example, Ceballos et al. [148] functionally analyzed metagenomes obtained from thermophilic enrichment cultures using Douglas fir as the substrate. The proportion of genes involved in lignin degradation increased progressively with enrichment. In addition, over time, lignin depolymerization-related genes became increasingly concentrated in thermophilic bacteria. This result indicates that artificial selection pressures can reshape the metabolic direction of microbial communities. Similarly, Paixão et al. [155] enriched mangrove soil consortia using sugarcane bagasse and identified approximately 18,000 CAZyme sequences, including cellulases, hemicellulases, esterases, and LPMOs. The enriched consortium consisted of both bacterial (Pseudomonas, Flavobacterium, Pseudoxanthomonas) and fungal (Basidiomycota, Ascomycota) taxa. This study highlighted that complex substrates promote enzymatic division of labor and strengthen functional interdependence within microbial consortia.

Omics-based studies conducted across various lignocellulose-rich habitats have demonstrated that microbial communities dynamically reorganize their enzyme profiles and metabolic networks in response to environmental variables, such as substrate properties, temperature, and degradation stage. These analyses reveal that community succession, the relative contribution of specific enzyme groups, and the distribution of degradation-related functional genes shift in a stepwise manner according to substrate and habitat characteristics. Such findings indicate that the metabolic flow and decomposition capacity of microbial communities can be continuously adjusted as environmental conditions change.

However, the number of existing omics studies remains limited, and current datasets are still insufficient to fully resolve the functional coordination among community members. Nevertheless, these studies provide an important basis for understanding the potential function and complementary metabolic structure of lignocellulose-degrading microbial communities. They also highlight the need for subsequent integrated omics approaches and more precise functional analyses.

Application and future perspectives for optimized consortia

Omics-based studies conducted in lignocellulose-rich environments have enabled an integrated characterization of microbial community structure, enzyme gene distribution, and metabolic complementarity. These studies reveal that different microorganisms functionally partition roles and interact during lignocellulose degradation [156]. Consequently, the functional roles of individual community members within natural assemblages have become increasingly clear. However, substantial technical limitations remain in directly translating such ecological insights into practically applicable consortium design strategies.

To date, research on fungal–bacterial consortia for lignocellulose degradation has commonly relied on enrichment-based, top-down approaches that start from natural microbial communities (Fig. 6a) [156, 157]. As detailed in “Synergistic mechanisms of fungal–bacterial interactions” section, such enrichment processes have repeatedly been shown to reinforce synergistic fungal–bacterial interactions and improve lignocellulose degradation [141, 142]. This strategy selectively enriches complex communities using defined substrates and cultivation conditions, enabling the acquisition of consortia with enhanced degradation performance. As such, it has been widely applied as an effective means of processing recalcitrant lignocellulosic substrates.Fig. 6Conceptual comparison between enrichment-based and omics-driven optimization strategies for lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia

Conceptual comparison between enrichment-based and omics-driven optimization strategies for lignocellulose-degrading microbial consortia

However, enrichment-based top-down approaches exhibit several structural limitations when aiming for design-based fungal–bacterial systems [156, 157]. First, enriched consortia often possess highly complex structures where multiple bacterial and fungal members coexist. This complexity makes it difficult to clearly attribute functional roles or to elucidate specific interaction mechanisms involved in lignocellulose degradation [156, 158]. Second, many microorganisms presumed to play key roles during enrichment are difficult to stably isolate and cultivate under laboratory conditions. Furthermore, they often exhibit strong variability in enzyme expression and metabolic pathway usage depending on cultivation conditions. This variability limits the reproducibility and controllability of these consortia [158]. Third, because community selection is achieved through environmental manipulation, this approach provides limited causal insight into which interactions are maintained or disrupted under specific conditions. As a result, predicting or systematically controlling changes in degradation efficiency or product profiles in response to environmental fluctuations remains challenging [157]. Consequently, while top-down approaches are effective for demonstrating the presence and degradation potential of fungal–bacterial interactions, they face inherent constraints in standardizing these interactions at the component level and extending them into generalized design principles.

As an alternative to overcome these limitations, omics-informed bottom-up optimization strategies have recently been proposed (Fig. 6b). Rather than attempting to reproduce entire complex natural communities, this approach aims to identify strains or functional groups that make key contributions to lignocellulose degradation through metagenomic and functional analyses. These selected members are then reassembled at the level of minimal functional units [156, 157]. In a representative study, Gilmore et al. [159] applied high-throughput sequencing to an anaerobic lignocellulose-degrading system to analyze community composition and functional partitioning patterns. Based on these analyses, only the core functional members were selected from the original complex community. This resulted in the reconstruction of a minimal consortium composed of Neocallimastix californiae, Anaeromyces robustus, and Methanobacterium bryantii. This simplified consortium maintained degradation efficiencies comparable to those of the original community while exhibiting improved stability and controllability. Similarly, Hu et al. [160] first screened synergistic consortia with high enzymatic activity by combining different cellulolytic fungi and subsequently introduced multiple bacterial species as additional members. By integrating enzyme activity measurements with metagenomic analyses, the authors identified core strains and interaction networks within the community. The resulting consortium—comprising Trichoderma, Aspergillus, Bacillus, Enterococcus, Lactococcus, Acinetobacter, and Pseudomonas—showed approximately 197% higher BGL activity than T. reesei monocultures [160]. In addition, the feasibility of function-based consortium design has been experimentally demonstrated elsewhere [158]. In that study, soil microorganisms enriched on sugarcane-derived substrates were subjected to molecular phenotyping and metabolic characterization, classifying 18 isolates into four functional groups. Synthetic consortia were then assembled by combining representative strains from each group. The results revealed that a consortium composed of Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Paenibacillus sp., Microbacterium sp., Chryseobacterium taiwanense, and Brevundimonas sp. exhibited the highest degradation efficiency [158]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that bottom-up design strategies grounded in functional roles and metabolic capabilities can yield simplified yet effective lignocellulose-degrading consortia. By selecting and assembling microbial members based on their specific functional contributions, these approaches provide experimental evidence that rational consortium design is feasible beyond purely empirical enrichment.

Despite these advances, bottom-up design approaches also have inherent limitations. Specifically, it remains challenging to predict the precise conditions under which interactions observed within an assembled consortium causally contribute to stability or degradation performance [156]. Even with identical strain compositions, community behavior and functional expression can vary substantially depending on cultivation conditions or substrate composition. This strong condition dependence makes it difficult to generalize design principles across systems. Given these limitations, computational community modeling approaches have recently been proposed as potential complementary tools to support bottom-up consortium design, rather than as standalone solutions [161, 162]. In synthetic microbial community research, stoichiometry-based metabolic models and in silico co-culture simulations have begun to be used to explore and interpret interactions among community members and their functional partitioning [161]. In particular, genome-scale metabolic models (GEMs) combined with flux-balance analysis allow the metabolic roles of individual microorganisms and their potential interactions to be quantitatively defined, providing a preliminary predictive framework for community-level functional dynamics. In addition, individual-based modeling (IBM) frameworks such as BacArena explicitly incorporate microbial metabolic networks and spatial interactions, offering a means to explore spatiotemporal interaction structures beyond simple coexistence [162]. However, these modeling approaches are still under active development and have so far been applied mainly to two- or three-species systems or otherwise simplified multispecies communities. Their extension to complex lignocellulose-degrading consortia, particularly those including fungi, remains at an early stage [162]. Accordingly, at present, computational community modeling should be viewed as a promising but still emerging approach that may help address some of the limitations of bottom-up design, rather than as a mature framework for consortium engineering. In this context, such models can contribute by probing the structural constraints and condition dependence of microbial interactions, thereby supporting the interpretation and refinement of omics-based bottom-up strategies.

Fungal–bacterial interactions observed in natural environments can provide a conceptual framework for the cross-kingdom design of industrial microbial consortia. In natural communities, functionality is maintained through functional partitioning and interdependent interactions, even under heterogeneous and highly variable lignocellulosic substrate conditions. These ecological characteristics can serve as design criteria for constructing consortia that maintain degradation performance despite changes in substrate composition or process perturbations. This consideration is particularly relevant for biorefineries and consolidated bioprocessing (CBP) systems, where operational variability and long-term stability are critical requirements. In such systems, interaction structures observed in natural communities can inform the design of consortia in which reduced performance of individual strains is compensated by other members. As a result, functional losses can be buffered at the community level through a coordinated division of labor.

Conclusions

This review synthesizes lignocellulosic biomass (LCB) degradation as a fungal–bacterial community-level process driven by functional complementarity rather than by individual strains or enzymes. Comparative analysis highlights the interdependent roles of fungi and bacteria, wherein fungi primarily restructure recalcitrant substrates and shape local physicochemical microenvironments, while bacteria facilitate downstream metabolic conversion through complementary enzymatic and catabolic capacities. Together, these functions overcome the intrinsic limitations of monoculture systems, demonstrating that degradation efficiency emerges from interaction networks rather than isolated activities.

Across diverse LCB-rich environments, including soil, compost, decaying wood, and host-associated systems, recurrent fungal–bacterial interaction patterns are consistently observed. This convergence indicates that cooperative degradation represents a conserved ecological principle rather than a context-specific phenomenon. Omics-based studies extend these ecological observations to the functional level, revealing that CAZymes, oxidative enzymes, and core metabolic pathways are functionally partitioned within communities. Importantly, these functional architectures reorganize dynamically in response to substrate properties and degradation stage, suggesting that LCB degradation is best understood as a dynamic functional network, not as a fixed enzyme repertoire.

Recent advances in omics technologies have enabled a shift beyond traditional top-down enrichment approaches. By resolving the functional division of labor within microbial communities, omics-based analyses allow the identification of core functional units that drive lignocellulose degradation. This functional resolution supports a paradigm shift toward the rational, bottom-up design of microbial consortia based on functional contribution rather than taxonomic complexity. Although challenges remain in fully capturing the complexity of microbial interactions, these omics-driven insights provide a solid foundation for constructing stable and efficient synthetic consortia for industrial LCB bioconversion.
