Action-type mapping principles extend beyond evolutionarily conserved actions, even in people born without hands
Authors: Florencia Martinez-Addiego, Yuqi Liu, Kyungji Moon, Elizabeth Shytle, Lénia Amaral, Caroline O’Brien, Sriparna Sen, Maximilian Riesenhuber, Jody C. Culham, Ella Striem-Amit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe motor system is organized coarsely by body part; however, beyond M1 it also has a suborganization for hand actions. We tested whether this suborganization is sufficient to drive brain organization on its own, when typically developed people perform actions with another body part and in people who were born without hands. We show that not only actions inherited from our evolutionary ancestry are mapped across acting body parts, but also more evolutionarily recent tool-use actions. These findings suggest that an action-type mapping is not shaped by the action’s evolutionary recency. Similarly, it is not dependent on personal manual sensorimotor experience. These results instead argue for an action-based map as a general principle of motor organization in the human brain.

During action planning, our motor system effortlessly translates conscious goals to elaborate, coordinated muscle contractions. There is evidence suggesting that a hierarchical organization in the motor system facilitates this translation (1–5): Basic motor features are encoded within the primary motor cortex (M1) and higher-level association areas represent more complex information such as the action type (5–7). M1 has a coarse somatotopic organization, whereby each major body part is preferentially represented by a distinct area of the cortex, and adjacent body parts are mapped to neighboring cortical regions (8, 9). This somatotopic organization in M1 represents lower-level, body-part-specific kinematic information by encoding muscle synergy information (10–13).

Motor association areas, such as the dorsal premotor cortex (PMd), ventral premotor cortex (PMv), supplementary motor area (SMA), and part of the parietal lobe, have some broad somatotopic preferences, but primarily represent goal-directed action information such as target location (14–17), motor sequence (18), and action type (19). As expected from areas higher in the motor hierarchy, there is evidence that these representations are partly invariant to the low-level motor features. In recent years, there has been accumulating evidence that these areas may contain action representations that generalize across executing body parts (effectors). During motor planning, the response patterns in the posterior parietal cortex (PPC) and PMd encode the upcoming action type (e.g., reaching or grasping) regardless of which hand would be used (20). For the execution of reaching and grasping actions, the premotor cortex and parietal lobe encode target location, target size, and orientation independent of whether an action was executed by the dominant or nondominant hand (21, 22). Additionally, the PMd and intraparietal sulcus (IPS) are involved in the planning of both hand and eye movements toward a specified direction (23–28). Importantly, these findings were not limited to effectors that are somatotopically close, homologous, or frequently involved in coordinated actions (i.e., hand–hand and hand–eye coordination). A few studies have found common activation in frontoparietal areas during action planning for pointing movements between the hand and foot, which are somatotopically distant and less coordinated (24–26). A shared action representation across the hand and foot has been extended to include the differentiation of reaching and grasping action-types. We have recently shown that foot reaching and grasping actions performed by individuals born without hands engaged some of the same areas as hand actions performed by typically developed participants [abbreviated TDs here; (29)]. Together, these findings suggest that motor association areas support reaching and grasping actions independently of the performing body part.

These results provide a theoretical grounding for a functional, action-type organization of motor association areas. In this scenario, the action type (e.g., reaching) and not the acting effector would be the leading organizational principle (29–32). Importantly, evidence supporting effector-independent action-type representations has so far been limited to these two specific evolutionarily conserved actions that are shared across nonhuman primates and other mammals (30, 31). Yet, humans have developed additional motor skills, such as sophisticated tool-use, with dedicated neural substrates (33–35). It is unclear whether motor association areas will also support an effector-independent action-type representation for additional actions, including this newly evolved action-type.

Generally, tools are defined as instruments that facilitate the completion of goals, through using objects to modify their environment (36); for a deeper discussion on definitions of tools, see ref. 37. Tool-use marks an important evolutionary milestone for humans (38). Indeed, though tool-use ability is shared with nonhuman primates (39, 40), humans have developed a unique dexterity, expertise, and understanding of the causal relationship between tool-use and the end outcome (41). This is reflected neurally as there are somewhat different neural substrates for tool-use in humans versus nonhuman primates (35). Humans have an expanded network of parieto-frontal areas (42), including the supramarginal gyrus, middle temporal gyrus, aIPS, and PMv that is engaged in tool-use (43–46).

Here, we test the hypothesis that the tool-use network is also organized by action-type, regardless of the executing effector. Evidence supporting this hypothesis would suggest that an action-type, effector-independent organization is not limited to evolutionarily preserved actions and further argue for a motor abstraction hierarchy. Alternatively, generalizing across effectors for the same action may only be possible for reaching and grasping, or even more broadly just for evolutionarily conserved actions. This instead would suggest longer evolutionary timelines are needed for action-type specialization, or for its generalization across effectors.

In this study, we used functional neuroimaging to investigate whether motor association areas support effector-independent tool-use representations. To this end, we recruited a group of typically developed individuals who performed in-scanner tool-use and basic grasping actions with their hands (primary effector) and their feet (atypical effector). Though a shared preference for tool-use between the hands and feet in typically developed individuals would provide evidence for effector-independent brain mechanisms, there is an alternative explanation. When asked to perform typical hand actions with the foot, the participant may imagine executing the action with their hand and only then translate it to the foot, resulting in shared activation with hand execution that is a product of hand motor imagery (47, 48). Likewise, any differential, effector-dependent activations may stem from a difference in experience between the hand and the foot. To avoid these possible confounds, we leveraged a population of individuals born without arms and hands (individuals with upper-limb dysplasia, abbreviated IDs) who use their feet as their primary effector. Since the IDs cannot imagine moving their own hands (c.f. ref. 49), including them allows us to bolster claims about effector-independent and effector-dependent networks. By mapping effector-independence and dependence we can test whether the motor hierarchy generalizes across body parts for evolutionarily recent, human-specific actions.

Results

To address our main question of whether the brain supports tool-use action-type representations that generalize across body parts (effectors), we scanned individuals with dysplasia (IDs, born without hands; n = 6) and typically developed participants (abbreviated TDs; n = 18; see Materials and Methods). Participants grasped or used (turned to use a spatula) tool objects under largely matched visual setups (see Materials and Methods and schematic in SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The recruited IDs use their feet as the compensatory body part to perform daily actions (29, 50–54) and therefore completed all in-scanner actions with their dominant foot (five IDs were right-footed, one ID, ID6, was left-footed). TDs were right-handed and right-footed and completed actions using their right hand or right foot in separate experimental runs. We used several approaches to test effector-independence in tool-use actions: 1) univariate main effects of action type across the effectors and groups; 2) consistent preference for tool-use across TDs’ hand, TDs’ foot, and IDs’ foot data; 3) multivariate decoding of the action type within and across effectors; 4) shared neural temporal signals for tool-use regions across effectors; and 5) decoding of action type from the temporal dynamics pattern across effectors. Together, these analyses provided converging results supporting the existence of effector-independent action representations for tool-use.

Univariate Effects of Effector and Action-Type.

We performed two random-effects (RFX) ANOVAs to determine the brain regions showing 1) a preference for a specific effector and 2) selectivity for action-type. One ANOVA was computed for TDs’ data with effector (hand and foot) and action type (grasping and tool-use) as within-subject factors (TDH+TDF; typically-developed hand + foot; Fig. 1A). Since typically developed individuals are not accustomed to using their feet for grasping and tool-use actions, they might engage hand imagery to perform their foot actions. In this case, a finding of effector-independence would be less interpretable. To control for this potential confound, an additional ANOVA was performed across TDs’ hand and IDs’ foot data with effector/group as a between-subjects factor and action type as a within-subjects factor (TDH+IDF; Fig. 1D). For each main effect, we calculated the spatial overlap between the two ANOVAs to investigate effector dependence and effector independence that generalizes across experience and dexterity while ruling out potential manual motor imagery.

We first probed each of the ANOVAs for areas with a main effect of effector across actions (consistent activation preference based on the acting body part), which indicated an effector-dependent representation. In the TDH+TDF ANOVA, we found a differential response based on the acting body part in the left primary sensorimotor (M1/S1 hand and foot regions), left lateral occipital temporal cortex (LOTC), left superior parietal lobule (SPL), right SMA, and lobules IV and VI in the left cerebellum (Fig. 1B). Similarly, the TDH+IDF ANOVA showed differential activations based on the acting effector in the left foot primary sensorimotor cortex and the left SPL (Fig. 1E). Accordingly, the two ANOVAs overlapped in the left foot primary sensorimotor area and the left SPL (Fig. 2A), suggesting an effector-dependent organization in these regions. Although at the whole-brain level there was no main effect of effector in hand-selective M1 at the chosen statistical threshold, it was found when directly sampling the hand M1 region (F(1, 21) = 9.89, P < 0.005).

We then asked whether there was evidence for a functional, effector-independent organization for tool use. To this end, we examined brain areas showing a main effect of action type regardless of the executing effector (consistent activation preference based on the performed action). Results from the TDH+TDF ANOVA revealed a widespread frontoparietal network containing the bilateral SMA, PMd, SPL, and LOTC, as well as lobules V and VI of the left cerebellum (Fig. 1C). The TDH+IDF ANOVA showed a similar pattern including the bilateral IPS, PMd, and SMA and the left LOTC, as well as lobules V, VI, and VIIIb in the left cerebellum (Fig. 1F). Overlapping the two ANOVAs, we found that the bilateral SMA, PMd, SPL, and left LOTC along with lobules V and VI of the left cerebellum showed selectivity to action type regardless of the effector and sensorimotor experience (Fig. 2B). Importantly, these effects were not simply driven by a potentially longer movement duration in tool use versus grasping, as consistent results were found in a control analysis accounting for the movement onset and duration (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Notably, these areas do not show a significant interaction effect (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), further corroborating effector-independence. We also computed a supplementary RFX ANOVA comparing the TDs’ foot and IDs’ foot actions (SI Appendix, Fig. S4) and found a consistent main effect of action type independent of limb experience in all the same areas. Several of these regions, namely the PMd, SPL, and anterior SMG, are known to be part of the tool-use network (46), suggesting that tool-use actions are represented by this network at a functional, effector-independent level.

Consistent Tool-Use Preference across Effectors.

Though above regions have been shown to be part of the tool-use network, the ANOVA main effects analysis does not inherently specify the direction of action preference. As a result, it is unclear which action is driving the observed main effect. To directly investigate whether tool-use areas may be effector-independent, we used TDs’ hand data to define tool-use selective regions-of-interest (ROIs) and tested for a consistent preference for tool-use over grasping in foot data. ROIs that preferred tool-use in TDs’ hand data included core tool-use areas in the left hemisphere—such as SMA, PMd, SPL (7pc), SPL (area 2), PMv, and LOTC [see Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Table S1; for additional areas that showed a tool-use preference, see SI Appendix, Fig. S5 and Table S2, ROI labels were drawn from the Julich Brain Atlas at the volume level; (55)]. Within these areas, we found a consistently stronger response for tool-use over grasping for both TDs and IDs when they executed the actions with their feet (Fig. 3B; for detailed statistics see SI Appendix, Table S3). Notably, the findings from PMd, SPL (7pc), and SPL (area 2) are robust to the correction of recorded movement onset and duration (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A).

To explore whether additional areas show effector-independent tool-use preference, we performed whole-brain contrasts of tool-use > grasp (in conjunction with tool-use > baseline to include only positive activations) for TDs’ hand, TDs’ foot, and IDs’ foot data separately, and computed a three-way overlap among them. We found a consistent, significant preference for tool-use across effectors in the left PMd, SMA, and SPL (areas 2 and 7pc) (Fig. 3C; for each group’s contrast see SI Appendix, Fig. S7). This result was replicated when controlling for action duration (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). Out of the six IDs, significant tool-use preference at the single-subject level was found in all, five, and four individuals in the left PMd, left SMA and SPL, and in LOTC, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Together, this shows that canonical hand tool-use regions can generalize to other body parts.

Across analyses, these findings suggest that tool-use areas, namely PMd, SPL (7pc), and SPL (area 2), generalize across the acting body parts and have a shared preference for tool-use across effectors.

Decoding Action Type across Effectors.

Next, we leveraged multivoxel pattern analysis (MVPA) to determine whether there are consistent, distinguishable, spatial neural response patterns for the different actions that extend across acting body parts. We first performed ROI-MVPA in the hand tool-use selective regions defined in Fig. 3A. To do this, we trained a linear support-vector machine (SVM) classifier to differentiate between grasping and tool-use either within an effector (TDs’ hand, TDs’ foot, IDs’ foot), or across effectors in TDs (the SVM was trained on hand data and then tested on foot data and vice versa, see Materials and Methods). Given the possibility for additional noise from motion in the data that the classifier may be sensitive to, thereby artificially increasing decoding, we further baselined the accuracies to a control ROI, the white matter of the bilateral anterior temporal lobe (ATL). As our task engages widespread visual, auditory, and motor networks, the bilateral ATL is among the few brain areas that are not expected to respond to our paradigm. For baselining, we subtracted each participant’s decoding accuracy in ATL or the chance level (0.5), depending on which one was greater, from that of each ROI, thereby maintaining within-subject variance. In all hand tool-use selective regions, action-type decoding was successful within TDs’ hand and foot, and across effectors. Importantly, the IDs also had significant action-type decoding in the PMd, SPL area 7pc, and SPL area 2 (Fig. 4A).

Further, we performed a whole-brain searchlight MVPA decoding action type across effectors in the typically developed group, as compared to chance (50%). This confirmed the decoding in the tool-selective network and additionally revealed a shared neural code for action type independent of the executing effector in the bilateral inferior parietal lobe and PMv (Fig. 4B). Combined with the ROI MVPA, these results robustly show effector-independent neural patterns distinguishing the two actions, regardless of the body part or sensorimotor experience.

Last, we performed a control analysis to test how early we can decode action information during action execution by each effector. Using time-resolved MVPA, we found significant decoding of the action already during early stages of the execution fMRI response (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). This suggests that the differential patterns were not driven by the added tool-use motion following grasping, but instead manifest earlier, due to a qualitative difference between these actions in execution or planning (56, 57).

Differential Time Courses across the Action Hierarchy.

Going beyond typical univariate and multivariate analyses, we tested whether evidence for an action hierarchy, namely effector-dependent representations in primary motor areas and effector-independent representations in motor association areas, would also manifest in temporal dynamics of the functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) response. Although BOLD signals are known for their low temporal resolution, a slow event-related design with a relatively short TR (782 ms) in our experiment allowed us to detect differential time courses between the conditions.

If the association motor areas encode effector-independent action-type information, the timing of their involvement during the action stages should be similar, regardless of the acting effector. Given that our experimental design was structured such that all participants used the same trial order (for both hand and foot runs), we tested this hypothesis by using the average time course from an effector-independent region from TDs’ hand data (PMd) as a predictor of the foot neural response in IDs in a whole-brain general linear models (GLM) analysis. The motor association cortices’ response, including PMd, PMv, and SPL, were synchronized to the same action stages for IDs’ foot actions as with TDs’ hand (Fig. 5A). In contrast, the time-course in M1 hand area for TDs’ hand predicted IDs’ foot responses along primary sensorimotor cortex, especially in foot M1 (Fig. 5B). These results suggest that higher and lower areas in the motor hierarchy share similar temporal dynamics in our task, regardless of being performed by the hand in TDs or by the foot in IDs. A direct contrast between the two, showing differential temporal response for the low- versus high-level areas of the motor hierarchy, revealed a frontoparietal network in IDs where the time-course resembles PMd in TDs’ hand and primary sensorimotor areas where the time-course resembles M1 in TDs’ hand (Fig. 5C). These results show that the motor hierarchy responds with similar temporal dynamics across acting body parts.

Temporal Dynamics Patterns along the Action Hierarchy.

We further performed a temporal MVPA [tMVPA, (58)] to test whether the temporal dynamics of the response pattern over the course of a trial carries effector or action-specific information. Tool-use action-selective ROIs (Fig. 3) and body-part selective areas (M1 hand and foot areas) defined from the TDs’ univariate results (Materials and Methods) were analyzed. Within each ROI and for each effector and action-type, we calculated a single-trial representational dissimilarity matrix (stRDM, see Materials and Methods) that depicts the distance (in 1 − Pearson’s correlation) in the multivoxel response pattern between each pair of time points. Trials were defined from the onset of the action instruction (e.g., “grasp”) to the rest period following each trial. The pattern of the stRDM reflects the temporal dynamics: Low dissimilarity along the diagonal indicates dynamic representations changing from timepoint to timepoint, and low dissimilarity also off the diagonal denotes stable representations over time. We then performed MVPA and hierarchical clustering on the stRDMs to test whether different effectors and action types are associated with distinct temporal dynamics patterns.

We first tested the expectation that the primary motor cortex would show effector-specific temporal dynamics (Fig. 6). First, a two-phase temporal dynamics pattern, dividing each trial into two main portions and likely corresponding to planning and execution stages of the action, was observed only for the “corresponding effectors,” i.e., hand actions in M1 hand area and foot actions in M1 foot area (stRDMs in Fig. 6 A and D). Second, the action type could be decoded based on the stRDMs in the M1 hand area mainly for TDs’ hand (bar graph in Fig. 6B), and hand actions form distinct clusters away from the less distinguishable foot actions (hierarchical clustering in Fig. 6C). Importantly, effector/group information could be successfully decoded both within and across action types (SI Appendix, Fig. S11A), suggesting a general effector representation regardless of action. Interestingly, the M1 foot area also shows distinct dynamics patterns for the hand and a tendency of cross-group action decoding (bar graph in Fig. 6E). Nevertheless, decoding of effector/group was significantly above chance both within and across actions (SI Appendix, Fig. S11A) in M1 foot area, and the hierarchical clustering shows an effector-based clustering pattern (Fig. 6F). Finally, an omnibus hierarchical clustering analysis on the dissimilarity patterns of all three groups and two regions revealed a body-part-based organization such that the temporal dynamic patterns of the corresponding effectors (foot actions in foot M1 and hand actions in hand M1) are clustered together within which the foot actions forming subclusters, and the noncorresponding effectors (foot actions in hand M1 and hand actions in foot M1) form a separate cluster that is less differentiable with no clear structure (Fig. 6G).

Importantly, the association motor areas display different, action-based, temporal dynamics patterns across effectors/groups. Despite variable execution manners across effectors, grasping and tool-use stRDMs could be decoded within and across effectors in left PMd, IPS, and SPL, providing evidence for a general action-type representation in the temporal domain (Fig. 7 B, E, and H). In contrast, effector/group information could no longer be decoded in these areas (SI Appendix, Fig. S11A), forming a double dissociation between the primary and association motor areas in terms of effector-dependent and action-dependent temporal patterns. Finally, hierarchical clustering analyses revealed a tendency of grouping the temporal dynamics patterns based on the performed action type as opposed to effectors (Fig. 7 C, F, and I). These results are unlikely simply driven by different execution times between actions and effectors, as we found a double- dissociation across brain regions. Further, to conclusively verify that this result could not have been driven by head motion induced by action execution, we replicated the tMVPA with stRDMs created using the six head motion parameters as the “response pattern.” This control analysis found a unique temporal pattern instantaneous to execution (SI Appendix, Fig. S11B) and dissociable from the neural stRDMs. Moreover, action-type information could not be decoded in this control analysis, effectively ruling out the possibility that the temporal patterns simply reflect head movements. Altogether, these results revealed effector-dependent and effector-independent temporal dynamics patterns in the primary and association motor cortex, respectively, lending further evidence for a representational action hierarchy from the temporal domain.

Discussion

An increasing amount of behavioral, electrophysiological, and neuroimaging evidence converges on the idea of a shared representation for reaching and grasping action types that are independent of sensorimotor specifics, including effector (20, 29, 59, 60). Yet, the extent to which 1) the brain supports an action-type representation beyond these actions, 2) the brain does so for more evolutionarily recent actions such as tool-use across somatotopically distant effectors, and 3) such representations are derived from manual sensorimotor experience remains poorly understood.

Here, we showed that tool-use actions are represented in motor association areas at an effector-independent level. Specifically, we found that the left PMd and SPL (areas 2 and 7pc), regions belonging to the typical tool-use network for hand actions, have a consistent preference for tool-use across TDs’ hand, TDs’ foot, and IDs’ foot. The multivoxel response pattern in these regions additionally differentiates between actions across TDs’ hand and foot and within IDs foot actions (in PMd and SPL area 2), lending further evidence to action-type representations that are independent of the executing effector. Finally, the temporal dynamics in motor association areas carry effector-independent action-type information. These findings argue that tool-use actions, while being newly evolved and almost exclusively associated with the hand, are effector-independent in higher-level motor association areas. In contrast, primary sensorimotor and adjacent cortices are selective for the acting body part. Temporal dynamics similarly show that the primary motor areas carry effector-specific information. Ultimately, these results make a case for an effector-independent tool-use network that is organized by action type and primary sensorimotor regions that are organized by effector. Additionally, our findings hold for a population of individuals born without hands, suggesting that there is an action-type organization in motor association areas, and that it does not require hand sensorimotor experience to develop. Together, this supports a hierarchical organization for action representations where the acting effector is one such property that can be generalized across.

The Tool-Use Network Contains Effector-Independent Representations.

Neuroimaging and lesion studies identified a left-lateralized frontoparietal network specialized for representing tool-use actions and tool-related knowledge. Typical regions include the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), PMd, PMv, SMG, SPL, IPS, and middle temporal cortex [MTC; (33, 46, 61)]. Further functional division is drawn between a more dorsal pathway connecting the SPL to the PMd that is involved in online tool-use action control and a ventral pathway including MTC, IPL, SMG, and PMV that subserves long-term conceptual knowledge about tools (33, 62–67). We found that areas of the dorsal pathway, namely PMd and SPL, contain effector-independent representations, suggesting these regions may be organized by action-type. Importantly, it is unlikely these findings are driven by differences in objects or at the motor output level such as limb trajectory or movement duration because different objects (for grasping) and tool-use trajectories (i.e., turning left or right) were used to balance low-level factors and univariate and multivariate results were reproduced after accounting for movement onset and duration. Likewise, the temporal unfolding of brain responses that reflect effector-independence, characterized by the univariate time-course analysis and tMVPA, may reflect common computations underlying each action regardless of effector. For example, grasping involves calculating object size, and tool-use requires one to compute the relative position between the tool and its target (59, 68).

Our findings extend existing ideas about the abstraction level of the tool-use network. Past studies observed a preference for pantomimed tool-use versus random gesturing in left IPS, PMd, PMv, SMG, and IFG, regardless of if the left or right hand was used (69, 70). Although this finding argues for hand-independent mechanisms, it is possible that the shared preference was driven by the semantic processing of objects involved only in the pantomiming condition and not necessarily an action representation (see also refs. 61 and 71). Moreover, this study did not perform any direct cross-effector analyses, making it difficult to argue for generalization across the hands for tool-use actions. By using daily tools in both the grasping and tool-use conditions, we controlled the level of object semantics, thus isolating processes related to action execution. More importantly, past studies have only tested effector-independence across dominant and nondominant hands which have homotopic cortical representations and are often coordinated in daily life. By performing MVPA across the hand and foot within TDs and testing IDs, we provide further evidence for effector-independent tool-use action representations that are generalizable to a novel and inexperienced effector (the foot in TDs) and are independent of hand-related motor experience [i.e., IDs (47, 48, 72–75)].

Critically, our data do not argue for effector-invariance in all areas of the tool-use network. Specifically, although PMv is thought to encode action goals and be involved in action execution and action understanding, we do not find it is consistently engaged in an effector-independent manner (SI Appendix, Figs. S2, S7, and S14). Similarly, the human tool-use region of anterior SMG [SMG area 2 (35)] did not show a consistent preference for tool-use across effectors. Though this region showed a preference for tool-use over grasping in TDs’ hand and IDs’ foot, there was no significant difference between the actions for TDs’ foot actions (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). This result suggests that SMG may encode some effector and/or experience information. One possibility is that these areas primarily encode conceptual knowledge about the object in addition to the action type (61, 63). If this is the case, our use of tools in both conditions could result in a less-differentiated response in these areas. Future studies are needed to examine the level of abstraction hierarchy in terms of action execution and semantic processing across tool-use areas to understand whether the ventral tool pathway generalizes across effectors.

Hierarchical Representations in the Motor System.

The idea that the brain leverages a hierarchical structure to facilitate generalization in the motor domain has a long history (5, 76–79) and has driven investigations into the organizational principle of the motor cortex (2, 80) and action control (81). Recent studies have provided additional evidence for a hierarchical organization of reaching and grasping actions in the motor cortex. Human neuroimaging work has found shared representations across dominant and nondominant hands for reaching and grasping in visuo-motor areas such as the PMd, the parietal lobe, and preSMA (20–22, 24, 26, 82). More recently, studies have tested effector independence for more distal effectors, the hand and foot, for pointing actions in the aIPS (24–26) and for reaching and grasping actions in additional parietal areas (aIPS, pIPS, midIPS), SMA, and PMd (29). However, despite behavioral support (68, 83), there is a paucity of neural evidence for effector-independence for other actions, and especially for uniquely human actions.

Here, we present evidence that effector is one such property that can be generalized across for tool-use representations. By expanding the scope of effector independence to include more evolutionarily recent actions, distal effectors, and individuals born without hands, our results further suggest that effector-independence is a general feature of action representations. Studies have shown that there are shared kinematic features and activation patterns in PMd/v and PPC between actions performed by the hand and hand-held tools (45, 84). This results in the possibility that motor association areas have co-opted effector-independent principles of evolutionarily conserved actions and applied them to more complex tool-use actions.

The idea that areas of the motor cortex are organized based on action type has been shown in nonhuman primates and other mammals, suggesting that it is a fundamental principle established through evolution. Studies applying long trains of electrical stimulation to motor regions have discovered an action map whereby each zone encodes for complete multijoint sequences of evolutionarily preserved actions including hand-to-mouth actions, defensive movements, object manipulation actions, and reach-to-grasp actions (30, 85–94). These studies provided evidence that the motor system is organized by the performed action in addition to the effector. However, such spatial accuracy is impossible to achieve noninvasively in human participants. We instead leveraged the spatial accuracy of fMRI to show that action type is the driving organizational principle in both typically developed individuals and people born without hands. While future studies should test this outside of the fMRI scanner in more naturalistic (ethological) settings, our findings extend past work by demonstrating that the action-type-based organization can be effector-independent and is not limited to evolutionarily conserved actions.

In contrast to higher-level motor areas, early areas in the motor hierarchy show selectivity for the executing body part, regardless of the action, supporting a somatotopic organization. The temporal dynamics revealed by tMVPA contain effector-specific features in primary motor areas but not association tool-use regions (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). Critically, hand actions elicited greater activation in M1 hand area in TDs than for IDs despite hand function being taken over by feet in these individuals’ daily lives. These findings extend past studies on IDs when they performed simple muscle flexes [i.e., flexing/extending toes, (52)] and reaching/grasping actions (29), together suggesting that across motor domains, the organization principle of the primary sensorimotor cortex is innately constrained by somatotopy and shows less functional plasticity compared to association cortex.

We are, however, by no means suggesting that the motor system’s organization is strictly hierarchical. Instead, the motor system may be organized compositionally such that there is a mixed code of multiple representation dimensions (95), or gradients of abstraction within an area. Our univariate analysis showed a main effect of effector and of action type in different areas of the parietal lobe (Figs. 1 and 2) and the temporal dynamic patterns in M1 areas carry action information for nonnative effectors (Fig. 6), suggesting information beyond one level of a hierarchical gradient. This is in accordance with evidence for coexisting representations of multiple effectors and of both effector and action types within given motor areas (96–104), proposed as an effective way of reducing dimensionality in representations (81, 103). Studies have also proposed the idea of a modular hierarchy with separate concrete to abstract gradients within the parietal lobe, premotor cortex, and even the primary cortex (7, 9, 25, 26, 29, 85, 105, 106) which could serve as a way to facilitate neuromotor redundancy such that the same action can be executed many different ways. The existence of a mixed code or of gradients of representations could further be a way of supplying feedback during online motor control such that even if there is a generalizable motor plan, the error corrections and feedback could still be specific to the executing body part (107, 108).

While our data support a large-scale hierarchical organization in the motor domain, future studies are needed to investigate how an abstract action type is translated into specific sensorimotor parameters through either mixed-coding at smaller scales or cortico-cortical connections.

Plasticity Principles in Congenital Handlessness.

The fact that the tool-use network supports tool-use for the feet in people born without hands is consistent with our previous findings of evolutionarily conserved actions in this population. We recently showed that the dissociation between reaching and grasping, and the preference for the action type between them develops in people born without hands (29). This suggests that the motor association areas maintain their computational roles for action regardless of the acting body part, even in people born without the primary effector (see also ref. 32). This is achieved through the typical generalization of this system: Effector independence in the typical brain could allow for maintaining the same action even when the primary effector is missing from birth. More broadly, our findings with IDs are consistent with findings in people born blind and people born deaf. In both these cases, it appears that association cortical regions maintain their typical computations by replacing the missing input channel: The visual cortex category selectivity (for script, body shapes, objects, or faces) seems to develop in people born blind for the same categories perceived through audition or touch (109–115). Similarly, the language system in congenital deafness retains its processing preferences for sign language (116–118), and visual tasks recruit the parallel-function auditory cortex (119–121). In this work, we expand these findings to the tool-use network, suggesting that much like for perception, the association action system at large can maintain its functional roles regardless of sensorimotor experience. This appears to contrast with the primary motor cortex in IDs, which maintains an effector-based preference, as described above. In the context of reorganization, it reinforces the link between the original organization principles and the plasticity afforded in deprivation: Association cortices whose organization is task- or domain-based but that generalize across inputs or outputs can flexibly accommodate the loss of a certain input or output channel. Primary cortices whose organization is specific to low-level properties are bound to this level of organization even in early deprivation. Together, this double dissociation in reorganization highlights the capacities of plasticity in the human brain.

Implications for Brain–Machine Interfaces.

The concept of effector-independence has potential implications for brain–computer interfaces [BCIs; (122, 123)]. Most current prosthetics rely on lower-level kinematic information to restore function (122). However, the existence of an effector-independent abstract code and temporal dynamics makes the generalization to alternative effectors easily feasible. Indeed, learning to leverage a shared code between the hand and the foot in the premotor area facilitated motor recovery via BICs in individuals with tetraplegia when compared to using effector-specific information only (95). Likewise, it is possible to record from areas of the parietal lobe to command neuroprosthetics and restore motor function to those living with paralysis (124). Similar claims have been recently made about speech rehabilitation, where neuroprosthetics may read out the desired language output even across languages (125). These support the readout of a more abstract code, and at a level of goal or action-type selection, toward driving BCIs.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we investigated whether effector-independence extended to evolutionarily recent tool-use actions. We found that several core regions of the tool-use network, including the PMd and areas of the SPL, had a shared preference for tool-use across the hand and the foot, that was independent of sensorimotor experience. These results were bolstered by multivariate and temporal dynamics analyses. Together, our findings point to a hierarchical representation of actions in the motor system where there is generalization across the executing effector and regardless of effector experience. This supports an organization based on action type in the motor association cortex. Last, our findings show the link between the hierarchical organization of the typically developed cortex and the reorganization present in deprivation.

Materials and Methods

Participants.

Eighteen typically developed participants (abbreviated TDs; 11 females; mean age: 31.05; age range: 20 to 65) and six individuals born with severely shortened or completely absent upper limbs (individuals with upper-limb dysplasia; abbreviated IDs; two females; mean age: 40; age range: 21 to 62; see SI Appendix, Table S5 for additional details) participated in the experiment. All recruited IDs perform everyday actions primarily using their feet, and none reported experiencing a phantom hand. TDs were required to be right-handed and right-footed (by self-report, shown to strongly correspond to experimental measures; (126)). However, due to the rarity of complete and bilateral congenital upper-limb dysplasia, one ID (ID6) was left-footed. All participants performed the experiment with their dominant effectors. ID6’s data was included in the analysis and processed identically to that of the other participants, given the left-lateralization of the praxis system (33, 34, 46). Further, ID6’s data was consistent with that of the right-footed IDs (Figs. 3 and 4 and SI Appendix, Figs. S8 and S14). All experimental protocols and procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Georgetown University in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was collected from each participant, and they were compensated for their time.

Experimental Design and Procedures.

The neuroimaging experiment was a slow event-related design, with each effector (feet, hands) tested in two runs. Due to technical considerations, each effector was tested in separate runs (846TRs per run). Each run began with a baseline period of 7.8 s (10TRs), followed by pseudorandomized tool-use and grasping trials (six grasping and 12 tool-use trials per run). For each grasp action, participants reached and grasped a spatula (14 cm long, 1 cm handle diameter) or a crayon (4 cm long, 1.5 cm in diameter) without lifting the object. The tool-use action was meant to mimic the action of frosting a cake or spreading butter on toast. For each tool-use action, participants were instructed to reach and grasp a spatula and use it to spread 5 cm balloons partly filled with 2 mm polystyrene balls located 9 cm to the sides of the centrally located tool (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The balloons were filled such that they could be smeared and flattened but restore their shape after each trial, providing visual and somatosensory feedback during the movement. This action adheres to the description of canonical tool-use (36) and is comparable in complexity to tool-use actions used previously (127), including in creating consequences (albeit temporary) in the environment (flattened balloons). Participants were instructed to use the same grasp and starting position across conditions.

Participants began with their arms and legs resting naturally, with their head positioned such that they could directly view the board with the tools (see SI Appendix, Fig. S1 for posture details). Objects were centrally placed on a “grasparatus” (128). An experimenter inside the scanner replaced the target objects on a trial-by-trial basis during rest periods between trials. During this “rest” period, participants fixated on a cross on a mirror-viewed screen. ID2 and ID5 could not directly see the board as they lay supine. They instead viewed the objects using a mirror and closed their eyes during action execution, which was performed using tactile feedback. For each grasping action, participants reached toward and grasped the object between the first and second digits of the hand or foot. Object order in a run was balanced and pseudorandomized.

Each trial began with an auditory instruction indicating the action (e.g., “turn right”). Following the instructions, participants had 4.7 s (6TRs; action planning) before a sound cued them to begin executing the action (action execution). One single grasping or spreading action was performed per trial. Participants had 5.5 s (7TRs) to complete the action and held the action’s end position until a second sound cued the return to the uniform effector starting position (return period). During the return period participants would either return the object to its starting position on the board (hand trial) or drop it (foot trial; as most TDs could not quickly return the object with their feet; 1.6 s, 2TRs). Trials were spaced by 8.6 s (11TRs). Each action stage was modeled separately for fMRI analyses; here we report only analyses of the “action execution” stage. Separate practice sessions preceded the experiment to familiarize the participants with the trial timing and instructions (SI Appendix). All trials were visually inspected by an experimenter in real time. Trials that were missed or wrongly executed were excluded.

Functional Imaging.

Imaging data were collected in a Siemens Prisma-Fit 3T scanner. For acquisition details, see SI Appendix. Data that support the main findings of this study, as well as the source maps for the figures data are deposited in OSF (https://osf.io/jwv9q/).

Data Analysis.

Preprocessing.

Imaging data were preprocessed and analyzed using BrainVoyager (Brain Innovation, BV22.4). Functional images were preprocessed using BrainVoyager’s standard procedures (see SI Appendix for details).

Additional nuisance regressors and data cleaning were included to account for head motion (see SI Appendix for full details): 1) ventricle and white matter time course nuisance regressors (129–131), 2) first temporal derivative of motion parameters nuisance regressors (132–135), 3) TR censoring based on framewise displacement (134, 136–138), and 4) data exclusion of runs with more than 50 censored TRs.

Univariate analysis.

For all univariate analyses, functional data were spatially smoothed using a 6 mm FWHM kernel. Each stage (action planning, action execution, return) of the conditions of interest (grasping, tool-use) was modeled separately. Three different GLMs were generated (one per group/effector), with RFX GLMs for TDs’ hand and foot separately and a fixed-effect GLM for the IDs due to the sample size (139). We calculated two Effector × Action-type RFX ANOVAs, focusing on action execution, to examine 1) the consistency across actions performed with different effectors by TDs (TDs’ hand and foot; TDH+TDF, Fig. 1C) and 2) actions performed with the dominant effector across the groups (TDs’ hand and IDs’ foot; TDH+IDFs, Fig. 1F). The second allowed us to control for the potential confound that TDs represent foot actions via manual imagery. Activation maps were thresholded at P < 0.001 and corrected for multiple comparisons at P < 0.05 using the spatial extent method (140, 141). Then, the spatial overlaps between the main effects of the two ANOVAs were calculated (Fig. 2).

Direct contrasts for tool-use preference (tool-use > grasp along with tool-use > rest, to focus on positive activation) were then performed for each effector/group separately (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Given the stricter contrast, each activation map was thresholded at P < 0.01 and corrected for multiple comparisons at P < 0.05 as described above. The spatial overlap between the three maps was then calculated (Fig. 3C). Given the use of a fixed-effects GLM in the IDs, this analysis was additionally performed for each ID to ascertain interindividual consistency (SI Appendix, Fig. S8).

To directly test whether typical left-lateralized tool-use regions are effector-independent, we sampled ROIs based on TDs’ hand (the peak F-value from the main effect of action type in an ANOVA) and tested their tool-use preference in the foot data of both groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 and Tables S3 and S4). See details in SI Appendix.

Motor behavior recording and analysis.

To verify that all main findings were not driven by a difference in either execution onset times or action duration, we replicated all core analyses while explicitly modeling the hemodynamic response with the movement onset and duration recorded with accelerometers during the scans. For details, see SI Appendix.

Multivariate analyses.

For all multivariate analyses, functional data were spatially smoothed using a 3 mm FWHM kernel.

ROI MVPA.

ROI MVPA was performed in hand tool-use selective ROIs (Fig. 3 A and B) using a linear support-vector machine classifier implemented in the CoSMoMVPA toolbox (142). GLM beta estimates were obtained for each trial using SPM12. Given that additional head movement-related noise could artificially increase the decoding accuracy, we baselined our responses to the white matter of the ATL in addition to the 0.5 chance level, depending on which one is greater. Given that the paradigm involved an auditory instruction and visuo-motor task, few areas could serve as control regions. We defined two 4 mm radius spheres, one in each hemisphere, to match the relative size of the tool-use ROIs.

Within-effector/group decoding was performed using leave-one-pair-of-trials-out cross-validation where one grasping and one tool-use trial was left out in each iteration. Cross-decoding in TDs used leave-one-effector-out cross-validation where the classifier was trained on hand runs and tested on foot runs and vice versa. For details of trial balancing and ATL baselining procedures see SI Appendix. A one-tailed, paired t test against zero (after baseline correction) was performed for each effector/group. Significance is reported at P < 0.05, and FDR-corrected across ROIs for each effector or group. Note that the decoding results are unlikely to be driven by object identity as object labels were not yoked with action type, and ROIs were previously found to not be sensitive to object-specific information (143, 144).

Cross-effector whole-brain MVPA.

To test for a shared action representation between the hand and foot beyond these tool-use areas, we additionally performed a whole-brain searchlight MVPA across TD’s hand and foot. See SI Appendix for details. The same cross-validation scheme was used as in ROI MVPA. Group-level results were thresholded at P < 0.0001 and corrected for multiple comparisons at P < 0.05.

Time-resolved MVPA.

To examine how early within trials the actions are discernable, we computed time-resolved action decoding. Within each tool-use ROI defined from the univariate analyses, we decoded the action at each TR, within each effector/group as well as cross-effector across the TDs’ hand and foot (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). The preprocessed time series were divided by temporal mean and segmented into trials of 26TRs from the beginning of the auditory cue to the last TR of the inter-trial baseline. Then, at each TR, we performed MVPA as described above. Group-level results were obtained by performing a one-sample t test against 0.5 at each TR. Significance is reported at P < 0.05, FDR-corrected across TRs for each ROI.

Temporal Analysis Methods.

Time-course GLM analysis.

To test whether the temporal dynamics of the action network are similar for hand and foot actions, we leveraged the identical trial order across body parts (except for participants TD1-TD4, where an additional condition was included). We used the time courses from the TDs’ hand data as predictors in a GLM of the IDs’ foot data. Average time courses across the TDs’ hand runs were sampled from one effector-independent region (PMd; defined from the TDH+TDF action-type effect ANOVA) and one effector-dependent region (hand M1; defined from the TDH+TDF effector effect ANOVA) for this analysis. GLM calculations and contrasts were computed as described above.

tMVPA.

Temporal multivariate pattern analysis was performed in accordance with (58), in ROIs defined in the univariate ROI analysis (tool-use effector-independent regions) and hand and foot M1 ROIs defined from the TDH+TDF effector effect ANOVA. The preprocessed time courses were divided by the temporal mean and segmented into trials of 26TRs. For each participant and ROI, a stRDM was calculated for each unique condition. Specifically, the 1 − Pearson’s correlation was calculated between the response pattern at each time point in Trial X with each time point in Trial Y, forming a 26-by-26 RDM between Trial X and Trial Y. An stRDM was calculated for all possible trial pairs and then averaged as a final stRDM for each individual. Finally, stRDMs were averaged across participants for each effector/group and action type (e.g., Fig. 6A). As in ref. 58, we performed multivariate analyses on the Fisher z-transformed correlation coefficients and plotted the (1 − raw correlation coefficient) for visualization.

Multivariate pattern analysis was performed on the stRDMs to test whether each effector and action is associated with distinct temporal dynamics patterns (Fig. 6, Middle). The within-effector decoding of action type was performed in a leave-one-subject-out manner. For the decoding of effector/group within action, in each iteration an stRDM from TDs’ hand, TDs’ foot, and IDs’ foot was randomly left out and a three-way SVM classifier was trained on the remaining data. We also decoded action across effectors/groups and decoded effectors/groups across actions. Significance in all cases was determined by permutation tests. See SI Appendix for details.

To rule out the possibility that differences in stRDM are driven by head motion signals, we conducted a control analysis using stRDMs based on the head motion parameters. See SI Appendix for details.

Finally, hierarchical clustering analyses were performed based on the Euclidean distance between stRDMs using the linkage function in MATLAB.
