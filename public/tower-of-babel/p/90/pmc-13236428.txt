Refining the Predictive Role of T2-Weighted Imaging Features in Breast Cancer: A Hypothesis Linking Tumor Characteristics to Extreme Chemoresistance
Authors: Jia Cao, Yanmei Tan, Liyan Zhang, Chengla Gu, Hui Yang, Lina Zhang
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the article by Youn et al. [1], titled “Association of T2-Weighted Imaging Features in Invasive Breast Cancer With Clinicopathologic Features and Neoadjuvant Treatment Outcomes,” published in the Korean Journal of Radiology. The authors should be commended for their meticulous work in demonstrating the prognostic value of T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) features, particularly the novel association between baseline T2 hyperintensity/prolonged relaxation time and disease progression during neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC). This provides a compelling rationale for incorporating routine T2WI into multiparametric assessments.

We wish to propose an integrative hypothesis to resolve the intriguing discrepancy noted in the results and to deepen the interpretation of the study’s findings. The authors reported that T2 features were significantly associated with disease progression during NAC (P < 0.05) but not with pathological complete response (pCR) (P > 0.05). While these results were presented separately, their juxtaposition suggests a more nuanced predictive role of T2WI than was initially apparent.

The dichotomous pCR/non-pCR endpoint, although clinically useful, masks significant heterogeneity in the non-pCR group. The Residual Cancer Burden (RCB) classification system provides a more granular stratification, ranging from minimal residual disease (RCB-I) to extensive chemoresistant disease (RCB-III) [2,3].

We hypothesize that the observed T2-high signal and prolonged T2 relaxation time may not simply predict generic “non-response,” but rather specifically identify the subset of tumors destined for the poorest chemosensitivity, which are those that will progress on therapy or achieve only RCB-III status [4].

This hypothesis elegantly reconciles the key results of the study. Disease progression during NAC is a clinical manifestation of extreme chemoresistance, which is highly correlated with RCB-III pathology. The significant link between T2 features and disease progression strongly supports their role in flagging the worst-outcome subgroup. If T2 features are specific to the RCB-III phenotype (a subset of non-pCR), their predictive signal would be diluted when the non-pCR group is analyzed as a whole (including RCB-I/II responders with better prognosis), leading to a non-significant association with the broad “non-pCR” category [2,3].

Furthermore, this framework explains the authors’ insightful observation that quantitative T2 relaxation time is significantly associated with hormone receptor status and molecular subtypes that were missed by visual grading. Visual assessment may be adequate for identifying overt necrosis. In contrast, quantitative mapping could be sensitive to more subtle yet biologically critical intratumoral environments, such as specific patterns of stromal edema, fibrosis, or vascular permeability, which are hallmarks of profoundly resistant and aggressive biology (i.e., the RCB-III phenotype) [5]. This aligns with recent advances in T2WI standardization. A study published in the Korean Journal of Radiology demonstrated that optimized T2 sequences (aided by deep learning) improved the detection of tumor microstructural heterogeneity, reinforcing the clinical value of refining T2-based assessments to capture biologically relevant tumor features [6].

If validated, this specificity would significantly enhance the clinical utility of T2WI. It would transition from a general risk indicator to a precise tool for the early identification of patients at the highest risk of treatment failure who might also be candidates for alternative or intensified treatment strategies upfront. We strongly encourage the authors and other researchers to test this hypothesis in future studies by correlating pretreatment T2WI features—both visual and quantitative—with postoperative RCB scores rather than with the binary pCR endpoint. A prospective study with RCB as the primary pathological outcome measure could definitively establish whether T2WI is a unique biomarker of extreme chemoresistance [4].

In conclusion, the work by Youn et al. [1] has successfully identified a promising biomarker. We believe that interpreting its predictive value through the lens of RCB classification provides a coherent, biologically plausible model that clarifies the results and directs highly focused future research. We thank the authors for their contributions to this field.
