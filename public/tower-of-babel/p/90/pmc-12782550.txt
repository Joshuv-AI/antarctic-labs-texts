Clinical outcomes of intravascular lithotripsy according to the timing of stent failure: Insights from the COIL registry
Authors: Michał Kuzemczak, Janusz Lipiecki, Jacek Legutko, Johan Bennett, Joseph Dens, Mohammad Alkhalil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCumulative data has highlighted the efficacy of intra-vascular lithotripsy (IVL) in patients with stent failure (SF). However, it remains unclear whether the effectiveness of IVL, and subsequent clinical outcomes, are influenced by the timing of SF. We aimed to evaluate the outcomes of patients with SF undergoing IVL according to the age of index stent implantation.MethodsThis is a pre-specified subgroup analysis of the COIL registry (coronary intravascular lithotripsy in patients with stent failure), which included patients who underwent IVL treatment for SF, divided according to the timing of SF, i.e., early (≤ 12 months), mid-term (12–36 months), and late SF (> 36 months). Procedural and clinical outcomes up to 12 months following IVL treatment were recorded. The primary endpoint was defined as the composite of cardiovascular death, spontaneous myocardial infarction, or target vessel revascularization (TVR).ResultsThere were 88 patients included in this sub-study, of whom 40 (45%), 17 (19%), and 31 (35%) had early, mid-term, and late SF, respectively. Final procedural results were better in those with early compared to mid-term or late SF. At one-year follow-up, the incidence of the primary endpoint was associated with the timing of SF (8% vs. 12% vs. 29%, p = 0.042). The difference among the subgroups was driven by TVR (3% vs. 12% vs. 29%, p = 0.005).ConclusionsProcedural and clinical outcomes following IVL treatment for patients with SF were influenced by the timing of index stent implantation. Further studies are needed to better understand the mechanisms behind recurrent SF.

Introduction

Despite significant progress in lesion preparation strategies alongside better design of coronary stents, stent failure (SF) remains a significant challenge in contemporary clinical practice [1]. The introduction of drug-eluting stents (DES) has resulted in a reduction in the incidence of in-stent restenosis (ISR) compared to the use of bare-metal stents (BMS) [2–4]. Nonetheless, data from the American College of Cardiology National Cardiovascular Data Registry CathPCI Registry demonstrated that ISR is a target lesion for further intervention in almost 10% of all percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) [5]. Moreover, this subset was associated with a significantly higher risk of adverse long-term major ischemic events compared to PCI of de novo lesions [5].

An important mechanism of SF is related to stent underexpansion, which has been reported in up to 42% of cases in some studies, particularly in calcified lesions [6–8]. Therefore, adequate lesion preparation is mandatory prior to stent implantation to ensure optimal stent expansion and to avoid early and late SF [9]. This becomes more important in patients with previous SF as the rate of long-term adverse outcomes can be as high as 40% [10]. An armamentarium of calcium modification tools is currently available when it comes to managing both de novo and previously stented segments [9, 11]. Intra-vascular lithotripsy (IVL) has been used in patients with previous SF [12, 13]. Nonetheless, it is still considered an off-label indication, despite accumulating data supporting its safety and efficacy in patients with previous SF [12, 14]. The pathophysiology of SF is known to be multifactorial, including clinical and procedural factors that may contribute to its future risk [15]. The treatment strategies may differ according to the mechanisms of SF, which is related to the timing of SF [15]. Notably, the use of IVL in patients with ISR has not been studied according to the timing of SF. Better understanding of IVL performance and its clinical outcomes may help in tailoring treatment to patients in the future. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate procedural results and future adverse events in patients with SF who received IVL treatment according to the timing of SF.

Methods

Study cohort

The present study is a pre-specified subgroup analysis of patients who were enrolled into the coronary intravascular lithotripsy in patients with stent failure (COIL) registry. This registry was a multicenter, international, retrospective collaboration evaluating clinical outcomes of patients undergoing IVL treatment for SF in 6 European centers [12, 16]. Patients with evidence of SF not responding to balloon angioplasty who underwent IVL were consecutively enrolled into the registry between January 2019 and May 2022. The study cohort was followed up for one year.

For patients to be included in the COIL registry, the target lesions for IVL had to meet one of the following criteria:

clear evidence of ISR in a previously stented segment [degree of stenosis (DS%) ≥ 70%] or

clear evidence of ISR in a previously stented segment [degree of stenosis (DS%) ≥ 70%] or

acutely under-expanded stent that did not respond to standard balloon angioplasty treatment with evidence of a “dog-bone” effect when using post-dilation, non-compliant balloon inflation.

acutely under-expanded stent that did not respond to standard balloon angioplasty treatment with evidence of a “dog-bone” effect when using post-dilation, non-compliant balloon inflation.

The study did not include patients with stent thrombosis. Intra-vascular lithotripsy treatment was delivered using a C2 Shockwave Lithotripsy device (Shockwave Medical Inc., Santa Clara, United States), which consists of an electrical pulse generator and lithotripsy electrodes mounted on a rapid exchange balloon catheter, as previously described [12, 16]. The latest iteration of IVL delivering up to 120 pulses was not used in the present study, and only balloons with a maximum of 80 pulses were included.

In the present sub-study, evaluation of clinical outcomes was stratified according to the timing of SF, defined as the time interval between the initial stenting procedure and IVL treatment for SF. Timing of SF was subsequently categorized into three groups of early (SF within 12 months following the stent implantation), mid-term (SF occurring more than 12 months and up to 36 months), and late SF (more than 36 months following the stent implantation).

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The need for informed consent was waived by the institutional Medical Ethical Committee because the investigators from participating centers ensured that all the data were anonymized and obtained from each center database utilized for routine patient care.

Data collection and coronary angiogram analysis

A pre-specified anonymized case report form was used by investigators in each center. It included clinical, procedural, and outcome data for each patient during their in-hospital stay and at one-year follow-up.

Pre- and post-IVL coronary angiograms were analyzed offline by an investigator blinded to the timing of SF and patients’ clinical outcomes. Quantitative coronary angiography (QCA) analysis was performed using QCA CAAS software (Pie Medical Imaging, Netherlands) or QAngio XA 3D/QFR solution software (Medis Medical Imaging Systems bv., Leiden, Netherlands) as described previously [12, 16]. The final QCA measurements were the average derived from two orthogonal views with the least amount of foreshortening and overlap. The reference coronary artery diameter, the minimal luminal diameter (MLD), and pre-procedure DS% were evaluated from baseline coronary angiograms. Final MLD, and DS% were derived from the final coronary angiography and after any adjunctive treatment which included stent implantation. Severe calcifications in coronary arteries were defined as the presence of radiopaque densities without cardiac motion prior contrast injection involving both sides of a vascular wall [17].

Study endpoints

Procedural success in the studied groups was defined as residual stenosis < 30% in the absence of serious procedure-related complications at the end of the procedure such as coronary dissection, coronary perforation, abrupt vessel closure, and reduced coronary flow (TIMI flow < 3). The primary endpoint was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) defined as a composite of cardiovascular death, spontaneous myocardial infarction (MI), or target vessel revascularization (TVR). Secondary endpoints included definite stent thrombosis, target lesion failure (TLF), and non-target vessel revascularization. Target lesion failure was defined as repeat PCI or coronary artery bypass graft surgery (CABG) of the coronary segment that was previously treated with IVL.

Statistical analysis

Data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data and compared using Student’s t-test. For non-normal data, results were expressed as a median with interquartile range (IQR) and compared by the Mann–Witney U test. Categorical data were presented as percentages, and frequencies were compared using the chi-square test. Kaplan–Meier curves were plotted, and a log-rank test was used to assess any differences in clinical endpoints between the patients with different timings of SF. All statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 28.0 (SPSS, Inc. Chicago, Illinois), and a p-value < < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 88 patients from the COIL registry were included in the present sub-study. There were 14 patients with unavailable dates of index stent implantation, who were excluded from this analysis. There were 40 patients (46%) with early SF, 17 patients (19%) with mid-term SF, and 31 patients (35%) with late SF. The median times of SF were 6, 24, and 90 months in patients with early, mid-term, and late SF, respectively.

There was no difference in age, gender distribution, or traditional cardiovascular risk factors among the three groups (Tab. 1). Previous CABG was more frequent in patients with late SF (8%, 6%, 26%, p = 0.049), and creatinine clearance was worse in those with mid-term SF (p = 0.036).

Similarly, procedural characteristics were comparable across the three groups, including lesion location (LAD versus non-LAD, or ostial versus non-ostial), severe calcification, and reference vessel diameter. However, radial access (93% vs. 65% vs. 87%, p = 0.024), pre-dilatation (98% vs. 77% vs. 97%, p = 0.01), and intra-vascular imaging (68% vs. 24% vs. 58%, p = 0.009) were less frequently encountered in patients with mid-term SF (Tab. 2). Importantly, lesion severity, defined as lesion length, pre-IVL MLD, and DS%, was similar between the three groups. Likewise, the use of IVL treatment, including the size of the balloon and number of shockwave pulses, were also comparable among the groups (Tab. 2). Baseline clinical and procedural characteristics are presented in Tables 1 and 2, respectively.

Following IVL treatment, procedural success was achieved in 70 (79.5%) patients in the whole cohort, and it was highest in those with early SF (93%). There was a stepwise success rate, according to timing of SF, in reaching larger final MLD (2.82 ± 0.60 vs. 2.47 ± 0.51 vs. 2.33 ± 0.54; p = 0.002) and final DS (14.7 ± 7.0 vs. 25 ± 12 vs. 25 ± 13; p < 0.001) (Fig. 1).

At one-year follow-up, the incidence of MACE was different according to the timing of SF, and patients with early SF had the lowest adverse events compared to those with mid- and long-term SF (8% vs. 12% vs. 29%, p = 0.042) (Tab. 3, Fig. 2). This was mainly driven by TVR rates (3% vs. 12% vs. 29%; p = 0.005) with no difference in rates of cardiovascular death or spontaneous myocardial infarction (Tab. 3, Fig. 3). Patients with late SF had more frequent TLR (3% vs. 12% vs. 26%; p = 0.013) with no difference in other secondary endpoints such as stent thrombosis or non-TVR.

In the whole cohort, patients with procedural success were less likely to have TVR (10% vs. 28%, p = 0.05) and TLF (10% vs. 22%, p = 0.16).

Discussion

The present sub-study of the COIL registry is the largest study evaluating clinical outcomes of patients with SF undergoing IVL according to the timing of SF. The main findings from this study can be summarized as follows: 1) Almost half of the patients received IVL treatment for early SF; 2) procedural success, final MLD, and DS were the worst in patients with late SF; and 3) this translated into more frequent adverse clinical events in this subgroup of patients, which was mainly driven by TVR and TLF rates.

Coronary interventions in patients with SF are common and associated with a high risk of future adverse events [5]. SF is a heterogenous condition with different patient-, procedural-, and stent-related factors that may contribute to this risk [15]. Diabetes, sub-optimal lesion preparation and the use of thick stent struts are among numerous factors that are recognized as potential causes of SF [15, 18–20]. Coronary calcifications remain an important cause of SF in de novo lesions as well as in previously stented segments [9, 12]. IVL has transformed the management of calcified lesions and has increasingly been utilized in cases with previous SF. Nonetheless, its efficacy has not been widely studied according to the timing of SF. This is important because timing of SF has been recognized as a relevant factor in determining the pathophysiology as well as treatment strategy for patients with SF [21].

The current analysis provided insights into the role of using IVL in patients with previously stented segments, according to the timing of SF. Procedural success was less frequent and final angiographic parameters were worse in patients with late SF. The median time from initial stent implantation and receiving IVL treatment in this group was 7.5 years. These results may reflect different atherosclerotic processes leading to SF or the use of first-generation DES or bare metal stents. The current sub-study did not include data on the type of index stent implantation, and therefore it would not be possible to ascertain the relationship between IVL treatment and stent platform. On the other hand, our study may suggest a differential treatment response to IVL treatment according to the timing of SF. Importantly, lesion severity as assessed by its length, MLD, and degree of stenosis was comparable across the three groups. This is particularly relevant because the use of IVL treatment was not different and the size of the balloon and the number of shockwave pulses were comparable across the studied groups.

Data on the relationships between the timing of SF targeted by IVL and clinical outcomes are lacking. Our study showed that patients with late SF who underwent IVL treatment had higher incidence of the primary endpoint at one year. This risk was predominately derived from TVR and TLF. There was no difference in the risk of cardiovascular death or myocardial infarction, although the number of events was small and definitive conclusions can therefore not be established. On the other hand, the risk of TVR and TLF was higher in patients with late SF. Whilst this may reflect more advanced coronary artery disease, lesion and procedural complexity were comparable among the three groups. Lesion length, location, MLD, DS, and severe calcification did not differ. Likewise, procedural duration and radiation exposure were also similar among the three groups. Interestingly, the use of intra-vascular imaging was not identical among the groups and may explain the difference in clinical outcomes [22–24]. However, only one-quarter of patients with mid-term SF underwent intra-vascular imaging compared to almost 60% of patients with late SF. Of note, patients who did not achieve good procedural results following IVL treatment were more likely to develop TVR and TLF. This underlines the importance of optimal lesion preparation to reduce the risk of future adverse events. Whether the use of other calcium modification tools would improve patients’ clinical outcomes is yet to be determined.

Our study has several limitations that need to be highlighted. Firstly, this was a retrospective registry with inherent limitations associated with the nature of this design. Nonetheless, the COIL registry remains the largest cohort of IVL treatment in patients with SF. There were few differences in baseline characteristics across the three groups, which are likely to be rendered non-significant once multiple testing are accounted. Secondly, the platform of the index stents was not available in all patients, hindering any meaningful analysis regarding possible association of IVL treatment and specific stent type. We do not have data on the patients’ compliance with well-established guideline-directed medical therapies. Moreover, it is highly likely that the majority of included patients were discharged on clopidogrel, given that more than half of the patients presented with stable angina. Additionally, the reimbursement policies in certain countries, which were in place at the time of the study, did not allow the use of potent P2Y12 in patients with ACS. This may have an impact on patients’ clinical outcomes over time, which could also have influenced their outcomes, e.g., the risk of in-stent restenosis in long-term observation. Lastly, intravascular imaging was not systematically performed or recorded. This did not allow for in-depth analysis of ISR phenotypes/mechanisms and their impact on adverse events. We have previously highlighted the role of intra-vascular imaging in patients with complex anatomy from the COIL registry [16].

Conclusions

Procedural and clinical outcomes following IVL treatment for patients with SF were influenced by the timing of index stent implantation. Further studies are needed to better understand the mechanisms behind recurrent stent failure.
