Improving foaming properties of fish gelatin by neutral protease and gum arabic
Authors: Yu Chen, Yi Pan, Yu-Ting Hu, Le-Ying Yang, Lin-Ru Wang, Xiao-Mei Sha, Zong-Cai Tu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neutral protease and gum arabic (GA) were combined to enhance foaming ability (FA) and foaming stability (FS) of fish gelatin. Meanwhile, structural changes of pure, single-modified and combined-modified fish gelatins were evaluated in detail to investigate the mechanism. FA and FS of combined-modified fish gelatin were boosted to the maximum of 158% and 55%, respectively. The enzymolysis enhanced the surface hydrophobicity (increased by 40%) and destroyed the microstructure. On the basis, hydrogen bond and electrostatic forces of combined-modified fish gelatin were strengthened by GA, which elevated viscosity and interfacial protein adsorption content (6 mg/g), enhancing FS and FA. The redshift of amide I and II wavenumbers indicated more stable secondary structure of combined-modified fish gelatin, reflected in the SEM-verified denser foam. The underlying mechanism for improving foaming properties of fish gelatin was related to the opened structure by neutral protease and further noncovalent interaction by GA. New insights for the development of sustainable foaming agents were provided by these findings.

Introduction

Gelatins have been extensively explored and used in fields of food, cosmetics, drug delivery, and tissue engineering due to their significant advantages, such as unique functional properties, favorable biocompatibility, biodegradability, and abundant extraction sources1,2. At present, the predominant gelatin available on the market is mammalian gelatin, including porcine gelatin and bovine gelatin, etc3. However, the consumption of gelatin from these mammalian species contradicts ethnocultural and religious norms of a number of religions, and is also associated with the risk of contracting prion diseases (in particular, spongiform encephalopathy)4,5. Fish gelatin is considered a kosher and halal foods6, with high safety performance, good market compatibility, sufficient raw materials, and other advantages7. Foaming is an important property affecting the application of fish gelatin. However, the regular foaming properties of fish gelatin are not very ideal, which is not enough to meet the needs of practical applications8. Therefore, increasing in foaming properties of fish gelatin is deserved to be investigated in detail.

The methods to modify foaming properties can be divided into physical modification, chemical modification, enzymatic modification9, and combined modification10. Examples of physical modification include gelatin/surfactant mixtures and gelatin/polymer composites. Examples of chemical modification include cross-linked gelatin nanoparticles, glycosylated gelatin, and succinylated gelatin11. Fish gelatin can form a more stable structure and higher viscosity with gum arabic12. Makri et al.13, found that gum arabic had a positive effect on the foaming of the Phaseolus vulgaris and Coccineus proteins, which improving the foaming stability by nearly 33%. Enzymatic modification is distinguished by high efficiency, greenness, and cost-effectiveness14. The foaming properties of enzymatically pre-treated gelatin by neutral protease have been observed to be enhanced15. Compared with single modification methods (physical, chemical, or enzymatic modification), combined methods might ideally induce significantly higher functional improvements16. Stevenson et al.17, reported indirect 3D printing combined with freeze-drying technology was proposed as a promising method for the preparation of gelatin-based products. Zi et al.16, reported that octenyl succinic anhydride and surfactant were combined to modify bovine bone gelatin for emulsion stabilization. However, to our best knowledge, there was no report about the combined methods using neutral protease and gum arabic.

Therefore, individual and combined treatments of neutral protease and gum arabic were executed to modify fish gelatin, as well as comparative measurements of foaming properties were investigated in this work. Furthermore, the effect of adding neutral protease and gum arabic in different sequences on the foaming properties and intermolecular interactions of fish gelatin was studied. Thereinto, interfacial adsorption protein, rheology, and free amino group were used to study the difference in foam density and stability. In order to further explore the intermolecular reaction mechanism, the binding sites of fish gelatin formed by neutral protease and gum arabic were researched by SDS-PAGE, particle size, zeta potential, and surface hydrophobicity. FTIR and endogenous fluorescence could further explain the changes of intermolecular force. Moreover, the structure of the foams was characterized by SEM. The results generated by this study can provide a theoretical basis for the combined modified fish gelatin by gum arabic and neutral protease. It is also great significance for improving the quality of fish gelatin as a foaming agent. To address the lack of mechanistic understanding regarding the combined modification of fish gelatin by gum arabic and neutral protease, this study investigates their synergistic effects, providing a theoretical basis for developing superior foaming agents.

Result and discussion

Foaming properties of modified fish gelatins

As an important performance, the foaming properties show the phenomenon during which air is incorporated into fish gelatin solution by whipping, stirring, shaking, or bubbling11. FA and FS are the key parameters to describe the foaming properties. After enzymatic hydrolysis, FA increased to 140%, but FS decreased to 11.11% compared to that of the pure group. Lyu et al.18, found that FA of egg white proteins was increased from 69.67 to 108.33% after 15 min of neutral protease-mediated hydrolysis. Hydrolysis might be the reason for the formation of finer and more foam19. Moosavi et al.20, studied the effects of the enzyme-assisted acid swelling process on gelatin obtained from fish by-products; the FS of the enzyme-treated gelatins became lower. The observed decrease in FS and increase in FA might be attributed to the hydrophobic amino acids in fish gelatin were hydrolyzed to form partial hydrophobic groups and the destruction of the network structure. By contrast, when gum arabic was added to the fish gelatin solution, the FA increased to 124.44%, while the FS showed no significant change. Gum arabic was composed of an arabinogalactan-peptide (AG), an arabinogalactan-protein (AGP), and a glycoprotein (GP) fraction21. Among them, the β-(1,6)-galactopyranose side chains of the AGP were linked to the hydroxyl groups of the polar amino acids in the fish gelatin, forming a network of hydrogen bonds21,22. The forces might be the reason for the improvement of the foaming ability of the composite.

By contrast, it was surprising that the foaming properties of fish gelatin modified by neutral protease and gum arabic had significant improvement, with FA and FS being approximately increased to 158.89% and 55.4%, respectively. There was no significant effect on the foaming properties, whether neutral protease or gum arabic was added first. The surface of pure fish gelatin molecules could be adsorbed by a few gum arabic molecules23. And more polar amino acid side chains in enzymatic fish gelatin were exposed to connect with gum arabic. Furthermore, Leucine was one of the most abundant amino acid residues in gum arabic24, it could also be cut by neutral protease. Therefore, some hydrophobic residues will be exposed to improve hydrophobicity, and FA will be further improved. Meanwhile, the free carboxyl group in gum arabic formed ionic bonds with basic amino acids (such as lysine and arginine) in fish gelatin24. More hydrogen bonds were formed between fish gelatin and gum arabic, contributing to the formation of a “film” around the foam25, and the foam structure became more stable. The formation of electrostatic force and more hydrogen bonding force enhanced the foaming property and foaming stability of the composite. Therefore, the FS could increase with the enhanced stability of the foam structure. Collectively, the foaming properties of the combined modified fish gelatin were significantly improved.

Interface absorbed protein content

Enhanced foam stability was associated with greater interfacial protein adsorption and the corresponding lowering of interfacial tension26. As shown in Fig. 2A, the interface absorbed protein contents of fish gelatins were determined to further characterize the foaming stability. Among them, the content of interfacial adsorption protein in enzymatic modified fish gelatin was the least, which was about 2 mg/g lower than that of pure fish gelatin. The content of fish gelatin modified by gum arabic was lower than that of pure fish gelatin about 0.7 mg/g. Abu-Ghoush et al.25, reported that the increase or the decrease in the interfacial tension within each polysaccharide–protein combination was due to the effect of the net attractive interaction and the degree of the adsorption and unfolding at the interface. The content of fish gelatin modified by gum arabic was lower could be due to competitive adsorption between the hydrophilic gum arabic and fish gelatin molecules at the air-water interface27, thereby causing the interfacial tension to be increased. In other words, the presence of gum arabic might alter the interfacial composition or the arrangement of protein molecules, thereby affecting the measured adsorbed protein content. The contents of the combined modified fish gelatins were higher than others, being 2 mg/g higher than that of pure fish gelatin. Based on the foaming stability results, in addition to the interfacial tension being reduced, the electrostatic and hydrogen bonding forces were further increased, leading to the formation of continuous viscoelastic films at the interface through non‑covalent intermolecular interactions, which resulted in enhanced foaming stability of the composite gelatin foam. The protein adsorption was accelerated by these intermolecular forces, especially in the diffusion and rearrangement phases28, thereby the interfacial adsorption protein content were increased. The content of fish gelatin initially modified by gum arabic was even higher. The observed effect was attributed to the more thorough pre-complexation of gum arabic with fish gelatin, thereby laying the groundwork for the later development of an interfacial network with enhanced elasticity. In this supposition, the formation of a film around the foam was contributed by the electrostatic and hydrogen bonding forces, which might be the reason for the higher interface absorbed protein content. Free amino group content

The free amino group is employed to characterize the degree of hydrolysis. The effects of neutral protease and gum arabic modification on the hydrolytic degree of fish gelatins were presented in Fig. 2B. The free amino groups in the enzymatically modified fish gelatin showed the highest content, which was increased by approximately 0.1 mg/mL. The molecular chain was partially broken, the internal structure was exposed, and more free groups emerged30,31. The molecular weight might also decrease, the intermolecular friction increased32, and the foaming ability was improved. Zhang et al.33, reported that the tilapia skin gelatin hydrolysate obtained by progressive hydrolysis using multiple sets of neutral protease had the highest degree of hydrolysis. With gum arabic added to fish gelatin, the content of free amino groups was lower than that of pure fish gelatin. As a hydrophilic colloid, the carboxyl group in the molecule binds to the free amino group of fish gelatin34. Therefore, more free amino and carboxyl groups were interconnected to form a tighter structure that the foaming properties of the combined modified fish gelatin were the best. In addition, there is no significant difference in the content of fish gelatin modified by the combination of modified substances in different addition orders.

Rheological properties analysis

Viscosity is influenced by multiple factors, including shear rate, pH, temperature, shear pressure, and time35,36. Supplementary Table 1 shows that the Power-Law model was used to analyze the influence of neutral protease and gum arabic on fish gelatin fluid properties. The characteristic viscosity (c) was increased with the composite modification of neutral protease and gum arabic. The fluid shows shear dilution when the fluid index (p) was <1. This is a non-Newtonian fluid (pseudoplastic fluid). When p = 1, the sample is an ideal fluid (Newtonian fluid); at p > 1, there is shear thickening, which implies a non-Newtonian fluid (expanded plastic fluid)37. The pseudoplastic degree of the solution is larger when the p-value is smaller; thus, shear dilution is more likely to occur38. Supplementary Table 1 shows the gelatin solutions exhibit non-Newtonian fluid properties and shear dilution behavior of fish gelatin with adding neutral protease was more obvious. As illustrated in Fig. 2C, D, the viscosity of fish gelatin solutions decreased with increasing shear rate, indicating that all formulations exhibited pseudoplastic behavior22. Compared to pure fish gelatin, samples modified with gum arabic or neutral protease individually was showed lower viscosity, whereas the combined modification resulted in higher viscosity. This trend in viscosity aligned with the variations observed in foaming stability. Modifications with neutral protease and gum arabic were known to alter the molecular conformation and intramolecular bonding strength of proteins22,39. Therefore, it could be inferred that foaming stability was likely affected by changes in molecular shape and the strength of intramolecular chemical bonds.

A greater extent of hydrolysis, coupled with diminished intermolecular interactions, was likely accounted for the observation that enzymatic fish gelatin showed the lowest viscosity30,40. The viscosity of the hydrolyzed collagen from sheepskins was decreased as the enzymatic hydrolysis time was increased40. The lower viscosity of gum arabic modified fish gelatin could be attributed to the molecular changes resulting from the attachment of the anionic polysaccharide groups to fish gelatin molecules23,41. Liu et al.23, investigated the physico-chemical and functional properties of silver carp myosin glycated with konjac oligo-glucomannan and found that the polysaccharide could change gelatin molecules and surface charges. The hydration layers and the electrostatic interaction of proteins would be altered by the gelatin molecules and surface charges, resulting in lower viscosity42. However, viscosities of combined modified fish gelatins were the highest, which meant the negative effects of gum arabic and neutral protease would be mutually reduced. In combined modified gelatins, the viscosity of the preferential enzymatic fish gelatin was changed from being higher to lower than that of the other, which with the increase of shear rate. Anvari et al.43, studied the rheological and structural characteristics of the composite condensed phase of fish gelatin and gum arabic. The formation of a coacervate phase with a larger volume fraction and higher biopolymer concentration was more viscous, more structurally resistant at low shear rates, more shear-thinning at high shear rates, and more condensed in microstructure. Thus, the foaming stability of fish gelatin was improved effectively when modifications were executed by neutral protease and gum arabic simultaneously.

Therefore, the effect of neutral protease and gum arabic modification at the molecular size would be further investigated by SDS-PAGE. The formation of the soluble complex was resulted from electrostatic attractions between these two biopolymers, primarily43. To better understand the interaction, the zeta potential was measured47 (Table 1). The zeta potential values for gum arabic or neutral protease-modified fish gelatins were found to be lower than those for pure gelatin. The decrease in the zeta potential of fish gelatin after enzymatic hydrolysis might be due to the increase of negatively charged groups in the peptide chain after cleavage30. Based on the results concerning free amino group interactions, the lower zeta potential observed in gum arabic modified fish gelatin could be due to the negative charge associated with the hydrophilic colloid41. However, the observed increase in zeta potential after combined modification might be attributed to conformational changes in fish gelatin upon interaction with gum arabic, exposing the additional positively charged residues within the bulk phase48,49. The negative charge of gum arabic was primarily attributed to the deprotonation of the carboxyl groups on glucuronic acid residues, while the basic amino acid residues in fish gelatin were bound to these carboxyl groups41. Therefore, a portion of the negative charge on the gum arabic was neutralized. In addition, the encapsulation of fish gelatin-bound peptides might be induced by the long-chain structure of gum arabic22, resulting in spatial shielding and a consequent increase in the detected zeta potential. The electrostatic interaction force between fish gelatin and gum arabic was further increased, thereby increasing the foaming stability of the combined modified fish gelatin.

Surface hydrophobicity analysis

Surface hydrophobicity stands as a pivotal factor influencing foaming properties. The trends in surface hydrophobicity among various fish gelatins were depicted in Fig. 3B, reflecting the results of the observed foaming abilities. Notably, the surface hydrophobicity of modified fish gelatins underwent diverse degrees of enhancement, especially the combined modified fish gelatins were increased by about 40%. Peptide bonds within hydrophobic amino acids were primarily targeted by neutral proteases, thereby exposing additional hydrophobic groups to the surrounding environment18,44. In line with this, Park et al.50, have reported that enzymatic protein hydrolysis facilitated the exposure of internal hydrophobic groups. More surface activity was exhibited by the hydrophobic modified protein than the original protein due to the enhanced hydrophobicity in structure51, resulting in increased surface hydrophobicity. What’s more, the improved hydrophobicity of gum arabic modified gelatin might be underlaid by the augmentation in hydrophobic interaction and electrostatic interaction52. Similarly, the foaming properties and surface hydrophobicity of soybean protein isolate hydrolysate were exhibited to increase, which was modified by gum arabic53. Therefore, combined with the above inference, the increase of surface hydrophobicity in combined modified fish gelatins was due to the exposure of internal hydrophobic groups and the enhancement of Intermolecular forces. After enzymatic hydrolysis, the binding sites of fish gelatin with gum arabic increase20, and the intensity of the hydrophobic interaction and electrostatic interaction formed between the two were further increased. Moreover, the foaming capacity of protein was promoted by exposing more hydrophobic residues of enzymatical fish gelatin, the positive correlation between surface hydrophobicity of proteins and foaming characteristics54. No consistent positive correlation was observed between the foaming stability of fish gelatin and its surface hydrophobicity. Furthermore, the stability of foams depends on different parameters, such as the rate of attaining equilibrium surface tension, bulk and surface viscosities, steric stabilization, and electrical repulsion between the two sides of the foam lamella55. Nevertheless, the hydrophobic interaction force and electrostatic interaction were identified as potential factors contributing to the enhancement of foaming stability.

Intrinsic fluorescence analysis

Changes in the endogenous fluorescence spectra reflect alterations in the tertiary structure of proteins56, Fig. 3C was depicted to illustrate the intrinsic fluorescence intensity of various fish gelatins. The maximum fluorescence emission peak around 305 nm, mainly due to the effect of high-level tyrosine residues in fish gelatin57. Notably, enzymatic fish gelatin was exhibited to the lowest intensity. The research of Jiang31 indicated that hydrophobic amino acids were subjected to enzymolyze by neutral protease. The exposure of hydrophobic groups, such as tyrosine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan, within the protein matrix was associated with enzymatic hydrolysis58. In contrast, all the gum arabic-modified fish gelatins were displayed higher intensity levels than pure fish gelatin, implying that the tertiary structure of fish gelatin was more expanded. The potential unfolding of the tertiary conformation consequently exposed the hydrophobic amino acids59. Moreover, the intrinsic fluorescence intensity of the combined modified fish gelatins was higher than that of the individually modified and pure fish gelatins. The beneficial effect of gum arabic on opening the tertiary structure of fish gelatin was further elucidated in an enzymatic hydrolysis environment. Furthermore, the fish gelatin initially modified with gum arabic was demonstrated to the highest intensity. The initial addition of gum arabic played a pivotal role in the open of the fish gelatin conformation, demonstrably enhancing endogenous fluorescence intensity.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR can be used to analyze the changes of protein intramolecular or intermolecular forces, such as hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions60. A typical FTIR spectrum of fish gelatin showed three major peak regions: 3600–2300 cm–1 (amide A), 1656–1644 cm–1 (amide I), 1560–1335 cm–1 (amide II)61. The amide A peak absorption is due to N–H stretching, the amide I peak to C=O stretching, the amide II peak to N–H bending and C–N stretching vibrations62. The Fourier transform infrared spectrum of modified fish gelatin is shown in Fig. 3D.

The unmodified fish gelatin amide A position was 3450.027 cm−1, whereas the modified fish gelatin amide A had a significant redshift. The formation of more hydrogen bonds between fish gelatin and gum arabic was probably complexed after enzymatic hydrolysis. Strong hydrogen bonds could be formed between the polar groups (carboxyl, amino groups) of severally63. The amide I peak indicated C=O stretching, and its peak value and wave number were related to protein conformation and hydrogen bond61. The amide I peak of pure fish gelatin was located at 1635.34 cm−1 and the amide I band of modified fish gelatin was shifted. The modification significantly changed the secondary structure of fish gelatin, which may be caused by electrostatic interaction and hydrogen bond formation. The wave number of amide I in the complex system was lower than that of the original, which was consistent with the research of ref. 64. Gum arabic was a complex carboxyl polysaccharide, the carboxyl group of which interacted with the hydrogen bond in fish gelatin by electrostatic interaction, thereby the C=O was moved to the higher wave segment64. Viscosity of fish gelatin was influenced by the electrostatic interaction formed by gum arabic that was demonstrated. Similarly, the increase of free carboxyl groups in enzymatic interacted with hydrogen bonds in the protein, which increased the wave number of enzymatic modified fish gelatin. Therefore, the viscosity of fish gelatins might only be improved by electrostatic interaction and strong hydrogen bond at the same time, which immensely enhanced the foaming properties.

SEM

The formation of foam by fish gelatin entailed the absorption of a substantial amount of water, which was subsequently removed in order to prevent artefacts during SEM imaging and to ensure operational functionality. However, this process induced structural collapse and irregular grid formation. The porous architecture also indicated high moisture retention. As shown in Fig. 4, the pore diameter of the foam was reduced by enzymatic modification versus pure gelatin. Concurrently, the foam density was increased due to the increased hydrophobicity65. Gum arabic modification generated surface micropores and wrinkles, which were attributed to microbubble nucleation via shear and free polysaccharide molecules66. These micropores increased foam stability by reinforcing structural integrity. In accordance with the findings presented in discussions and Fig. 5, schematic models have been proposed to depict the molecular structures and structural characteristics of fish gelatins modified by neutral protease and gum arabic. The impact of different modifications on the foam morphology and molecular structure of fish gelatin was illustrated in Fig. 6. The protein structure of fish gelatin was opened by enzymatic hydrolysis, while gum arabic enhanced the intermolecular forces. The fish gelatin with the open structure was further combined with gum arabic in the joint modification to form a denser structure, thus producing the densest and richest foam. Integrated analyses of interfacial adsorption protein, zeta potential, surface hydrophobicity, fluorescence spectroscopy, FTIR, and SEM were revealed significant structural alterations in fish gelatin induced by combinatorial treatment. Enzymatic hydrolysis exposed hydrophobic groups of fish gelatin, elevating surface hydrophobicity and unfolding the molecular chains. The glycoprotein branched chains and free carboxyl groups in gum arabic were connected with hydroxyl and amino groups of fish gelatin, respectively. The hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions were promoted, which induced swelling of the tertiary structure and enhanced microstructural porosity. In the combined modification, the enzymatic fish gelatin was further combined with gum arabic through those groups, thus the forces of the complex were stronger. Consequently, the foams produced by the combined modified fish gelatin were smaller and denser. This densification was corroborated by elevated viscosity and interfacial protein adsorption, key determinants of foaming stability. In conclusion, this study demonstrated that the foaming ability of fish gelatin was enhanced by enzymatic modification, while foaming ability and foaming stability were most significantly improved by combinatorial treatment with gum arabic. The gelatin molecules were partially cleaved enzymatic hydrolysis by neutral protease, resulting in the opened molecular structure and many exposed groups. The hydrophobic force was increased with the exposure of hydrophobic groups, thereby elevating the foaming capacity. The electrostatic forces and hydrogen bond networks were established between the free carboxyl and glycosyl side chains of gum arabic and the basic and polar amino acids in enzymatic fish gelatin, respectively. Consequently, the foaming properties of the combined modified fish gelatin were further strengthened through these intermolecular forces. This modified fish gelatin serves as a high-performance, bio-based foaming and stabilizing agent. It can be utilized in the production of sustainable confectioneries (e.g., marshmallows, mousses), edible foams, and light-textured desserts.

Methods

Materials

Fish gelatin from Tilapia scale (260–270 Bloom) was purchased from Jiliding Marine Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). Neutral protease (from Bacillus subtilis phenol blue (BPB) came from Aladdin Industrial Corporation Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Gum arabic (345–700 kDa) was provided by Foodchem International Corporation Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). O-Phthalaldehyde (OPA) was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) kit, the loading buffer, and markers (11–245 kDa) used in SDS-PAGE were purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). The rest of the chemical reagents, such as NaOH, were of analytical grade.

Fish gelatin modified by neutral protease and gum arabic

Fish gelatin (5.00% w/v) was dissolved in distilled water at 40 °C for 30 min. The pH of the solution was then adjusted to 6.5, after which hydrolysis with neutral protease (10 U/g) was carried out at 40 °C for a further 30 min. Enzymatic inactivation was then achieved by heating to 100 °C for 5 min, after which the pH was re-adjusted to 6.5. Pure fish gelatin was abbreviated as FG, and pure enzymatic fish gelatin was abbreviated as FG10. Interface adsorbed protein content determination

Interface adsorbed protein content was determined by the method of Lechevalier et al.69, with some modifications. The sample was prepared into a 2% solution, and the protein content of the fish gelatin solution was determined by the BCA kit. The sample was beaten at 10,000 rpm at 25 °C for 2 min with a high-speed homogenizer, and the protein concentration in the bottom effluent was measured after standing for 30 min. The adsorption of interfacial protein was defined as the weight ratio (mg/g) of foam protein content to the initial protein content.

Degree of hydrolysis determination

According to the method proposed by Zheng et al.70, the free amino content of fish gelatin solution was determined with slight modifications. In brief, the OPA reagent contained 0.8 mg/mL OPA, 1 mL methanol, 10 mg/mL sodium dodecyl sulfonate (SDS), 19.0685 mg/mL borax, and 100 μL β-mercaptoethanol. 200 μL of sample was added to 4 mL of OPA reagent and mixed thoroughly. After reacting at room temperature for 2 min, the absorbance of the mixed solution at 340 nm was measured. Lysine was used as a standard to determine the content of free amino groups [y = 1.6211 x + 0.1769 (y: absorbance at 340 nm; x: lysine concentration, mg/L), R2 = 0.999].

SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE)

SDS-PAGE was performed on samples according to the method reported by Thuy et al.73, with slight modifications. 2 mg/mL gelatin solution was mixed with 4× protein loading buffer at a ratio of 3:1. The 4% stacking gel and 7.5% separating gel was used3. The mixture was boiled for 10 min in a water bath. After electrophoresis, the samples were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 for 30 min, and then decolorized until the substrate was colorless. The molecular weight of gelatin was estimated by comparison with that of standard protein markers.

Determination of particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta-potential

According to the method of Liu et al.23, with slight modification, the particle size distribution, PDI values, and zeta potential at 25 °C were measured by nanoparticle size and potential analyzer (zs180, Dandong Baite Technology Co., Ltd., Liaoning, China). The modified fish gelatins were diluted to 1 mg/mL with distilled water and directly injected into zeta potential dishes, and the bubbles in the dishes were removed. The refractive index and absorption parameters were 1.590 and 0.01, respectively.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

According to the improved method of Shi et al.60, with slight modification. At 25 °C, 2 mg of the samples were milled together with 200 mg of potassium bromide powder for the formation of slices. The slices were then scanned by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy at an interval of 400 –4000 cm−1 at a scanning frequency of 16 times.

Endogenous fluorescence determination

The method of measuring endogenous fluorescence was referred from Chen et al.75, and it was modified slightly. The modified fish gelatins were diluted to 0.5 mg/mL times, and the test parameters were as follows: ex = 280 nm, wavelength range was 200–400 nm, incidence and emission slit width were 5 nm, PMT voltage was 400 V, and scanning rate was 1200 nm/min.

SEM

Foam generated from a 10 mg/mL dispersible fish gelatin by homogenization (13,600 rpm, 2 min) was immediately transferred to containers, flash-frozen at −80 °C for 1 h, and fixed for 12 h with malondialdehyde76. The fixed samples were then sectioned and freeze-dried77. Dried modified gelatins were sputter-coated with platinum for 50 s to enhance the conductivity and then observed by SEM (S-3400, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) with an accelerated voltage of 15.0 kV11.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean values ± standard deviation. All experiments were repeated three times. SPSS Statistics 24 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used to perform one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA; p < 0.05). Statistical significance among means was evaluated using Duncan’s test78. Graph Prism 9 was used to the rendering of the image.
