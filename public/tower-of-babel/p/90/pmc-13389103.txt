Sarcoglycanopathy with absent expression of all sarcoglycan proteins in a young cat with clinical features of feline hypertrophic muscular dystrophy
Authors: Arian Doroudi, Anna Sirochman, Allison Mooney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Case summaryA 1-year-old, male castrated domestic shorthair cat presented for chronic mobility decline and muscle stiffness, first noted at 3 months of age, and persistently elevated serum creatine kinase levels (>19,000–53,000 IU/l). Clinical examination revealed macroglossia and selective hypertrophy of cervical and appendicular muscles. Neurologic evaluation indicated a myopathic gait without pain or neurologic deficits. Muscle biopsy was supportive of muscular dystrophy (MD) and immunohistochemistry demonstrated loss of the sarcoglycan complex with complete absence of all tested sarcoglycan proteins, consistent with a diagnosis of sarcoglycan-deficient MD. These findings contrast with prior feline reports that were mostly limited to reduction or absence of beta-sarcoglycan on immunostaining without overt clinical evidence of MD. A follow-up echocardiogram revealed mild to moderate right ventricular changes without indications for treatment. At 2 months after diagnosis, the patient remained clinically static, suggesting a relatively slow progression despite absent sarcoglycan expression.Relevance and novel informationThis case represents the first reported feline sarcoglycanopathy characterized by the absence of all tested sarcoglycan subunits on immunohistochemistry and concurrent presentation of classic Duchenne-like MD signs, including macroglossia and muscular hypertrophy. It expands the phenotypic spectrum of muscular dystrophies in cats, underscoring parallels to limb-girdle muscular dystrophies in humans and dogs. Given the cat’s static clinical course and diagnostic overlap with dystrophinopathies, this case suggests sarcoglycan-deficient MD in cats may present with a broader spectrum of severity than previously appreciated. The findings support the potential utility of feline sarcoglycanopathies, when phenotypically consistent with forms appreciated in other species, as spontaneous animal models for comparative medical research.

Introduction

Muscular dystrophy (MD) represents a heterogeneous group of inherited disorders characterized by progressive muscular degeneration and weakness. Although over 30 forms of MD have been described in human medicine, it is rarely diagnosed in cats, with X-linked dystrophinopathies being by far the most common underlying pathology described.

Genotypic and phenotypic analogs of Duchenne and Becker-type MD, the more and less clinically significant forms of human dystrophinopathies, respectively, have been described in cats with corresponding clinical signs and disease progression.1–3 Affected muscles commonly experience hypertrophy as well as the prominent atrophy seen in human and canine MD, although selective hypertrophy of muscle groups can be seen in those conditions as well. Dystrophin-deficient MD has also previously been called hypertrophic feline MD, although this classification is controversial because of the aforementioned hypertrophy seen in human and canine MDs.4,5 Other forms of MD reported in cats include laminin alpha (α)-2 deficiency, beta (β)-sarcoglycan deficiency and a familial α-dystroglycan deficiency in Sphynx and Devon Rex cats.6–10

The following report describes a cat evaluated with clinical features consistent with Duchenne-like MD, including macroglossia and selective muscular hypertrophy, that was eventually diagnosed with sarcoglycan-deficient MD. To the authors’ knowledge, this report represents the first case of feline MD characterized by a complete lack of expression of all tested sarcoglycan proteins, and the first such feline sarcoglycanopathy that presented with the expected phenotype in dogs and humans. The following case highlights sarcoglycan-deficient MD and the potential for sarcoglycanopathy in cats to be used as a model for human disease.

Case description

A 1-year-old, male castrated domestic shorthair (DSH) presented to the neurology department of a veterinary emergency and referral specialty hospital for further evaluation of chronic and slowly progressive decline in mobility and muscle stiffness first noted at approximately 3 months of age. As a younger kitten, isolated seizures were also reported, but no anti-seizure medications were prescribed. Before presentation, numerous diagnostics had been performed, including serial complete blood counts (CBCs) and biochemistry panels, which revealed mild neutrophilia, mild lymphocytosis, mild to moderately elevated alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase, low creatinine and markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) in the range of 19–24 × 103 IU/l. Previously performed Toxoplasma gondii IgM and IgG titers, feline immunodeficiency virus and feline leukemia virus testing were negative. A diagnostic abdominal ultrasound examination with a board-certified radiologist was also performed before presentation, revealing mildly coarse hepatic echotexture, mottled splenic echotexture with mild splenomegaly, a focally thickened loop of jejunum with mucosal thickening, mild to moderate lymphadenopathy affecting various abdominal lymph nodes and mild peritoneal effusion. Previous treatment with clindamycin and a recent tapering course of anti-inflammatory dexamethasone did not improve the clinical signs.

Physical examination at the time of presentation revealed moderate to marked macroglossia with lingual protrusion and marked hypertrophy of the intrinsic cervical muscles, rectus abdominus and proximal appendicular muscles. Mild bilateral elevation of the nictitans was also appreciated, with no discernible primary ocular abnormalities. The patient’s inspiratory effort was mildly increased; cardiorespiratory auscultation was otherwise unremarkable (Figures 1–3).

Upon neurologic examination, the patient was strongly ambulatory with a severely restricted swing phase most notable in the thoracic limbs and a lordotic posture consistent with proximal muscle weakness. The remainder of the neurologic examination was unremarkable. No spinal hyperpathia was identified. The patient had generalized muscle stiffness that was most severe in the cervical region, but no sign of discomfort was noted on muscle palpation. An in-house CBC revealed mild neutrophilia and eosinopenia. No significant abnormalities were observed on in-house venous blood gas evaluation. Serum CK levels reported by an external reference laboratory were markedly elevated at 53,807 IU/l.

After informed consent, the patient was placed under general anesthesia for collection of biopsies from the cleidocephalic muscle. Given the concern for MD and the associated rhabdomyolysis seen with MD under anesthesia, a total intravenous anesthetic protocol was followed using propofol and midazolam for induction and an alfaxalone constant-rate infusion for maintenance. Methadone was administered intravascularly before induction for systemic analgesia and a lidocaine block was administered for local analgesia. After the procedure, robenacoxib was administered subcutaneously. Fresh and formalin-fixed muscle samples were submitted to the Comparative Neuromuscular Laboratory at the University of California San Diego. Histopathologic evaluation revealed degenerative and regenerative myopathy consistent with MD. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was then performed, revealing absent expression of all tested sarcoglycan proteins (SGPs), specifically α-, β- and gamma (γ)-sarcoglycan, with reduced expression of dystrophin (Figure 4). Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and western blot were not performed owing to financial constraints.

Immunofluorescent staining of muscle cryosections from the cleidocephalic muscle. Staining for α-, β- and γ-sarcoglycans was markedly reduced to absent when compared with a control

The patient was rechecked 2 months after this visit and had a static physical and neurologic examination. A complimentary echocardiogram was performed without sedation by a board-certified cardiologist, which revealed mild to moderate right ventricular (RV) dilation with borderline reduced RV function, equivocal left ventricular dilation, and mild hypokinesis of the interventricular septum and basilar portions of the left and right ventricles (Figure 5). Based on the echocardiogram, no cardiac medications were indicated.

Discussion

Sarcoglycanopathies are a form of limb-girdle MD (LGMD), a category of MD that predominantly affects the skeletal muscles of the pelvic and shoulder girdle. Unlike the X-linked dystrophinopathies, the sarcoglycan-deficient LGMDs are autosomal recessive inherited diseases. Sarcoglycans make up the sarcoglycan complex, which contributes to the integrity of the myofiber sarcolemma. Mutations in any of the genes encoding an SGP, namely SGCA, SGCB, SGCG and SGCD, cause destabilization of the entire sarcoglycan complex, resulting in deficiencies in all the other sarcoglycan subunits. This has been found to be especially true in sarcoglycanopathies secondary to primary β- or delta (δ)-sarcoglycan deficiency.11,12 In some cases, a cardiomyopathy may also be associated with sarcoglycan-deficient MD, although with less frequency than the dystrophinopathies. 13 Specifically, dilated and hypokinetic cardiomyopathy phenotypes have been associated with human dystrophinopathies and LGMDs. The presence of dilated cardiomyopathy can be subtle in human patients, with variable age of onset of cardiac involvement depending upon the type of dystrophinopathy. Progression is also variable and may be slow or rapid. In contrast, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy phenotypes in humans have been associated with mitochondrial myopathies, such as glycogen storage disorders. 14 One feline report examining a breeding colony of cats with dystrophin-deficient hypertrophic feline MD revealed a variable hypertrophic cardiomyopathy phenotype on echocardiogram and gross cardiac examination. 15 In this population of cats, mild concentric hypertrophy developed started at 6–9 months, with no further progression noted.

Phenotypically, sarcoglycanopathies in humans are expected to cause a severe Duchenne-like MD, although milder phenotypes have been observed, particularly with deficiencies in SGCA and SGCG. A true genotype–phenotype correlation has not yet been observed. However, it is believed that severe SGP deficiencies, defined as sarcoglycan expression below 30%, are prognostic for more severe phenotypes and more rapid progression of disease.12,16,17

Sarcoglycanopathy has been reported a few times in veterinary medicine, namely in Boston Terriers.18,19 In a recent case series, one affected Boston Terrier and three of its unaffected family members, as well as an affected, unrelated Boston Terrier from the same kennel, were found to have independent SGCD mutations causing δ-sarcoglycanopathy with absent expression of all sarcoglycans. 19 Another δ-sarcoglycanopathy was also recently reported in a young Lagotto Romagnolo. 20 In addition, a cohort of miniature Dachshunds with SGCA mutations causing phenotypically milder clinical signs has been reported. Previously, sarcoglycan-deficient MD had been reported without distinction of the specific genetic mutation in a Dobermann 21 and a Boston Terrier. 18 All cases had reduced to absent expression of all sarcoglycan subunits and showed markedly elevated serum CK levels in young animals, in addition to gait abnormalities, at a minimum. Overall, sarcoglycanopathy in dogs appears to be mostly analogous to LGMDs in humans, following a similar clinical pattern with presenting signs and loss of the sarcoglycan complex on IHC and western blotting.

To date, two reports of sarcoglycanopathies have been described in the cat. In the first, a 6-month-old, male intact DSH was presented for weakness, reluctance to move and dyspnea, and was eventually found to have reduced β-sarcoglycan and normal expression of the remaining sarcoglycans on western blotting, with an additional reduction in expression of γ-sarcoglycan seen on IHC. 7 This kitten was eventually euthanized despite reportedly static clinical signs. In the other case, a 5-month-old, male intact domestic longhair presented for cough and intractable bronchopneumonia with no signs attributable to myopathy or neuromuscular disease, and was eventually found to have markedly reduced β-sarcoglycan expression with normal expression of γ- and α-sarcoglycan on post-mortem examination. 6 In both cases, the cats presented with somewhat non-traditional signs of MD. In addition, both cats were diagnosed with a primary β-sarcoglycanopathy, with minimal effect on other parts of the sarcoglycan complex, which does not follow similar disease patterns of sarcoglycanopathy in humans and dogs that have been commonly described in the literature to date.

In contrast, the cat described in this report presented with classical signs of MD, including macroglossia, truncal and proximal appendicular muscle hypertrophy, and gait abnormalities. This clinical presentation is consistent with a Duchenne-like phenotype, commonly seen in human sarcoglycanopathy. In contrast to the first two reports of feline sarcoglycanopathy, the primary sarcoglycan deficiency in this case is undefined, as all subunits were absent and western blot and WGS were not performed. Interestingly, despite absent sarcoglycan expression, the patient in this report remains relatively static on follow-up and has displayed a relatively slow progression of signs over approximately 14 months at the time of writing. This could indicate that the absence of sarcoglycan expression is not as prognostic in cats as it may be in humans, although absent expression of all sarcoglycans causing only mild disease has previously been described. 22 Although the echocardiographic examination revealed generally normal measurements for wall thickness and systolic function, the remainder of the findings may be due to cardiomyocyte involvement.

Currently, treatment for MD in animals does not exist. Studies of adeno-associated viruses for the treatment of sarcoglycanopathy have shown promising early results, although long-term safety has not yet been assessed. 16 This is where identifying animal models of the disease may be of the greatest value.

Conclusions

This report describes the third case of feline sarcoglycanopathy and is the first in which all tested sarcoglycan subunits had an absence of protein localization on immunohistochemistry. In addition, this is the first described case of sarcoglycan-deficient MD in a cat presenting with the typical signs and findings observed in both canine and human sarcoglycanopathy. This report highlights sarcoglycan-deficient MD and its associated clinical signs, diagnostic findings and factors that could influence prognosis of progression. This case adds to the growing literature on feline MD and provides evidence for the utility of feline sarcoglycanopathy as a comparative model of disease for the analogous condition in humans. Finally, similar to prior reports, we submit that MD must be considered in any young cat with persistently elevated serum CK levels, especially in association with the classical signs of MD reviewed in this report.
