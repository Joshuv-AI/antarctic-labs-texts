Active Nerve Management for Below-knee Amputation: A Comparison of Approaches
Authors: Sri Charan Kanthala, Nikhi P. Singh, Jeffrey N. Gross, Cameron Harmon, Aladdin H. Hassanein, Rachel Danforth, Erin L. Weber, Anthony Archual, Brian Christie, Joshua M. Adkinson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Summary:Targeted muscle reinnervation and regenerative peripheral nerve interface (RPNI) are well-described techniques used to treat or prevent postamputation neuroma and phantom limb pain after below-knee amputation (BKA). These techniques can be performed via a posterior approach (PA) or a through-the-wound approach (TTWA). We performed a review of patients who underwent active nerve management at the time of BKA between February 2019 and May 2023. Patients were stratified into PA and TTWA cohorts. A total of 36 patients underwent active nerve management at the time of BKA—16 PA and 20 TTWA. The PA was found to take significantly longer than the TTWA, with an operative time of 251 minutes compared with 208 minutes, respectively (P = 0.03). All patients in both the TTWA and PA cohorts underwent targeted muscle reinnervation. However, in the TTWA cohort, 95% also underwent RPNI compared with 56% in the PA group (P = 0.01). Patients who underwent the TTWA were more likely to experience wound dehiscence postoperatively (TTWA, 40% of subjects; PA 25% of subjects; P = 0.01). There was no difference in infection rates or time to prosthetic clearance between groups. Though more time-consuming, the PA provides more direct access to recipient motor nerves, as evidenced by the lower use of RPNI nerve management in this cohort. These findings should be considered by surgeons who perform active nerve management at the time of BKA.

Takeaways

Question: Is there a difference in active nerve management techniques between the through-the-wound approach and the posterior approach during below-the-knee amputation?

Findings: A retrospective review demonstrated the safety of the posterior approach, although it took longer than the through-the-wound approach. Patients who underwent the posterior approach were less likely to experience wound dehiscence. Regenerative peripheral nerve interface techniques were used more in those who underwent the through-the-wound approach.

Meaning: The posterior approach provides more straightforward anatomy and direct access to recipient nerves.

INTRODUCTION

Below-knee amputation (BKA) is the most common amputation performed and is preferred, as it improves functional outcomes and decreases energy expenditure compared with more proximal amputations.1 Postamputation pain is common and is estimated to affect 45%–95% of those who undergo amputation.2,3 This pain dramatically impacts quality of life, limits prosthetic use, and decreases productivity.4 Targeted muscle reinnervation (TMR) and regenerative peripheral nerve interface (RPNI) are well-described nerve management techniques used to combat neuroma and phantom limb pain and to provide greater control of a myoelectric prosthetic.5 Although active nerve management for BKA is most commonly performed via a through-the-wound approach (TTWA), an alternative technique via the popliteal fossa (ie, posterior approach [PA]) has been developed.6 The PA was developed to improve visibility of neurovascular structures and to allow for dissection in an unoperated field. Accessing nerves and muscles from an undisturbed location, separate from the amputation site, provides straightforward anatomy. In the PA, patients are turned from supine to prone position, adequately padded, reprepared, and redraped, with or without a tourniquet. The incision is made in the midline popliteal fossa, where minimal dissection is needed to precisely locate sensory nerves (ie, sural, tibial, and peroneal branches) and target motor branches of the tibial nerve (Figs. 1–3).7 For further review, Eberlin et al8 discuss specific nerve coaptation options and recommendations. In this study, we compare operating time, complication rates, and time to prosthetic clearance between the PA and TTWA.

Following institutional review board approval (no. 19952), a retrospective review of patients undergoing BKA with active nerve management between February 15, 2019, and May 11, 2023, was performed. Orthopedic or vascular surgeons were responsible for the amputation, and nerve management and closure were managed by plastic surgery teams. A total of 36 patients were identified. Patients who underwent nerve management at a time separate from BKA were excluded from analyses. Active nerve management was performed by 5 surgeons at our institution. Orthopedic surgery, plastic surgery, physical/occupational therapy, and amputee clinic notes provided a comprehensive source of data regarding complications. Follow-up for TTWA patients ranged from 17 days to 3 years, with a mean of 1.2 years. PA follow-up ranged from 49 to 2.8 years, with a mean of 1 year. Postoperative infection was defined by the presence of infection noted at the surgical site during postoperative follow-up appointments. Clearance for prosthesis fitting indicated stability of the surgical site, and this was obtained via amputee clinic documentation. Descriptive statistics, Shapiro–Wilk tests, independent samples t tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, χ2 tests, and Fisher exact tests were conducted using Microsoft Excel (Seattle, WA) and the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (Armonk, NY) with a predetermined level of statistical significance set at a P value of less than 0.05.

Demographic information for the 36 patients is shown in Table 1. There was a significant difference in the number of men and women between cohorts (P = 0.008). There was no difference in mean age at surgery (TTWA mean 48 y; PA mean 47 y; P = 0.9). There were no statistically significant differences in medical comorbidities between groups. Chronic infection and chronic pain were the most common indications for amputation in both cohorts (Table 1). On average, when performed at the time of BKA, the PA took longer to perform than the TTWA: 251 minutes (range 204–309 min, SD ± 30 min) compared with 208 minutes (114–370 min, SD ± 71 min), respectively (P = 0.03) (Fig. 4). All patients in both the TTWA and PA cohorts underwent TMR. However, in the TTWA cohort, RPNI was also performed in 95% of patients to appropriately address the major sensory nerves compared with 56% in the PA group (P = 0.01). Those who underwent the TTWA were more likely to experience wound dehiscence postoperatively (40% of TTWA versus 25% of PA, P = 0.01). There was no difference in infection rates between groups. There was no difference in time to prosthetic clearance.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we found that active nerve management performed via the PA is not associated with a higher rate of complications, although the PA required, on average, 43 minutes more operative time than the TTWA. Although the PA offers enhanced visualization through an unoperated surgical field that may allow for more straightforward identification of recipient motor nerves, the addition of a second surgical site requires additional time for anesthesia reorientation, padding, repreparing, and redraping. The records from the operating room did not allow for distinction between time spent on the BKA versus active nerve management. Therefore, it is not yet clear if the nerve management component of the surgery is the primary determinant of the difference in operating time between the 2 groups. This is an area for future investigation.

A greater number of patients who underwent nerve management via the TTWA received RPNI in conjunction with TMR compared with the PA cohort, with similar results reported by Chang and Kleiber7 in 2023. This may be secondary to enhanced visualization offered by the PA through which a larger number of recipient motor nerves may be more readily identified. TMR was considered the default nerve management technique for all patients and was performed when recipient nerves were adequately identified. We hypothesize that RPNI was performed at a higher rate in the TTWA cohort due to the inability to identify sufficient recipient motor nerves, even with the assistance of an intraoperative nerve stimulator. This may be the main benefit of the PA—a straightforward and unoperated surgical site with easy access to recipient nerves for TMR. As a result, we propose that there is a lower learning curve for the PA. Additionally, TMR has been associated with improved outcomes in reducing phantom limb pain and postoperative discomfort compared with RPNI.9 Finally, it has been demonstrated that TMR performed via the TTWA is associated with higher rates of nerve transfer entrapment within heterotopic ossification, further complicating prosthetic fitting, and approaching the nerves via the PA may decrease such risks.9

Those who underwent a TTWA had a higher rate of postoperative wound dehiscence than the PA cohort. This may be secondary to prolonged operative times through the same wound (this wound is closed immediately after BKA in the PA cohort). Delayed prosthesis fitting is known to increase patient morbidity through a decrease in function and mobility.10,11 Although there was an increased risk for wound dehiscence in the TTWA cohort, there was no difference in the time to prosthetic clearance between the groups.

This study has limitations, including its retrospective nature. We were unable to collect postoperative patient-reported outcome metrics for pain, function, or quality of life due to lack of patient engagement. Further studies on patient-reported pain metrics, opioid usage, ambulation ability, and mortality outcomes via the PA and TTWA are necessary to better understand the relative indications of each approach for active nerve management. Additionally, we found a significant difference in the sex composition between groups. Our analysis, however, was not powered adequately to assess sex-based differences. In conclusion, we found that the PA increased operative times but was safe and more frequently allowed for the use of TMR-only active nerve management compared with the TTWA in patients undergoing BKA.
