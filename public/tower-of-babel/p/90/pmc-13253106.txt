Sexual identity of Akh neurons shapes Nucb1-dependent metabolic and reproductive plasticity
Authors: Narsimha Pujari, Kamea Chevrier, Natalie Turriff, Yona Al-Tahir, Dipesh Shahi, Aidin Shaikhei Gorjan, Alex SO Li, Shreyas V Jois, Erika Donà, Maud Ferrari, Suraj Unniappan, Adelaine KW Leung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySex differences in metabolic physiology are widespread across species and are tightly linked to reproductive demands. Here, we identify nucleobindin 1 (Nucb1), a conserved calcium-binding protein, as a female-specific metabolic regulator in the adipokinetic hormone (Akh)-producing cells (APCs) of Drosophila melanogaster. APC-specific nucb1 knockdown increased energy storage and body mass in females but not in males. Cellular sex reversal experiments revealed these female-specific functions of Nucb1 to be dependent on the intrinsic sexual identity of the APCs, rather than the sex of the organism. Furthermore, under nutrient restriction, females lacking nucb1 in APCs failed to suppress oocyte maturation, indicating disrupted coupling between nutrient status and reproductive output. In contrast, we identified slowpoke-binding protein (Slob) in the APCs as a regulator of male-specific body fat storage. Together, these findings demonstrate that sex-specific metabolic and reproductive regulation arises from differential regulatory factors within the same neuroendocrine cell type.Subject areas: physiology, neuroscience, endocrinology, model organism

Introduction

Sexual dimorphism in energy metabolism is a conserved feature across metazoans, shaping nutrient storage strategies, hormonal sensitivity, and, ultimately, reproductive investment.1 In diverse species, females typically store more lipids to support reproductive functions, while males often allocate energy into lean mass or behaviors that enhance reproductive fitness.1 These allocation strategies produce sex-biased physiology in lipid storage,1,2 insulin sensitivity,3 and glucagon release.4,5 While systemic regulators such as sex steroids and metabolic hormones play well-established roles in orchestrating these differences,6,7 emerging evidence shows that tissue-autonomous sexual identity can shape metabolic outcomes independently of systemic cues.8,9,10,11,12,13 Nutrient checkpoints in reproduction provide a striking example of the need to coordinate metabolism with reproductive output: in Drosophila melanogaster, oogenesis is arrested in females under nutrient-poor conditions, conserving energy to ensure survival until resources improve.14 How cell-intrinsic sex identity contributes to the regulation of such nutrient-sensitive reproductive checkpoints remains an open question.

The nucleobindin protein family provides an entry point into investigating this knowledge gap. Nesfatin-1, derived from nucleobindin 2 (Nucb2), was identified in 2006 as an anorexigenic peptide with roles in energy homeostasis and neuroendocrine function.15,16 Its paralog, nucleobindin 1 (Nucb1), can give rise to nesfatin-1-like-peptide (Nlp), which has since been shown to play similar roles in energy homeostasis across multiple species.17,18,19 Both paralogs exhibit sex-biased expression and physiological effects in humans,20,21,22,23 rats,24,25,26 mice,27,28 and fish.18,29,30 Systemic sex hormones can, in turn, modulate the expression of these peptides, as demonstrated in rats,25,31 mice,32 and fish.18,33 These findings collectively implicate the Nucb protein family as a potential molecular factor linking reproductive physiology to metabolic regulation. Drosophila melanogaster encodes only Nucb1, making it a tractable model to dissect the sex-specific roles of this conserved protein family. Although recent work has linked Nucb1 to metabolic regulation in flies,34,35 its role in sex-specific energy regulation in reproductive physiology has not been examined.

Here, we identify Nucb1 as a key regulator of sex-specific lipid metabolism in Drosophila. Building on our observation that pan-neuronal nucb1 knockdown elevates lipid storage selectively in females, we test whether Nucb1 functions within Akh-producing cells (APCs), the fly analogs of mammalian pancreatic α cells. Our results reveal that Nucb1 is required in female APCs to maintain Akh-dependent energy mobilization and to regulate nutrient-dependent control of ovarian reproductive output, thereby identifying Nucb1 as a sex-specific regulator that links metabolism and reproduction.

Results

Sex-specific effects of pan-neuronal nucb1 knockdown

Building on vertebrate studies implicating Nucb1 in regulating sex-specific energy metabolism, we investigated its potential conserved role by probing its function in the fly nervous system. To evaluate its contribution to systemic metabolism, we used a pan-neuronal driver (nSyb-GAL4) to knock down nucb1 throughout development. Three commercially available UAS-nucb1-RNAi lines were tested for knockdown efficiency by qPCR. The strongest line reduced nucb1 mRNA in the whole body by 37% (p < 0.05) and by 95% in the head (p < 0.005), relative to its corresponding TRiP vector control, and was therefore selected for all subsequent assays (Figure S1).

The impact of pan-neuronal nucb1 on systemic metabolism was assessed at 7 days post-eclosion, a time point corresponding to reproductive maturity. We first asked whether nucb1 knockdown alters baseline body mass as a readout for metabolic output. In control flies, virgin females exhibited significantly higher body mass than males (30.49% higher), consistent with previous reports.36,37 Pan-neuronal nucb1 knockdown resulted in a significant reduction in body mass in both sexes (Figure 1A). In females, knockdown reduced body mass by 7.82% (p < 0.001), while in males, the reduction was smaller at 6.42%, yet significantly different from controls (p < 0.01). Although the knockdown effect appeared more consistent across female replicates, a genotype-by-sex interaction was not statistically significant (p = 0.076) (Figure 1A).

To test whether changes in body mass were associated with altered feeding behavior, food intake was inferred from 24-h cumulative excreta output using a dye-labeled excreta quantification (ExQ) assay.38 Pan-neuronal nucb1 knockdown significantly reduced food intake in both sexes (main effect of genotype: p < 0.0001), with post hoc contrasts confirming reductions in both males and females (p < 0.0001 for each). Pan-neuronal knockdown females showed a 46.95% reduction in food intake, while males showed a 37.63% reduction. However, the genotype-by-sex interaction did not reach statistical significance (interaction p = 0.27), even though the magnitude of the reduction appeared greater in males (Figure 1B).

To determine whether neuronal nucb1 knockdown alters energy storage in a sex-specific manner, we quantified whole-body triacylglycerides (TAGs) and glycogen content normalized to total protein, using established enzymatic colorimetric assays.39 Total body protein levels did not differ between control and nucb1 knockdown flies in either sex (Figure S2), validating the use of protein normalization for comparison of energy stores. TAGs and glycogen are conserved energy storage molecules found across species. TAG functions as the primary long-term lipid reservoir, whereas glycogen provides a rapidly mobilized, short-term carbohydrate reserve. Both are regulated by evolutionarily conserved neuroendocrine pathways involved in systemic energy homeostasis and exhibit sex-specific regulation under various physiological states.40

Consistent with previous reports showing that adult female flies accumulate more lipid reserves than males,12 control females exhibited significantly higher TAG levels than control males (p < 0.01, Figure 1C). In females, knockdown significantly increased TAG levels by 39.87%, while no effect was observed in males (estimated difference 0.36%, p = 0.97) (Figure 1C). Linear mixed-effects modeling with replicate as a random factor revealed a highly significant genotype-by-sex interaction (p < 0.0001), indicating that the metabolic response to nucb1 knockdown differs by sex.

In contrast to TAGs, carbohydrate storage did not exhibit a sex-specific response to pan-neuronal nucb1 knockdown. Control male flies exhibited significantly higher glycogen levels than control females (+19.55%, p < 0.001). Nucb1 knockdown significantly reduced glycogen in both sexes (females: −20.01%, p < 0.01; males: −20.05%, p < 0.001). The genotype-by-sex interaction was not statistically significant (p = 0.53), indicating that the knockdown effect on glycogen does not differ robustly between sexes.

Together, these results indicate that pan-neuronal nucb1 knockdown alters the metabolic status of adult flies, with whole-body TAG storage showing the most robust sex-specific effect. To exclude off-target effects, the female-specific elevation in whole-body TAGs was independently validated using a second UAS-nucb1-RNAi line, which produced a comparable increase in TAG levels in females but not in males (Figure S3).

APCs, but not insulin-producing cells, mediate the female-specific lipid accumulation phenotype of Nucb1

To localize the neuronal source of Nucb1’s sex-specific metabolic effects, we restricted its knockdown to discrete populations of neuroendocrine cells known to control energy homeostasis. Specifically, we targeted insulin-producing cells (IPCs) (Dilp2-, Dilp3-, and Dilp5-producing cells) and the glucagon-like APCs using specific GAL4 drivers. These cell types are central to the fly’s neuroendocrine control of metabolism and have known mammalian counterparts in pancreatic β cells and α cells, respectively.41,42 This approach allowed us to test whether sex-specific phenotypes observed in pan-neuronal knockdown could be mapped to functionally defined peptidergic subsets.

We focused on TAG content as the most robust and sexually dimorphic metabolic phenotype resulting from pan-neuronal nucb1 knockdown. Knocking down nucb1 in IPCs yielded variable outcomes in TAG storage. dilp2-GAL4>UAS-nucb1-RNAi exhibited significant TAG elevation in both sexes (females: +21.6%, p < 0.05; males: +30.5%, p < 0.05). dilp3-GAL4>UAS-nucb1-RNAi exhibited reduced TAG levels in females (−12.2%, p < 0.05) but no change in males (p = 0.45), while dilp5-GAL4>UAS-nucb1-RNAi had no effect in females (p = 0.17) but significantly reduced TAG levels in males (−28.6%, p < 0.0001) (Figures 2A–2C). While Dilp2, 3, and 5 are co-expressed in brain IPCs, Dilp3 and Dilp5 also have peripheral expression.43,44,45,46 The divergent outcomes of nucb1 knockdown in the Dilp-producing cells may reflect tissue-specific Nucb1 functions beyond the IPCs.

In addition to the female-specific elevation of TAG storage, APC-specific nucb1 knockdown also caused a significant increase in glycogen levels selectively in females (+28.79%, p < 0.0001) (Figure 3A). Consistently, body mass was also increased in females but unchanged in males (+19.99%, p < 0.0001) (Figure 3B), indicating a sex-specific defect in energy mobilization. Supporting this interpretation, qPCR revealed a reduction of akh mRNA expression in knockdown females (−77%, p < 0.001), whereas the knockdown in males showed no significant change (p = 0.5) (Figure 3C). Notably, food intake was decreased in both sexes (females: −41%, p < 0.05; males: −75.54%, p < 0.0001) (Figure 3D). Together, these data indicate that changes in food intake do not mirror the sex-specific metabolic phenotypes and suggest that impaired Akh signalling contributes to the female-biased effects of nucb1 knockdown.

APC-specific nucb1 alters energy allocation and female reproductive output

To evaluate the physiological consequences of sex-specific lipid accumulation, we measured starvation resistance in akh-GAL4>UAS-nucb1-RNAi flies. Consistent with previous findings,12 control females exhibited greater starvation resistance than males (p < 0.0001) (Figure 4A). Nucb1 knockdown in APCs significantly extended survival in both sexes under nutrient-deprived conditions. However, the effect was significantly more pronounced in females, as evidenced by Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and a significant genotype-by-sex interaction in uncensored two-way ANOVA (F1,359 = 23.85, p < 0.0001) (Figure 4A). These findings extend the relevance of the lipid phenotype by demonstrating that excess lipid stores resulting from loss of nucb1 translate into improved survival under energetic stress, with females gaining a greater functional benefit.

We next asked whether increased lipid storage affected reproductive output. Akh-GAL4>UAS-nucb1-RNAi females mated with wild-type males laid significantly more eggs than controls (+28.39%, p < 0.0001), whereas knockdown males mated with wild-type females showed no difference in reproductive success (p = 0.91) (Figure 4B). Thus, the excess TAG accumulated in APC-specific nucb1 knockdown females may be used to support increased egg production.

Since ovarian developmental progression is constrained by nutrient-dependent checkpoints that are regulated by insulin signaling,14 and that Nucb1 has been linked to insulin pathway regulation in multiple species,27,35 we next evaluated whether APC-specific nucb1 knockdown alters nutrient-dependent regulation of oogenesis. The mid-oogenesis nutritional checkpoint occurs at stages 8–9, after which vitellogenesis proceeds only when sufficient dietary protein is available.14 To assess ovarian developmental progression, we staged egg chambers using established nuclear staining criteria.48

When fed with a standard diet, ovaries from APC-specific nucb1 knockdown females contained an increased number of post-vitellogenic egg chambers compared with controls (Figures 4C′, 4C″, and 4D, +49.12%, p < 0.0001), consistent with their elevated egg-laying phenotype. Under sucrose-only dietary conditions, control females exhibited the expected arrest of oogenesis prior to the mid-oogenesis checkpoint, producing egg chambers up to stage 8 but almost never progressing to post-vitellogenic stages (Figures 4C‴ and 4D). In contrast, APC-specific nucb1 knockdown females retained a substantial number of post-vitellogenic egg chambers under nutrient-restrictive diet, at levels only modestly lower to those observed in control females maintained on a complete diet (Figures 4C⁗ and 4D, p < 0.05). These findings indicate that loss of nucb1 in APCs is associated with continued progression of oogenesis beyond the mid-oogenesis nutritional checkpoint under protein-restricted conditions.

To assess whether an increased number of eggs produced by APC-specific nucb1 females were viable, we quantified egg-to-adult viability under standard feeding conditions. Eggs laid by APC-specific nucb1 knockdown females exhibited a modest but significant reduction in eclosion rate compared with controls (−10.4%, n = 13 females, p < 0.05) (Figure 4E), indicating that although more eggs were produced, a subset failed to complete development. Thus, APC-specific loss of nucb1 promotes increased ovarian output under normal dietary condition but with a measurable cost to offspring viability.

Transcriptomic and functional analyses identifies sex-biased effectors in akh+nucb1+ cells

To determine whether the sex-specific effects of nucb1 knockdown depend on the systemic cues from the chromosomal sex of the organism or the intrinsic sexual identity of the APCs, we performed a cellular sex-reversal experiment in these cells. In Drosophila, sexual identity is specified in a cell-autonomous manner through sex-specific alternative splicing. In female cells, this pathway produces the splicing factor transformer (Tra), which directs female-specific gene expression programs; in the absence of functional Tra, cells adopt a male identity independent of gonadal or chromosomal sex. We masculinized APCs by co-expressing RNAi for both nucb1 and tra and used whole-body TAG level as a readout. Females with nucb1 knockdown in masculinized APCs (akh-GAL4>UAS-nucb1-RNAi; UAS-tra-RNAi) had lipid levels statistically indistinguishable from both male genotypes (akh-GAL4>UAS-control [II] and akh-GAL4>UAS-nucb1-RNAi [II]; p = 0.95 and p = 0.99, respectively; Figure 5A). These data show that masculinization of APCs abolishes the elevated lipid phenotype observed in females with nucb1 knockdown restricted to these cells, as well as the normal female-biased elevation in lipid levels observed in control flies (akh-GAL4>TRiP controls). Together, these results suggest that the functional outcome of nucb1 depletion is primarily dependent on the intrinsic sexual identity of the APCs, rather than the systemic cues from the sex of the organism as a whole.

To investigate the molecular basis by which cellular sex identity influences Nucb1 function in APCs, we analyzed a single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) dataset that profiled cells from the CC in the Fly Cell Atlas (FCA).49 This publicly available dataset includes nuclei sorted by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)- obtained from 5-day-old flies expressing GFP under the control of the akh-GAL4 driver.49 A total of 224 male and 178 female cells are present in this dataset (Figures S4A and S4B). Given the sexually dimorphic phenotypes observed in akh-GAL4>UAS-nucb1-RNAi manipulations, nucb1 mRNA expression was examined within the akh+ cluster. Surprisingly, the nucb1 transcript was detected only in a small fraction of cells within this cluster, similar to the rest of the clusters in this dataset (Figure S4C). To confirm Nucb1 protein expression in the APCs of the CC, we used an antibody generated against the N terminus of the protein. Antibody staining for Nucb1 revealed its expression in all the APCs labeled by akh-GAL4 (Figure 5B). The discrepancy between the snRNA-seq dataset and antibody signals could possibly be due to sub-threshold levels of nucb1 mRNA in the APCs that could hinder their detection. Confocal imaging revealed an apparent sex-specific localization pattern of Nucb1 in APCs. In both sexes, Nucb1 signal was detected throughout the cytoplasm, accompanied by punctate staining. The primary sex difference was observed in nuclear localization. In males, nuclear Nucb1 staining was consistently detected in akh+ cells across all samples examined (10/10), with an average of 12.9 akh+ nuclei per preparation exhibiting nuclear signal (Figures 5B and S5C–S5F). In females, nuclear signal within akh+ cells was observed in only a subset of samples (2/8) (Figures 5B and S5G–S5J). In those samples, 2 and 5 akh+ cells exhibited detectable nuclear Nucb1 signal, whereas the remaining female samples showed no evident nuclear enrichment within labeled cells. When present, the nuclear signal in females also appeared qualitatively weaker than that observed in males (Figures 5B and S5G). The nuclear localization of Nucb1 in males was further confirmed by co-localization with GFP containing a nuclear localization signal expressed under the akh-GAL4 driver (Figure S5A). Antibody specificity was assessed by RNAi-mediated depletion of nucb1 in APCs (Figure S5B).

To identify effectors of sexual dimorphism in the APCs, a differential expression analysis was performed between male and female cells that co-express nucb1 mRNA (akh+nucb1+ cells) in the akh+ cluster. Two candidates stood out for both the magnitude of differential expression and relevance to neuroendocrine regulation: amon, a prohormone convertase-like gene was enriched in males,50 and slob (slowpoke-binding protein), a cytosolic regulator of the calcium-activated BK-type potassium channel slowpoke (Slo) was enriched in females51 (Figure S4D). The sex-specific differential expression of these genes was observed only in APCs positive for akh+nucb1+ (Figure S4F), not in all akh+ cells (Figure S4E).

To assess if these candidate genes give rise to the sex-specific TAG phenotype, we silenced amon or slob in APCs and quantified whole-body TAG levels. Silencing amon decreased TAGs in both sexes (Figure 5C, females: −33.26%, p < 0.0001; males: −50.84%, p < 0.0001), whereas silencing slob increased TAGs only in males (+50.38%, p < 0.05) but had no effect in females (p = 0.51) (Figure 5D). These results show that Amon function in APCs is independent of sex despite differences in expression, whereas Slob contributes to male-specific regulation of TAG homeostasis. Together, these findings support a model in which Nucb1 and Slob may function as sex-specific regulators of APC endocrine output, as reflected by their sex-specific effects on TAG storage.

Discussion

Cellular sex identity modulates Nucb1-dependent regulation of energy allocation

Sexual dimorphism in energy metabolism is a conserved biological principle across taxa, affecting nutrient distribution, stress tolerance, and reproductive success. While systemic regulators like insulin and steroid hormones have been extensively studied, the cell-intrinsic mechanisms that generate sex-biased metabolic outputs remain poorly defined.1 In this study, we identify the calcium-binding protein Nucb1 in APCs as a sex-biased regulator of energy metabolism in Drosophila.

Using pan-neuronal knockdown, we observed that among the multiple metabolic traits tested, TAG storage was uniquely elevated in females, despite shared reductions in ExQ-measured feeding and body mass across sexes. We note that the ExQ assay infers food intake from excreta output and cannot exclude effects from gut motility, renal excretion, or fecal water content. Nevertheless, the female-specific elevation of TAGs directed our focus to APCs, which mediate lipid mobilization in flies and serve as functional analogs of glucagon-secreting α cells in mammals. Indeed, targeted knockdown of nucb1 in APCs, but not insulin-producing cells (IPCs), recapitulated the female-specific TAG phenotype, establishing these cells as one principal site of Nucb1’s sex-biased metabolic function.

Further characterization of APC-specific knockdown of nucb1 identified that the female-specific physiological effects, which also include an increase in total body mass and glycogen, are consistent with impaired Akh-dependent mobilization of stored energy. Strikingly, this altered energy allocation was coupled to changes in female reproductive output, indicating that Nucb1-dependent metabolic regulation in APCs has functional consequences beyond somatic energy balance. Notably, the female-specific increase in lipid storage depended on the intrinsic sexual identity of the APCs themselves; masculinization of female APCs abolished the elevated TAG phenotype associated with nucb1 knockdown. This finding indicates that cell-autonomous sexual identity within APCs is required for Nucb1-dependent regulation of lipid storage, independent of the chromosomal sex of the organism. Together, these results position Nucb1 as a cell-autonomous regulator that enables APCs to perform sex-specific regulation of energy allocation in females.

Distinct molecular requirements underlie sex differences in APC function

Consistent with Wat et al., we confirmed by qPCR that males display higher akh transcript abundance despite comparable APC numbers (Figure 3C).13 Wat et al. also reported elevated akhR expression in males, yet artificial silencing or activation of APCs altered TAG storage similarly in both sexes, suggesting that female APCs have equivalent capacity for Akh-dependent metabolic regulation despite lower baseline transcript levels.13 Although our transcriptomic analysis suggested male-biased amon expression within a very small akh+nucb1+ subset, functional silencing of amon resulted in a reduction of whole-body TAG elevation similarly in both sexes, indicating that peptide processing represents a core, sex-independent component of APC function. Since amon disruption is expected to impair the maturation of multiple APC-derived peptides,52,53,54 the phenotype likely reflects a broad reduction in neuroendocrine output rather than sex-specific regulation.

In contrast, APC-specific nucb1 knockdown reduced akh mRNA only in females, perhaps due to a feedback mechanism, indicating a female-specific reliance on Nucb1 that underlies the female-specific Akh-dependent metabolic phenotypes we observed. Interestingly, APC-specific silencing of slob, identified through transcriptomic analysis of a limited number of akh+nucb1+ cells in the FCA dataset, resulted in increased TAGs in males only, with females buffered against its loss. Slob encodes a cytosolic negative modulator of the BK-type potassium channel Slo. Slo channels are calcium-activated, voltage-gated potassium channels; their opening hyperpolarizes the membrane, thereby shortening action potentials and limiting calcium influx.55 Thus, Slo channels function to modulate action potential duration and vesicle release probability.55 Slob functions to restrain Slo opening, as slob-null flies display increased synaptic transmission due to constitutively active Slo channels.56,57 Notably, APC-specific knockdown of slo has been reported to have no effect on starvation resistance in either sex,58 indicating that metabolic phenotypes do not arise from Slo activity alone. However, Nucb1 has been identified as a binding partner of Slo in a proteomic study of mammalian excitable tissues.59,60 Together, these observations suggest that sexual dimorphism in APC function may arise from Slo-associated regulation of excitation-secretion coupling, tuned by Slob in males and Nucb1 in females (Figures S7B and S7C). Future experiments will be required to determine how these factors influence calcium dynamics and secretory output in APCs.

Although nucb1 transcripts are more abundant in male APCs (Figures S4E and S4F), our confocal analysis revealed sex-biased subcellular distribution: Nucb1 was predominantly nuclear in males but diffuse in females (Figures 5B and S5C–S5J), suggesting distinct subcellular roles. Nucb1 contains two EF-hand motifs with markedly different Ca2+ affinities (∼6 and ∼74 μM)61 and undergoes Ca2+-dependent conformation changes.62 Male APCs exhibit higher baseline Ca2+ activity than females,13 which may bias Nucb1 toward nuclear accumulation through Ca2+-dependent exposure of predicted nuclear localization sequences (as suggested by classical nuclear localization signal [cNLS] Mapper analysis63; Figure S6). Elevated cytosolic Ca2+ could thereby promote conformational changes that transiently expose nuclear localization signals, facilitating import of Nucb1 into the nucleus. This raises the possibility that Nucb1 performs Ca2+-regulated nuclear functions in males distinct from its cytoplasmic role in females (Figure S7A).

A proposed model for sex-biased APC function

Our data support a model in which sexual dimorphism in APCs arises from sex-specific functional requirements for distinct regulatory factors, rather than differences in cell number, core expression of genes that are important for peptide production or maturation. Using APC-specific perturbation, we identify Nucb1 as a regulator required specifically in females and Slob as a regulator required selectively in males. These findings suggest that male and female APCs rely on distinct molecular strategies to regulate hormone output that gave rise to our observed physiological phenotype (Figure S7).

We propose that this divergence reflects differences in basal activity level. Previous work has shown that male APCs exhibit higher basal calcium activity than female APCs.13 In this cellular environment, regulatory mechanisms that influence membrane repolarization and channel activity are likely to have a greater impact on overall APC output. Slob, a known modulator of the BK-type potassium channel Slo, may therefore play a more prominent functional role in males, consistent with the male-specific phenotype observed upon slob perturbation.

In contrast, female APCs appear less sensitive to perturbations of Slob but exhibit a specific dependence on Nucb1. As mentioned before, Nucb1 is a calcium-binding protein that undergoes calcium-dependent conformational changes, suggesting a role in calcium-dependent cellular processes. In females, where APC activity is low, Nucb1 may be required to support appropriate hormone output under conditions that demand dynamic regulation, such as changes in nutrient availability.

Together, this model proposes that sex-biased APC function arises from differential reliance on regulatory factors that function within the physiological context of each sex. In males, higher basal activity renders APC output more sensitive to Slob-dependent regulation, whereas in females, Nucb1 becomes essential for maintaining appropriate endocrine responses.

Physiological relevance of sex-specific Nucb1 function

The sex-specific role of Nucb1 reflects the divergent reproductive and metabolic demands of males and females following eclosion. In Drosophila females, mature oocytes first appear approximately 24 h post-eclosion and progress through 14 stages of oogenesis.64 In 7-day-old virgin females maintained on a standard diet in the absence of mating cues, most oocytes arrest development between stages 8 and 10.65,66,67 Although this developmental progression is paused, the follicles continue to take up nutrients, particularly lipids. Further maturation is contingent on both mating cues and nutrient availability. Nutritional checkpoints are therefore required to dynamically coordinate energy storage with reproductive output. In contrast, male reproductive tissues, particularly the accessory glands, which are functionally analogous to the mammalian prostate and seminal vesicles, undergo continuous post-eclosion growth and can nearly double in size by day 10.68 While the exact metabolic costs of this growth remain unknown, the sustained anabolic activity in males suggests a consistently high basal energy demand not observed in age-matched females.

Our physiological analyses reveal that Nucb1 is required in APCs to appropriately align the metabolic state with female reproductive output. Normally, females arrest egg development on nutrient-poor diets, and this checkpoint regulation is under systemic insulin signalling control.14 Akh-GAL4>UAS-nucb1-RNAi females accumulated TAGs and glycogen, consistent with impaired Akh-mediated energy mobilization via its receptor AkhR in the fat body, the primary energy storage organ of Drosophila. Under standard dietary conditions, akh-GAL4>UAS-nucb1-RNAi females also exhibited increased fecundity, indicating that the excess energy stored can be allocated toward reproduction.

A particularly striking finding was that the APC-specific nucb1 knockdown females did not exhibit normal suppression of late-stage oocyte production when maintained on a sucrose-only diet. Rather than arresting oocyte maturation as observed in controls, these females retained and produced late-stage oocytes, indicating an impairment in nutrient-dependent restraint of reproductive output. Although this process is known to be influenced by systemic insulin signalling,14 we did not assess insulin pathway activity in this study. Reduced Akh output alone is unlikely to fully account for this phenotype, as prior work shows that disruption of Akh signalling in the fat body does not significantly affect insulin pathway activity.69

In addition to Akh, APCs have been shown to also secrete limostatin (Lst), a decretin-like hormone known to suppress secretion from insulin-producing cells (IPCs) during starvation in adult flies.52 Recent work further demonstrated that larval APCs can internalize circulating Dilps and co-package them with Akh in dense-core vesicles,70 though this phenomenon has been described in larvae and has not yet been demonstrated in adult APCs. Together, these findings raise the possibility that APC-derived signals could influence systemic insulin activity either indirectly through Lst signaling or directly through the release of stored Dilps. Although we did not examine the release of APC-derived signals into the circulation, loss of nucb1 could alter the balance of APC-derived hormonal outputs and disrupt insulin-dependent reproductive processes under nutrient limitation. Together, these findings support a model in which Nucb1 contributes to the appropriate integration of metabolic and reproductive signals in females, and its loss leads to a physiological state permissive for continued late-stage oocyte production despite limited nutrient availability (Figures S7B and S7C).

Broader implications and future directions

Although we did not detect a metabolic phenotype in males upon nucb1 knockdown (no change in either TAGs or glycogen), the potential for Nucb1 to contribute to alternative outputs remains an open possibility. Confocal analysis revealed a nuclear localization of Nucb1 in male APCs, raising the question of whether Nucb1 has nuclear functions. Nucb1 contains a conserved DNA-binding domain,71 and its nuclear localization has been detected in other species.27,72,73 Additionally, Nucb1 has been reported to interact with Gαi1,61 which itself has been found to localize to the nucleus in several studies.74,75,76 Although direct evidence is lacking, these features raise the intriguing possibility that Nucb1 engages in calcium-dependent nuclear functions, potentially influencing transcriptional regulation in some sex-specific context.

A second possibility is that Nucb1 serves as a prohormone precursor. Nucb1 harbors a conserved N-terminal dibasic cleavage site predicted to generate an Nlp via PC2 processing. Although the existence of Nlp in flies has not been demonstrated, its sequence conservation and known bioactivity in other systems17,18,27,29,77,78 raise the possibility that APC-derived Nlp represents an alternative hormonal output.

More broadly, our study highlights Nucb1 as a potential candidate modulator of BK channel function and underscores a conserved regulatory logic linking calcium buffering, channel activity, and peptide secretion. Similar principles may operate in other excitable tissues, such as the central nervous system or the pancreatic islets, where BK channels and EF-hand-containing proteins co-exist. Determining whether Nucb1 fulfills comparable roles in these systems is an important direction for future research.

Limitations of the study

The genetic manipulations used in this study were not temporally restricted, and therefore developmental contributions to the observed phenotypes cannot be excluded. However, extensive genetic studies demonstrate that Akh signalling is largely dispensable for Drosophila melanogaster development under standard nutritional conditions. Galikova et al. characterized Akh-null mutants generated by CRISPR-Cas9 and reported no gross developmental abnormality, including normal body size, hatchability, viability, developmental timing, and female fecundity.53 Moreover, Akh signalling was shown to be dispensable for lipid and glycogen mobilization during development. Consistent with this, developmental consequences of perturbing the Akh pathway have been observed only under conditions of larval nutritional stress, as demonstrated by akh-Gal4-driven knockdown of dg2, a key component of the Akh signalling pathway.79 In our experiments, flies were maintained on standard diets throughout development and adulthood. Together, these findings support the interpretation that the phenotypes we report reflect adult physiological roles of APCs, rather than developmental defects arising from altered Akh signalling. Finally, the proposed model linking Nucb1 to calcium-dependent regulation of excitation-secretion coupling remains hypothetical and will require direct experimental validation through electrophysiological and secretion assays.

Experimental model and study participant details

Flies

Drosophila melanogaster was used for all the experiments. Flies were raised at a maximum density of 20 flies/vial on 10 mL of fly food at 25°C, 50-70% humidity, and maintained in a normal photoperiod condition (12 h light: 12 h dark). Animals were raised on Bloomington standard cornmeal food with 16 g/L instant dry yeast, 9 g/L soy flour, 67 g/L yellow cornmeal, 5 g/L agar, 4.5 mL/L of 13.4 M propionic acid, and 0.22% tegosept made with ethanol. Virgin male and female flies were collected in separate vials. Flies were flipped to fresh food vials every 2 days. Age matched 7-day old male and female flies were used in all assays for a sex-based comparative analysis. Females used for oocyte maturation assay (Figures 4C and 4D) were kept on a 100 mM sucrose food for 5 days. Further information on source/strain is listed in the key resources table.

Method details

Body weight measurement

Knockdown flies with their appropriate controls were briefly anesthetized and weighed in milligrams using an analytical balance in groups of 5. At least 20 replicates were weighed for each genotype. Weight measurements were recorded between zeitgeber time (ZT) 0-2.

Food intake quantification

Food consumption was quantified using an adapted version of the excreta quantification (Ex-Q) method described by Wu et al.38 To induce feeding, 10 flies per replicate (5-7 replicates per genotype and sex) were placed in 1% agarose (PBS) starvation food for 16 hours. Afterwards, the flies were transferred to Ex-Q vials containing standard Bloomington cornmeal food supplemented with 1% (w/v) Acid Blue 9 dye. Vials were constructed from 50 mL conical tubes with a central hole in the cap to hold a 10 μL pipette tip with its wide end facing the inside of the tube. Up to 100 μL of cornmeal food mixed with blue dye was added to each of the tips in the ExQ vial. Flies were briefly anesthetized for transfer, and assays were initiated between Zeitgeber Time 0–2. Flies were allowed to eat under the standard incubator conditions for 24 hours. The following day, flies and food tips were removed, and vials were sealed. To extract dye-labelled excreta on the walls of the tube, 6 mL of distilled water was added to each vial, followed by a quick vortex to mix the dye and water. A 100 μL aliquot of the solution was transferred to a 96-well plate, and absorbance at 630 nm was measured using a Varioskan LUX microplate reader. Relative food intake was compared between genotypes, with at least 10 replicates (∼100 flies) for each genotype.

Glucose-glycogen measurements

For glucose and glycogen estimation, 35 flies were divided into seven replicates for each genotype/sex. To avoid the effect of circadian rhythm on energy molecules, all enzymatic assays were performed between ZT 0-2. Each replicate of flies was weighed using an analytical balance before being placed in homogenization vials with 500 μL of 50 mM sodium acetate buffer, pH 4.3, on ice. Flies were homogenized for 1 minute at 6.5 m/s speed using an MP Bio FastPrep-24 homogenizer. 25 μL of the lysate was stored in the fridge for protein estimation later. Endogenous enzymes of lysate were then heat-inactivated for 10 minutes at 70°C. Lysate debris was spun down at 21,200 × g in a tabletop microcentrifuge for 5 minutes. 30 μL of each lysate supernatant was plated in a 96-well plate. One row was added with 50 μL of sodium acetate, and the other one was incubated with 50 μL of amyloglucosidase (7 U/mL, sodium acetate, pH 4.3) for an hour at 37°C. 100 μL of hexokinase was added to both wells to estimate free glucose and glucose derived from glycogen, respectively. Samples were incubated with hexokinase for 10 minutes at 37°C. Absorbance was measured at 340 nm. Glucose released from glycogen was estimated by subtracting the absorbance of lysate with a buffer from that of lysate incubated with amyloglucosidase. Absolute values of total glucose and glycogen were calculated from a standard curve created with glucose and glycogen solutions with concentrations ranging from 0.16 mg/mL to 0.01 mg/mL, prepared with sodium acetate buffer. All values were normalized to the total body protein measured using the BCA assay.

Triacylglycerides measurement

Total body fat in the form of triacylglycerides (TAG) was measured with 35 flies/genotype divided into seven replicates. Homogenization was performed as described above with 500 μL of ice-cold 1xPBS buffer with 0.5% Tween-20. 25 μL of the lysate was stored in the fridge for protein estimation later. Lysate was heat-inactivated at 70°C for 10 minutes, followed by a 5-minute spin at 21,200 × g. 50 μL of the supernatant was incubated with 200 μL of triglycerides reagent in a 96-well plate for 5 minutes at 37°C. 50 μL of glycerol standards with a concentration range from 1 mg/mL to 0.125 mg/mL were incubated with the triacylglycerides reagent under the same conditions to generate a standard curve. Absorbance was measured at 540 nm. The absolute amount of TAG was estimated from the standard curve. All values were normalized to the total body protein measured using the BCA assay described below.

Total protein estimation

Total protein amount was determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit following the manufacturer’s microplate protocol. Briefly, the BCA working reagent was prepared by mixing Reagent A and Reagent B at a 50:1 ratio (v/v). 25 μL of each sample or bovine serum albumin (BSA) standard was added to the microplate, followed by 200 μL of working reagent. Solutions were mixed, incubated at 37°C for 30 min, allowed to equilibrate to room temperature, and absorbance was measured at 562 nm. Protein concentrations were calculated from a BSA standard curve prepared according to the kit instructions and were used to normalize downstream samples.

Fecundity assessment

To assess egg laying under basal conditions, 7-day-old virgin female flies were paired with an equal number of wild-type Canton-S (CS) males in fresh Bloomington food vials for 24 hours to allow mating. Grape agar egg-laying plates were prepared using 2% agarose, 30% (v/v) pure grape juice, 3% sucrose, and 0.5% (v/v) of a 20% Tegosept stock. Five millilitres of the medium were dispensed into each 35-mm plate. After solidification, plates were coated with a 6% (w/v) yeast solution prepared in water using instant yeast.

The following day, between Zeitgeber time (ZT) 0–2, males were removed, and individual mated females were transferred to fresh grape agar plates. Females were allowed to lay eggs for 48 hours in an incubator under standard rearing conditions. After the egg-laying period, eggs on each plate were manually counted under a light microscope. Each female and corresponding plate were treated as independent biological replicates. A minimum of 10 biological replicates were analyzed per genotype.

Egg staging with nuclear staining under nutrient restriction

Egg development and ovarian maturation were assessed in flies maintained on a sucrose-only diet. The protein-restricted sucrose diet was prepared using 100 mM sucrose dissolved in 1× PBS (pH 7.4) and dispensed into standard fly vials. Ten 7-day-old virgin females were housed with an equal number of males on the sucrose diet for five days to allow mating under protein-restricted conditions. Flies were transferred to fresh vials containing the same diet every two days.

On day six, males were removed, and ovaries from mated females were dissected in cold 1× PBS (pH 7.4). To characterize egg developmental stages in females maintained on the sucrose-only diet, ovaries were fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with Hoechst 33258 for 10 minutes to label nuclei. Following staining, ovaries were mounted in Vectashield and imaged by confocal microscopy. Egg chambers were manually staged according to a published protocol (Jia et al._2016). Stage 9 egg chambers, defined by the accumulation of yolk protein occupying approximately 50% of the oocyte volume, were used as a marker for passage through the nutritional checkpoint. Egg chambers between stages 9 and 14 were classified as having passed the nutritional checkpoint and were treated as individual data points representing maturing eggs.

Starvation resistance assay

To assess survival in starvation conditions, 60 male or female flies of each genotype were divided into three replicates and placed in starvation vials. Starvation vials were prepared with 1% agarose in 1× phosphate buffer saline, pH 7.2. Once flies were placed in the starvation vials, survival was assessed by counting deaths every 4 hours until all flies were dead. A Kaplan-Meier curve was used to statistically assess and compare the starvation resistance between control and knockdown flies.

Immunochemistry

Adult male and female flies of the genotypes akh-Gal4>UAS-mCD8::gfp or akh-Gal4>UAS-stinger::gfp were anesthetized using CO2 and dissected in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (1xPBS, pH 7.4). The foregut, along with the attached corpora cardiaca, was isolated to visualize adipokinetic hormone-producing cells (APCs). Dissected tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 20 minutes at room temperature, followed by four 15-minute washes in 1× PBST (PBS with 0.1% Tween-20). Samples were blocked in 1× PBST containing 5% normal goat serum for 90 minutes at room temperature. Primary antibody incubations were performed for 48 hours at 4°C using rabbit anti-dNucb1 (1:200) along with either rat anti-mCD8 (1:100) for mCD8::GFP samples or mouse anti-GFP (1:200) for stinger::GFP samples, diluted in blocking buffer.

Following primary incubation, tissues were washed four times in PBS at room temperature and incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 hours at room temperature. The following secondary antibodies were used: Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200), Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rat IgG (1:200) or Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG (1:200). After staining, samples were mounted in Vectashield antifade mounting medium and sealed with coverslips on superfrost slides. Imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope. Z-stacks were acquired and processed using Fiji (ImageJ, NIH).

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

For whole animal qPCRs, 10 flies were used for each replicate. Each experiment contained 3-5 biological replicates. Flies were homogenized in homogenization vials with 1 mL trizol for 1 minute at 6.5 m/s using a FastPrep-24 homogenizer. Chloroform (200 μL) was added to each lysate and vortexed to separate the aqueous layer from the organic layer. The aqueous layer was removed and further washed with 70% ethanol and isopropanol. Pellet was generated after the washes following centrifugation at 21,200 × g for 15 minutes. RNA was resuspended using 30-50 μL of pure molecular biology-grade water. 1 μg of RNA was converted to cDNA for each replicate using the iScript cDNA synthesis kit. Relative mRNA transcript levels were quantified with rp49 as a housekeeping gene with a SYBR green kit. Data was collected with two technical repeats and at least three biological replicates. Analysis was performed using the 2(-ΔΔCt) method. Primers used in qPCR are listed in the key resources table.

RNAseq analysis

To investigate the molecular context underlying the sex-specific function of Nucb1 in Akh-producing cells, we analyzed publicly available single-nucleus RNA-sequencing (snRNA-seq) data from the Fly Cell Atlas (FCA), a comprehensive atlas of over 580,000 nuclei from dissected adult Drosophila melanogaster tissues.49 Specifically, we mined the “fca_biohub_akhcc_ss2” dataset, which was generated using the high-sensitivity Smart-seq2 platform and focuses on corpora cardiaca and associated neuroendocrine cell types.

After quality control filtering (nFeature >200 and <4000, nCount_RNA <3,000,000), we retained 402 high-quality nuclei for downstream analysis. Dimensionality reduction revealed at least 5 distinct clusters, with only one cluster containing the majority of akh+ cells (63 males, 20 females) (Figures S4A and S4B). Marker gene analysis suggests that other clusters correspond to contaminating cell types (Table S1). We focused our analysis on the akh+ cluster, which is also nSyb positive, supporting the neuroendocrine nature of these cells (Figure S4E). Within this cluster, we identified 11 cells co-expressing both akh and nucb1 (7 males, 4 females). Differential expression analysis of these cells by sex was determined.

Prediction of nuclear localization signals

Potential classical nuclear localization signals (cNLSs) in Drosophila Nucb1 were predicted using cNLS Mapper with the full-length protein sequence corresponding to UniProt accession Q9VVK7. cNLS Mapper predicts importin-α/β–dependent nuclear localization signals and reports both monopartite and bipartite cNLS motifs with associated activity scores. A cutoff score of 3.0 was used, based on the cNLS Mapper scoring scheme in which scores of 3–5 are consistent with proteins showing both nuclear and cytoplasmic localization. Using this threshold, predicted monopartite and bipartite NLS sequences were identified in Nucb1.63 cNLS mapper was accessed using the following link: https://nls-mapper.iab.keio.ac.jp/cgi-bin/NLS_Mapper_form.cgi .

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2 (2024-10-31)) with lme4, emmeans, and effectsize packages for linear and mixed-effects modelling76. The data for each endpoint were analyzed using a two-tiered modelling framework to evaluate genotype and sex effects while accounting for biological replicate variability. First, a fixed-effects linear model (lm) was fit including Genotype, Sex, and their interaction, to estimate group means and evaluate main effects and interactions across the pooled dataset. Second, to account for replicate-to-replicate variation and to provide more reliable estimates of interaction terms, a linear mixed-effects model (lmer) was implemented with replicate modelled as a random factor. Estimated marginal means (EMMs) and standard errors (SEs) were derived using the emmeans package. Each data point represents the combined average of 5 (mass/TAG/Glycogen) or 10 (ExQ/qPCR) flies. η2 is a measure of effect size and represents how much of the variance is explained by the factor (η2 > 0.14 is considered a large effect size). GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1) was used for additional statistical testing and figure generation.
