Mechanisms of lncRNA in Diabetic Foot Ulcer Wound Healing
Authors: Yiming Chen, Mengting Wang, Xiaoliang Cao, Shengfeng Gao, Yasu Jiang, Zhenhua Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disease, and some diabetic patients develop diabetic foot ulcers (DFU), which are one of the most prevalent and serious complications among diabetic patients. These ulcers can lead to neuropathy, vasculopathy and severe infections. LncRNAs, a class of long‐stranded noncoding RNAs that regulate gene expression, have been shown to play a critical regulatory role in diabetic foot ulcer wound healing. These molecules have been demonstrated to modulate numerous facets of the inflammatory response, cell proliferation and angiogenesis, which collectively influence the outcome of wound healing. A substantial body of research has emerged in recent years, highlighting the critical role of these noncoding transcripts in DFU. This paper reviews recent research advances on the regulatory functions and potential mechanisms of noncoding transcripts in the healing of diabetic foot ulcers, based on a literature search of databases such as PubMed and Web of Science over the past decade.Keywords: diabetes, DNA technology, RNA technologies

Introduction

Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) are a prevalent and severe complication among individuals with diabetes. The incidence and disability rates of these conditions are increasing, which poses a significant challenge to global public health. According to the latest report from the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), approximately 589 million adults worldwide have diabetes [1], with an estimated DFU prevalence of around 6.3% [2]. Annually, 18.6 million new cases of diabetes are diagnosed among people worldwide [3]. The lifetime risk of developing a DFU ranges from 19% to 34%, with nearly 20% of patients ultimately requiring amputation [4]. DFUs have been shown to have a considerable impact on quality of life [5]. The prevalence of DFUs is marginally higher in male patients than in females, and the risk increases substantially with age [6]. The incidence of DFUs exhibits geographical variation. Developed countries demonstrate comparatively lower rates, attributable to early intervention and abundant medical resources. In contrast, developing countries are characterised by high incidence and elevated amputation risks [7]. The major risk factors that have been identified include neuropathy, vascular disease and severe infections [3] (Figure 1). Numerous studies have demonstrated the deleterious effects of these factors on the healing process of wounds [8]. From a clinical perspective, the condition poses significant challenges due to the high recurrence, disability and mortality rates that are characteristic of the condition. The considerable economic burden and the intricate multidisciplinary treatment requirements further underscore the limitations of current prevention and treatment methods [9]. Consequently, there is an imperative for in‐depth exploration of its molecular mechanisms and the development of innovative treatment strategies to promote wound healing and reduce the risk of amputation. This paper conducted a literature review by searching relevant reviews, clinical trials and other articles published over the past decade in databases such as PubMed and WOS.

Diabetes patients experiencing prolonged hyperglycemia develop neuropathy, leading to diminished or lost sensation; vasculopathy causes insufficient blood supply and reduced repair capacity; immune dysfunction weakens the body's defenses and exacerbates infections, accelerating tissue necrosis and ulcer formation.

Neuropathy

Neuropathy is a significant contributor to the development of ulcers. Chronic high blood sugar may cause damage to peripheral nerve fibres, leading to impaired nerve function. Sensory neuropathy is the most prevalent type of diabetic foot neuropathy, resulting in a loss or reduction of sensation in patients. Patients may experience pain, tingling, numbness or prickling sensations, as well as reduced tactile perception in their feet. As a result of motor neuropathy, foot muscles atrophy and become less functional, which affects balance and gait. Autonomic neuropathy affects the autonomic nervous system, which controls internal organs and blood vessels. This condition may lead to insufficient blood flow to the feet, increasing the risk of ulcers and infection [10]. Diabetic neuropathy, which affects sensory, motor and autonomic nerves, can cause changes in the skin barrier, increasing the risk of skin damage and infection [11].

Vasculopathy

The majority of patients with DFUs exhibit persistent hyperglycemia, which can lead to endothelial cell (EC) dysfunction and apoptosis [12]. This dysfunction impairs the secretion of multiple angiogenic and vasoactive factors, thereby inhibiting angiogenesis [13, 14, 15]. Research indicates that endothelial dysfunction is a key factor impeding wound healing, significantly delaying epithelialization and tissue remodeling processes and ultimately affecting healing outcomes [16, 17, 18]. Moreover, it has been demonstrated that chronic hyperglycemia exacerbates oxidative stress and promotes the production of pro‐inflammatory molecules, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation and migration and delaying wound repair [19, 20]. Furthermore, a hyperglycemic environment has been demonstrated to promote the formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs). Upon binding to specific receptors (RAGEs) on ECs, AGEs activate NADPH oxidase‐mediated reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, intensifying inflammatory responses and further impeding wound healing processes [21].

Infections

The immune system of diabetic patients is significantly weaker than that of healthy individuals. Consequently, infection in diabetic patients poses a grave and often fatal threat [22]. A wide range of bacterial species, including Staphylococcus aureus , Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa , frequently infect diabetic wounds. These microorganisms penetrate deep into the fascia through irritated or poorly perfused skin, leading to the progression of sepsis and life‐threatening infections [23, 24].

Wound Healing

The process of wound healing in DFUs is a dynamic and complex biological process that can be divided into the following four phases that overlap partially: haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodeling (Figure 2). These phases involve a large number of cell types, extracellular components, growth factors and cytokines. Diabetes has been demonstrated to impair wound healing by affecting one or more of the biological mechanisms of these processes [25]. Impaired wound healing can lead to the development of chronic wounds, which often necessitate amputation. Diabetes is the most prevalent cause of nontraumatic lower limb amputations, with 15%–20% of foot ulcers eventually requiring amputation [26]. Persistent hyperglycaemia, the most fundamental pathological factor in DFUs, can lead to neurological dysfunction, which can in turn result in neurogenic DFUs. Furthermore, hyperglycaemia can cause alterations in the peripheral arteries of the foot, leading to local ischaemia and increasing the risk of ischaemic DFUs. Furthermore, DFU may also occur because of factors such as long‐term compression of the foot, traumatic injury to the skin and infections caused by poor hygiene practices [27]. Current treatment strategies, encompassing wound debridement, wound dressings, infection treatment, haemodialysis, medication unloading, hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) [28] and negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) [29], are effective in alleviating symptoms and decelerating disease progression. The limited efficacy of conventional treatments underscores the ongoing significance of DFU therapy as a clinical challenge, underscoring the necessity for sustained research endeavours aimed at exploring novel approaches to promote healing of diabetic foot disease, with a view to reducing morbidity and mortality.

The three key stages of wound healing: the inflammation phase, the angiogenesis phase and the extracellular matrix remodeling phase.

Inflammation

The inflammatory phase of wound healing is a pivotal initiating process for tissue repair, involving the synergistic action of multiple cells and molecules. In the aftermath of an injury, the initial phase of infiltration is characterised by the predominance of neutrophils. These cells are pivotal in the elimination of pathogens through phagocytosis, the release of ROS and the production of antimicrobial peptides. The regulatory dynamics of this process are orchestrated by chemokines (e.g., IL‐8) and complement fragments (C5a) [30]; macrophages are then polarised from the pro‐inflammatory M1 type (secretion of TNF‐α, IL‐1β) to the reparative M2 type (secretion of TGF‐β, VEGF), and by clearing apoptotic cells and releasing anti‐inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL‐10), it regulates inflammation abatement. In contrast, platelets release growth factors (PDGF, TGF‐β) to pave the way for subsequent repair [31]. At the molecular level, the following processes occur: cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐6) and chemokines (CCL2) activate ECs and immune recruitment [32]; the complement system (C3a/C5a) enhances bactericidal and permeabilising properties; the TLR recognises damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) to amplify inflammatory signals; and lipid mediators (PGE2, LTB4) regulate vascular responses and leukocyte chemotaxis [33]. The timely termination of inflammation depends on the inhibition of neutrophil infiltration by pro‐ablative mediators (e.g., lipoxins, abrogens), adenosine signalling to promote the release of anti‐inflammatory factors and the clearance of apoptotic cells [34]. In the event of dysregulation of this phase (e.g., M1/M2 imbalance or insufficient SPMs), the result may be chronic wounds (e.g., diabetic ulcers). This underscores the significance of maintaining a dynamic balance for the purpose of healing [20].

Angiogenesis

The angiogenic phase of wound healing is pivotal in re‐establishing oxygen supply and nutrient delivery through the neovascular network [35]. The core mechanism of this phase involves the synergistic action of ECs [36], pericytes and macrophages with sophisticated regulation of molecular signals. Following injury, ECs are driven to differentiate into tip cells, which secrete matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) to guide migration, and stalk cells, which inhibit over‐branching via Notch/DLL4 signalling, to form luminal structures [37, 38]. Pericytes are recruited by PDGF‐BB/PDGFRβ and wrap around the vessel, and TGF‐β promotes their differentiation into smooth muscle cells to enhance stability. Macrophages (M2‐type) support vessel maturation by secreting vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), angiopoietin‐1 and removing debris. In contrast, platelet‐released stromal cell‐derived factor‐1α (SDF‐1α) recruits endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) to participate in the process of repair [39]. At the molecular level, the process of hypoxia‐activated HIF‐1α results in the upregulation of VEGF expression, while the FGF2/FGFR1 and Ang/Tie systems regulate migration and stability, respectively. Furthermore, ECM remodeling (e.g., MMP degradation of collagen to release growth factors) and metabolic reprogramming (glycolytic energy supply) further promote angiogenesis [40]. From a pathological perspective, diabetic wounds exhibit impaired angiogenesis due to the presence of hyperglycaemia, which has been demonstrated to inhibit the HIF‐1α/VEGF pathway. AGEs have also been shown to impair EPC function, while TNF‐α–induced endothelial apoptosis in chronic inflammation has been shown to trigger vascular leakage [41]. Imbalance at this stage can lead to delayed healing or excessive scar proliferation and targeted regulation of VEGF, Notch and other signals has become a therapeutic focus.

ECM Remodeling

The ECM (extracellular matrix) remodeling phase of wound healing is responsible for the restoration of tissue function through a dynamic balance of matrix synthesis, degradation and remodeling. The molecular and cellular mechanisms that underpin this process are subject to multidimensional and sophisticated regulation. Following injury, the TGF‐β1/Smad pathway is activated, leading to the differentiation of fibroblasts to myofibroblasts [42]. This process induces the expression of α‐smooth muscle actin (α‐SMA) and the synthesis of collagen (types I and III) [43]. Initially, type III collagen dominates to maintain flexibility, and lysyl oxidase (LOX)‐mediated cross‐linking of collagen progressively enhances the mechanical strength of the matrix. Macrophages (type M2) have been shown to synergistically regulate collagen deposition by secreting TGF‐β and IL‐10, while MMP family members (e.g., MMP‐1/2/9) have been observed to scavenge temporary matrices (e.g., fibronectin) driven by inflammatory factors (e.g., TNF‐α). The activity of these enzymes is tightly regulated by tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) to prevent excessive degradation [44]. Integrins (α2β1/α5β1) have been shown to activate the FAK/RhoA pathway by recognizing ECM components such as fibronectin (FN). This, in turn, signals mechanical stress and senses matrix stiffness in concert with the YES/TAZ nuclear transcription factors to form an ‘ECM stiffness‐cellular contraction’—positive feedback loop to promote tissue remodeling [45, 46]. In pathological conditions, the persistent activation of TGF‐β1 signalling has been demonstrated to lead to collagen overdeposition and keloid formation. Furthermore, chronic wounds, such as diabetic ulcers, have been shown to be affected by ECM destabilisation due to uncontrolled matrix metalloproteinase‐9 (MMP‐9) activity or disruption of collagen cross‐links by AGEs [41]. Consequently, the targeting of TGF‐β signalling, MMP activity or mechanotransduction pathways (e.g., Yap inhibitors) has become a focus of research in order to improve ECM remodeling and scar regulation.

LncRNA

Long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) is a class of RNA molecules characterised by a length that exceeds 200 nucleotides (nt). The classification of these molecules is typically based on their location in the genome in relation to proximal protein‐coding genes. LncRNAs can be categorised into various classifications, including intergenic, intronic, sense, antisense and bidirectional [47]. These molecules have been demonstrated to regulate a wide range of biological processes, including cellular metabolism, development, proliferation, transcription and posttranscriptional modifications through indirect means [48]. It is a commonly held belief that these noncoding RNA molecules do not encode proteins; rather, it is thought that they exert their functions through epigenetic modifications, transcriptional regulation and posttranscriptional regulation. These molecules have the capacity to recruit transcription factors, thereby activating or repressing gene expression. In addition, they can act as endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) or as ‘miRNA sponges’ (Figure 3), modulating the activity of miRNAs and influencing their expression [49, 50]. Furthermore, the function of these noncoding molecules extends to the regulation of gene expression in various oxidative stress cells, including macrophages (MФs), ECs and vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) [51].

lncRNA sequences contain miRNA binding sites, enabling them to competitively adsorb miRNAs like a sponge absorbing water. This prevents miRNAs from binding to and inhibiting their true target mRNAs, thereby indirectly relieving gene silencing.

LncRNAs are regarded as vital intracellular regulatory molecules that function in a variety of physiological processes. Aberrant expression of these molecules has been demonstrated to disrupt the orderly process of DFU wound healing. These molecules have been demonstrated to function as regulators of gene expression, through processes such as the absorption of microRNAs (e.g., H19, MALAT1, etc.), the recruitment of enzymes related to epigenetics (e.g., TETILA, H19, etc.) and the regulation of protein stability (e.g., Lethe, Plod1, etc.) [52]. It is noteworthy that the regulatory function of lncRNAs has been demonstrated in the context of wound healing. Some notable examples of such regulatory functions include the mitigation of adverse effects of inflammation on wound healing by XIST, MEG3, FABP5B3, PRINS and KLDHC7B‐DT [53, 54, 55], through participation in the inflammatory response. Furthermore, the role of MALAT1 in regulating angiogenesis in vivo has been demonstrated to enhance blood supply to the wound, thereby accelerating the healing process [56]. A substantial body of research has emerged in recent years, highlighting the critical role of these noncoding transcripts in DFU [57]. This review aims to provide a concise overview of the reported regulatory functions and potential mechanisms of action of these noncoding transcripts in the context of DFU wound healing.

The Role of lncRNA in Wound Healing

The process of wound healing is a complex biological process involving a large number of cell types, growth factors and cytokines (Table 1). The process can be categorized into the following four phases that partially overlap: haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodeling. Impaired wound healing is a potential consequence of diabetes, attributable to the impact of this condition on one or more of the biological mechanisms that underpin the process. Current treatment strategies encompass aggressive wound debridement, adequate pressure offloading and the utilisation of topical dressings [76]. However, the efficacy of current therapeutic strategies for wound repair is limited by persistent inflammation, impaired angiogenesis and insufficient extracellular matrix remodeling in the wound microenvironment, particularly in cases of severe burns and chronic skin ulcers [77]. In recent years, stem cell transplantation and stem cell–derived exosome therapy have emerged as promising treatments for chronic diabetic wound healing, with studies demonstrating their capacity to ameliorate wound inflammation, stimulate granulation tissue formation and enhance angiogenesis [78]. In addition, bio‐3D printed cellulose‐based wound dressings have been found to promote wound healing and reduce the risk of infection, with potential benefits for treating wounds such as chronic wounds, burns and DFUs [79]. LncRNAs play an important regulatory role in diabetic foot wound healing, accelerating or delaying wound healing, mainly by affecting processes such as inflammatory response, neovascularisation and cellular proliferation.

Inflammation Stage

Macrophages have been shown to play a central role in the regulation of the chronic inflammatory phase of DFU, and their abnormal function can lead to persistent inflammation and delayed healing [80]. Recent studies have revealed the involvement of lncRNAs in the inflammatory process by regulating immune metabolism and intercellular communication, thus providing potential targets for DFU treatment. For instance, the expression of the lncRNA GAS5 was found to be significantly upregulated in macrophages under conditions of high glucose levels, thereby exacerbating the inflammatory response by activating the Signal Transducer Activator of Transcription 1 (STAT1) signalling pathway. Conversely, the knockdown of GAS5 has been observed to promote wound healing [58]. However, further research is required to elucidate the intricate interactions of GAS5 with metabolic pathways, such as glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, in order to comprehend the precise mechanisms by which it regulates metabolic reprogramming. Another key molecule, WAKMAR2, has been observed to undergo a reduction in expression during the inflammatory phase. The defects associated with this molecule have been shown to enhance chemokine secretion from keratinocytes and to promote leukocyte recruitment to the trauma site by activating the NF‐κB (NF‐κB is a class of transcription factors that can trans‐activate genes involved in various biological processes, including immune responses, inflammation, cell growth and survival. Dysregulation of NF‐κB activation can lead to acute and chronic inflammatory diseases [81].) signalling pathway. These findings suggest that targeting WAKMAR2 may have the potential to remodel the trauma microenvironment by inhibiting excessive inflammatory responses [59, 60]. Furthermore, the downregulation of lncRNA MALAT1 expression in infected DFUs has been demonstrated to be closely associated with elevated pro‐inflammatory factors, including TNF‐α and IL‐6, and reduced anti‐inflammatory factor IL‐10. The latter has been shown to inhibit high glucose‐induced inflammatory imbalance through the activation of SAA3 [61, 62]. In the context of high glucose‐treated umbilical cord MSCs, MALAT1 has been observed to regulate the SIRT1/Nrf2 pathway (The SIRT1/Nrf2 pathway collectively constitutes the core defense system for cells against oxidative stress, inflammation and toxic substances [82].) through the adsorption of microRNA‐142 (miRNA‐142), thereby enhancing the antioxidant and repair functions of stem cells and accelerating ulcer healing [63]. Despite the fact that the functions of GAS5, WAKMAR2 and MALAT1 in the regulation of inflammation have been partially elucidated, the mechanisms by which they interact with metabolic pathways (e.g., glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation) in macrophages and keratinocytes remain unclear. It is anticipated that further investigation of the manner in which hyperglycaemia affects chronic inflammatory processes through the means of lncRNA‐mediated metabolic reprogramming will result in the formulation of novel strategies for the reversal of the pathological state of DFU.

Angiogenesis Stage

It has been established that epidermal cells play a pivotal role in the process of re‐epithelialisation, which is integral to the healing of DFU wounds [83]. This process entails the rapid proliferation and multilayered structure formation of keratin‐forming cells (e.g., HaCaT cells), thereby ensuring the restoration of tissue integrity [20, 84]. Recent studies have revealed the multilevel mechanism of lncRNA in regulating DFU healing. In a high glucose environment, the downregulation of X‐inactive specific transcript (XIST) expression in HaCaT cells led to the aberrant upregulation of microRNA‐126‐3p, which significantly weakened the proliferation and migration ability of keratinocytes through the inhibition of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR). This suggests that targeting XIST may restore the EGFR signalling (The EGFR pathway serves as a primary regulator of cellular responses to external growth signals, precisely controlling fundamental life processes such as cell growth, division and repair [85].) to ameliorate re‐epithelialisation [64]. Epithelialisation disorders are a category of diseases characterised by the aberrant accumulation of cells in the epithelium, resulting in the formation of abnormal accumulations of tissue. Furthermore, the long noncoding RNA MALAT1 has been observed to upregulate the zinc finger protein ZNF148 by adsorbing microRNA‐106a‐5p, thus forming a self‐reinforcing positive feedback loop that enhances HaCaT cell function [65]. It has been established that H19 exerts its regulatory influence over the microenvironment through a dual pathway. The molecular mechanism of action of the microRNA‐130 b‐3p sponge is to deregulate bridging granule core protein 1 (Dsg1), thereby promoting keratinocyte differentiation. In contrast, the molecular mechanism of action of the microRNA‐152‐3p inhibitor is to upregulate PTEN, which in turn enhances fibroblast activity and mitigates pathological injury [66, 67]. With regard to vascular complications, the circulating marker DLEU1 has been shown to exacerbate EC oxidative stress and apoptosis by inhibiting microRNA‐96‐5p [68]. In contrast, the lncRNA ANRIL has been observed to activate hypoxia‐inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF1A)/VEGFA signalling through a FUS‐dependent pathway [69, 70]. The restoration of the migratory and tubular capacity of lymphatic ECs subjected to high‐glucose injury was found to be complementary to the GAS5/TAF15‐regulated HIF1A/VEGF pathway (The HIF‐1α/VEGF axis is a core pathway that initiates adaptive responses in hypoxic conditions, delivering increased blood flow and oxygen to oxygen‐deprived tissues through the formation of new blood vessels [86].). This observation underscores the multitarget synergistic potential of lncRNAs in the context of angiogenesis [71, 87]. Despite the fact that the aforementioned mechanisms provide new ideas for targeted therapies, the current studies still have limitations. The majority of the evidence is based on in vitro models and needs to be validated in in vivo experiments and clinically heterogeneous samples. The regulatory role of H19 in the exosome of MSCs is therapeutically promising, but its delivery efficiency and targeting need to be optimised. Furthermore, synergistic or antagonistic relationships among different lncRNAs (e.g., XIST and ANRIL) are not yet clear, and systematic regulatory frameworks need to be constructed in the future. The nature of the relationship, whether synergistic or antagonistic, between different lncRNAs (e.g., XIST and ANRIL) remains to be elucidated.

ECM Remodeling Stage

In the phase of wound healing characterised by remodeling, which commences 3 weeks after injury and persists for months or even years, fibroblasts progressively restore tissue structure and function through the synthesis and remodeling of the ECM [88]. The core mechanism of this process involves a dynamic switch between type I and type III collagen. Type III collagen provides flexibility in the early stages and is subsequently replaced by type I collagen to enhance mechanical strength, and cross‐linking and rearrangement to improve tissue stability [89]. Subsequent to this process, type I collagen is deposited, thereby enhancing the mechanical strength and improving tissue stability through cross‐linking and rearrangement. Concurrently, fibroblasts secrete elastin, glycosaminoglycans and other components, which collectively function to regulate ECM metabolism and tissue elasticity repair through the balanced regulation of MMPs and their inhibitors (TIMPs) [90, 91]. Recent studies have revealed the multifaceted regulatory function of lncRNAs in this phase. For instance, the lncRNA H19 activates the MAPK signalling pathway (The MAPK signalling pathway is one of the core signal transduction systems through which cells convert external signals into internal responses. It amplifies signals stepwise via a three‐tiered kinase cascade (MAPKKK → MAPKK → MAPK), regulating multiple critical biological processes including cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis and stress adaptation. Its most classic RAS–RAF–MEK–ERK branch pathway is primarily activated by growth factors, strongly promoting cell growth and survival. Additionally, branch pathways such as JNK and p38 predominantly respond to stress and inflammatory signals, participating in apoptosis and immune regulation [92, 93, 94]) by recruiting the transcription factor SRF to the CTGF promoter region, thereby promoting fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition, and accelerating diabetic wound healing. However, overactivation of H19 may lead to keloidal scarring [72].

Hyperactivation of MMP‐9 has been identified as a contributing factor to delayed diabetic wound healing. The present study investigates the effects of the knockdown of TET2‐interacting lncRNA TETILA on TET2 protein expression, TET2 activity and MMP‐9 transcription. The results demonstrate that TETILA knockdown reduces TET2 protein expression, TET2 activity and MMP‐9 transcription, while promoting HaCat migration and reversing age‐induced wound healing defects [73].

In contrast, the lncRNA URIDS demonstrates spatiotemporally specific bidirectional regulation, inhibiting Plod1 [95, 96] enzyme activity in response to the stimulation of AGEs [74]. This results in a reduction of collagen cross‐linking and a delay in wound closure. Concurrently, lncRNA URIDS inhibits the MAPK signalling pathway through the fibroblast exosome (fibroblast exosome), thereby promoting wound healing and collagen deposition. Fibroblastosome (Fib‐EVs) have been observed to enhance keratinocyte MMP‐9 expression in a YTHDF2‐dependent manner, thus disrupting the equilibrium between collagen synthesis and degradation, and indirectly promoting ECM remodeling [75]. This paradoxical effect suggests that lncRNA URIDS may possess a spatiotemporal‐specific regulatory function and that its homeostatic mechanism merits further exploration. Furthermore, the metabolic regulation of ECM components, such as elastin and glycosaminoglycans, may involve the action of certain lncRNAs, including MALAT1 and H19. However, the precise mechanisms through which these phenomena occur remain to be fully elucidated. In the context of diabetic scarring, the imbalance between MMP‐9 and its inhibitor, tissue inhibitor of matrix metalloproteinase‐1 (TIMP‐1), has been identified as a significant marker. The restoration of this balance through targeted intervention, including the use of specific lncRNAs, has been proposed as a potential therapeutic strategy to enhance the quality of scar tissue. It is noteworthy that the mechanism of lncRNA delivery between fibroblasts and keratinocytes via exosomes reveals the importance of intercellular communication in the dynamic regulation of the ECM. In future research, it is necessary to explore whether other lncRNAs, such as ANRIL and GAS5, etc. The coordination of multicellular repair behaviours is facilitated through analogous pathways, and the impact of immune cell (e.g., macrophages)‐derived lncRNAs on the activity of fibroblasts is investigated to establish systemic regulatory networks and identify more precise combinations of therapeutic targets.

Prospective Developments

lncRNAs have been identified as key molecules in epigenetic regulation, and it has been hypothesised that they may have the capacity to modulate inflammation, angiogenesis and cell proliferation in the context of wound repair in DFUs. Nevertheless, the clinical applications of these peptides are constrained by challenges such as low delivery efficiency and off‐target effects.

Firstly, sequence similarity may lead to nonspecific binding. In this instance, the complementarity of the sequence of lncRNAs with some nontarget gene regions may result in the binding of the aforementioned molecules to nontarget mRNAs or DNA, thereby interfering with the regulation of unintended gene expression. Secondly, the ability of these noncoding transcripts to interact nonspecifically with a variety of RNA‐binding proteins (RBPs) and transcription factors within the cell may lead to the disruption of the precision of the original gene regulatory network. Furthermore, the function of lncRNA as a ceRNA is intricate. By acting as a molecular sponge for miRNAs, lncRNAs have the capacity to regulate the stability and translation of specific mRNAs. However, their dynamic binding with miRNAs may affect multiple downstream targets, which may result in a global perturbation of gene expression profiles. Finally, during in vivo delivery, the nonspecific organisational distribution of the carrier system may lead to enrichment of lncRNAs in nontarget regions, which in turn triggers biological effects at unintended sites. Collectively, these mechanisms unveil the multifaceted origins of off‐target phenomena in the context of lncRNA functional studies, underscoring the imperative for the formulation of precise regulatory strategies [97]. Recent studies have seen an increased focus on the use of CRISPR‐dCas9 and single‐cell sequencing technologies. The combination of these two technologies has the potential to create a precise intervention system that targets lncRNAs. This system could offer a breakthrough solution for the treatment of DFU.

Single‐Cell RNA Sequencing (scRNA‐Seq)

Single‐cell sequencing has been demonstrated to overcome the limitations of traditional bulk sequencing by its ability to resolve genomic, transcriptomic or epigenomic heterogeneity at the level of individual cells. The technology in question has been demonstrated to reveal the diversity of cell populations, for example, in the context of different subpopulations present within the tumour microenvironment. Furthermore, it has been shown to trace developmental trajectories, such as those exhibited by embryonic differentiation processes, and to identify rare cell types, including circulating tumour cells and stem cells [98]. The heterogeneity of lncRNA expression among different cell types (e.g., keratinocytes, fibroblasts, immune cells) in the microenvironment of DFU trauma can be resolved by single‐cell RNA and epigenome sequencing.

CRISPR‐dCas9

CRISPR‐dCas9 represents a revolutionary development in the field of gene editing. By modifying the traditional Cas9 nuclease (rendering it inactive for DNA cleavage) to achieve ‘shearless’ functionality, dCas9 is able to precisely target specific gene loci and achieve targeted regulation of gene expression. This process is facilitated by fusion proteins (e.g., transcriptional activators or repressors, epitope‐modifying enzymes). Based on targets screened from single‐cell data, the CRISPR‐dCas9 system can be designed to intervene spatiotemporally and spatio‐temporally specific to lncRNAs.

Inhibition of pro‐disease lncRNAs: fusion of dCas9 with transcriptional repressor structural domains (e.g., KRAB) to target and silence promoter regions of pro‐inflammatory or fibrotic lncRNAs;

Activation of repair lncRNAs: specific upregulation of pro‐angiogenic or epithelial regeneration lncRNAs using dCas9‐VPR (transcriptional activation system) [99].

The combination of CRISPR‐dCas9 and single‐cell sequencing technology with the delivery of lncRNA to diabetic foot ulcer wounds has resulted in a significant increase in the research focus on two emerging classes of delivery systems: nanoparticles (NPs) and exosomes.

MSCs‐Exosomes

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are adult stem cells that possess self‐renewal and multidirectional differentiation potential. They are widely distributed in bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cord and other tissues. Recent studies have revealed that the paracrine effects of MSCs, especially the release of exosomes, play a key role in tissue repair, immunomodulation and disease treatment [100, 101].

Exosomes are defined as extracellular vesicles with a diameter of approximately 30–150 nm that are released extracellularly by the fusion of intracellular multivesicular bodies with the cell membrane. Exosomes secreted by MSCs carry biologically active molecules from the mother cell, including proteins (e.g., growth factors, cytokines), lipids, mRNAs, miRNAs and noncoding RNAs. These components have the capacity to modulate gene expression and the function of target cells through receptor‐mediated signalling or content delivery [102].

MSCs‐exos possess the ability to promote wound healing through multiple mechanisms. Diabetic patients exhibit persistent chronic inflammation [103]. In their study, Li X and their colleagues evaluated the physiological characteristics of diabetic patients, revealing significantly elevated levels of fasting blood glucose, glycated haemoglobin and inflammatory markers (NOX1, NOX4). Furthermore, the therapeutic effects of ADMSCs‐exos were investigated, with the conclusion being that high Nrf2 expression in ADMSCs‐exos reduces ROS and inflammatory cytokines [104].

In conditions of hyperglycaemia, the process of angiogenesis is impaired and endothelial function is compromised. MSCs‐exos have been demonstrated to promote vascular EC proliferation, migration and luminal formation through multiple signalling pathways [105]. A recent study evaluated a potential therapeutic approach involving controlled release of synovial mesenchymal stem cell (SMSC)‐derived exosome overexpressed with chitosan. This approach entailed the forced expression of microRNA‐126‐3p, resulting in the transfer of enhanced angiogenic properties from endothelial progenitor cells to SMSCs. Subsequent analysis revealed that the composite exhibited the capacity to sustainably release exosomes, thereby significantly stimulating MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT pathways. These pathways have been identified as being critical for activating angiogenesis, accelerating re‐epithelialization, promoting collagen maturation in diabetic wounds and enhancing overall healing efficiency [106].

The adverse microenvironment of diabetic wounds has been demonstrated to impede keratinocyte migration and collagen synthesis [107]. MSCs‐Exos have been demonstrated to promote re‐epithelialization and collagen remodeling. Wang et al. successfully fabricated an injectable FHE hydrogel combining the peptide (F127/OHA‐EPL) with ADMSCs‐Exos, exhibiting self‐healing and antimicrobial capabilities. This hydrogel demonstrated significant efficacy in enhancing full‐thickness skin wound healing in diabetic patients and markedly enhanced proliferation, migration and tubulogenesis of HUVECs in vitro, indicating its potential to promote complete skin regeneration [108].

The present study investigates the role of MSC‐exosome‐derived lncRNA H19 in promoting healing in DFU by regulating the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway (The PI3K/AKT pathway serves as the ‘command centre’ for cell survival, growth and metabolism. [109]) and coordinating the mutual interaction between microRNA‐152‐3p and PTEN [67]. Exosomes derived from hair follicle mesenchymal stem cells (HF‐MSC‐Exos) have been shown to elevate levels of lncRNA H19 and to inhibit the stimulation of NLRP3 inflammation. These effects promote HaCaT cell proliferation and migration, while simultaneously inhibiting cellular pyroptosis [110].

Nanoparticles

Nanoparticles (NPs) are minute particles with a diameter ranging from 1 to 100 nm (nm). Their distinctive physicochemical properties (e.g., elevated specific surface area, quantum effects and surface modifiability) have rendered them highly versatile, with a broad spectrum of applications in the domains of materials science, biomedicine, energy and the environment. These particles can be prepared by chemical synthesis, biosynthesis or physical methods (e.g., ball milling, laser ablation) and can be endowed with targeting, biocompatibility or sensing capabilities through surface functionalization (e.g., attachment of antibodies, ligands or fluorescent markers). Through the targeted delivery of RNA (e.g., siRNA, miRNAs) to the lesion, NPs have the capacity to regulate gene expression with a high degree of precision [111, 112].

Limitations and Potential Solutions

At present, there are certain limitations in the use of lncRNA for treating DFU wounds (Figure 4):

Low targeting efficiency: LncRNA therapies necessitate highly efficient and specific targeting of specific cell types within diabetic foot ulcer wounds (e.g., microvascular ECs, macrophages). Current delivery systems frequently demonstrate low efficiency and an absence of tissue specificity. Research indicates that engineered exosomes (e.g., ADSC‐derived exosomes), as natural nanocarriers, can encapsulate lncRNAs and achieve targeted delivery, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy. The modification of these particles has been shown to enhance their capacity to target specific cell types within the wound.

Conclusions

DFUs represent a severe complication of diabetes, characterised by impaired wound healing driven by persistent inflammation, dysregulated angiogenesis and aberrant ECM remodeling. Emerging evidence highlights the pivotal role of lncRNAs in modulating these pathological processes. Key lncRNAs, including GAS5, H19, MALAT1 and ANRIL, have been shown to regulate inflammatory responses by targeting signalling pathways (e.g., STAT1, NF‐κB), metabolic reprogramming and immune cell communication. In the context of angiogenesis, lncRNAs such as MALAT1 and ANRIL have been shown to orchestrate HIF1A/VEGF signalling. In contrast, other lncRNAs, including H19, have been observed to influence fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition during ECM remodeling. Notwithstanding these advances, extant research predominantly relies on in vitro models, with limited validation in vivo or clinical settings.

It is evident that current research is still beset by a plethora of challenges. Firstly, the low in vivo delivery efficiency and insufficient targeting specificity of lncRNAs limit their therapeutic efficacy. Secondly, concerns regarding their potential immunogenicity and off‐target effects have been raised. In addition, while there is significant individual heterogeneity among DFU patients, there is currently a lack of reliable lncRNA biomarkers to guide personalised treatment. In the field of translational medicine, the production of large‐scale, stable and clinically effective lncRNA therapeutics represents a significant challenge. Despite the fact that the technology is still in the exploratory phase, with ongoing technological breakthroughs and deepening research, the potential of lncRNA‐based gene therapy to become a crucial component of comprehensive DFU treatment systems in the future is promising, offering new hope to patients.
