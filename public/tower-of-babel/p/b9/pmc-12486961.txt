Navigating the clinical complexity of acute heart failure in an untreated adult with dextrotransposition of the great arteries (D-TGA) and multiple cardiac anomalies: a case report and review of literature
Authors: Mihret Hailu Mecha, Lensa Million, Elsah Tegene Asefa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTransposition of the great arteries is a critical congenital heart defect characterized by the reversal of the aorta and pulmonary arteries, resulting in two parallel circulatory pathways. This condition is typically incompatible with life unless a shunt, such as an atrial or ventricular septal defect, facilitates the adequate mixing of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood in the heart. Survival into adulthood without surgical correction is exceptionally rare. While a limited number of cases have been reported globally, none, to the best of our extensive search, have been associated with a patent ductus arteriosus and pulmonary artery aneurysm.Case presentationWe present a unique case of a 25-year-old Ethiopian male with complex dextrotransposition of the great arteries, who survived into adulthood and presented with acute decompensated heart failure. Remarkably, this patient had a constellation of associated anomalies, including an atrial septal defect, ventricular septal defect, patent ductus arteriosus, and severe pulmonary hypertension. Owing to resource limitations, insufficient medical knowledge, and financial constraints, the patient was managed conservatively. Despite these challenges, the patient showed symptomatic improvement with the conservative management provided.ConclusionThis case offers novel insights into the natural history of untreated dextrotransposition of the great arteries with multiple associated anomalies in adults. It also underscores the challenges of managing complex congenital heart defects in resource-limited settings and highlights the urgent need for accessible advanced diagnostic and therapeutic interventions to improve outcomes for similar patients.Keywords: Adult congenital heart disease, Case report, Ethiopia, Transposition of the great arteries (TGA), Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), Pulmonary artery aneurysm

Introduction

Dextrotransposition of the great arteries (D-TGA) is a congenital heart defect in which the aorta arises from the right ventricle and the pulmonary artery from the left ventricle while maintaining atrioventricular concordance. This abnormal configuration causes a complete separation of systemic and pulmonary circulation, leading to severe physiological consequences if not corrected surgically [1]. Transposition of the great arteries (TGA) is the second most common cyanotic congenital heart defect, following tetralogy of fallot (TOF), with a prevalence of 2.3 per 10,000 births. It tends to occur more frequently in boys [2]. The exact cause of TGA remains unclear; its underlying mechanisms are still debated. However, certain risk factors have been suggested. While not definitively proven, infants exposed to prenatal sex hormone therapy and those born to diabetic mothers are considered to be at an increased risk [3]. Familial cases have been reported, although they occur very rarely [4]. The natural prognosis is extremely poor, with survival to adulthood without surgical intervention being exceptionally rare [4]. Without treatment, 89.3% of patients with D-TGA do not survive beyond the first year of life [5].

We present the case of a 25-year-old male with unoperated D-TGA who had reached adulthood. This is only the second documented case from Africa, the first being reported in Nigeria. Although a few cases have been reported worldwide, none have been associated with a pulmonary artery aneurysm and a patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) according to our extensive search.

Case presentation

A 25-year-old Ethiopian man presented to our hospital with a 1-week history of shortness of breath at rest. He reported additional symptoms, including palpitations, orthopnea requiring three pillows, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND), a dry cough, and easy fatigability. His first encounter with medical care occurred at age 5 years when he was diagnosed with a cardiac condition; however, he did not receive follow-up care. Since that time, he has experienced dyspnea with moderate exertion, categorized as New York Heart Association (NYHA) Class II.

The patient was born at home following a full-term, 9-month pregnancy, with no reported complications. There was no maternal history of diabetes or drug use during pregnancy. His family history was negative for cardiac disease, and no dimorphic features were noted. He is the eldest of four siblings.

On medical evaluation, his vital signs at admission were as follows: blood pressure (BP) of 100/60 mmHg, pulse rate of 70 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 26 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation (SpO2) of 70% preductal and 72% postductal, without supplemental oxygen. Physical examination revealed cyanosis of the lips and digital clubbing. Chest examination showed intercostal and subcostal retractions, along with diffuse crepitation posteriorly in the infrascapular region.

Cardiovascular examination findings included raised jugular venous pressure (JVP), a point of maximum impulse (PMI) located in the seventh intercostal space lateral to the midclavicular line, a grade 3 holosystolic murmur best heard at the lower left sternal border, an accentuated P2, and a grade 3 diastolic murmur at the second left intercostal space.

Initial blood tests were unremarkable (Table 1). A chest X-ray (Fig. 1) showed bilateral hilar haziness with peribronchial cuffing, suggestive of grade II pulmonary edema. The electrocardiogram (ECG, Fig. 2) revealed M mitrale on inferior leads and V1, suggesting biatrial enlargement with left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) (voltage criteria on V1–2 and V5) and a narrow-based peaked T wave on precordial leads V4–V6, with a T wave inversion in lead III and augmented vector foot (aVF). A cardiac computed tomography (CT) scan (Figs. 3, 4, 5) showed transposition of the great arteries, the aorta arising from the right ventricle and the pulmonary artery arising from the left ventricle, accompanied by a significantly enlarged pulmonary artery aneurysm measuring 7 cm in diameter. A 1.2-cm PDA connected the pulmonary artery to the aortic arch distal to the origin of the left subclavian artery. Additionally, there were atrial septal defects (ASD) and ventricular septal defects (VSD), along with a reflux of contrast into the inferior vena cava (IVC) and hepatic veins, secondary to the right heart dysfunction. Echocardiography (Figs. 6, 7) revealed atrioventricular concordance, a 26-mm large ostium secundum ASD with a predominant left-to-right shunt, a small 16-mm perimembranous VSD, severe tricuspid regurgitation with right ventricular systolic pressures of 78 mmHg, and a preserved ejection fraction. Together, the findings from cardiac CT and echocardiography established the diagnosis of D-TGA.

The patient was diagnosed with decompensated heart failure secondary to dextrotransposition of the great arteries with severe pulmonary hypertension, ASD, VSD, and PDA. During his hospital stay, he was treated with intravenous furosemide (40 mg twice daily) and oral spironolactone (25 mg once daily) to treat pulmonary edema. With this treatment, his oxygen saturation improved to 87%. After 1 week, he was discharged with clinical improvement and was appointed for monthly follow-up in a chronic outpatient care setting.

Discussion

This case involves D-TGA with a perimembranous VSD, a large secundum ASD, PDA, pulmonary artery aneurysm, and pulmonary hypertension. It is unique, as there are no reports in medical literature of individuals with this combination of defects surviving to the age of 25 years. The presence of a large, nonrestrictive ASD facilitated adequate blood mixing, while the PDA and VSD further contributed to intracirculatory mixing. However, the presence of posttricuspid shunts increases pulmonary blood flow, which may contribute to the development of pulmonary hypertension [3].

The definitive management of D-TGA is surgical correction [5]. However, irreversible pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) presents significant challenges. This condition is strongly associated with postoperative PAH, which substantially reduces survival rates compared with those observed in nonoperated patients with PAH-congenital heart disease (CHD). Large posttricuspid shunts and older age at the time of corrective surgery are well-established risk factor for the development of irreversible disease [6]. Furthermore, a study from China reported that patients with complete transposition of the great arteries and severe pulmonary hypertension had a 50% mortality rate within the first 2 years following corrective surgery, which is considered an unsatisfactory outcome [7]. Our patient has severe pulmonary hypertension with two posttricuspid shunts: VSD and PDA, both of which are risk factors for irreversible pulmonary vascular disease. Moreover, his advanced age make him a poor surgical candidate.

A case report from Nigeria identified factors such as advanced age, Eisenmenger syndrome, symptomatic polycythemia, and chronic congestive heart failure (CHF) as contraindications for corrective surgery [8]. This case was similar to ours in that both patients had D-TGA without other significant risk factors and presented with ASD, VSD, and moderate hypoxia. However, unlike the Nigerian case, our patient also had an additional cardiac lesion, PDA, which likely contributed to the development of severe pulmonary hypertension. In contrast, the Nigerian case exhibited only mild pulmonary hypertension.

Another case report emphasizes the potential benefits of corrective surgery in patients with normal pulmonary vascular resistance, resulting in improved outcomes [9]. In that case, the patient had D-TGA, VSD, and pulmonary stenosis (PS). The presence of PS significantly reduced the pulmonary blood flow, thereby mitigating the risk of irreversible pulmonary vascular disease. On the contrary, our case had both VSD and PDA, which increased pulmonary blood flow and consequently heightened the risk of irreversible pulmonary disease.

Additionally, the other case highlighted the possibility of surgical correction after hemodynamic testing, which was unfortunately not feasible in our case owing to resource limitations. This further underscores the unique challenges faced in managing complex cardiac conditions in resource-limited settings.

There is no evidence to support the use of angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), beta-blockers, or aldosterone antagonists, either individually or in combination, to improve outcomes in systemic right ventricular (RV) systolic dysfunction [4, 8]. Additionally, the exact mechanism of pulmonary hypertension (PH) must be clarified before considering medical treatment [4].

In our case, the presence of intracirculatory mixing facilitates survival by allowing oxygenated and deoxygenated blood to mix, ensuring that oxygen is delivered to vital organs to sustain their function. However, it does not entirely mitigate hypoxia. This is evidenced by the development of secondary polycythemia, cyanosis, and digital clubbing, indicating persistent hypoxemia. The coexistence of a large ASD and PDA, both of which increase pulmonary blood flow, predisposes the patient to severe pulmonary hypertension, which subsequently increases the risk of pulmonary artery aneurysm.

Our patient was treated with furosemide and spironolactone and was discharged in an improved condition with a follow-up appointment scheduled. Although a similar case from Nigeria was managed using furosemide and digoxin, our patient presented with a heart rate ranging from 65 to 72 beats per minute. Considering this clinical presentation and the lack of evidence to support the aforementioned management approach in such scenarios, our chosen management strategy is justified. Furthermore, in the absence of hemodynamic testing such as right heart catheterization, the unavailability of surgical options to correct the defect, and financial constraints, the management approach was necessarily confined to supportive care.

By documenting this case, we aim to contribute to existing medical literature by presenting a rare and unique constellation of cardiac lesions associated with TGA, including PDA, VSD, ASD, and pulmonary artery aneurysm. This uncommon combination of lesions provides novel insights into disease presentation and highlights the management challenges that have not been adequately addressed in prior studies. Furthermore, it offers valuable perspectives on the natural history and complexity of managing such conditions, particularly in resource-limited settings, where data on similar cases remain scarce. This underscores the critical importance of early diagnosis to improve outcomes in these contexts.

Conclusion

This case represents a rare and unique presentation of an unoperated adult with D-TGA surviving into adulthood, who was managed with supportive care owing to limited resources, insufficient knowledge, and financial constraints. By documenting this case, we aim to contribute novel insights to the field of cardiology, as it offers critical perspectives on the natural course of untreated TGA and associated anomalies in adults. Additionally, it highlights the challenges of managing complex congenital heart defects in resource-limited settings and underscores the urgent need for accessible advanced diagnostic and therapeutic options to improve patient outcomes.
