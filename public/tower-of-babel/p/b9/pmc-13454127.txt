Multiple source enrichment model of organic matter in fifth member of Xujiahe Formation of Upper Triassic, northeastern Sichuan Basin
Authors: Junhui Li, Youzhi Wang, Xiandong Wang, Ce An, Shun He, Tingting Shen, Di Zhang, Xiaohui Li, Wencan He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn order to clarify the heterogeneity of organic sources in the Upper Triassic fifth member of the Xujiahe Formation (T3x5) on the northeastern margin of the Sichuan Basin, three typical wells (DS2, LQ1, PA3) were selected. Using petrology, organic geochemistry, biomarkers, kerogen maceral analysis, and rock pyrolysis, the sedimentary environment and organic matter (OM) sources were investigated, leading to a multi-source OM enrichment model. Results distinguish three sedimentary facies and corresponding OM assemblages. Well DS2 (east) deposited in delta plain swamp microfacies; well PA3 (south-central) in delta front and littoral-shallow lake transitional facies; well LQ1 (mid-west) in littoral-shallow lake facies. These facies control multiple OM sources (higher plants, cyanobacteria, algae). OM type is ultimately determined by a ternary coupling of depositional environment, biological assemblage, and preservation conditions. DS2 is dominated by vitrinite (93%), no sapropelinite, low HI—typical type III kerogen from higher plants. PA3 shows coexistence of sapropelinite (35–49%) and vitrinite (45–53%), average HI 111 mg/g (range 0.9–279.7), indicating mixed type II and III kerogen with local algae-rich intervals. LQ1 is enriched in sapropelinite (41–45%) and liptinite (8–11%), hydrogen-rich components > 50%, average HI 126.7 mg/g, representing stable type II kerogen. The enrichment model thus controlled by multiple organic sources and facies: in the eastern delta plain swamp, oxidation and degradation formed hydrogen-poor type III OM; in the central transitional environment, fluctuations produced mixed type II-III; in the mid-western lacustrine facies, a stable reducing environment promoted hydrogen-rich type II source rocks. The humid tropical-subtropical Late Triassic climate provided abundant biological sources, and the sedimentary facies—through proximity to provenance, redox conditions, and organisms—ultimately determined OM composition.Keywords: Sichuan Basin, Xujiahe Formation, Organic matter source, Enrichment modelSubject terms: Biogeochemistry, Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Ocean sciences, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

The Upper Triassic Xujiahe Formation in the Sichuan Basin is a crucial terrestrial shale gas exploration interval in China. Specifically, the fifth member of the Xujiahe Formation (T3x5), characterized by widely developed dark mudstone and shale, has become key target strata for shale gas exploration within the basin, particularly in the northeastern area1–3. The generation and enrichment of shale gas were primarily controlled by the characteristics of organic matter in source rocks4–9. The source (biological composition) of organic matter serves as its material basis. It directly determines the original organic matter abundance, type, and hydrocarbon generation potential10,11, while also profoundly influencing the petrophysical properties and pore evolution of shale12–14. Therefore, accurately characterizing the biological source composition of organic matter in the T3×5 shale is not only a core step in reconstructing its sedimentary paleoenvironment and revealing the organic matter enrichment mechanism, but also a fundamental geological basis for scientifically evaluating its shale gas resource potential and predicting the distribution of sweet spots.

In recent years, extensive geochemical research has been conducted on the organic matter sources of the T3x5 shale, yielding significant insights. Previous studies generally have indicated that the mudstones and shales of the T3×5 possessed relatively high total organic carbon (TOC) contents, classifying them as medium to good source rocks15–17. The organic matter type predominantly consists of Type II2–III (humic type, in this paper, Type II2 refers to mixed kerogen with hydrogen index (HI) between 100–300 mg/g), with vitrinite reflectance (Ro) values mostly ranging from 1.0 to 2.0%, indicating generally high to over-mature in gas generation stage18–20. The molecular geochemical indicators have provided key evidences on source identification: the predominance of C29 steranes among the regular steranes suggested significant contribution from terrestrial higher plants21. Moreover, relatively low tricyclic terpane contents and the presence of C27 and C28 sterane signals reflect mixed inputs from lower aquatic organisms (e.g., algae)22. Overall, these indicators have implied weakly oxidizing to oxidizing water column conditions, consistent with the setting of delta-lacustrine depositional system for the T3×523,24. The enrichment of organic matter appeared not primarily reliant on strongly reducing preservation conditions but was co-controlled by paleoproductivity and sedimentation rate. Specifically, the relatively high primary productivity and fast burial rate facilitated the effective preservation of organic matter25.

Despite these significant progresses, several key scientific questions regarding the in-depth analysis of organic matter sources in the T3×5 shale of the northeastern Sichuan area remain to be clarified. Firstly, the quantification and refinement of organic matter sources are insufficient. The precision regarding the relative contributions of different macerals (e.g., higher plants, alginite) and their spatial distribution differences has been insufficient, restricted the comprehensive understanding about the heterogeneity of high-quality source rocks26,27. Secondly, these studies still suffer from insufficient accuracy in characterizing the spatial heterogeneity of organic matter at the microfacies scale and on the multi-source coupling mechanism between specific sedimentary microfacies and different types of organic-generating organisms. How these factors specifically regulated the transport and input of terrestrial plant debris and the productivity of aquatic organisms, thereby influencing the preserved organic matter source assemblages, and their intrinsic relationships, remain unclear. Conventional methods, such as biomarker analysis, are often compromised in high‑maturity terrestrial shales (Ro ~ 1.0 to 2.0%) because thermal alteration can significantly degrade or rearrange diagnostic molecules (e.g., steranes, hopanes), obscuring original biological sources. These limitations call for an integrated approach combining organic geochemistry (n-alkane, Rock‑Eval, HI‑Tmax cross‑plot) with refined maceral analysis, which this study aims to establish. Consequently, an integrated evaluation method applicable to high-maturity terrestrial shales that simultaneously incorporates biomarkers, organic maceral analysis, and sedimentological constraints has not yet been established.

To solve these issues, this study selected 3 typical wells (DS2, LQ1, PA3) in the northeastern Sichuan area, focusing on the T3×5 shale. Based on the data of petrology, organic geochemistry, biomarker analysis, kerogen maceral examination, kerogen maceral analysis, and rock pyrolysis, the sedimentary facies and organic sources of the T3×5 in the study area have been identified. Section “Sedimentary environment characteristics of the T3×5” describes the sedimentary facies of the three wells; Section “Main types of hydrocarbon-generating organisms in the T3×5” identifies source organisms based on biomarkers and macerals; Section “Main sources of organic matter in typical wells of the T3×5” links sedimentary facies to maceral assemblages and hydrogen index data; Section “Conclusions” (Discussion) presents the organic matter enrichment model. On this basis, the multi-source organic matter enrichment model has been built under the control of sedimentary environment. This work could provide the theoretical basis for understanding the shale gas enrichment mechanisms in the T3×5.

Geological setting

The study area is located in the northeastern part of the Sichuan Basin, northwestern margin of the Yangtze Craton, (Fig. 1), where spans the transitional zone between the central Sichuan gentle structural belt and the eastern Sichuan high-steep fold belt. Since the Late Triassic, this area has undergone multi-stage tectonic superposition and reworking28, resulting in the current complex structural framework, which provided unique geological background for the preservation and evolution of the Xujiahe Formation shale29,30. The Upper Triassic succession in the northeastern Sichuan Basin is characterized by a multi‑provenance fluvio‑deltaic to lacustrine system. Previous paleogeographic and provenance studies have demonstrated that sediment supply during the Late Triassic originated from both the northern Qinling orogenic belt and the eastern Xuefeng uplift, leading to significant spatial variability in deltaic architecture31,32. This multi‑provenance setting provides the basis for our subsequent interpretation of contrasting microfacies assemblages. The Upper Triassic Xujiahe Formation is significant coal-bearing clastic rock sequence deposited during the transition from marine to continental environments following the Indosinian Movement in the Sichuan Basin33–35. The Xujiahe Formation has been traditionally divided into the first to sixth members from bottom to top. The T3×5 is widely developed in the northeastern Sichuan area, conformably or locally disconformably overlying the underlying the Forth Member and underlying the overlying Sixth Member. During the deposition of the T3×5, the tectonic setting of study area was relatively stable. The paleogeographic pattern of the northeastern Sichuan area was primarily controlled by the Dabashan-Micangshan provenance areas and the Xuefengshan Ancient Land (Fig. 1)36. The depositional system was characterized by large-scale delta interacting with littoral-shallow lake37–39. The lake basin was extensive during the deposited stage of the T3×5, making this interval one of the most developed source rock layers within this terrestrial sequence40. Its lithological association mainly consists of dark gray to grayish-black mudstones and shales, carbonaceous mudstones, interbedded with thin layers of siltstone, fine sandstone, and coal beds, collectively forming a fine-grained sedimentary sequence deposited under the lacustrine transgressive-highstand system tract (Fig. 2). Sedimentary environment analysis has indicated that during the middle to upper part of the T3×5, the lake basin had moderate water depth, brackish to freshwater salinity, and overall weakly oxidizing to weakly reducing sedimentary geochemical conditions. This setting favored the primary productivity of aquatic organisms while also receiving substantial quantities of terrestrial higher plant debris from surrounding uplifts, laying the foundation for the enrichment of mixed-source organic matter34,39.

Samples and methods

The samples used in this study were collected from Well LQ1, Well DS2, Well PA3, for organic geochemical data, kerogen extraction, biomarkers, rock-eval and organic maceral. The data of organic geochemical data were provided by Daqing Oilfield Company. The samples were selected for kerogen extraction by dissolving inorganic matter with acids (HCl and HF) and heavy liquid flotation. Extracted kerogens were identified using optical microscope. The samples were grinded into 100 mesh and were selected for the extraction of organic components with Soxhlet extraction for 72 h. The organic components extracted from samples were used for testing saturated hydrocarbons by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) using the equipment of Agilent 7890-5975c. The crushed samples of rock-eval were placed into a pyrolysis furnace and heated under inert gas atmosphere. During heating, the free hydrocarbons (S1) and pyrolyzed hydrocarbons (S2) contained in the sample volatilized and cracked sequentially, which could be detected and recorded by hydrogen flame ionization detector (FID). Then, the pyrolysis spectrum and key parameters (such as Tmax) were ultimately obtained. The kerogen extraction, biomarkers and rock eval were carried out in the State Key Laboratory of Biological Geology and Environmental Geology, China University of Geosciences (Wuhan). In the organic maceral analysis experiment, the samples were crushed to < 1 mm, embedded and cured with epoxy resin, polished to a mirror-like surface, and observed under the fluorescence microscope. Various macerals were identified under reflected light/fluorescence, whose volume percentages were calculated using the point counting method. The organic maceral experiment was carried out in Key Laboratory of Oil and Gas Geochemistry, Yangtze University.

A comparison between wells reveals distinct differences. LQ1 displays relatively high TOC values overall, ranging from 0.1 to 2.1%, with an average of 0.7%. DS2 shows intermediate TOC values, primarily distributed between 0.35 and 0.79%, averaging 0.51%. PA3 exhibits generally lower and highly dispersed TOC values, ranging from 0.02 to 1.25%, with an average of 0.44%.

Kerogen extraction and identification

Based on the kerogen extraction experiments and analytical methods described in the previous section, the types of microorganisms in samples from the T3×5 of Well P3 in the study area were identified under a microscope. The microorganisms could be generally classified into two categories: terrigenous higher plants (Fig. 3) and aquatic organisms (Figs. 4 and 5a, e and h). The aquatic organisms are predominantly cyanobacteria (Fig. 4) and dinoflagellates, with a minor presence of green algae (Fig. 5f and h).

Organic macerals

Vitrinite: grey to light grey reflectance, smooth surface, no or very weak fluorescence. Inertinite: high reflectance (white to yellowish white), often with cellular structure or high relief. Solid bitumen: medium grey reflectance, irregular shape (filling intergranular pores or fractures), isotropic, no fluorescence at high maturity (Ro > 1.0%). Sapropelinite (algal-derived): low reflectance (dark grey) under white light, strong yellow‑green fluorescence under UV (if not fully quenched by maturity). Liptinite (sporinite, cutinite): characteristic morphological forms (spores, cuticle) with moderate to strong fluorescence. Based on the kerogen maceral data, the organic matter types and compositions of the source rocks in the T3×5 exhibit significant inter-well variations. Samples from DS2 are overwhelmingly dominated by vitrinite (93%), with sapropelinite being absent, inertinite accounting for 6%, and liptinite comprising only 1% (Table 2; Fig. 6). Samples from LQ1 have a stable organic matter type classified as Type II2 (humic-sapropelic). They are characterized by relatively high sapropelinite content (41–45%) and liptinite content reaching 8–11%. The sum of these hydrogen-rich components exceeds 50%, while vitrinite and inertinite contents are moderate (Table 2; Fig. 6). Samples from Well PA-3 show variability in organic matter type determination, ranging between Type II2 and Type III (humic), indicating strong heterogeneity (Table 2; Fig. 6). Their common characteristics include relatively high sapropelinite content (35–49%) but extremely low liptinite content (1–4%), accompanied by significantly elevated vitrinite content (45–53%).

Rock pyrolysis

The hydrocarbon generation potential (S2) is generally low, ranging from 0.001 to 3.924 mg/g, but exhibits significant variations both between wells and within individual layers. Well LQ-1 demonstrates relatively good hydrocarbon generation potential, with some shale samples showing S2 values greater than 2.0 mg/g and reaching a maximum of 3.924 mg/g. S2 values for PA3 are mostly concentrated between 0.3 and 1.3 mg/g. In contrast, DS2 displays extremely low hydrocarbon generation potential, with the vast majority of samples having S2 values less than 0.01 mg/g (Table 1).

The Hydrogen Index (HI) shows an extremely wide distribution range (0.168–855.961 mg/g), influenced by the combined effects of organic matter type and thermal maturity. HI values for DS2 are generally below 100 mg/g. For LQ1, HI values are predominantly distributed between 100 and 300 mg/g TOC. Data from PA3 are more complex, exhibiting both numerous HI values between 50 and 150 mg/g and several anomalously high values (HI > 600 mg/g TOC) (Table 1).

Maturity parameters (Tmax) indicate distinct differences in thermal evolution across the study area: Tmax for DS2 is concentrated between 465 and 470 °C (with a minimum of 414 °C). Tmax values for LQ1 and PA3 are mainly distributed between 440 and 460 °C, placing them in the mature to high-mature stage; both wells also contain a few points with Tmax below 400 °C (Table 1).

Biomarkers

The distribution of n-alkanes in all samples from DS2 is highly consistent, characterized by the complete absence of low-carbon number components (nC10–nC16). The predominant carbon peaks are nC23 or nC24, displaying a distinct post-peak unimodal distribution with carbon numbers mainly concentrated in the nC17–nC36 range (Table 3; Fig. 7). They exhibit very low Pr/nC17 (0.41–0.68) and Ph/nC18 (0.16–0.51) ratios. Samples from PA3 show a complete distribution of n-alkanes, with abundant low-carbon number components (nC10–nC17). The main carbon peaks are typically nC11, nC12, or nC17, presenting a distinct pre-peak or bimodal distribution. The carbon number range is wide, but the abundance of the high-carbon number fraction (> nC25) drops sharply (Table 3; Fig. 7). The Pr/nC17 ratios are generally between 0.83 and 1.25, except for one sample (PA3-004) with a ratio as low as 0.03; Ph/nC18 ratios range from 0.38 to 1.54.

Discussion

Sedimentary environment characteristics of the T3×5

In the eastern part of the study area, represented by DS2 (Fig. 1), the lower part of the T3×5 was consist of delta front subfacies, including subaqueous interdistributary bay, distal bar, and subaqueous distributary channel deposits (Fig. 2a). The subaqueous interdistributary bay is mainly composed of mudstone intercalated with silty mudstone, exhibiting high gamma-ray values. Distal bars are characterized by siltstone interbedded with silty mudstone, showing medium-low serrated gamma-ray curves. Subaqueous distributary channels primarily developed medium to fine-grained sandstone, displaying box-shaped low-value gamma-ray curves. Its proximal position relative to the southeastern provenance, experienced higher sediment load and more confined flow, favoring the development of erosive subaqueous channels. The upper part comprises delta plain subfacies, developing swamp microfacies interbedded with natural levee microfacies. The swamp microfacies consists of mudstone intercalated with coal beds, characterized by serrated high-value gamma-ray curves. The natural levee microfacies is composed of siltstone, showing low-amplitude serrated gamma-ray curves (Fig. 2a).

In the south-central part of the study area, represented by PA3 (Fig. 1), the lower part was delta front subfacies, including subaqueous interdistributary bay, distal bar, and mouth bar deposits (Fig. 2b). The subaqueous interdistributary bay is mainly mudstone deposits, showing the stable high gamma-ray mudstone baseline, with intermittent low-amplitude peaks often representing thin crevasse splay siltstones. Distal bars primarily developed siltstone and argillaceous siltstone, displaying low-amplitude gamma-ray values. River mouth bars mainly developed fine-grained sandstone and siltstone, showing funnel-bell shaped composite medium-low gamma-ray values with gradual top transitions. The upper part comprised littoral-shallow lacustrine subfacies, with peat swamps interbedded with beach bars. The peat swamp, similar to LQ1, developed dark gray-black mudstone intercalated with coal streaks, exhibiting high gamma-ray values. The beach bar microfacies mainly consist of fine-grained sandstone and siltstone (Fig. 2b).

In the mid-western part of the study area, represented by LQ1, the T3×5 was primarily lacustrine environment, specifically the littoral-shallow lacustrine subfacies. It was generally characterized by interbedded peat swamps and beach bars. The peat swamp developed dark carbonaceous mudstone intercalated with coal beds, with rock colors ranging from dark gray to black, rich in plant debris and roots, showing overall high natural gamma-ray curve values with occasional anomalously high values. The beach bar microfacies mainly consist of fine-grained sandstone and siltstone, with relatively high lithic and feldspar content, displaying medium-low value box-shaped natural gamma-ray (GR) curves, forming composite rhythms in vertical sequences (Fig. 2c).

Main types of hydrocarbon-generating organisms in the T3×5

In the T3×5 of the northeastern Sichuan area, influenced by the transitional environment between a multi-source lake and a delta, the hydrocarbon-generating organisms are primarily terrigenous higher plants, also containing lower aquatic organisms. Kerogen microscopic examination and organic maceral analysis reveal that the hydrocarbon-generating organisms in the T3×5 of the study area are mainly higher plants, cyanobacteria, dinoflagellates, and green algae.

Higher Plants (Fig. 3): the plant fossils in the T3×5, affected by high thermal evolution, are not primarily complete body but exist in the form of plant fragments or humic amorphous matter. Fibrous structures can be observed in the plant fragments. Under the microscope, macerals such as vitrinite and inertinite are visible, with vitrinite being absolutely dominant. The liptinite group contains sporopollen and cutinite. The higher plants of the T3×5 likely belong to the fern-cycad flora under the tropical-subtropical coastal humid climate of the Late Triassic. High plants could evolve to vitrinite and inertinite.

Cyanobacteria (Fig. 4): the fossils mainly exist in the form of filamentous clusters, with multiple cells connected into chains. The cell shapes are mostly cylindrical or barrel-shaped. The chain-like fossils are generally several millimeters long, with diverse filament morphologies, which can be straight, curved, spiral, or irregularly coiled. Cyanobacteria could turn into sapropelite.

Dinoflagellates (Fig. 5a, e and h): they are preserved as corrosion-resistant cysts with diverse morphologies (spherical, ovoid, rhombic, etc.), often featuring horn-like or spine-like processes. The dorsal side of the cysts frequently exhibits regular openings formed by the loss of one or a group of plates. Their lengths are mostly between tens to hundreds of micrometers. Dinoflagellates could turn into sapropelite.

Green algae (Fig. 5f and g): they exhibit significant morphological differences, appearing as spherical, tubular, dendritic, foliose, etc. Some fossils show preliminary differentiation of structures resembling roots, stems, and leaves. Their sizes range from millimeters to centimeters. Green algae could turn into sapropelite.

Main sources of organic matter in typical wells of the T3×5

Rock pyrolysis data primarily determine organic matter type through parameters such as the Hydrogen Index (HI), pyrolysis peak temperature (Tmax), and S2. The core principle is based on the differences in hydrocarbon generation potential and chemical composition among different kerogen types: Type I (sapropelic) is hydrogen-rich, with HI typically > 600 mg/g and high oil generation potential; Type II (mixed) has HI ranging from 100 to 600 mg/g; Type III (humic) is hydrogen-poor, with HI < 100 mg/g, primarily gas-prone; HI decreases significantly in high-maturity samples, requiring Tmax to exclude maturity interference during application41,42.

In the DS2, the organic macerals are overwhelmingly dominated by vitrinite (93%), with complete absence of sapropelinite and only trace amounts of liptinite (1%) and inertinite (6%) (Table 2; Fig. 6). Rock pyrolysis data show extremely low HI values for this well (average 10.9 mg/g, < 100 mg/g), with Tmax reaching up to 460.4 °C (mainly concentrated between 465 and 470 °C), characteristic of typical Type III (humic). The hydrocarbon generation potential (S2) has been nearly exhausted, and the cross-plot of S2 versus TOC also indicates typical Type III kerogen (Fig. 8). This composition suggests that the hydrocarbon-generating organisms were almost entirely terrigenous higher plants. Biomarker characteristics further confirm this terrigenous attribute: n-alkanes exhibit distinct post-peak unimodal distribution, with the complete absence of low-carbon number components (nC10–nC16) and main carbon peaks at nC23 or nC24 (Fig. 7). The swamp environment on the delta plain is characterized by oxic to suboxic, acidic water conditions and abundant terrigenous higher plant inputs. The preferential preservation of lignin and cellulose, along with the lack of hydrogen-rich algal materials, leads to the formation of hydrogen-deficient Type III organic matter. Under the tropical-subtropical humid climate during the Late Triassic, the fern-cycad flora, after transport and deposition, was preserved in the delta plain swamp environment.

Cross-plot of rock pyrolysis parameters versus TOC for organic matter classification.

In the PA3, the organic matter type is the most complex, showing variations between Type II2 and Type III, with strong heterogeneity in maceral composition: sapropelinite content ranges from 35% to 49%, liptinite is extremely low (1%-4%), and vitrinite is significantly elevated (45%-53%) (Table 2; Fig. 6). This composition indicates multiple sources for the hydrocarbon-generating organisms, including both terrigenous higher plants and lower aquatic organisms, but lacking stable preservation conditions for liptinite. Rock pyrolysis data also show significant variation, with an average HI of 111 mg/g, ranging from 0.9 to 279.7 mg/g. The S2 versus TOC cross-plot also shows abundant Type III organic matter, Type II organic matter (Fig. 8). Biomarker characteristics of n-alkanes show pre-peak or bimodal distribution, with abundant low-carbon number components (nC10–nC17), main carbon peaks at nC11, nC12, or nC17, and sharp drop in abundance for the high-carbon number fraction (> nC25) (Fig. 7). The coastal shallow lake environment receives both terrigenous and aquatic organic inputs under relatively dysoxic conditions. The moderate water depth and fine-grained sediments promote the preservation of a mixed assemblage, yielding Type II organic matter with intermediate hydrogen index. In the delta front environment, the organic matter originates from both aquatic cyanobacteria and algae as well as input from terrestrial higher plants.

In the LQ1, the organic matter type is mainly Type II2. The maceral composition shows relatively high sapropelinite content (41%-45%), liptinite content reaching 8%-11%, hydrogen-rich components (sapropelinite + liptinite) exceeding 50%, and moderate vitrinite and inertinite contents (27-44%) (Table 2; Fig. 6). This composition also reveals dual sources for the hydrocarbon-generating organisms: input from terrigenous higher plants as well as abundant lower aquatic organisms, with greater proportion of aquatic organisms compared to PA3. Rock pyrolysis HI values are mainly distributed between 100 and 300 mg/g, with an average of 126.7 mg/g. Tmax averages 415.2 °C (mainly 350–460 °C). Some shale samples show S2 > 2.0 mg/g, reaching a maximum of 3.92 mg/g. The S2 versus TOC cross-plot indicates Type II organic matter, with several individual samples reaching Type I (Fig. 8). The relatively deep, stratified water column in the shallow to semi-deep lake environment favors the accumulation and preservation of hydrogen-rich algal organic matter (dinoflagellates, green algae) under anoxic conditions, which directly accounts for the formation of Type II₁ organic matter with high HI values. Combined with the lacustrine sedimentary environment, it has reveal the increased proportion of aquatic organism sources for organic matter in this well, while still containing a certain amount of higher plant input, indicating weakly reducing to reducing sedimentary environment. However, direct geochemical evidence is not yet available to confirm this interpretation. Accordingly, this conclusion should be regarded as tentative and subject to further verification.

Multi-source organic matter enrichment model

The Pr/nC17 and Ph/nC18 ratios are commonly used as the parameters to indicate organic matter source and depositional environment.43,44 Pristane (Pr) and phytane (Ph) were derived from the phytol side chain of chlorophyll. Under oxidizing condition, phytol could be readily converted to pristane, whereas under reducing conditions, it would tend to form phytane. Therefore, lower Pr/nC17 ratios typically indicate reducing environments, while higher values reflect oxidizing conditions. The n-alkanes (nC17, nC18) are directly derived from biological lipids, therefore higher aquatic primary productivity can lead to lower Pr/nC17 and Ph/nC18 ratios.

The DS2 exhibits relatively low Pr/nC17 (0.41–0.68) and Ph/nC18 (0.16–0.51) ratios, reflecting weakly oxidizing depositional environment (Fig. 9). It indicates that the delta plain swamp environment where was located had shallow and turbulent water, unfavorable for the survival and preservation of lower aquatic organisms. It also subjected the input terrestrial plant organic matter to significant oxidative degradation. As a result, only hydrogen-poor lignocellulosic tissues (vitrinite, inertinite) were preserved, forming pure Type III (humic) kerogen (Fig. 10).

The Pr/nC17 (0.03–1.25) and Ph/nC18 (0.38–1.54) ratios of PA3 show significant variation, reflecting frequent fluctuations in the depositional environment from oxidizing to reducing conditions. It allowed for the mixing of terrestrial plant fragments with lower aquatic organisms (cyanobacteria, dinoflagellates) brought in by water (Fig. 9). However, the turbulent conditions were unfavorable for the stable and sustained preservation of organic matter, leading to the damage of hydrogen-rich components such as liptinite, resulted in the maceral composition characterized by high sapropelinite and vitrinite but extremely low liptinite. The organic matter type fluctuates dramatically between Type II and Type III. The anomalous biomarker characteristics (complete low-carbon number distribution, extremely high HI values) may also indicate locally increased contributions from aquatic organisms, potentially forming algae-rich intervals (Fig. 10).

The littoral-shallow lacustrine environment represented by LQ1, provided a relatively stable, consistently reducing environment. The quiet water not only promoted the flourishing of lower aquatic organisms but also reduced the oxidative decomposition of terrestrial organic matter. This allowed hydrogen-rich sapropelinite and liptinite to be effectively enriched and mixed with terrestrial vitrinite, thereby forming stable Type II kerogen with a high proportion (> 50%) of hydrogen-rich components (Fig. 10).

Integrating the data of sedimentary facies, hydrocarbon-generating organisms, and organic geochemistry, the original organic matter in the study area was derived from both abundant terrestrial vegetation and flourishing lower organisms in the water column, under the humid climate of the Late Triassic. The ultimate source and type of organic matter were controlled by the distance from provenance and the type of sedimentary facies. Specifically, transitioning from the proximal delta plain in the east (DS2), through the delta front transitional zone in the south-central area (PA3), to the lake center in the mid-west (LQ1), with increasing water depth, weakening hydrodynamic conditions, and strengthening reducing conditions, the organic matter source gradually shifted from being dominated solely by terrestrial higher plants to mixture of terrestrial plants and aquatic organisms (cyanobacteria, dinoflagellates, etc.) (Fig. 10). Ultimately, the overall contribution of aquatic organisms increased significantly in the lacustrine facies.

Conclusions

The sedimentary environment of the Tx5 in the study area exhibits distinct facies, transitioning from the delta plain swamp microfacies in the east (DS2), through the delta front-shallow lacustrine transitional facies in the south-central area (PA3), to the littoral-shallow lacustrine facies in the mid-west (LQ1). The delta-lacustrine sedimentary framework directly controlled the multi-source composition of organic matter, including higher plants, cyanobacteria, and algae (dinoflagellates and green algae).
