Membrane remodeling by the collective action of caveolin-1
Authors: Korbinian Liebl, Gregory A Voth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCaveolin-1 proteins scaffold 50-100 nm large invaginations in the plasma membrane to mediate critical cellular processes. As revealed recently by cryo-electron microscopy, several caveolin-1 protomers can fold into a disk-like structure that embeds in the cytoplasmic leaflet. This 8S complex represents a basal component to drive membrane curvature via higher-order interactions. The biophysical mechanisms behind the membrane remodeling, however, are elusive. To address this shortcoming, we develop a bottom-up coarse-grained model to overcome the substantial computational limitations for this large system. During simulations with the coarse-grained model, the complexes increasingly coordinate as partially mediated by attractive electrostatic interactions between scaffolding domains. The coordination of complexes strongly correlates with membrane protrusion, as approaching complexes amplify localized stress in the exoplasmic leaflet. Thus, proximity of two CAV1-8S complexes induces dynamic curvature generation that can facilitate access for signaling partners. This mechanism is further explored through simulations of clusters of multiple CAV1-8S complexes that form large-scale membrane invaginations, suggesting that caveolin-mediated membrane remodeling arises collectively from the coordinated action of multiple complexes rather than from isolated complexes.Subject terms: Computational biophysics, Membrane biophysics, Biophysical chemistry, Molecular modelling

Introduction

Many cellular functions require remodeling of the membrane shape or composition. This behavior is strikingly manifested in the biogenesis of caveolae, where caveolin proteins, together with other cofactors (e.g., cavin), generate 50–100 nm large plasma membrane invaginations and mediate various cellular processes, including mechanoprotection, lipid homeostasis, and endocytosis1–8. Dysregulation of caveolin-1 is hence implicated in several diseases such as cardiovascular hypertrophy, cancer, lipodystrophy and diabetes5,9–13.

Caveolae assembly and function have been characterized with high-resolution imaging techniques as well as lipid-binding and membrane tension assays, showing that caveolin-1 (CAV1) proteins cluster cholesterol at the plasma membrane and drive membrane curvature even in the absence of cavin14–21. Binding by cavin, however, further stabilizes and compacts the initial caveolae architecture20,22,23. These assemblies fulfill versatile and contrasting roles, regulated by the concentration of EHD2 and other recruited components: Caveolae assemblies act as buffers to membrane tension but can also bud off from the plasma membrane, a critical step in endocytosis8,17,20,22,24.

Despite the extensive characterization of CAV1 function, the biophysical basis underlying these processes has largely remained elusive. In particular, the initial flat-to-curved transition of the plasma membrane induced by CAV1 is still an enigma25 that we address in this study. The understanding of caveolae is thus still in its infancy, mainly because its structural basis has been determined only recently. Using cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), it has been resolved that eleven CAV1 protomers assemble into a disc-like structure (~14 nm diameter) with a central beta-barrel formed by the C-termini of the protomers26–28. The hydrophobic profile of this CAV1-8S complex suggests a monotopic arrangement (embedding only in the cytoplasmic leaflet) in which the beta-barrel remains solvent-exposed (Fig. 1A). This leaflet replacement model is also supported by webserver predictions, Langmuir film-balance measurements and experiments that showed that the CAV1-8S complex incorporates into lipid vesicles only upon removal of phospholipids from the outer leaflet29,30. To get a better understanding of the lipid-CAV1-8S interactions, various groups have studied this system with both all-atom (AA) and coarse-grained (CG) molecular dynamics (MD) simulations29–33. All-atom MD simulations indicate that the CAV1-8S complex extracts cholesterol from the lipid membrane and accommodates it in its beta-barrel, whereas cholesterol accumulation at the distal leaflet is likely enhanced by post-translational modifications30. Moreover, these AA MD simulations indicate that a single CAV1-8S complex induces only slight, local bending in the exoplasmic leaflet, hence suggesting a collective action that involves several complexes to remodel the membrane shape29,30. Atomistic simulations of such large-scale processes, however, reach the limits of current state-of-the-art computational hardware, whereas top-down CG models such as Martini can induce nonphysical membrane curvature for this system30.

In this work, we develop a bottom-up coarse-grained (CG) model for the CAV1-8S complex embedded in a heterogeneous lipid bilayer to overcome these substantial limitations. Importantly, our CG model is also transferable to (self-assembling) lipid-bilayer systems without CAV1 and is solvent-free. Thus, it is scalable beyond 100 nm and allows us to track diffusion of the complexes on similar length-scales.

Simulations with this CG model permit us to monitor the coordination of the complexes. By leveraging Bayesian machine learning techniques, we also show that membrane protrusion and curvature arise due to the collective action of multiple CAV1-8S complexes, not from a single one. Based on the statistics sampled in the CG simulations, we infer and propose a mechanism for curvature generation by the CAV1-8S complexes: For approaching complexes, the local height deformations in the exoplasmic leaflet interact. We interpret this as a membrane-mediated sensing-and-amplification mechanism, which causes pronounced elevation in the interstitial space and tilting of the CAV1-8S disks in the near-distance regime. Furthermore, we suggest that caveolae assembly may be promoted by the distinctive electrostatic profiles of the CAV1 scaffolding domains, characterized by four particular amino acids. The sequence of CAV1 thus not only encodes self-assembly into the 8S-oligomers, but also specific higher-order interactions that drive clustering of the CAV1-8S complexes.

In addition, the CG-model enables us to sample formation of ~70 nm broad invaginations in the membrane at CAV1-cluster sites and hence to obtain molecular insight into this initial scaffolding step of caveolae biogenesis. Recorded energy profiles show a minimum at intermediate invaginations that are consistent with NPT-simulations, and further protrusion leads to a reduction in the high-l modes, indicating a loss of polyhedral symmetry. Our simulations, hence, also suggest that tighter, more restricted invaginations need additional cofactors to sustain an ordered symmetric pattern of the CAV1-8S-induced invaginations.

Results

Dynamic membrane remodeling and coordination of CAV1-8S complexes

Utilizing bottom-up techniques, we have trained a coarse-grained model that captures the monotopic and hence highly asymmetric embedding of the CAV1-8S complex in the heterogeneous lipid bilayer (70/30% POPC/CHOL, see Supporting Information Movie S1). Note that our CG model is significantly coarser than commonly used top-down CG-models such as Martini, especially since it is solvent-free (Fig. S1). We emphasize that solvation effects are not neglected but intrinsically incorporated in our CG interactions that represent potentials of mean force between CG sites. Moreover, the lipid parameterization accurately describes self-assembly, bending modulus, distribution functions of symmetric lipid bilayers (Movie S2, Figs. S2–S4) and captures cholesterol flip-flops. For these reasons, our CG-model is transferable and highly scalable, enabling the systematic simulation of membrane systems larger than 100 nm that include multiple CAV1-8S complexes. Thus, we harness the power of this model to study membrane remodeling on scales larger than even the typical size of caveolae assemblies.

As a first step, we have simulated 12 CAV1-8S complexes embedded in a large ~125 x 125 nm² membrane bilayer (Fig. 1, Movie S3). During the simulation, the protein complexes show substantial diffusion along the cytoplasmic leaflet and increasingly interact with each other (Figs. 1B–D, S5). Intriguingly, distinct complexes can approach each other and partition the membrane into CAV1-rich and CAV1-depleted regions. Based on the recorded statistics, we investigated the relationship between CAV1-density and membrane topology. To obtain a smooth, continuous representation of the sampled membrane structures, we interpolated the membrane heights z* across a dense grid in the xy-plane via Gaussian Process Regression (GPR). Figure 1E, F and Fig. S6 show representative, interpolated height profiles of the cytoplasmic leaflet. Domains of strong protrusion (dark blue) appear in the proximity of multiple CAV1-8S complexes, whereas sinks (light color) occur at CAV1-depleted regions. In this way, the CG simulation displays the characteristic behavior of CAV1 sensing or inducing membrane invaginations. We quantified this relationship by computing CAV1-densities (unitless sum of Gaussian kernels) for all points of the GPR-derived membrane surfaces (Figs. 1G, S7). This approach reveals a nonlinear trend: For small CAV1 densities, we obtain a strong positive correlation to the elevation of the membrane. Proximity to a single CAV1-8S complex thus causes membrane protrusion. The correlation attenuates for larger CAV1 densities up to ~1.0, followed by a sudden increase. Larger invaginations (>100A¨, Fig. 1G) hence appear at CAV1-densities of at least ~1.25, showing that the action of more than one complex is required.

By overcoming major limitations of previous computational approaches regarding length-scale and accuracy, our results hence support the idea that membrane remodeling during caveolae assembly is driven by the collective action of several caveolin-complexes. This view is in line with theoretical models and atomistic simulations30,34. We further stress this view based on mean curvature profiles computed via derivatives of z* w.r.t. x*,y* (see “Methods” section). Note that typical procedures to quantify membrane bending rely on discretization techniques, whereas our GPR-based protocol facilitates more granular profiles (Figs. 1H, I, S8). While the interpolated mean curvature may amplify noise in the derivatives, we notice that the profiles exhibit distinguished contour lines of positive mean curvature (purple and orange dots in Fig. 1H, I) covering CAV1-rich domains in consistency with the height profiles.

We highlight as well that the curvature profiles hence describe convex invaginations, i.e., bending into the cytoplasm by the CAV1-8S complexes, that are fully consistent with the process of caveolae budding. As illustrated in Figs. 1H, I, S8, these invaginations can stretch over more than 50 nm, which is in the range of experimentally characterized caveolae assemblies4,35 and reveal long-ranged interactions between CAV1-rich domains. Furthermore, these invaginations are highly dynamic as they adjust to the diffusion of the CAV1-8S complexes. This dynamics, however, is not merely stochastic but shows a pronounced preference for coordination between different complexes (Fig. 1D) that is correlated to the bending of the membrane (Fig. S9), a relationship that we further examine in the following section.

Mechanism behind membrane remodeling by CAV1-8S complexes

To elucidate the mechanistic basis of collective membrane remodeling mediated by CAV1-8S complexes, we tracked the interactions between distinct complexes throughout the simulation. As shown in Fig. 2A, diffusion can drive distant complexes into proximity that allows them to bind to each other. Intriguingly, the bound state between two complexes roughlyclassifiedbyintercomplex−distances≤15nm shows a high degree of stability and is characterized by an edge-to-edge arrangement (as the center-of-mass distances correspond to twice the radius of the CAV1-disks, ~14 nm).

Furthermore, we have observed that the complexes are not rigidly embedded in the xy-plane during our simulations, but undergo orientational fluctuations, hereafter denoted as tilting, which quantifies the angle between the z-axis and direction of the beta-barrel (Fig. S10). In this way, the complexes adjust to their environment (e.g., membrane undulations), a mechanism that is particularly leveraged when two complexes approach each other: Upon binding, the convex tilting between complexes increases significantly (Fig. 2B, distances around ~15 nm). Such mechanistic coupling means the emergence of a curved domain in the membrane, as the complexes are no longer in planar orientation.

Based on our CG MD simulations, we argue that the binding of different complexes is promoted by the electrostatic profile of the CAV1-8S complex. We noticed a nuanced distribution of charges characterized by an alternating 3-to-1 composition of positive and negative charges. As a consequence, distinct complexes can rotationally align to form electrostatically attractive interactions (Fig. 2C). This CG charge distribution can be projected back to the atomistic structure, so that we can identify four amino acids attributable to the binding of two CAV1-8S complexes: D82, K86, K96 and R101. In our simulations, we find a particular preference for D82-R101 but also notice frequent D82-K96 interactions (Fig. 2D). We emphasize that D82, K86 and R101 show an exposed configuration (in the atomistic structure, Fig. S11) that may allow for direct contacts by other protein complexes. Intriguingly, these residues are part of the scaffolding domain of caveolin-1, which is disease-associated and involved in numerous signaling pathways28,36. For these reasons, the scaffolding domain is hypothesized to undergo conformational changes to directly mediate protein-protein interactions - an idea supported by our simulations and consistent with single-molecule localization microscopy data15,36. It is thus very plausible that K96 becomes accessible upon protein-protein contacts, too, especially since high-throughput screening experiments have revealed a critical role of K96 in eNOS binding37. Similarly, the neighboring F92 residue - which is also oriented inward—is known to mediate interactions with binding partners38.

These results imply that the sequence of caveolin-1 may have been functionally optimized not only to encode folding into the 8S oligomers, but also higher-order interactions essential for caveolae formation.

By analyzing the relationship between lipid heights and CAV1-8S configurations, the simulations also allow us to infer a mechanism for curvature generation by caveolin-1 (schematically shown in Fig. 3A): Consistent with previous atomistic simulation studies29,30,32,33, isolated CAV1-8S complexes bend the membrane only locally, with maximal protrusion of the exoplasmic leaflet at the center beneath the beta barrels (highlighted in light blue in Fig. 3A). Upon approaching each other, the height profiles of the complexes superpose, resulting in an elevation of the interstitial space. This amplification mechanism is quantified in Fig. 3B that shows a significant height-difference between interstitial and appositional lipids in the near-distance/binding regime, which results in a curved and locally elevated domain where the two complexes are tilted (Fig. 3A, C). This process is depicted by structural snapshots in Fig. 3D, E.

Qualitatively, this cooperative term may arise because interstitial amplifications cause pressure differences between the binding and opposing flanks of the CAV1-8S complexes. This pressure difference is interrelated to convex tilting of the rigid complexes (Fig. 3A, C). As shown in the SI Section 3, the analytical description of the height profiles yields tilting estimates in good agreement with the simulation data. Since the outlined mechanism for curvature generation can propagate across neighboring complexes, it offers an explanation for how they collectively drive larger membrane invaginations.

Formation of large-scale invaginations

While the aforementioned simulation has given insight into the collective and dynamic functioning of caveolin-1, the methodology is slightly limited by two factors: First, the constant box size may suppress stronger membrane curvature. Second, simulating the transition from isolated systems to an organized cluster is an inherent sampling issue even for CG modeling. The insights discussed above, however, allow us to design a simulation setup that addresses these issues.

Note that the binding pose shown in Fig. 2C occupies approximately two of the eleven edges of one CAV1-8S complex. Thus, we suggest that one complex can be coordinated by six others, and we then started simulations from a structure arranged in this way (Fig. 4A). In a first simulation of this system, we continuously compressed x- and y-dimensions to screen the energy landscape for membrane curvature generation and hence to sample formation of ~70 nm large membrane invaginations scaffolded by the CAV1-cluster (Fig. S13, Movies S4, S5). This setup can be seen as a computational analog to experiments (e.g., optical stretching or magnetic tweezers) that probe the buffering of membrane tension by caveolae assemblies20,39.

The screened energy profile shows a minimum at intermediate curvature, but the system remains relatively flexible with respect to further compression/bending (Figs. 4B, S14). Intriguingly, curvature formation induces gradients in cholesterol concentration across the membrane. While we observe homogeneous levels of Chol/POPC ratio for flat membranes, growing invaginations lead to continuous increases of cholesterol concentration at the curved neck regions around the cluster, at the expense of bound cholesterol within the cluster region (Fig. S15). Thus, our coarse-grained simulations show dynamic cholesterol partitioning to minimize energetic costs for large-scale membrane bending. Furthermore, the shape of the invagination undergoes distinct changes as quantified with a spherical harmonic analysis (see “Methods”). Initial budding shows systematic increases in the symmetric coefficients (Fig. 4C), after reaching the energy-minimum, however, the symmetric modes begin to attenuate, and small or asymmetric modes dominate (Figs. 4C, S16). We note that cryo-electron tomography experiments have revealed polygonal shapes for the caveolae vesicles4,14,40. Since the minimum of the energy profile coincides with the maximum of the l = 12 mode (Fig. 4B, C), our CG simulations suggest that CAV1-8S complexes collectively scaffold the membrane to deformations with a noticeable polyhedral symmetry. Additional cofactors may then be needed to preserve the symmetry for further restriction of the invagination. We also emphasize that our membrane system maintains structural integrity even for large deformations characterized by hemispherical-like subdomains (Fig. S17), which demonstrates the robustness of the CG model also for more curved bilayer structures.

The CG simulation also substantiates our previously discussed mechanistic coupling between membrane bending and tilting of the CAV1-8S complexes (Fig. 5). Formation of the ~70 nm large invagination correlates with increased tilting of the complexes, especially for those at the contour of the invagination that can tilt by more than 50° (Fig. S18). Thus, our simulation shows the formation of a caveolae-bud with very similar characteristics to hypothesized models for caveolae vesicles that suggest that CAV1-8S complexes are embedded in flat, local membrane surfaces of different orientations, forming a polyhedral shape4,27. Note that the formation of CAV1-mediated membrane invaginations was also confirmed in an additional simulation of five complexes, where we have observed clustering of three complexes that localizes the membrane stress upon compression (Figs. S19–21). Moreover, compression of a pure lipid-bilayer shows an energy minimum at higher box-dimensions, which further demonstrates that the CAV1-8S complexes are responsible for scaffolding the sampled invaginations (Fig. S22). In addition, we have performed pressure-coupled (NPT) simulations. While the representation of coarse-grained pressure is not unambiguous and hence requires careful interpretation41, simulations at zero lateral reference pressure (p∣∣=pxx=pyy=0) also corroborate the compression simulations: When starting from a configuration close to the screened energy minimum, fluctuations of the box-dimensions as well as invagination size remain close to the initial values (green curve in Fig. 6A, B). Starting from the extended configuration, however, results in rapid box-shrinking (Fig. 6A) and converges closely to trajectories started from the energy minimum. This behavior is in excellent agreement with the energy-landscape screened in the compression simulations, but means larger convergence times (as shown by slow invagination growth in Fig. 6B). We therefore initiated NPT simulations at different lateral pressures from the configuration at the energy minimum. As shown in Fig. 6C, the box dimension decreases with increasing lateral pressure, hence also modulating the invagination size (Fig. 6D). For larger negative lateral pressure (corresponding to an increase in applied surface tension), the size of the invagination is reduced, which demonstrates the buffering of membrane tension by the CAV1-8S complexes. Consistent with previous simulations, reducing the box size by positive lateral pressure results in larger invaginations at the site of the CAV1-8S cluster.

Discussion

Caveolin-1 proteins mediate substantial remodeling of the membrane which can be critical to many cellular processes. The mechanistic basis for their functioning has remained elusive, since experiments offer only limited resolution, and all-atom MD simulations only limited length-scales. Previous atomistic simulation studies have therefore largely focused on simulations of a single CAV1-8S complex embedded in one leaflet of a lipid bilayer. These simulations show only very localized deformations of the membrane shape29,30, hence suggesting that the flat-to-curved transition in caveolae biogenesis must be orchestrated collectively by numerous complexes.

CG simulations, on the other hand, have the potential to provide for both high resolution and scalability if done as rigorously as possible. Top-down CG models, however, appear to cause membrane curvature at odds with all-atom simulations for such a monotopic system. For this reason, we have trained a bottom-up CG model that captures the insertion of the CAV1-8S complex as well as relevant membrane properties. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first bottom-up model for such a system. We therefore emphasize that our protocol may be extended to a broader class of protein-membrane systems, e.g., peripheral or transmembrane proteins.

In our CG MD simulations, we have observed significant diffusion of the individual CAV1-8S complexes with a strong tendency for clustering. Leveraging Gaussian Process Regression to densely interpolate the membrane surface, we find a nonlinear correlation between CAV1-occupancy and membrane height, that can result in ~ 50 nm broad membrane invaginations.

In that case, collective action between the complexes is presumably also enhanced by electrostatic interactions, as we noted that in the correct rotational alignment the complexes may attract each other. This attraction can be attributed to four amino acids in the scaffolding domain, D82, K86, K96 and R101. Attractive electrostatic interactions between scaffolding domains of two 8S complexes is a testable hypothesis. Note that interactions between different complexes are described in a top-down approach in our CG model, but metastable pairing was consistently obtained for different dielectric constants. Nevertheless, this robustness should not be interpreted as a quantitative validation of the CAV1-CAV1 interactions, and our low-resolution CG model may miss specific microscopic interactions between scaffolding domains. We therefore deem the electrostatically mediated inter-complex contacts as a more phenomenological hypothesis. Future atomistic studies directed to his problem are therefore of great interest to elucidate possible interactions between CAV1-8S complexes on a molecular structural level. We argue, however, that these interactions are structurally very plausible, as three of these charged amino acids are in place to form salt bridges. It is also fully in line with network analysis of single molecule localization microscopy and biochemical fractionation experiments that captured dimerization of 8S complexes (referred to as S1B scaffolds)14–16,42. Dimers of CAV1-8S then likely drive lattice formation into the polyhedral 70S complex. Strikingly, the F92A and V94A mutations in the scaffold domain have been shown to maintain competence of caveolae assembly but have a pronounced impact on the size of the scaffolds (including S1B)15. In addition to these hydrophobic mutants, mutation of R101 has also been related to disease, and mutagenesis screening experiments have identified K86 and K96 as critical determinants in the binding of eNOS28,37.

In addition, our CG simulations suggest that proximate complexes show coupled, membrane-mediated rearrangements: The height deformations in the exoplasmic leaflet interact with each other causing an elevation in the interstitial space that leads to convex tilting of the CAV1-8S complexes. We interpret this as a sensing-and-amplification mechanism that originates from the exoplasmic leaflet and can be leveraged through orientational adaption by the CAV1-8S complexes. Recently, Barnoy et al. have proposed that differential interaction energies between lipid-lipid and lipid-CAV1 interactions at the outer rims of the CAV1-8S complexes drive curvature formation34. Their 2d-model assumes a constant energy difference for the complexes that are described as homogeneous disks. In fact, however, the CAV1-8S complex is a heterogeneous, toroidal structure including also a central beta barrel. In agreement with other simulation studies29,30,32,33,43, the mechanism proposed by us suggests that isolated CAV1-8S complexes induce localized curvature of the exoplasmic leaflet, with maximal protrusion near the center of the complexes (beneath the beta barrel). This local curvature has also been observed in recent cryo-EM experiments43, and is likely shaped by multiple features, including membrane insertion depth of the complex, the adaptability to a funnel-like conformation43–45 and the central beta barrel. The study by Connolly et al. further suggests that absence of local curvature, as observed in evolutionary distinct caveolins, also eradicates the capability for large-scale remodeling43. This aligns with our work, in which we propose that larger, collective curvature emerges from constructive interference of these local deformations. We emphasize that our model and data represent a direct biophysical explanation and partial structural evidence of the dynamic curvature mechanism proposed by Lim and coworkers36. Such dynamic curvature generation facilitates access by further cofactors and has therefore been hypothesized to modulate signaling pathways such as eNOS36.

Compressing the lateral-dimensions of the membrane, triggers these mechanistic couplings and therefore forms ~70 nm large invaginations at the center of the CAV1-cluster. We find an energy minimum for intermediate invaginations that are characterized by maxima in the high l-modes obtained from a spherical harmonics analysis, suggesting that additional cofactors are needed to further restrict the invagination and maintain the polyhedral symmetry. These results are further supported by semi-isotropically pressure coupled simulations that exhibit a clear correlation between applied lateral pressure and invagination size. Thus, our CG simulations show that assembled CAV1-8S complexes self-organize to scaffold initial membrane invaginations that can buffer membrane tension—in agreement with high-resolution imaging techniques and fragility assays14,16,20.

In the future, it will be of value to include cavin-trimers and PIP2 lipids (that bind to the HR1 domain of cavin46,47) in our CG model. Such simulations are of particular interest and are likely to extend the biophysical and molecular view of caveolae assembly from this paper, as cofactors may also sense the electrostatic profile of the CAV1-8S complex. Based on the presented success to develop bottom-up large-scale models for a protein-membrane system, we deem it possible and highly valuable to extend and generalize our coarse-grained force field to a broader range of lipid types and inclusion of other proteins. Note that this, however, would require additional training, as our CAV1-lipid parameters are not simply transferable to other protein-lipid interactions. The development and application of asymmetric membrane models represents another intriguing extension that would open the way to systematically study the impact of membrane composition on the mechanics of membrane invagination. Related to this, a valuable future effort will be to transfer the solvent-free protein-membrane CG model to vesicular systems. Such an initiative will require development of robust structure and topology generation protocols together with validation, as highly coarse-grained bottom-up models can exhibit increased sensitivity to membrane composition and initial conditions. While this effort lies beyond the scope of the presented work, it represents a natural next step to customize this type of CG model for protein membrane simulations.

Methods

MD simulations

Atomistic MD simulations were performed with the Gromacs/2020 package for two different systems, a CAV-1 8S complex embedded in a lipid membrane with 70/30% POPC/CHOL composition, and a pure membrane system of same composition48. Resulting trajectories were used to train the CG models. For the CAV-1 system, we used the output structure from a previously published Metadynamics simulation as starting structure30. This system was simulated in the constant NVT-ensemble without any bias for ~ 80 ns, using otherwise the same simulation-setting as in the Metadynamics simulation. Note that this system assumes standard physiological protonation states of the amino acids of the CAV1-8S complex. The membrane system was generated with CHARMM-GUI adjusting x- and y-dimensions of 40 nm and an extension in z-direction of 11 nm49. The Charmm36m force field was employed to describe interactions between the atoms of the systems50. Energy minimization was carried out for 5000 steps using the steepest descent method. The membrane system was then equilibrated following the CHARMM-GUI generated input-files51: First, the system was simulated at a reference temperature of 310 K employing a Berendsen thermostat with a coupling constant of 1 ps52. This equilibration was performed in the NVT-ensemble for 250,000 time steps. Subsequently, a semi-isotropic Berendsen barostat was applied with a reference pressure of 1 bar, a compressibility of 4.5⋅10−5bar−1 and a coupling constant of 5 ps52. The first 375,000 steps of the entire equilibration were performed with a time-step of 1 fs, and the remaining 750,000 steps with 2 fs. Throughout equilibration, conventional restraints were used and gradually reduced. The production run was performed for ~50 ns in the NVT-ensemble using a Nose-Hoover thermostat53,54.

Coarse-grained modeling

Mapping rules for the lipid and caveolin-1 molecules were derived based on the essential-dynamics coarse-graining (ED-CG) method using the OpenMSCG package55,56. In this procedure, one groups atomistic particles to coarse-grained beads by minimizing a residual for atomistic fluctuations in the subspace spanned by principal components:

with △rit=rit−rit evaluated in the PCA subspace.

Each cholesterol and POPC lipid molecule was mapped to three and six CG beads, respectively (Fig. S1). For the CAV1-8S complex, identical mapping was performed for each monomer, resulting in 30 CG beads per caveolin-1, and hence 330 beads per CAV1-8S complex (Fig. S1). This corresponds to a resolution of 4–5 amino acids per bead. The CAV1-8S disk was then modeled as a hetero-elastic network model (hENM). In this method, the spring constants between CAV1 CG beads are iteratively updated to reproduce atomistic fluctuations. We parameterized the intra-protein interactions with OpenMSCG using a cutoff of 30 Angstrom55,57. The CG potentials for bonds and angles of the lipids were derived with force matching as tabulated (4th-order B-spline interpolated) potentials58,59.

Non-bonded interactions were trained iteratively through the relative entropy minimization (REM) technique60. In the first step, we trained non-bonded lipid-lipid and lipid-protein interactions as tabulated 4th-order B-spline interactions. To increase stability of the protein-lipid interactions, we have replaced the tabulated protein-lipid interactions by training truncated Gaussian potentials in the second step of training. In consecutive steps, we have trained perturbations in the form of 9-6 Lennard-Jones and truncated Gaussians. The objectives for these perturbations are to make the CG model transferable also to a pure membrane system and to more accurately capture the bending modulus. Additionally, excluded volume interactions were added between protein and lipid beads to increase repulsion in the marginally sampled short-range distances. A detailed overview of the training is given in Table S1 of the SI. As shown in Fig. S2, Movie S1, REM training of the protein-membrane interactions results in a correct monotopic binding mode of the CAV1-complex, and our procedure also facilitates self-assembly of the lipid membrane (Movie S2) and captures the bending modulus in quantitative agreement with experiments (κb=29.6kBT,Fig. S3). Given the large system size, the parameterization of non-bonded interactions between CAV1-8S complexes from atomistic simulation data is computationally too expensive. We therefore describe these interactions in a top-down approach based on repulsive excluded volume and electrostatic interactions. Note that CG models typically employ a uniform effective dielectric constant. The non-polarizable MARTINI models, for instance, use ε=15 as a compromise between hydrophobic regions and bulk water61–63. Bulk water conditions, however, are not as relevant for our system. Moreover, since we employ a coarse mapping, where each bead represents the center-of-masses of 4–5 amino acids, we consider short-range interactions to occur in a protein-like dielectric environment which motivates a lower ε (for larger distances, interactions are effectively screened by κ). For a model protein, Patargias et al. have estimated local dielectric constants in the range of ~2–964. Since our CG model lacks attractive interactions between distinct CAV1-8S complexes, we use a reduced effective dielectric constant of ε=2 with κ=0.1274Å−1 to compensate for missing attractive hydrophobic and polar interactions as well as for strong repulsion (to prevent steric overlap) in the CG model. To test the sensitivity of this inter-protein description, we have performed additional simulations at ε=4 and ε=8 as well as reduced repulsion settings (see Fig. S23 and next section). These simulations show frequent edge-to-edge binding also for higher dielectric constants. Moreover, distributions of the distances between oppositely charged beads of complexes in close vicinity remain within a physically plausible range, since they do not show population of states that would mean steric overlap in an atomistic representation, and demonstrate robustness toward higher ε (Fig. S24). In Eq. (6), the constant C converts to units of energy. Following previous studies65,66, the charges of the protein beads Qb and Qc were assigned as sum of the partial charges of all atoms mapping to the corresponding CG bead. For protein-protein interactions, the repulsion coefficients Ab,c were set in a range from 0 to 18.75 kcal/mol, depending on charges and radial distance from the center of the complex. Analogously, Rbc0 was set in a range from 0.0–12.0Å. For protein-lipid interactions, Hbc was trained via REM based on σbc=2.0Å and Rbceq as mean-distance (measured in the atomistic reference simulation), with exclusions specified by Abc=25kcal/mol and Rbc0 at the transition between repulsion and attraction. Refinements of the lipid-lipid interactions were trained with a σbc corresponding to the width and Rbceq to the position of the first peak in the radial-distribution function, constraining ∣δHbc∣<0.15kcal/molÅ and ∣δϵbc∣<2⋅10−4kcal/mol. Apart from the hENM-interactions, a cutoff of 25.0 Å has been used in all CG simulations.

CG system initialization and simulation

All CG simulations were performed with the LAMMPS simulation package (version 21st Nov 2023)67. The topology-files that also contain the starting configuration were built with in-house developed python-scripts. Following recent suggestions68, REM-training was started from disordered structures which were generated with packmol (version 2014)69.

Large-scale NVT CG simulations were performed for three different systems with different numbers of CAV1-8S complexes: 12, 19 and 5 (termed 12CAV, 19CAV and 5CAV in the following). The x- and y- dimensions for the three systems were 126.4 nm, 187.6 nm and 113.4 nm, respectively. Magnitudes of the z-dimensions are of lesser significance, since the models are solvent-free, and were set to 200.0, 200.0 and 50.0 nm, respectively. Periodic boundary conditions were applied.

The CG systems were minimized for 1000 steps with the conjugate gradient method and afterward simulated at T = 310 K using a Langevin-thermostat with a coupling time-constant of 10 ps67. Neighbor lists were updated every step, and the systems were simulated in the constant NVT-ensemble, except the compression-simulations. Coordinates were written out every 12,500 steps (12CAV) or 25,000 steps (19CAV, 5CAV). An integration time-step of 15 fs was employed. The systems were simulated for ~120⋅106,14.8⋅106,19.3⋅106 (12CAV, 19CAV, 5CAV) CG steps. The compression rates for the 19CAV and 5CAV systems were ~1.0and0.3nm/(106CG steps), respectively. We performed three additional replicates for the 12CAV system starting from different velocities for 65⋅106 CG steps (Fig. S5, S7). Similarly, two additional compression-simulations were performed for the 19CAV systems (Fig. S14). A control simulation was performed using the same initial box-size and compression rate for a pure lipid-bilayer system (Fig. S22). Two semi-isotropically pressure coupled simulations with p∣∣=pxx=pyy=0 and uncoupled z-dimension were performed for the 19CAV system using the Berendsen barostat52. One simulation was initialized from the same extended structure as the compressing simulations, and one from a structure at the energy-minimum (~181.9 nm). Four additional simulations were initialized from the energy-minimum with lateral reference pressures of −0.05, −0.01, 0.01 and 0.05 atm. The NPT simulations were performed for at least 12⋅106CG steps using relaxation constants of 1 ps and 5 ps for the thermostat and barostat, respectively.

A randomized lipid structure was simulated for 14.7⋅106 steps in the NVT ensemble (with x- and y-dimensions of 31.6 nm) at 310 K. The last 0.7⋅106 steps (after reassembly) were used to estimate the bending modulus.

Three additional simulations were started from the final structure of the 12CAV simulation. The three simulations employed higher dielectric constants and one of them reduced repulsion parameters. These simulations were performed for 30⋅106 CG steps under otherwise identical conditions.

The trajectories were processed with the MDAnalysis library (version 2.9.0) for subsequent analysis70. Normal vectors on the disks were computed as vector between center of masses of beads with type 20 and beads of type 28 (direction of the beta barrel). Angles between the normal vectors represent the tilt angles. For the generation of Fig. 2B, sampled distances between complexes and their corresponding tilt values were categorized into bins. Plotted data show the averages of the distance and corresponding tilt bins, and error bars the standard deviations. Analogous averaging was also carried out for other statistical analyses. Structural snapshots were generated with VMD (version 1.4.9)71.
