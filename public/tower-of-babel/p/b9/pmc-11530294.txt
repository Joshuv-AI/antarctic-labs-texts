DEWNA: dynamic entropy weight network analysis and its application to the DNA-binding proteome in A549 cells with cisplatin-induced damage
Authors: Shisheng Wang, Wenjuan Zeng, Yin Yang, Jingqiu Cheng, Dan Liu, Hao Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCisplatin is one of the most commonly used chemotherapy drugs for treating solid tumors. As a genotoxic agent, cisplatin binds to DNA and forms platinum-DNA adducts that cause DNA damage and activate a series of signaling pathways mediated by various DNA-binding proteins (DBPs), ultimately leading to cell death. Therefore, DBPs play crucial roles in the cellular response to cisplatin and in determining cell fate. However, systematic studies of DBPs responding to cisplatin damage and their temporal dynamics are still lacking. To address this, we developed a novel and user-friendly stand-alone software, DEWNA, designed for dynamic entropy weight network analysis to reveal the dynamic changes of DBPs and their functions. DEWNA utilizes the entropy weight method, multiscale embedded gene co-expression network analysis and generalized reporter score-based analysis to process time-course proteome expression data, helping scientists identify protein hubs and pathway entropy profiles during disease progression. We applied DEWNA to a dataset of DBPs from A549 cells responding to cisplatin-induced damage across 8 time points, with data generated by data-independent acquisition mass spectrometry (DIA-MS). The results demonstrate that DEWNA can effectively identify protein hubs and associated pathways that are significantly altered in response to cisplatin-induced DNA damage, and offer a comprehensive view of how different pathways interact and respond dynamically over time to cisplatin treatment. Notably, we observed the dynamic activation of distinct DNA repair pathways and cell death mechanisms during the drug treatment time course, providing new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the cellular response to DNA damage.

Introduction

Cisplatin is widely recognized as one of the most effective chemotherapy drugs used in the clinical treatment of various solid tumors [1–3]. Its efficacy stems from the ability to bind directly to DNA, forming platinum-DNA adducts that induce significant DNA damage [4, 5]. This damage initiates a cascade of cellular responses, including the activation of various DNA-binding proteins (DBPs), which play essential roles in mediating signal pathways that ultimately lead to cell death [6, 7]. Despite the critical importance of DBPs in the cellular response to cisplatin, there is a notable lack of systematic studies exploring the temporal dynamics of these proteins in response to cisplatin-induced DNA damage. Understanding the dynamic behavior of DBPs is crucial for elucidating the molecular mechanisms by which cisplatin exerts the anti-tumor effect [8]. Such insights could aid in the development of more effective therapeutic strategies and improve the precision of existing treatments [9, 10]. However, the complexity of temporal protein expression data poses significant challenges for traditional analytical methods, necessitating the development of advanced computational tools.

Traditional proteomic approaches typically provide static snapshots of protein expression (e.g., ProteoSign [11], prolfqua [12], ProStaR [13], MixProTool [14]), which are insufficient to capture the dynamic changes and complex interactions that occur over time in response to DNA damage [15, 16]. Other tools such as CoexpressionNetwork [17], Qcut [18] and DNB [19] enable dynamic network entropy analysis and gene co-expression network analysis, which have proven effective in identifying potential biomarkers and functional co-expressed gene modules associated with complex human diseases [20]. Nonetheless, these methods may fall short in integrating protein temporal expression changes with pathway-level information. DNEA [21] discretizes gene expression data into bins during dynamic network entropy analysis, which may result in information loss by oversimplifying continuous gene expression values. This binning process involves arbitrary selection of bin boundaries, and the sensitivity to bin size can compromise the accuracy and robustness of the analysis results. Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) [22] often requires critical prior information, such as numerical thresholds of power for defining interaction, which can introduce biases. SoBiAl [20] relies on unweighted networks defined by hard thresholds, which can lead to the inclusion of numerous false positive interactions, further complicating the accurate representation of protein interactions. These challenges highlight the need for more sophisticated analytical tools that can accurately reflect the continuous nature of protein expression and the complex, dynamic interactions within biological systems.

To address these limitations, we developed DEWNA (Dynamic Entropy Weight Network Analysis), a novel and user-friendly stand-alone software designed to facilitate the dynamic analysis of proteins and their associated functions. DEWNA implements the entropy weight method, a weighting model that assigns weights to indices based on the degree of value dispersion [23]. This approach evaluates the perturbation of clusters or pathways by examining the expression profiles of proteins within these groups. The rationale behind this method is that proteins exhibiting greater variability (or entropy) in expression under treatment conditions have a more substantial influence on pathway perturbations, leading to higher weight assignments. Conversely, proteins with lower variability are assigned lesser weights [23]. This approach enables the generation of entropy-weighted expression matrices for subsequent analyses. Moreover, DEWNA integrates the co-expression network analysis to reveal meaningful multi-scale organizations of co-expressed protein network and identifies novel therapeutic targets [24]. For pathway analysis, DEWNA employs the generalized reporter score-based analysis (GRSA) to assess the enriched pathways based on the dynamic weighted protein expression matrices [25]. By combining these approaches, DEWNA provides a comprehensive framework for identifying protein hubs and elucidating entropy-weighted profiles of clusters and pathways during disease progression. In this study, we demonstrated the utility of DEWNA by applying it to a dataset of DNA-binding proteins from A549 cells exposed to cisplatin over eight time points, revealing key insights into the dynamic proteomic landscape during drug treatment.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Cisplatin, dithiothreitol (DTT), iodoacetamide (IAM), formic acid (FA), and ammonium bicarbonate (ABC) were obtained from Sigma (St.Louis, MO, United States). Acetonitrile (ACN) was purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Sequencing-grade trypsin was obtained from Enzyme and Spectrum (Beijing, China). Deionized water was prepared using a Milli-Q system (Millipore, Bedford, MA, United States). All other chemicals and reagents of the best available grade were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich or Thermo Fisher Scientific.

Cell culture and drug treatment

Human non-small cell lung cancer cell line A549, were obtained from KeyGen Biotech (Nanjing, China) with the authentication by short tandem repeat detection. A549 cells were cultured in F-12 K (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 100 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin (Hyclone) at 5% CO2 in a humidified incubator at 37 °C. Cisplatin was dissolved in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) to prepare a 1.0 mM stock solution. When reaching 80% confluence, A549 cells were treated with the culture medium containing 30 μM cisplatin for 3, 6, 9, 12, 18, 24, and 36 h in four replicates, respectively.

Protein extraction

DBPs crosslinking and extraction was conducted by referring to the protocol of chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) [26, 27]. Specifically, the culture medium was removed and A549 cells were treated with PBS containing 1% formaldehyde for 20 min at 37 °C to crosslink DNA and DBPs. The crosslinking reaction was quenched by adding glycine at a final concentration of 125 mM and then incubating for 5 min at room temperature. The supernatant was discarded, and cells were washed once with PBS, harvested using a scraper, centrifuged at 3000 g for 5 min, and washed twice with PBS. Collected cells were lysed by 500 μL of 4% (w/v) sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) for 10 min at room temperature, and then mixed with 1.5 mL of UA buffer (8 M urea, 0.1 M Tris, pH 8.5). Cell lysate was centrifuged at 16,000 g for 30 min and the pellet was washed twice with water for 5 min each, followed by the digestion by 50 μg RNase A for 1 h at 37 °C to degrade RNA-protein complexes. Afterwards, the mixture was centrifuged to obtain DNA-protein crosslinks which were washed twice with 4% SDS and water, and subsequently dispersed in water. After being sonicated for 15 min at 4 °C to fragment DNA, the DNA-protein crosslinks were incubated for 30 min at 95 °C to de-crosslink DNA and DBPs. The protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA) kit (Beyotime Biotech., Shanghai, China), and stored at −80 °C.

Protein preparation for MS analysis

DBPs were prepared in four biological replicates for each drug-treatment time point from the cell culture step, and the untreated cells were used as negative controls. The proteins were processed for MS analysis following a modified filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) protocol [28]. Briefly, 100 μg of proteins were loaded onto a 30-kDa filter (PALL, USA) and centrifuged at 13,000 g for 15 min. After being reduced by DTT (20 mM) for 4 h at 37 °C and alkylated with IAM (50 mM) for 30 min at 25 °C in the dark, the protein mixture was washed twice with 200 μL of UA buffer and three times with 200 μL of 50 mM ABC by centrifugation at 13,000 g for 15 min at room temperature. Next, 200 μL of 50 mM ABC containing 2.0 μg of trypsin was added to each sample and incubated for 16 h at 37 °C. Finally, the filter tubes were washed three times with 100 μL of water by centrifugation at 13,000 g for 10 min. The peptide mixtures were dried under vacuum and then stored at −80 °C.

LC–MS/MS analysis

All samples were detected by LC–MS/MS in the DIA mode using an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer coupled with an EASY-nLC 1200 UPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) [29]. Specifically, peptides were dissolved in water containing 0.1% FA, added with the synthesized iRT peptide standards (KI-3002-2, Biognosys), and separated on a column (ReproSil-Pur C18-AQ, 1.9 μm, 75 μm × 25 cm; Dr Maisch) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min over an 82-min gradient (solvent A, 0.1% FA in water; solvent B, 0.1% FA in 80% ACN; 0–4 min, 2–6% B; 4–64 min, 6–26% B; 64–75 min, 26–38% B; 75–76 min, 38–95% B; and 76–82 min, 95% B). In the DIA mode, each cycle consisted of one full MS scan followed by 42 isolation windows ranging from 350 to 1500 (m/z) at variable isolation widths [25]. MS parameters for peptide detection were set as follows. (1) MS1: orbitrap resolution = 60,000; scan range (m/z) = 350–1500; RF lens = 40%; AGC target = 4.0E5; maximum injection time = 50 ms. (2) MS2: high-energy collision dissociation (HCD) energy = 30%; detector type = orbitrap; orbitrap resolution = 15,000; scan range (m/z) = 150–2000; RF lens = 40%; AGC target = 3.0E5; maximum injection time = 22 ms.

Proteomics data processing

For the identification and quantification of DBPs, MS raw data files were searched against the SwissProt database (human; version 2020_08; 20,368 entries) with Spectronaut software (version 15; Biognosys, Switzerland) using the direct DIA analysis mode [30]. The iRT peptide standards were used for the retention time calibration. Trypsin were set as the proteolytic enzymes, and two missed cleavage sites were allowed for protein digestion. Cysteine carbamidomethylation (+ 57.02 Da) was set as the fixed modification. Methionine oxidation (+ 15.99 Da) and protein N-terminal acetylation (+ 42.01 Da) were used as variable modifications. False discovery rates (FDRs) of the peptide spectrum matches, peptides, and protein groups were set to <1%.

Software implementation

DEWNA is a web-based platform that built in R (Version 4.3.1, https://www.r-project.org/) [27], and the GUI was implemented using R Shiny framework (version 1.6.0, https://github.com/rstudio/shiny) [28]. The online version was deployed on an in-house sever with an Ubuntu Linux system according to the Shiny server professional administrator’s guide and can be accessed freely without any login requirement. It is platform independent and is fully compatible with many common browsers (Google Chrome, Mozilla Firefox, Safari, etc.). Furthermore, users can also operate this tool locally by a simple command ‘DEWNA::DEWNA_app()’ in R after installation (Supplementary Notes). The source codes of DEWNA are available in the GitHub repository: https://github.com/wangshisheng/DEWNA under an MIT license supporting for local installation. Detailed operations about DEWNA are shown in the Supplementary Notes.

Modularization

The uploaded data containing a protein-by-sample matrix at the entrance portal for analysis in DEWNA can be xlsx, xls, csv, and txt formats, which are easily output with Spectronaut software or other similar tools. By default, the median normalization was implemented using the median intensity of every sample. Those proteins with missing value (NA) ratio above 0.5 will be removed and the NAs were processed with the k-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) algorithm [31]. Subsequently, the pre-processed data were modularized based on one clustering method or one function database (e.g., every pathway in KEGG [32]). The clustering analysis was implemented using the kmeans function in the stats packge [33]. The pathway information (e.g., protein ids, descriptions) was extracted using the clusterProfiler package [34].

Other bioinformatic analysis.

Protein co-expression networks and hubs were detected by the MEGENA package [24]. The heatmaps were visualized by the pheatmap package [35]. KEGG enrichment analysis was performed by clusterProfiler package [34]. GRSA was implemented using the ReporterScore package [25].

The overall wokflow of DEWNA. There are several main steps in the whole analysis process: 1. Data upload. In this step, users should upload the protein intensity matrix, in which the rows are protein IDs (i.e., UniProt IDs), the columns are samples. 2. Data pre-processing. Based on the user’s choice, those proteins with excessively high proportion of NA and large coefficient of variation (CV) can be discarded in this step. Missing values are imputed with the KNN method and median normalization was performed by default. 3. Modularization. The pre-processed data are segmented into some clusters using the kmeans method or categories based on one function database (e.g., KEGG). 4. Entropy value calculation of each cluster or category. This step consists of five parts: (i) calculate the proportion of each protein (Pij), zij is the normalized expression level of the protein j in sample i, m is the total sample number. (ii) calculate the entropy of every protein (Ej). (iii) calculate the information utility value of every protein (Uj). (iv) calculate the weight of each protein (Wj). (v) Calculate the activity of a cluster or category based on the expressions and weights of all proteins within it. 5. Results. Here shows the main results obtained from this tool, including the entropy weight matrix, the cluster activity value matrix and relative heatmap plot, the protein hubs networks and functions, the pathway activity value matrix and relative heatmap plot, the network of enriched pathways. All results and figures in all above steps can be downloaded for users in the format of csv or pdf.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Workflow for dynamic proteomic analysis using DEWNA.

The workflow of DEWNA, as illustrated in Fig. 1, encompasses six primary stages designed to facilitate comprehensive and dynamic analysis of proteomic data (details provided in Supplementary Notes): 1. Uploading of proteomics data. Users are required to upload an original intensity data matrix, formatted with protein names or identifiers in rows and sample names in columns. This data matrix can be obtained directly from various common software platforms, including Spectronaut [30], MaxQuant [36] and Proteome Discoverer [37]. During this step, users must also input the correct sample information to ensure accurate analysis (Supplementary Notes 1). 2. Data pre-processing. In this step, users have the option to discard proteins that have an excessively high proportion of missing values (NAs) or a large coefficient of variation (CV). This ensures that the data used for analysis is of high quality and reliability. Missing values in the dataset are imputed using the k-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) method, which estimates missing values based on the similarity between neighboring data points [31]. Additionally, median normalization is performed by default to standardize the data, making it more comparable across samples. 3. Modularization. The pre-processed data are segmented into clusters using the k-means clustering method or categorized based on a functional database, such as KEGG [32, 33]. Clustering organizes the data into groups of similar proteins, while categorization aligns the data with known biological pathways and functions, facilitating a more focused analysis. 4. Entropy weighted value calculation of each cluster or category. This step is multifaceted and involves several key calculations (Methods part): (i) Proportion calculation. Calculating the proportion of each protein (Pij). (ii) Entropy calculation. Determining the entropy of every protein (Ej), which measures the degree of disorder or variability in protein expression. (iii) Information utility calculation. Calculating the information utility value of each protein (Uj), which reflects the importance of each protein based on its entropy. (iv) Weight calculation. Assigning a weight to each protein (Wj) based on its information utility value. (v) Activity calculation. The activity of a cluster or category means the entropy-weighted profiles calculated by combining the expressions and weights of all proteins within it. This provides a quantitative measure of the cluster or category’s overall activity and its changes over time. 5. Presentation of results. The results generated from DEWNA include: (i) Entropy weighted expression matrix, which provides insights into the variability of protein expression. (ii) Cluster activity value matrix and heatmap plot, which visualizes the activity levels of different clusters. (iii) Protein hubs networks and functions, which elucidates key regulatory nodes within the co-expression networks. (iv) Pathway activity value matrix and heatmap plot, which depicts the dynamic changes in pathway activities. (v) Network of enriched pathways, whcih highlights the interconnectedness and functional relationships between different pathways. All result tables and figures generated throughout the analysis process can be freely downloaded, ensuring that users have full access to the detailed insights and visualizations produced by DEWNA.

This comprehensive workflow ensures that DEWNA provides a robust and detailed analysis of proteomic data, enabling the identification of critical proteins and pathways that respond dynamically to treatments such as cisplatin-induced damage in A549 cells.

Identification of dynamic protein clusters and key hubs in cisplatin-treated A549 cells.

We applied DEWNA to the analysis of DNA-binding proteome in A549 cells with cisplatin-induced damage across 8 time points (0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 18, 24, and 36 h, Supplementary Table S1). We firstly aimed at identifying dynamic entropy weighted expression changes of protein clusters and then uncovering key protein hubs and pathways involved in the cellular response to DNA damage. The heatmap (Fig. 2A) illustrated the activity profiling of different clusters over the 8 time points (Supplementary Table S2). Samples were ordered sequentially by the treatment time, allowing for the visualization of temporal trends in cluster activity. This representation highlighted how specific protein clusters respond dynamically to cisplatin treatment. Notably, several clusters showed interesting changes in activity. For example, some clusters (Clusters 2, 5, 21, 24, 27, 35, 38, 46, and 57) as highlighted in the green box in Fig. 2A exihibited gradual increase in activity, indicating their potential roles in the cellular response mechanisms. The boxplots (Fig. 2B) demonstrated a clear trend of increasing activity over time, with clusters showing a gradual upward trend corresponding to the extended duration of cisplatin exposure. Next, we explored the protein hubs and pathways from the selected clusters. The network highlighted key proteins (red points) that exhibit high connectivity within the clusters, acting as hubs in the protein interaction landscape (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Table S3). The identification of these hubs provided insights into the regulatory mechanisms at play and identifies potential targets for therapeutic intervention. The enrichment analysis reveals several significantly enriched pathways, highlighting the biological processes and molecular functions most affected by cisplatin treatment (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Table S4), such as cell cycle, chemical carcinogenesis, p53 signaling pathway, DNA replication. The involvement of these pathways underscores the complexity of the cellular response to cisplatin and the critical roles played by the identified protein hubs. Specifically, the cell cycle pathway is modulated as cisplatin-induced DNA damage triggers cell cycle arrest involving key proteins like cyclins and CDKs, thereby allowing time for DNA repair or apoptosis [38]. Chemical carcinogenesis pathway highlights the mutagenic potential of cisplatin, implying the need for monitoring long-term risks. The p53 signaling pathway underscores the tumor suppressor’s role in regulating DNA repair and apoptosis in response to damage, aligning with its function as the genome’s guardian [39, 40]. Additionally, DNA replication disruption by cisplatin-DNA adducts emphasizes the drug’s efficacy in blocking cancer cell proliferation while also posing risks of replication stress and genomic instability [41]. Understanding these enriched pathways and the roles of central protein hubs like CDKs, JUN, and HSPA1A provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms driving the cellular response to cisplatin.

Temporal patterns in pathway activity revealing cisplatin-induced cellular responses.

The overall changes in pathway activities in A549 cells treated with cisplatin can be categorized into four distinct trends (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table S5): 1. Pathways exhibiting high activity at the first four time points, followed by a decrease in the middle three time points, and an increase at the last time point. 2. Pathways with higher activity in the first four time points, which then decrease in activity over the last four time points. 3. Pathways showing relatively low activity in the initial time points, with a gradual increase in activity over the last time points. 4. Pathways with no regular pattern of change in the first seven time points, but exhibiting a significant increase in activity at the last time point. These patterns indicate that the influence of cisplatin on cellular pathways is multifaceted and temporal, with each pathway responding differently over time. Further examination of these pathways provided deeper insights. Metabolism pathways predominantly followed the third trend (Fig. 3B), suggesting that the intracellular metabolic processes become increasingly disrupted and chaotic. This progressive metabolic dysregulation underscores the increasing stress and damage within the cells. Genetic Information Processing pathways mostly displayed the third trend (Fig. 3C). This trend indicates a gradual onset of genetic information disorder, becoming more pronounced with prolonged cisplatin exposure. Particularly, since DNA repair is a key player in removing cisplatin-induced DNA lesion, we focused on the dynamic changes of distinct DNA repair pathways during the drug treatment time course. Nucleotide excision repair (NER), mismatch repair (MMR), homologous recombination (HR), and fanconi anemia pathway (FA) have been reported to function in repairing cisplatin-induced DNA damage [42]. We found that FA and NER were activated mainly during 12 to 18 h. Accordingly, RNA polymerase and transcription factors involved in NER were also active in the meantime. In contrast, MMR and HR were enhanced during 24 to 36 h. The differential activation of DNA repair pathways over time may indicate the time-dependent disparity in the forms of cisplatin-induced DNA lesion within cells. Generally, NER is involved in the repair of intrastrand cisplatin-DNA adducts, while FA plays a role in interstrand crosslink (ICL) repair. HR is required for the repair of double-strand breaks (DSBs). MMR can recognize DNA lesion caused by cisplatin that induces postreplicative mispairing through translesion synthesis, but it is unable to repair the lesion and will start this process repeatedly, leading to a so-called futile cycle that eventually results in the formation of DSBs and the activation of apoptosis [43]. Based on these findings, we assume that cisplatin forms intrastrand and interstrand crosslinks with DNA in the early stage, which recuit relevant NER and FA proteins, whereas DSBs arising from ICL repair and MMR futile cycle become prominent in later period. Moreover, the activated MMR pathway may enhance cisplatin lethality as MMR proteins mediate apoptosis induced by DNA damage and also shield cisplatin adducts from NER removal [44]. Additionally, base excision repair was shown to be activated during 18 to 36 h, which is found to mediate cisplatin cytotoxicity but the underlying mechanism remains unclear [43]. Environmental Information Processing pathways primarily followed the second trend (Fig. 3D). This pattern indicates a diminishing response to growth factors and other external stimuli over time, reflecting a decline in cellular signaling and adaptive responses as the damage from cisplatin accumulates. The reduced responsiveness could lead to impaired cellular communication and function, contributing to the overall cytotoxic effects of the treatment. Pathways involved in Cellular Processes were split between the second and third trends (Fig. 3E). Half of these pathways, including autophagy, mitophagy, and apoptosis, showed initial activation (second trend), indicating that programmed cell death and survival mechanisms are triggered early in the cisplatin treatment. This early activation likely represents the attempt to manage and mitigate the damage in cells. As time progresses, pathways like ferroptosis, necroptosis, cell cycle regulation, and p53 signaling pathway followed the third trend, suggesting a shift towards other cell death pathways and profound disruptions in cell cycle control. This shows the dynamic changes and complex interplay between cell survival and death. In addition, the involvement of p53 pathway highlights the role of this tumor suppressor in mediating cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in response to DNA damage. Overall, the diverse trends in pathway activities underscore the complex and dynamic nature of cellular response to cisplatin. The unique temporal pattern of each pathway reflects its specific role and mechanism of action in response to drug-induce DNA damage, revealing potential targets for enhancing therapeutic efficacy and minimizing adverse effects.

Dynamic pathway activation and suppression in response to cisplatin-DNA damage.

Dynamic entropy-weighted pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using DEWNA, which integrates GRSA to assess the pathway enrichment for dynamic entropy-weighted protein expression matrices (Supplementary Table S6). The results of this pathway enrichment analysis are depicted in Fig. 4A, and they align closely with the pathway activity trends observed in Fig. 3. For instance, pathways associated with crucial cellular processes such as cell cycle, DNA replication, base excision repair, and mismatch repair exhibited a significant and increasing enrichment pattern over the 8 time points. This increasing trend indicates a progressive activation of these pathways as the cells respond to cisplatin-induced damage. Conversely, other pathways, including autophagy, JAK–STAT signaling pathway, and cAMP signaling pathway, showed a significant decrease in enrichment over the same period. The decreasing enrichment patterns of these pathways suggest a downregulation or suppression of these processes due to cisplatin-DNA damage. DEWNA further enhances the analysis by enabling users to visualize networks of pathways that are either increasing or decreasing in activity. Figures 4B and4C illustrate these networks, providing a comprehensive view of how different pathways interact and respond dynamically over time to cisplatin treatment. This network visualization is particularly valuable for identifying key regulatory nodes and understanding the broader context of pathway interactions. Additionally, DEWNA offers the capability to export z-scores of all proteins involved in the enriched pathways (Supplementary Table S7). These z-scores provide a quantitative measure of protein enrichment or depletion across the entire time course, offering deeper insights into the dynamic changes in protein expression. As shown in Fig. 4D, this feature helps researchers discern which proteins are most significantly affected by cisplatin treatment, allowing for a more targeted investigation of the molecular mechanisms at play. The ability to track protein expression changes in such detail aids in identifying potential biomarkers of cisplatin-DNA damage response and in understanding the underlying biological processes driving the cellular response to the drug. The dynamic and detailed analysis provided by DEWNA, encompassing pathway enrichment trends, network visualizations, and protein-specific data, presents a powerful approach for studying the complex cellular responses to cisplatin-induced DNA damage. It highlights the importance of both upregulated and downregulated pathways and offers a holistic view of the molecular alterations induced by the treatment. This comprehensive analysis not only corroborates previous findings but also expands our understanding of the intricate biological networks involved [45, 46], paving the way for future research aimed at enhancing cisplatin efficacy and reducing its side effects.

Limitations

Despite the advancements provided by DEWNA in analyzing time-course proteomic data, several limitations remain. One primary concern is the inherent complexity and variability of biological systems, which can introduce noise and affect the accuracy and reproducibility of results. While DEWNA offers systematic analysis of protein interactions and pathway activities, it may not fully capture the multifaceted nature of cellular responses to DNA damage. Integrating multi-omics data could provide a more comprehensive understanding but introduces additional challenges. Additionally, interpreting DEWNA’s results requires some biological and computational knowledge, necessitating careful validation through experimental verification and literature cross-referencing. The effectiveness of DEWNA relies on the availability of high-quality, comprehensive datasets, and suboptimal data quality or quantity may limit its broader applicability. Furthermore, while DEWNA is accessible as both a web application and stand-alone software, performance can be influenced by internet speed and local computational capabilities. Addressing these limitations through methodological improvements, resource accessibility, and comprehensive guidelines will be essential to enhance DEWNA’s utility in studying the dynamic behavior of complex diseases.

Conclusions

Overall, we described a new tool, DEWNA, for elucidating the dynamic changes of DBPs in response to cisplatin-induced DNA damage in A549 cells, providing profound insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying cisplatin’s cytotoxic effects. The diverse activity trends of clusters/pathways over time underscore the temporal complexity of cellular responses to cisplatin. Our findings, enabled by DEWNA’s comprehensive analysis, not only corroborate previous insights but also expand our knowledge of the intricate biological networks affected by chemotherapy drugs. The user-friendly web-based platform of DEWNA ensures broad accessibility and facilitates a deeper understanding of the complex interactions within biological systems. We anticipate that DEWNA could greatly facilitate multi-omics studies, especially proteomic research, in addressing the dynamic responses of biological systems to various treatments and the dynamic alteration of pathways during disease progression, thereby possessing a great potential in unraveling key regulators and relative functions in complex biological processes.
