The State of Posttransplant Malignancies in Vascularized Composite Allotransplantation
Authors: Alexis K. Gursky, Allison N. Horn, Hailey P. Wyatt, Sachin R. Chinta, Alay R. Shah, Bruce E. Gelb, Daniel J. Ceradini, Eduardo D. Rodriguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Vascularized composite allotransplantation (VCA) restores complex defects when conventional reconstruction is not feasible. However, lifelong immunosuppression introduces substantial risks, including posttransplant malignancies. This study aims to characterize the incidence, timing, management, and outcomes of posttransplant malignancies in VCA recipients to inform future surveillance and management guidelines.Methods:A systematic review was conducted according to PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) 2020 guidelines using PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane. Eligible studies included patients who underwent VCA and developed a subsequent malignancy. Collected data included demographics, transplant and malignancy details, and survival.Results:Fifteen studies comprising 15 unique VCA recipients were included. Transplants involved the face (37.5%), upper extremity (37.5%), scalp (12.5%), lower extremity (6.25%), and abdominal wall (6.25%). Median time to first malignancy was 1.89 years posttransplant. Across all cases, 24 malignancies were reported, most commonly nonmelanoma skin cancer (60%) and posttransplant lymphoproliferative disorder (33%). Notably, 85% of skin cancers developed at nonallograft sites. Overall survival was 53.3%. All patients with skin cancer alone survived, whereas posttransplant lymphoproliferative disorder carried a 50% mortality rate. Median time from malignancy to death was 5.5 years, and from transplant to death, 10.1 years.Conclusions:Recipients of VCA demonstrate increased malignancy risk, particularly nonmelanoma skin cancers and lymphoma, with a typical onset of 2 years posttransplant. Dermatologic surveillance focusing on native skin, Epstein–Barr virus viral load monitoring in high-risk recipients, and regular follow-up are essential and may aid early detection.

Takeaways

Question: What are the incidence, management, and outcomes of posttransplant malignancies in vascularized composite allotransplantation (VCA)?

Findings: Fifteen VCA recipients developed 24 malignancies, with a median time to first malignancy of 1.89 years posttransplant. The majority were nonmelanoma skin cancers and posttransplant lymphoproliferative disorders. No deaths occurred among recipients with skin cancer only, whereas posttransplant lymphoproliferative disorder was associated with a 50% mortality rate. The median time from malignancy to death was 5.5 years, and from transplant to death, 10.1 years.

Meaning: VCA recipients most often develop nonmelanoma skin cancer and lymphoma within 2 years posttransplant, making vigilant dermatologic surveillance, Epstein–Barr virus monitoring, and regular follow-up essential for early detection.

INTRODUCTION

Vascularized composite allotransplantation (VCA) involves the transplantation of multiple nonautologous tissues as a single functional unit, such as skin, muscle, bone, vasculature, and nerves. The most common procedures are face and hand transplants, performed for patients with complex traumatic injuries, tissue loss following severe infections, or congenital anomalies that cannot be adequately addressed with conventional reconstruction.1,2 By restoring appearance, sensation, and motor function, VCA can significantly improve quality of life, independence, and social reintegration.3,4

Lifelong immunosuppression remains a major challenge in VCA, requiring recipients to weigh the life-enhancing benefits of the allograft against long-term risks. Among these, posttransplant malignancies are particularly concerning, with rates exceeding 25% in solid organ transplantation (SOT).5,6 This elevated risk stems from immunosuppression impairing immune surveillance and antiviral activity, enabling oncogenic virus reactivation and tumor proliferation.5–9 Malignancy is now a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in SOT, driven by prolonged maintenance immunosuppression and rising incidence of posttransplant lymphoproliferative disorders (PTLDs), cutaneous malignancies, and solid tumors.6,10,11

Although posttransplant malignancies are well characterized in SOT, corresponding data in VCA are sparse, limiting the development of guidelines for surveillance and management in this population. To address this gap, we conducted a systematic review to evaluate the reported incidence, timing, management, and outcomes of posttransplant malignancies in VCA recipients. These findings aim to raise awareness and provide a comprehensive overview of malignancy risk and reported cases in VCA recipients, laying the groundwork for future surveillance and management guidelines in this growing field.

METHODS

A systematic review was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines.12 The databases PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane were searched in January 2025 using Medical Subject Headings terms and keywords. There was no restriction on publication date. (See table, Supplemental Digital Content, which displays the search strategy for systematic review, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E925.)

Eligible studies included patients who underwent VCA and developed a subsequent malignancy. Only studies reporting patient-level data that could be individually analyzed were included. Studies were excluded if they involved SOT recipients or VCA recipients with nonmalignant tumors, premalignant lesions, or pretransplant malignancies without posttransplant recurrence. Two independent reviewers (A.K.G. and A.N.H.) performed title, abstract, and full-text screening, with discrepancies resolved by a third reviewer (H.P.W.) using Covidence, a web-based collaboration software platform that streamlines the production of systematic reviews.13 Data extracted included patient demographics, transplant details, malignancy characteristics, treatment, and overall survival. Supplemental patient-level data were obtained from prior publications cited by the included studies when relevant details were not directly reported. In cases where multiple studies described the same patient, all studies were included, and duplicate patients were noted. To enable patient-level analysis, each recipient was assigned a unique anonymized identifier (patients 1–15).

All statistical analyses were performed using R (RStudio version 12.1; R Core Team 2025),14 and figures were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 10.5.0).15 Continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical variables with χ2 or Fisher exact test. Significance was set at a P value of less than 0.05.

These patients collectively underwent 16 VCA procedures between 2000 and 2016, including face, upper extremity, scalp, lower extremity, and abdominal wall transplantation (Fig. 2). Indications for transplantation included burns, gunshot wounds, limited soft tissue coverage, sepsis, and other trauma. Comorbidities were infrequently reported, but notable examples included cirrhosis secondary to chronic hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection (patient 6),26,30 cardiomyopathy with reduced ejection fraction (patient 11),29 and a history of scalp leiomyosarcoma and kidney-pancreas transplantation (patient 14). Across the cohort, 24 distinct malignancies were identified. Most patients (60.0%) developed a single malignancy after transplantation, whereas 3 patients developed 2 (20.0%) or 3 (20.0%) malignancies. Skin cancer was the most common (54.2%), with 6 basal cell carcinomas (BCCs),17–19,23,25,27,28 7 squamous cell carcinomas (SCCs),24,25,27,30 and no melanoma. The majority of recipients (60.0%) were diagnosed with cutaneous malignancies. Of the 13 skin cancers, 84.6% presented on nonallograft sites. The second most frequent malignancy was PTLD, diagnosed in 5 (33.3%) patients16,20–22,24,29 with some EBV-associated subtypes, including high-grade B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, and primary central nervous system (CNS) PTLD. Less common malignancies included cervical carcinoma in situ, small cell lung carcinoma,19,23 EBV-associated posttransplant smooth muscle tumors,20,22 gastric cancer,25 hepatocellular carcinoma with peritoneal carcinosis,26,30 and marginal zone lymphoma (Fig. 3).27 No significant associations were found between the type of malignancy diagnosed, whether PTLD, skin cancer, or both, and sex, age, viral serostatus, VCA type, number of human leukocyte antigen mismatches, panel reactive antibody score, or induction agent.

The median time from transplant to first malignancy was 1.89 years (range 0.34–10.4 y). The median time to diagnosis was 2.2 years for skin cancer and 1.0 year for PTLD. Approximately 53.3% of recipients were alive and in remission, whereas 46.7% had died. All deaths were due to malignancy except for 1 patient with preexisting cardiomyopathy, who died from cardiac causes (patient 11).29 Median time from malignancy diagnosis to death was 5.52 years (range 0.49–10.2 y), and median posttransplant survival among deceased patients was 10.1 years (range, 0.90–12.1 y). All 6 recipients with skin cancer as their only malignancy survived.17,18,25,27,28,30 Of the 2 recipients with PTLD only, 1 died from malignancy-related causes.16,21 The single patient with both skin cancer and PTLD also died.24 Roughly 50% of patients who were diagnosed with PTLD died from malignancy-related causes.16,20–22,24 Also, recipients diagnosed with other malignancies in addition to skin cancer or PTLD had a 66.7% mortality rate.19,23,25–27,30 Furthermore, 1 SCC became invasive and was the cause of death for patient 5.27 Survival status did not significantly differ by malignancy type (P = 0.08), between recipients with PTLD only, skin cancer only, and both PTLD and skin cancer.

DISCUSSION

Lifelong immunosuppression is essential for graft survival in SOT and VCA but confers long-term risks, including opportunistic infections, end-organ toxicity, and malignancy.31,32 In SOT, malignancy risk is well documented, with a 2- to 3-fold increase over the general population,33,34 but whether VCA recipients face a similar risk remains unclear. As VCA expands and graft survival improves, prolonged immunosuppression raises concerns regarding oncological complications. This review highlights reported posttransplant malignancies in VCA and demonstrates the need for vigilant surveillance and tailored management strategies.

Types of Malignancies

Posttransplant malignancies are classified into 3 categories: donor-transmitted malignancies, recurrence of preexisting malignancies, and de novo malignancies.6,35 In this cohort, the majority of malignancies were de novo, consistent with established findings in SOT literature.32 The predominant malignancies were nonmelanoma skin cancer and PTLD, both of which are well-recognized complications of chronic immunosuppression in SOT. Less commonly reported malignancies included marginal zone lymphoma, gastric cancer, posttransplant smooth muscle tumors, and lung, hepatic, and cervical carcinomas, reflecting a broad spectrum of malignancies among VCA recipients.

Incidence and Risk Factors

Cutaneous malignancies were most prevalent, affecting 60% of recipients, mirroring trends in renal transplant patients.36 In SOT, SCC is typically more common than BCC due to immunosuppression and ultraviolet (UV)-related DNA damage,37–39 but in this cohort, rates were nearly equal. This may reflect lower cumulative UV exposure in VCA recipients and greater emphasis on sun protection practices, particularly for the allograft. No melanomas were observed, an unexpected finding, as they typically comprise a small but lethal minority of skin cancers in SOT.40 Whether this absence reflects limited sample size, underdiagnosis, or distinct immunologic mechanisms specific to VCA is uncertain and warrants further investigation. Interestingly, 85% of skin cancers occurred at nonallograft sites, suggesting that malignancy risk in VCA, similar to SOT, relates more to systemic effects of immunosuppression than intrinsic properties of transplanted skin and tissue.41

The second most common malignancy was PTLD, one of the most serious oncological complications following transplantation, characterized by uncontrolled B-cell proliferation. One-third of recipients in this cohort were diagnosed with PTLD, and all cases were aggressive subtypes. This lymphoproliferative disorder is strongly associated with EBV infection, particularly in EBV-naive recipients of EBV-positive allografts (R−/D+). However, only 1 recipient with PTLD had this high-risk serostatus; the remainder were lower risk (R+/D+ or R+/D−). When EBV risk is low, factors such as cumulative immunosuppression, induction regimen, and VCA type, reflecting the extent of lymphoid tissue transplanted, may contribute to PTLD risk.42 Despite this, no significant association was found between these risk factors and PTLD development in this cohort. In renal transplantation, mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitors, such as sirolimus or everolimus, demonstrate antiproliferative effects on lymphocytes and reduce PTLD and nonmelanoma skin cancer risk compared with calcineurin inhibitors such as tacrolimus.43–45 Over half of the cohort transitioned to a mammalian target of rapamycin–based regimen to mitigate malignancy risk, but no protective effect was observed, leaving their antineoplastic role in VCA indeterminate.

Timing of Malignancy Diagnosis

The median time to first malignancy was 1.89 years, or about 23 months posttransplant, underscoring the importance of sustained surveillance beyond the first year. For skin cancer, the median time to diagnosis was around 2.17 years or 26 months, emphasizing the need for early and proactive dermatologic surveillance. For PTLD, the median time to diagnosis in this cohort was 1 year posttransplant, consistent with SOT, where most cases follow a bimodal distribution: a first peak within the first year and a second between 5 and 15 years.46–48 Early-onset PTLD is frequently associated with EBV mismatch (R−/D+), whereas late-onset disease is more commonly seen in EBV-negative recipients and is often attributed to prolonged immunosuppression.42,49 Notably, 1 recipient developed very late-onset PTLD, over a decade posttransplant, without classic risk factors, suggesting chronic immunosuppression may contribute to malignancy even in lower risk individuals.29 The majority of early-onset cases reinforce the need for vigilant surveillance during the first posttransplant year, a high-risk period also marked by acute rejection in up to 85% of face and hand transplant recipients.50

Surveillance

For cutaneous malignancies, full-body skin examinations should be performed routinely at follow-up visits, with particular attention to native skin, as most lesions develop at nonallograft sites. These recommendations align with the International Immunosuppression & Transplant Skin Cancer Collaborative, which advocates for regular dermatologic follow-up for SOT recipients, if possible with a specialist in transplant dermatology, to facilitate early malignancy detection.51–53 Similar practices should be considered in VCA. Prophylactic measures, including hats, UV-protective clothing, and early treatment of precancerous lesions with cryotherapy or topical agents, have been shown to reduce nonmelanoma skin cancer in SOT and should be extended to VCA.51,54,55

Given the variable and often nonspecific clinical presentation of PTLD, maintaining a high index of suspicion is essential. In face transplantation recipients, lymphadenopathy within the graft may mimic benign causes such as minor infections, delaying diagnosis.29 Because PTLD can mimic acute rejection, allograft biopsy with histopathologic confirmation of EBV in tissue is crucial to distinguish between the 2 and avoid unnecessary augmentation of immunosuppression.56,57 High-risk patients, particularly those with EBV mismatch, should undergo EBV-DNAemia monitoring at routine follow-ups. Although not diagnostic, elevated or rising levels can serve as a valuable biomarker for EBV-positive PTLD and should prompt further evaluation with laboratory studies and imaging. However, this tool has limited specificity and poor sensitivity in cases involving the CNS, and does not detect EBV-negative disease.58,59 Therefore, EBV monitoring should complement, not replace, clinical judgment, and evaluation should not be delayed in symptomatic patients even if viremia is absent. Although no consensus exists regarding monitoring frequency, routine EBV-DNAemia testing for high-risk recipients is recommended during the first posttransplant year, when PTLD risk is highest. For recipients who are at lower risk of PTLD (R+/D− or R+/D+), the utility of EBV-DNAemia monitoring is not well established and should be individualized. Personalized risk assessment, modeled after SOT guidelines and factors such as allograft type, acute rejection history, and immunosuppression duration, may help optimize surveillance.56

Survival and Management Strategies

The overall survival rate in this cohort was 53.3%, and patients lived for nearly 5.5 years after malignancy diagnosis. Management of cutaneous malignancies consisted primarily of surgical excision and was performed in all cases. Recipients with skin cancer as their only malignancy achieved 100% survival, compared with mortality rates of 18% for BCC and 23.8% for SCC in renal transplant recipients.60 This difference in survival may reflect the benefit of more intensive surveillance in VCA recipients, though it may also be influenced by a small sample size and limited follow-up.

Outcomes for PTLD were less favorable, with 50% of affected patients dying from malignancy-related causes. In SOT, mortality rates have ranged between 40% and 70%, depending on subtype and immunosuppressive regimen.61 The mainstay of PTLD treatment is reduction or discontinuation of immunosuppression, typically by reducing calcineurin inhibitors by 30%–50% and withdrawing antimetabolite agents, such as MMF.42,62 If unresponsive, rituximab and various chemotherapy agents may be added in a stepwise manner.56,62–64 In severe cases, allograft explantation may be required to discontinue immunosuppression, as seen in patient 10, whose lower extremity grafts were removed following primary CNS PTLD, and patient 12, where immunosuppression was halted, leading to acute rejection and eventual facial allograft removal. In VCA, where the allograft is life-enhancing rather than life-saving, explantation presents profound psychosocial and functional consequences, and in face transplant recipients, necessitates retransplantation, a process associated with its own surgical risks and technical challenges. Recipients facing this decision require careful counseling.29

Comparison of Incidence to SOT

To contextualize our findings, we compared malignancy incidence rates in this cohort to renal transplantation, using face transplantation as a reference due to its more robust documentation among VCA types. The median follow-up time for these recipients was 10.1 years. Among 50 face transplants, the incidence of malignancy is 12%, with 6 documented cases,65 compared with 26% at 10 years in US kidney transplant recipients.6 Nonmelanoma skin cancer incidence was 8%, or 4 cases in our cohort, compared with 10.3% in kidney recipients.66 Two face transplant recipients, or 4%, developed PTLD, versus 1.4% in kidney recipients.67 Overall, face transplantation recipients had lower rates of all malignancies, a similar incidence of nonmelanoma skin cancer, and a higher rate of PTLD compared with renal transplant recipients. These comparisons should be interpreted with caution, given differences in sample size, patient selection, immunosuppression, and follow-up, but nonetheless highlight the importance of ongoing surveillance in VCA and offer a preliminary comparison to malignancy risk in renal transplantation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, VCA data may be underreported, particularly for less severe malignancies such as nonmelanoma skin cancer, leading to underestimation of true incidence. The exclusion of non-English studies may further contribute to underestimation and limit generalizability, as relevant data from non-English sources could have been overlooked. Second, inconsistent reporting of EBV serostatus, immunosuppressive regimens at malignancy diagnosis, and rejection history prevents evaluation of specific associations. The absence of a centralized registry limits our ability to track malignancy outcomes and accurately estimate incidence, especially for VCA types other than face transplantation. Third, heterogeneity among studies, the evolving nature of multiagent immunosuppressive regimens, and the occurrence of multiple malignancies complicate interpretation. Variation in follow-up duration may further affect the timing and completeness of malignancy reporting, contributing to heterogeneity. Notably, no statistically significant associations were found, likely reflecting the study’s limited power due to the small sample size. Finally, incomplete population-level data preclude reliable comparison of malignancy risk between VCA recipients, SOT recipients, and the general population. Multicenter collaboration and prospective data collection are critical for more robust risk stratification and evaluating oncological risk in VCA.

CONCLUSIONS

As VCA becomes more common and allograft survival improves, recipients face greater cumulative immunosuppression, thereby increasing malignancy risk. Nonmelanoma skin cancer and PTLD were the most frequently reported malignancies, often occurring within the first 2 years and highlighting the importance of early and ongoing surveillance. Malignancy-related mortality was substantial, particularly among recipients with PTLD or multiple cancers. Routine full-body skin examinations, especially of native skin, should be standard practice, and in high-risk EBV-mismatched recipients, EBV-DNAemia monitoring may aid early PTLD detection. The oncological risk profile in VCA is likely multifactorial, shaped by immunosuppression, viral serostatus, and rejection history, yet data remain sparse. Multicenter collaboration and prospective studies are needed to better characterize malignancy risk in VCA relative to SOT and the general population.
