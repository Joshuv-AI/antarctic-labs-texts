Pheochromocytoma With Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis: A Rare Tumor‐in‐Tumor Case
Authors: Cheng Lei, Yulong Guo, Yixin Luo, Qingchun Liang, Jiadi Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLangerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) occurring in a Pheochromocytoma in the adrenal gland is exceptionally rare and prone to misdiagnosis. The special coexistent tumors harbor distinct genetic mutations. This uncommon case could introduce novel considerations and a strong teaching message to all the clinicians and pathologists.

Introduction

A pheochromocytoma is a neuroendocrine tumor located in the adrenal glands. Most cases are sporadic, but approximately one‐third are caused by hereditary genetic mutations [1, 2, 3].

Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a rare histiocytic proliferative disorder characterized primarily by the diffuse proliferation and infiltration of Langerhans cells. In the 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) classification of histiocytosis, LCH has been categorized as an inflammatory myeloid neoplasm [4]. LCH is predominantly seen in children, and its clinical manifestations are diverse and lack specificity, often leading to delayed diagnosis. These two tumors seem entirely unrelated. This case is the first one to simultaneously identify LCH in the adrenal pheochromocytoma, at the protein and gene level.

Case History/Examination

A 38‐year‐old male patient was found to have a heterogeneous, low‐density, round‐like mass measuring 60 × 52 mm in the right adrenal gland on CT imaging (Figure 1). The patient reported no back pain, dizziness, or headaches. His blood pressure was slightly elevated at 147/114 mmHg, and he had never taken any antihypertensive medication. Blood biochemistry test results showed that the concentration of adrenaline and metanephrine was 36.8 and 28.8 pg/mL separately, which is normal; while the concentration of norepinephrine and normetanephrine was 6184.7 and 1868.4 pg/mL separately, much higher than the normal values. The concentration of adrenal cortical hormones remained normal. Consistantly, 24 h urine volume test displayed that the level of norepinephrine and normetanephrine was also abnormally high.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment)

While the clinical presentation suggests an adrenal tumor, definitive distinction between benign and malignant lesions relies on histopathological classification. Surgical excision of the adrenal mass was performed. The resected lesion exhibited dimensions of 5 × 4.5 × 4 cm and presented a soft, grayish‐brown cut surface that was clearly demarcated from surrounding tissues. Microscopically, the tumor was predominantly composed of oval‐shaped cells arranged in an organoid pattern, with abundant cytoplasm and salt‐and‐pepper chromatin. Mitoses were rare seen (Figure 2A–C). Within the mass, a distinct elliptical area approximately 1 cm in diameter exhibited a different histological appearance. This area consisted of mononuclear‐like cells with numerous scattered eosinophilic granulocytes, and nuclear grooves were observed in a minority of cells (Figure 2D,E). Immunohistochemistry revealed that the main tumor component expressed Synaptophysin (Syn), Chromogranin A (CgA), and neural cell adhesion molecule CD56 (Figure 3A, only showing CgA). The heterogeneous component within the mass expressed Langerhans cell markers CD207 (also called Langerin), CD1a, and S‐100 protein (Figure 3B, only showing Langerin). Given the histologically ambiguous features, it was difficult to determine whether this lesion was an isolated tumor accompanied by heterologous tissue or two coexistent tumors. Therefore, molecular characterization is essential to resolve diagnostic uncertainty and inform clinical management. Further Next‐Generation Sequencing (NGS) analysis of the specimen identified a von Hippel–Lindau tumor suppressor gene (VHL gene) mutation (p.W88*) in the main tumor component. The heterogeneous component showed concurrent B‐Raf proto‐oncogene (BRAF gene) mutation (p.V600E) and VHL gene mutation (p.W88*) (Figure 4).

Conclusions and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The final diagnosis for this case was pheochromocytoma with Langerhans cell histiocytosis. The patient has been followed regularly for 1 year and 10 months, during which no tumor recurrence has been observed.

Discussion

Pheochromocytoma is a rare type of neuroendocrine tumor originating from chromaffin cells in the adrenal medulla [1]. In the 5th edition of the WHO classification of endocrine and neuroendocrine tumors, the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD‐O) code of Pheochromocytoma is 8700/3, categorizing it as a malignant tumor [2]. The exact etiology of pheochromocytoma remains unclear, but due to excessive secretion of catecholamines, it can cause hyperactivation of the sympathetic nervous system, with patients commonly experiencing paroxysmal hypertension. Pheochromocytomas are often readily identified through blood biochemical testing and imageological examination [3]. The most frequent molecular alterations in pheochromocytoma include mutations in tricarboxylic acid cycle‐related genes, somatic VHL mutations, kinase pathway genes such as ret. proto‐oncogene (RET), and neurofibromin 1 (NF1) [5]. There have also been occasional reports of BRAF V600E mutations [6, 7]. The clinical presentation, immunohistochemistry, and NGS sequencing results in this case are consistent with a diagnosis of pheochromocytoma.

In the 5th edition of the WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumors, LCH is assigned the ICD‐O code 9751/1, placing it among tumors of uncertain biological behavior. The exact cause of LCH remains unclear, but recent studies suggest that LCH is associated with mutations in genes related to the Mitogen‐Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway, particularly mutations in the BRAF gene (such as BRAF V600E), which may lead to abnormal proliferation and functional alterations of Langerhans cells [8]. Localized LCH (e.g., solitary bone lesions) may present with localized pain and swelling, while systemic LCH (e.g., multiple bone lesions, skin involvement, or lung disease) can be accompanied by systemic symptoms such as fever, weight loss, and lymphadenopathy [9]. Diagnosis of LCH typically relies on tissue biopsy, combining tissue morphology with immunohistochemical markers. In this case, the scattered eosinophilic granulocytes and the langerhans cell‐like morphological features in the heterogeneous components remind the possibility of LCH. Further, the expression of histiocytic markers S100, CD1a, and CD207, and the detection of BRAF V600E mutation, reach to a thorough diagnosis of LCH.

Genomic instability is a major driving force behind the development of most tumors. VHL, located on chromosome 3p25.3, is frequently associated with mutations linked to VHL syndrome. According to the literature, VHL mutation can lead to the activation of the Hypoxia‐Inducible Factor (HIF) signaling pathway even under normal oxygen conditions, resulting in elevated erythropoietin levels and overproduction of red blood cells. The pseudohypoxic signaling pathway, an important molecular genetic pathway in pheochromocytoma, is notably influenced by VHL mutations, which promote the tumor's occurrence and progression [10, 11]. Interestingly, a BRAF‐V600E mutation was also identified in this case, found exclusively in the LCH lesion. As a hallmark genetic alteration of LCH, BRAF mutations cause aberrant activation of the MAPK signaling pathway, leading to the development of LCH. The distinct distribution of genetic alterations in this case suggests different origins for the two tumors, though further studies are needed to determine if they interact or influence each other. The detection of these genetic mutations also provides a basis for considering targeted therapies in future clinical management.

An intriguing finding in this case is the detection of the same somatic VHL mutation (p.W88*) in both the pheochromocytoma and the LCH components. This observation raises important questions regarding tumor origins. Two primary explanations are plausible. First, it may suggest a common clonal origin, where an ancestral progenitor cell harboring the VHL mutation underwent divergent differentiation, giving rise to two distinct tumor lineages—one evolving into pheochromocytoma and the other acquiring a secondary BRAF mutation to develop into LCH. Alternatively, technical factors must be considered. From Figure 3, we can observe that the LCH components display unclear boundaries, since CgA staining is focally positive in the heterogeneous area. Despite careful microdissection, complete physical separation of the two intimately associated components during DNA extraction may have been imperfect, leading to mutual contamination of genetic material. Distinguishing between these possibilities—a shared clonal origin versus technical admixing—has significant implications for understanding the pathogenesis of this composite tumor. Based on the available evidence, it is more likely to be the technical issue which leads to VHL mutation in both the pheochromocytoma and the LCH components. Further studies, such as more precise microdissection followed by deep sequencing or single‐cell genomic analysis, would be necessary to resolve this question definitively.

Cases of LCH in a pheochromocytoma in adrenal gland haven't been reported before. Similar to our case, Dandan Dai and Jing Xie reported an inferior vena cava metastatic pheochromocytoma complicated with LCH [12]. In their reported case, LCH harbored somatic mutations of BRAF and RAD54B, while pheochromocytoma showed an EPAS1 mutation. From the NGS data of our case, both VHL and BRAF are somatic mutations, and neither of the patient's parents has a similar history of tumors. Gene alteration might be the main driving factors of tumorgenesis. Besides, the interactions between tumor and the tumor microenvironment (TME) also play important roles. In fact, it's almost unrelated between VHL and BRAF pathways. And there is a case of LCH occurring in adrenal gland reported [13], indicating some cell component in adrenal gland could develop into LCH. Thus, it remains uncertain whether this tumor‐in‐tumor phenomenon is a simple collision tumor or LCH induced by pheochromocytoma. More evidence should be achieved to explore the possible biological mechanisms behind this comorbidity in the future.

Pheochromocytoma and LCH are both relatively rare tumors, and no cases of pheochromocytoma co‐occurring with LCH in adrenal gland have been reported so far. In this case, the pheochromocytoma presented as a prominent adrenal mass with slight hypertension. Meanwhile, the LCH component was hidden within the pheochromocytoma, with only a small lesion area, making it highly prone to being overlooked. Any clinician may encounter such a concealed coexistent tumor case, although it is an extremely rare event. Thus a thorough examination is necessary for the comprehensive diagnosis. This fairly exceptional case could introduce novel considerations and a strong teaching message to all the clinicians and pathologists. Further studies using more refined research methods, such as spatial transcriptomics, may be needed on larger cohorts to determine whether this mixed composition is a more widespread phenomenon.
