Improvement and development of immersion fixatives for enhanced tissue preservation in the whole eyeball specimens
Authors: Muneyoshi HYOTO, Hiroyuki IMAI, Akari NISHIDA, Kaoru FUJINO, Ryoji AOYAGI, Kiyoshi KANO, Takeshi KUSAKABE
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The wall of eyeball limits the penetration of the common fixatives, raising inadequate fixation or degeneration of internal tissue and posing challenges for preparing thin sections of the whole eyeball. In addition, many formaldehyde-containing fixatives exhibit the strong cross-linking effect that can excessively mask antigenic epitopes, thereby hampering immunological analyses. This study tried to develop an available fixative that exerts high permeability to mouse whole eyeballs and applicability to immunostaining. Glyoxal is known to have milder cross-linking capacity compared to formaldehyde, bringing better preservation of epitopes. We substituted the formaldehyde component in Davidson’s solution with glyoxal and supplemented 1-butanol, methanol, 2-mercaptoethanol, and tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP-HCl). Using modified fixatives and the GBMT solution, which was named by the initial letters of each component listed above, mouse eyeballs were immersion-fixed with the modified fixatives, and both tissue morphology and immunostaining quality were evaluated. The results showed that the GBMT solution prepared with glyoxal and specific combinations of additives improved morphological preservation of the eyeball tissues and enhanced immunofluorescence signals for certain cytoplasmic antigens, compared to the standard Davidson’s solution. Our findings demonstrated that the successful development of a novel fixative enables both high-quality whole eyeball sectioning and improved immunostaining performance through immersion fixation alone.

INTRODUCTION

Histological analysis of the retina usually requires trimming of the eyeball wall to infiltrate with fixative solution inward as soon as possible. For immunostaining of the retina and iris, quick infiltration of fixative to the interior of the eyeball and trimming of non-target tissues is also recommended [30, 38]. However, since the mouse eyeball is small, making proper trimming is very difficult. Inaccurate or prolonged trimming procedure results in insufficient fixation and induces degeneration of retinal tissue. In many studies, whole-body perfusion fixation is not suitable since fresh retinal tissue is necessary for molecular analyses. In addition, trimming of the eyeball wall is often unnecessary for the observation of entire eyeballs or for the creation of educational specimens of eyeballs. Margo and Lee (1995) showed that even when 10% neutral buffered formalin solution (NBF) or 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) was used quickly, deformation of the eyeball shape and irregularities of the layered structure in the retina occurred frequently [25]. These tissue specificities and applicability of eyeball specimens require improvement or development of fixative solutions.

To improve the difficulty of ocular histological analysis, the following efforts have been undertaken. Permeabilization with butanol instead of xylene facilitates thin sectioning of the lens [20, 21]. Butanol has been also used in plant tissues for the preparation of paraffin blocks from grape embryo [17]. Methanol has the potential to infiltrate into the lens tissue uniformly. However, the hardness of lens makes it difficult to prepare paraffin sections from eyeball specimens. Use of 2-mercaptoethanol and tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP-HCl) promotes cleavage of disulfide bonds in tissue proteins and is expected to soften lens tissue. Appropriate additives to the fixative solution may facilitate thin sectioning of the lens and whole eyeballs.

Cross-linking effect of formaldehyde on proteins interferes with the binding affinity of antibody by causing significant conformation changes in antigen and masking epitopes. Especially, the detection of synaptic proteins is sometimes difficult to find by immunofluorescence following formaldehyde fixation, even if it was conducted with antigen retrieval [22]. On the other hand, specimens of the hippocampus, myocardium, and inner ear fixed with glyoxal showed good immunofluorescence performance equivalent to or superior to those obtained with PFA [33]. Furthermore, brain specimens fixed with glyoxal showed strong effects in the detection of synaptic proteins [22, 23]. Also, glyoxal has an effect to fix faster than formaldehyde and does not excessively shrink tissues [9, 43]. Glyoxal moderately hardens tissues when mixed with acetic acid or ethanol, maintaining the structure of soft tissues such as a brain and facilitating thin slicing [22].

The eyeball has both a retina that is easily degenerated and is located in a difficult position to reach fixative solution, and a thick sclera that prevents the infiltration of the fixative solution. This study aimed to develop a fixative solution that uses glyoxal instead of formaldehyde and improve sectioning efficiency by adding other components. We evaluated whether the new fixative solution (1) exhibited higher permeability and fixative effects by immersion method than those of conventional fixatives, and (2) could apply to immunostaining without losing antigenicity. Furthermore, we selected additives to the new fixative solution that (3) can promote preparing thin sectioning of lens and whole mouse eyeballs with high quality.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals and eyeball collection

A total of 128 mice [ICR (n=92), B6C3F1 (n=17), and B6D2F1 (n=19); Japan SLC, Hamamatsu, Japan] were used, ranging in age from 15 days to 15 months. Euthanasia was performed by cervical dislocation following isoflurane inhalation or by CO2 inhalation, and eyeball sampling was performed immediately. Micro-tweezers were inserted into the orbit, and the right and left eyeballs were carefully collected by severing the optic nerve along with extraocular muscles and orbital fat body. The animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committees of Yamaguchi University (Approval number: 557 and 667).

Morphological evaluation

After hematoxylin and eosin (H-E) staining, sections were observed using a microscope (Eclipse CiE, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and tissue images were taken with WinROOF2018 image analysis software (Mitani Corp., Tokyo, Japan). From the images, the degrees of structural retention in the external shape of eyeball, cornea, iris, lens, and retina were evaluated by each fixative.

Immunofluorescence

Sections were deparaffinized and immersed in 100, 90, and 70% ethanol and distilled water (5 min each). Then, sections were treated with an antigen retrieval solution pH 9 (Nichirei Bioscience, Tokyo, Japan) using a high-pressure heater for 20 min. After washing with PBS, the sections were blocked with 10% skim milk for 30 min and treated with the primary antibody (Supplementary Table 1) at 4°C for overnight with gentle agitation. For the non-labeled primary antibodies, goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L with Alexa Fluor® 594 (ab150080, abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) was applied at 1:250 for 1 hr at room temperature. Sections were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 340-07971, FUJIFILM Wako, Osaka, Japan). The sections were mounted in Fluoromount/Plus™ (Diagnostic BioSystems,Pleasanton, CA, USA). Photographs were taken with a Mica confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) and LasX software (Leica).

RESULTS

PFA and Davidson’s solution showed difficulty in retaining the normal structure of mouse eyeballs

In PFA-fixed eyeballs, the anterior and posterior wall of eyeball denatured as a wavy figure and most areas of the retina were detached from the choroid (Fig. 1a). The lens was shattered and cracked, and a part of the nucleus did not remain (Fig. 1b). The iris was displaced toward the cornea, and the anterior chamber area was narrowed (Fig. 1c). Swelling was observed in the stroma of cornea, showing the space expansion among the collagen fibers (Fig. 1d). The retina detached artificially not only between the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and the photoreceptor layer (PL), but also between the outer and inner segments of the photoreceptors (Fig. 1e). Abnormal spaces were formed between the PL and the outer nuclear layer (ONL), as well as within the ONL and inner nuclear layers (INL, Fig. 1f). The staining properties of nuclear chromatin differed from those of sDS (described below, Fig. 1g).

In the specimens fixed with sDS, the ring shape of ocular wall was well preserved (Fig. 1h). However, the nucleus and cortex of the lens were shattered and cracked by sectioning. Abnormal detachment happened between the anterior capsule and epithelium (Fig. 1i). The iris was bent and deformed (Fig. 1j). The cornea showed stromal swelling with expansion of collagen interspaces and waving of endothelium (Fig. 1k). The retina showed no dissociation in every cell layer, and the parallel 10-layer structure was maintained (Fig. 1l). Hematoxylin staining showed dense condensation of chromatin in the nuclei of ONL, and pale stainability in those of INL and ganglion cell layer (GCL, Fig. 1m). Fixative effects using sDS were better than those of PFA.

DISCUSSION

In the eyeball, the sclera packs various tissues with different properties, making internal infiltration of the fixative and rapid, uniform fixation difficult. The eyeball tissues shows significant differences in contraction rates following fixation and dehydration, especially at the vitreous body, which is over 99% water, and the retina, choroid, sclera [25, 32, 35]. The Davidson’s fixative (DS) exhibits strong dehydration and protein coagulation due to the components of ethanol and acetic acid. These effects enable rapid fixation and DS have been recognized as a useful fixative for eyeballs, particularly for preserving the external shape and the retina [11, 14, 24, 26]. We used 3.70% formaldehyde instead of the commonly used 37–40% formaldehyde in DS to reduce protein cross-linking [24, 31]. While this DS preserved many structures and was more effective than PFA, the preservation status in the cornea and lens was incomplete (Fig. 1h, 1i, 1k).

Glyoxal can demonstrate fixative ability at lower concentrations [44]. However, glyoxal was not sufficient to maintain eyeball tissues, especially of the retina (Supplementary Fig. 1). Normally, the retina is moderately pressed by intraocular fluid and the vitreous body [12]. When the consistent tissues shrink or disproportionate expansion occur, the mechanical power balance would be disrupted and the retina could detach frequently at the RPL-PL interface, where the adhesion is embryologically and histologically weak. Replacement of formaldehyde with glyoxal alone is unlikely to maintain the morphology of the weakly bound sites. Furthermore, a property that glyoxal fixing tissue more softly [8, 18, 22] may cause tissue tears during thin sectioning. Therefore, the use of additives seemed to be essential to support fixation by glyoxal.

Addition of 1-butanol improved structural preservation especially in the lens and retina (Fig. 2). The 1-butanol has a larger molecular weight and lower polarity than ethanol and therefore, exhibits a mild dehydrating effect [39]. The slow dehydration, especially at the vitreous body, was assumed to decrease intraocular pressure gradually, involving with maintenance of the external shape of eyeballs. Similarly, slow dehydration may contribute to uniform tissue contraction at the choroidal and neural retina sides and may prevent retinal artificial detachment. It was also suggested that 1-butanol addition decreased polarity and hydrophilic properties in grDS and retarded fixation processing especially at the outer wall of eyeball. Slow fixation might delay aggregation and meshwork shrinkage of protein in sclera and induce infiltration of glyoxal to interior tissues in the eyeball.

Dislocation of the iris appeared in several fixative solutions was divided into two types. One involves the detachment of the lens capsule and epithelium. Since these tissues contain high water content, whereas the lens cortex and nucleus are composed of dense proteins with low water content, it was thought that different contraction reactions occurred in the lens during dehydration process [35]. This might enhance tension at boundaries in lens and induce lens separation and anterior movement along with the iris. The other hypothesis is due to tissue changes of the vitreous body. It is assumed that insufficient dehydration process induced transformation of the vitreous body, which then pushed the lens and iris. In both cases, it was suggested that optimizing the dehydration process is effective for preserving intraocular tissues.

In contrast, methanol has a small molecular weight and high polarity [39]. From a general chemical standpoint, methanol is more permeable to tissues than ethanol or 1-butanol, which can lead to faster dehydration and protein coagulation. Therefore, it was speculated that methanol addition elevated penetration effect for grDS and promote coagulation of nucleoprotein in the lens (Fig. 3). In the swelling corneal stroma, delamination of collagen fibers was markedly event. In the cornea, collagen fibers and proteoglycans form hydrogen and ionic bonds. Excessive alcohol addiction may attribute to the weakening of intermolecular interactions caused by dehydration of bound water or by disruption of osmotic regulation [5, 19]. Alternatively, highly hydrophobic profile of the corneal epithelium [15, 16] may interfere fixative infiltration and contribute to stromal swelling.

Crystallin, a main protein component of the lens, contains thiol groups (-SH), which form disulfide bonds when oxidized. It is thought that the difficulty of preparing the lens sections in old-aged mice and rats is due to increase of the crystallin disulfide enhancing lens hardening [29, 45]. We tried to break disulfide bonds in lens by addition of 2-mercaptoethanol and TCEP-HCl, however, no significant results were obtained when they used alone (Supplementary Fig. 2). It was considered that the softening of lens by reducing agent required the permeation effect of methanol (Fig. 4). Additionally, TCEP-HCl was more effective for preserving tissue structure of iris and corneal stroma (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Fig. 4).

Mouse retinas have a unique profile for hematoxylin staining that the cell nucleus in ONL forms a “chromocenter” structure where chromatin is concentrated in the center region [41]. This nuclear structure is thought to be effective for light transmission through the retina [36], and a prominent characteristic in the retinas of nocturnal animals [37]. Meanwhile, mouse ganglion cells have a lightly stained nuclei by hematoxylin [13], perhaps due to high transcription activity in the euchromatin. Difference of hematoxylin stainability in ONL and GCL seems to be a useful indicator for assessing retinal preservation. Acetic acid and alcohol-based additives have possibilities to decrease tissue pH, change osmotic pressure, and induce protein coagulation [40, 42]. However, in this study, nuclear staining properties in the retina were maintained except for PFA-fixed specimens. Glyoxal [44] and every additive used in this study were not suggested to affect the staining properties of hematoxylin.

The mouse eyeball size increases 2.33-fold in volume between 14-days-old and 14-months-age, and the sclera thickens also increase from 35.94 ± 11.97 μm at 15-days-old to 59.42 ± 13.47μm at 15-months-old (our observation, unpublished data). In this study, GBMT solution was evaluated on the eyeballs from 28-days-old to 8-month-old mice, however, the outer shape of the eyeball and the parallel 10-layered retinal structure were well preserved in all specimens. The penetration rates of formalin into tissue have been reported to be approximately 700 μm per hour in liver [6]. These suggest that GBMT solution can rapidly penetrate the interior of the eyeball regardless of age-dependent increase of mouse eyeball size or scleral thickness. On the other hand, age-related hardening of the lens has a possibility to reduce permeability and decrease the effectiveness of fixation.

When immersion-fixation with GBMT solution was performed on the rat eyeballs, which sizes are larger than those of mice, the 10-layered retinal structure was well preserved, and smooth sectioning on the lens was achievable. However, spherical shape of the eyeball was deformed, and the lens was fragmentated (data not shown). For preserving larger eyeballs, post-collection method involving the injection of fixative into the vitreous cavity through the cornea using a fine needle has been reported [4, 28]. This technique may improve osmotic imbalance between the GBMT solution and intraocular tissues and help the fixing effects to maintain the shape of larger eyeballs.

Low applicability has been reported for DS in the immunostaining for nuclear antigens (cyclinD1) and cytoplasmic antigens (Brn3a, γ-aminobutyric acid, glutamine synthetase, nestin, αB-crystallin, Iba1) than NBF [7, 8]. On the other hand, glyoxal has superior retention of many antigens regardless of their localization compared to formaldehyde [33]. Indeed, the specimens fixed with GBMT solution improved and showed higher reactivity to anti-PKC α and synaptophysin antibodies compared to those fixed with sDS (Fig. 5a–d). Immunostainings for calbindin D-28K, GFAP, and Tuj1 showed comparable results between two groups (Fig. 5i–n). GBMT solution was demonstrated to be highly effective for detection of the antigens distributing in cytoplasm. In contrast, immunoreactivity for the nuclear antigens Chx10 and NeuN reduced in the specimens of GBMT solution than those of sDS (Fig. 5e–h). When organic solvents contain in aqueous solutions, conventional pH measurement loses accuracy. This is due to specificities of solvents regarding the incompatibility of pH scales with aqueous solutions, the uniqueness of liquid junction potentials, and the low ionic conductivity. Also, organic solvents can affect functions of glass electrodes, which together prevent determination of the true pH in mixed solutions [3, 10, 27, 34]. Therefore, we compared the aqueous components of each fixative and showed the GBMT solution a lower pH value (1.70 ± 0.01, Table 3) than Davidson’s solution (2.39 ± 0.02, Table 1). The reduced detectability of nuclear proteins in GBMT specimens may be attributable to excessively acidic conditions, as fixation with acidic Bouin’s solution resulted in decrease or nonspecific reactions for nuclear protein detections [1, 2].

We developed GBMT solution in this study exhibiting high permeability as well as uniform and rapid fixation capabilities. This GBMT solution preserved the tissue structure of mouse eyeballs by the method of immersion fixation and enabled high-quality thin sectioning of the whole eyeball including lens capsule and nucleus. Immunostaining cytosolic antigens showed sensitive detection and stronger signal intensity. However, immunoreactivities of the nuclear antigens remain to be solved. Since GBMT solution does not require trimming eyeballs and whole-body perfusion fixation, size measurements and freeze preservation of one side can be performed in parallel with less time and effort. Whole eyeball sections with correctly preserved tissue microstructure can be expected to be used effectively in histological analysis and education.
