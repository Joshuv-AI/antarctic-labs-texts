Implementation evaluation of a comprehensive CARE record to reduce hospital-acquired complications: a mixed-methods pre–post study
Authors: Rebecca Leigh Jessup, Maree Glynn, Deborah Zilm, Hanife Mehmet, Mark Tacey, Jason Talevski, Hazel Heng, Uyen Phan, Donald Campbell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo evaluate whether implementing the Comprehensive Assessment and Risk Evaluation (CARE) record, developed to operationalise Standard 5: Comprehensive Care of the Australian National Safety and Quality Health Service Standards was associated with improvements in patient safety and quality of care. A secondary objective was to explore staff and patient experiences of implementation.DesignMixed-methods pre–post evaluation combining quantitative analysis of routinely collected hospital data with staff surveys and patient interviews.SettingA metropolitan hospital network in Melbourne, Victoria, AustraliaParticipantsQuantitative analysis included 88 041 admissions pre-implementation and 23 765 post implementation. Staff surveys were completed by 117 staff (84% nurses, 60% with >5 years at the service). Structured interviews were conducted with 39 patients (mean age 65.6 years, 64% male).InterventionThe CARE record was a structured record embedding validated risk assessments (STRATIFY, Braden Scale, Malnutrition Screening Tool, Confusion Assessment Method/4 ‘A’s Test, Broset Violence Checklist, Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale) and care planning into routine workflows, to meet requirements of Standard 5.Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcomes were the incidence of falls and pressure injuries. Secondary outcomes were completion of the CARE record, staff and patient experiences, and documentation of other hospital-acquired complications (HACs) (delirium, malnutrition, violence and aggression, suicide and self-harm). Data were compared for two time periods: 1 January 2016 to 28 February 2019 (before CARE record implementation) and 1 March 2019 to 30 March 2020 (after CARE record implementation but before COVID-19).ResultsPost implementation, overall falls decreased (OR 0.85; 95% CI 0.78 to 0.93), though severe falls increased (OR 1.89; 95% CI 1.17 to 3.07). Pressure injuries decreased in coded data (OR 0.41; 95% CI 0.23 to 0.73) and incident reports (OR 0.79; 95% CI 0.68 to 0.92). Documentation of delirium (OR 2.02), malnutrition (OR 2.03), aggression/violence (OR 2.09) and suicide/self-harm (OR 1.92) increased. Half of the staff (50.4%) felt they knew more about patients, but 61.2% reported more time to admit patients. Communication between nursing and allied health improved (65.8%). Patients were generally satisfied, though 50% repeated information frequently and 20% felt unprepared for discharge.ConclusionsCARE record implementation, mandated by the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care’s Standard 5, was associated with fewer pressure injuries and overall falls (though there was an increase in falls resulting in serious injury), and greater documentation of other complications. Staff and patient experiences highlighted some benefits but also documentation time challenges. Findings provide lessons for the national implementation of Standard 5 and the need to balance structured processes with clinical judgement.

INTRODUCTION

Preventing hospital complications and unintended patient harm remains a constant challenge for health service providers striving to deliver high-quality care.1 2 These events, which can include medication errors, infections, surgical complications, pressure injuries and falls, compromise patient safety and can often lead to prolonged hospital length of stay, increased healthcare costs and even irreversible harm or death.3 The prevalence and severity of these events increase the need for a comprehensive approach to patient safety, which in turn can create safer hospital environments and enhance overall healthcare quality, so that patients can improve patient experience and outcomes.

The Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care (ACSQHC) is a corporate Commonwealth entity of the Australian Government established to lead and coordinate national improvements in the safety and quality of healthcare. Together with the Australian government, states and territories, in 2018 they launched a second edition of the National Safety and Quality Health Service Standards.4 The standards set the national benchmark against which all health services are accredited and require organisations to establish systems to measure their performance against requirements to ensure they reduce patient risk of in-hospital harm and support safe, high-quality care.

A major addition to the second edition of these standards was Standard 5: Comprehensive Care, which requires health services to ensure that every patient receives an individualised, evidence-based care plan and that structured systems are in place to reduce the risk of six priority hospital-acquired complications: falls, pressure injuries, delirium, malnutrition, violence and aggression, and suicide/self-harm.4 This includes providing appropriate screening (using existing, validated screening tools) to identify and mitigate risks associated with hospitalisation.4 Hospitals were required to implement strategies to meet the requirements of the Standard for accreditation, but each service could determine its own approach, resulting in variation across hospitals.5

Internationally, only a small number of countries have national standards for comprehensive care.6 Reported barriers include the absence of standardised care plans, lack of clear patient-centred goals in documentation and excessive paperwork, while facilitators include the use of structured templates, efficient documentation processes and proactive collaboration among patients and clinicians.6 Against this backdrop, there remains a pressing need for empirical evaluations of structured methods designed to operationalise comprehensive care standards in routine practice.

The assessment and reporting of in-hospital falls and pressure injuries have been important metrics for evaluating hospital quality over time. Falls are a prevalent occurrence within the hospital setting, impacting approximately 2% of hospital stays.79 Among these, more than 25% result in injury, with 2% leading to fractures.9 The reported prevalence for pressure injury in hospitals is over 6%, which increases considerably in some long-term care and intensive care environments.10 Delirium impacts a large number of hospitalised patients, with prevalence rates ranging from 3% to 29%.11 12 Malnutrition affects up to 40% of hospitalised individuals, although it is often inadequately identified and documented.13 Patients experiencing delirium and/or who are malnourished also face an increased risk of other HACs, including falls1416 and pressure injuries.17 18 Occupational violence is an unfortunate reality within healthcare settings; however, it often goes unreported, with estimations suggesting that less than 20% of events are reported.19 Though infrequent, occurrences of patient suicides hold important significance, acting as indicators for potential inadequacies within hospital systems and protocols.20 Instituting methods to reduce these incidents aligns with the government’s objective of delivering public hospital services that are safe and of high quality and may also contribute to the sustainability of the health system through reducing preventable costs.21

The Comprehensive Assessment and Risk Evaluation (CARE) record was developed as a structured method to embed existing risk assessments and care planning practices into everyday workflows in order to meet this national mandate. CARE is not a new clinical instrument but a mechanism to ensure that the requirements of Standard 5 are consistently enacted at the point of care. The objective of this study was to evaluate whether implementing the CARE record as a response to the ACSQHC’s Standard 5 led to measurable improvements in patient safety and quality of care, and to examine staff and patient experiences with its implementation in a hospital setting.

METHODS

Study design

This study comprised a mixed-methods, pre-post implementation evaluation. A detailed study protocol has previously been published.22 The results of this study have been reported in accordance with the Good Reporting of A Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS) checklist.23

Study population and context

Northern Health (NH) is the major provider of acute, subacute and ambulatory specialist services in Melbourne’s north. The hospital provides a comprehensive range of primary, secondary and tertiary healthcare services. The NH catchment is located in one of Australia’s fastest-growing areas, with residents originating from more than 184 countries and speaking more than 106 languages.24 The top five languages spoken after English are Arabic, Italian, Assyrian, Turkish and Greek. Residents in the NH catchment have lower levels of income, educational attainment and health literacy, and higher rates of unemployment than state averages.25

Intervention

The CARE record was a paper-based (a electronic medical record (EMR) was not used at the hospital at the time of this study) structured method developed at NH to address the requirements of the National Standard of Safety and Quality Health Service (NSQHS) Standard 5: Comprehensive CARE (pressure injury, falls, malnutrition, delirium, violence and aggression, suicide and self-harm). Five subcommittees were established, consisting of Nursing Executives, Nurse Unit Managers, Allied Health Managers, Medical Staff, Quality Managers, Clinical Risk Managers and Consumers, to develop each component of the CARE record based on previous literature and clinical experience. The final CARE record included:

The NH Stratify Falls Risk (STRATIFY) assessment, a locally developed and validated risk assessment tool to identify patients at risk of falling.26

The NH Stratify Falls Risk (STRATIFY) assessment, a locally developed and validated risk assessment tool to identify patients at risk of falling.26

The Braden Pressure Injury Risk (Braden Scale) assessment is a validated risk screening tool to foster early identification of patients at risk of developing pressure injuries.27

The Braden Pressure Injury Risk (Braden Scale) assessment is a validated risk screening tool to foster early identification of patients at risk of developing pressure injuries.27

The Malnutrition Screening Tool (MST), a simple and validated malnutrition screening tool used to identify patients at risk of malnutrition.28

The Malnutrition Screening Tool (MST), a simple and validated malnutrition screening tool used to identify patients at risk of malnutrition.28

The Confusion Assessment Method (CAM), a validated delirium screening tool for detection of delirium,29 which, if delirium risk is identified, will lead to Part 1 and 2 of the 4 ‘A’s Test (4AT), a rapid risk screening of delirium.30

The Broset Violence Checklist (BVC), a validated 6-item checklist which assists in the prediction of imminent violent behaviour.31

The Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS), a validated screening tool to assess the severity and immediacy of suicide risk.32

Where patients were identified as ‘at risk’, the CARE record stipulated that the patient receive risk mitigation strategies in line with risk screen recommendations and best practice clinical care standards. All staff who implemented the CARE record attended a 1-hour presentation, followed by bedside training, referred to as ‘bedside CARE simulation’. A train-the-trainer method was used so that these staff could provide training for the remaining ward staff. A more detailed account of the development of the CARE record and implementation training methods is reported in our protocol paper.22

Patient or public involvement

As part of Standard 5, clinical teams worked collaboratively with patients, families and carers to develop personalised care plans that reflected individual needs, goals and preferences, supported by screening tools and assessment systems. This partnership approach ensured comprehensive care delivery throughout the patient journey. The sub-committee overseeing all activities for Standard 5 included consumers.

Data sources

Data for the primary and tertiary outcomes were gathered from the data warehouse across two data sources: routinely collected patient data reported according to the Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10-AM) codes,33 and data reported to the Victorian Health Incident Management System (VHIMS). The VHIMS data are further classified across Incident Severity Ratings (ISR) levels 1 and 2. ISR level 1 refers to sentinel events that result in death or permanent severe harm. These incidents require immediate reporting and thorough root cause analysis. ISR level 2 includes major incidents leading to significant but temporary harm or permanent moderate harm. To prevent the risk of double counting episodes across the two datasets, incident rates between the two time periods were compared separately across the two datasets. Data were compared for two time periods: 1 January 2016 to 28 February 2019 (before CARE record implementation) and 1 March 2019 to 30 March 2020 (after CARE record implementation but before COVID-19). Demographic and health-related data were also collected from the data warehouse, including age, gender, indigenous status, language spoken, relationship status and whether the individual resided in an aged care facility.

Data for secondary outcomes included the audit of completion of the CARE record as well as staff and patient experience. Audit data were collected directly from a random sample of 50 medical records 3 months post implementation. Data on staff experience were collected as a survey administered through REDCap (with consent implied by completion, made explicit in the explanatory statement). Data on patient experience were collected via interviews conducted by qualified research nurses (informed written consent provided by participating patients).

The date range of routinely collected data for primary and tertiary outcomes was shortened to 1 January 2016 - 30 March 2020 (from the intended 1 January 2016 to 31 December 2020). The date range was changed to exclude all data collected after the implementation of lockdown measures in Melbourne, as these measures were found to be associated with an increase in both falls and pressure injuries in hospitalised patients.3436

Participants

Primary and tertiary outcomes

Data were retrospectively gathered for the period 1 January 2016 to 28 February 2019. These data were used to provide a historical performance of NH prior to the implementation of the CARE record, and to compare with the same outcomes post implementation. The post implementation time period was from 1 March 2019 to 30 March 2020.

Secondary outcomes

Structured patient interviews

Structured interviews were conducted with patients who were hospitalised in the previous month on the implementation ward, who were over the age of 18, spoke English, Arabic, Italian, Assyrian, Turkish, Greek, Macedonian, Mandarin/Cantonese, Persian, Vietnamese or Croatian. 10 patients were randomly selected via computer-generated stratified sampling to ensure random selection of patients across wards, age, gender and reason for admission (health condition). 10 patients from three wards were identified via randomisation and were invited to participate in semi-structured interviews by phone. The structured interview questions (online supplemental file 1) focused on patient experience of communication, involvement in care and discharge planning. Patients were asked about the adequacy of information provided, how frequently they had to repeat health details, perceived involvement in decision-making and sufficiency of discharge preparation. Questions required a dichotomous (yes or no) response with patients then invited to elaborate on positive aspects of their care or suggest areas for improvement. Interview questions were co-developed by the clinical implementation team and a researcher with experience in qualitative interviews (RLJ), with a focus on focus on identifying aspects of communication and care processes relevant to refinement of the CARE record. It was trialled with three patients before use and all interviews were conducted by a research nurse independent of the implementation team.

Staff survey

An invitation to participate in the staff survey (online supplemental file 2) was sent out via email from the Chief Executive Officer to all clinical staff at NH (including nurses, medical and allied health staff). Staff were asked to participate only if they had used the CARE record. The survey used a 5-item Likert scale responses asking staff to rate the usefulness (extremely useful, moderately useful, neither useful nor useless, moderately useless, extremely useless) of the CARE record in improving communication between health professionals and patients, in identifying risks, in formulating and achieving care plans for patients. The survey was co-developed by the clinical implementation team and a researcher with experience in survey design (RLJ) and was trialled with clinicians before use.

Statistical methods

The evaluation of all HACs was assessed through voluntarily reported incidents (as well as incidents documented and coded in the medical record for falls and pressure injury) on patients known to have been admitted, transferred through or discharged from the wards where the CARE record was introduced. The CARE record had a staggered introduction on the wards over a period of 4 months, from March 2019 to July 2019. Therefore, the pre-post CARE record periods were different across the wards considered in the patient cohort. If a patient was transferred through multiple wards with different CARE record start dates, the patient was assigned to the first ward and its corresponding start date. Descriptive analysis was conducted to provide an estimate of the proportion of episodes in which a defined outcome occurs, with χ2 tests conducted to test for differences between the pre-implementation versus post implementation of the CARE record time periods. Logistic regression analysis was conducted in order to express the likelihood of a patient having either a HAC or VHIMS incident in the post-CARE period in comparison to the pre-CARE record period. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to adjust for the effect of age, gender, indigenous status, language spoken, relationship status and being a resident of an aged care facility. The clinical details included admission type (emergency, elective), primary, associated and in-hospital complication diagnosis. All analyses were performed using Stata software (V.17, Stata Corporation, College Station, Texas, USA). A statistical significance level was set to a p value of less than 0.05.

Survey and interview data collected post implementation of the CARE record were analysed using descriptive statistics to assess staff and patient perceptions of the CARE record’s impact on patient care, communication and workflow efficiency. Descriptive statistics were also used to provide an overview of staff satisfaction with the CARE record’s usability and its perceived benefits in clinical practice. For patient interviews, responses to dichotomous (yes/no) questions were summarised using frequencies and proportions. Given the structured nature of the interview, a full thematic analysis was not conducted. Free-text responses were reviewed internally at NH to provide context to quantitative findings and to identify opportunities for improvement in the CARE record, but were not included in the formal analysis.

The incidence of delirium, malnutrition, aggression and violence and suicide and self-harm reported via VHIMS increased compared with levels recorded pre-implementation.

Secondary outcomes

CARE record completion audits

50 medical records of patients from each ward (prior to and post implementation) were audited. Clinical history and clinical assessments were well documented in the CARE record, with most wards achieving 100%, except for ward E (89% for clinical history) and ward F (88% for clinical history and 88% for clinical assessment).

Figure 1 provides an overview of completion rates for risk screening and management plans for pressure injuries, falls and malnutrition. All wards achieved 100% completion for pressure injury and falls risk assessments, demonstrating consistent adherence to the screening process. The completion of management plans for high-risk patients varied, with ward D performing well in falls management plans (88%) and wards B, C and D achieving 100% for pressure injury management plans. While malnutrition risk assessments had high completion rates (above 90%), dietitian referrals for patients with identified malnutrition risks were inconsistently documented, ranging from 50% in ward A to 100% in wards B, D and G.

Demographics for patients completing interviews

Overview of patient satisfaction with care and overall experience.

The majority of patients felt adequately informed throughout their hospital stay. Specifically, 80% (n=32) reported having enough time to discuss their information with the admitting nurse. 20 patients (50%) reported that they had to repeat health information frequently to different staff members. Additionally, most respondents (n=34) believed that the hospital staff knew enough about them to provide necessary care, though a small number (n=4) raised concerns about certain personal details not being consistently addressed, suggesting a need for better information-sharing practices among the care team.

Patients generally expressed satisfaction with the level of involvement in their care. Over three-quarters (n=31) indicated that they were as involved as they wanted to be in decisions about their treatment. Almost two-thirds of participants (n=24) felt that family members were able to participate in discussions and decisions about their care. A similar number of participants (n=23) also reported that their daily care goals were consistently discussed.

Patients provided mixed feedback about discharge planning and highlighted areas for improvement. One in five (n=8) did not feel they were adequately informed about their anticipated discharge date and did not have enough time to plan. Additionally, 13% (n=5) indicated that they were not given sufficient information on how to manage their condition at home post-discharge, suggesting that more detailed or tailored discharge instructions might benefit those with complex conditions.

Over 50% of participants had more than 10 years of experience as a health professional, and just over 60% had been working at NH for more than 5 years.

Demographics for staff completing the staff survey

Staff satisfaction with the CARE record and utility of implementation in a healthcare context is presented in table 4. Just over one-third (n=40; 34.5%) of staff were able to complete the CARE record in less than 30 min, with most (n=53; 45.7%) taking between 30 min and 1 hour. One in five staff found it very time-consuming to complete, with time required ranging from 1 to 2 hours. There were variable reports on the reduction of duplication of current admission forms and processes due to the CARE record, with 37 staff (31.9%) indicating a reduction, while 32 (27.6%) indicated that there had been no reduction in duplication and 27 staff (40.5%) observed an increase in duplication. The majority of participants (n=71; 61.2%) experienced an increase in the time taken to admit a patient using the CARE record.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first real-world study examining the impact of the implementation of Standard 5: Comprehensive Care on patient outcomes. The implementation of our localised response to the standard, the CARE record, resulted in a decrease in the overall rate of reported falls and pressure injuries when compared with pre-implementation. The difference in rates between the two time frames remained statistically significant even after controlling for variables including age, gender and primary language other than English. There was also an increase in reported incidents of delirium, malnutrition, aggression and violence and suicide and self-harm when compared with the pre-implementation timeframe, which was expected as these were not routinely reported prior to the implementation of the CARE record. Taken together, these findings provide real-world lessons about the introduction of mandated standards. The CARE record appears to have heightened staff awareness and vigilance regarding all HACs, leading to both improvements in the implementation of targeted preventative strategies and more consistent reporting. At the same time, the findings highlight opportunities to refine the CARE record itself, draw attention to implementation challenges such as workload pressures and coordination across disciplines.

There is limited published research evaluating structured methods to operationalise Standard 5 of the NSQHS standards in the Australian context. Existing studies have largely examined implementation at a system or organisational level rather than through specific tools with measured outcomes. A national survey of clinicians found that although many felt confident delivering comprehensive care, insufficient organisational support, training and resources were significant barriers to effective implementation.39 Accreditation data from 495 assessments of health service organisations also demonstrates the challenges associated with operationalising the comprehensive care standard, with 15% of organisations not meeting all the requirements and 12% required to implement further action in order to meet the requirements.40 Similarly, the ACSQHC’s own review of the new standard highlighted wide variation in how guidance materials were used, with many services struggling to embed the Standard into routine workflows.41 These studies align with our own findings from the staff surveys and suggest that while the policy framework is broadly recognised, there is a significant burden on staff in the implementation of the standard. It also highlights that there remains limited evidence assessing the impact of a structured, ward-level implementation method on patient outcomes.

Our study identified an unexpected increase in the incidence of falls with an ISR 1 or 2 rating, consistent with the reported increase in the incidence of coded ICD-10-AM falls. This may be an indication of potentially unpreventable falls, where falls prevention practices have been unsuccessful in mitigating risk. Implementing best practice falls prevention must consider priorities for the patient and the need to ensure patients remain mobile.42 These falls likely reflect the challenge of balancing these needs along with more complex risk factors and underlying conditions that may lead to a higher risk of ISR 1 and 2 incidents.

It is also possible that the implementation of the CARE record resulted in unintended consequences, including alterations in falls patterns and related injuries. These effects could potentially be attributed to a shift away from emphasising the clinical judgement and critical thinking of nurses, towards a more task-oriented approach focused on mandatory documentation completion. This shift may have meant nurses spent more time focused away from the bedside, thus inadvertently missing opportunities to identify individuals susceptible to falls at the bedside beyond those indicated by risk scores, and to ensure the appropriate application of mitigation strategies, such as employing high falls risk interventions for patients deemed low risk on a risk assessment scale. Furthermore, the increased paperwork load associated with the CARE record may have restricted opportunities for patient observations and assessment of changing needs outside of the risk assessment schedule.43 Direct interaction with patients is essential for identifying potential risks and addressing needs. Experienced nurses may pick up on subtle cues and factors that might not be covered by standardised falls risk assessments; recent research suggests that the clinical judgement of an experienced nurse is at least as reliable, if not more reliable, than a risk screen in reducing falls.43

Staff perceptions of the CARE record revealed mixed feelings on the impact on communication, collaboration and the utility of risk assessment tools. There were concerns about multidisciplinary teamwork and goal-setting, with fewer staff reporting that they felt there was effective collaboration post implementation, and even fewer felt that the CARE record helped them to identify individual patient care goals. These findings suggest that while the CARE record seems to benefit some forms of communication (particularly between patients and allied health professionals), it falls short in fostering multidisciplinary collaboration or in helping nursing staff identify individual care goals. Again, this may be due to the perceived increase in workload which may have left clinicians inadequate time to collaborate with other members of staff or to spend enough time with patients setting care goals. This finding is in line with the research of Xiong and team, who identified heavy workload and lack of multidisciplinary collaboration as key barriers to implementing comprehensive care standards.5 Since initial implementation, effort has been made to further streamline the CARE record and minimise redundancy wherever possible to ensure that it adds tangible value to clinical workflows and patient outcomes.

Staff reported variable ease of use for the risk assessment tools in the CARE record, with STRATIFY and the Braden Scale rated as the easiest to complete, while the CAM and the C-SSRS received lower ratings. This variation may be related to a lack of familiarity with the CAM and C-SSRS, as opposed to the STRATIFY and Braden Scale which were in use prior to the implementation of the CARE record. This may also explain some of the low rates of compliance with actioning the findings of these risk screens into management plans. This evaluation highlights that there may be a need for some additional training to increase staff confidence in the use of the newly implemented tools.

The implementation of the CARE record has provided valuable insights into its impact on communication, collaboration and risk assessment within healthcare settings. With many health services transitioning to EMRs, it is important to consider how tools like the CARE record can integrate into or evolve within digital environments. Future research should focus on evaluating the transferability of the CARE record’s principles and functionality within EMR systems. EMRs have the potential to streamline documentation, reduce duplication and improve access to patient information, but their success in fostering multidisciplinary teamwork and individualised care planning remains an area for further exploration. Research should include whether the structured format and risk assessment tools of the CARE record can be adapted to EMRs to maintain or enhance their utility in improving communication and care coordination. Additionally, understanding the potential for EMRs to address some of the challenges identified with the CARE record, such as workload and duplication, will be important for optimising patient-centred care in digitally advanced health systems. In addition, future research should also consider using co-design methodologies to further develop and refine the CARE record, ensuring it remains user-centred, efficient and responsive to patient needs. Such work should also incorporate more rigorous evaluation designs, including randomised or multi-site studies, to establish generalisability and inform sustainable scale-up.

Conclusion

The implementation of the CARE record may have improved the identification of some types of HACs and it is appealing from a management compliance perspective; however, its role as part of broader attempts to improve quality and safety needs further development. The focus should shift towards achieving meaningful outcomes for patients without creating unnecessary workload burden on staff. Without this shift, there is a risk of defaulting to blaming staff for the ineffective use of a tool that may not be fully fit for purpose. Efforts should be directed towards ensuring the tool aligns with the goals of care delivery and meets the needs of both staff and patients.
