Multi-omics analysis reveals the differences in meat quality and flavor among three breeds of white goats with geographical indication of China
Authors: Miaoqiang Zhang, Lei Chen, Haiyan Liu, Wenshuai Si, Tao Feng, Bing Bai, Yuexia Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The goat meat with geographical indication has attracted the market attention because of its unique quality and culture attribute. This study employed multi-omics techniques to reveal the variations in meat quality, metabolites profiles, lipids profiles, and aroma compounds among Chongming white goat (CM), Yichang white goat (YC), and Xiaoxian white goat (XX). The YC exhibited lower intramuscular fat (IMF) but better meat color than CM and XX. Metabolomics and lipidomics results revealed differential regulation of amino acids, purine, glycerophospholipid, and glycerolipid metabolism among the three breeds of white goats. Based on OAV (odor active values, OAV), 19 volatiles might be the key aromas of white goats. Additionally, Mantel test indicated that variation in the 32 molecules influenced the aroma composition among three breeds of white goat meat. These results provide comprehensive insights into goat meat quality and flavor, promoting theoretical development for breed optimization and meat production.

Introduction

Goat meat has become increasingly preferred by Chinese consumers, who concurrently place greater emphasis on meat quality attributes. Geographical indication (GI) is generally recognized as a symbol of superior goat meat quality, signifying correlates between product characteristics and the unique natural and cultural factors of a specific region (Rabadán et al., 2021). Fu et al. (2024) reported that Chinese consumers are more inclined to pay for beef labeled with geographical indication. In China, GI products are regulated under the Trademark Law and the Provisions on the Protection of Geographical Indication Products. At present, the native Chinese white goat population encompasses numerous breeds distributed across multiple provinces. The Chongming White Goat (CM) is native to Chongming Island (Shanghai), the Yichang White Goat (YC) to Yichang's mountainous areas (Hubei), and the Xiaoxian White Goat (XX) to Xiaoxian County of the Huang-Huai Plain (Anhui). Owing to their excellent meat quality and unique genetic resources, CM, YC, and XX have been recognized as GI products. However, the quality characteristics of goat meat with different GI have not been systematically studied. Therefore, conducting systematic investigations on the meat quality and flavor of white goats with GI can highlight the special characteristics of different geographical indication labeled goat meat.

Meat quality, as a multifactorial index, including flavor, Intramuscular fat (IMF), protein, pH, cooking loss and meat color (Hou et al., 2023). The IMF concentration significantly affects flavor, tenderness, and release of aromas in goat meat (Li, Yang, et al., 2022). Consequently, comprehensive profiling of lipid composition in goat meat represents critical research for premium meat production. The pH is a crucial index that influence sensory and processing in goat meat (Farouk et al., 2019). The flavor is a significant index for consumers to purchase meat (Han, Zhang, Fauconnier, & Mi, 2020). During meat cooking, the complex chemical reactions between water-soluble metabolites and lipids drive aromas generation. These aromas are mainly aldehydes, alcohols, acids, ketones, esters, sulfide and heterocyclic compounds. Sohail et al. (2022) reported that the water-soluble metabolites of meat are responsible for the basic aroma of meat and the IMF is the reason for the Species-unique aroma. IMF generated over half of the aroma compounds in cooked meat. Zhang et al. (2021) suggested that phospholipids (PCs) have a higher proportion of unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs) and are more oxidized compared to triglycerides (TGs). Furthermore, the oxidative capacity of fatty acids is also associated with the classes of lipids, as well as the binding sites of fatty acids (Li, Al-Dalali, et al., 2022). The production of meat aroma is dependent on the presence of flavor precursors, which exhibit variations in content and composition among different goats. However, there is a lack of comprehensive investigations on metabolites, lipids and aromas of different goat meat with geographical indications.

In terms of meat quality and flavor, volatilomics, metabolomics and lipidomics have been widely used to depict the disparities (Wang, Wang, et al., 2024) . Compared to GC–MS, the GC × GC-TOF-MS has superior separation ability and compound identification capacity in the characterization of aroma compounds. Li et al. (2021) applied GC × GC-TOF-MS to investigate the volatile profile in dry-cured hams from different areas. Hou et al. (2023) used metabolomics and lipidomics to evaluate the metabolite and lipid profile of Laiwu and Yorkshire pigs, providing valuable insights for the formation of pork flavor. Integrating multiple omics technologies is beneficial for conducting comprehensive research on meat quality and the formation of flavor (Hwang et al., 2023). Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate meat quality characteristics, volatiles, and metabolic profiles among three white goat breeds, with objectives to identify key metabolic pathways influencing meat flavor and quality, and to explore the relationship between flavor precursors and characteristic aroma compounds.

Materials and methods

Animal samples collection

In this study, all experimental procedures involving goats were conducted in strict compliance with the Animal Welfare Guidelines of the Animal Ethics Procedures and Guidelines of the People's Republic of China and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai Academy of Agricultural Sciences, China (Permit ID: SAASPZ0522048). Animal handling protocols adhered to international standards for ethical treatment of research subjects. The CM, YC, and XX goats were obtained from Shanghai (Shanghai, China), Yichang (Hubei province, China), Xiao xian (Anhui province, China), respectively. Different breeds of castrated male white goats were randomly selected. All goats were weaned at 50 days of age and fed until 12 months old. Each goat was housed in separate unit (1.0 × 2.0 m2). All goats were fed the same diet and water. In brief, the diet of white goat was mainly silage feed, supplemented with concentrated feed as a supplement. Concentrated feed is shown in Table S1. Six similar weight white goats were randomly selected from each feeding group and fasted overnight for a period of 16 h prior to slaughter. All goats were weighted (31.42 ± 1.83 kg for the CM group, 30.65 ± 1.46 kg for the XX group, 29.67 ± 1.76 kg for the YC group) before slaughter. All goats were stunned using electrical methods, euthanized, and slaughtered by qualified personnel in accordance with standardized commercial protocols. Then, all goats were exsanguinated, skinned, eviscerated, and split along the midline. A 100 g sample (longissimus thoracis between the 1th and 6th rib) was excised from the left side of each goat carcass, with all visible external fat and connective tissues removed. Finally, the sample was rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. Aliquots of the samples were stored at −20 °C for the determination of meat quality parameters.

The analysis of meat quality parameters in goat meat

Six white goats from each group were randomly selected for the determination of quality traits. The pH meter (HI8424, Hanna, Italy) was used to analyze meat pH after 24 h of slaughter. Before the measurement, the pH meter was calibrated in standard buffers (Shanghai Institute of Measurement and Testing Technology, Shanghai, China). The colorimeter (CR-400, Konica Minolta, Japan) was used to measure the meat color index ((lightness (L*), redness (a*) and yellowness (b*)) of three different positions of each sample after 30 min of bloom time. Before meat color testing, the colorimeter was calibrated by the standard whiteboard. Notably, the pH and color in meat were measured at local standard laboratory. To determine cooking loss, each sample with retort pouch were placed in thermostat water-bath (HH-2, Ronghua Instrument Manufacturing, Jiangsu province, China) until the sample central temperature reached 70 °C. Once the sample has reached to room temperature, any moisture on sample surface should be removed. Then, the sample before and after cooking was weighed, and the percentage of weight loss was calculated. The moisture of sample was determined according to the China National Standard Determination of moisture in foods (GB 5009.3–2016). The Automatic Kjeldahl Apparatus (Kjeltec8400, FOSS, Denmark) was used to measure the content of protein according to the China National Standard (GB 5009.5–2016). According to the China National Standard (GB 5009.6–2016), the content of IMF was measured using the automatic Soxhlet extractor (Soxtec8000, FOSS, Denmark).

Metabolites extraction and analysis

The sample was ground evenly with liquid nitrogen and subsequently weighed 20 mg of the sample into tube with 500 μL of the extracting solution (70% methanol (MeOH)). The mixture was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 8 min. After being placed in ice for 20 min, the samples were centrifuged at 4 °C (12,000 rpm) for 10 min. Next, 400 μL of the supernatant fluid were collected and placed in room temperature for 25 min. Finally, the sample was then centrifuged at 4 °C (12,000 rpm) for 3 min. The 200 μL of the supernatant was collected for analysis.

The samples extraction was analyzed using the ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) (LC-30 A, Shimadzu, Japan) combined with Triple TOF MS (TripleTOF 6600+, AB SCIEX, USA). An HSS T3 column (Waters ACQUITY Premier, 1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) was used to separation. The solvent consisted of A (0.1% formic acid in pure water) and B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). The initial column temperature was maintained at 40 °C. The initial flow velocity was 0.4 mL/min. The elution procedure was as follows: 0–2 min, 95% A-80% A (A/B, v/v); 2–5 min, 80% A-40% A; 5–7.5 min, 40% A-1% A; 7.5–9 min, 1% A - 95% A, 9–10 min, 95% A. The injection volume was 4 μL.

The MS parameters were as follows: Ion Spray Voltage was sat at 5000 V (positive) and − 4000 V (negative), respectively; Temperature, 550 °C (positive)and 450 °C (negative); declustering potential (DP), 60 V, or − 60 V; Ion Source Gas1 (psi), 50; Ion Source Gas2 (psi), 60. The MS1 TOF masses scan range was m/z 50–10,000 Da, and the MS2 TOF Masses scan range was m/z 25–10,000 Da. The MS1 Accumulation time and MS2 Accumulation time were 0.2 s and 0.04 s, respectively. TOF MS scan parameters included: mass range, 50–1000 Da; accumulation time, 200 ms; dynamic background subtraction, enabled. Product ion scan parameters were: mass range, 25–1000 Da; accumulation time, 40 ms; collision energy, 30 V (positive) or -30 V (negative); collision energy spread, 15; resolution, UNIT; charge state, 1; intensity threshold, 100 cps; isotopes excluded within 4 Da; mass tolerance, 50 ppm; maximum candidate ions per cycle, 18.

The original data was converted into m/z Extensible Markup Language format by Proteo Wizard. Next, the XCMS (version 2.1) was employed for the purposes of peak extraction, alignment, and retention time correction. Peaks with detection rate lower than 50% were discarded for each group of samples. Finally, metabolites were identified by the Self built database with available authentic standards, public database and met DNA.

Lipids extraction and analysis

The goat meat was ground evenly with liquid nitrogen and subsequently weighted 20 mg of samples into tube with 60 μL of the pure methanol. Then, the mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C (12,000 rpm) for 1 min and subsequently homogenized (30 Hz) the samples with the steel ball for 20 s. Next, 800 μL of the extraction solvent (methyl tert-butyl methyl ether (MTBE): MeOH = 3:1, v/v) was added. After whirling the mixture for 20 min, 200 μL of the pure water was added and the resulting mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C (12,000 rpm) for 10 min. Subsequently, the supernatant was collected evaporated using a vacuum concentrator. Then, the dry sample was reconstituted with the 200 μL of solution (acetonitrile: isopropanol = 1:1, v/v). Next, the collection was centrifuged at 12000 rpm (4 °C) for 30 s and collected for analysis.

The analysis was performed using a UPLC combined with electrospray ionization–triple quadrupole-linear ion trap–MS/MS (UPLC-MS/MS) system (UPLC, ExionLC AD; MS/MS, QTRAP 6500+, AB SCIEX, USA). The parameters of UPLC were as follows: the C30 column (2.6 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm, Thermo Accucore™) was used to separation. The initial column temperature was 45 °C. The initial flow velocity was 0.35 mL/min. The solvent consisted of A (10 mmol/L ammonium formate in acetonitrile/water (60/40, v/v)) and B (10 mmol/L ammonium formate in acetonitrile/isopropanol (10/90, v/v)). The gradient elution program was set as follows: 0-2 min, 80%–70% (A/B, v/v) solvent A; 2-4 min, 70% A-40% A; 4-9 min, 40% A-15% A; 9-14 min, 15% A-10% A; 14–15.5 min, 10% A-5% A; 15.5–17.3 min, 5 %A; 17.3–17.5 min, 5% A-80% A; 17.5-20 min, 80% A. The injection volume was 2 μL.

The Ion Spray Voltage was sat at 5500 V (positive) and − 4500 V (negative), respectively. The source temperature was set at 500 °C. Ion Source Gas1 (psi) was set at 45 psi and Ion Source Gas2 (psi) was set at 55 psi. The curtain gas was 35 psi and the collision gas was set to medium. Triple quadrupole scans were acquired via multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) experiments. The DP and collision energy (CE) after optimization were used to set individual MRM transitions. The original data was analyzed by software Analyst 1.6.3. The MultiQuant software was utilized for the purposes of peak extraction, alignment, and retention time (RT) correction. The lipid molecules were identified through utilization of the self-built database according to RT, molecular weight of parent ion and characteristic fragment ion. Lipids were quantified by the MRM mode.

Volatile compounds analysis

The samples were incubated in water until the central temperature of sample reached 75 °C, and subsequently ground in liquid nitrogen. The 5.0 g of the goat meat sample was weighted into a 20 mL headspace vial. Then, 20 μL of internal standard (1,2-dichlorobenzene in methanol, 10 mg/L) was added into the headspace vial. The headspace bottle was incubated at 70 °C for 30 min. The volatiles were extracted at 70 °C for 30 min using SPME-Arrow (DVB/PDMS, 110 μm × 20 mm, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and desorbed into gas chromatography (GC) injector for 5 min at 250 °C.

According to the (Wang, Gao, et al., 2024), the GC × GC-TOF-MS conditions were modified as follows. The volatiles were performed using GC × GC-TOF-MS system (Agilent 8890 GC, USA; EI-TOF-MS, 0620, Hexin, Guangzhou, China) equipped with DB-5 MS column (0.25 μm, 30 m × 0.25 mm, Agilent, USA) and DB-17 column (0.8 μm, 1.00 m × 0.18 mm, Agilent, USA). The conditions of GC analysis were as follows: the high-purity helium (99.99 %) at a flow rate 2.0 mL/min served as carrier gas. The initial column temperature was set at 40 °C, and then increased to 280 °C at 4 °C/min, subsequently held at 280 °C for 5 min. The EI parameters were as follows: the temperature and ionization energy of EI were sat at 230 °C and 70 eV, respectively; MS scan range was m/z 30–450 amu. Spectra were conducted by the full scan mode with an acquisition rate of 101 spectra/s. The Canvas software was used to analyze the 2D data. The retention index (RI) values were calculated by n-alkanes (C7 ∼ C30) under the same conditions. The volatiles were identified according to NIST 20 database, which compared their RI, 1st and 2nd dimension retention time (RT) and mass spectra. The volatiles were quantified through the calculation of the ratio of the target compound peak area to the internal standard. The odor activity values (OAV) of meat aroma were calculated using the equation, which is the concentration of the aromas divided by their odor threshold. Aroma compounds with OAV > 1 were considered the key odors (Yang et al., 2023).

Statistical analysis

In this study, the data values were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The GraphPad Prism 9.0 was utilized to conduct the bar chart. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed by the IBM SPSS statistics 27 (IBM, USA). Post hoc statistical power (1 - β) was calculated using the G*Power (version3.1.9.7), based on the observed effect sizes, sample size (n = 6, per group), and α = 0.05. The principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminate analysis (OPLS-DA) were conducted using SIMCA software (Umetrics, Sweden). The network diagram was generated by Cytoscape (version 3.10). The pathway analysis of differential Metabolites and lipids were performed using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 platform (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca).

Results

The meat quality traits in three types of white goats

The meat quality of white goat was measured in this study (Fig.1). The results showed that there was no significant difference (P > 0.05) in pH, cooking loss and moisture content among the three types of white goats (Fig. 1A-C). The IMF content of CM and XX was significantly higher than YC, while significant difference was not observed between CM and XX samples (Fig. 1D). Additionally, the post hoc power of CM vs. YC, XX vs. YC, and CM vs. XX were 0.91, 0.84, and 0.08, respectively. The high power (power > 0.8) of CM vs. YC and XX vs. YC suggested that the results were authenticity and repeatability. The non-significant result (P > 0.05) and low power (power < 0.2) of CM vs. XX prevents definitive conclusions regarding the presence or absence of an effect. The protein content of CM and YC samples was significantly higher (P < 0.05) than that of XX sample (Fig. 1E). There was no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05) in lightness (L⁎-value) and yellowness among three samples (b⁎-value). Aroma compounds analysis in three types of white goats

In this study, the GC × GC-TOF-MS was performed for aroma compounds analysis. A total of 140 volatiles were identified among three breeds of white goats (Table S7), including 49 hydrocarbons, 34 aldehydes, 18 alcohols, 13 esters, 12 ketones, 5 acids, 4 furans, 2 lactones, 1 sulfide, 1 pyridine, and 1 unclassified flavor compound. The proportion of different class volatiles were displayed in Fig. 5A. It was observed that Aldehydes, alcohols, and acids were the primary aromas in all samples. Sulfide concentrations were significantly higher (P < 0.05) in the YC group than in the other two groups (Fig. 5B). Although intergroup comparisons of aldehyde content (CM vs. XX, CM vs. YC, and XX vs. YC) were not statistical significance (P > 0.05), post hoc power analysis showed corresponding power values of 0.09, 0.34, and 0.11, respectively. Given the low power observed, the present study cannot conclude the absence of differences in aldehyde content among the three sample groups. Compared to XX group, the furan concentrations significantly increased in the CM group (P < 0.05). Ketone content significantly elevated (P < 0.05) in the CM group. Compared to YC group, alcohol content significantly increased (P < 0.05) in both CM and XX groups. In terms of concentration of hydrocarbons, XX group exhibited significantly higher content (P < 0.05) than the CM and YC groups. We constructed the network to depict the between the aromas and their sensory description, based on the sensory odor of each volatile compounds (Fig. 5C, Table S7). Based on OAV, 30 aromas with OAV >1 were identified (Table S7). A radar chart based on 30 compounds with OAV > 1 (Fig. 5D) was conducted to reveal the differences in odor among three breeds of white goats. These results suggested that the fatty and floral in the both CM and XX groups were stronger than YC group. The almond of odor in the XX group was weaker than the CM vs. YC, while the sweet of odor in the CM was seemingly stronger than XX vs. YC. Three breeds of white goats exhibit analogous sensory odor characteristics in the citrus, green, mushroom, fruit, grass and meaty. According to these results, the CM group exhibit strongest predicted sensory profile. A total of 19 aromas with OAV > 1were identified in all groups, while 11 aromas were identified in one or two groups. The most potent odors were (E, E)-2, 4-decadienal (OAV > 1100), (Z)-4-decenal (OAV > 700), and dimethyl trisulfide (OAV > 600). However, the OAV of (E, E)-2,4-decadienal and dimethyl trisulfide exhibited big variation in different breeds white goat. It was evident that the concentrations of aroma compounds exhibited increased or decreased trends in different groups. These results suggested a correlation between odor variation and changes in the concentrations of aroma compounds among the three breeds of white goats. Correlation analysis between aromas, metabolites, and lipids

Meat aroma is generated via chemical reactions involving flavor precursors. The preceding results suggest potential interconnections between metabolite and lipid alterations and aroma variations. Therefore, the correlation analysis was conducted between the selected metabolites, lipids and key difference aromas (Fig. 6). As shown in Fig. 6, the thickness of the line represented the Mantel's correlation coefficient (Mantel's r), while the color denoted the statistical significance (Mantel's p). The yellow and green thick lines represented statistically significant correlations between different variables. Overlap results of differential metabolites, lipids (Fig. S5), and aromas was selected in each comparison group. As shown in Fig. 6, the selected aromas were significantly correlated with most of free amino acids (FAAs), small peptide and lipids (Table S8). For example, (E)-2-octenal, heptanal, undecanal, (E)-2-decenal, as fatty aldehydes, was significantly correlated with lipids such as PE (P-18:2_20:5), PG (18,2_18:3) and CAR (C18: 2-OH). A significant correlation was observed between dimethyl trisulfide and methionine. Similarly, 1-Octen-3-ol correlated significantly with TG (15, 0_18, 1_20: 4) and TG (16, 0_18, 1_20: 4). Discussion

Goat meat is widely consumed in China owing to its palatability and absence of religious restrictions. This study conducted comprehensive analysis of the lipid profile, metabolite profile and aromas, among three breeds of white goats. In terms of meat quality traits, the pH value ranged from 5.70 to 6.10 (Fig. 1A), which is within the acceptable parameters (Dhanda et al., 2003). We found that CM and XX goats exhibited significantly higher IMF level than YC goat, while XX goat suggested significantly higher protein levels than CM and YC goats (Fig. 1D and 1E). Higher IMF contributes to juicy, tender and flavor in meat (Realini et al., 2021). Furthermore, the changes of IMF concentration were related to lipids profiles variation (Li, Yang, et al., 2022). Our present research also indicated that TG, PA, and PE levels were reduced in the YC group (Fig. 4B). These results supported that the IMF variations principally governed by the TG, PC and PE levels. The YC goat exhibited higher redness than CM goat, suggesting its meat color may confer greater market acceptance (Chen et al., 2024).

Water-soluble metabolites contribute to the meat flavor formation. Metabolomics was used to characterize the metabolites profile of meat. The OPLS-DA results revealed distinct metabolite profiles among breeds. It was noteworthy that amino acids and its metabolites, nucleotides and its metabolites, and lipids were identified as important flavor precursors (Fig. 2A). Amino acids and peptides serve dual roles as both taste-active compounds and precursors for aroma generation (Liang et al., 2022). In this study, amino acids and its metabolites were identified as polypeptides, small peptides, amino acids, and amino acid derivatives. Consequently, KEGG analysis of differential metabolites identified seven significantly enriched pathways (Fig. 3D). The abundance of most amino acids and its metabolites were increased in the XX group, involving in cysteine and methionine metabolism, taurine and hypotaurine metabolism and alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism. Methionine, as sulfur-containing amino acids, generates the sulfur-containing compounds with meat aroma characteristics via the Maillard reaction (Zhao, Wang, et al., 2017). Increased concentrations of methionine in meat intensify the Strecker degradation process, thereby leading to the accumulation of the sulfur-containing odor compound dimethyl trisulfide in cooked meat. L-Glutamic acid, a representative umami amino acid, synergistically enhances meat umami taste when combined with alanine (Yin et al., 2022). Taurine, as an essential amino acid crucial for enhancing the nutritional value and flavor of goat meat, inhibits lipid oxidation and reduces the generation of lipid oxidation odor. (Han, Zhang, Yan, et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2023). Taurocholic acid palys an key role in the glycolipid metabolism (e.g., taurocholic promotes the absorption of lipids) and cholesterol homeostasis (Wang et al., 2023). Pyruvic acid, a central glycolytic node, serves as an important meat biomarker potentially associated with cooking loss (Xu et al., 2024). In this study, pyruvic acid also involved in multiple amino acid metabolic pathways. Glucosamine, which is an endogenous monosaccharide, is synthesized from glucose by means of the hexosamine pathway. It serves as a crucial precursor for the synthesis of a variety of large molecules, including glycoproteins and proteoglycans (Liu et al., 2024). Xanthine and xanthosine, intermediate metabolites in purine metabolism, play vital roles in cellular energy pathways and signal transduction (Lu et al., 2022). Purine metabolites also consider as the crucial components of meat flavor (Wang et al., 2023). In addition, we also identified several lysophospholipid molecules (Fig. 3E). Previous study demonstrate that lysophospholipid modulate cellular energy metabolism and adipose deposition by activating the protein kinase C and the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (Zhao et al., 2025). KEGG analysis identified seven significantly enriched pathways (Fig. 3D). Glycerophospholipid metabolism, the primary pathway for GP synthesis, play a key role in the structure and function of cell membranes and fat deposition (Zhao et al., 2025). UFAs generated via the hydrolysis of GP constitute a key source for aroma formation in meat. Taurine and hypotaurine pathway directly regulates the synthesis of taurine and are key factor in morphological and functional maintenance (Miyazaki, 2024). Purine derivatives (xanthine/xanthosine) modulated energy metabolism while contributing to umami accumulation (Pedley & Benkovic, 2017). Overall, these metabolic pathways appear to collectively modulate energy metabolism, adipogenesis, and cellular homeostasis, potentially contributing to observed interbreed flavor variations.

Lipids serve as flavor precursors, generating volatile compounds through degradation reactions, and also as solvents modulating aroma release dynamics. In this study, a total of 988 lipid molecules were identified using lipidomics, the majority of which were classified as GP, GL, and FA (Fig. 4A). We found that the main classes of differential lipid were GP, GL and FA among three comparison groups (Fig. 4B). The dynamic changes of GL were related to the growth of white goats, including TG, diglycerides (DG), monoglycerides (MG), and monogalactosyldiacylglycerols (MGDG). Notably, TG was the major types of GL in the differential lipids (Fig. S3). Compared to phospholipid, TG, as neutral lipid, exhibit a low degree of unsaturation in fatty acids and a relatively minor contribution to the formation of meat aroma (Du et al., 2021). Phospholipids are the primary constituents of the cell membrane, and also participate in the regulation of meat quality and flavor. Within the differential GP molecules, PC and PE were the principal subclasses (Fig. S4). Cao et al. (2024) reported that the ratio of PC/PE influences lipid droplet formation. Similarly, phosphatidylinositol (PI), the main phospholipid of endoplasmic reticulum, has been demonstrated to enhance the formation of lipid droplets when present in increased quantities (Hou et al., 2023). Notably, PC and PE contain a high proportion of UFAs. Phospholipid supplementation significantly increased volatile compound content compared to phospholipid-deficient samples, with different phospholipid types inducing distinct volatile profiles (Zhang et al., 2021). Although many phospholipid molecules may contribute to aroma, certain PE species have been pinpointed as important odor precursors (Wu and Wang, 2019). Lysophosphatides (e.g., LPC, LPE, LPG, LPI) are one of the products of GP degradation. Zhang et al. (2023) reported that LPG, LPI and LPS were degradation products of PG, PI and PS, respectively. Acylcarnitines (CAR) are produced from β-oxidation of the long-chain fatty acids (Zhang et al., 2023). Overall, the variations in TG, PC, and PE may underlie the differences in IMF level among the three breeds of white goat meat.

A total of 140 volatiles were identified using GC × GC-TOF-MS. Aldehydes, alcohols, and ketones constituted the predominant compound classes. The concentrations of aldehydes, alcohols, ketones, furans, and sulfides were affected by the metabolite and lipid abundance. Notably, the YC sample, despite exhibiting the lowest fat content, demonstrated the highest aldehyde concentration (Fig. 5). This results agree with previous studies that found the IMF retains or prolongs the release of lipophilic aroma compounds (Frank et al., 2017; Li, Yang, et al., 2022). Nevertheless, this point not apply to all aroma compounds. Zhao et al. (2017) reported that (E)-2-heptenal and (E, E)-2,4-decadienal inhibited the reaction of Amadori compounds and reduced the generation of sulfur-containing compounds. The concentration of individual aromas is contingent upon its lipophilicity, the level and composition of flavor precursors, and interactions with other aromas. These findings suggest IMF may inhibit the release of specific aroma compounds, corroborating previous reports (Frank et al., 2012; Li, Yang, et al., 2022). Based on OAV, 30 aromas with OAV >1 were identified (Table S7). Among them, 19 aromas were existed in all groups, which considered the key odors of white goats (Table S7). These aromas were the aliphatic aldehydes of C6 ∼ C10 carbons, 1-Octen-3-ol, sulfur-containing and oxygen-containing compounds. Aliphatic aldehydes, with low odor thresholds, typically provide green, fruity, floral, fatty, and sweet notes characteristic of cooked meat (Li, Al-Dalali, et al., 2022). Furthermore, certain aldehydes (e.g., nonanal) exhibit unpleasant odors at high concentrations (Li, Yang, et al., 2022). In general, saturated fatty aldehydes are generated from the oxidative degradation of UFAs, such as oleic acid (18:1), linoleic acid (18:2) (Sohail et al., 2022). In brief, fatty acids generate peroxides by removing hydrogen radicals from alkyl groups, and then the peroxides break down to form aroma compounds. Merlo et al. (2021) reported that the degradation of 8- and 11-hydroperoxides of oleic acid can produce to octanal and decanal, respectively. It is reported that (E, E)-2,4-decadienal is generated through the oxidation of linoleic acid and inhibits the sulfur-containing compounds formation (Sohail et al., 2022). Notably, this phenomenon was observed in XX and YC (Table S7). 1-Octen-3-ol usually exhibit the mushroom odor note and is generated from arachidonic acid (20:4), which is considered as the off-flavor of meat (Chen et al., 2021). Significantly, we found that 1-Octen-3-ol was significantly correlated with lipid molecules containing arachidonic acid branched chains (TG (15:0_18:1_20:4) and TG (16:0_18:1_20:4), Fig.6). Sulfur-containing compounds impart the meaty note, which are generated from the reaction of cysteine or glutathione with reducing-sugar (Sohail et al., 2022). The significantly higher sulfide content in the YC group compared to CM and XX groups indicates superior meat odor intensity, potentially attributable to elevated reducing sugar levels (Fan et al., 2018). 2-pentyl-Furan, contributing a fruity note, is generated by the oxidation degradation reaction of linoleic acid (Sohail et al., 2022), or thermal degradation of thiamine (Li, Al-Dalali, et al., 2022). In addition, 9 aromas (heptanal, (E)-2-octenal, (E)-2-decenal, undecanal, 1-hexanol, 1-heptanol, 1-octen-3-ol, 1-nonanol, dimethyl trisulfide) were screened to distinguish the three breeds of white goat meat. We explored the correlation between key aromas, metabolites, and lipids (Fig. 6). A significant correlation was observed between aldehydes, alcohols, and lipids. For example, according to the study (Yang et al., 2018), hexanol is generated by the oxidation reaction of C18:1 or C16:1. As shown in Fig. 6, the PE, PC, and TG had the unsaturated fatty acid branched chain. Collectively, variations in 32 molecules and IMF content may underlie the observed aroma differences among three breeds of white goat meat. Frankly, it should be noted that these results of this study provide preliminary evidence, and further validation through larger-scale investigations is required due to limitations in sample size (n = 6 per group).

Conclusion

This study investigated the meat quality, metabolites, lipids profiles, and volatile compounds among three breeds of white goats with geographical indication. Three breeds of white goat meat exhibited differences in metabolites, lipids, and aroma compounds. The results of meat quality indicated that YC exhibited lower IMF content and had better meat color compared to CM and XX. The CM and YC had a higher protein content than XX. The results of lipidomics and metabolomics suggested that variations in lipid and metabolite profiles could influence aromas characteristic among three breeds of white goats. Additionally, we speculated that variations in TG, PC and PE may affect the IMF deposition. The correlation analysis suggested that the aromas variation of three breeds of white goats may be related to the levels of 32 molecules. These insights may contribute to comprehensive understanding of the meat quality and flavor characteristic in different goat meat with geographical indication, thereby providing a scientific basis for breed optimization and meat production.
