Heart Failure sub-phenotyping and in-hospital and 28-day mortality prediction based on mean arterial pressure trajectory modeling
Authors: Patrick E Gelbach, Roger A Edwards, Yukiko Fukuda, Joe Alexander
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute decompensated heart failure patients follow a broad range of clinical pathways during hospitalization. Efficient patient sub-phenotyping based on early in-hospital trajectories, particularly when paired with clinical measurements, may inform optimal treatment strategies using digital twins.MethodsUsing the MIMIC-IV database, we selected patients admitted to the ICU from 2008 to 2022. Patients were categorized into three distinct classes based on changes in mean arterial pressure (MAP) during the first 24 h of hospitalization, using Group-Based Trajectory Modeling (GBTM). Additional measures (including vital signs, serum biomarkers, hemodynamic data, and respiratory metrics) were then used as inputs to logistic regression and random forest analyses to identify important covariates associated with in-hospital and 28-day mortality.ResultsA total of 4817 patients had sufficient data for analysis and were categorized into three trajectory classes: 2737 with relatively steady MAP (Class 1), 721 with increasing MAP (Class 2), and 1361 with decreasing MAP (Class 3). Overall mortality did not differ significantly among groups; however, variables associated with in-hospital and 28-day mortality varied across classes.ConclusionThis data-driven, trajectory-based sub-phenotyping approach of changes in MAP during the first 24 h of a hospital stay can characterize the heterogeneity of mortality risk among heart failure patients and can inform digital-twin–based scenario modeling to evaluate targeted management strategies for critically ill heart failure patients.Keywords: Group-based trajectory modeling, Heart failure, Mean arterial pressure, Phenotypes, MIMIC-IV, Machine learning

Background

Heart failure is one of the most prevalent and resource-intensive causes of hospitalization, and patients admitted to the ICU and CCU have substantially higher mortality rates than the overall HF population. Extensive previous research aimed at identifying heart failure risk factors and developing novel prognostic tools have provided valuable insights. However, subgroups of patients' outcomes have not always been as adequately predicted as they could be. Development of empirically-driven sub-phenotypes offer opportunities to capture the time-dependent patterns that accompany or precede clinical deterioration and death [1], [2], [3]. Better temporal characterization and dynamic-focused sub-phenotyping of heart failure patients is therefore a promising avenue for improving patient care.

Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP) is one of the most frequently collected and clinically utilized vital signs in the ICU, as it is central to perfusion physiology, easily interpretable at the bedside, and has been consistently associated with shock, severe organ injury, and mortality [3], [4]. Prior studies show that MAP trajectory over time in the ICU is linked to patient outcomes, but the use of trajectory-focused categorization of HF subgroups based on MAP is currently under-utilized [4]. Group-based Trajectory Modeling (GBTM) is a statistical, data-driven, framework for identifying those subpopulations whose temporal profiles follow similar patterns. GBTM-driven classification of patients based on 24-h changes in MAP has previously been shown to be efficacious in managing outcomes for patients with acute kidney injury [5], but has not been applied to a heart failure population. That grouping of patients via GBTM can then be paired with common machine-learning techniques aimed at identifying the covariates that meaningfully differ across those populations [6], [7]. Together, the GBTM and ML approach can stratify patients and identify each subgroup's important covariates. Recent advances in computational modeling, particularly in the emergence of digital twins, dynamic mechanistic frameworks that represent individual patients to integrate physiological data across time scales, offer promising approaches to achieve this personalization of risk prediction. By characterizing patients with time-dependent trajectories rather than static baselines, such frameworks could enable real-time clinical decision support.

In this study, we use the MIMIC-IV database to identify latent MAP-derived subgroups among HF patients in the ICU and then determine subgroup-specific predictors of 28-day and in-hospital mortality using random forest and logistic regression modeling [8]. The combination of trajectory-driven sub-phenotyping with data-driven variable importance can enable insights that could eventually lead to more personalized stratification of critically ill HF patients.

Materials and methods

MIMIC-IV data source

The Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC-IV, version 3.1) database is a collection of deidentified electronic health records from patients admitted to the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2022 [8]. It is broadly organized into (1) an ICU module, containing all interventions (drug and device), charted information, procedures, and monitoring data from the patient's time in the ICU, and (2) a hospital module, containing all patient demographic information, diagnoses, triage, vital signs, ordered lab data, and imaging data if applicable (notably x-ray and echocardiogram), generally taken in the emergency department. The two subsets can be matched by the unique patient identification number and unique admission ID. The patient's date of death, if applicable, is taken from hospital records or state records.

Heart failure patient population

We included only patients with at least one of 42 heart failure-related diagnosis codes, using the ICD-9 and ICD-10 medical coding systems, in place from 2008 to September 2015 and from Oct 2015 on, respectively (Supplemental Table 1 [9]). Of the approximately 65,000 patients in the MIMIC-IV ICU database, we were then left with 16,840 patients who fit these diagnostic criteria.

We then excluded patients who were under 18 years old and included data only from each patient's first ICU stay, provided that the stay was longer than 24 h. Patients with an insufficient number of vital signs, hemodynamic, biomarker, laboratory, and treatment measurements- defined as having less than 70% were excluded [10].

Group based trajectory modeling

Group Based Trajectory Modeling (GBTM) identifies subsets of subjects who followed similar longitudinal patterns of response [11]. It is an application of finite mixture modeling that uses trajectory subsets as a statistical technique for approximating unknown trajectories across population members and enables complex patterns in longitudinal data to be identified, summarized, and communicated. The approach discerns the trajectory of identified subsets, estimates the subset membership probability for each subject, and then assigns subjects to the trajectory subset for which they have the highest probability. Put simply, it is a data-driven approach to identify groups of patients that have similar longitudinal sequences of repeated clinical measures.

We used mean arterial pressure (MAP) over the patient's first 24 h in the ICU as the transient measurement of interest, as it has previously been used for similar trajectory-based studies, is a well-established clinical marker of patient state, and is one of the most abundant measurements in the dataset [5]. To create a uniform temporal structure across patients with varying measurement frequencies, MAP values were aggregated into 2-h intervals spanning the first 24 h of ICU admission, yielding 13 consecutive bins. Within each bin, the mean of all available measurements was used as the representative value. Patients were excluded if they had fewer than 12 of those 13 measurement values. MAP interval data were arranged in wide format (one row per patient, one column per 2-h interval). Missing interval values, arising when no MAP measurement was recorded within a given 2-h window, were imputed using the Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) algorithm, run 5 times using predictive mean matching. The median imputed value across the 5 iterations was substituted for each missing interval, producing a complete trajectory matrix for GBTM fitting. We ran the analysis with 2, 3, 4, and 5 classes, and used a combination of the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and a visual separation between the classes that still maintained adequate sample sizes in each class (to avoid overfitting) to select the number of groups [12].

We also implemented explanatory analyses to identify differences among the GBTM-identified groups. We compared death rates, demographic data (age and sex), and treatment (particularly cardiac assist device use) across the groups. Categorical variables (primarily race, sex, and death) were compared using Fisher's exact test or chi-square tests. Continuous variables were compared using independent-samples t-tests when residuals were approximately normally distributed and variances were homogeneous, as assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene's test, respectively. Otherwise, comparisons were performed using the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test. For each comparison, p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure, with adjusted p < 0.05 considered statistically significant [13]. The resulting measurement p-values are shown in Supplementary Table 2.

Machine learning approaches: data selection, imputation, and analyses

Once the groups were identified, we sought to identify differences in measurable covariates that drive patient outcomes, particular to each of the classes. While GBTM tells us what subgroups exist with different longitudinal trajectories, the machine learning approaches tell us what covariates are important to each of the subgroups for an outcome of interest (in this case, in-hospital and 28-day survival). We performed Logistic Regression and Random Forest analyses using covariates in the MIMIC-IV data, for each of the GBTM-identified classes of patients [14].

The MIMIC-IV dataset contains measurements taken both in the emergency department and in the ICU. We selected clinical and laboratory measurements from either of the sources from the first day of the patient hospital stay, provided they were (even if loosely) connected to cardiac function and disease. Of those 1063 measurement types, 642 were unique and 421 were matched between the two sources, and were aligned based on units of measure and collection method. The data types included demographic characteristics, vital signs, hemodynamics, comorbidities, laboratory or clinical test results, and treatment information [8]. We removed questionnaires or scoring systems (such as the APACHE, SOFA, and SAPS scores), as they are combinations or adjustments of the other measurements already included in the analyses.

Of those remaining measurements, we removed any that were missing in at least 30% of our patients. We then removed any patients who were missing at least 30% of that list of measurements. The bi-directional censoring (eliminating measurements and patients with substantial missing data) limited bias in the downstream analyses [15]. Our final dataset included 127 covariates and 4816 patients.

We then imputed the missing data using the multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE) algorithm [16]. The algorithm was run 5 times, and the median measurement imputation across those iterations was used for our analyses.

We performed Logistic Regression and Random Forest analyses on this dataset, divided into our three MAP-trajectory classes from the GBTM approach. The analyses were performed twice: once with the output of interest as the in-hospital mortality and once with 28-day mortality as the output variable. For each, we applied synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) to account for the imbalanced classification categories (with most groups having approximately 85% survival and only 15% death rates) [17]. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity were used as the evaluation metrics. Each model used 3-fold cross validation, and the reported evaluation metrics were therefore reported as a mean and standard deviation [18].

We allowed a maximum of 1 predictor per 15 events (deaths) to maintain confidence in our predictors [19]. We used a stepwise logistic regression, applied with the “binomial” family input in the “glm” function in R, with a probability threshold of 0.5 for binary class assignment, as we predicted survival or death. The random forest analysis used 1000 trees, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regularization, and was applied with the “randomForest” package in R [20], [21]. Variable Importance was based off the mean decrease in accuracy and the mean decrease in Gini index for the random forest, and based off the coefficients, p-values, and odds ratios for the logistic regression. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 in RStudio version 2024.04.2 + 764.

Results

Baseline characteristics and GBTM classification

Of the over 65,000 patients in the MIMIC-IV ICU database, a total of 4816 patients with heart failure met all criteria and were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). We then applied group-based trajectory modeling, a data-driven method for identifying patient subpopulations that have similar transient dynamics with respect to a variable of interest. We tested two, three, four, and five classes for the GBTM. There was substantial improvement in AIC and BIC when moving from two to three classes. However, there was minimal improvement in AIC and BIC when going from the three-class model to the four or five-class models. Therefore, the 3-class model was chosen for the balance of simplicity, explainability, and performance. The patients were categorized by GBTM into three distinct trajectory classes: Trajectory Class 1 (2735 patients, showing a steady or stable time course), Class 2 (720 patients, characterized by a decreasing MAP over the 24 h), and Class 3 (1361 patients, with MAP increasing over time). The mean measurements at each hour (relative to ICU admission) for the different groups is shown in Fig. 2A.

Across the full cohort, age, gender, race, and ethnicity patterns were comparable among the trajectory classes (Table 1, with none of the comparisons being statistically significant). Hospital length of stay was similar across classes, averaging approximately 12 to 13 days. Hemodynamic parameters show differences but align intuitively with the MAP observations used to categorize the patients (in Supplementary Table 2); for example, Trajectory 1 showed the lowest systolic and diastolic blood pressures, consistent with the MAP being lowest of the groups. Laboratory markers relevant to cardiac injury and function, such as Troponin T, creatine kinase, and CK-MB, showed no difference among classes. Hematologic values, including platelet count, hematocrit, and hemoglobin, had minor variation but all remained within expected ranges for a critically ill population. Death rates were consistent across the groups (Fig. 2B), (χ2(2) = 0.43, p = 0.81). In sum, the groups demonstrate divergence in the output of interest (MAP) and related measures but did not differ substantially in many of the measurements that are often used for classifying patients, highlighting the need for temporal information to be used in patient phenotyping.

The usage of cardiovascular devices, broadly grouped into “assist devices” (including VAD, LVAD, PVAD, RVAD, IABP, and ECMO), “pacemakers” (whether single-chamber, dual-chamber, biventricular, or leadless), and “ventilators” (whether through endotracheal tube or a tracheostomy), did see substantial differences among the classes. Trajectory class 2, the group with decreasing MAP, had substantially higher assist device use (Fig. 2C), consistent with the expectation that mechanical circulatory support is deployed most often in patients with hemodynamic deterioration. Trajectory class 1, with steady MAP, had much higher use of ventilators and pacemakers. The increased pacemaker use is likely because those patients have already had their conduction disease identified and managed so the stable MAP at ICU presentation could be expected. Though overall ventilator use is quite high (87% of the full population), Class 1 patients have the lowest absolute MAP of the three groups (∼70 mmHg), which may reflect a phenotype of chronic, low-output heart failure with greater prevalence of chronic cardiopulmonary compromise, which requires ongoing respiratory support; which in turn explains the higher ventilator rates compared to the other classes. Thus, use of assist devices (χ2(2) = 42.9, p < 0.001), pacemakers (χ2(2) = 292.6, p < 0.001), and ventilators (χ2(2) = 96.8, p < 0.001) differed significantly by trajectory class. However, we cannot determine whether hemodynamic deterioration necessitated device placement or whether devices altered MAP patterns and thus affected class assignment.

Nevertheless, to ensure the MAP trajectory phenotypes were robust to use (and would not confound the trajectory classes), we performed three sensitivity analyses, stratifying the cohort by the presence or absence of each device category (assist devices, pacemakers, and ventilators) in the first 24-h and then re-ran the GBTM approach, with equivalent intervals, data imputation, and trajectory parameters. In all six resulting subgroups, the three MAP phenotypes were preserved, supporting the conculsion that the classes reflect intrinsic hemodynamic physiology and not device effects.

For the circulatory assist devices, the device-exposed subgroup (n = 465) showed shifting proportions relative to the full cohort: a smaller “stable” class (49%, vs the 57% in the full population), a larger “decreasing” class (25%, vs the 15% in the full population), and an nearly equivalent “increasing class” (27%, compared to 28% in the full cohort). The no-device subgroup (n = 4264) closely mirrored the full cohort.

For pacemakers, the pacemaker-using subgroup (n = 2046) was substantially enriched for the Low-Stable class (69%) and depleted for the Decreasing class (8%), compared with 57% and 15% in the full cohort. This is a mechanistically expected finding, as pacemakers regulate heart rate and thereby protect against the rate-mediated hemodynamic instability that characterizes the Decreasing phenotype. Among patients without pacemakers (n = 2770), the Decreasing class was correspondingly more prominent (20%) and the stable class was reduced (46%).

For ventilators, mechanical ventilation was nearly universal in this cohort (87% of all patients), and the ventilated subgroup (n = 4182) reproduced the full cohort class distribution almost exactly (Low-Stable 58%, Decreasing 13%, Increasing 28%). Among the 634 non-ventilated patients, all three phenotypes persisted with modest shifts.

In each case, the shifts in class proportions across device subgroups were mechanistically coherent and directionally expected. Patients receiving circulatory assist devices are, by clinical necessity, those who are the most hemodynamically unstable, and we therefore see an enriched “decreasing” class in our GBTM approach. Patients with pacemakers have regulated heart rates that protect against rate-mediated hemodynamic collapse, and we therefore see an enriched “stable” class and smaller “decreasing” class. Ventilator use was nearly universal (87% of the full cohort), so differences in ventilator rates across trajectory classes reflect differences in overall illness burden rather than any causal effect of ventilation on MAP trajectory.

Taken together, these findings confirm that the three MAP trajectory phenotypes are robust to stratification by all three device categories and represent a device-independent feature of this critically ill heart failure population.

Overall, the GBTM approach identified three distinct MAP trajectory classes among the 4816 heart-failure patients, with minimal differences in mortality or demographics.

Machine Learning Approaches for Identification of Factors Associated with Predicting Death.

While there is substantial value in characterizing subpopulations of patients based on the transient dynamics, the goal of that characterization is to affect clinical decision-making and improve outcomes. The grouping of similar patients based on trajectory is a substantial improvement compared to the more common classification based on single (often binary) measurements; however, trajectory-based sub-phenotyping is descriptive, not predictive, and thus should be paired with predictions of what leads to or prevents death for those specific subgroups of patients. We therefore used each of our GBTM-derived classes as the subjects for a machine-learning approach of identifying the variates that are likely to be associated with death or survival. We applied Logistic Regression and Random Forest models to each of our trajectory classes, using clinical and lab measurements as the input variables to predict death in hospital and at 28 days after admission. Thus, we had a total of twelve individual models: two techniques (random forest and logistic regression), for the three GBTM-derived classes, for two outputs of interest (in-hospital and 28-day death).

Factors associated with predicting death in each subgroup results

Each of the logistic regression and random forest models was assessed using 3-fold cross validation, reporting mean and standard deviation for AUROC, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. Overall, the performance was relatively consistent across each of the 12 models, with the AUC ranging from 0.69 to 0.83, and accuracy ranging from 0.77 to 0.84. The two machine learning methods were equally able to predict death, with average AUC values of 0.75 and 0.77, for the logistic regression and random forest, respectively. The models were equally able to predict death for the two time points, with average AUC values of 0.76 for both the 28-day and the in-hospital time point outputs. Trajectory 1 was slightly better performing than the other two classes, with average AUC scores of 0.81, compared to 0.76 for Trajectory Class 2 and 0.71 for Class 3 (see Table 2 Supplementary Tables 3 and 4 below). Though each of those metrics is sufficient to derive confidence in model predictions, the slight variation in performance among the classes highlights the influence that the underlying MAP pattern and time horizon has on the ability to predict death.

Important variables

Across the logistic regression and random forest modeling approaches and time output, several clinical and laboratory covariates emerged as consistent predictors of mortality within each MAP trajectory class. Key covariates included respiratory rate, oxygen saturation (SpO2), urea nitrogen, creatinine, bicarbonate, and platelet count; all of which are commonly utilized measurements for clinical decision-making (shown in Supplemental Table 5). The logistic regression's odds ratios inform the directionality of the influence of the predictor variable (an increase in the predictor being associated with an increase in likelihood of the outcome, death, in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4 is shown in red). For example, higher lactate was associated with death; this been previously shown clinically, as lactate is a well-recognized predictor of mortality. When the two machine learning approaches gave the same variable as impactful, that variable was bolded.

Class 1 (with a steady MAP) was characterized by predictors related to stable organ function and respiratory status, as much as could be expected for a heart failure cohort. Across both logistic regression and random forest models, respiratory rate, anion gap, bicarbonate, pO₂, urea nitrogen, RDW, creatinine (serum), base excess, and pH emerged as important indicators for in-hospital death. Several of these variables, notably urea nitrogen, pO₂, bicarbonate, and white blood cell count, were also central in predicting 28-day mortality, highlighting their value across distinct time scales. Those covariates can be grouped broadly into relating to renal function (urea nitrogen, creatinine), acid-base balance (anion gap, pH, bicarbonate), and respiratory function (respiratory rate, pO2). Collectively, those groupings of measurements suggest that mortality risk is due primarily to organ-specific dysfunction (especially in the renal and pulmonary systems).

Patients in Trajectory Class 2, characterized by decreasing MAP over the first 24 h, had the most drastic and recognizably high-risk predictors emerge as impactful. For in-hospital mortality, strong predictors included increased respiratory rate, anion gap, lactate, RDW, serum creatinine, BUN, base deficit, and decreased bicarbonate and pH. That combination of variables is classically associated with circulatory insufficiency, hypoperfusion, and metabolic acidosis [22], [23]. Additional markers such as MCHC (mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase, phosphate, O₂ saturation/pulse oximetry, and platelet count further point to multi-organ derangement, including hepatic and hematologic stress responses. Many of those predictors also emerged when analyzing 28-day mortality, along with serum glucose and Prothrombin time (PT), suggest issues with hyperglycemia and difficulty in managing proper coagulation.

Patients in MAP Class 3, identified as having an increasing MAP over the first 24 h, can broadly be categorized as patients with improving hemodynamics but continued metabolic problems. Key predictors of in-hospital death included anion gap, bicarbonate, lactate, urea nitrogen, RDW, creatinine (serum), BUN, bilirubin (total), PT, platelet count, and MCV. For 28-day mortality, both models emphasize urea nitrogen, PT, bicarbonate, admission weight, and lactate, with additional contributions from chloride (serum), sodium, bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, and PTT. With the understanding that MAP is increasing or stabilizing, it seems that mortality risk is more dependent on residual or underlying renal, hepatic, and metabolic dysfunction.

We also applied the same machine learning approach to our device-specific and no-device subgroups. The consensus predictors were consistent across the subgroups (Supplementary Table 6). BUN and lactic acid were consensus predictors for 28-day mortality across all three classes, confirming their class-agnostic prognostic role independent of device effects. Respiratory parameters (respiratory rate, tidal volume, and mean airway pressure) were again prominent in Classes 2 and 3, consistent with the ventilatory burden of the Decreasing and Increasing MAP phenotypes. In the device subgroup, predictors shifted slightly toward end-organ injury markers (AST, ionized calcium, total bilirubin, pH), reflecting the severity of illness in device-supported patients and reinforcing the biological coherence of the phenotype across both subgroups. With these results, we further believe that the is mechanistically coherent and further validates the trajectory classification.

Discussion

In this work, we paired group-based trajectory modeling of MAP during the patient's first 24 h in the ICU with trajectory-class specific machine-learning prediction to classify patient subgroups and characterize the heterogeneity of mortality risk among heart failure patients in the MIMIC-IV dataset. Often traditional risk stratification methods depend on static measurements or (often reductive) global risk scores like HFRS or SOFA. While they have their appropriate roles, there is also substantial value in a trajectory-focused approach, recognizing that early hemodynamic changes themselves offer clinically meaningful information that can shape the relevance of subsequent covariates on patient outcomes. Those unique phenotypes or physiological categorizations of the patients based on important variables (organ-specific dysfunction and hemodynamic stability for Class 1, widespread derangement consistent with a systemic shock for Class 2, and the “partial recovery but residual organ injury” for Class 3), all highlight the value of incorporating the transient or dynamic information contained in the MAP GBTM approach. By repeating the analysis with no-device and device-treated subgroups, we showed the three MAP trajectory phenotypes identified are not attributable to circulatory support device effects, but are consistent with intrinsic hemodynamic physiology across both the device-exposed and device-free subgroups. Even with similar death rates, the mechanisms by which the patients are dying (or surviving) seem to differ substantially across the three classes. This pipeline, of pairing trajectory-based classification with data-driven assessment of covariate importance, is therefore quite useful in enabling more precise risk stratification and ultimately supporting clinical decision making. This approach of identifying time-focused sub-phenotypes to then learn the predictors that are particularly impactful for each of those latent subgroups is a practical step toward more patient-focused exploration and modeling. These analytical approaches can in turn support improved digital twin systems aimed at simulating individual patient disease progression and response to treatment, ultimately informing optimal clinician decision-making [24], [25].

Consistent with previous publications, a central insight from our work is that patients with similar baseline clinical characteristics ultimately diverge into distinct patterns of MAP within their first 24 h of ICU admission [5]. Notably, none of the distinct classes had mean MAP values that in themselves are clinically alarming, but their longitudinal trajectories highlight the importance of quantifying transience in a patient hospital stay [26]. When paired with machine-learning on the clinical measurements and covariates, those trajectories show distinct relationships associated with mortality. Even if death rates are comparable, the underlying variables driving death are unique, highlighting an opportunity for directed and intentional interventions particular to the trajectory-derived subgroups. For example, patients in the steady-MAP group (Class 1) demonstrated risk profiles dominated by renal, respiratory, and acid–base dysfunction, highlighting stable hemodynamics but evolving organ-specific injury. The decreasing-MAP group (Class 2) exhibited physiologic signatures consistent with circulatory collapse, shock physiology, and global metabolic failure. Patients with increasing MAP (Class 3) appeared to be in partial recovery (evidenced by the rising MAP) but remained vulnerable to residual renal, hepatic, and metabolic abnormalities. In further support of the cross-validation performed in the analysis pipeline, each of the different representative predictors are validated with clinical (cohort-based) or in vivo data, shown in Supplementary Table 5. Collectively, those class-specific patterns highlight the understanding that the specific route a patient takes to a measurement value matters, and that a particular clinical measurement must be contextualized within the patient's overall (and especially hospital-visit) history. Individualized modeling, and ultimately individualized patient treatment, must be conditioned on the patient's temporal physiology, as patients do not exist on an equal risk landscape, but a changing horizon existing in the context of their previous conditions and treatments.

Among the specific variables that emerged, it is worth noting that several, including lactate, BUN, and creatinine, are themselves strong independent predictors of ICU mortality. The trajectory classification adds meaningful information beyond these individual markers alone, by providing the hemodynamic context within which those markers should be interpreted. Elevated lactate in a patient with declining MAP (Class 2, where lactate suggests circulatory collapse) carries different mechanistic implications than the same value in a patient with rising MAP (Class 3, where the trajectory suggests partial recovery); similarly, worsening renal function in the context of stable MAP (Class 1) points to organ-specific injury rather than global hypoperfusion. The trajectory class does not replace individual biomarkers but contextualizes them, and formal quantification of the incremental prognostic contribution of trajectory class membership, beyond individual predictors alone, remains an important direction for future work.

An important finding is that overall mortality did not differ significantly across the three trajectory classes (χ2(2) = 0.43, p = 0.81), The trajectory classification and machine-learning approach described here is designed to characterize how and why patients are at risk, conditional on their hemodynamic course suggesting subphenotypes associated with MAP trajectories in the first 24 h. This is a slightly different goal than direct identification of covariates that are correlated with death. Trajectory-based phenotyping can generate insights that improve clinical decision-making by enriching what is available to the clinician in these patients with many uncertainties and substantial variation. The value of this trajectory-based approach is supported by a growing body of work demonstrating that sub-phenotyping of heterogeneous syndromes can reveal clinically meaningful variation in prognosis and disease mechanism that aggregate classifications or single measurement correlations can obscure. [1], [2], [3], [4], [5]

That idea of personalization of risk prediction, especially with a focus on transient dynamics, is central to the developing interest in digital twins for healthcare. Digital twins are computational frameworks that represent individual patients, thus being able to integrate/align disparate physiological data across time scales to predict optimal treatment interventions for the specific person [24], [27], [28], [29], [30]. Though this study was not in itself a digital twin analysis, our work highlights several components essential to the ultimate success of digital twins in clinical decision making. First, this trajectory mapping focuses on representing the patient in a dynamic phenotype rather than a static category. A proper digital twin framework must simulate in time, to be temporally predictive rather than simply descriptive [25]. Next, the approach acknowledges that the importance (and the interplay) of clinical variables can vary depending on the patient's current physiological trajectory. What matters to a patient at the present very much depends on where the patient is along their clinical journey– what initial conditions existed (including particular comorbidities and demographics) and what prior interventions have been applied. For example, in Class 2 patients (with decreasing MAP), an early identification of lactate elevation, widening anion gap, or abnormal liver enzymes is particularly important, as it may indicate worsening shock physiology. Those same changes would be less meaningful and less actionable in Class 1 patients with a stable MAP, whose clinical decline is better characterized by peripheral organ dysfunction. Finally, the fact that this analysis used data from an electronic health record dataset is meaningful. All future digital twin work, if it is to be utilized by clinicians, must work within the existing medical frameworks in our current health system. Digital twin models must align with the naming conventions in the existing infrastructure, must reflect the specific data collection methods, and reflect an understanding of why particular measurements are taken and why others are not- as most missing data in healthcare is “missing not at random”, but with missingness that directly relates to the unobserved measurement.

Ultimately, translating this work toward clinical practice would lead to trajectory-aware decision support systems that can update predictions and risk drivers as the patient's physiological path evolves and patient state changes in time. A future digital twin model could identify deviation from expected MAP (or other hemodynamic measures of interest) and predict which covariates are increasingly predictive of deterioration or mortality for the patient. The clinician could then select between alternative medications or dosages, could order alternate tests, or could recommend triaging the patient to a higher level of care with adequate support.

There are several limitations worth noting in this work. First, as a retrospective, single-center study from an academic medical center (Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center), the findings may not generalize to other academic medical centers and community hospitals across geographies and patient populations with different practices for MAP monitoring and hemodynamic management. The observed relationships represent statistical associations within this dataset, and suggest valuable directions for exploration and validation with future research. Second, the bidirectional 70% data completeness threshold applied to both patients and measurements introduces potential selection bias. Patients excluded due to insufficient documentation (for example, those who deteriorate rapidly or die early in their ICU course) may systematically differ from the included cohort, potentially skewing the identified trajectory classes and their associated mortality predictors. We recognize that potential bias in our work but preferred it over the bias that would have been introduced by including patients and measurements with insufficient coverage in the dataset (and thus requiring substantive data imputation). Third, neither the three-class GBTM solution nor the class-specific mortality predictors have been validated in an external cohort. The findings combined with literature summarized in Supplementary Table 5 suggest that validation of these trajectory phenotypes is an important next step toward subsequent clinical applications.

Beyond these methodological limitations, several analytical constraints are worth acknowledging. MAP over the first 24 h of ICU admission, while meaningful and abundant, is not exhaustive as a hemodynamic representation. It may miss later hemodynamic changes that are especially relevant to longer-term outcomes, such as 28-day mortality, and it is not the only hemodynamic trajectory variable capable of informing sub-phenotyping. Central venous pressure (CVP) and heart rate were included in the machine learning models in this work, but building trajectories from multiple simultaneous inputs, using joint trajectory modeling approaches, would likely be more powerful. Multi-variable group trajectory modeling, such as pairing MAP trajectories with heart rate, Lactate, CVP, or more invasive right-heart catheterization data, would be a substantial development in available GBTM methods in heart failure and would produce a richer hemodynamic phenotyping framework.

The interplay between clinical interventions and observed measurements is a further limitation inherent to real-world EHR data. Device use differed significantly across trajectory classes and may have contributed to the MAP patterns observed. To assess the robustness of the trajectory classification to this potential confound, we conducted sensitivity analyses stratifying the cohort by circulatory device exposure, ventilator use, and pacemaker use during the first 24 h; the three-class trajectory structure was broadly preserved in all six resulting subgroups subgroups (Supplementary Fig. 1), supporting the interpretation that the phenotypes reflect intrinsic hemodynamic physiology; shifts in class proportions across those subgroups were mechanically coherent. Nevertheless, the combination of pharmacological therapies, drug-device interactions, and pre-existing treatments discontinued upon ICU admission all influence the observed measurements in ways that could not be fully captured in this analysis.

Finally, this study used two machine learning approaches, logistic regression and random forest, and the field offers many additional techniques with distinct strengths; expanding to other modeling frameworks is a natural direction for future work. Importantly, machine learning techniques identify significant associations rather than causal relationships, and the identified predictors should yet not be interpreted as validated therapeutic targets without further experimental validation. Replication and validation in databases beyond MIMIC-IV, ideally across diverse institutions and patient populations, remains an important next step toward clinical translation of these findings.

Conclusion

In this study, we paired group-based trajectory modeling with class-specific machine-learning analyses and show that the predictors of mortality differ depending on a patient's hemodynamic trajectory, even when overall outcomes appear similar. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating temporal physiological information when subphenotyping patient populations and when modeling risk in the ICU. This work is a practical step toward patient-specific computational modeling and the development of digital-twin systems oriented toward individualized clinical decision-making.
