TROP2 promotes bone metastasis of colorectal cancer through interaction with the fibronectin-integrin axis
Authors: Le-Xin Xiao, Yan-Lin Huang, Chun-Hong Liao, Hua-Ping Xie, Hao Hu, Dong-Mei Chen, Jia-Liang Wang, Lin Gu, Yu-Rong Gu, Jian Xiao, Yi-Fan Lian, Shan-Shan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer in the world. Bone metastasis, which occurs in advanced cancer, causes severe damage and accelerates patient death. TROP2, encoded by the TACSTD2 gene, is highly expressed in multiple types of cancer and is associated with tumor proliferation, invasion and metastasis. However, the role of TROP2 in CRC is unclear.MethodsTROP2 expression was detected in tumor tissues of CRC patients with or without bone metastases, as well as CRC cell lines. The functional role of TROP2 on CRC cells was validated by in vitro cell culture models and in vivo mouse experiments. Comparative proteomics was used to discover the interactome of TROP2, and later confirmed by immunoprecipitation and immunofluorescent studies. The therapeutic role of TROP2-targeted agent in CRC was verified by intratibial injection mouse models.ResultsTROP2 was highly expressed in the tumor tissues of CRC patients, with largely negative expression in the normal counterparts. CRC patients with bone metastasis had even higher TROP2 expression in the tumors than those with primary carcinoma only. We demonstrated that TROP2 promoted the proliferation and migration of CRC cells by in vitro and in vivo experiments, and the pro-invasive and pro-adhesive effects of TROP2 in CRC cells could be enhanced by co-culturing with bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). Mechanistically, we validated that TROP2 was able to interact with fibronectin (FN) expressed by BMSCs, which increased the expressions of α5, αV, and β3 integrin subunits and facilitated the expression of integrin-linked kinase (ILK) on CRC cells. This interaction was required for the downstream activation of PI3K-Akt signaling and the CRC cell adhesion to BMSCs. Furthermore, intratibial injection mouse experiments verified that TROP2 promoted the colonization of CRC cells in bone marrow, and the combination of the TROP2-targeted antibody-drug conjugate, Sacituzumab Govitecan, and anti-integrin α5β1 monoclonal antibody, Volociximab, was effective in inhibiting bone colonization of CRC cells.ConclusionTROP2 promotes CRC bone metastasis by interacting with bone marrow-derived FN-integrin axis. Through its adhesive function, TROP2 may coordinate with the specialized bone niche to facilitate metastatic colonization and establish a permissive environment for stromal-tumor interactions. Targeting this axis offers a promising therapeutic strategy for managing bone metastasis in CRC and potentially other TROP2-overexpressing malignancies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06315-w.Keywords: TACSTD2, Tumor microenvironment, Cell-cell adhesion, Extracellular matrix, Targeted therapy

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common human malignancy and causes the second most cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. Tumor metastasis accounts for an important reason of death in CRC patients, and nearly 50% of the patients eventually develop metastasis. With the continuous progress of treatment and prolonged survival time of CRC patients, the incidence of bone metastasis is also rapidly increasing [2]. Bone is the most likely site for solid cancer to metastasize in the late stage, and once it occurs, it is usually considered incurable [3]. Bone metastasis will bring a series of bone-related events to patients who will show serious complications such as pain, pathological fractures, spinal cord compression, etc [2]. A large number of reported data show that the occurrence of bone metastasis significantly shortens the survival time of CRC patients, and is also very relevant to poor prognosis [4]. Therefore, it is urgent to verify the mechanism of CRC bone metastasis and make progress to the therapeutic practice.

Trophoblast cell surface antigen 2 (TROP2) is a type I transmembrane glycoprotein produced by the TACSTD2 gene, which is an epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) family member and also known as EpCAM2 [5]. TROP2 expression is elevated in various tumors, and several in vivo and in vitro studies have demonstrated that high TROP2 expression promotes the proliferation and migration of tumor cells, thereby facilitating cancer progression [6]. Studies have shown that overexpression of TROP2 is correlated significantly with tumor metastasis in breast cancer and prostate cancer, which causes heavy damage to patients and is considered a poor prognostic biomarker [7, 8]. However, the functional role of TROP2 in CRC bone metastasis is unclear.

The type of high-grade metastatic bone lesions from CRC are osteolytic lesions. Previous studies have usually focused on the mechanisms involved in osteolysis triggered by the interaction of cytokines released by CRC cells on osteoclasts [9, 10]. The bone marrow microenvironment is complex and unique in components, which contains diverse cells with different physiological functions that form a specific bone marrow niche [3], we believe that not only osteoclasts but other important cells play an important role in the process of bone metastasis. Studies have shown that mesenchymal stem cells are associated with various aspects of tumor cell behavior, and importantly, bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) act as a critical population in the bone niche to promote tumor cell colonization [11, 12]. Cell-cell adhesion has a vital role in tumor metastasis, and fibronectin (FN), an important extracellular glycoprotein, can facilitate the formation of a pre-metastatic niche that provides suitable soil for tumor colonization, and thereafter tumor cell adhesion and survival [13]. FN functions as a crucial integrin-binding protein through the RGD (Arg-Gly-Asp) motifs, contributing to metastatic disease progression [14].

In this study, we demonstrated that an elevated level of TROP2 in CRC patients is associated with bone metastasis, and serves as a poor prognosis marker. TROP2 promotes the aggressive phenotypes of CRC cells both in vitro and in vivo. Especially, it increases cellular adhesion capacity in the co-culture system of CRC cells with BMSCs, benefiting CRC bone metastasis. Mechanically, we detected that TROP2 of CRC cells interacts with FN of BMSCs, promoting the cell-cell communication to form the bone metastasis niche of CRC cells. This interaction is required for the activation of downstream signaling events and tumor colonization in bone environment. Finally, we confirmed the promising therapeutic value of a TROP2-targeted antibody-drug conjugate (ADC), Sacituzumab Govitecan (SG), by the intratibial injection mouse model in CRC bone metastasis.

Methods

Bioinformatics analysis

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database was utilized to analyze the prognostic value of TROP2 expression in CRC. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was used to enrich the predefined gene sets different between TROP2-high and -low CRC cells. The Contaminant Repository for Affinity Purification (CRAPome) database was used to analyze the specificity of the candidates in the mass spectrum results by uploading them to the database. InterPro website (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/) was used to predict the functional motifs of TROP2 to generate relevant truncation mutants.

Reagents

Commercially available antibodies are as follows: TROP2 antibody (ab214488) was purchased from Abcam. Antibodies recognizing Fibronectin (26836 S), Integrin sampler kit (4749T), Akt (9272 S), and phospho-Akt (4060T) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology. Antibodies against ILK (12955-1-AP), Tubulin (51064-2-AP), GAPDH (60004-1-Ig) and Flag (66008-3-Ig) were obtained from Proteintech. Ki67 antibody (MA5-14520) was obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. P1F6, a neutralizing antibody of integrin αVβ5 and HFN 7.1, a neutralizing antibody of FN, were purchase from DSHB (Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank). MK-2206 (S1078), Sacituzumab Govitecan (D4002), and Volociximab (A3106) were brought from Selleck. All other chemical reagents were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich unless otherwise indicated.

Cell culture

The human embryonic kidney cell line HEK-293T (CC4003) and human CRC cell lines, HCT116 (CC0506) and HT29 (CC0504), were acquired from Cellcook Biotech (Guangzhou, China), and these cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM, Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco). BMSCs were sourced from Cyagen Biosciences (HUXMA-01001, Guangzhou, China), were cultured in OriCell Human Bone Marrow Mesenchymal Stem Cell Basal Medium (HUXMA-90011, Cyagen Biosciences). All cell lines were contamination free and incubated in a humidified incubator with 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Tissue samples

We obtained a paraffin-embedded sample cohort consisting of primary CRC tumors from 77 patients with no metastatic lesions and 42 patients who eventually developed bone metastasis at the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Guangzhou, China) for prognosis and correlation analysis. The Institute Research Ethics Committee approved the study at the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval number: L2023ZSLYEC-045). Written informed consent was obtained from each patient.

Animal experiments

BALB/c nude mice were purchased from Guangdong Sja Biotechnology Co., Ltd to establish the bone and lung metastasis model. NSFG (NOD-Prkdcscid B6-lL2rgem1Smoc) immunodeficient mice were purchased from The RUIYE Laboratories (Guangdong, China). All mice were handled in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval number: [2022]02–086).

For bone metastasis analysis, 5 × 105 HCT116-luc cells in 10 µL 1×PBS were injected directly into the tibia of 8-week-old mice. Mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane (R510-22) purchased from Ruiwode Technology Company (Shenzhen, China) during the injection. After the exposure of the patellar ligament, a 29-gauge needle was inserted at the left tibia into the tibial cavity. The 29-gauge needle was then replaced with a 30-gauge needle containing 10 µL of tumor cell suspension for injection. Tumors were measured every week by BLI value. To assess the treatment effect of SG and Volociximab, 2 weeks after intratibial injection, the mice were injected with SG (5 mg/kg, i.v.) or Volociximab (10 mg/kg, i.v.) twice weekly for 4 weeks. BLI was performed once a week to monitor for potential metastases. BLI was measured using the preclinical optical imaging system (IVIS spectrum, PerkinElmer) after i.p. with 150 mg/kg D-Luciferin (GC43497, GLPBIO). Micro-CT (Quantum GX2, PerkinElmer) analysis was performed to measure the bone metastatic burden at week 2 after tumor injection.

To construct the lung metastasis model, 4 × 105 HCT116-luc cells were resuspended with 200 µL 1×PBS and injected into the tail vein of the mouse. The tumor burden of lung metastases was measured every five days by BLI, and mice were sacrificed at 40 days. The lung was isolated and sent for H&E staining.

To measure the in vivo proliferation ability, 5 × 106 HCT116 or HT29 cells were subcutaneously injected in NSFG mice aged 4–5 weeks. Tumor volume was measured every 4 days, and 20 days after tumor implantation, the mice were sacrificed. Xenograft tumors were isolated and weighed, and their volumes were calculated using the formula: tumor volume = π/6 × large diameter × smaller diameter2.

Plasmid construction, siRNA and transfection

The cDNA encoding full-length human TACSTD2 was subcloned into the pSIN-EF2-3×FLAG vector. Serial TROP2 truncation mutants were generated by deleting the corresponding protein domains: △35–77, deleting amino acids 35 to 77; △78–158, deleting amino acids 78 to 158; and △159, deleting amino acids 159 to 331. Correct constructs were all confirmed using DNA sequencing. The plasmid for expressing human plasma Fibronectin (pMAX-FN, #120401) was purchased from Addgene. Plasmids for different integrin subunits, including pCMV-ITGAV-3×FLAG-SV40-Neo (P12193), pCMV-ITGA5-3×FLAG-Neo (P41607) and pEnCMV-ITGB3-3×FLAG (P10491), were obtained from MiaoLing Plasmid Platform (Wuhan, China). TROP2 shRNA expressing pLKO.1 vectors were based on the following sequences: 5’-GAGAAAGGAACCGAGCTTGTA-3’ (shTROP2-1), and 5’-CGTGGACAACGATGGCCTCTA-3’ (shTROP2-2). The plasmids were transfected using jetPRIME Transfection Reagent (101000046, Polyplus-transfection) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

All siRNAs were synthesized and obtained from IGE Biotechnology (Guangzhou, China). The sequences of siRNAs used are as follows: siTROP2-#1, 5’-GCACGCTCATCTATTACCT-3’; siTROP2-#2, 5’-CGTGTCCCACCAACAAGAT-3’; siTROP2-#3, 5’-TCAAGGGCGAGTCTCTATT-3’; siFN-#1, 5’-GGAAAACACTATCAGATAA-3’; siFN-#2, 5’-CTGCGAGAGTAAACCTGAA-3’; siITGA5, 5’-TGGCTCAGACATTCGATCC-3’; siITGAV, 5’-GAAUAUCGGUUGGAUUAUATT-3’; siITGB1, 5’-GCGAGUGUGAUAAUUUCAATT-3’; siITGB3, 5’-CCAAGACTCATATAGCATT-3’; siITGB4, 5’-CAGAAGAUGUGGAUGAGUU-3’; siITGB5, 5’-GCCAACGAGUACACUGCAUTT-3’; siILK, 5’-GACCCAAAUUUGACAUGAUTT-3’. siRNAs were transfected using INTERFERin Transfection Reagent (101000028, Polyplus-transfection) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA isolation and real‑time quantitative PCR (qPCR)

The cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured to approximately 70%-80% confluence. Total RNA from cultured cells was isolated with TRIzol reagent (T9424, Sigma-Aldrich). RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (AG11728, Accurate Biology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Next, qPCR was performed in a LightCycler 480 real-time system (Roche) using the Platinum SYBR Green qPCR SuperMix-UDG (11744500, Invitrogen). The relative gene expression was normalized to GAPDH and determined based on the 2−ΔΔCt method.

The sequences of primers used are as follows: TACSTD2 forward, 5’-ACAACGATGGCCTCTACGAC-3’, and reverse, 5’-GTCCAGGTCTGAGTGGTTGAA-3’; FN forward, 5’-CGGTGGCTGTCAGTCAAAG-3’, and reverse, 5’-AAACCTCGGCTTCCTCCATAA-3’; ITGA5 forward, 5’-GGCTTCAACTTAGACGCGGAG-3’, and reverse, 5’-TGGCTGGTATTAGCCTTGGGT-3’; ITGAV forward, 5’-ATCTGTGAGGTCGAAACAGGA-3’, and reverse, 5’-TGGAGCATACTCAACAGTCTTTG-3’; ITGB3 forward, 5’-GTGACCTGAAGGAGAATCTGC-3’, and reverse, 5’-CCGGAGTGCAATCCTCTGG-3’; ILK forward, 5’-GACGACATTTTCACTCAGTGCC-3’, and reverse, 5’-ACGGTTCATTACATTGATCCGTG-3’; GADPH forward, 5’-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3’, and reverse, 5’-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3’.

Proliferation assay

The cells (2 × 104 cells per well) were seeded in a 6-well plate and then cultured in the IncuCyte Live Cell Analysis System incubator. Three different fields of view per well were photographed once every 24 h, and the cell number of each well was calculated and recorded accordingly.

Colony formation assay

In brief, 1 × 103 cells were placed per well in six-well plate. After 14 days, cells were washed with 1×PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min at room temperature (RT), and tinted with 0.1% crystal violet (C6158, Sigma). After 20 min, the crystal violet was washed with water and left to dry. The number of colonies was calculated using Image J.

Wound-healing assay

The cells were plated on a 6-well plate and left to full confluency. The cell monolayer was scratched by a 200 µL pipette tip and then cultured in DMEM with 1% FBS. Images were captured every 12 h using the IncuCyte Live Cell Analysis System, and the wound width was analyzed using Image J.

Transwell assay

BMSCs were seeded on the lower chamber. Upon approximately full confluence, HCT116 (5 × 104 per well) cells were plated onto the upper chamber of a Transwell filter with 8-µm pore size (3374, Corning) with serum-free medium. After 24 h of culture, cells were washed with 1×PBS and fixed with 4% PFA. Cells on the underside of the filter were stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min. Non-migrated cells on the upper side of the filter were removed with a cotton swab. The number of migrating cells was counted using an Inverted Fluorescence Microscope (Axio Observer 5, Zeiss).

Adhesion assay

A monolayer of BMSCs was seeded prior to co-culture with the 5 × 103 GFP-labeled CRC cells. Thirty minutes after co-culture, the unattached CRC cells were washed away by 1×PBS, and the fluorescent dots were evaluated using an Inverted Fluorescence Microscope (Axio Observer 5, Zeiss) to measure the adhesive ability.

Fibronectin (11051407001, Roche) was used in 5 µg/cm2 and coated in 24 well plates, which were kept at 4 ◦C overnight. The next day, the Fibronectin solution was removed, and the plate was dried at RT.

3D invasion assay

In brief, 5 × 104 GFP-labeled CRC cells in 10 µL 100% Matrigel matrix (354234, Corning) were cultured in a 24-well plate in DMEM with 10% FBS for 7 days. The cell-Matrigel drops were imaged every 24 h using the IncuCyte Live Cell Analysis System.

Western blotting

The cells were washed in 1×PBS and lysed with RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime). The protein concentration was determined by the Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) sample kit (23225, Pierce). Next, 5× protein sample buffer was added, followed by boiling the samples at 100 °C for 10 min. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE gel and then transferred onto a PVDF membrane. The membrane was incubated with the indicated antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing, the membrane was subsequently incubated with relative HRP-labeled secondary antibodies (W4011 for rabbit and W4021 for mouse originated primary antibodies, Promega). The immunoreactive bands were visualized by the enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, Merck Millipore). GAPDH or Tubulin was used as the control. Band quantification was done via ImageJ.

Immunoprecipitation

The cells were lysed with NETN (100 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-Cl pH = 8.0, 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.5% Nonidet P-40 (56741, Sigma-Aldrich)) buffer. After centrifugation, the supernatants were incubated with Flag-agarose beads (A2220, Sigma-Aldrich) at 4 °C overnight. Beads were washed 5 times with NETN buffer, resuspended in loading buffer, and then boiled at 95 °C for 5 min to separate the protein and beads. Protein samples were run on SDS-PAGE gel, followed by Western blotting.

Immunofluorescence

The cells were fixed with 4% PFA at RT for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (ST795, Beyotime) for 5 min, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, 0332, GBCBio) for 30 min at a 37 °C incubator. After washing, the cells were incubated with the indicated primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by the secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor-488 (A11029, Life Technologies) or -594 (A11005, Life Technologies) for 45 min at RT. The nucleus was stained with 50 ng/mL DAPI (D21490, Invitrogen) for 5 min at RT. Fluorescence signals were imaged using a Zeiss LSM710 con-focal microscope.

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue samples were paraffin-embedded and cut into 5-µm sections. The slides were then heated at 65 °C for 120 min, followed by de-paraffinized, hydrated and antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidases were blocked with 3% peroxide for 15 min, then in 5% BSA for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated primary antibodies, followed by the anti-rabbit/mouse secondary antibody (K5007, Dako) at RT for 45 min. Signals were detected in freshly prepared DAB substrate solution (K5007, Dako) at RT for 5 min. Finally, the sections were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted. Staining results were captured using microscopy (DM4000B, Leica). Each section was evaluated by two independent pathologists blinded to the clinical status of patients and graded as follows: the expression extent scores: 1 (< 10%), 2 (10%–50%), 3 (50–75%), and 4 (> 75%); and the positive staining intensity scores: 1 = no staining, 2 = weak staining, 3 = moderate staining, 4 = strong staining. Finally, the IHC score was calculated by multiplying the scores of expression extent and staining intensity, which ranged from 1 to 16.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software). All data represent the mean ± SEM. Fisher’s exact test was used in the contingency tables. The Kaplan–Meier analysis was employed for the survival analysis. The multiple comparisons were tested using a 2-tailed Student’s t-test or 1-way ANOVA. P value less than 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Elevated TROP2 expression is associated with bone metastasis in CRC

To discover the molecular events lying under bone metastasis in CRC patients, we collected three pairs of cancer and adjacent non-tumor tissues from CRC patients who developed bone metastasis for RNA sequencing. Within the most differentially expressed genes, TACSTD2 was consistently upregulated in all three cancer tissues (average fold change = 102.1, P value = 1.8E-21, Fig. 1A and B). To verify the association between TROP2 expression and bone metastasis, we collected a tissue cohort of primary cancers consisting of 42 CRC patients who developed bone metastasis (CRC-BM) and 77 metastasis-free CRC patients for immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis of TROP2 expression. Positive staining of TROP2 was rarely seen in the adjacent normal colon mucosa of all patients. However, it was already highly expressed in metastasis-free CRC tissues, and an even higher TROP2 expression score was observed in cancer tissues of the CRC-BM group (Fig. 1C and D). Correlation analysis confirmed that TROP2 expression was significantly associated with the M stage and incidence of bone metastasis (Table 1), and CRC patients with bone metastasis had higher levels of T stage, N stage and CA125 (Table 2). Kaplan-Meier analysis showed that elevated TROP2 expression was associated with reduced overall survival (OS) in our CRC patient cohort (Fig. 1E). In addition, we further verified the above results using online databases. In the TCGA cohort, TACSTD2 was highly expressed in CRC samples compared to the normal counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 1). These results suggest that TROP2 is highly expressed in cancer tissues and may play an essential role in the development of bone metastasis in CRC.

TROP2 promotes the malignant phenotype of CRC cells

Given the important role of TROP2 in CRC progression, we constructed TROP2 stably knockdown or overexpression HCT116 and HT29 CRC cell lines via a lentivirus-mediated infection system (Supplementary Fig. 2A-2D). We demonstrated that upregulation of TROP2 could significantly promote the proliferation of CRC cells, while TROP2 depletion suppressed the cell viability in vitro (Fig. 2A and B). In colony formation assays, TROP2 knockdown led to a significant decrease in the number of cell colonies, while its overexpression significantly enhanced colony formation (Fig. 2C and D). Ectopic expression of TROP2 also enhanced the migration ability of CRC cells and loss of TROP2 resulted in a significant decrease in the scratch recovery as shown by the wound-healing assays (Fig. 2E and F). To investigate the role of TROP2 in tumor progression in vivo, we subcutaneously implanted TROP2 overexpression HCT116 and HT29 cells into the immunodeficient NSFG mice. By measuring the growth of xenograft tumors, we revealed that TROP2 overexpression significantly enhanced the growth rate and tumor weight compared to the control group (Fig. 2G and I and Supplementary Fig. 2E-2G). IHC analysis also confirmed that enhanced Ki67 staining was observed after TROP2 overexpression in the xenograft tumor (Fig. 2J and Supplementary Fig. 2H). Additionally, a lung metastasis model by tail vein injection of luciferase-labeled CRC cells was also established to evaluate the metastatic ability of TROP2 in vivo. By comparing the quantification bioluminescence imaging (BLI), IVIS (in vivo imaging systems) spectrum showed that mice injected with TROP2 overexpression CRC cells experienced with heavier burden of lung metastases (Fig. 2K and L), and H&E staining also confirmed that more metastases were observed and occupied larger area of the lung in TROP2 overexpression group (Fig. 2M). These results suggest that TROP2 overexpression could effectively induce the aggressive phenotypes of CRC cells in vitro and in vivo.

TROP2 enhances CRC cell adhesion to BMSCs

Transwell assays were also carried out to measure the ability of TROP2 to promote cell invasion of CRC. Interestingly, we found that TROP2 overexpression only slightly enhanced the invasive ability of CRC cells, and no significant difference was observed (Fig. 3A). Cancer cell colonization and growth in the bone marrow are prerequisites for bone metastasis formation. Various studies have demonstrated that mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are associated with tumor development, and the interaction of MSCs with tumor cells has led to widespread attention [15, 16]. To explore whether BMSCs could promote tumor cell colonization, we spread BMSCs in the lower Transwell chamber. As we expected, BMSCs increased the invasive ability of CRC cells, and TROP2 overexpression significantly enhanced the tumor invasion compared with the control under BMSC-added condition (Fig. 3A). These results prompted us to further explore the relationship between CRC cells and BMSCs.

In the early metastatic cascade, the cancer cell receives vital support in the metastatic microenvironment through adhesive interaction with stromal cells to complete the colonization step [17]. Cell adhesion plays a crucial role in promoting cancer cell migration and survival [18]. Gene ontology enrichment analysis of the RNA sequencing data revealed that elevated TROP2 expression was significantly associated with active adhesion pathways, such as “cell adhesion”, “biological adhesion”, and “extracellular matrix organization” (Fig. 3B). Therefore, we tried to figure out whether TROP2 expression influenced the adhesion of CRC cells to BMSCs. GFP fluorescent protein was used to label CRC cells and a monolayer of BMSCs was seeded prior to co-culture with the GFP-labeled CRC cells. Thirty minutes after co-culture, the unattached CRC cells were washed away and the adhesive ability was determined by measuring the fluorescent dots. We found that TROP2 overexpression significantly promoted the adhesion of CRC cells to BMSC monolayer, and TROP2 depletion had an inhibitory effect (Fig. 3C and D). Concurrently, cell morphology was also examined over time by the IncuCyte live-cell imaging system. When directly seeded onto the culture plates, only a small fraction of CRC cells exhibit spindle-like cell shape, an indicative of cell spread and adhesion, at 9 h post seeding. Notably, when co-cultured with a monolayer of BMSCs, CRC cells began to develop adhesion within 0.5 h, and significantly more TROP2 overexpression CRC cells exhibited the adherent morphology compared to the control at the very first time point (Fig. 3E). These results suggest that TROP2 promoted the adhesive ability of CRC cells to BMSCs.

Adhesion of CRC cells to BMSCs is mediated by the interaction between TROP2 and FN

To investigate how TROP2 promotes the adhesion of CRC cells to BMSCs, we conducted TROP2 protein purification tandem with mass spectrometry analysis using lysates from either TROP2 overexpression CRC cells or control CRC cells co-cultured with BMSCs (Fig. 4A). The comparative results showed that FN was the top binding candidate associated with adhesion function within the TROP2 interactome, but it was not detected in the purified lysates of TROP2 overexpression CRC cells alone or BMSCs co-culture with HCT116 control cells (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Material 1). The same assays were performed on TROP2-overexpressed HT29, another CRC cell line, and similar results were obtained (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Material 1). We also analyzed our mass spectrometry results using the tool on CRAPome database, a website for background contaminant analysis of affinity purification mass spectrometry, and FN was only found on 8.4% of the experiments, suggesting that it was a rather specific binding candidate of TROP2 (Table S1 and Supplementary Material 1).

FN is the most abundant component of the extracellular matrix (ECM) and is the key to cell adhesion with multiple physiological functions. Therefore, we sought to validate the mass spectrometry results by immunoprecipitation assays. When CRC cells were co-cultured with BMSCs, FN was able to be immunoprecipitated with endogenous TROP2 protein (Fig. 4C). In addition, we co-transfected HEK293T cells with the plasmids encoding TROP2-Flag and FN, immunoprecipitation using either anti-Flag-tag or FN antibody both showed reciprocal interaction between exogenous TROP2-Flag and FN (Fig. 4D). To further verify the key region responsible for the interaction, a serial of TROP2 truncation mutants were generated (Fig. 4E). Deletion of either EpCAM domain (∆35–77) or Thyroglobulin domain (∆78–158) located in the TROP2 N-terminus abrogated its ability to interact with wild-type FN, implying that an intact N-terminus ectodomain was requisite for its adhesion to ECM (Fig. 4F). Immunofluorescence assays also showed that a proportion of TROP2 staining showed obvious co-localization with FN, in contrast to the other member of the same protein family, EpCAM, that overlapped signal with FN was hardly seen, further proving the specificity of the interaction between TROP2 and FN (Fig. 4G).

We then performed cell adhesion assays by encapsulating FN protein on plates. In consistent with the function of BMSCs, FN coating significantly promoted cell adhesion and TROP2 overexpression CRC cells showed stronger adhesive and invasive abilities in the presence of FN compared to the control cells (Fig. 5A and B). Live cell imaging showed that TROP2 overexpression CRC cells were the fastest to acquire a stretched cell shape in the FN coating plate, indicating that cell adhesive ability was enhanced (Fig. 5C). To further verify whether BMSCs were relied on FN to promote CRC adhesion, we used siRNAs to knock down FN in BMSCs before co-culture (Fig. 5D and Supplementary Fig. 3), and CRC cell adhesion promoted by TROP2 overexpression was significantly diminished under this situation. Moreover, overexpression the C-terminus deletion mutant of TROP2 (∆159–331), containing the function to interact with FN, reserved the adhesive ability of CRC cells to BMSCs as the wild-type TROP2, while other mutants show a reduction (Fig. 5E). The above results confirm that TROP2 promotes CRC cell adhesion to BMSCs by interacting with FN.

Integrin is required for TROP2-FN interaction mediated CRC cell adhesion

RGD (Arg-Gly-Asp) structure containing integrin is the primary receptor for FN and has been known for its role in the metastatic process of tumor cells. We first explored whether TROP2 affected integrin expression in CRC cells. Several common integrin subunits were selected for testing, and we found that three subunits, integrin α5, αV, and β3, were positively correlated with the expression of TROP2 (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. 4A-4 C). Subsequently, we knocked down individually the three integrin subunits in TROP2 overexpression CRC cells, and reduced expression of any integrin subunit significantly inhibited the binding of TROP2 to FN (Fig. 6B). Consistently, integrin depletion similarly inhibited the pro-adhesion ability of TROP2 in CRC cells (Fig. 6C). We then used P1F6, a neutralizing antibody of integrin αVβ5, and the immunoprecipitation assay again confirmed that integrin blocking could effectively diminish the interaction between TROP2 and FN (Fig. 6D). P1F6 and another neutralizing antibody of FN, HFN7.1, when incubated in the co-culture system, both effectively reduced the number of CRC cells adhering to BMSCs (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, the expression status of TROP2, FN, and integrin in patient tissues was examined by multiplex IHC (mIHC) staining. Within the same CRC patient, the expression levels of TROP2 and FN were higher in the bone metastases (BM) than those in the primary tumor (PT), and TROP2 showed significantly overlapped with integrin α5 in BM but hardly observed in PT (Fig. 6F). Together, these results suggest that integrin function is involved in TROP2-FN interaction, in support of its contributing role for CRC cell adhesion to bone marrow environment.

The ILK-Akt axis mediates the downstream signaling of TROP2-promoted cell adhesion

To further explore the downstream signaling of TROP2-promoted CRC cell adhesion, we first tried to verify if TROP2 has a direct interaction with integrin subunits, but it turned out that though FN was confirmed to interact with integrin α5, αV and β3, TROP2 was unable to immunoprecipitate with any of the above endogenous integrin subunits (Supplementary Fig. 5A and 5B). We then reviewed the mass spectrometry results of TROP2-purified cell lysates and one candidate, named integrin-linked kinase (ILK), aroused our interest (Table S1). ILK functions as a signal transductor and a scaffold protein through its interaction with integrins, facilitating further protein complex constitution to regulate various cellular processes including cell-matrix interactions, cell adhesion and anchorage-dependent cell growth [19]. Due to its fundamental role in mediating integrin-related signaling events, we examined whether ILK was associated with TROP2-regulated CRC cell adhesion. Firstly, exogenous TROP2 was showed to immunoprecipitate with and be co-localized with endogenous ILK in CRC cells (Fig. 7A and B), and ILK protein level was increased after TROP2 overexpression in CRC cells (Fig. 7C). ILK depletion also reduced the TROP2-FN interaction and functionally led to inhibitory effect on CRC cell adhesion (Fig. 7D and E). Previous research has reported that PI3K-Akt pathway is among the most important signalings for ILK-regulated cancer proliferation, invasion and metastasis [20, 21]. Immunoblotting assays showed that TROP2 overexpression could effectively promote the phosphorylation of Akt, and ILK depletion reduced its activation (Fig. 7F). In addition, TROP2 overexpression CRC cells treated with MK-2206, a small molecule inhibitor of Akt, showed decreased level of phosphorylated Akt as well as reduced number of adhesive CRC cells to BMSCs (Fig. 7G and H). These results suggest that the ILK-Akt axis is involved in TROP2-regulated CRC cell adhesion.

Targeting TROP2 alleviates CRC cell colonization and growth in the bone of mouse model

To investigate the in vivo effect of TROP2 overexpression on bone colonization, intratibial injection of CRC cells was adopted using nude mice (Fig. 8A). IVIS imaging showed that TROP2 overexpression CRC cells continued outgrown in the leg bone of mice compared to the control cells (Fig. 8B and C). The micro-CT analysis performed at a late stage of tumor colonization showed that a severe bone fracture was observed in mice injected with TROP2 overexpression CRC cells (Fig. 8D). We further tested the therapeutic efficacy of SG, a TROP2-targeted ADC drug, or volociximab, a monoclonal antibody targeting integrin α5β1, on the intratibial injection mouse models. Twice a week administration of either SG or volociximab individually with a recommended dose was quite effective to reduced tumor colonization and growth in the leg bone of mice (Fig. 8E and F). Taken together, these results suggest that targeting the TROP2- FN-integrin signaling may be a promising therapeutic strategy for CRC bone metastasis.

Discussion

Identifying new drivers is imperative for understanding the mechanisms underlying CRC bone metastasis and for defining novel therapeutic strategies. In this study, TROP2 was identified as a driver for CRC bone metastasis. We are the first to demonstrate that elevated TROP2 level was associated with bone metastasis in CRC patients. Considering the important role of BMSCs in cancer metastasis, we found that TROP2 overexpression enhanced CRC cell adhesion to BMSCs by co-culturing system. Mechanism study revealed that TROP2 interacted with FN expressed by BMSCs and promoted the assembly of Integrin-ILK signaling complex, thus facilitating the colonization of CRC cells in bone environment and further the formation of metastases (Supplementary Fig. 6).

TROP2 is found to be highly expressed in a variety of human cancers with minimum expression in normal tissues [22, 23]. Although previous research reported that TROP2 was also highly expressed in metastatic CRC [24, 25], the underlying mechanism has not yet been elucidated, neither to mention the correlation to bone metastasis. Several studies have indicated that TROP2 serves as a stemness marker [26] and drives epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and self-renewal through the activation of signaling cascades like Wnt/β-catenin pathway in breast cancer [8]. While in this study we focused on clarifying the adhesive function of the physical interaction between TROP2 and the bone marrow-derived FN, it is plausible that the role of TROP2 in promoting CRC bone metastasis may also be linked to its function in maintaining cancer stem cell (CSC)-like properties, especially given that TROP2 is encoded by the TACSTD2 gene located on chromosome 1p32, a region frequently altered in aggressive epithelial tumors. These findings suggest that TROP2 may enable CRC cells to adapt to the foreign bone microenvironment and initiate secondary tumor growth after the initial integrin-mediated adhesion. Consistently, the histopathological analysis of our CRC cohort showed that TROP2 expression was associated with M stage, and further analysis also confirmed that we cannot exclude its association with distant metastasis to other sites besides bone metastasis. These data suggest that targeting TROP2 may be an effective therapeutic approach in treating metastatic CRC.

SG is a TROP2-targeted ADC for delivery of moderately cytotoxic drug SN-38 and has received approval from the FDA for use in metastatic triple-negative breast cancer [27, 28]. In addition, SG showed promise in the clinical treatment of NSCLC [29, 30], pancreatic cancer [31], and prostate cancer [32]. In CRC, the use of SG is still on the clinical trial stage [33, 34], and the standard treatment for CRC bone metastatic lesions is to relieve the patient’s pain and to extend survival time through chemotherapy and bisphosphonates [2, 35]. Our results found that SG was quite effective in the mouse model of CRC with bone metastases, as TROP2-overexpressing CRC bone metastases retrieved in a short period after intravenous injection of SG. Therefore, our findings provide new application ideas for SG in metastatic CRC.

FN binds to different integrin subunits to regulate various physiological processes, and in cancer metastasis, it is mainly involved in regulating cell invasion and adhesion [36, 37]. Integrins are cell surface receptors that recognize the RGD motif of FN and mediate cell-to-extracellular matrix and cell-to-cell adhesion [38]. In our study, we observed that TROP2-mediated adhesion specifically relied on the upregulation of integrin subunits α5, αV, and β3. These subunits were selected because they are primary components of the major FN-binding integrins, which are critical for early-stage tumor colonization in the metastatic niches and our Western blotting results also confirmed that TROP2 overexpression significantly increased the expressions of these specific subunits in both HCT116 and HT29 cell lines. We further demonstrated that these integrins are not merely downstream effectors but are requisite for the stability of the TROP2-FN complex, because knockdown of ITGA5, ITGAV or ITGB3 effectively disrupted the interaction between TROP2 and FN, as well as the adhesion of CRC cells to BMSC monolayers, highlighting their dispensable role in facilitating the initial tethering of metastatic CRC cell to the bone marrow environment.

Meanwhile, based on the discovery that integrin α5 was involved in the mechanistic axis of TROP2-FN interaction, we became interested in whether volociximab, a therapeutic monoclonal antibody targeting integrin α5β1, showed treatment efficacy in CRC bone metastases. Volociximab-related studies in a wide range of cancers are still in phase II clinical trials [39–42], with differing opinions on this drug in phase III clinical trials [40]. However, the results of available studies showed that volociximab was a promising anti-cancer agent when combined with chemotherapy [42, 43]. Specifically, Pantano et al. found that integrin α5 was highly expressed in bone metastases compared to other metastases in breast cancer [44], bearing a similar expression pattern in our CRC cohort. The use of volociximab markedly reduced tumor outgrowth in breast cancer bone metastases and blunted cancer-associated bone destruction. Although integrin α5β1 usually act as a complex to mediate the binding to FN [45], our results excluded the regulatory role of integrin β1 in the interaction between TROP2 and FN. Previous reports have shown that integrin α5 and β1 exert different functions. Integrin α5 mainly regulates the assembly process of FN matrix, whereas integrin β1 mainly acts on the RGD structures [46, 47]. This may be one reason why the two subunits perform differently in our scenario. Furthermore, integrin α5 was found to prevent BMSC anoikis in a study based on rat model [48], which provides some correlative indication to our histopathological findings that substantial co-localization of integrin α5 with FN and TROP2 was only observed in bone metastatic lesions, but not in lung metastatic lesions and primary CRC tissues. Also, we were interested in how TROP2 affects the expression and function of integrins. In the mass spectrum results derived from co-cultured system of CRC cells and BMSCs, we found that ILK was also enriched by TROP2 overexpression. Further analysis confirmed that TROP2 regulated the expression of integrins as well as downstream PI3K-Akt pathway through its interaction with ILK. ILK was known to be associated with CRC metastasis [49], and in our study, reducing ILK expression attenuated the interaction between TROP2 and FN, and inhibited CRC cell adhesion to BMSCs. Due to their obvious importance, the role of integrin α5 and ILK in the CRC bone metastasis is worthy of further exploration.

Lastly, despite the promising efficacy of SG and Volociximab in our in vivo studies, several challenges remain for clinical translation. First, our study utilized a xenograft model in immunodeficient mice. While this model is effective for studying tumor colonization and growth, it may not fully reflect the intricate interactions between the drugs and a functional human immune system—a critical factor for the therapeutic action of ADCs like SG. Second, clinical bone metastases are often characterized by extensive osteolysis and established structural damage by the time of diagnosis, whereas our model targeted relatively early colonization. Finally, while Volociximab has shown a favorable safety profile in Phase II trials for other cancers, the long-term safety and potential for emergent resistance when combined with TROP2-targeted therapies require further investigation in advanced CRC patients. Therefore, future clinical investigations will be essential in order to offer more precise treatment options for the patients.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that TROP2 promotes the development of CRC bone metastasis by interacting with bone marrow-derived FN-integrin axis. Through its adhesive function, TROP2 may coordinate with the specialized bone niche to facilitate metastatic colonization and establish a permissive environment for stromal-tumor interactions. Targeting this axis offers a promising therapeutic strategy for managing bone metastasis in CRC and potentially other TROP2-overexpressing malignancies.
