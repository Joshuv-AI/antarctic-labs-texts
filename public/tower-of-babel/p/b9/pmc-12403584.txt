A single-cell transcriptome atlas of pig skin reveals cellular heterogeneity from embryonic development to postnatal aging
Authors: Ting Zheng, Rong Yuan, Yu Zhang, Qin Zou, Yifei Wang, Yujing Li, Zhengyin Gong, Zhengli Chen, Yanzhi Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMammalian skin exhibits profound cellular and molecular restructuring across lifespan, yet an integrated single-cell mapping from embryogenesis to senescence remains limited. The Chenghua (CH) pig, with exceptional skin thickness characteristics, provides a promising model for investigating human skin development and physiology.ResultsWe constructed a comprehensive single-cell RNA atlas of 443,529 cells from CH pig skin spanning 10 developmental stages (embryonic day 56 to postnatally year 7). Our analysis identified eight major skin cell types and revealed stage-specific shifts in cellular composition. Fibroblasts (FBs) and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) dominated embryonic development while smooth muscle cells and endothelial cells increased postnatally, with aging marked by FB dysfunction and significant dermal thinning. Pseudotime trajectory analysis identified that FBs differentiated from a common progenitor with MSCs, diverging into five functionally distinct subpopulations including papillary, reticular, mesenchymal, pro-inflammatory, and a novel AUTS2⁺ subtype with neuromodulatory roles. Critically, FBs regulated postnatal skin aging via COL1A1-(ITGA1 + ITGB1) and MDK-SDC1 interaction signaling pathways, with the transcription factor EGR1 regulating collagen-related genes (DPT, COL12A1, COL5A2) during development; the age-dependent suppression of FBs coincided with collagen downregulation, reduced intercellular communication, and elevated transcriptional noise. Concurrently, immune cells including dendritic cells (DCs) and T cells (TCs) exhibited a marked decrease of cell numbers perinatally, with cytotoxic NKT cells reaching peak abundance at rapid growth stage; DCs and TCs primarily utilized SPP1 and TGF-β signaling pathways to regulate skin immunity during development and aging. Cross-species analysis confirmed the evolutionary conservation of skin cell types and FB functional gene profiles related to ECM deposition and inflammatory responses across pigs, humans, and mice during development and aging.ConclusionsThis work delineates cellular dynamics underpinning skin homeostasis, uncovers the vital physiological functions of FBs and immune cells during skin development and aging, and validates the pig model for human cutaneous physiology research. The atlas serves as a pivotal resource for skin mechanistic and translational studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-025-02390-w.

Background

As the outermost protective barrier of the body, mammalian skin is composed of two primary layers, including the upper epidermis and the lower dermis, each performing distinct yet vital physiological functions [1]. The epidermis serves as the primary interface with the external environment, with its protective function predominantly mediated by terminally differentiated, non-proliferating keratinocytes (KCs) [2]. Beneath this layer, the dermis contains a complex extracellular matrix (ECM) populated by dermal fibroblasts (FBs), which collectively confer mechanical strength and elastic properties to the skin [3, 4]. While KCs and FBs represent the predominant cell types, the skin also harbors numerous specialized cell types, including peripheral nervous system components, vascular endothelial cells, melanocytes, and both innate and adaptive immune cells, all of which contribute to cutaneous diverse functionalities [5].

Dermal FBs play critical roles in multiple biological functions, including collagen synthesis, regulation of epidermal hair follicle formation, and modulation of the skin immune responses [6, 7]. On the other hand, immune cells participate in various immunological processes such as cutaneous wound healing, tolerance induction, and disease prevention [7]. Notably, FBs can directly interact with immune cells to enhance ECM protein production [8], suggesting their synergistic contribution to skin homeostasis maintenance. Nevertheless, the underlying mechanisms through which skin FBs and immune cells coordinately regulate skin homeostasis from embryogenesis through aging remain elusive.

As a highly heterogeneous and dynamic organ, mammalian skin undergoes profound phenotypic and functional transformations between prenatal and postnatal stages, necessitating precise coordination among specialized cell types. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) offers a powerful approach to systematically decipher the intricate cellular organization of physiological skin across embryonic development and postnatal aging. During fetal skin morphogenesis, scRNA-seq analyses have elucidated the dynamic heterogeneity of human immune cells and murine hair follicle epithelial/dermal lineages [9–11]. Similarly, recent studies have provided mechanistic insights into cell-type-specific drivers of human skin aging at the single-cell resolution [12, 13]. While Gary Reynolds et al. mapped transcriptional landscapes from fetal to adult human skin and compared cellular states across developmental and homeostatic conditions [14], a comprehensive, high-resolution single-cell atlas spanning mammalian skin from embryogenesis to senescence remains to be fully established.

The pig (Sus scrofa) serves as an established model for human skin research due to its anatomical and physiological similarities with human skin [15, 16]. The Chenghua (CH) pig, a Chinese indigenous breed, exhibits exceptional skin thickness characteristics. Our previous measurements showed that CH pigs possess significantly thicker skin (8.5 vs. 3.0 mm) and 2.7-fold higher collagen content (58.6 vs. 21.8 g/cm2) compared to Large White pigs [17]. Notably, dermal FB-derived small extracellular vesicles (SEVs) from CH pigs showed enhanced efficacy in promoting FB proliferation, migration, collagen synthesis, and contraction compared to Large White pig SEVs [18]. Recently, we identified the conservation of skin cell types between CH pigs, humans, and mice, with shared expression of most marker genes [19]. The findings provide the CH pig as a promising model for investigating human skin development and physiology.

Key time stages in pig skin morphogenesis have been well characterized. During embryogenesis, skin precursor was established by embryonic day 52 (E52) with robust differentiation and development capacity [20]. Primary hair follicles’ initiation occurs between E55 and 73, with central/lateral follicles appearing first [21]. Dermal stratification begins at E67 alongside initial collagen deposition [22]. A critical window for skin accessory development occurs at E76–85, marked by completion of sebaceous glands and arrector pili muscle formation in secondary follicles [21]. By late gestation (E105), fetal skin achieves structural maturity, serving as a prenatal functional benchmark [23, 24]. Postnatally, neonatal skin undergoes rapid changes from an amniotic fluid environment to air exposure. Two key developmental phases occur at postnatal day 90 (D90) and D180, coinciding with growth spurts and sexual maturation, when FB activity and sebaceous gland function peak [25–27]. Aging progression shows distinct milestones that 3-year-old (Y3) pigs exhibit middle-aged characteristics including collagen loosening and elastin reduction [27], while Y6 pigs demonstrate conserved aging signatures with humans, as evidenced by cross-species epigenetic clock correlations in multiple tissues [28].

To systematically characterize cellular transcriptional dynamics during skin development and aging, we conducted scRNA-seq analysis of CH pig skin across embryonic (E56, E66, E76, E86, E105) and postnatal (D3, D90, D180, Y3, Y7) stages. Using these samples, we established a comprehensive single-cell transcriptome atlas and identified eight distinct skin cell types with unique gene expression profiles. We then delineated dynamic changes in cellular heterogeneity across developmental stages. Particular focus was given to FBs and immune cells to elucidate their developmental and aging-associated dynamics. The cross-species conservation of skin cells in development and aging was uncovered. This work provides a valuable resource for understanding skin biology, offering insights into developmental programming and aging mechanisms underlying cutaneous homeostasis.

Results

scRNA-seq profiling of pig skin from embryonic development to postnatal aging

To delineate the dynamics of skin cell types and their transcription profiles from embryonic development to postnatal aging, we collected skin tissues from CH pigs across ten stages spanning E56 to Y7 (Fig. 1a; Additional file 1: Fig. S1a). Phenotypically, skin thickness increased substantially postnatally, concomitant with continuous ECM accumulation of in the dermis. However, aging (Y7) was associated with a significant reduction in skin thickness (~ 1600 μm decrease compared to Y3) (Additional file 1: Fig. S1b). Histological analysis revealed that embryonic skin at E56 and E66 possessed thin epidermal and dermal layers. Hair follicle morphogenesis initiated from E76 onwards, alongside gradual dermal thickening. By the perinatal period (E105 and D3), the skin exhibited a fully developed morphological structure (Fig. 1b). scRNA-seq of these samples was performed on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (Fig. 1a). Following quality control, we obtained a total of 443,529 high-quality skin cells, and the number of cells per stage, derived from three biological replicates, ranged from approximately 30,000 to 66,000 (Fig. 1b; Additional file 2: Table S1). On average, 1549 genes and 5922 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) were detected per cell (Additional file 1: Fig. S1c; Additional file 2: Table S1).

Subsequent clustering and annotation identified 24 clusters representing 8 major cell types, which comprised fibroblasts (FBs, 51.4% of total cells, n = 227,911), endothelial cells (ECs, 15.3%, n = 67,649), smooth muscle cells (SMCs, 20.1%, n = 89,267), skin-derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs, 8.4%, n = 37,278), keratinocytes (KCs, 0.8%, n = 3749), dendritic cells (DCs, 1.8%, n = 8176), T cells (TCs, 0.7%, n = 3205), and neural cells (NCs, 1.4%, n = 6294) (Fig. 1c; Additional file 3: Fig. S2; Additional file 4: Table S2). Cell type-specific marker genes were identified based on expression patterns (Additional file 1: Fig. S1d; Additional file 5: Table S3a). For example, FBs specifically expressed COL1A1 and COL1A2, PECAM1, and CDH5 for ECs, ACTA2 and MYH11 for SMCs, TOP2A and CENPF for MSCs, KRT5 and KRT15 for KCs, SLA-DQA1 and SLA-DQB1 for DCs, CD3E and CD247 for TCs, and MPZ and MBP for NCs, respectively (Fig. 1d, e; Additional file 1: Fig. S1e). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of cell type-specific genes (Additional file 5: Table S3b) revealed distinct functional associations that FBs were enriched for ECM organization, and DCs and TCs for immune system process, while SMCs, ECs, MSCs, KCs, and NCs were linked to regulation of metabolic process, blood vessel morphogenesis, cell cycle process, peptide biosynthetic process, and ensheathment of neurons, respectively (Additional file 1: Fig. S1f).

Dynamic cellular changes and differential gene profiles in skin cells from embryonic development to postnatal aging

The emergence of distinct skin cell types is stage-specific during development [14]. Marked dynamic changes in the cell type proportions occurred throughout skin histogenesis and development. FBs predominated during the embryonic stage, followed by MSCs (Fig. 2a, b), a finding supported by high expression of cell cycle-related genes in the two embryonic cell populations (Additional file 6: Fig. S3). Postnatally, SMCs and ECs became the dominant cell populations, with TCs and KCs showing relative increases compared to prenatal levels (Fig. 2b), demonstrating the diversification of functional cell populations in the skin after birth. Notably, aged skin (Y7) exhibited an increase in the number of FBs but a decrease in ECs (Fig. 2b), indicating that the number of blood vessels represented by ECs declines in aging skin. To resolve temporal gene dynamics, we performed cross-stage differential analysis on each cell type. Based on the pseudotime trajectory construction of pseudobulk RNA-seq profiles for all samples, we identified six critical phases (E66, E86, E105, D90, Y3, and Y7) exhibiting maximal transcriptomic divergence (Additional file 8: Fig. S5a). Comparative analysis revealed sustained gene expression dynamism in FBs throughout development, whereas MSCs, SMCs, ECs, and DCs displayed stage-restricted differentially expressed genes (DEGs) patterns (Additional file 8: Fig. S5b). Strikingly, a significant subset of DEGs across all cell types encoded immune-related factors, including CCL2, C1QA, C1QC, IL6, the CXCL gene family, and the S100A gene family (Additional file 8: Fig. S5b; Additional file 9: Table S4), indicating notable changes in immune responses across skin development and aging. Heatmaps visualizing expression of these significant DEGs across all 10 time points further illustrated the temporal dynamics of key gene expression changes within each of eight skin cell types from embryogenesis through aging (Fig. 2c).

GO analysis of these stage-specific DEGs indicated functional maturation and aging of skin cell populations (Fig. 2d; Additional file 10: Table S5). From E66 to E86, FBs exhibited enhanced functional involvement in ECM organization, KCs displayed enhanced skin morphogenesis activity, and NCs exhibited heightened glial cell differentiation, which corresponded to the dynamic expression of COL1A2, KRT15, and MPZ genes, respectively (Fig. 2c, d). These cellular activities correspond to key biological processes of skin organogenesis. From E86 to E105, FBs-mediated ECM organization and DCs-mediated immunomodulation capacity decreased, while the heat response capabilities of NCs and immune cells increased (Fig. 2d), indicating that skin exhibits dynamic immune response in the late stages of embryonic development. Comparison of postnatal D90 and embryonic E105 revealed increased regulation of the immune system process and response to stimulus in D90 skin, suggesting postnatal enhancement of skin immunity (Fig. 2d). Functional declines were observed in adult skin (Y3) compared to D90, including reduced cell migration, inflammatory response, blood vessel development, and ECM organization (Fig. 2d). Finally, DCs in aged skin (Y7) significantly upregulated functions related to immune endocytosis and defense responses (Fig. 2d), suggesting that chronic inflammation may persist in aging skin, also known as “inflammatory aging” [29]. Collectively, these results demonstrated significant temporal dynamics in cellular heterogeneity and gene expression programs across embryonic, postnatal, and senescent stages of skin, contributing to our understanding of the biological processes underlying skin development and aging.

Origin of skin cell differentiation during embryonic development

The complex heterogeneity of skin cells arises from the skin differentiation and functional maturation during the embryonic period [30]. To delineate the developmental origin of embryonic skin cells, we employed cell differentiation trajectory analysis spanning the embryonic to neonatal stages (E56 to D3). Our 3D trajectory analysis revealed that embryonic skin MSCs and FBs share a common progenitor cell population (Fig. 3a). The common progenitor status and high proliferative capacity of embryonic MSCs and FBs (Additional file 6: Fig. S3) explained their numerical dominance in embryonic skin (Fig. 2b). Subsequently, during late embryonic development, these cells exhibited bidirectional plasticity, with a subset of FBs further differentiating into SMCs (Fig. 3a; Additional file 11: Fig. S6a, b). Pseudotime trajectory analysis further delineated two critical branching points governing skin cell fate commitment (Fig. 3b; Additional file 11: Fig. S6c). At the first branch point, the common MSC/FB progenitor diverged, with one path leading to FB differentiation and the other specifying the SMC lineage. The branching point of differentiation accounts for the number expansion of SMCs (Fig. 2b). The second branch point involved other subpopulation of FBs, with the remaining cells diversifying into KCs, NCs, and ECs. The branching point contributes to temporally with the emergence of various cell types during late embryonic and postnatal stages (Fig. 3b), such as KCs’ first appearance at E86 (Additional file 7: Fig. S4). Early embryonic stages showed the appearance and differentiation of MSCs and FBs, while SMCs, ECs, and NCs emerged in large numbers later, with T cells appearing perinatally (Additional file 11: Fig. S6d). These patterns aligned with alterations in cell-type proportions at various developmental stages (Fig. 2b), as elucidated by the pseudotime analysis.

To characterize the underlying transcriptional dynamics, we first performed pseudotime-based gene cluster analysis, identifying five distinct gene clusters with unique expression patterns (Additional file 12: Table S6). GO enrichment analysis of these clusters revealed some key biological processes in the skin cell differentiation, including cell cycle regulation (e.g., representative genes for CCND2, CDK4, CENPF), intracellular signal transduction (e.g., TGFBR2, STAT1, RASA1), ECM organization (e.g., COL1A1, COL3A1, ITGB3), cellular protein metabolic regulation (e.g., HSPD1, HSP90AB1, UBE2K), and negative regulation of response to stimuli (e.g., DUSP1, NUPR1, CD44), respectively (Fig. 3c; Additional file 12: Table S6). Furthermore, the expression of marker genes for each cell type further illustrated the temporal transcriptional dynamics (Additional file 11: Fig. S6e). For example, COL1A1, a marker gene of FBs, was highly expressed early and middle in differentiation, while PECAM1, a marker gene of ECs, was highly expressed late in differentiation. Collectively, embryonic skin development follows a branched differentiation path, with stem cell-like progenitors evolving into specialized lineages crucial for skin structure and function, such as FBs, SMCs, ECs, and KCs.

DCs were classified into macrophage-like DCs, Langerhans cells, conventional DCs, proliferative DCs, and migrative DCs, characterized by distinct marker genes (C1QC, IL1R2, CADM1, CENPF, and AQP1, respectively) (Fig. 4b; Additional file 13: Fig. S7a; Additional file 14: Table S7). GO analysis revealed functional specialization that macrophage-like DCs exhibited macrophage-like properties, including endocytosis regulation, while conventional DCs displayed classical dendritic cell functions linking to cytokine secretion and T cell activation. Proliferative DCs were associated with the cell cycle process, while migrative DCs were enriched for functions related to the cell motility regulation (Additional file 13: Fig. S7c). During embryonic stages (E56–E86), macrophage-like DCs, proliferative DCs, and conventional DCs predominated (Fig. 4c; Additional file 15: Table S8), suggesting their primary roles in supporting prenatal skin cell proliferation and development. Postnatally, a significant shift occurred that migrative DCs emerged, and the proportion of Langerhans cells substantially increased (Fig. 4c; Additional file 15: Table S8), indicating a functional transition toward immune surveillance, antigen presentation, and T cell activation.

TCs were categorized into immune effector T cells, NKT cells, proliferative T cells, and TM4SF1+ T cells based on gene expression (Fig. 4e; Additional file 13: Fig. S7b; Additional file 16: Table S9). GO analysis showed that immune effector T cells served as primary effectors exerting immune responses; NKT cells possessed strong cytotoxic capabilities; proliferative T cells were linked to the cell cycle regulation; and TM4SF1+ T cells were implicated in the regulating cell motility and localization (Additional file 13: Fig. S7d). Immune effector T cells consistently constituted the most abundant T cell subtype in the skin. Notably, the number of NKT cells urged dramatically at postnatal D90, reaching peak abundance (Fig. 4f; Additional file 17: Table S10). Quantification of total TCs revealed a dynamic trajectory characterized by an increase from E56 to E86, followed by a decline throughout the perinatal period (E105 and D3) extending to D180, with subsequent rapid rebound at Y3 and sustained elevation into old age (Y7) (Fig. 4f; Additional file 17: Table S10). Overall, this pattern underscores the heightened immune activity mediated by skin TCs, particularly in adulthood and senescence.

A striking finding was the markedly reduced abundance of both DCs and TCs specifically during the perinatal period (E105 and D3), contrasting sharply with other developmental stages. Furthermore, we identified several chronological genes associated with immune cell development dynamics (Fig. 4g, h; Additional file 13: Fig. S7e). CCL5 and SLA-DQA1, which were upregulated in DCs during the senescence period, were associated with immune effects, while ANXA1 and LITAF, which were upregulated in TCs at D90, were associated with NKT cell cytotoxicity (Fig. 4g, h). Analysis of intercellular communication revealed DCs primarily engaged in the SPP1 signaling pathway, associated with the inflammatory responses and the activation of immune cells [31], while TCs predominantly utilized the TGF-β signaling pathway, related to T cell differentiation and immune defense [32] (Fig. 4i, j). The changes in the communication intensity of these two signaling pathways indicated that the immune activation of DCs weakened with age, while the immune defense of TCs remained relatively stable throughout the entire development process (Additional file 18: Fig. S8).

Dynamic changes in fibroblasts during skin development and aging

Dermal fibroblasts, the major cellular source of cutaneous ECM, develop into highly heterogeneous subpopulations during skin development and adulthood [33]. Quantification of subtype proportions revealed dynamic changes across the lifespan. The proportion of reticular FBs significantly increased postnatally but declined markedly in aged skin (Y7) (Fig. 5b; Additional file 21: Table S12). Conversely, mesenchymal FBs were significantly more abundant during embryonic development than postnatally, suggesting a developmental trajectory where mesenchymal-like precursors differentiate into functionally specialized FB subtypes (Fig. 5b).

Furthermore, analysis of genes associated with collagen formation (Additional file 22: Table S13) demonstrated a progressive downregulation commencing postnatally, with the most pronounced reduction observed in aged FBs (Y7) (Fig. 5d, e). This decline in collagen gene expression coincided with diminished COL1A1-(ITGA1 + ITGB1) interaction signaling (Additional file 19: Fig. S9b), collectively indicating a progressive attenuation of cutaneous ECM synthesis capacity after birth. Downregulated genes during postnatal development and aging encompassed established collagen-related genes (COL1A1, COL6A2, ECM2) as well as less frequently implicated genes (HTRA1, RCN3, BMP1) (Fig. 5f).

Dynamic transcriptional and cellular communication changes in fibroblasts during skin development and aging

Cell–cell communication is critical for coordinating biological processes in multicellular organisms [34]. We performed intercellular communication analysis on skin cells from all samples, revealing FBs as the most active signal-secreting cells in skin, followed by MSCs, with the COLLAGEN being their predominant outgoing signaling pathway (Additional file 23: Fig. S10a, b). The results demonstrated that FBs consistently secreted signals to other cell types throughout skin development and aging (Additional file 23: Fig. S10c, d). Notably, identical cell types exhibited distinct cellular communication patterns at different developmental stages. For example, the embryonic NCs primarily communicated with ECs via the ANGPTL signaling pathway, whereas postnatal D90 NCs mainly interacted with MSCs and FBs through pathways including NCAM and COLLAGEN (Additional file 24: Table S14), indicating the functional dynamic effects of cells at different developmental stages.

Intercellular communication is mediated through specific signaling pathways that regulate target gene expression via downstream transcription factors (TFs), thereby altering cell fate and function [35]. We therefore characterized key TF profiles of each cell type. Results showed that FBs and MSCs possessed the most similar transcriptional profiles, while the two immune cell types also exhibited similarity (Additional file 25: Fig. S11a). Further analysis of TF-gene regulatory networks revealed cell type-specific transcriptional regulation patterns associated with cell properties (Additional file 25: Fig. S11b). For instance, in FBs, the TF EGR1 regulated the highest number of target genes associated with ECM depositions, such as COL3A1, COL5A2, and ECM2 (Additional file 25: Fig. S11b, c; Additional file 26: Table S15). Clustering analysis of regulon activities indicated dynamic changes in the transcriptional activities of these specific TFs, potentially regulating skin cell development and aging (Additional file 25: Fig. S11d; Additional file 27: Table S16). The activity scores of EGR1(+) regulon in FBs were elevated during the embryonic period and progressively declined throughout the postnatal aging process (Additional file 25: Fig. S11e). The expression of collagen-related genes like DPT, COL12A1, and COL5A2, targeted by EGR1(+), aligned with dynamic alterations in EGR1(+) transcriptional activity (Additional file 25: Fig. S11f).

Senescent organisms exhibit extensive alterations in cytokine secretion quantity and type [37]. Our analysis of cellular communication showed a decreasing number of interactions among skin cells with skin aging (Fig. 6d). While diverse cellular interactions occurred between different cell types in D3 skin, communication in Y7 skin was primarily maintained between FBs and other cell types (Fig. 6e), indicating a dominant role for FBs in aged skin communication. Comparative analysis of FBs-sourcing signaling pathways in D3 and Y7 skin cells showed significant downregulation of LAMININ and COLLAGEN signaling, but upregulation of MK signaling in aged skin (Fig. 6f, g). Receptor-ligand pairs analysis confirmed a decrease of LAMA2-(ITGA1 + ITGB1), COL1A1-(ITGA1 + ITGB1), and an increase of MDK-SDC1 interactions in aged FBs (Fig. 6h; Additional file 19: Fig. S9b). Furthermore, analysis of incoming communication patterns demonstrated substantial differences in signaling received by various cell types in aged (Y7) versus young (D3) skin (Fig. 6i).

Cross-species analysis of skin cells from pigs, humans, and mice

To validate pigs as a reliable model for human skin biology through conserved cellular traits in skin across species, we performed a cross-species comparative analysis by integrating single-cell transcriptome data from pig, human, and mouse skin across corresponding developmental (embryonic and young) and aging (aged) stages (Additional file 29: Fig. S13a–d). Cell-type annotation revealed that major skin cell types, including FBs, SMCs, ECs, KCs, NCs, MSCs, and immune cells, were largely conserved across all three species (Fig. 7a). However, adipose-associated FBs and neural crest-derived mesenchyme were predominantly detected in mouse and human skin, while a myogenic cell population was annotated in all three species (Fig. 7a). Quantitative analysis indicated higher proportions of FBs, ECs, and SMCs in pig skin compared to those in human and mouse skin, whereas human skin exhibited a particularly high proportion of KCs (Additional file 29: Fig. S13e). Despite these compositional differences, the expression patterns of classical marker genes defining the major skin cell types were remarkably consistent across three species (Fig. 7b). Given the pivotal role of FBs in regulating dermal structure formation, we isolated FBs from each species for focused cross-species comparative analysis. We identified six distinct FB clusters exhibiting differential distributions across the three species (Additional file 29: Fig. S13f, g). Notably, pig FB clusters (2, 3, 6) showed significant enrichment for GO terms associated with ECM organization compared to human and mouse counterparts (Additional file 29: Fig. S13h). To delineate conserved and divergent transcriptional dynamics of FBs during skin maturation and aging, we performed differential gene expression analysis across developmental (young vs. embryonic) and aging (aged vs. young) transitions within each species, followed by cross-species comparisons to identify species-specific and conserved gene sets. Analysis revealed substantial species-specific differences in FB gene expression patterns within the same age group. However, humans and mice shared a greater number of overlapping genes with each other than either species shared with pigs (Additional file 30: Fig. S14a). We next identified DEGs (upregulated and downregulated) during skin development transition (young vs. embryonic) and the skin aging transition (aged vs. young) for each species, enabling subsequent identification of species-specific and conserved DEG signatures.

First, we compared the skin development transition (young vs. embryonic) across three species. Heatmap visualization of DEGs identified 309 genes specifically upregulated in pig (pig-up) and 2543 genes commonly upregulated in human and mouse (human/mouse-up) (Fig. 7d; Additional file 30: Fig. S14b; Additional file 31: Table S17). Conversely, 2048 genes were uniquely downregulated in pig (pig-down), versus 1165 genes commonly downregulated in human and mouse (human/mouse-down) (Fig. 7d; Additional file 30: Fig. S14b; Additional file 31: Table S17). GO enrichment analysis confirmed these distinctions that pig-up genes were significantly enriched for processes including vasculature development, and chaperone-mediated protein folding (Additional file 30: Figure S14c; Additional file 31: Table S17). In contrast, human/mouse-up genes were enriched for terms such as phosphorylation, and negative regulation of protein modification process (Additional file 30: Fig. S14c; Additional file 31: Table S17). Pig-down genes were predominantly associated with RNA processing, and protein transport. Human/mouse-down genes, however, showed enrichment for regulation of mitotic cell cycle phase transition (Additional file 30: Fig. S14c; Additional file 31: Table S17). Despite these species-specific differences in DEGs during development, key genes governing fundamental development processes like cell differentiation and ECM deposition exhibited highly consistent directional changes across all species. Specifically, the expression of cell differentiation-associated genes MDK and SFRP2 was coordinately downregulated in young skin compared to embryonic skin across pigs, humans, and mice. Conversely, expression of ECM-associated genes COL6A3 and ELN was coordinately upregulated during this developmental transition in all three species (Fig. 7e). These findings demonstrated that despite species-specific differences in DEGs function during skin development, core biological processes like cell differentiation and ECM accumulation were consistently conserved across the three species.

Next, we examined transcriptomic changes associated with the skin aging transition (aged vs. young) across the three species. The aging-associated DEGs heatmap showed 1571 genes specifically upregulated in pig (pig-up) and 755 genes commonly upregulated in human and mouse (human/mouse-up) (Fig. 7f; Additional file 30: Fig. S14b; Additional file 31: Table S17). Similarly, 604 genes were specifically downregulated in pig (pig-down), versus 372 genes commonly downregulated in human and mouse (human/mouse-down) (Fig. 7f; Additional file 30: Fig. S14b; Additional file 31: Table S17). GO enrichment analysis revealed a notable pattern that pig-up genes and human/mouse-down genes were both significantly enriched for processes related to peptide biosynthesis, amide biosynthesis, and translation (Additional file 30: Fig. S14c; Additional file 31: Table S17). Conversely, human/mouse-up genes and pig-down genes were co-enriched for terms including cell-substrate adhesion, and angiogenesis (Additional file 30: Fig. S14c; Additional file 31: Table S17). These results uncovered that genes specifically up- and downregulated during skin aging were functionally opposite in pig and human/mouse. Despite this overall pattern of opposite functions, key genes regulating ECM deposition again demonstrated consistent directional changes across species. Specifically, expression of the ECM-related genes ELN and FBN2 was coordinately downregulated and upregulated, respectively, in aged skin relative to young skin across all species. Furthermore, expression of inflammation-associated senescence genes CXCL14 and MASP1 was coordinately upregulated during aging in pigs, humans, and mice (Fig. 7g). These results indicated that while global transcriptomics during skin aging varied between species in FBs, core aspects of aging biology, including ECM degradation/remodeling and inflammatory responses, exhibited strong evolutionary conservation.

Discussion

Mammalian skin, a highly heterogeneous and dynamic organ, undergoes dramatic phenotypic and functional shifts before and after birth, requiring coordinated action by specialized cell types. Given the anatomical and physiological similarities between pig and human skin [38–40], and recognizing CH pigs could as a potentially ideal model for human skin biology research [17–19], we constructed a large-scale single-cell atlas of CH pig skin spanning embryonic development to postnatal aging, uncovering the cellular heterogeneity underlying these transitions. This resource provides a foundation for investigating physiological skin aging, dermatosis pathogenesis, and clinical research related to pig skin xenotransplantation.

We annotated eight major skin cell types in CH pig. FBs, ECs, KCs, and DCs, commonly identified in human and mouse studies, shared classical marker genes [14, 41, 42]. Notably, SMCs were significantly more abundant in postnatal pig skin, likely reflecting the skin’s rich vascularity and well-developed arrector pili muscles (APMs) during maturation [43]. This finding aligns with our prior characterization of CH pig skin [19], but contrasts with reports in humans or mice [44, 45]. Discrepancy in captured cell types and proportions may arise from species heterogeneity, distinct skin developmental programs, or differences in scRNA-seq platforms. Intriguingly, the NCs, associated with glial cell formation, were markedly more numerous at E105 and D3 compared with other stages, suggesting heightened activity of the skin nervous system during the pig perinatal period, potentially enhancing environmental perception. Furthermore, this finding may inform research on sensory responses in pig skin xenotransplantation.

Given skin as a reservoir for adult stem cells of diverse embryonic origins [46], elucidating the developmental origins and differentiation trajectories of skin cells is crucial for guiding skin regeneration, repair, and organoid research. Here, we analyzed cell differentiation trajectories across six timepoints (embryonic and neonatal), modeling the origins and key branch points during embryonic skin development. Our results reveal distinct cellular lineage origins and bifurcating differentiation pathways. Embryonic skin MSCs and FBs originate from a common progenitor, partially differentiating into SMCs in late embryogenesis, while the remainder gives rise to FB subpopulations, KCs, and ECs. Previous studies using Monocle2 focused on hair follicle-associated cell trajectories in mouse embryos (E13.5, E16.5, P0) [11], while PAGA analysis in human embryonic skin (7–10 PCW) emphasized keratinocyte lineage development [14]. In contrast, our analysis encompassed mid-to-late embryonic stages with denser temporal sampling. By integrating multiple trajectory methods (Monocle2 and Monocle3), we specifically explored the origins and fates of key skin cell types. The sequential gene expression dynamics, ranging from proliferation (CCND2, CDK4) and signaling activators (TGFBR2, STAT1) to ECM producers (COL1A1, COL3A1) and protein metabolizer (UBE2K), provide a mechanistic basis for the differentiation trajectories of skin cells, with driving skin morphogenesis. This work addresses a current research gap and offers valuable insights into skin regenerative medicine.

FBs, the predominant cell type in mammalian skin [33], were similarly prevalent in our dataset. However, classifications of FB subtypes vary considerably across studies [33, 47, 48]. Our analysis identified five transcriptionally distinct FB subtypes, including conventional papillary and reticular FBs, proinflammatory FBs and mesenchymal FBs involved in tissue repair, and a novel subtype uniquely characterized by AUTS2 expression (AUTS2⁺ FBs). Notably, while Cai et al. reported inflammation-induced Tnc⁺ FBs exhibiting neuromodulatory features in mouse models [49], our defined AUTS2⁺ FBs displayed functional enrichment in axonogenesis and neuronal projection guidance. This suggests that specialized FB subtypes, including both Tnc⁺ and AUTS2⁺ FBs, may mediate skin neuromodulation.

We specifically investigated the heterogeneity of skin FBs across developmental stages. Integration of these findings with differential expression analysis of collagen synthesis-related genes in FBs reveals intrinsic mechanisms underlying senescent skin phenotypes and corroborates prior observations of FB functional decline in aged human skin [12]. At the transcription factor level, EGR1 was identified as FBs-specific. EGR1 transcriptional activity progressively declined during aging, correspondingly decreasing the expression of its collagen-associated target genes such as DPT, COL12A1, and COL5A2. We posit that collagen loss in skin aging may occur through this EGR1-mediated transcriptional pathway. This hypothesis parallels findings in maladaptive kidney repair, where autophagy-activated EGR1 induces FGF2-driven fibroblast activation and fibrosis [50]. Concurrently, COL1A1-(ITGA1 + ITGB1) interaction signaling diminished with postnatal aging. Collectively, these results elucidate the mechanistic basis for progressive collagen depletion during skin maturation and aging, providing critical insights into FB aging characteristics.

Notably, we observed significant time-dependent dynamics in skin immune cells during development and aging. Our analysis identified DCs and TCs as the predominant immune populations. In contrast, human skin studies report additional immune cell types beyond these two major subsets, including mast cells, monocytes, B cells, and macrophages [9, 51, 52]. We hypothesize that this discrepancy may stem from the high FB abundance in pig skin diluting rarer immune populations, potentially limiting their detection. Alternatively, differences in scRNA-seq platform sensitivity could contribute to this variation.

Within the immune system, DCs serve as pivotal antigen-presenting cells, activating T cells and presenting antigenic information, while activated T cells directly execute immune responses through target cell cytotoxicity and cytokine secretion [53–56]. Our comparison of DC and TC subtype proportions across developmental stages revealed shifts that potentially explain age-associated changes in skin immune status [57]. Specifically, DC activity predominated in embryonic skin, whereas T cell activity increased notably during skin aging. Intriguingly, both DC and TC numbers were markedly reduced in perinatal pig skin. We propose that fetuses/perinatal individuals attenuate cutaneous immune responses to minimize excessive stress from birth-associated environmental challenges, a protective mechanism potentially analogous to maternal–fetal immunoregulation [58], though further mechanistic validation is warranted.

Aged skin exhibits characteristic features including dermal thinning, reduced elasticity, wrinkling, and immune dysregulation [59–61]. Our analysis revealed increased transcriptional noise across nearly all cell types in aged skin, with FBs predominating in cellular communication. Concurrently, elevated MK signaling in aged skin FBs suggested enhanced inflammatory immune and repair responses. Given that MDK, a key component of the MK signaling pathway, promotes macrophage immune polarization [62], we hypothesize that immunomodulation in aging skin occurs primarily via the MK signaling pathway.

Furthermore, we conducted cross-species analyses of single-cell transcriptomic data from embryonic, young, and aged skin of pigs, humans, and mice. The results showed that the correlation of gene expression patterns between human and pig skin cells was stronger than that between human and mouse skin cells, indicating that the overall evolutionary conservation of human and pig skin cells was relatively high. We performed a cross-species comparative analysis of the expression patterns of FB genes during skin development and aging. Our results revealed certain species-specific differences in the expression of FB genes among pigs, humans, and mice. Nevertheless, key biological processes including cell proliferation/differentiation, ECM organization, and inflammatory responses remained conserved across species during skin development and aging. These findings confirm the pig’s superiority over mice as a cellular-level mammalian model for human skin regenerative medicine and anti-aging therapy research. Additionally, our study provides critical insights into skin cell characteristics and communication dynamics, informing clinical dermatology therapeutics. The public dataset offers essential support for regenerative medicine translation from mechanistic analysis to clinical application.

Conclusions

In summary, we established a large single-cell transcriptional atlas of pig skin, identifying eight major cell types. Dynamic time-dependent heterogeneity in skin cell composition was observed throughout embryonic development to postnatal aging. Embryonic MSCs and FBs share a common origin and undergo bifurcated differentiation during late embryogenesis. Furthermore, FB, DC, and TC transcriptomic profiles exhibit aging-associated alterations. Cellular communication analysis revealed that DCs and TCs predominantly utilize SPP1 and TGF-β signaling pathways to regulate skin immune development and aging, respectively. Concurrently, the diminished COL1A1-(ITGA1 + ITGB1) and enhanced MDK-SDC1 interaction signaling in aged FBs participate in the ECM degradation and immunomodulation of aged skin, notably, the transcription factor EGR1, deactivating in FBs during postnatal maturation and aging to modulate downstream collagen synthesis-related genes like DPT, COL12A1, and COL5A2. Cross-species comparisons demonstrated the evolutionary conservation of skin cell types, marker gene expression, and FB functional gene profiles across pigs, humans, and mice during skin development and aging. Collectively, this resource provides a foundation for advancing cellular-level research on skin homeostasis and pathogenesis.

Methods

Skin sample collection and pretreatment

All CH pigs used in this study were obtained from the Chengdu Livestock and Poultry Genetic Resources Protection Center. Skin samples were collected from 30 female CH pigs at the following developmental stages, including embryonic days 56, 66, 76, 86, 105 (E56, E66, E76, E86, E105), postnatal days 3, 90, 180 (D3, D90, D180), and postnatal years 3 and 7 (Y3, Y7). Three biological replicates were included per time point. During the sampling experiment, a total of 20 healthy sows, including 5 pregnant sows at five gestation stages and 15 female pigs at five developmental stages after birth, were selected and transported for 1 h from the preservation farm to the laboratory and then were allowed to rest for 2 h. All pigs (except those at 3 days old) were euthanized by electrocution (110 V, 60 Hz, 5–10 s), followed by severing the blood vessels in the neck and draining the blood to ameliorate their suffering. For the 3-day-old piglets, they were euthanized by manually applying blunt force to cause trauma to their foreheads. For the pig fetuses, they were already dead after the mothers were euthanized, and the embryos were removed from the body for sampling. Dorsal midline interscapular skin was excised from each animal and immediately immersed in ice-cold Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (DPBS; Biosharp, Beijing, China). Surface hair, debris, and subcutaneous fat were removed aseptically using a scalpel. Samples were then washed twice with ice-cold DPBS prior to downstream processing.

Histological analysis

Pre-treated skin samples were dissected into 5 × 5 mm pieces and fixed overnight at 4 °C in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Solarbio, Beijing, China). Samples were subsequently dehydrated through an ethanol series, cleared in xylene for 30 min, and embedded in paraffin blocks. Sections (5 μm) were cut using a microtome and mounted on 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane (APES)-coated slides (ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China). Slides were dewaxed in xylene (2 × 15 min) and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed following standard protocols. Finally, stained sections were mounted with neutral resin and imaged using light microscopy.

Single-cell suspension preparation

Dissociating tightly structured skin into single-cell suspensions poses significant challenges due to dense collagen/elastin networks, particularly for pig skin which is structurally denser than human skin. We optimized a dissociation protocol as follows. Pre-treated skin samples were minced into 1 mm3 pieces and transferred to 50-mL tubes containing digestion mixture Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Meilunbio, Dalian, China) with 0.5–3 mg/mL collagenase I (Sigma-Aldrich, USA); 0.5–2 mg/mL collagenases II, IV, and V (Sigma-Aldrich, USA); 1 mg/mL elastase (Coolaber, Beijing, China); and 2 U/mL DNase I (Coolaber, Beijing, China). Enzyme-to-tissue volume ratio was maintained at 5:1 with collagenase concentration adjusted per tissue thickness. Digestion proceeded at 37 °C for 120–180 min with gentle agitation every 10 min. Reactions were terminated using DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA). The suspension underwent sequential filtration through 70-μm and 40-μm strainers, followed by centrifugation (350 × g, 5 min, 4 °C) until complete dissociation. Erythrocytes were lysed with 2 mL RBC Lysis Solution (Roche, Switzerland) at room temperature (5 min), then pelleted by centrifugation. After DMEM washing, persistent aggregates were treated with 2 mL TrypLE Express Enzyme (Gibco, USA) at 37 °C (45 min). Cells were washed twice and resuspended in cold DMEM/10% FBS. Viability and concentration were determined via 0.4% Trypan Blue staining (Solarbio, Beijing, China) using Countess cell counting chambers (Thermo Fisher, USA).

Single-cell library construction and sequencing

Cell viability was assessed microscopically. Samples exceeding 80% viability proceeded to library construction. Single-cell RNA libraries were prepared using the DNBelab C Series Single-Cell Library Prep Set (MGI, 1,000,021,082) per manufacturer’s protocol and published methods [63]. Briefly, single-cell suspensions were co-encapsulated with oil and beads in C4 scRNA slides using automated instrumentation. Barcoded libraries were generated through emulsion disruption, bead recovery, reverse transcription, and cDNA amplification. Library quality was verified using the Qubit™ ssDNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Q10212). Qualified libraries underwent paired-end sequencing (PE100) on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (China National GeneBank, Shenzhen) with read configurations of 41 bp (read 1) and 100 bp (read 2). Three biological replicates per group were sequenced at average depths of 25,000 reads/cell. Post-filtering data retained approximately 15,000 valid cells/sample (Table S1), with > 85% Q30 bases across all samples (Table S1).

Immunofluorescence staining

All skin samples were paraffin-embedded. Immunofluorescence staining followed established protocols [19]. Antibodies from Abmart (Shanghai, China) included the following: COL1A1 (P28372-B2S; 1:500), COL1A2 (TD3549S; 1:500), TOP2A (TA0793S; 1:500), CENPF (TD2310S; 1:500), MPZ (PU197342S; 1:800), MBP (TA4085S; 1:500), HLA-DQA1 (T56996S; 1:200), HLA-DQB1 (PH4849S; 1:200), CD3E (TD6594S; 1:500), and CD247 (TD6788S; 1:500). Note that HLA-DQA1 and HLA-DQB1 represent human orthologs of pig SLA-DQA1 and SLA-DQB1, respectively.

Single-cell RNA-seq data processing

Raw scRNA-seq data were processed using PISA (v0.2) for filtering and demultiplexing. Reads were aligned to the Sus scrofa reference genome (Sscrofa11.1; Ensembl Release 106) via HISAT2 (v2.2.1) with default parameters. The genome FASTA file, including complete mitochondrial sequence (MT:1–16,613), was obtained from Ensembl FTP (release-106). Gene expression matrices were generated using DNBelab C4 scRNA analysis software. Cell quality control was performed based on detected gene counts and mitochondrial read percentages. Retained cells met these criteria: (1) gene counts between 200 and the 90th percentile maximum; (2) mitochondrial gene percentage < 5%. Doublets were identified and removed per library using DoubleFinder (v2.0.3) [64]. The thirty scRNA-seq datasets were integrated through initial merging, followed by expression matrix normalization and variance stabilization in Seurat (v4.3.3) [65]. Technical batch effects were mitigated using Harmony (v1.2.0) with default parameters [66].

Cell clustering and cell type identification in scRNA-seq

Following data integration, unsupervised clustering was performed using Seurat (v4.3.3) [67]. All sequencing libraries underwent normalization and scaling. Highly variable genes were identified via Seurat’s “FindVariableFeatures” function (selection.method = “vst,” nfeatures = 2000). Cell clusters were resolved using the “FindClusters” function (resolution = 0.8–1.5) on significant principal components (p < 0.01). Cluster visualization was achieved through t-SNE and UMAP (“RunTSNE”/“RunUMAP,” reduction = “harmony”). Marker genes for each cluster were detected with “FindAllMarkers” (logfc.threshold > 0.25, min.pct > 0.1, adj.p.val < 0.05). Cell type annotation combined SCSA automated classification [68] with manual curation.

DEG identification and Gene Ontology enrichment analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using Seurat’s “FindAllMarkers” function (logfc.threshold > 0.25, min.pct > 0.1, adj.p.val < 0.05) across clusters and cell types. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of DEGs was conducted and visualized using OmicShare online tools (R-based implementation).

Cross-species comparison for skin cell atlas in pigs, humans, and mice

Published skin single-cell datasets from humans and mice were downloaded from GSA, EMBL-EBI, and GEO (10X platform), including human fetal (7–10 PCW) skin samples [69, 70], human young (18–23 years) and aged (70–76 years) skin samples [13, 71], mouse fetal (E13.5, E16.5) skin samples [11, 72], and mouse young (7-week) and aged (88-week) skin samples [73, 74]. Corresponding pig samples (embryonic, E56/E66; young, D180; aged, Y7) were selected for cross-species matching. Homologous genes from Ensembl were harmonized after retaining genes expressed in ≥ 1 cell across species. Preprocessed matrices were integrated using Seurat, retaining cells expressing > 200 genes, with batch effects removed via Harmony (v1.2.0; group.by.vars = “Species,” reduction = “pca”) [66]. Inter-cluster markers were identified using FindAllMarkers (min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.25). Fibroblasts were categorized by developmental stage: embryonic (E_pig, E56/E66; E_human, 7–10PCW; E_mouse, E13.5/E16.5), young (Y_pig, D180; Y_human, Y-18/Y-22/Y-23; Y_mouse, 7wk), and aged (A_pig, Y7; A_human, O-70/O-73/O-76; A_mouse, 88wk). Differential expression analysis (FindMarkers; avg_log2FC > 0.5, adj_p < 0.05) identified species-specific DEGs for Y vs. E and A vs. Y transitions. Unique DEGs were visualized via Venn diagrams (ggplot2), heatmaps, and GO enrichment. Key genes (differentiation, ECM, senescence) were plotted as fold-change scatter plots to assess species-specific patterns.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis

Cell differentiation trajectories were reconstructed in three-dimensions using Monocle 3 [75]. Briefly, cells were organized into potentially discontinuous trajectories, followed by statistical testing to identify genes exhibiting expression variation along these paths. We analyzed developmental trajectories across six stages (E56, E66, E76, E86, E105, D3) during porcine skin organogenesis using Monocle 3 for visualization. Complementary lineage trajectories were inferred with Monocle 2 [76] based on cluster-specific or cell-type-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The “differentialGeneTest” function performed differential expression and pseudotime analyses across developmental periods to delineate differentiation trajectories. In addition, to examine the overall situation of transcriptional expression of all samples, we constructed the pseudotime trajectory of pseudobulk RNA-seq profiles for all samples, referring to the method described in Cao et al.’s research [75].

Cell–cell communication patterns analysis

Cell–cell communication networks were analyzed using CellChat (v1.0.5) with default parameters [34]. The human ligand-receptor database was employed, selecting orthologous genes conserved between pigs and humans. Putative ligand-receptor interactions were identified based on cell-type-specific co-expression patterns. Communication networks were visualized through (1) Circos diagrams (netVisual_aggregate), (2) interaction-weighted network plots, (3) chord/circle diagrams for individual pathways, and (4) river plots depicting communication patterns.

Transcription factor-target gene network analysis

Transcriptional regulatory networks were analyzed using the SCENIC workflow (v1.1.2.2) with RcisTarget (v1.4.1) under default parameters [77]. Cell-type-specific transcription factors were identified from differentially expressed genes screened with the Seurat’s FindAllMarkers function (min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.25). Regulator activities were calculated and visualized in Cytoscape (v3.7.2), displaying only high-confidence transcription factor-target gene interactions.

Transcriptional noise analysis

Transcriptional noise quantification followed established methods [36, 78]. To address UMI count variations, all cells were down-sampled to an equal number of total UMI counts. Cell-type frequency differences were mitigated by down-sampling to achieve balanced young (D180) and aged (Y7) cell numbers. From 10,000 randomly selected skin cells, transcriptional noise differences between maturation (D180) and aging (Y7) stages were quantified. Following gene expression screening, Euclidean distances between individual cells and their cell-type group centroids were computed. Young-to-aged transcriptional noise ratios were subsequently calculated. Spearman correlations of down-sampled expression matrices across all genes were converted to noise metrics (1—ρ). Wilcoxon rank-sum tests assessed noise-age associations per cell type, with p-values adjusted via Bonferroni-Hochberg correction using R’s p.adjust function.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance of differential gene expression was assessed using Bonferroni correction for multiple testing. Gene Ontology enrichment analysis employed Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate adjustment.
