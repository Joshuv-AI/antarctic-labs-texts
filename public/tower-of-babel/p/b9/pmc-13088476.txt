Contrasting evolutionary trajectories of pyrimethamine resistance in human and zoonotic malaria parasites
Authors: Chaturong Putaporntip, Jidapha Somkuna, Rattanaporn Rojrung, Taweesak Tia, Napaporn Kuamsab, Somchai Jongwutiwes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPyrimethamine resistance in Plasmodium falciparum is driven by mutations at dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) residues N51I, C59R, S108N/T, and I164L, with parallel substitutions in P. vivax (N50I, S58R, S117N/T, S173L). To evaluate the influence of drug pressure on parasite evolution, we compared the dihydrofolate reductase–thymidylate synthase (dhfr-ts) genes of human and zoonotic malaria parasites exposed to different drug environments.MethodsBlood samples were collected from patients with symptomatic malaria in Thailand (P. falciparum, n = 241; P. vivax, n = 115) and from sympatric zoonotic infections, mainly in naturally infected macaques (P. knowlesi, n = 18; P. inui, n = 64). Complete dhfr-ts sequences were determined and validated.ResultsAmong 356 human and 145 zoonotic dhfr-ts sequences, zoonotic parasites exhibited markedly higher mutation counts, haplotype diversity, and nucleotide diversity. In P. falciparum, the high prevalence of the quadruple IRNL mutant indicated strong directional selection. In P. vivax, the coexistence of wild-type alleles and mutations outside resistance-associated residues suggests incomplete fixation. In zoonotic parasites, elevated rates of synonymous versus nonsynonymous substitutions, along with an excess of rare variants, indicate that purifying selection and genetic drift act against slightly deleterious mutations, likely due to protein functional or structural constraints.ConclusionsAntifolate drug pressure has driven human malaria dhfr-ts genes toward fixation, whereas zoonotic orthologues evolve under a mutation–selection–drift balance. These findings highlight both the potential utility of antifolates against zoonotic malaria and the evolutionary consequences of sustained drug pressure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05841-z.

Introduction

Protozoa of the genus Plasmodium comprise more than 200 species that parasitize a wide range of vertebrate hosts, with blood-sucking insects serving as vectors. Of these, five species including Plasmodium falciparum, P. vivax, P. malariae, P. ovale curtisi, and P. ovale wallikeri sustain their life cycles in humans and are transmitted by diverse Anopheles mosquitoes [1, 2]. Nevertheless, cross-species transmission of Plasmodium from their natural non-human primate hosts to humans has been well documented, particularly in Southeast Asia. Examples include P. knowlesi, P. cynomolgi, P. inui, P. fieldi, and P. coatneyi, with additional species likely to be identified [3–5]. Although antimalarial drugs remain central to the clinical management and control of human malaria, Plasmodium parasites consistently evolve resistance after periods of widespread drug use. Drug resistance phenotypes arise through complex molecular mechanisms governed by one or more genetic loci, and resistant traits can become established in parasite progeny.

Pyrimethamine acts by inhibiting dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), blocking the conversion of 7,8-dihydrofolate to 5,6,7,8-tetrahydrofolate, an essential precursor for thymidylate (dTMP) synthesis. Resistance of P. falciparum to sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) was first reported in the 1960 s at the Thai-Cambodia border, and global resistance appears to have arisen from multiple independent origins [6]. At the molecular level, pyrimethamine resistance in P. falciparum results from nonsynonymous mutations in the DHFR gene (pfdhfr) at four key codons: N51I, C59R, S108N/T, and I164L [7, 8]. An additional mutation, A16V, confers resistance to cycloguanil [9]. These amino acid substitutions alter the enzyme’s binding site, reducing inhibitor affinity [10, 11]. Simulated mutagenesis studies suggest that S108N is the primary resistance-conferring mutation, with additional stepwise substitutions progressively increasing resistance levels [12–14]. However, not all amino acid replacements are tolerated in essential residues, highlighting structural and functional constraints within DHFR [10]. Similarly, pyrimethamine resistance in P. vivax is associated with mutations at corresponding residues in pvdhfr, including N50I, S58R, S117N/T, and I173L/F [15, 16]. The functional role of mutations outside these key residues remains unclear.

Human malaria parasites are frequently exposed to drug pressure during blood-stage infection, which can reduce or eliminate drug-susceptible parasites from circulation. Resistant mutants, however, gain a survival advantage under such conditions and may gradually increase in frequency until fixation [13, 14]. In contrast, non-human primate malaria parasites circulating in their natural hosts are unlikely to experience such selective pressures, and drug-resistant variants are therefore not expected to predominate in these populations. We thus hypothesized that natural selection acting on dhfr genes differs markedly between human and non-human primate malaria parasites. To test this, we comparatively analyzed the complete coding sequences of dhfr from populations of P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. knowlesi, and P. inui in Thailand. Our findings revealed distinct selective pressures on this locus among sympatric malaria species. The absence of resistance-associated mutations at key codons in pkdhfr-ts and pidhfr-ts, together with the broad distribution of nucleotide diversity across their coding regions, suggests that preexisting mutations in these species likely reflect random genetic drift rather than selection imposed by antifolate drugs.

Methods

Sources of parasite DNA

DNA samples of P. falciparum (n = 241), P. vivax (n = 115), P. knowlesi (n = 18) and P. inui (n = 64) were randomly selected from previous studies [4, 5, 17–22], and some from recent isolates (Supplementary Table S1). Human malaria blood samples were obtained during 1994 and 2024. It was not until 1996 that SP was discontinued nationwide for malaria treatment. Non-human primate malaria parasites in Thailand were collected between 2006 and 2019 and had not been exposed to SP pressure. DNA was extracted from 200 μL of EDTA-preserved venous blood sample from each isolate, stored at −40ºC, using Qiagen DNA mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instruction. The eluted samples contained approximately 30—40 μL.

DNA amplification and sequencing

The complete coding sequences of pfdhfr-ts, pvdhfr-ts, pkdhfr-ts, and pidhfr-ts were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using specific primers (Supplementary Table S2). Nested PCR was employed to increase yield. Thermal cycling profiles were optimized, with amplification limited to 25 cycles in both primary and secondary PCRs to minimize errors from excessive cycling [23]. Ex-Taq DNA polymerase (Takara, Seta, Japan), which possesses 3′ → 5′ exonuclease activity, was used, while Phusion High-Fidelity DNA polymerases (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) were applied during re-amplification to confirm novel and rare substitutions. The PCR product was purified by using QIAquick PCR purification kit (QIAGEN, Germany), following the manufacturer’s recommendation. DNA sequences were determined directly from purified amplicons. Amplicons from isolates with overlapping electropherogram signals were subjected to TA cloning. In brief, the nested PCR products were excised from agarose gel, purified by using a QIAquick PCR purification kit (Qiagen), and ligated into pGEM-T-Easy Vector (Promega, Madison WI, USA). After incubation at 4 °C for overnight, the reaction mixture was precipitated, dissolved in 10 µl of double-distilled water, and transformed into Escherichia coli strain JM109. Recombinant plasmid clones were screened by colony PCR. All singleton substitutions detected by direct sequencing were verified by independently amplifying the same genomic DNA template. Rare variants and singleton substitutions identified from recombinant plasmid templates were further confirmed by sequencing re-cloned products from independently amplified genomic DNA. Sanger sequencing was performed by Macrogen (Seoul, Korea) using capillary electrophoresis platform. To ensure accuracy, overlapping sequences were generated using sequencing primers in both forward and reverse directions (Supplementary Figure S1). Detailed procedures for the acquisition and verification of dhfr-ts sequences are provided in Supplementary Figure S2.

Data analysis

Sequences were aligned by codon using CLUSTAL_X with default parameters [24]. Unverified rare variants and singletons were excluded. For isolates with multiclonal infections, each haplotype was treated as an independent unit in the downstream analysis. Reference sequences used for comparison included pfdhfr-ts (3D7 strain, GenBank AL844503), pvdhfr-ts (Salvador 1, NC009910), pkdhfr-ts (H strain, LR701165), and pidhfr-ts (San Antonio 1, XM008818109). The numbers of mutations and haplotypes, as well as haplotype diversity and Tajima’s D statistics, were determined using DnaSP v6.12.3 under default settings [25]. Significance of Tajima’s D was tested by coalescent simulation with intermediate recombination and no recombination after 10,000 replicates. Nucleotide diversity (π) was estimated from mean pairwise differences, while substitution rates (d) were estimated using the maximum composite likelihood model [26]. Synonymous (dS) and nonsynonymous (dN) substitution rates were calculated following Nei and Gojobori [27] with Jukes–Cantor correction [28]. Standard errors for π, d, dS, and dN were obtained by bootstrapping based on 1,000 pseudoreplicates in MEGA v6 [26]. The statistical significance of d, dS, and dN values was assessed by Z-test with p < 0.05. Codon-level selection analysis was performed using the FUBAR method with default parameters [29], after excluding recombinant sites. Intragenic recombination was assessed using the GARD method on the Datamonkey server in normal run mode, employing universal genetic code and two rate classes without site-to-site rate variation [30]. Deleterious amino acid substitutions were predicted with SIFT, which assesses the likelihood that a substitution affects protein function based on sequence homology [31].

Results

DHFR-TS haplotypes of P. falciparum and P. vivax

All P. falciparum and P. vivax isolates carried single dhfr-ts haplotypes. Among 241 P. falciparum isolates, four haplotypes were identified based on amino acid substitutions at residues 51, 59, 108, and 164 in the PfDHFR domain. The IRNL haplotype predominated (68.05%), followed by IRNI, NRNL, and NRNI. No wild-type haplotype (NCSI) was detected (Table 1, Supplementary Table S3). From 115 P. vivax isolates, five PvDHFR haplotypes were identified at residues 50, 58, 117, and 173, which correspond to the resistance-associated sites in PfDHFR. The NRNI haplotype was most frequent (55.65%), followed by NRTI, NSSI, and either NSNI or NRSF. The NSSI haplotype, identical to the pyrimethamine-sensitive Salvador 1 strain, was considered wild type (Table 1, Supplementary Table S4). No amino acid substitutions were observed in the junction or TS domains of PfDHFR-TS or PvDHFR-TS. Rare synonymous mutations were detected: c.438 T > G (p.Gly) in the ts domain of pfdhfr-ts; c.207 T > C (p.Tyr) in the dhfr domain of pvdhfr-ts; and c.738C > A (p.Ala) in the junction domain of pvdhfr-ts (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). In addition, nonsynonymous mutations were found in pvdhfr-ts, though outside residues critical for pyrimethamine resistance (Supplementary Table S4). DHFR-TS haplotypes of P. knowlesi and P. inui

A total of 29 pkdhfr-ts sequences were obtained from four patients and 14 macaques, nine of which had multiclonal infections. Twenty-one haplotypes were identified, derived from 23 amino acid substitutions and a deletion at codon 105. Of these, nine substitutions occurred in the junction domain and five in the TS domain (Supplementary Table S5). Among 64 P. inui isolates, 25 contained a single haplotype, while 21 isolates contained two and 13 isolates contained three. In total, 102 amino acid substitutions were distributed across DHFR (49 residues), junction (38 residues), and TS (15 residues) domains (Supplementary Table S6). An insertion of degenerate pentapeptide repeats was observed in the junction region (Supplementary Table S7). Analysis of 116 Pidhfr-ts sequences from 64 P. inui isolates (Table 2) revealed 110 unique amino acid haplotypes, with only four being shared between isolates (Supplementary Table S7). Importantly, residues corresponding to key pyrimethamine-resistance sites were completely conserved in both PkDHFR (residues 50, 58, 117, and 173) and PiDHFR (residues 50, 58, 124, and 180) (Table 1). Comparative diversity across malarial dhfr-ts

Sliding-window analysis revealed that nucleotide diversity (π) in pfdhfr-ts and pvdhfr-ts was largely restricted to specific codons in the dhfr domain (Fig. 1A, B). By contrast, diversity was more broadly distributed across all domains of pkdhfr-ts and pidhfr-ts (Fig. 1C, D). Haplotype diversity (h) in pkdhfr-ts and pidhfr-ts was significantly higher than in pfdhfr-ts and pvdhfr-ts (Table 2). The near-maximal h values in pkdhfr-ts and pidhfr-ts indicate a low frequency of shared haplotypes. Likewise, π values for pkdhfr-ts and pidhfr-ts were significantly greater than for pfdhfr-ts and pvdhfr-ts (p < 0.001), with consistent differences across individual domains. Tests for deviation from neutrality

In pkdhfr-ts and pidhfr-ts, the rate of synonymous substitutions per synonymous site (dS) was significantly greater than the rate of nonsynonymous substitutions per nonsynonymous site (dN) in both the dhfr and ts domains (p < 0.005; Table 2). Although nucleotide diversity was higher in the junction regions of both genes compared to their respective dhfr or ts domains, there were no significant differences in dN and dS values within these regions (Table 2). By contrast, no significant dS–dN differences were observed across domains of pfdhfr-ts or pvdhfr-ts. Notably, pidhfr-ts exhibited significant negative Tajima’s D values at both total and nonsynonymous mutation sites (Table 3). Because multiclonal infections were common among P. knowlesi and P. inui isolates, selecting one representative haplotype per infection yielded consistent results for Tajima’s D statistics. Codon-based selection

Using FUBAR [29], we detected one positively selected codon (N51I) and one negatively selected codon (G438) in pfdhfr-ts. In pvdhfr-ts, three codons were under positive selection (F57L, S58L, S117N/T) and two under negative selection (Y69, A245). In pkdhfr-ts, six codons (10.53%) showed evidence of positive selection, while 17 codons (29.82%) were negatively selected (Table 5). In pidhfr-ts, 19 codons (9.85%) were positively selected and 58 codons (30.05%) negatively selected. The overall proportions of codons under neutral, positive, and negative selection did not differ significantly between pkdhfr-ts and pidhfr-ts (p = 0.989).

Deleterious amino acid substitutions

SIFT analysis predicted two deleterious mutations in pfdhfr-ts (S108N and I164L), both of which occurred at high frequency (Table 4). In the ts domain of pfdhfr-ts, the negatively selected c.438 T > G (p.Gly) variant (Table 5) was intolerant to amino acid substitutions. In P. vivax, deleterious changes were predicted at residues F8S, I13L, F57L, T61M, and I173F. Among these, F57L and T61M were relatively common, whereas the others were found in only one or two isolates. In P. knowlesi, four deleterious substitutions were identified, while in P. inui 12 were detected. Approximately half of these occurred as singletons or at low frequency (Table 4). Discussion

In Thailand, SP was introduced as the first-line treatment for falciparum malaria between 1974 and 1982, following widespread chloroquine failure [32]. After a brief interim (1983–1985) using quinine combined with tetracycline or erythromycin, SP was reintroduced in combination with mefloquine until 1995. Since 1996, artemisinin-based combination therapy has been the national standard, marking the end of SP usage against malaria in Thailand [19, 22, 32].

Among the 241 P. falciparum isolates analyzed (1994–2014), neither the wild-type (S108) nor the single mutant S108N allele of PfDHFR was detected (Table 1, Supplementary Table S3). These alleles were likely outcompeted by parasites carrying double or quadruple mutations during periods of strong antifolate pressure and were subsequently eliminated. Evolutionary trajectories suggest that NRNL and IRNI haplotypes can give rise to IRNL [12–14]. Under intense drug pressure, as in Thailand, IRNL could have been selectively favored, aided by compensatory mutations that mitigated fitness costs [13]. Unfortunately, the samples obtained in this study were insufficient to document temporal changes in the frequencies of resistant haplotypes. Nevertheless, even after the withdrawal of SP, the IRNL haplotype remained predominant across endemic provinces, suggesting long-term stability of this resistant lineage. In contrast, the absence of mutations at antifolate resistance-associated codons in the dhfr-ts loci of zoonotic malaria parasites over the extended sampling period (2006–2019) is consistent with the lack of antifolate drug pressure in these parasite populations.

In contrast, drug pressure on P. vivax dhfr-ts evolution appears to have been much weaker. SP was never adopted as first-line therapy for vivax malaria, as chloroquine remained the preferred drug. Selective pressure on pvdhfr-ts thus arose primarily during mixed infections with P. falciparum, while P. vivax infections were often underdiagnosed by microscopy [3, 4]. Consequently, much of the P. vivax population in Thailand was not heavily exposed to pyrimethamine. Mutations at residues 50, 58, 117, and 173 in PvDHFR have been linked to SP treatment failure [15]. Wild-type parasites identical to the Salvador 1 strain remain detectable in recent isolates, while quintuple mutants (I13L/F57L/S58R/T61M/S117T) were reported in Tak in both 1996 and 2013 (Supplementary Table S4). Unlike P. falciparum, however, P. vivax shows no clear evidence of sequential mutation accumulation, suggesting more complex evolutionary trajectories in resistance development [12–14, 33].

Compared with human malaria parasites, P. knowlesi and P. inui exhibited greater amino acid variation in DHFR, junction, and TS domains. Despite the limited number of human P. knowlesi cases, the presence of shared Pkdhfr-ts haplotypes between human and macaque isolates (haplotype I, Supplementary Table S6) supports zoonotic spillover from reservoir hosts rather than the existence of independent parasite populations. Nonetheless, residues corresponding to pyrimethamine-resistance sites in PvDHFR (N50/S58/S117/I173) were invariant in both zoonotic species (Table 1), supporting the absence of drug-driven selection. The relatively few haplotypes, high haplotype diversity, and low nucleotide diversity observed in pfdhfr-ts and pvdhfr-ts among Thai isolates reflect fixation of resistance-conferring alleles due to drug pressure. For pvdhfr-ts, the presence of sensitive haplotypes, rare synonymous mutations, and nonsynonymous changes outside resistance-determining codons indicate weaker selection compared to P. falciparum (Table 1, Supplementary Table S4). Moreover, the minimal diversity observed in junction and ts domains of both P. falciparum and P. vivax is consistent with selective sweeps surrounding resistant dhfr alleles [34, 35], similar to patterns reported in P. ovale curtisi and P. ovale wallikeri [36].

In zoonotic species, nucleotide substitutions appeared randomly distributed across domains, consistent with the neutral mutation-drift model of molecular evolution [37]. Approximately 60% of codon substitutions in zoonotic dhfr-ts genes were neutral, suggesting maintenance through mutation-drift balance. Some substitutions may occasionally confer beneficial effects and undergo positive selection. However, most amino acid changes are deleterious or mildly deleterious, and thus nonsynonymous substitutions are largely removed by purifying selection, while synonymous changes accumulate [37, 38]. Indeed, negatively selected codons outnumbered positively selected ones in zoonotic dhfr-ts (Tables 2 and 5). The similar proportions of neutral, positive, and negative sites between pkdhfr-ts and pidhfr-ts further support a drift-driven process in the absence of drug pressure. Notably, negative Tajima’s D values at nonsynonymous sites in pidhfr-ts (Table 3) suggest an excess of rare variants under ongoing purifying selection, whereas the non-significant D values in pkdhfr-ts may reflect limited sample size.

Although multiclonal infections are common in P. falciparum and P. vivax in Thailand based on analysis of polymorphic loci [22, 39], no mixed Pfdhfr-ts and Pvdhfr-ts alleles were detected in this study. This likely reflects the limited number of circulating dhfr-ts haplotypes, whereby parasites carrying distinct alleles at other loci may nonetheless share identical dhfr-ts haplotypes. Consequently, only single Pfdhfr-ts and Pvdhfr-ts haploptypes were detected per infection. In contrast, the high prevalence of mixed dhfr-ts haplotypes in P. knowlesi and P. inui raises concerns regarding potential inflation of molecular diversity indices and the detection of rare variants that could affect downstream analyses. However, estimates of molecular diversity-including haplotype diversity, nucleotide diversity, synonymous and nonsynonymous substitution rates, and Tajima’s D-did not differ significantly whether multiple haplotypes or a single representative haplotype per infection were included (Table 3 and Supplementary Table S9).

Crystal structure analyses and in silico predictions have identified the active sites for DHFR inhibitor binding in PfDHFR, PvDHFR, and other malarial DHFRs at conserved amino acid residues [11, 40, 41]. Although several amino acid substitutions were observed in PkDHFR-TS and PiDHFR-TS, the core residues involved in enzymatic activity, substrate and inhibitor binding pocket, and dimmer interface were fully conserved across isolates (Supplementary Figures S3 and S4). Notably, the junction regions between the dhfr and ts domains exhibited higher nucleotide diversity than the corresponding coding domains (Table 2). While signatures of purifying selection were detected within the dhfr and ts domains of Pkdhfr-ts and Pidhfr-ts, no significant deviation from neutrality was observed based on dN-dS comparisons. This suggests that polymorphisms are preferentially tolerated in less functionally constrained regions, particularly the inter-domain junctions, allowing a higher number of nonsynonymous substitutions in zoonotic malaria species.

Higher genetic diversity in P. knowlesi and P. inui may reflect host population structure, effective population size, and host ecology, rather than selection pressure alone. While P. falciparum and P. vivax are considered non-cryptic species in their human hosts [42], cryptic lineages and highly divergent subpopulations have been reported in P. knowlesi and P. inui [43, 44]. However, phylogenetic analyses of Pkdhfr-ts and Pidhfr-ts in this study did not reveal substantial intraspecific divergence (Supplementary Figure S5), suggesting that the observed polymorphisms are unlikely to represent analytical artefacts. Unlike human-restricted malaria parasites, which have experienced severe population bottlenecks due to eradication efforts, zoonotic malaria parasites maintain large and stable reservoirs in wild primates. This large effective population size facilitates the accumulation and maintenance of genetic diversity that would otherwise be lost through genetic drift. Although geographical barriers can generate distinct subpopulations-such as the divergence seen between P. knowlesi populations in Borneo and Peninsular Malaysia [43], ecological changes including deforestation and land-use modification increase contact among macaques, humans, and mosquito vectors, promoting gene flow across previously isolated habitats. Furthermore, frequent concurrent infections in macaque hosts enhance within-host genetic complexity and facilitate recombination during the mosquito stage, thereby further increasing population-level diversity [45, 46].

Potential artefactual nucleotide substitutions arising from PCR amplification and DNA sequencing were also considered. Ex Taq DNA polymerase exhibits approximately 3–4.5-fold higher fidelity than standard Taq polymerase, with an estimated error rate of 2.2 × 10–6 to 3.3 × 10–6 errors per base per cycle [47]. Across a 2 kb fragment amplified over 25 PCR cycles, this corresponds to an estimated error rate of ~ 8.25 × 10–5 per base. Given an additional Sanger sequencing error rate of approximately 1 × 10–4 per base [48], the combined effective error rate is ~ 1 × 10–4, corresponding to an expected 0.2 errors per 2 kb consensus sequence. This error magnitude would generate a nucleotide diversity of ~ 0.0001, which is 2.8-, 12.1-, 74.9-, and 125.9-fold lower than the observed diversity in Pfdhfr-ts, Pvdhfr-ts, Pkdhfr-ts, and Pidhfr-ts, respectively. Although the nucleotide diversity of Pfdhfr-ts was only 2.8-fold higher than the estimated artefactual level, two of the three substitutions were shared among isolates. Meanwhile, the proportions of singletons versus shared substitutions for Pfdhfr-ts, Pvdhfr-ts, Pkdhfr-ts, and Pidhfr-ts were 0.25 (1/4), 0.375 (3/8), 0.196 (10/51), and 0.272 (49/180), respectively. Despite differences in the number of polymorphic sites, the proportions of singletons versus shared substitutions in the dhfr-ts genes were not statistically significant (Yates’s χ2 = 0.776, p = 0.855). Importantly, nucleotide diversity estimates for Pkdhfr-ts (π = 0.0075) and Pidhfr-ts (π = 0.0125) are comparable to those reported for other antigenic genes from similar parasite populations, including merozoite surface protein 1, Duffy-binding protein α, β and γ of P. knowlesi (Pkmsp1, PkDBP-α, -β and -γ, π = 0.0388, 0.0178, 0.0198 and 0.0193) [49, 50], and apical membrane antigen 1 of P. inui (Piama1, π = 0.0055)[39]. These findings indicate that the high polymorphism observed in zoonotic malaria dhfr-ts genes is not locus-specific or artefactual. Taken together, the overall robustness of these data strengthens the study’s results and conclusions.

In summary, we documented a strong anthropogenic selective pressure on malarial parasites by comparative analysis of dhfr-ts sequence diversity in sympatric human and zoonotic malaria parasites. Our study highlights the strong selective influence of antimalarial drugs on P. falciparum and, to a lesser extent, P. vivax. Despite reduced drug pressure in recent decades, resistant haplotypes remain fixed in parasite populations. In contrast, P. knowlesi and P. inui appear to evolve primarily under a mutation-selection-drift balance. These findings underscore the need for ongoing monitoring of therapeutic efficacy in zoonotic malaria to enable timely interventions against emerging drug resistance.
