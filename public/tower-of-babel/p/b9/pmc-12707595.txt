Acute postoperative parotitis following frontal interhemispheric craniotomy: A case report
Authors: E. Ianick Rodrigues Pires BS, Jacob J. Knittel, Daniel G. Gridley, Syed A. J. Zaidi, Salvatore C. Lettieri, Iman Feiz-Erfan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Acute postoperative major salivary gland adenitis is a rare complication characterized by swelling of the salivary glands, most commonly associated with skull base neurosurgical procedures. Its occurrence following supratentorial non-skull base craniotomies is uncommon and may be overlooked.Case Description: We report a 45-year-old man who developed ipsilateral parotid gland inflammation within 24 h after undergoing a frontal interhemispheric craniotomy for resection of a craniopharyngioma. The procedure was performed in the supine position with 90° head rotation and 45° neck flexion. Postoperative imaging confirmed acute parotitis without abscess or hematoma. The patient was treated conservatively with hydration, warm compresses, and empiric antibiotics, leading to complete resolution of swelling without airway compromise.Conclusion: Acute postoperative parotitis can occur after supratentorial non-skull base procedures, particularly in cases involving extreme and prolonged head positioning. Although we presented a rather mild case of postoperative parotitis following a non-skull base craniotomy, acute major salivary gland adenitis after craniotomy may cause significant morbidity and early recognition and conservative management are essential to prevent airway compromise and other complications. Awareness of this rare entity may help neurosurgeons implement preventive measures during surgery.

INTRODUCTION

Acute parotitis and submandibular sialadenitis, also referred to as “anesthesia mumps,”[9] is a well-documented yet rare complication of neurosurgical procedures, particularly those involving the skull base, characterized by acute swelling of the salivary glands, most commonly the submandibular glands.[1,5,7] The single-institution-based incidence of submandibular sialadenitis occurring after retrosigmoid or far-lateral skull-base approaches has been reported to be 0.84%.[5] Berker et al.[1] reported five cases of postoperative parotitis following neurosurgical procedures performed in the sitting position. These cases represented 0.16% of all craniotomies and 1.9% of those conducted in the sitting position. Although the precise pathophysiology remains unclear, several perioperative factors have been associated with its development. These include venous congestion, perioperative dehydration,[13] pharmacologic suppression of salivary flow from agents such as dexmedetomidine, atropine, and morphine derivatives,[11] prolonged general anesthesia, and excessive head rotation with neck flexion, which may result in mechanical obstruction of Wharton’s duct, draining the submandibular gland.[5,13] In severe cases, the resulting glandular inflammation can lead to significant facial edema and airway compromise, sometimes necessitating emergent airway management and contributing to prolonged hospitalization and delayed recovery.[8]

Postoperative submandibular sialadenitis has typically been described in the context of neurosurgical procedures involving the skull base, where factors such as prolonged operative time, extreme head positioning, and mechanical compression are thought to contribute to this.[5,7]

In contrast, postoperative isolated parotitis following cranial neurosurgical procedures has been rarely reported.[1]

Here, we present a rare case of parotitis following a supratentorial frontal non-skull base interhemispheric craniotomy for tumor resection.

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained for retrospective case report. Due to retrospective nature of this report, the need for informed consent was waived by the IRB.

CASE REPORT

A 45-year-old male patient presented with a 6-month history of progressive and severe short-term memory impairment with bitemporal hemianopsia. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with contrast revealed a third ventricular tumor consistent with a craniopharyngioma [Figure 1]. Endocrine laboratory results demonstrated a mildly elevated serum prolactin, presumed secondary to stalk effect.

(a) Coronal T1 and (b) sagittal T1 post-contrast MRI demonstrate a heterogeneously enhancing, partially cystic suprasellar mass consistent with craniopharyngioma.

The patient underwent a frontal craniotomy through an interhemispheric transcallosal approach to the third ventricle, resulting in gross total resection of the tumor [Figure 2]. The patient was placed supine with head rotated at 90° to the right and neck flexed 45° as previously described elsewhere.[6]

(a) Coronal and (b) sagittal post-contrast T1-weighted post-operative MRI showing resection of the suprasellar mass with expected post-operative changes.

Total operating time was approximately 4 h. The endotracheal tube (ETT) was placed orally and tape secured to the right side of the face. Its size was 7.5 mm. The patient received drips during general anesthesia consisting of propofol, remifentanil, and phenylephrine. During surgery, we lost 100 mL blood, and 800 mL of urine output. We infused 2,000 mL intravenous (IV) fluids. Therefore, the patient was not volume depleted. His blood pressure was supported with pressor medication at that time, as well. The patient was left intubated postoperatively as part of the plan to extubate after the postoperative MRI had been completed. The patient also received 10 mg IV dose of dexamethasone intraoperatively followed by daily 10 mg prednisone taken orally.

On postoperative day 1, the patient disclosed an acute swelling in the right parotid gland ipsilateral to the side of head rotation. We did not appreciate a sooner sign of facial edema retrospectively. The edema was progressive over the following days. Computed tomography of the neck revealed an edematous right parotid gland without evidence of abscess or hematoma, indicative of acute parotitis [Figure 3]. The patient became febrile on postoperative day 2 at 38.4°C. Therefore, cultures of blood, urine, and cerebrospinal fluid were drawn which were negative. A serum amylase was not obtained. The white blood cell count (WBC) had increased from 9,200 before surgery, to 20,200 on postoperative day 1, and 12,400 on postoperative day 2. A diagnosis of acute postoperative parotitis was made and believed to be the reason for the fever, and partly also the reason for the elevated WBC, although steroid application was also contributing to this elevation. We started empiric antibiotics consisting of Vancomycin 15 mg/kg iv every 8 h and Cefepime 2 g IV every 8 h for 2 weeks. Antibiotics were provided to help reduce a surgical site infection in the presence of acute postoperative parotitis. The patient also received hydration and warm compresses. Swelling started to resolve after 7 days without airway compromise. The ETT had been successfully removed after the postoperative MRI had been obtained on day 2 post-surgery. We did not detect any symptoms or signs of airway compromise, dysphagia, facial pain, numbness, or weakness during this course of parotitis consistent with a mild form of this disease entity with a self-limited course.

(a) Axial and (b) coronal contrast-enhanced CT images demonstrate asymmetric enlargement with mild enhancement of the right parotid gland, along with increased density of the overlying subcutaneous fat, consistent with parotitis (arrows).

Neurologically, the patient experienced significant recovery of short-term memory and improvement in bitemporal hemianopsia. However, he developed panhypopituitarism and central diabetes insipidus postoperatively, needing long-term hormone replacement. Histopathologic evaluation confirmed an adamantinomatous craniopharyngioma.

At 12 months, MRI revealed a small, asymptomatic recurrent lesion that was managed conservatively [Figure 4].

(a) Coronal and (b) axial post-contrast T1-weighted MRI of the brain obtained 12 months after surgery demonstrate a small enhancing focus in the suprasellar region, consistent with recurrent craniopharyngioma (arrows).

DISCUSSION

We presented a case of mild and isolated acute parotitis following a right frontal interhemispheric craniotomy for resection of a craniopharyngioma. This was associated with mild right facial swelling, without airway compromise, or neurological deficit and with rapid improvement following conservative therapy. Proposed mechanisms reported in the literature to cause major salivary gland adenitis following cranial neurosurgical procedures include fixed head positions, often turned and/or tilted for several hours. This has been found to typically lead to contra-lateral major salivary gland adenitis.[1,5,7,13,15]

Furthermore, the ETT and/or bite blocks may obstruct the outflow ducts of Stenson (parotid gland outflow) and/or Wharton (submandibular gland outflow) and, therefore, may lead to salivary stasis and subsequent swelling of the involved gland.[5,7,13,15] This is compounded by relative dryness of the patient typically from surgical blood loss, and/or medications that aggravate hypovolemia. In neurosurgical cases, drugs involved include the application of mannitol[1] which can contribute to this mechanism. Furthermore, vascular compression has been described as a factor in these cases. This may be associated with compromise of arterial flow leading to glandular ischemia followed by re-perfusion after the procedure, which may explain the often-noted occurrence of gland swelling typically after several hours following the surgery.[2,5,7] But also, venous compression of the jugular veins in neck rotation or tilting can lead to venous outflow obstruction and facilitate major salivary gland adenitis.[13]

We think that the extreme head position (90° rotated to the right and 45° flexed for several hours) in our patient was the main reason for postoperative parotitis. Furthermore, the ETT was taped to the right side of the mouth likely contributing to mechanical obstruction of Stenson’s duct, along with direct pressure exerted by the tape on the right side increasing potential vascular ischemia and/or inhibiting venous return. We specifically believe that our case of ipsilateral parotitis was due to ETT being placed on the same side. Uchino et al. also noted that the ETT in their two cases was taped on the same side as the submandibular sialadenitis.[12]

The majority of reported cases in the literature typically involve submandibular sialadenitis after skull base surgery.[5,7] Parotitis after cranial neurosurgical procedures are less often reported.[1,3] Submandibular sialadenitis and parotitis are rare complications following skull base neurosurgical procedures.[1,5,7,15] Most reported cases have followed retrosigmoid craniotomies, often for vestibular schwannoma resections, which are typically characterized by prolonged operative times, head rotation, and tilting.[1,5,7] However, acute postoperative submandibular sialadenitis and parotitis have also been documented, albeit rarely, following non-skull base supratentorial neurosurgical procedures, including right parieto-occipital craniotomy in the prone position,[2] right frontal tumor resection,[4] left temporal lobe surgery with amygdalo-hippocampectomy,[10,13] frontal craniotomy for pericallosal aneurysm clipping,[14] and parasagittal meningioma resection.[1] Whereas the cases found in the literature are often severe forms of major salivary gland adenitis frequently associated with major airway compromise, peripheral nerve deficits including brachial plexopathy, and surgical wound complications.[2,4,5,7,8,11,12,14-16] Our case was rather a mild form of parotitis with a self-limited benign course following conservative management.

Our case is also unique in that it describes an instance of isolated acute parotitis after non-skull base cranial procedure. Cases of isolated parotitis after non-skull base craniotomies are much less common than submandibular sialadenitis with only four cases reported after cranial neurosurgery.[1,3,10,14]

Several strategies have been proposed to mitigate this risk, including minimizing neck flexion and lateral rotation when feasible, intermittently adjusting head position during prolonged procedures, and securing the ETT in a firm yet atraumatic manner.[1,7,12] Increased awareness and standardization of these practices may help reduce the incidence of this rare complication. We believe that a frontal interhemispheric craniotomy, if done with the head positioned neutrally, may have minimized the risk of suffering parotitis postoperatively.

CONCLUSION

We have presented a rare case of mild, self-limited parotitis following a frontal interhemispheric approach for third ventricle craniopharyngioma resection. This case underscores the importance of recognizing postoperative parotitis or submandibular sialadenitis as a potential complication following supratentorial non-skull base procedures. Early recognition is essential to ensure proper diagnosis and management, thereby minimizing associated morbidity. Our findings highlight the need for heightened intraoperative vigilance regarding parotid gland vulnerability, even in supratentorial non-skull base surgeries to help minimize the risk.
