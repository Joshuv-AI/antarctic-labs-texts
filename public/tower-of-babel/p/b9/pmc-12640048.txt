Chemical, microbiological and histological properties of refrigerated Asian seabass (Lates calcarifer) as influenced by different bleeding methods
Authors: Balamanikandan Vijayakumar, Suriya Palamae, Watcharapol Suyapoh, Kigen Takahashi, Bin Zhang, Soottawat Benjakul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bleeding improves fish quality by removing blood, a source of nutrients for microbes and a prooxidant for lipid oxidation. However, different bleeding techniques for Asian seabass particularly related with microbial quality and lipid oxidation of bled fish during storage have not been investigated. This study aimed to compare the effects of water perfusion (WP) and gill-cut (GC) bleeding on quality, and shelf life of Asian seabass. Both WP and GC reduced heme‑iron and non-heme‑iron contents and microbial load, compared to unbled sample. During the refrigerated storage, GC slices showed similar total viable count to WP slices up to 9 days. However, the latter had higher count on day 12. Lipid oxidation was more pronounced in WP than GC slices after 6 days. GC method could maintain the quality and tissue integrity of fish slices more effectively than WP. Thus, GC was a superior method to WP for bleeding.

Introduction

Asian seabass (Lates calcarifer) is significant in aquaculture with higher economic value. Asian seabass has been effectively cultivated in Southeast Asia. Farming commenced in Thailand during the 1970s and widely expanded to Taiwan, Malaysia, Indonesia, and Australia over several years because of its significant market value and high consumer demand (Zhu et al., 2006). Recently, the demand for Asian seabass product has been rising, owing to its delicacy with desired quality attributes. With reference to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), worldwide Asian seabass production of year 2022 was 154,281 metric tons (FAO FisStaJ, 2024), in which its market value was expected to exceed 1012 million USD by 2024, highlighting its economic significance and potential for its growth. Thailand's aquaculture sector generated approximately $130 million USD in 2022 from the production of 45,415 tones (Department of Fisheries, 2022). These trends may stem from a heightened understanding of the health advantages offered by protein and omega-3 fatty acid, the important nutrients abundant in Asian seabass meat (FAO-FAD, 2020).

The multifaceted attribute of freshness, a fundamental aspect of perceived quality in fish, is intricately linked to the acceptability of fish meat, encompassing taste, odor, color, and texture (Ahimbisibwe et al., 2010). Owing to its chemical composition, fish is a highly perishable food since it is rich in protein and lipid with high content of water (Tavares et al., 2021). The inherently short shelf life of fresh fish is primarily attributed to microbial growth and enzymatic reactions, which diminish its quality as time passes. It is well known that the shelf life of seafood products depends on various factors, such as fish species, fishing location, methods of fishing and bleeding, the season of catch and storage temperatures (Van Nguyen et al., 2013). In the whitefish industry, adequate bleeding of fish has been proven as the essential process for achieving high product quality. Fish muscles can retain blood mainly due to the stress during the catch and rough handling or improper bleeding once the fish have been caught (Olsen et al., 2014). Insufficient bleeding in fish leads to a discolored, dark or reddish flesh, especially whitefish, which can negatively impact their market value (Skjelvareid et al., 2017). In addition, spoilage can be expedited, primarily because the retained blood in the muscle serves as an excellent nutritional substrate for the microbial proliferation and ideal environment (Addeen et al., 2014). The color and flavor of fish muscle are directly attributed to the concentration of both myoglobin and hemoglobin (Richards & Hultin, 2002). Both hemoglobin and myoglobin in fish meat are able to accelerate the lipid oxidation (Maqsood & Benjakul, 2011b). This effect can be governed by the lipoxygenase action and the subsequent release of heme or non-heme iron when these proteins undergo autoxidation (Faustman et al., 2010). After slaughtering, the iron atom within the heme ring of heme proteins is initially in the ferrous (+2) state. This ferrous heme protein then undergoes autoxidation, converting it to met (+3) heme protein (metHP). This autoxidation is pivotal for boosting lipid oxidation, as metHP engages with peroxides to drive the generation of compounds that both induce and extend lipid oxidation (Everse & Hsia, 1997). Apart from this concern, blood is identified as a key contributor to the post-harvest quality deterioration of fish (Wu et al., 2024).

Bleeding, typically achieved by cutting a major blood vessels like gill arches or tail veins to allow passive blood drainage, represents the conventional method for blood removal in fish (Wang et al., 2024). To increase the efficacy of bleeding, water perfusion techniques, e.g. the Tsumoto-shiki method, etc. have been developed and have garnered considerable interest across the fisheries sector and seafood processing industry. Water perfusion (WP) removes blood via freshwater injection into the blood vessels of fish. This technique has demonstrated efficacy in blood removal. Narisawa et al. (2023) reported that WP more effectively removed blood from horse mackerel muscle, compared to conventional bleeding methods like gill cutting. Although WP on blood removal and water behavior in fish muscle was documented, no comparison with the conventional bleeding, especially GC method, was investigated. Therefore, our study aimed to analyze the impact of WP method and GC method on the blood removal, muscle tissue histology and postmortem quality changes of Asian seabass slices during the storage at 4 °C. The insights gained from this research could provide the proper bleeding method for the farmer or fishermen, in which the high-quality fish meat with the prolonged shelf life could be achieved.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

All chemicals used were of analytical grade. Acetone (CAS 67-64-1, 99.5 %), hydrochloric acid (CAS 7647-01-0, 37 %), trichloroacetic acid (CAS 76-03-9, 99 %), sodium acetate (CAS 127-09-3, 99 %), chloroform (CAS 67-66-3, 99.8 %), methanol (CAS67-56-1, 99.9 %), sodium chloride (CAS 7647-14-5, 99.8 %), ammonium thiocyanate (CAS 1762-95-4, 99 %), sodium hydroxide (CAS 1310-73-2, 98 %), and iron chloride (CAS 10025-77-1, 97 %) were purchased from Merck (Damstadt, Germany). Bathophenanthroline disulfonic acid (CAS 52746-49-3, 98 %), thioglycolic acid (CAS 68-11-1, 80 %), thiobarbituric acid (CAS 504-17-6, ≥ 98 %), malonaldehyde (CAS 122-31-6, 97 %), and cumene hydroperoxide (CAS 80-15-9, 80 %) were procured from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Microbial media (Plate count agar) were bought from Himedia (Mumbai, India).

Fish samples and preparation

Live Asian seabass (Lates Calcarifer) (average weight: 1.5–2 kg/fish) were collected from Ko Yo dock (7°09′09.4″N 100°32′56.6″), Songkhla province, Thailand. Fish were placed in the container filled with well oxygenated clean water and delivered to the laboratory within 1 h.

Bleeding

Fish were killed and bled by either gill cut or water perfusion methods. Fish were subsequently cleaned by washing with sterile water and stored in crushed ice.

Gill cut method

Gill cut method was adopted for bleeding as described by Harrysson et al. (2020). The gills were severed, and the fish were then bled in iced water for 30 min. The fish were placed on the screen for draining at 4 °C for 5 min.

Water perfusion (WP) method

WP was performed as described by Narisawa et al. (2023) with a slight modification. Initially, live fish underwent bleeding by cutting the blood vessels (primary dorsal arteries) beneath the gill membranes and immersing them in iced water for 15 min. Thereafter, the tail was severed. Distilled water was perfused via 1.1 mm diameter nozzle through the largest visible blood vessels (tail vein). During post-perfusion, fish were subsequently kept in a refrigerator at 4 °C, in which the head faced downward, for approximately 30 min to facilitate water drainage.

Unbled fish were sacrificed by penetrating captive bolt stunning method and used as the controls. For all three fish samples, fish were first washed thoroughly with sterile water, then descaled and beheaded. Then the fish were subsequently eviscerated and cleaned with sterile water to remove the debris. The filleting was done and the dorsal portions of fillets were cut into the slices having 2–3 cm thickness (Table 1). Slices (15 g) were then individually placed by polystyrene tray and inserted in polythene bags before sealing. Analyses

Chemical composition and lipid oxidation

Heme iron content

Heme iron content in fish muscle was examined following the protocols given by Cheng and Ockerman (2004). Specifically, samples (2 g) were mixed with 9 ml of an acid-acetone solution (90 % acetone, 8 % deionized water, 2 % HCl, v/v/v). This mixture was thoroughly macerated with a glass rod and subsequently equilibrated for 1 h at room temperature. Filtration of the supernatant was performed using a Whatman No. 4 filter paper, and the absorbance was recorded at 640 nm against a blank of acid-acetone solution. The heme iron content was subsequently computed utilizing the following formula and a conversion factor of 0.0882 μg/μg hematin (Eqs. (1), (2)).(1)Heme iron contentmg/100gof sample=Total pigmentmg/100g×0.0882

Total pigment was computed as detailed by Ockerman (1985).(2)Total pigmentmg/100gof sample=A640×680where A640 represents the absorbance of the filtrate at 640 nm, and 680 is a conversion coefficient factor.

Non-heme iron content

Non-heme iron content was quantified using a protocol adapted from Schricker et al. (1982). Extraction was done by incubating the sample (1 g) in a mixture of 40 % trichloroacetic acid and 6 N HCl (1:1, v/v) at 65 °C for 22 h in an incubator shaker, followed by cooling. The extract (400 μl) was reacted with 2 ml of color reagent containing 1:20:20 (w/v/v) ratio of bathophenanthroline disulfonic acid, thioglycolic acid, and sodium acetate for 10 min at room temperature. Non-heme iron concentration was determined against a standard curve generated from iron standard solution (0–2 ppm) and expressed as mg/100 g of sample.

Lipid oxidation

Peroxide value (PV) was measured according to the protocol established by Sae-leaw and Benjakul (2014). Ground muscle (1 g) was homogenized for 2 min at 13,500 rpm in 11 ml of chloroform /methanol (2,1, v/v). After being filtered, the resulting filtrate (7 ml) was combined with 0.5 % NaCl (2 ml), vortexed for 30 s and centrifuged (3000×g, 3 min, 4 °C). Three ml of lower layer were mixed with 25 μl of 30 % ammonium thiocyanate and 23 μl of 20 mM iron (II) chloride (20 min, room temperature). Absorbance was read at 500 nm. Cumene hydroperoxide (0.5–2 ppm) was used for standard curve preparation. After subtracting the blank, PV was computed and reported as mg of cumene hydroperoxide/kg of sample.

The thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) value was examined (Benjakul & Bauer, 2001). Ground sample (0.5 g) was dispersed in 2.5 ml of solution comprising 0.0375 % thiobarbituric acid, 15 % trichloroacetic acid, and 0.25 N HCl. After being heated for 10 min in a boiling water bath, the mixture was cooled with running tap water and centrifuged (3600×g, 20 min). The TBARS value was computed from a standard curve of malonaldehyde (MDA) (0–5 ppm) and expressed as mg of MDA/kg sample.

Fatty acid profile

Oil extraction from the fresh slice and those stored for 12 days was performed using a chloroform/methanol (2:1, v/v) solvent system (Raju et al., 2022). The extracted oil was then converted to fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) through a transmethylation two-step process involving methanolic-NaOH and methanolic-HCl. Gas chromatography (Agilent GC 7890B, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a flame ionization detector was used for FAME analysis. The fatty acid content was reported in percentage.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra

FTIR spectra were analyzed with the aid of a Bruker Vertex 70 FTIR spectrometer (Bruker Co., Ettlingen, Germany). Oil extracted from fresh slice and those stored for 12 days were used. For each analysis, 200 μl of oil was introduced into a crystal cell. Spectra acquisition was performed in the mid-infrared region (4000–400 cm−1) with 32 scans and a resolution of 4 cm-1. Normalization of the acquired signal was carried out by referencing an empty cell spectrum. Data processing was conducted using 3.0 software (Bruker Co., Billerica, MA, USA).

pH

The pH of the Asian seabass meat was determined from the homogenate, which was prepared by homogenizing 2 g of sample in 10 volumes of deionized water for 2 min at 10,000 rpm. The pH meter (Sartorious North America, Edgewood, NY, USA) was used to measure the pH (Nirmal & Benjakul, 2011).

Microbiological quality

Total viable count (TVC) and psychrophilic bacterial counts (PBC) were determined as tailored by Palamae et al. (2024). The sample (25 g) was accurately weighed and transferred into a stomacher bag containing 225 ml of 0.85 % saline solution. Homogenization was done in a stomacher blender (M400, Seward, West Sussex, UK) (220 rpm, 1 min), followed by sequential dilution with 0.85 % saline solution. For enumeration, 100 μl of diluted samples were `spread plated. TVC and PBC were enumerated on plate count agar (PCA) after incubation at 37 °C for 2 days and 4 °C for 10 days, respectively.

Color

Color measurements for all fish muscle samples were performed with the aid of Colourflex colorimeter (Hunter Lab, Reston, VA, USA). Following the CIE system (L* = Lightness, a* = Redness and greenness, b* = Yellowness and blueness), the data were reported. A standard white tile (L* = 94.86, a* = −1.17, b* = 0.86) was utilized for calibration (Singh et al., 2021).

Histological images

Histological images of Asian seabass muscle were visualized as per the method of Chanchi Prashanthkumar et al. (2023) and Jantrakajorn et al. (2025). Fish muscle was evaluated for tissue alterations, including gap formation between myofibers, increased eosinophilic staining of muscle fibers, rupture of connective tissue sheaths (fascicle), loss of muscular striations, and fluid accumulation within the tissue structure (edema). These morphological changes were documented to differentiate between unbled, gill-cut bled, and water-perfused bled muscle tissues.

Statistical analysis

Completely randomized design (CRD) was used for the whole study. All experiments and analyses were performed in triplicate and the data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corporation, NY, USA). An analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, and Duncan's multiple range test was utilized for comparing mean values. A p-value of less than 0.05 (p < 0.05) was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Effect of bleeding methods on chemical compositions and lipid oxidation

Heme iron and non-heme iron contents

Heme and non-heme iron contents in slices of UB, GC, and WP samples during refrigerated storage of 12 days are illustrated in Fig. 1 (a) and (b), respectively. After bleeding (day 0), both bled samples had the lower heme and non-heme iron contents than UB sample (P < 0.05). This finding substantiated the successful removal of bleeding from the muscle via Gill cut bleeding (GC) and water perfusion bleeding (WP) processes. When comparing between both bleeding methods, WP sample had the lower heme and non-heme iron contents than GC sample (P < 0.05). This result indicated the greater efficacy for the former than the latter in blood removal from the fish muscle. Blood contains hemoglobin, a key heme protein, whereas myoglobin is a hemoprotein found in muscle cells (Alves et al., 2022). Hemoglobin basically involves four polypeptide chains, and each chain possesses a heme group. Each heme group contains an iron atom centrally coordinated within its ring structure (Richards et al., 2007). The heme iron content of all samples decreased continuously up to day 6 (P < 0.05). The lowering of heme iron content might be due to the breakdown of porphyrin ring, thus releasing non-heme iron into the muscle. Nonetheless, similar heme iron content was found between bled and unbled samples during 9–12 days of storage (P > 0.05). Maqsood and Benjakul (2011a) documented that at pH 6, methemoglobin was formed in tilapia and grouper hemoglobin, favoring heme release or porphyrin destruction. In contrast, at pH 7, a reduced form of iron in heme persisted, suggesting that higher pH helped maintain heme integrity. The observed reduction in heme iron content over increasing storage time was likely attributable to heme destruction, leading to the liberation of non-heme iron (Benjakul & Bauer, 2001). Moreover, the process of fish deterioration reduces the solubility of heme proteins, thereby contributing to the observed decline in total heme iron content (Chaijan et al., 2005). Maqsood and Benjakul (2011b) confirmed that severing the tail of Asian seabass (Lates calcarifer) to release blood into seawater reduced its hemoprotein content. Similarly, Richards and Hultin (2002) observed a decrease in hemoglobin content in rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss) and mackerel (Scomber scombrus) after bleeding in water via gill or tail excision for 30 min, when compared to unbled samples. These reports confirmed that conventional gill bleeding was efficient for blood removal in Asian seabass muscle. Furthermore, heme iron content can be used to assess the extent of blood removal (Narisawa et al., 2023). WP lowered the heme iron content in fish samples, compared to both GC and UB samples (P < 0.05). Conventional bleeding, which relies on passive blood outflow from cut vessels, had the quick loss in effectiveness due to cardiac arrest and blood coagulation (Harrysson et al., 2020). In contrast, the perfusion method actively removes blood by introducing water, primarily through the dorsal artery or rail vein, allowing it to flow through muscle capillaries and rapidly flush out blood (Narisawa et al., 2023). The non-heme iron content was lower in bled samples than that of unbled samples on day 0. Thereafter, non-heme iron upsurged in all samples, whereas a substantial increase was seen in the unbled counterpart (Fig. 1b). The rising non-heme iron content during storage occurred concurrently with a decline in heme iron content (Fig. 1a). These findings indicate that the porphyrin ring is more prone to disruption during prolonged storage, leading to a more significant release of free irons. According to Decker and Hultin (1990), the denaturation of heme pigment or other iron containing proteins over increasing storage time is a potential cause for iron release. Maqsood and Benjakul (2011b) found that prolonged storage led to increased non-heme iron content in fish muscle, through heme degradation. The degradation of heme iron diminishes the nutritional quality of fish by reducing iron's bioavailability, as heme iron is more readily absorbed than non-heme iron (Hunt & Roughead, 2000). However, it was noted that non-heme iron content of WP sample became slightly higher than that of GC sample during 9–12 days of storage. Water jet used for bleeding might injure the muscle tissue or blood vein, thus facilitating the liberation of free iron from the destructed heme. Along with the enhanced microbial growth, the disrupted tissue could be more degraded, thus releasing more free iron. Therefore, bleeding presented a potential method to lower both heme iron and non-heme iron contents, which were the crucial factors that contribute to lipid oxidation and off-odor development in refrigerated Asian seabass slices. Although bleeding by both methods could remove the blood from fish muscle as indicated by the decreases in both heme and non-heme iron content, only live fish must be used for bleeding. This would limit the application of bleeding, especially for deceased fish after being caught.

Lipid oxidation

Peroxide value (PV)

PVs of UB, GC, and WP sampled during refrigerated storage are illustrated in Fig. 2(a). Similar PV in UB, GC, and WP samples were observed at day 0 (P > 0.05). PV upsurged during the first 6 days of storage, in which the unbled showed the higher PV than others. After day 6, PV of the unbled still showed the higher PV than others. However, PV of WP sample was slightly greater than that of GC counterpart until the end of storage. Harrysson et al. (2020) reported higher PV in unbled minced trout, compared to bled counterparts during a one-month period of iced storage. By day 18, PV of bled samples reached that of the unbled samples. Similarly, PV in unbled cobia fillets increased modestly during the first four weeks of storage at −20 ± 2 °C, and then declined from week 4 to 8 (Van Nguyen, 2018). Maqsood and Benjakul (2011b) reported a significant PV increase in unbled samples within the first 9 days, with no subsequent changes until day 15. Consistent with these findings, the PV of unbled samples in the current study was generally higher than that of bled samples throughout the storage. The unbled samples containing higher heme and non-heme iron content were prone to lipid oxidation than bled samples with less amount of prooxidant, especially non-heme iron Nonetheless, it was noted that WP samples tended to show slightly higher PV than GC sample with the extended storage period. The injured or disrupted muscle tissue containing membrane lipids might be susceptible to oxidation, since those lipids were more exposed to oxygen during the prolonged storage. Phospholipids have been known to constitute in muscle, serving as the membrane lipids (Ali & Szabó, 2023). Phospholipids, primarily phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylethanolamine, are major constituents of fish muscle membranes (Takama et al., 1994). Thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) value

TBARS value for UB, GC, and WP samples increased (P < 0.05) with extended storage duration up to 12 days Fig. 2(b). Slight increase in TBARS value was found during the first 6 days. Subsequently, the drastic increase was found in the unbled sample until the end of storage. Furthermore, the increment was also observed in the samples bled by two methods. When comparing WP and GC sample, the former showed the higher upsurge in TBARS value. Higher increasing rates of TBARS formation of unbled samples throughout the storage period compared to bled samples were probably owing to the presence of blood rich in iron, serving as prooxidant. Maqsood and Benjakul (2011b) similarly found the elevated TBARS in unbled Asian seabass, compared to the bled samples. Van Nguyen (2018) highlighted that unbled samples exhibited increasing TBARS levels, while bled samples showed the constant value within a certain period. Moreover, Sakai et al. (2006) reported that bled skipjack contained lower level of malonaldehyde and 4-hydroxyhexanal, compared to unbled controls after 2 days at 0 °C. Heme iron catalysis of lipid oxidation is distinguished from lipoxygenase-mediated mechanisms by its potential involvement of both one and two electron transfer processes in different iron coordination environments (Carlsen et al., 2005). In addition to the hemoglobin, fish blood contains considerable quantity of white blood cells capable of generating reactive oxygen species, e.g., superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radicals. Furthermore, these cells produce lipoxygenase product (Pettitt et al., 1989). However, WP seemed to accelerate lipid oxidation in the late stage of storage than GC. This was plausibly due to the harsh condition used for water jet, which could dissipate or disrupt the tissue membrane, favoring the oxidation of lipids in the muscle. These current findings reinforce the critical role of bleeding in suppressing the accumulation of lipid oxidation byproducts.

Fatty acid profile

The fatty acid compositions of UB, GC, and WP samples at the initial and final day (12 days) of refrigerated storage are shown in Table 2. For all samples, the predominant fatty acids identified were palmitic acid (16,0), which accounted 23.05–24.26 %, followed by oleic acid (18,1) accounting for 20.11–21.47 % of total fatty acids. Eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA, 20:5ω3), and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, 22:6ω3) were also found as the dominant fatty acids in all the samples. Özyurt and Polat (2006) similarly reported these specific fatty acids as the primary component of seabass fillets. DHA ranged from 13.78 to 14.96 %, while EPA constituted 7.63–8.51 %. This finding was consistent with the established literature indicating that DHA concentrations typically exceed EPA levels in fish lipids (Song et al., 2023). The PUFA content of unbled sample decreased after 12 days of storage when compared to that found at the initial day of storage (P < 0.05). This observed decrease was consistent with the well-documented susceptibility of PUFAs to oxidation during storage, primarily driven by their high degree of unsaturation (Sahena et al., 2014). Coincidentally, the content of saturated fatty acids increased (Table 2). Apart from non-heme iron, lipoxygenases were able to induce the dioxygenation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (Wood et al., 2020). Lipid hydroperoxides undergo subsequent cleavage catalyzed by iron, yielding either peroxyl radicals via an oxidative pathway or alkoxyl radicals through the reductive pathway (Carlsen et al., 2005). The inherent lability of polyunsaturated lipids to oxidation, combined with the radical chain reaction nature of this process, makes them exceptionally prone to catalytic acceleration by minute quantity of iron. pH

The pH of Asian seabass without and with bleeding by 2 methods during storage are presented in Fig. 2(c). The initial pH values for the UB, GC and WP samples were similar (6.57–6.59) (P > 0.05). The observed initial pH values in the samples are similar to those documented for trout by Harryson et al. (2020), indicating a general similarity in the initial muscle pH across different fish species. Living muscle tissue usually exhibited a pH that is close to neutral. After death, muscle pH drops sharply. This is because anaerobic glycolysis continues, thereby producing lactic acid (Wang et al., 2024) and dissolution of CO2 in fish muscle takes place (Manju et al., 2007). During storage, the pH of the UB samples was consistently higher than those of both GC and WP samples. As depicted in Fig. 2(c), the rise in pH observed during storage was more likely caused by the formation of volatile basic components, including ammonia and trimethylamine (Sivarajan et al., 2017). These compounds are generated through the activity of either microbial or endogenous enzymes (Li et al., 2013). Since the unbled sample still have heme proteins, serving as the nutrients for microbes, the decomposition of proteins and nitrogenous compounds led to the formation of volatile basic compounds. Temdee et al. (2022) also found the increase in pH of Asian seabass slice during the storage at 4 °C. This resulted in the rise in pHs. However, the bled samples showed the lower increases in pH than unbled counterpart. When comparing between WP and GC sample, the former exhibited higher pH than the latter. Therefore, bleeding methods showed the effect on the pH changes of Asian seabass during the storage.

Effect of bleeding methods on microbiological quality

TVC and PBC in UB, GC and WP samples during refrigerated storage are shown in Fig. 4(a) and (b) respectively. Both the TVC and PBC upsurged with extended iced storage period (P < 0.05) for UB, GC and WP samples. The initial TVCs were 3.86 ± 0.12 log CFU/g, 3.79 ± 0.12 log CFU/g and 3.81 ± 0.06 log CFU/ g for UB, GC and WP samples, respectively. Low TVC in all samples reflected high fish quality and good manufacturing practices. TVC and PBC of UB sample were significantly higher than those of bled samples during the entire storage (P < 0.05), demonstrating the effectiveness of bleeding in retarding bacterial growth in the slices. Nevertheless, the bleeding had no impact on TVC in the slices on day 0. The established microbial limit for fish is 6 log CFU/g (Swanson, 2011). TVC of UB sample reached this level on day 9 of storage. Nonetheless, the GC and WP samples reached the microbial limit on day 12. On day 12, higher TVC was found for WP than GC sample (P < 0.05). The disrupted tissues caused by water jet might allow the microorganisms to invade into the muscle with ease, thus favoring their proliferation. The presence of blood, abundant in nutrients for microbial growth, could lead to bacterial proliferation. The microorganisms could be contaminated during the dressing of Asian seabass. The gram-positive bacteria acquire heme via an energy-independent cell wall relay, followed by ATP-binding Cassette (ABC) transporter-mediated membrane transport. When heme get inside the cytoplasm of either gram-negative or gram-positive bacteria, it is degraded by heme oxygenase for iron release or incorporated into bacterial proteins through molecular hijacking (Pishchany & Skaar, 2012). For PBC, a similar trend to mesophilic bacteria was observed. UB samples showed higher increase in PBC during the storage. Apart from heme iron content, fish spoilage is critically driven by intrinsic factors such as high postmortem pH, limited carbohydrate and availability and decomposition of non-protein nitrogeneous fraction, particularly sulfur-containing amino acids (Herbert & Shewan, 1975). At lower temperatures, psychrophilic bacteria become dominant, inhibiting the growth of mesophilic bacteria. On day 10 and 12, PBC of GC sample was lower than that of WP sample (P < 0.05), most likely due to the tissue breakdown caused by water jet used for WP. Apart from the ease for microbial invasion, this looser or disrupted tissue could provide more available nutrients to microorganisms for their growth. Gill cutting improved preservation by reducing residual blood, thereby lowering heme iron–induced lipid oxidation and microbial spoilage (Sterniša et al., 2018). Maqsood and Benjakul (2011b) documented that unbled fish samples showed the higher microbial load than bled counterpart. Furthermore, spinal cord disruption combined with bleeding effectively improved postharvest quality by delaying rigor mortis, and reducing drip loss (Wang et al., 2024). The result suggested that bleeding could delay bacterial growth by depriving them with essential nutrients present in blood.

Color

Color is a key determinant in assessing product quality at the point of sale. Muscle structure and pigment concentration are the primary factors affecting the color of fish meat (Cai et al., 2014; Ginés et al., 2004). Color parameters (L*, a*, and b*) were monitored over 12 days of storage period. Table 1 indicates a gradual decrease in L* values across all three samples during storage of 12 days. Similar L* values was detected among the all samples on day 0 and 3 (P > 0.05). Subsequently, significant differences became apparent during 6–12 days of storage (P < 0.05). However, the a* value of the UB sample was different from both GC and WP samples throughout the entire storage period. Due to the effective bleeding, the oxidation of heme pigments in the GC and WP samples was reduced, compared to the unbled (UB) samples. This reduction in heme pigment oxidation subsequently lowered the oxidation of lipid, especially PUFAs, thus lowering the loss of redness. These findings aligned with those of Mosavinia et al. (2022) and Lan et al. (2021). Moreover, the b* value of the fillet exhibited a decline with extended storage. Lan et al. (2021) documented that Lateolabrax japonicus fillets subjected to distilled water (DW) immersion treatment had the discoloration due to the confirmational changes in heme protein as well as lipid oxidation (Cropotova et al., 2021). Initially, some variations in b* values were present across the group, but these differences became negligible by the end of the storage period. The a* and b* color values in fish are thought to be related to the denaturation of hemeproteins and lipid oxidation (Hui et al., 2016). Therefore, bleeding showed the impact on color of Asian seabass meat to some degree, affecting the appearance of slices.

Effect of bleeding methods on histological images

The histological or microstructure changes of UB, GC, and WP samples showed some discrepancies in muscle tissue morphology, including gap formation between myofibres, increased eosinophilic staining of muscle fibers, rupture of connective tissue sheaths (fascicle), and loss of muscular striations (Table 1). Irrespective of bleeding, initial postmortem morphology reflected shrunken muscle fibers, after intense eosinophilic staining. The formation of intercellular spaces and the clear separation of muscle fibers from the surrounding connective tissue (fascicles) were more prominent in the WP samples. Additionally, edema around blood vessels and connective tissue was evident as fluid-filled space, which were markedly pronounced in the WP group (Table 1). These findings confirm that waterjet application during water perfusion induces structural disruption of muscle tissue. In other words, the links between fibers and myocommata were weakened (Chéret et al., 2005). Overall, the WP sample exhibited a significantly higher degree of gap formation relative to the GC and UB groups. Moreover, basophilic colonies were found as a specific histological feature of the WP muscle, observed in close proximity to small capillaries and embedded within the connective tissue. This plausibly indicates bacterial contamination during process. In the case of WP bleeding, blood vessels and connective tissue were damaged by mechanical force. This disruption led to the infiltration of excess water into the surrounding areas, resulting in fluid accumulation within the connective tissue and the formation of gaps between muscle fibers—lesions indicative of edema. This finding aligned with observations from Narisawa et al. (2023), who reported a similar pattern of increased water content after 30–60 s of prolonged treatment. The presence of this excessive fluid altered the extracellular environment, impeded nutrient and physically disrupted the delicate organization of cellular and fibrillar structures (Scallan & Huxley, 2009). The tissue injury provided the invasion of microorganisms into the gap or cavity as shown by the presence of basophilic bacterial colonies. However, these microorganisms could not grow in the initial stage at refrigerated temperature as shown by the similar TVC and PBC between GC and WP samples during the first 6 days. Nonetheless, those bacteria could adopt themselves and regrow in the later stage of storage. In the presence of nutrients leached out from muscle, microorganisms could proliferate at the faster rate as shown by high TVC and PBC of WP sample during 9–12 days of storage.

Conclusion

Both bleeding methods effectively enhanced blood removal from Asian seabass muscle compared to unbled counterpart. This was indicated by lowered heme and non-heme iron contents. In addition, bleeding could retard lipid oxidation and retain PUFAs in fish muscle. However, during the late storage, the WP samples showed increased microbial load, non-heme iron content, and lipid oxidation likely due to tissue disruption caused by the high-pressure water jet, which facilitated microbial invasion and spoilage. In contrast, the GC method minimized tissue damage and preserved fish quality more effectively than WP. Therefore, GC is recommended as a more suitable bleeding technique for Asian seabass due to its effectiveness and operational simplicity. To enhance the efficacy for quality maintenance, the integration of bleeding with sustainable packaging derived from food and agricultural waste or non-thermal technologies should be further studied. This would help maximize preservation to ensure prime quality and safety of fish and fish products.
