Another day in FARADISE? Lessons learned from a real-world global registry of pulsed field ablation with the pentaspline catheter for atrial fibrillation ablation
Authors: Marco Schiavone, Luigi Di Biase
Published: 2025

This editorial refers to ‘Real-world experience with the pentaspline pulsed field ablation system: one-year outcomes of the FARADISE registry’ by L.V.A. Boersma et al. https://doi.org/10.1093/europace/euaf182.

Over the last two decades, thermal catheter ablation technologies for atrial fibrillation (AF)—radiofrequency (RF) and cryoablation—have demonstrated robust efficacy but remain constrained by risks inherent to thermal injury, including oesophageal damage, phrenic nerve injury, and pulmonary vein stenosis.1 The emergence of pulsed field ablation (PFA) has brought a paradigm shift in AF ablation, offering tissue-selective myocardial injury via irreversible electroporation, with the promise of preserving adjacent structures.2 Early pre-clinical3,4 and clinical studies5 have consistently demonstrated rapid lesion formation, procedural efficiency, and a favourable safety profile, fuelling widespread enthusiasm and accelerating adoption across geographies.

In this context, the FARADISE registry published in this issue of Europace by Boersma et al.6 represents a landmark in the clinical journey of PFA technology. As one of the largest global, prospective, real-world registries to assess the pentaspline PFA catheter in routine clinical use, FARADISE captures data from 1158 patients across 48 centres in 21 countries. This first interim analysis reports 1-year procedural, safety, and effectiveness outcomes, offering valuable insights into the device’s performance outside the structured confines of randomized trials. FARADISE enrolled consecutive patients with a clinical indication for AF ablation, treated per operator judgment and institutional standard-of-care using the commercially available pentaspline PFA catheter. Two-thirds of patients had paroxysmal AF, and 90.1% were undergoing de novo ablation. Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI)-only procedures predominated (72.5% overall), with more extensive lesion sets in non-paroxysmal AF (42.5% received additional ablation). Procedural efficiency was notable: Median skin-to-skin time 51 min, left atrial dwell time 31 min, and fluoroscopy 12 min. The safety profile was favourable: 1.5% early serious adverse events and no deaths. Haemolysis occurred in four cases, none of which required dialysis, and one coronary spasm was observed without sequelae. At 1 year, clinical effectiveness (freedom from arrhythmia recurrence, cardioversion, or repeat ablation) was 80.8% in paroxysmal AF and 67.7% in non-paroxysmal AF, with no differences between PVI-only and PVI-plus strategies within each AF type. De novo procedures achieved superior outcomes compared with redo cases across AF subtypes.

The registry’s most compelling strength lies in its scope and representativeness. Its prospective design and high follow-up completeness (>97% at 1 year) lend robustness to the outcome data. The observed clinical effectiveness in FARADISE compares favourably with both prior PFA studies and historical thermal ablation registries. In the VOLT trial,7 using a novel balloon-in-basket PFA system, 6-month success was 88.2% in paroxysmal AF and 76.7% in persistent AF, with freedom from symptomatic recurrence documented in 90.2% and 74.4%, respectively. The ADVENT trial,8 which like FARADISE employed the pentaspline catheter, demonstrated 73.3% 1-year success for PFA, confirming non-inferiority to conventional thermal ablation, with a low 2.1% serious adverse event rate. The AdmIRE trial,9 evaluating the variable-loop catheter, reported 74.6% freedom from arrhythmia at 12 months with an adverse event rate of 2.9%, underscoring a consistent safety profile across PFA technologies. Meanwhile, the pivotal PULSED AF trial,10 using the circular array catheter, showed somewhat lower success rates—66.2% at 1 year for paroxysmal AF and 55.1% for persistent AF—but with the lowest primary safety event rate reported to date (0.7%). The FARADISE findings also align closely with outcomes from the largest real-world PFA registries. In the EU-PORIA registry,11 1-year clinical effectiveness was 83% in paroxysmal AF and 72% in persistent AF, with a low 2.3% serious adverse event rate. The MANIFEST-PF registry,12 the largest to date with >1500 patients, reported a 1-year arrhythmia-free survival of 81.6% for paroxysmal AF and 71.5% for persistent AF, with a 1.9% major complication rate and no evidence of energy-specific injuries such as atrioesophageal fistula or PV stenosis. However, when interpreting registry data, the varying rates of concomitant antiarrhythmic drug use must always be considered, as they may artificially inflate apparent success and complicate cross-study comparisons. Indeed, treatment success off AADs was significantly lower (64.1% overall; 69.2% in paroxysmal AF and 54.4% in persistent AF; P < 0.001), underscoring the importance of stratifying outcomes by concomitant drug therapy. Taken together, these converging data not only confirm the favourable safety and efficacy of the pentaspline catheter but also place its outcomes at the upper range of reported effectiveness across the spectrum of contemporary PFA platforms (Table 1).

Equally relevant is the demonstration of procedural efficiency across experience strata, reinforcing previous real-world observations that PFA’s short learning curve facilitates rapid integration into routine practice. That said, the learning curve data deserve a closer look. As shown in Figure 4C of the article commented on in this editorial, the blue curve (operators with 11–25 prior procedures; 1-year success rates 70.4%) paradoxically performed slightly worse than both the least experienced group (0–10 cases; 1-year success rates 73.9%). This counterintuitive dip in outcomes is not explained in the manuscript. Encouragingly, outcomes improved again beyond 25 cases, with non-statistically significant gains in clinical effectiveness thereafter (1-year success rates 77.5% after 50 procedures).

While the FARADISE data are encouraging, several limitations—some inherent to real-world registries—warrant consideration. Most importantly, without a thermal ablation control group, FARADISE cannot provide the ‘holy grail’ answer to the field’s most pressing debate: is pulsed field ablation truly superior to conventional thermal energy? The ADVENT trial8 concluded that PFA was non-inferior but not superior to thermal ablation in paroxysmal AF, when assessed with conventional follow-up strategies. In contrast, the more recent SINGLE SHOT CHAMPION13 trial suggested a superiority signal of PFA over cryoballoon ablation (37.1% vs. 50.7% recurrence at 1 year), but this discrepancy can be partly explained by its use of continuous implantable cardiac monitoring, which detects a higher number of recurrences than intermittent Holter-ECGs. By increasing the sensitivity for arrhythmia detection, SINGLE SHOT CHAMPION may have amplified its statistical power to uncover between-group differences that earlier studies, including ADVENT, could not. Of course, FARADISE is a registry, and therefore any formal comparison of PFA vs. thermal energy lies outside its scope. Another important limitation, however, is the heterogeneity of follow-up: outcome assessment relied on local standard-of-care, ranging from scheduled Holter monitoring to symptom-driven ECGs. This variability introduces potential bias in recurrence detection, particularly for asymptomatic events. Although implantable loop recorder data were available for a subset of patients, they were insufficiently complete to permit a robust sub-analysis.

Although lesion sets were broadly categorized as PVI-only vs. PVI+, the interpretation of these comparisons is limited. The registry reported no differences in outcomes between the two approaches within each AF subtype, but the study was not powered to detect such differences. In patients with persistent AF, treatment strategies were far from homogeneous: only a minority underwent posterior wall ablation (16.5%), no patient received left atrial appendage (LAA) isolation, and lesion sets varied considerably across centres. Moreover, the 1-year follow-up horizon is too short to appreciate the potential incremental benefit of ablating non-PV structures, which often manifests beyond the first year, as mentioned by the ATHENA investigators.14 Taken together, the absence of observed differences between PVI-only and PVI-plus strategies in FARADISE should be interpreted with caution—particularly in the context of persistent AF, which represented 34.6% of the study population. Ongoing randomized clinical trials (e.g. AVANT guard and PIFPAF-PFA trials) will better define the role of adjunctive ablations in persistent AF patients.

Haemolysis emerged as one of the most relevant safety caveats in FARADISE. Four cases were reported (1 paroxysmal, 3 non-paroxysmal AF), each occurring after a high number of PFA deliveries (66, 93, 140, and 161 applications). None required dialysis, which is reassuring, but these observations confirm prior concerns that haemolysis may be a dose-dependent complication of extensive PFA. The phenomenon is consistent with prior reports, with Venier et al. suggesting ∼70 applications as a predictive threshold.15 Mechanistically, haemolysis likely results from excess exposure of circulating blood to the electric field, particularly when tissue contact is suboptimal. Adjunctive imaging modalities such as intracardiac echocardiography (ICE), by improving contact and lesion placement, could therefore reduce risk. Yet in FARADISE, ICE use was rare (16.1%), and peri-procedural hydration protocols were not reported, limiting further interpretation. Taken together, while the absolute incidence of haemolysis in FARADISE was low, the complication highlights the need for procedural vigilance—avoiding unnecessary energy applications, ensuring adequate tissue contact, and eventually adopting systematic hydration post-ablation protocols. Importantly, the fact that none of the FARADISE cases resulted in dialysis underscores that haemolysis is usually manageable, but its recognition is critical as PFA expands into broader, more complex lesion sets.

In conclusion, the FARADISE registry offers one of the largest prospective real-world datasets on the pentaspline PFA catheter, confirming its procedural efficiency, favourable safety, and consistent 1-year effectiveness across AF subtypes. Its findings are broadly aligned with prior PFA studies and registries, supporting the reproducibility of outcomes and the feasibility of rapid adoption. At the same time, several questions remain open and yet to be fully investigated: whether PFA is truly superior to thermal energy, how heterogeneous follow-up influences recurrence detection, what role additional lesion sets play in persistent AF, and how best to mitigate haemolysis associated with extensive applications.
