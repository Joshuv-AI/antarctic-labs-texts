Trends in influenza vaccine disparities in the U.S. following the introduction of COVID-19: Insights from the national health interview survey
Authors: Margaret L. Lind, Emily Goldmann, Gregory S. Lalonde
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesSeasonal influenza vaccination coverage in the US remains below the Healthy People 2030 goal of 70 % and differs by race and ethnicity. This study examines the impact of flu risk factors and healthcare access on racial and ethnic disparities in coverage.MethodsWe estimated vaccination coverage among the US population (≥6 months) using 2020–2023 National Health Interview Survey data collected August–June. Coverage was estimated via Kaplan Meier analysis and self-reported vaccination between July and May in the prior 12 months.ResultsIn the analytic sample (N = 131,147), vaccination coverage ranged between 49.1 % (CI: 48.2,50.0 %) in 2021–2022 to 50.1 % (CI:49.2,51.0 %) in 2022–2023. Non-Hispanic Black people had the lowest coverage for all seasons but experienced the second largest increase in coverage (7.5 [CI:3.4,11.3 percentage points]). Vaccination coverage declined among non-Hispanic White people by 2.7 (CI:1.3,4.2 percentage points), with the greatest declines in this group among children 0–4 years (9.2 [CI:0.5,17.1 percentage points]) and insured people with financial barriers to healthcare (6.5 [CI:2.4,11.1 percentage points]).ConclusionsDespite notable gains among historically unvaccinated populations, disparities persisted and coverage among non-Hispanic Black people remained substantially below the Healthy People 2030 goal. Study findings suggest that addressing financial barriers and targeted messaging may help improve uptake.

Introduction

Influenza (flu) vaccination coverage in the US remains below the Healthy People 2030 target of 70 %.(National Center for Health Statistics, 2025a) Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) data from the National Immunization Survey-Flu and Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System reveal that less than half of eligible Americans receive seasonal flu vaccines annually.(CDC, 2024a, pp. 2023-, 2025a) This substantial gap in preventive care is complicated by persistent racial and ethnic disparities in vaccination coverages, exacerbating broader health inequities.(Black et al., 2022; Irving et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2015, Lu et al., 2021; Mahmud et al., 2021; O'Halloran et al., 2021; Sanders-Jackson et al., 2021).

During the 2009–2010 season, flu vaccine coverage was 43.7 % overall and 43.2 % and 37.0 % among non-Hispanic White and non-Hispanic Black people, respectively.(McIntyre et al., 2013) Nearly a decade later, disparities persisted, with 2017–2018 National Health Interview Survey data showing coverage of 49.5 % and 39 % among non-Hispanic White and non-Hispanic Black people, respectively.(Lu et al., 2021).

The COVID-19 pandemic fundamentally altered healthcare-seeking behaviors, public perception of vaccines, and population-specific access to preventive services and likely impacted flu vaccination patterns. (Abraham et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024; Nguyen et al., 2024; Read et al., 2023) According to Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System data, coverage among adults rose from 48.4 % in the 2019–2020 season to 50.2 % in 2020–2021, before declining to 46.9 % in 2022–2023. Interestingly, coverage among non-Hispanic Black adults declined between the 2020–2021 and 2019–2020 seasons but increased over the subsequent two seasons.(CDC, 2024a).

In this study, we build on prior findings by examining racial and ethnic disparities in flu vaccination coverage since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Specifically, we assessed whether vaccination disparities persisted after stratification for likely drivers of coverage, including age, health care worker status, underlying health conditions, and access to healthcare. By exploring these factors during this unique period of heightened vaccine awareness, our work aims to provide a nuanced understanding of how structural and individual factors contribute to vaccination inequities and inform strategies for achieving equitable flu vaccination coverage across diverse populations in a post-pandemic context.

Methods

Data source and study population

We estimated flu vaccination coverage among the US population (≥6 months) using self-report data from the 2020–2023 National Health Interview Survey, representing three flu seasons (2020–2021, 2021–2022, and 2022–2023). The National Health Interview Survey is an annual cross-sectional household survey of a representative sample of the civilian non-institutionalized US population conducted by the CDC continuously throughout the year among people residing in the 50 states and the District of Columbia.(CDC, 2025b) A detailed description of the 2020–2023 National Health Interview Survey can be found elsewhere.(National Center for Health Statistics, 2025b) The protocol was deemed exempt by the Institutional Review Board of Boston University (H-45279).

Following methods published by the CDC, we defined flu seasons as running from July through May and included only people interviewed between August and June.(Health and Human Services, 2025; Lu et al., 2021) Since National Health Interview Survey no longer provides birth month, and flu vaccination eligibility begins at 6 months, we restricted the sample to people who were at least one year old at interview to ensure full-season eligibility.(CDC, 2025a) We excluded people with missing age or race/ethnicity data. Also based on CDC methods, vaccination coverage for each season was estimated using self-reported vaccination in the prior 12 months. Missing month or year records were present for 4.8 % of people reporting a vaccine in the last 12 months and were imputed using hot-deck imputation matched on month of interview, age group, region of residency, and race/ethnicity. For people who reported receiving a vaccine dose in the prior 12 months but whose date of last vaccination was either more than a year prior or after the interview date, we retained the reported month and added or subtracted a year to force the date of vaccination within the 12 months prior to the interview.(Lu et al., 2013).

Of the 147,430 people included in the 2020–2023 National Health Interview Survey, 131,147 were interviewed between August and June, with known flu vaccination status, race and ethnicity and provided the sample for the race and age-group stratified vaccine coverage estimates. Among this population, 673 had missing financial barriers to healthcare statuses (n = 130,474). Exposure and stratification factors

Our primary exposure of interest was self-reported race and ethnicity. Due to sample size limitations, we combined the “Non-Hispanic American Indian” and “Non-Hispanic Other single and multiple races” categories.

Stratification factors included age-group (0–4, 5–17, 18–64, ≥65 years at interview), record of ≥1 high-risk condition, healthcare worker status (provided care directly to patients; Caban-Martinez et al., 2010) and reported financial barriers to healthcare access. We defined high-risk conditions based on previously published lists and expert opinion (see Supplement High-Risk Condition Definition). (Freedman, 2020; Louie et al., 2022; Trobonjača et al., 2024) We defined financial barriers to healthcare access as having no insurance or reporting ≥1 of the following in the last 12 months: delayed care due to cost, needed medical care but not received due to cost, needed medication but did not get due to cost, unable to pay medical costs. Based on data availability, the healthcare worker status and the high-risk clinical condition analyses were restricted to people aged ≥18 years.(CDC, 2025c).

Statistical analysis

While the National Health Interview Survey is cross-sectional, when the interviews were conducted relative to the beginning of the flu season creates follow-up time that differs between subjects. As a result, the CDC employes survival analysis methods to estimate flu vaccine coverage. Following CDC methodology, we estimated vaccination coverage, defined as the cumulative proportion of the population who reported receiving a flu vaccine during the season, for each of the three flu seasons, using a Kaplan Meier survival estimation approach.(Health and Human Services, 2025) First, we defined person-time at risk as the time between the first month of the flu season (July) through the month of vaccination, month of interview, or end of flu season (May), whichever came first. Next, we reformatted the data to a pooled-logistic format in which each row represented a person-month. We then estimated the probability of being vaccinated each month using a logistic regression model that included sample weights and primary sampling units that account for the complex sampling structure of the data. Using posterior simulation, we generated 1000 draws of the probability of being vaccinated each month. For each draw, we estimated the cumulative vaccination probability as the probability of being vaccinated in that month times the probability of not been vaccinated previously. The point value and 95 % confidence intervals were then defined as the mean and 2.5 % and 97.5 % percentiles. This manual estimation approach was applied due to the large computational burden of standard error estimation from the pre-scripted Kaplan Meier R function that accounts for survey weights, the svykm function from the survey package.(Lumley, 2024) To ensure our findings were similar to those from the pre-scripted function and the estimates reported by the CDC, we compared our findings to svykm generated estimates and CDC reported estimates by age-group for the analyzed seasons.(Health and Human Services, 2025).

To examine racial and ethnic disparities in vaccination coverage by flu season, we included race/ethnicity with an interaction with flu season in the logistic regression and estimated draws for each racial/ethnicity group and flu season. We examined the impact of flu risk factors and healthcare access on racial and ethnic disparities in coverage by stratifying this analysis by each factor of interest (age group, health care worker status, underlying health conditions, and access to healthcare). All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.(R Core Team, 2021).

Results

Population characteristics

Across the three analyzed flu seasons, most people were non-Hispanic White (range: 62.7 % in 2021–2022 to 66.6 % in 2020–2021) and mean age 43–46 years. Approximately 75 % reported no financial barriers to healthcare and 65 % had ≥1 reported high-risk condition. Vaccinated people were older than unvaccinated people (mean: 48–50 vs. 39–43 years). A higher proportion of vaccinated people reported ≥1 high-risk condition (68.9–71.2 %) compared to unvaccinated people (62.0–63.3 %). Seasonal flu vaccination coverage

For each season, the monthly probability of vaccination peaked in October. The peak was highest for the 2020–2021 season (24.1 % [95 % Confidence Interval (CI): 23.2,25.0 %]) followed by the 2022–2023 season (21.6 % [CI:20.9,22.4 %]). However, the probability of vaccination in December was lower for the 2020–2021 (4.9 % [CI:4.4,5.4 %]) than the 2021–2022 (6.4 % [CI:6.0,7.0 %]) or 2022–2023 seasons (6.2 % [CI:5.7,6.8 %]; Figure1.A). Cumulatively, the coverage was within 2 percentage points for the three seasons (2020–2021: 49.7 % [CI:48.8,50.6 %]; 2021–2022: 49.0 % [CI:48.2,50.0 %]; 2022–2023: 50.1 % [CI:49.2,51.0 %]; Fig. 2).Fig. 2Monthly and Cumulative Influenza Vaccination Coverage Estimates Among U.S. Residents, National Health Interview Survey 2020–2021 Through 2022–2023 Seasons.Legend: Seasonal vaccination was estimated using a weighted Kaplan Meier analysis among people who were interviewed between August through June and reported receiving a flu vaccine dose between July through May in the prior 12 months.Fig. 2

Racial/ethnic differences in flu vaccination coverage

Vaccination coverage was below the Healthy People 2030 goal of 70 % for each racial and ethnic category. Non-Hispanic Asian people had the highest coverage (2020–2021: 60.5 % [CI:57.4,63.7 %]; 2021–2022: 60.5 % [CI:57.1,64.4 %]; 2022–2023: 62.4 % [CI:59.1,65.7 %]; Fig. 2). Non-Hispanic Black people had the lowest coverage (2020–2021: 37.4 % [CI:34.8,40.3 %]; 2021–2022: 38.2 % [CI:35.8,40.9 %]; 2022–2023 44.9 % [CI:42.1,47.9 %]). However, non-Hispanic Black people experience a large increase in coverage over the three seasons (7.46 [CI:3.38,11.30 percentage points]) and by the 2022–2023 season, coverage within this group was within 1 percentage point of the coverage among Hispanic people (45.4 % [CI:43.3,47.6 %]). Age stratified racial/ethnic differences in flu vaccination coverage

Vaccination coverage was highest for non-Hispanic Asian people for all age-groups and flu seasons (Fig. 4). Non-Hispanic Black people had the lowest vaccination coverage for all age-groups for the 2020–2021 seasons. However, for people aged 5–17 years, increases in coverage among non-Hispanic Black people led to their coverage for the 2022–2023 season (49.0 % [CI:43.0,56.0 %]) being higher than that of Hispanic people (48.6 % [CI:43.5,56.0 %]), non-Hispanic White (46.2 % [CI:43.6,49.0 %]), and people include in the Other race category (47.2 % [CI:39.9,55.7 %]). Declines in coverage were observed among non-Hispanic White people of all age-groups with the largest declines observed among children 0–4 years (decline of 9.2 [CI:0.5,17.1] percentage points). Vaccination coverage was higher for healthcare workers than non-healthcare workers (Figure4.B; Supplement Table3). Coverage among non-Hispanic Black non-healthcare workers increased between the 2020–2021 and 2022–2023 seasons by 5.7 (CI:0.5,10.5) percentage points. However, no change was observed among non-Hispanic Black healthcare workers. As of the 2021–2022 and 2022–2023 flu seasons, non-Hispanic Black healthcare workers were the only group of healthcare workers whose vaccination coverage was below that of the Healthy People 2030 Goal of 70 % (Fig. 5B; Supplement Table3).

People who were uninsured and reported ≥1 financial barrier to care had lower vaccination coverage relative to people who were insured (Figure4.C; Supplement Table3). In 2022–2023, coverage among people who were uninsured was lowest among non-Hispanic White people, regardless of if they reported financial barriers (No Barriers: 17.8 % [CI:13.1,23.4 %], ≥1 Barriers: 17.1 % [CI:11.7,25.4 %]), as well as insured people with ≥1 barrier (45.9 % [CI:43.2,48.7 %]). Although non-Hispanic White people who were insured and reported no barriers to care had higher coverage than Hispanic or non-Hispanic Black people in 2022–2023, they were the only group in this category to experience declines over time. Coverage declined among non-Hispanic Asian people who reported ≥1 financial barrier, regardless of insurance status (Fig. 5C; Supplement Table3).

Discussion

Using nationally-representative data, we examined trends and disparities in flu vaccination coverage by race/ethnicity and potential factors associated with healthcare access and flu risk. Our study focused on three recent flu seasons—2020–2021, 2021–2022, and 2022–2023—capturing a critical period shaped by the COVID-19 pandemic and its impacts on healthcare utilization and vaccine behaviors.

Compared with CDC estimates from the same data and seasons, our estimates are 0.3 percentage points lower on average (Supplement Figure1). The largest discrepancy is for the 2021–2022 flu season 0–4-year-old estimate, which is 1.8 percentage points higher than the CDC estimate. Our estimate likely slightly overstates coverage because, lacking birth month in the National Health Interview Survey, we restricted the sample to children aged ≥12 months at interview to ensure flu season eligibility (≥6 months old), thereby excluding those who became eligible later in the season. Our confidence intervals are of similar size with a mean difference of 0.2 percentage points.

Our analysis revealed persistent racial and ethnic disparities in flu vaccination coverage, with coverage consistently highest among non-Hispanic Asian people and lowest among non-Hispanic Black people. These persistent disparities align with prior estimates from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System, National Immunization Survey-Flu, and Vaccine Safety Datalink.(Black et al., 2022; CDC, 2025d; 2024b; Irving et al., 2023) However, while we observed increases in coverage across all groups except non-Hispanic White, estimates from these sources suggest declining coverage for most (Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System & National Immunization Survey-Flu data suggest an increase in non-Hispanic Black coverage between 2021 and 2022 and 2022–2023 seasons) if not all racial groups since the 2019–2020 season.(CDC, 2025d; Irving et al., 2023) Such differences have been previously noted by the CDC, primarily attributed to variations in sampling methods.(CDC, 2024c).

Across seasons, non-Hispanic Asian and non-Hispanic White individuals who were insured and did not report financial barriers had consistently higher coverage than non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic individuals—patterns consistent with Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System and Vaccine Safety Datalink data for insured populations.(Black et al., 2022; Irving et al., 2023) Also consistent with Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System data, uninsured non-Hispanic White individuals—regardless of whether they reported financial barriers—had lower coverage than Hispanic individuals and coverage similar to (<1 percentage point) or below that of non-Hispanic Black individuals.(Black et al., 2022).

However, our stratified analysis revealed patterns for healthcare workers and individuals with high-risk conditions inconsistent with prior estimates. While, Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System 2021–2022 data suggested non-Hispanic Asian people had the highest coverage for both subpopulations, followed by non-Hispanic White people, we found that among healthcare workers, coverage was highest among those in the Other race category, followed by non-Hispanic Asian individuals. (Black et al., 2022) Among people with high-risk conditions, we observed higher coverage for the Other race category than for non-Hispanic White people. These discrepancies likely reflect differences in sample design and analytic approach. For instance, the high-risk condition analysis from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System data was limited to people 18–65 years and the healthcare worker analysis included anyone who worked in healthcare, whereas our high-risk condition analysis included anyone 18 or older and our healthcare worker analysis was limited to patient-facing healthcare workers.(Black et al., 2022) Together, these findings underscore how structural barriers and methodological choices shape observed disparities and subgroup analyses can uncover variation potentially obscured in aggregate estimates.

Despite encouraging gains in coverage for historically under vaccinated populations, disparities remain significant when considering national immunization targets. By the 2022–2023 season, flu vaccination coverage among people who identified as non-Hispanic Asian and non-Hispanic Black remained below the Healthy People 2030 goal of 70 %. However, notable progress towards this goal was achieved in some age and racial/ethnic subgroups. For instance, by 2022–2023, coverage exceeded the goal among people aged 0–4 and 5–17 years who identified as non-Hispanic Asian. Further, vaccination coverage surpassed the target for all individuals aged ≥65 years, regardless of race and ethnicity, though the uncertainty intervals remained below the goal for people who identified as Hispanic, non-Hispanic Black, or whose racial/ethnicity was categorized as Other.

Limitations

Our study was limited to data collected on or after 2020 due to substantial changes to the National Health Interview Survey questionnaire and weighting design in 2019,(CDC, 2024d) as well as the absence of survey month reporting for that year.(“IPUMS NATIONAL HEALTH INTERVIEW SURVEY,” 2025) Due to data captured within the National Health Interview Survey, analyses of high-risk condition and worker categories excluded those under 18 years. Additionally, missing data was present ∼5 % and imputation was required. National Health Interview Survey provides age in years which prevented us from properly defining person-time at risk of vaccination within people 0–4 years. Our subsequent exclusion of people aged zero at the time of interview may explain the difference we observed in our 2021–2022 coverage estimates for the 0–4-year-old group compared to the CDC estimates. Finally, many of the stratified analyses had limited precision, including large point estimate declines observed among people in the Other race category, were uninsured but experience no financial barriers to healthcare and people who identified as non-Hispanic Black, uninsured and reported ≥1 financial barrier to healthcare. However, our study has several strengths, including the inclusion of nationally representative data and CDC-aligned methods to generate contemporary, post-pandemic estimates of disparities across key demographic and healthcare access factors.

Conclusion

We observed notable improvements in flu vaccination coverage among historically under-vaccinated racial and ethnic groups, namely people who identified as non-Hispanic Black. However, significant disparities persisted and declines in coverage were observed among people who identified as non-Hispanic White, with varying magnitudes of coverage decline by flu risk factors and financial barriers to healthcare. We identified declines in seasonal flu vaccination coverage among those who were uninsured and/or experienced financial barriers to healthcare. To reach immunization Healthy People 2030 targets, public health strategies that reduce financial and structural barriers and address declines in vaccine confidence are needed.
