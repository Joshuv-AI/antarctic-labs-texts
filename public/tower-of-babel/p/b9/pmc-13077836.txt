The association between resilience and athletic performance: findings from a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies
Authors: Zhengyang Mei, Chenyi Cai, Chifong Lam, Tingfeng Wang, Haichang Jiang, Shi Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to quantitatively synthesize existing evidence regarding the overall correlation between resilience and athletic performance and explore potential moderating variables.MethodsObservational studies of the association between resilience and athletic performance were identified using electronic databases and manual searches. The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies, developed by the National Institutes of Health. Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were used as effect sizes for the pooled results. This systematic review and meta-analysis was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (registration number: CRD42024590706).ResultsFifteen studies met the inclusion criteria, encompassing 15 independent samples, 27 effect sizes, and 4654 participants. The main effect test indicated a significant positive correlation between resilience and athletic performance (r = 0.437, z = 6.862, 95% CI = 0.322 to 0.538). The moderating effect test indicated that the resilience measurement used significantly moderated the association between resilience and athletic performance (QB = 54.716, df = 1, p < 0.001), whereas no significant moderating effects were observed for the remaining variables.ConclusionsThe findings of the review provide evidence-based support for the positive development and appropriate guidance of resilience in athletes, thereby effectively enhancing their physical and mental health and athletic performance.Keywords: Resilience, Athletes, Sport performance, Meta-analysis

Introduction

In the field of sports, athletes are subjected to persistent biological, psychological, and social stressors from demanding long-term training and intense competition. The cumulative impact of these stressors significantly elevates their susceptibility to mental health problems [1], and mental health is increasingly recognized as a major resource for athletes in relation to their performance and development [2]. Evidence suggests that mental health symptoms and disorders are prevalent among elite athletes, with prevalence rates ranging from 19% to 34% among current elite athletes and from 16% to 26% among former elite athletes [3]. In this context, resilience, as a key psychological factor, plays a significant role in helping athletes effectively adapt to negative stimuli by successfully coping with and overcoming adversity and stress in competitive sports, which is crucial for achieving optimal performance [4–7]. Multiple studies have shown that resilience is closely associated with a series of psychological states and behavioral patterns in athletes, including perceived stress [8–10], competition anxiety [11, 12], athlete burnout [13], and athletic performance [14, 15].

Resilience refers to an athlete’s ability to evaluate their own thoughts, feelings and behaviors during sports-related adversity, and has the effects of stimulating potential, invigorating emotions, and improving health [16]. During the process of experiencing stressors, resilience can serve as the ability to return to an adaptive and healthy behavioral pattern following stressful events [17]. Important to note is that the theoretical construction and conceptual definition of resilience exhibit some ambiguity, often leading to confusion with the term “mental toughness,” although the two are fundamentally distinct [18, 19]. Specifically, while both resilience and mental toughness are associated with successfully coping with and overcoming stressful situations, mental toughness is typically applied in positive contexts whereas resilience is more applicable in negative or adverse contexts [20]. In terms of measurement, several self-report scales are commonly employed to assess resilience, including the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale-25 (CD-RISC-25) [21], the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale-10 (CD-RISC-10) [22], the Resilience Scale (RS) [23], the Brief Resilience Scale (BRS) [24], the Resilience Scale for Adults (RSA) [25], and so on. Among them, the CD-RISC-25 and CD-RISC-10 are widely used in sports [26]. The CD-RISC-25, developed by Connor and Davidson, comprises 25 items with a five-factor structure: personal competence, stress reaction, positive adaptation, perceived control, and spiritual influence [21]. The CD-RISC-10 is a simplified version developed by Campbell-Sills and Stein based on the CD-RISC-25 by extracting 10 items [22]. By eliminating the redundant and unstable items from the CD-RISC-25, the CD-RISC-10 demonstrates a more concise item content and a more stable structural dimension [22], suggesting that the two may exhibit varying degrees of association with athletic performance. Therefore, systematic evaluation of these scales and their measurement standards is essential for a more comprehensive and accurate understanding of the relationship between resilience and athletic performance.

Athletic performance refers to the behaviors and related results exhibited by athletes under the supervision of sports organizations in accordance with established written rules. As a result of specific sports activities, athletic performance is related to specific measurement standards [27]. Specifically, the measurement standards for athletic performance can be differentiated on the basis of objective and subjective levels. Each type of measurement has its unique strengths and limitations, and understanding these differences is essential for a comprehensive assessment of athletic performance [28]. On the one hand, objective measurements (including winning or losing, completion time, etc.) provide quantifiable data that can be directly compared, effectively eliminating the influence of evaluators’ subjective biases and thus having a high degree of reliability and consistency. However, owing to the varying characteristics, rules, and environments of different sports, establishing a unified standard for cross-sports comparisons of objective measurements is challenging. On the other hand, subjective measures (including scales and external evaluation) can better capture the psychological states and emotional changes of athletes and have good consistency in cross-sport comparisons. However, these measures are more susceptible to evaluators’ subjective biases. Therefore, the assessment of athletic performance should consider both objective and subjective measurements to better understand the actual situation of athletes during training and competition, and their developmental potential, enabling a comprehensive and precise analysis of athletic performance.

At present, evidence regarding the intrinsic connection between resilience and athletic performance remains inconsistent. For instance, a study based on latent profile analysis examined the relationship between resilience and athletic performance, and showed a significant positive correlation between resilience levels and athletes’ subjective performance in competition [29]. However, this finding is not supported by other studies [12, 30]. Furthermore, effect sizes between resilience and athletic performance also vary considerably in existing research, with Pearson correlation coefficients (r) reported ranging from 0.1 to 0.7. This suggests the available evidence is highly heterogeneous and is potentially influenced by certain characteristic factors. In light of this, the questions of whether resilience is associated with athletic performance, the degree of its association, and the potential moderating variables involved have become pressing issues that need to be addressed. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to quantitatively synthesize existing evidence regarding the overall correlation between resilience and athletic performance and explore potential moderating variables.

Methods

Search strategy

A literature search was conducted across six electronic databases—PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, EBSCOhost, and APA PsycINFO—from their respective inceptions until August 2024. Furthermore, the Google Scholar search engine served as an additional way to retrieve potential evidence. The following search terms were included in Boolean search strategies: (“Resilien*”) AND (“Perform*” OR “Competiti*” OR “Outcome*” OR “Recovery” OR “Fatigue”) AND (“Athlet*” OR “Elite*” OR “Sport*”).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included in the present systematic review and meta-analysis if they met the following criteria: (a) the study population comprised athletes; (b) the study design was observational; (c) the data utilized in the study were original research data; (d) the measurement tools used in the study were clearly described; (e) the study results reported Pearson correlation coefficients (r) between total resilience and performance scores or subscale scores, or values that could be converted to r, including F, t, χ2, or β values from univariate linear regression; and (f) in case of duplicate publication of data, only the one with the more comprehensive reporting of content was included.

Studies were excluded in the present systematic review and meta-analysis if they met the following criteria: (a) the study population did not involve athletes; (b) the study design was a literature review, case study, or qualitative research; (c) the data utilized in the study were not original; (d) the measurement tools used in the study were either unvalidated or lacked appropriate descriptions; (e) the relationship between resilience and performance was not reported in the study results; and (f) the language used in the study was not English.

Study selection, data extraction, and quality appraisal

According to the established inclusion and exclusion criteria, two independent researchers used EndNote 20.6 for study selection. A data form was used to extract the sociodemographic characteristics, measures of resilience and athletic performance, and study design of the included studies. The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies developed by the National Institutes of Health, in which a score of 1 indicated compliance and 0 indicated non-compliance with the criteria. The total score ranged from 0 to 8 for cross-sectional studies and from 0 to 14 for longitudinal studies, with higher scores indicating better study quality. In each of the aforementioned processes, any controversies were discussed and addressed by consulting the third author.

Statistical analysis

Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were used as effect sizes for the pooled results. The main and moderating effects were tested using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis (CMA) Version 3.3 software. To ensure the independence of effect sizes in estimating the main effect, multiple effect sizes reported in a single study were averaged and combined before the main effect estimation. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the Chi-square test based on the Q-test and I2 statistics [31]. According to the criteria established by Higgins, I2 of 25%, 50%, and 75% were used as thresholds for low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [31]. In terms of the method for calculating effects sizes, when the p-value of the Q-test was less than 0.05 or I2 exceeded 75%, a random-effect model was used to merge effect sizes. Otherwise, a fixed-effect model was used to merge effect sizes [32]. A moderating effect test was conducted to identify the sources of heterogeneity. For continuous moderating variables, their moderating effects were examined using meta-regression with maximum likelihood estimation (MLE). For categorical moderating variables, their moderating effects were examined using a subgroup analysis. To ensure the representativeness of the moderating effect test in the subgroup analysis, the number of effect sizes at each level should be no less than 3. Sensitivity analysis was conducted using a stepwise elimination method to assess the robustness of the pooled results [33]. Publication bias was assessed by observing funnel plots and calculating Egger’s regression intercepts, with p-value < 0.05 as a threshold for statistical significance [34, 35]. All statistical analyses were performed using CMA 3.3 software.

Results

Literature search and eligible studies

The initial electronic database and additional sources searches identified 5,351 records, of which 2,114 were duplicates. Following the title and abstract screening of 3,237 records, 335 records were retrieved and assessed for eligibility, resulting in 15 eligible studies [12, 36–49]. Study characteristics and quality appraisal

The final analytical sample included 15 studies, encompassing 15 independent samples, 27 effect sizes, and 4654 participants. Evidence regarding the relationship between resilience and athletic performance was widely available in various countries. Three studies were conducted in the UK, three in Spain, and two in China. One study was conducted in each of Portugal, France, Norway, Australia, Iran, India, and South Korea. The study populations comprised trail runners, show jumping riders, baseball players, volleyball players, soccer players, elite swimmers, Taekwondo sparring athletes, elite table tennis players, and athletes from other sports. The most utilized resilience instrument across the studies was the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC, n = 6) [37, 38, 40, 43, 47, 48]. Athletic performance was measured using different outcomes including subjective performance, race speed, competitive performance, individual and team performance, relative performance index, sports competence and endurance, cross sports athletic rating, and championship performance. Among the 15 studies included, 12 employed a cross-sectional design [12, 36–39, 42, 44–49], while 3 employed a longitudinal design [40, 41, 43]. While one study employed a longitudinal design, the statistical analysis used to assess the relationship between resilience and athletic performance was cross-sectional [41]. Therefore, the effect size reported in this study was considered cross-sectional (see Table 1). In terms of quality assessment, the scores of the included cross-sectional studies ranged from 4 to 6, with a mean of 5.08, which exceeded the theoretical mean of 4. The scores for the included longitudinal studies were uniformly 10, with a mean of 10.00, exceeding the theoretical mean of 7. Overall, the included studies were of good quality (see Table 2).

Heterogeneity test

A heterogeneity test was conducted on 15 effect sizes to determine the appropriate method for calculating effects sizes and whether to perform a moderating effect test. The results indicated considerable heterogeneity among the pooled results (I2 = 94.85%, Q = 272.08, p < 0.01), suggesting that 94.85% of the variation in effect sizes related to resilience and athletic performance was attributable to true differences in effect sizes across studies. Consequently, a random-effect model was used to merge effect sizes. Additionally, differences in estimates across studies may be influenced by certain characteristic factors; thus, a moderating effect test was performed to identify the sources of heterogeneity.

Main effect test

The random-effect model was used to estimate 15 effect sizes, and the results indicated a significant positive correlation between resilience and athletic performance (r = 0.437, z = 6.862, 95% CI = 0.322 to 0.538). According to the latest criteria proposed by Gignac and Szodorai, the r-value between resilience and athletic performance was greater than 0.3, indicating a strong correlation between the two [50]. A forest plot for the association between resilience and athletic performance is presented in Fig. 2.

Forest plot for the association between resilience and athletic performance

Moderating effect test

The moderating effects of continuous and categorical variables were tested, including year of publication, mean age, total sample size, percentage of males, resilience measurement, performance measurement, and study design. Meta-regression analysis indicated that the covariates included in the regression model did not demonstrate a statistically significant association with heterogeneity between studies. A subgroup analysis indicated that the resilience measurement used significantly moderated the association between resilience and athletic performance (QB = 54.716, df = 1, p < 0.001). Furthermore, the association between the CD-RISC-25 and athletic performance (r = 0.665, p < 0.001) was stronger than that between the CD-RISC-10 and athletic performance (r = 0.201, p < 0.001). However, performance measurement and study design did not moderate the association between resilience and athletic performance (QB = 0.494, df = 1, p = 0.482; QB = 0.051, df = 1, p = 0.821). Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to quantitatively synthesize existing evidence regarding the overall correlation between resilience and athletic performance and explore potential moderating variables. Fifteen studies met the inclusion criteria, encompassing 15 independent samples, 27 effect sizes, and 4654 participants. In terms of quality assessment, the average scores of the included cross-sectional and longitudinal studies both exceeded the theoretical value, suggesting that the overall quality of the included studies was good. The main effect test indicated a significant positive correlation between resilience and athletic performance (r = 0.437, z = 6.862, 95% CI = 0.322 to 0.538). The moderating effect test indicated that the resilience measurement used significantly moderated the association between resilience and athletic performance (QB = 54.716, df = 1, p < 0.001). Furthermore, the association between the CD-RISC-25 and athletic performance (r = 0.665, p < 0.001) was stronger than that between the CD-RISC-10 and athletic performance (r = 0.201, p < 0.001), whereas no significant moderating effects were observed for the remaining variables.

The association between resilience and athletic performance

Existing evidence was quantitatively synthesized to evaluate the overall correlation between resilience and athletic performance, indicating a significant positive correlation between resilience and athletic performance. This evidence supports the findings of most previous studies suggesting that athletes with high resilience levels are more likely to achieve outstanding performance in stressful situations [29, 51, 52]. Based on existing theories and empirical research, the relationship between resilience and athletic performance can be explained from various perspectives, including basic psychological needs, sports motivation, and coping strategies. According to self-determination theory [53], athletes with high resilience levels are usually better able to satisfy basic psychological needs, specifically, autonomy, competence, and belonging. Their motivation for training and competition primarily stems from love for the sport and intrinsic satisfaction, focusing on the enjoyment of the process rather than solely on outcomes. This positive sports motivation enables them to maintain an optimistic mindset when faced with stressful situations, as well as to better manage their emotions and behaviors, thus buffering the negative effects of stressful situations on athletic performance [54]. Furthermore, athletes with high resilience levels tend to adopt more adaptive coping strategies under pressure, such as task-oriented approaches, and to cope positively by mobilizing self-regulatory resources towards specific situations (e.g., sports-related adversity) [8]. In contrast, athletes with low resilience levels may resort to non-adaptive coping strategies such as giving up, venting negative emotions, mental distraction, and disengagement. These strategies will diminish their capacity to cope effectively with situational demands, making athletic performance more susceptible to stressful situations [8]. Collectively, these findings underscore that resilience can equip athletes with the motivational drive and regulatory capacity to thrive under pressure. The observed positive association suggests that resilience plays a meaningful role in differentiating performance outcomes, with effect sizes indicating a practically significant contribution to athletic performance. These insights highlight the potential value of integrating resilience screening and development programs into athlete support systems, as fostering resilience may be as critical to performance as physical or technical training.

However, it is crucial to acknowledge that the relationship between resilience and athletic performance is not unequivocally supported across all studies. Some evidence does not support a significant relationship between the two [12, 30]. These inconsistencies in the literature warrant a balanced interpretation and can be attributed to several methodological and contextual factors. On the one hand, cross-sectional designs, which capture a momentary snapshot, may be less capable of detecting a consistent effect than longitudinal designs that track the interplay of resilience and athletic performance over time. On the other hand, the nature of the sport, competitive level, and athletes’ experience may affect the resilience-performance relationship. Future research should therefore strive to explore these factors to delineate more precisely the specific conditions under which resilience is most beneficial for athletic performance. Addressing these nuances would not only help reconcile disparate findings but also deepen our understanding of the resilience-performance dynamic.

Recently, several interventional studies have examined the relationship between resilience and athletic performance. A quasi-experimental study of NCAA athletes demonstrated that, after implementing interventions aimed at enhancing resilience, the experimental group showed significant improvements in subjective performance and reductions in levels of depression, anxiety, and perceived stress compared with the control group [55]. Similarly, a study based on a pre- and post-test design indicated that college athletes’ stress perception levels and decision-making performance in the face of stressful events significantly improved after the completion of a resilience training program [9]. This research suggests that by implementing systematic and targeted resilience training during training and competitions, athletes can manage stress and cope with challenges more effectively, ultimately benefiting from these experiences. Drawing from theories of stress inoculation [56], it has been suggested that exposure to adversity in moderation can help individuals successfully cope with and overcome future stress situations. This implies that resilience training can help athletes simulate and adapt to competitive stress, gradually building confidence, and enabling them to exhibit greater stability and performance levels when faced with similar challenges. While this theory holds great promise, the observational nature of the included studies precludes causal conclusions. Future research employing rigorous experimental designs is essential to determine the causal effect of resilience interventions on athletic performance.

Moderating effect analysis

Based on the available evidence, there is significant heterogeneity in effect sizes between resilience and athletic performance, suggesting that the pooled results of this study may be potentially influenced by certain characteristic factors. The moderating effect test indicated that the resilience measurement used significantly moderated the association between resilience and athletic performance. Furthermore, the association between the CD-RISC-25 and athletic performance was stronger than that between the CD-RISC-10 and athletic performance.

The reasons for this difference can be analyzed based on the scale structure, item content, and the sensitivity and precision of the measurements. As previously mentioned, the CD-RISC-25 comprises 25 items and a five-factor structure: personal competence, stress reaction, positive adaptation, perceived control, and spiritual influence. This comprehensive structure allows measurement of multiple facets of resilience, thereby providing a more careful assessment [21]. Furthermore, as the CD-RISC-10 is a simplified version, a reduction in both the number and content of items may lead to the omission or inadequacy of certain key dimensions; thus, it may not reflect the full picture of resilience. Evidence suggests that compared with the CD-RISC-25, while the CD-RISC-10 provides a better model fit and measurement convenience, it still has major limitations. More specifically, this scale conceptualizes resilience as a single-factor structure and does not cover items related to experiences of adversity and positive adaptations in athletes, which means that the CD-RISC-10 cannot effectively assess resilience, resulting in a reduced association with athletic performance [57, 58]. Notably, in addition to the CD-RISC series of scales, other scales such as the SRS, CREST, and ACSI—developed directly within the context of competitive sports—may offer superior ecological validity and domain-specific advantages. Although the application of these instruments in this field remains relatively limited, future research should validate them across different sports and cultural contexts to evaluate their predictive utility for athletic performance and their potential for generalization.

No significant moderating effects were observed for the remaining moderating variables, including year of publication, mean age, total sample size, percentage of males, performance measurement, and study design. In terms of publication year, there were no significant moderating effects, indicating that the relationship between resilience and athletic performance remained stable across different research periods. By conducting subgroup analyses to compare effect sizes from the past five years with those from earlier periods, the results showed that the r-value for the two periods ranged from 0.41 to 0.45, reflecting the intrinsic stability of the relationship between resilience and athletic performance, and thus the impact of year of publication on the relationship was limited.

In terms of participant characteristics, mean age, total sample size, and percentage of males did not exhibit significant moderating effects on the relationship between resilience and athletic performance, indicating a convergence effect across age, sample size, and sex. According to the Grounded Theory of resilience [4], resilience is conceived as a product of specific contexts, such as stressful situations, and its relationship with athletic performance primarily depends on stressors, cognitive evaluation, meta-cognition, and personality; it involves five protective factors, namely, positive personality, motivation, confidence, focus, and perceived social support. These core factors are universal and consistent across different population backgrounds; thus, the relationship between resilience and athletic performance is less susceptible to the influence of participant characteristics. Notably, the majority of participants in the included studies were student-athletes, and the limited number of studies focusing exclusively on professional athletes precluded a direct comparison between these two populations. Student-athletes are a frequently studied demographic due to their accessibility, yet their psychological profiles and stressors may differ significantly from those of career professionals. Future research with more diverse samples is warranted to examine whether the resilience-performance relationship varies across different career stages.

In terms of performance measurement, differences in the measurement standards used across various studies complicate a more detailed classification. Although performance measurement (both subjective and objective) did not significantly moderate the relationship between resilience and athletic performance in this study, this does not imply that there is a complete convergence effect across the measurement standards; at least, such an effect is not fully uniform. Subgroup analyses based on performance measurement indicated some variability in the relationship between resilience and athletic performance when using subjective and objective measurements, with the r-value ranged from 0.37 to 0.47. In light of this, future studies should further subdivide performance measurement to analyze and discuss the potential moderating effects of different measurement standards. This approach would enable a more comprehensive and dynamic interpretation of the intrinsic connection between resilience and athletic performance.

In terms of study design, there was no significant moderating effects of data type, which may be attributed to the fact that 13 effect sizes of cross-sectional data were included in this study, whereas there were 2 effects sizes from longitudinal data. Furthermore, the uneven distribution of effect sizes across different data types may have affected the detection of the moderating effects. This suggests that more longitudinal studies should be conducted to explore the long-term effects of resilience on athletic performance, thereby revealing the dynamic developmental relationship between the two and validating the reliability of the findings of this study.

Limitations and prospects

The findings of the current systematic review and meta-analysis must be interpreted in the context of limitations. First, because of the diverse tools and standards currently employed to measure athletic performance, meeting the grouping criteria for moderating variables is challenging. Thus, the impact of performance measurement on the relationship between resilience and athletic performance warrants further investigation. Future research should focus on standardizing the performance measurement tools to capture the relationship between the two more accurately. Second, substantial differences in the number of effect sizes within subgroup analyses (such as in the study design) may have influenced the results of the moderating effect test. Future research should be built on a more comprehensive data foundation to validate the reliability of this study’s findings. Finally, the pooled results of this study only indicate a linear relationship between resilience and athletic performance, and the causal relationship remains unclear. Therefore, there is a pressing need for longitudinal tracking studies to investigate the dynamic developmental relationship between the two.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to quantitatively synthesize existing evidence regarding the overall correlation between resilience and athletic performance and explore potential moderating variables. A significant positive correlation was found between resilience and athletic performance, and resilience measurement demonstrated a moderating effect on this relationship. Fostering athletes’ resilience is an effective strategy for enhancing their physical and mental health and athletic performance. Coaches and management staff are encouraged to pay sufficient attention to the development of resilience when designing training and competition programs. Furthermore, updated concepts and measurement standards of resilience should be explored to examine the association between resilience and athletic performance, thereby elucidating the impact of resilience on athletes’ psychological states and behavioral patterns.
