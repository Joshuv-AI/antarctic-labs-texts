Maximizing public health benefits: Policy perspectives on coverage and cell-based influenza vaccines in the US
Authors: Van Hung Nguyen, Joaquin F Mould-Quevedo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCell-based influenza vaccines can increase vaccine effectiveness by avoiding egg adaptations associated with traditional egg-based manufacturing techniques. This study evaluated the impacts of switching from use of an egg-based trivalent influenza vaccine (TIVe) to a cell-based vaccine (TIVc) for vaccination of individuals <65 y of age in the US. We used a dynamic age-stratified susceptible-exposed-infectious-recovered model combined with an age-structured decision tree model to compare the health and economic impacts of using TIVc in individuals <65 y combined with adjuvanted trivalent influenza vaccine (aTIV) in older adults (≥65 y) with two baseline scenarios: 1. vaccination of all individuals with TIVe; 2. vaccination of individuals <65 y with TIVe and ≥65 y with aTIV. Use of TIVc+aTIV resulted in substantial reductions in influenza burden across all vaccine coverage levels evaluated, with 8.5 million cases, 127,000 hospitalizations, and 19,000 deaths averted compared with TIVe at 65% coverage. The combination of TIVc and aTIV was cost-saving compared with either baseline scenario, irrespective of vaccine coverage level. Analysis by age group showed that TIVc+aTIV was either cost-effective or cost-saving across all pediatric and adult age groups, with greatest direct cost benefits seen in adults 50–64 y. Overall, our findings support the preferential use of TIVc over TIVe in individuals <65 y in vaccination programs, along with inclusion in programs together with an enhanced vaccine in adults ≥65 y.KEYWORDS: Cost-effectiveness, ICER, cell-based influenza vaccine, egg-based influenza vaccine, vaccine policy, TIVc, aTIV

Introduction

Vaccination continues to play a critical role in reducing the burden of seasonal influenza and maintaining the resilience of healthcare systems. In the US, seasonal influenza vaccination is currently recommended for all individuals 6 months of age and older, with adjuvanted, high-dose, or recombinant trivalent influenza vaccine (TIV) preferentially recommended for individuals ≥65 y of age.1 Both egg-based and cell-based TIVs are recommended for individuals <65 y of age. Evolving public health priorities have highlighted the need for assessment of vaccine strategies to include impacts on healthcare resource use and economic feasibilities.

Despite vaccination recommendations, seasonal influenza continues to present a substantial disease and economic burden in the US. In the 2024–2025 season, influenza caused an estimated 47 million cases, including 610,000 hospitalizations, and 27,000 deaths, predominantly in older adults and those with underlying chronic diseases such as hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and obesity.2 From an economic viewpoint, the annual economic burden of seasonal influenza was estimated at $11.2 billion ($6.3 to $25.3 billion) in 2015,3 with earlier estimates projecting a total economic burden of influenza epidemics including loss of future productivity of up to $87.1 billion annually.4 Given the number of different influenza vaccines on the market and their differing effectiveness across age cohorts, evaluation of the potential for cost-savings through vaccine choice is warranted, to maximize the potential societal-level savings through reduced healthcare utilization and absenteeism.

Traditionally, viruses for inclusion in seasonal influenza vaccines have been propagated in chicken eggs. However, the replication of viruses in eggs can result in acquisition of egg-adaptation mutations, which reduces the potential match with circulating influenza viruses, making the resulting vaccine potentially less effective.5–7 Cell-based influenza vaccines, first approved in the US in 2012 (for adults ≥18 y), overcome this potential limitation, but manufacturing costs remain higher than for egg-based vaccines, impacting cost-effectiveness.8 In analyses performed prior to the apparent extinction of the B/Yamagata lineage during the COVID-19 pandemic,9 we demonstrated the incremental benefits of use of a cell-based quadrivalent influenza vaccine (QIVc) over traditional egg-based QIV (QIVe) in children and adults (18–64 y), with robust cost-savings across varying vaccine effectiveness scenarios.10,11 Similarly, in an updated version of the model which did not include strain-specific analysis, we observed reductions in acute and intensive care unit (ICU) hospital bed utilization in a seasonal influenza epidemic, against a background of circulating COVID-19.12 In the current analysis, we use the same model to compare the population-level health and economic impacts of the use of cell-based versus egg-based influenza vaccines in children and adults <65 y living in the US, evaluating both the effect of vaccine coverage levels and the switch to a more effective product. We also included scenarios assessing the use of enhanced vaccines or egg-based vaccines in adults ≥65 y, as some individuals in this group may still receive standard egg-based vaccines despite ACIP’s preferential recommendation for enhanced products. Together, these analyses provide evidence-based insights to inform vaccination policy decisions aimed at maximizing both clinical outcomes and economic efficiency.

Methods

Model design

The analysis described in this manuscript extends a previously published analysis modeling the impact of influenza vaccination on acute and ICU bed hospital bed occupancy against a background of co-circulating COVID-19.12 In brief, we used a dynamic age-stratified susceptible-exposed-infectious-recovered (SEIR) model of seasonal influenza transmission in the United States,10,12 updated to allow incremental analysis by vaccine coverage rate. The model was calibrated using historical CDC data for a high incidence (2017–2018) influenza season13 and incorporated age-specific contact matrices, hospitalization probabilities, ICU needs, and bed capacity constraints.14–19 A sensitivity analysis was performed evaluating impacts in a low incidence season (2011–2012).13 Vaccination was assumed to reduce the susceptible (S) population, thereby indirectly affecting transmission dynamics. Vaccine coverage was assumed to vary by age group (see [12] Table S3), with an overall vaccine coverage of varying between 20% and 65% across ages, based on observed rates reported by the CDC (Supplementary Table S1). Scenarios evaluated

The scenario of use of standard-dose cell-based TIV (TIVc) for individuals aged 6 months to 64 y and adjuvanted TIV (aTIV) for adults ≥65 y (TIVc+aTIV) was compared with two baseline scenarios: 1. standard-dose egg-based TIV (TIVe) for all individuals aged ≥6 months of age, and 2. standard-dose TIVe for individuals aged 6 months to 64 y, and aTIV for adults ≥65 y (TIVe+aTIV). While enhanced vaccines (aTIV or high-dose [HD] TIV) are preferentially recommended for adults ≥65 y in the US,20 we included a baseline scenario where this population received TIVe as a comparator, as adults ≥65 y may still receive a standard-dose unadjuvanted vaccine.21

Vaccine effectiveness

Vaccine effectiveness was estimated per vaccine and age group based on recent comparative effectiveness data and CDC estimates,17,22,23 and were assumed to remained fixed across the modeled influenza season. Absolute vaccine effectiveness of TIVe was estimated based on the mean values for the 2011–2012 to 2019–2020 seasons in the US and were adjusted per age based on peer reviewed studies in outpatient settings, resulting in vaccine effectiveness estimates of 58% in children, 40% in adults aged 18–64 y, and 35% in older adults.24,25 For aTIV, vaccine effectiveness was estimated at 44% for older adults, based on studies comparing relative vaccine effectiveness of aTIV vs TIVe.26–28 Since our dynamic transmission model simulated vaccine effectiveness against influenza infection (which subsequently influences downstream outcomes including hospitalization), we calibrated the age-specific absolute vaccine effectiveness values for TIVc such that the model would reproduce the observed overall rVE of 12% for TIVc versus TIVe against hospitalization.29–31 This calibration process, which accounted for age-specific contact patterns, transmission dynamics, disease severity distributions, and baseline vaccine coverage rates observed in the US (overall coverage: 52%), yielded absolute vaccine effectiveness estimates for TIVc against symptomatic influenza of 63.8% in children and 44% in adults aged 18–64 y. Sensitivity analysis was performed using effectiveness levels which reproduced an observed overall rVE of half the baseline assumptions (i.e. approximately 6%).

Health and economic outcomes

The health outcome and economic impacts of TIVc+aTIV versus the two baseline scenarios were evaluated using an age-structured decision tree model based on that published in De Boer et al.17 The decision tree model used available economic parameters, and was split into low- and high-risk patients, based on chronic disease status.32 Health outcomes were then stratified within risk groups (high or low) into not medically attended, outpatient (GP), hospitalization, or death, with outpatient visits further stratified in patients <18 y (uncomplicated, otitis media, pneumonia/other complications).

Economic analysis was performed from both a societal and payer perspective, taking into account productivity loss due to death. Inputs were based on health outcomes derived from the decision tree model, with disease costs and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) based on a recent health economic analysis on the economic costs of influenza in the US, with costs adjusted to 2025 values.10 Workday costs for children <18 y were assumed to be related to parental absenteeism.17 Vaccine prices (per dose) were set at $20 for TIVe, $32 for TIVc, and $73 for aTIV, based on US vaccine pricing as of September 2025.33 Evaluation of cost-effectiveness of vaccination scenarios was based on the most frequently used willingness to pay threshold in the US of $50,000USD per QALY.34

Results

Health outcomes

The model demonstrated consistent and substantial reductions in influenza burden when comparing use of TIVc+aTIV with TIVe or TIVe+aTIV across all evaluated coverage levels. At an overall coverage level of 20% across age groups, use of TIVc+aTIV led to approximately 1.8 million fewer symptomatic cases, 823,000 fewer GP visits, 31,000 fewer hospitalizations, and 6,200 fewer deaths across age groups compared with TIVe. When compared with the combination of TIVe+aTIV in older adults, TIVc+aTIV resulted in 1.4 million fewer symptomatic cases, 576,000 fewer GP visits, 16,600 fewer hospitalizations, and 2,200 fewer deaths across age groups (Figure 1, Supplementary Table S2). As coverage increased, the impact of switching to a cell-based vaccine became more pronounced, with over 8.5 million symptomatic cases, 3.9 million GP visits, 127,000 hospitalizations, and 19,000 deaths prevented vs TIVe across age groups with 65% vaccine coverage. Impacts of increasing vaccination coverage were not linear across the health outcomes due to herd immunity effects and a higher percentage of higher risk individuals being assumed to be covered at lower overall coverage rates compared with lower risk individuals (Supplementary Table S1).

Reductions in negative health outcomes with the use of TIVc+aTIV were seen across all age groups analyzed, with greatest reductions in cases, hospitalizations, and deaths seen in the older adult age groups vs TIVe alone (Supplementary Table S2) mainly driven by the effect of herd immunity conferred by vaccination of the younger population. When compared with a combination of TIVe+aTIV, greatest percentage reductions in negative health outcomes were observed in children at lower vaccine coverage rates (≤50%), with similar relative incremental benefits observed across age groups at higher vaccine coverage rates (Supplementary Table S3).

Economic outcomes

Overall economic costs

Use of TIVc in combination with aTIV resulted in both direct and societal cost savings compared with either the scenario of use of TIVe across age groups or TIVe+aTIV (Table 2). At a low vaccine coverage rate of 20%, which is considerably lower than any vaccine coverage rate reported in previous influenza seasons in the US, use of TIVc+aTIV would result in direct cost reductions of $2.27 million compared with TIVe, and $279 million compared with TIVe+aTIV. Savings were lower vs the TIVe scenario due to the higher vaccine cost of aTIV vs the unadjuvanted vaccine. By 65% coverage, these direct cost savings had increased to $2.73 billion and $4.48 billion, representing a 9.2% and 14.1% reduction compared with the baseline scenarios, respectively (Figure 2(a)). Significant reductions were also seen in societal costs, with reductions of up to 15.9% vs TIVe-aTIV and 11.9% vs TIVe with 65% vaccine coverage (Figure 2(b)). Compared with the two baseline scenarios, TIVc+aTIV would result in approximately 30,000 to 233,500 QALYs gained, across the range of vaccine coverage scenarios evaluated. From both a payer and societal perspective, use of TIVc+aTIV would be cost-saving compared with either TIVe or TIVe+aTIV, irrespective of vaccine coverage (Table 2). As our analysis was highly sensitive to vaccine price, evaluations at 20% coverage showed that TIVc remained cost-effective at the commonly used threshold of $50,000/QALY for prices up to $60, with cost-effectiveness maintained up to a price of $48 at a threshold of $25,000/QALY and up to $83 at $100,000/QALY. TIVc became increasingly cost-effective with higher vaccine coverage, with a coverage rate of 65% yielding a cost-neutral price (i.e. the price at which the incremental costs of TIVc are fully offset by downstream savings) of $58 for TIVc (vs $20 for TIVe).

Costs by age group

In evaluations of the incremental economic impacts of TIVc+aTIV by age, the use of the cell-based vaccine in combination with aTIV was found to be cost-effective or cost-saving across all age groups and vaccine coverage levels assessed. Across all vaccine coverage levels, direct and societal costs were reduced with TIVc+aTIV for the 50–64 y age group, when compared with either baseline scenario (Figure 3). At higher vaccine coverage levels (≥40%), direct costs were also reduced in the 18–49 y age group vs either baseline scenario. When compared with TIVe+aTIV, cost reductions were also seen in the 65–74 y and ≥75 y groups across all vaccine coverage levels. Direct costs in the pediatric groups remained higher than either baseline scenario, driven by the limited negative health impacts of influenza in these age groups. Societal costs showed similar trends to direct costs, although cost reductions were observed in the 6 months to 4 y age group at vaccine coverage ≥40% compared with either baseline strategy (Figure 3).

aTIV, adjuvanted trivalent influenza vaccine; TIVc, cell-based trivalent influenza vaccine; TIVe, egg-based trivalent influenza vaccine.

Overall, use of TIVc+aTIV resulted in reductions in QALYs due to death or sickness in all age groups, irrespective of vaccine coverage or baseline scenario (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5). From a payer perspective, compared with the use of TIVe in all age groups, TIVc+aTIV was cost-saving at all vaccine coverage levels in the 50–64 y age group, and cost-effective in other age groups. TIVc+aTIV also became cost-saving in the 18–49 y age group from vaccine coverage levels of 40% or higher. From a societal perspective, TIVc+aTIV was generally cost-saving in the 6 months to 4 y, 18–49 y, and 50–64 y age groups, and cost-effective in the other age groups (Supplementary Table S4). Compared with TIVe+aTIV, TIVc+aTIV was cost-saving in adults ≥50 y and cost-effective in younger individuals from both a payer and societal perspective, across all vaccine coverage levels. At higher vaccine coverage rates (≥40%), TIVc+aTIV was also cost-saving for the 18–49 y group from a payer perspective. From a societal perspective, by 65% coverage, TIVc+aTIV was cost-saving in all age groups except 5–17-y-olds (Supplementary Table S5).

Sensitivity analysis – low incidence influenza season

When cost-effectiveness analysis was repeated based on a lower incidence influenza season with reduced rates of transmission, TIVc+aTIV was found to be cost-saving vs either baseline scenario at coverage rates ≤ 40% (payer perspective) or ≤50% (societal perspective). From a payer perspective, TIVc+aTIV was no longer cost-effective compared with TIVe at coverage rates ≥60% or compared with TIVe+aTIV at 65% vaccine coverage. From a societal perspective, TIVc+aTIV remained cost-effective at all coverage rates compared with TIVe+aTIV but was not cost-effective at coverage rates ≥60% vs TIVe (Supplementary Table S6).

Sensitivity analysis – reduced vaccine effectiveness

Reducing rVE of TIVc vs TIVe to 50% of baseline levels resulted in absolute VE of TIVc of 60% in children and 42% in adults. These lower VEs did not have any substantial effects on cost-effectiveness analysis across age groups. When compared with TIVe, TIVc+aTIV remained cost-saving in the 50–64 y age group and cost-effective at all vaccine coverage levels in the other age groups, with the exception of the 5–17 y age group (Supplementary Table S7). When compared with TIVe+aTIV, TIVc+aTIV remained cost-saving in adults ≥50 y, and cost-effective in young children (6 months to 4 y) and younger adults (18–49 y) across all vaccine coverage levels, from both a payer and a societal perspective (Supplementary Table S8).

Discussion

The results of our analysis show that use of TIVc in individuals <65 y of age in combination with aTIV in older adults ≥65 y has large epidemiological and economic benefits, with reductions of up to 8.5 million symptomatic cases, 3.9 million GP-attended cases, 127,000 hospitalizations, and 19,000 deaths compared with use of TIVe, across age groups. Use of TIVc+aTIV was also cost-saving at all coverage levels evaluated, with direct costs savings of more than $2 billion and $4 billion compared with TIVe or TIVe+aTIV at 65% vaccine coverage. QALYs gained from use of TIVc instead of TIVe ranged from 30,000 to 233,500 across coverage levels, with all scenarios considered dominant, i.e. TIVc was more effective at improving health outcomes with lower costs. Evaluations by age group showed that TIVc+aTIV was cost-effective or cost-saving at all vaccine coverage levels for every age group.

Our findings reflect the higher relative vaccine effectiveness of cell-based vaccines in individuals younger than 65 y, where TIVc provides the largest incremental benefit in health outcomes over TIVe. In a meta-analysis of published estimates of influenza vaccine effectiveness prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, QIVc consistently out-performed QIVe across the three seasons analyzed, with rVE estimates of 6–16% across seasons in persons aged 4–64 y.35 In line with this, recent evaluations of antibody responses following seasonal influenza vaccine have shown that repeat vaccination with egg-based vaccines can preferentially boost antibodies targeting epitopes linked to egg adaptation, resulting in lower vaccine immunogenicity compared with use of cell-based vaccines. The findings of the study suggest that in individuals who have previously received egg-based influenza vaccines, a number of seasons of non-egg-based vaccinations may be required to redirect the immune system away from these egg-adaptation epitopes to improve match with circulating strains and thereby vaccine effectiveness, particularly to A/H3N2.36 Overall, based on the findings of our study, TIVc would be the preferred strategy for seasonal influenza vaccination of individuals <65 y of age, with the potential for additional benefits in increased vaccine effectiveness over the following few years, should there be a widespread switch to cell-based vaccines instead of traditional egg-based manufacturing techniques.

Our analysis showed lowest health and economic benefits of switching to TIVc+aTIV were in the 5–17 y age group, with cost-effectiveness conclusions predominantly driven by substantial indirect population-level gains in QALYs for this age group. Children play an important role in herd immunity and influenza transmission to more vulnerable groups (e.g. older adults) and therefore are a key target population for prevention of seasonal influenza.37 In the UK, where universal seasonal influenza vaccination across age groups is not currently recommended, guidelines recommend children together with high-risk populations (e.g. older adults, patients with chronic diseases) are vaccinated annually against seasonal influenza to reduce the transmission of disease to more vulnerable populations,38 a move which has resulted in substantial reductions in outpatient and hospitalized influenza both in vaccinated children and at a population level.39 While we found that the direct health and economic impacts of switching to TIVc for school-aged children were lower than for other age groups, preferential use of the cell-based vaccine in this group still had incremental benefits in the reduction in cases and hospitalizations, although it was offset by the higher vaccine cost. However, use of TIVc remained cost-effective compared with traditional egg-based vaccines in this age group, given the reductions in transmission to higher-risk populations.

One of the major strengths of our analysis is the combination of influenza transmission modeling, cost evaluation, and QALY analysis to provide a comprehensive assessment of the most favorable vaccination strategy from both a health and economic outcome perspective, with similar findings to previous cost-effectiveness analyses of TIVc.40 As vaccine coverage can vary from season to season, and is generally less than optimal, we also evaluated health and economic outcomes across a range of feasible vaccine coverage scenarios, with findings generally consistent across coverage levels. Although the model explicitly accounted for indirect effects through a dynamic transmission framework, the magnitude of these effects, particularly among adults aged ≥65 y, remains sensitive to contextual factors. For example, during low incidence seasons, reductions in transmission driven by younger age groups may be smaller in absolute terms than observed in higher incidence seasons. In addition, the co-circulation of other respiratory pathogens such as COVID-19 and associated changes in behavior may alter contact patterns in ways that were not fully captured by age-specific contact matrices alone. As such, while indirect effects were considered as part of the model, the conclusions of this analysis mainly focus on the direct benefits of TIVc vs TIVe, with indirect effects presented as complementary and subject to greater uncertainty. It should also be noted that the findings of our analysis rely on the assumed vaccine effectiveness estimates and rVE between the vaccines, which may vary annually. While our cost-effectiveness analysis remained robust in sensitivity analysis with lowered rVE, resulting in very similar VE estimates between the two vaccines, the actual costs and cost savings may vary on a season-by-season basis.

One of the major limitations of the analysis was the assumption of vaccine prices. While we used the current US vaccine prices for seasonal influenza vaccines, changes in real-world pricing and procurement strategies, particularly if these alter the relative prices between the vaccines, could substantially affect the results. While it is unexpected to be the case, greater increases in the cost of TIVc relative to TIVe may result in different cost-effectiveness conclusions; however, it is likely that TIVc would remain cost-effective relative to TIVe across realistic fluctuations in relative vaccine pricing. In addition, TIVc would remain cost-saving in situations where the vaccine prices are more similar than those assumed in the current analysis, due to the incremental benefits of TIVc on preventing negative health outcomes. Another limitation was the vaccine effectiveness assumptions, as vaccine effectiveness against seasonal influenza and relative effectiveness of the individual vaccines vary seasonally. In line with this, we also performed an additional analysis using a relative vaccine effectiveness level of half that used in the main analysis. We found no substantial effects across age groups in cost-effectiveness of TIVc+aTIV versus the baseline scenarios, showing the incremental benefits of cell-based and enhanced vaccines, even in seasons with higher degrees of vaccine mismatch. Additionally, while we did not directly evaluate the impacts of the other enhanced vaccine recommended for older adults (i.e. HD-TIV), we would expect very similar impacts on health and economic outcomes, given the same vaccine cost and estimated effectiveness.27,33 Finally, our study assumed a fixed vaccine effectiveness per vaccine product, rather than varying across strains or age groups.

In summary, in our modeling analysis, use of TIVc+aTIV for the prevention of seasonal influenza was the preferred strategy over TIVe or TIVe+aTIV, in terms of both cost-saving and health gains. Overall, our results support a differentiated vaccine strategy by age group, with preferential use of a cell-based vaccine in children and younger adults to maximize public health benefits while minimizing economic costs.
