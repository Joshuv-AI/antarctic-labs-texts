Role of GLP‐1 Receptor Agonists in Managing Cancer Therapy–Related Cardiac Dysfunction
Authors: Aravinthan Vignarajah, San Kim, Moath Albliwi, Hyunjun Max Ahn, Aleksandar Izda, Faris Naffa, Nishanthi Vigneswaramoorthy, Shimoli Barot, Gautam Shah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrent anticancer treatments can result in cancer therapy–related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD). While glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) have demonstrated cardioprotective properties, their role in CTRCD has not been previously explored.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study utilized the TriNetX research network. Adults aged ≥18 years who had a cancer history and who underwent antineoplastic therapy and developed CTRCD between January 1, 2012 and January 1, 2023 were included. Among patients on guideline‐directed medical therapy for heart failure, 2 groups were identified: GLP‐1 RA users and nonusers. Propensity score matching (1:1) was conducted based on demographics, comorbid conditions, and concurrent medications, yielding a matched sample of 837 patients. Outcomes were assessed over a 1‐year follow‐up period.ResultsThe study cohort found 4982 patients with CTRCD; 837 received GLP‐1 RA therapy (mean age: 69.0 years; 43.8% female; 70.4% White). Compared with nonusers, those treated with GLP‐1 RAs alongside guideline‐directed medical therapy had significantly lower rates of all‐cause mortality (hazard ratio, 0.57 [95% CI, 0.43–0.77]; P<0.001), acute heart failure exacerbations (hazard ratio, 0.69 [95% CI, 0.56–0.85]; P<0.001), and all‐cause hospitalizations (hazard ratio, 0.83 [95% CI, 0.72–0.96]; P=0.009). While reductions in atrial fibrillation/flutter and ventricular tachycardia were observed, they were not statistically significant.ConclusionAmong patients with CTRCD receiving guideline‐directed medical therapy, GLP‐1 RA use may be linked to better 1‐year survival and improved heart failure–related outcomes.Keywords: cancer, cancer therapy‐related cardiac dysfunction, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists, heart failureSubject Categories: Cardio-Oncology, Heart Failure

Cancer therapeutics are rapidly advancing, with numerous novel agents introduced annually. Alongside this progress, the incidence of cancer therapy–related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD) is rising, driven by both new and established anticancer drugs. 1 CTRCD, defined as a decline in left ventricular ejection fraction exceeding 10% or falling below 53% due to cancer treatment, affects ≈10% to 19% of patients receiving conventional therapies. 2 Heart failure (HF) resulting from chemotherapy carries a 3.5‐fold higher mortality risk compared with idiopathic cardiomyopathy, and over time, the likelihood of death from cardiovascular complications can exceed the risk of cancer recurrence. 3

Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs), originally developed for glycemic control in type 2 diabetes (T2D), have since been recognized for their cardiovascular benefits. 4 Multiple randomized controlled trials have confirmed the efficacy of GLP‐1 RAs in improving cardiovascular outcomes among patients with T2D, solidifying their role in managing patients with concurrent cardiovascular disease. 5

Animal models have shown that GLP‐1 RAs may offer cardioprotective benefits in the context of anticancer therapy, largely through mechanisms involving antioxidant activity, anti‐inflammatory effects, and improvements in cardiac biomarkers and function. 6 However, most clinical trials evaluating the cardiovascular benefits of GLP‐1 RAs have excluded patients undergoing cancer therapy. 5 As a result, their potential role in CTRCD remains unexplored in clinical settings. This study aims to assess the effects of GLP‐1 RAs on cardiovascular outcomes in individuals diagnosed with CTRCD.

METHODS

We utilized TriNetX, a global federated health research network that offers access to aggregate statistics from electronic medical records, including diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory results, and genomic data, covering ≈134 million patients across 102 major health care organizations, mostly based in the United States. As a federated system, TriNetX obtained a waiver from the Western Institutional Review Board because it provides only de‐identified statistical summaries and aggregated counts, without disclosing protected health information, and no study‐specific interventions are involved in these retrospective analyses. All data and materials used in this study are publicly accessible through the TriNetX database platform, and the authors did not have direct access to individual‐level raw data. Data are de‐identified, and Institutional Review Board approval is not required.

Study Population and Design

The study included patients aged ≥18 years who had a prior cancer diagnosis and who had received cardiotoxic antineoplastic therapies and subsequently developed CTRCD. Patients treated between January 1, 2012 and January 1, 2023 were eligible. The antineoplastic drugs assessed for cardiotoxicity comprised anthracyclines, alkylating agents, antimetabolites, monoclonal antibodies, small‐molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and proteasome inhibitors. These agents were chosen based on evidence from previous literature demonstrating their cardiotoxic potential. 7 CTRCD was defined as new‐onset cardiomyopathy, HF, or left ventricular ejection fraction ≤40% after receiving cancer treatment. Individuals with prior acute coronary syndromes, coronary artery bypass grafting, percutaneous coronary intervention, or ischemic cardiomyopathy were excluded to minimize ischemia‐related confounding in cardiac dysfunction. International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes were used to identify cancer history, HF, acute coronary syndromes, coronary artery bypass grafting, percutaneous coronary intervention, and ischemic cardiomyopathy. International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) codes were mapped to ICD‐10 codes by the TriNetX network, which enables longitudinal real‐world data analysis across different ICD coding systems. The majority of the participants received optimal guideline‐directed medical therapy, including angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, or angiotensin receptor‐neprilysin inhibitors, β‐blockers, sodium‐glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists in differing combinations. The study population was divided into 2 groups: those who received GLP‐1 RA and those who did not. Time zero was defined as the initiation of GLP‐1 RA therapy for the treatment group, and as the date of CTRCD diagnosis for the GLP‐1 RA nonuser cohort (Data S1).

Study Outcomes

The study analyzed primary and secondary outcomes over a 1‐year follow‐up period. The outcomes included were HF exacerbations (identified by ICD‐10 codes or the need for intravenous loop diuretics), all‐cause hospitalization or emergency department visits, all‐cause mortality, atrial fibrillation/flutter, and ventricular tachycardia. Gastroenteritis was analyzed as a falsification outcome.

Statistical Analysis

The patient cohort was categorized into 2 groups based on whether they were prescribed GLP‐1 receptor agonists. Continuous variables are presented as mean±SD and compared using Welch's t test to account for potential unequal variances between groups, while categorical variables are expressed as n (%) and analyzed via the χ2 test. To address baseline differences between the cohorts, 1:1 propensity‐score matching (PSM) was applied using a built‐in algorithm that utilizes a greedy nearest‐neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.1 times the pooled SD of the linear propensity scores. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences, a measure that expresses the difference in means between groups in units of standard deviation, independent of sample size. A standardized mean difference of <0.1 was considered indicative of adequate balance between cohorts. PSM factors were selected based on the prior studies as well as the known confounders for cardiovascular outcomes. Extensive PSM performed with multiple parameters including comorbidities, medications, and socioeconomic status. Following PSM, adjusted outcomes between cohorts were evaluated using hazard ratios. Survival analyses were performed with Kaplan–Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards models, with a significance threshold of P<0.05. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals within the TriNetX platform's Cox model framework. No violations of the assumption were observed for the primary outcomes. All statistical analyses were conducted using integrated R within the TriNetX platform. To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by computing the E‐value, to help determine the impact of probable unmeasured confounding on the outcomes. Additionally, we analyzed falsification outcome to further validate our approach. We used gastroenteritis as the unrelated random outcome for this analysis.

DISCUSSION

This is the first study to demonstrate a potential benefit of GLP‐1 receptor agonists in improving cardiovascular outcomes specifically in patients with CTRCD receiving standard HF therapy. In our propensity‐matched cohort, the addition of a GLP‐1 RA was associated with markedly lower 1‐year all‐cause mortality and fewer HF exacerbations and hospitalizations. The magnitude of risk reduction observed underscores a possible cardioprotective effect in this high‐risk population of cancer survivors with cardiomyopathy. These findings, if confirmed, have the potential to reshape management strategies for CTRCD by incorporating GLP‐1 RAs as adjunctive therapy to conventional HF regimens.

Our results align with the cardioprotective profile of GLP‐1 RAs observed in major cardiovascular outcome trials, though there are important differences in patient populations and outcomes. In the general diabetic population, landmark trials such as LEADER (liraglutide) and SUSTAIN‐6 (semaglutide) demonstrated significant reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events with GLP‐1 RAs. 8 , 9 More recently, the SELECT trial extended these benefits to nondiabetic individuals with obesity, showing that weekly semaglutide reduced cardiovascular events in high‐risk patients. 10 Notably, our study in patients with CTRCD found a larger relative risk reduction in all‐cause mortality and HF hospitalizations than those trials, which is likely reflective of differences in end points, patient characteristics, and potential unmeasured confounders given the retrospective nature of the study. The prior trials focused on atherosclerotic events and included patients without active cancer, whereas our cohort consists of cancer‐treated patients with established cardiac dysfunction, a group generally excluded from those trials. It is encouraging that GLP‐1 RA therapy was associated with improved HF‐related outcomes in our study, since earlier HF trials had yielded mixed results in noncancer populations with HFrEF. 11 , 12 By contrast, the reason our population with CTRCD may derive greater benefit could be due to the distinct pathophysiology of CTRCD and the high event rates in this group. Additionally, our cohort included patients with and without diabetes (though a large proportion were diabetic, likely reflecting greater access to GLP‐1 RAs in those patients), whereas the cardiovascular outcome trials enrolled only patients with T2D or at high cardiovascular risk. These differences highlight that while our findings are consistent with the overall cardioprotective trend of GLP‐1 RAs, direct comparisons to prior trials should be made with caution.

The cardioprotective mechanisms of GLP‐1 RAs provide biological plausibility for the improved outcomes seen in our study. Beyond glucose lowering, GLP‐1 RAs exert multiple beneficial effects on the heart and vasculature at the cellular and molecular level. GLP‐1 RA activation in cardiomyocytes enhances antioxidant defenses and preserves mitochondrial integrity. Preclinical studies have shown that semaglutide can attenuate doxorubicin‐induced cardiotoxicity by reducing reactive oxygen species and ameliorating mitochondrial dysfunction, partly through inhibiting the pro‐apoptotic protein BNIP3, thereby preventing mitochondrial injury and cell death. 6 Treated cardiac cells exhibit improved mitochondrial energy homeostasis and less oxidative damage compared with controls, which helps maintain contractile function under stress. GLP‐1 RAs also modulate inflammatory pathways that contribute to cardiac injury. In experimental models of cancer drug cardiotoxicity, GLP‐1 RA treatment has been associated with lower expression of nuclear factor κ‐light chain‐enhancer of activated B cells and inflammatory cytokines, translating into reduced myocardial inflammation and apoptosis. For instance, liraglutide attenuated cardiotoxicity from the cancer drug gefitinib by suppressing the mitogen‐activated protein kinase/nuclear factor κ‐light chain‐enhancer of activated B cells signaling cascade, thereby reducing inflammatory injury in cardiac tissue. 13 Similarly, in a rat model of methotrexate‐induced cardiac dysfunction, liraglutide was shown to suppress inflammatory mediators and cardiomyocyte apoptosis while enhancing cardioprotective growth factors, ultimately preserving cardiac function. 14 GLP‐1 RAs favorably influence the endothelium, which is often impaired in both HF and cancer therapy–related cardiac damage. Activation of GLP‐1 receptors on endothelial cells increases nitric oxide bioavailability through endothelial nitric oxide synthase upregulation, leading to improved vasodilation and tissue perfusion. 15 In addition, GLP‐1 RAs decrease endothelial expression of adhesion molecules such as vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 and other markers of inflammation, which can mitigate the microvascular dysfunction that exacerbates myocardial injury. Enhanced angiogenesis has also been reported with GLP‐1 RA therapy in injured cardiac tissue, suggesting these agents help stimulate new blood vessel formation that could aid in cardiac repair. 14 Collectively, these anti‐oxidative, anti‐inflammatory, mitochondrial protective, and endothelial‐stabilizing actions create a more favorable milieu for the heart under stress. Indeed, histologic analyses in animal studies confirm substantially less myocardial fibrosis and inflammatory cell infiltration in hearts pretreated with GLP‐1 RAs before anthracycline (doxorubicin). 16 Biochemically, markers of cardiac injury such as troponin and creatine kinase‐MB are significantly lower with GLP‐1 RA treatment in these models, consistent with attenuation of cardiomyocyte damage. 16 These findings support the idea that GLP‐1 RAs directly protect cardiac structure and function during and after cardiotoxic cancer treatments. Notably, this protection does not appear to come at the expense of anticancer efficacy. There is no evidence that GLP‐1 RAs interfere with the cancer‐killing actions of chemotherapy. Although data are limited, one analysis found that adding a GLP‐1 RA did not diminish the anti‐tumor effect of an eGFR inhibitor (gefitinib) in a preclinical model, 13 and generally GLP‐1 RAs have not been observed to promote cancer progression in clinical trials. This is a critical consideration in cardio‐oncology: a cardioprotective therapy is far more attractive if it protects the heart without shielding the tumor.

Although GLP‐1 RAs are known for their efficacy in managing T2D and their favorable safety profile in patients with HF, they are not exempt from adverse events. Transient nausea and vomiting are the most common adverse reactions with GLP‐1 RAs. There are more serious concerns about these drugs with pancreatitis, pancreatic cancer, and thyroid cancer; however, at present there is no conclusive evidence to support it. The number of patients with these malignancies was extremely limited in our data to evaluate for any realistic benefit in them. Our findings therefore highlight GLP‐1 RAs as a promising adjunct for patients receiving cardiotoxic cancer therapies, meriting further investigation in prospective studies.

Limitations

Despite the encouraging outcomes, our findings must be interpreted considering potential confounding and bias. Immortal time bias is a concern in our retrospective analysis. By design, patients in the GLP‐1 RA group had to survive long enough after developing CTRCD to be prescribed a GLP‐1 RA, which means there is a period during which they were “immortal” (unable to experience the studied outcomes) before starting the therapy. If not properly accounted for, this can artifactually inflate the survival advantage of the treatment group. In our analysis, we defined cohorts and outcomes over a fixed 1‐year follow‐up from the index CTRCD diagnosis, and we attempted to mitigate immortal time bias by using propensity matching at baseline and including only new GLP‐1 RA users.

It is possible that GLP‐1 RA users had better overall health status, better nutrition or exercise habits, or were monitored more closely than nonusers, factors that could independently improve outcomes. We controlled for known comorbidities, concurrent medications, and socio‐economic status, but unmeasured variables could influence the results. CTRCD often results from specific cancer treatments (eg, anthracyclines, HER2‐targeted therapy, and radiation). Our database did not allow us to reliably extract the exact chemotherapy agents or cumulative doses each patient received. Most patients received multi‐agent chemotherapy, and we could not determine whether GLP‐1 RAs provided particular benefit with certain drugs or types of cancer therapy due to lack of sample size for subgroup analysis. The inability to stratify or adjust for different anticancer regimens means there could be heterogeneity in risk that we did not capture. Future studies should incorporate detailed oncology treatment data to clarify this interaction.

An additional limitation is the potential influence of the obesity paradox, where higher body mass index has been associated with improved outcomes in the cancer population. Since GLP‐1 RAs promote weight loss, this could confound the observed benefits. We tried to mitigate this confounding effect by including this in the PSM.

Another limitation of our study is the potential for selection bias, particularly in patients who received GLP‐1 receptor agonists. A substantial proportion of our cohort comprised individuals with cardiovascular comorbidities such as T2D and chronic kidney disease, populations in which the cardiovascular and renal benefits of GLP‐1RAs are already well established. As such, the observed improvements may reflect known effects of GLP‐1 RA therapy in these subgroups rather than a specific benefit in the broader population with CTRCD. This raises the possibility that the utility of GLP‐1 RAs as an adjunct to guideline‐directed medical therapy in CTRCD may be more relevant to patients with underlying diabetic cardiomyopathy or chronic kidney disease, rather than universally applicable across all patients with CTRCD. We acknowledge this limitation and emphasize the need for future studies to explore the role of GLP‐1 RAs in CTRCD populations without these comorbidities.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, while our study suggests a potential cardioprotective role for GLP‐1 receptor agonists in patients with CTRCD, several limitations temper the strength of our conclusions. While we have demonstrated a potential beneficial role of GLP‐1 RA in CTRCD, the results should be viewed as hypothesis‐generating. Prospective randomized controlled trials are needed to validate the cardioprotective effects of GLP‐1 RAs in patients with CTRCD and confirm that such an approach is safe and effective across diverse patient groups. Despite the limitations, our study provides an important first step suggesting that GLP‐1 RAs may emerge as valuable agents in the care of patients with CTRCD. The integration of metabolic therapies such as GLP‐1 RAs into cardio‐oncology holds promise, but it requires confirmation through rigorous future research.
