Recurrent idiopathic ischemic priapism in a healthy pediatric patient: A case report
Authors: Daniel Ajabshir, Kenneth Softness, Siam Oottamasathien
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present a rare case of recurrent idiopathic ischemic priapism in a healthy 14-year-old male with no identifiable risk factors such as hematologic disorders, trauma, or medication use. The patient required surgical intervention after failing conservative measures, including aspiration and intracavernosal phenylephrine. A bilateral T-shunt ultimately achieved successful detumescence. Postoperative rehabilitation with tadalafil and a vacuum erection device preserved erectile function. This case highlights the importance of early recognition and a stepwise approach to pediatric priapism. It also raises the possibility of a novel association between chronic constipation and impaired venous outflow contributing to priapism in children.Keywords: Pediatric priapism, Ischemic priapism, Recurrent priapism, T-shunt surgery

Introduction

Priapism is a rare urologic emergency defined by a prolonged penile erection lasting more than 4 h without sexual stimulation.1 The incidence of priapism varies among different populations and age groups, with an estimated occurrence of 2.9 cases per 100,000 individuals per year, most commonly affecting men between the ages of 20 and 50.1 In children, priapism is significantly rarer, with a reported incidence of 0.3–1.5 cases per 100,000 children annually.2

Priapism is classified into three subtypes: ischemic (low-flow), non-ischemic (high-flow), and stuttering priapism. Ischemic priapism, which is the most serious type, occurs due to impaired venous outflow.3 This blockage can lead to hypoxia and acidosis, potentially causing irreversible damage to the smooth muscle if not treated expeditiously.3 In children, priapism is most commonly associated with sickle cell disease (SCD), followed by leukemia, trauma, pharmacological factors, or unknown causes.4 Although there is a well-established link between priapism and hematologic disorders, idiopathic cases in pediatric patients are rare and often underreported in medical literature.

Herein, we report a rare occurrence of recurrent idiopathic ischemic priapism in a healthy 14-year-old male with no identifiable risk factors such as sickle cell disease, malignancy, trauma or accidental ingestion.

Case

A 14-year-old previously healthy male presented to the local emergency department with a 36-h history of a painful, persistent, and rigid erection. Upon admission, the patient denied any accidental medication ingestion, illicit drug use, or trauma and reported no bowel movements for the prior three weeks. He had experienced similar episodes linked to constipation in the past, but those resolved spontaneously without medical intervention.

On physical examination, the patient exhibited 10/10 penile rigidity with severe pain upon palpation. Laboratory findings at presentation were unremarkable, with white blood cell counts within normal limits and no significant abnormalities in hemoglobin, hematocrit, or coagulation studies. A corporal blood gas analysis confirmed ischemic priapism, showing a pO2 of 22.3 mmHg, a pCO2 of 202.0 mmHg, and a pH of 7.373 (Table 1, Table 2).

During his visit in the local emergency department, the initial intervention included aspiration and irrigation with intracavernosal injections of phenylephrine, which provided brief detumescence; however, significant ecchymosis of the penile shaft and increased rigidity was noted soon after.

The patient was subsequently transferred to our hospital for further management. Initial ultrasound demonstrated patent bilateral cavernosal and dorsal penile arteries, although there was limited evaluation of flow velocities in the dorsal penile arteries. The ultrasound also revealed symmetric waveforms, preserved diastolic flow without elevated resistive indices in the cavernosal arteries, and a 3.1 x 1.9 × 1.4 cm hematoma in the mid-shaft beneath Buck's fascia. The patient was then taken to the operating room (HD1) for repeat irrigation, aspiration, and intracorporeal phenylephrine injections. Additionally, a modified Winter's shunt was performed using an 18-gauge needle on both sides of the glans into the corporal bodies, temporarily resolving the priapism.

During the course of the acute hospitalization, consultations were made with colleagues in the Department of Urology and several andrology specialists across the country due to the rarity and severity of this condition.

The following day (HD2, POD1), priapism persisted, and repeat ultrasound indicated no detectable flow within either cavernosal artery on color or power doppler evaluations, but there was prominent flow in the soft tissue surrounding the penis. Based on the imaging and clinical findings, the patient was brought back to the operating room where bilateral T-shunts were performed using an 11-blade, followed by gentle dilation with a 6-French Hegar dilator, successfully achieving detumescence.

The patient's hospital course afterward was uneventful. He was evaluated by the hematology and gastroenterology services, revealing no apparent cause for the severe episode of ischemic priapism. He was discharged to home on HD3.

Upon discharge, the patient was seen locally by an andrology specialist and started on a daily dose of 5 mg tadalafil, along with regular application of a vacuum erection device to maintain penile length and oxygenation. His physical exam was without obvious penile fibrosis, and the patient reported normal spontaneous erections and expressed satisfaction with the quality of his erections.

Discussion

Pediatric priapism is an exceptionally rare condition, affecting only 0.3 to 1.5 children per 100,000 annually.2 However, due to its rarity, the exact incidence remains uncertain4. While SCD, trauma, leukemia, and certain medications account for most pediatric cases, up to 50 % have no identifiable underlying cause, making diagnosis more challenging4,5. Additionally, embarrassment or fear may prevent children from discussing symptoms, leading to delays in seeking medical attention.4 Ultimately, our patient and his family denied any trauma, a history of hematologic problems, or medication use, which complicated the differential diagnosis.

Ischemic priapism, or low-flow priapism, is the most common form seen in pediatric patients. It occurs when venous outflow from the corpora cavernosa is obstructed, leading to increased intracavernosal pressure within the tunica albuginea.4 This pathophysiology is similar to compartment syndrome, where rising pressure further restricts blood flow, worsening hypoxia, and acidosis1,2,.4 If left untreated, the accumulation of metabolic waste in stagnant blood can cause tissue damage, fibrosis, and permanent erectile dysfunction1,4.

Our patient had a history of recurrent episodes of stuttering priapism but had never sought medical care. Stuttering priapism consists of self-limiting, intermittent episodes of prolonged erections and is commonly associated with a history of ischemic priapism.6 It is frequently linked to SCD but can also occur in individuals without an underlying hematologic disorder.7 Although these episodes typically resolve within a few hours, they often become progressively painful and more frequent over time.7 Common pediatric triggers include nocturnal erections, dehydration, fever, and cold exposure, all of which can disrupt the balance between arterial inflow and venous drainage.8 Without proper intervention, stuttering priapism can progress to an acute ischemic episode requiring urgent treatment.9 Delayed management beyond 24 hours significantly increases the risk of corporal fibrosis and erectile dysfunction.9

A notable finding in this case was the patient's history of chronic constipation, reporting no bowel movements in the three weeks prior to presentation. While constipation is not widely recognized as a trigger for priapism, a recent report by Mercurio et al. suggests that excessive stool burden and increased intra-abdominal pressure may impair venous drainage from the pelvis.10 Given the close anatomical relationship between the penis and the rectosigmoid colon, increased intra-abdominal pressure could compress pelvic veins, leading to venous congestion and obstructed blood outflow from the corpora cavernosa, thereby precipitating priapism.10 In the absence of other inciting factors, we consider this a plausible mechanism for this patient's presentation. While enemas are a recognized acute management approach in pediatric priapism, documentation of recurrent ischemic priapism associated with chronic constipation in otherwise healthy patients remains limited10.

Prompt diagnosis is essential to prevent complications from ischemic priapism.1 Per AUA/SMSNA guidelines, evaluation includes a detailed medical, surgical, and sexual history, along with a physical examination of the genital and perineal region.1 Corporal blood gas analysis is a key diagnostic tool, as hypoxia, hypercarbia, and acidosis confirm ischemic priapism.1 In this case, blood gas analysis revealed a pO2 of 22.3 mmHg, a pCO2 of 202.0 mmHg, and a pH of 7.373, consistent with ischemic priapism.1 Doppler ultrasonography further confirmed the diagnosis, demonstrating preserved arterial flow without systolic waveforms, indicative of venous obstruction.11 Early intervention within 36 hours is critical for preserving erectile function.1

Current AUA guidelines recommend aspiration and intracavernosal phenylephrine as first-line measures, with surgical intervention if conservative management fails to prevent complications.1

Given the failure of conservative management, surgical intervention was required. Although bowel cleanout alone may resolve some cases of pediatric priapism, our patient experienced persistent and recurrent episodes despite constipation management, requiring surgical intervention. Per AUA guidelines, a modified Winter's shunt was performed as the next step.1 A Winter's shunt is a surgical procedure in which a large-bore biopsy needle is inserted through the glans penis into the corpus cavernosum multiple times12,13. This creates small fistula that allow blood to drain from the corpora cavernosa, thereby relieving intracavernosal pressure.12 This technique is preferred because it is less invasive than other shunting procedures and is well-known among urologists, making it particularly useful in pediatric patients12,13.

Despite its common use, Winter's shunt does not always achieve complete detumescence and carries a risk of recurrence, as demonstrated in this case. It can also lead to complications, such as urethral strictures.13 When Winter's shunt fails to resolve priapism, the next step in management is a more aggressive approach, such as the bilateral T-shunt.1

The bilateral T-shunt procedure involves creating a vertical incision through the glans into the corpus cavernosum using a scalpel.13 The blade is then rotated 90° laterally to form a fistula.13 This procedure can be enhanced by intracavernous tunneling with a 20–24 French straight urethral sound to improve blood drainage, especially in cases of prolonged priapism14,15. While the T-shunt is highly effective, it does carry the risk of converting low-flow priapism into high-flow priapism due to the potential formation of intracorporeal fistulas.14

When comparing the two techniques, the T-shunt with tunneling has demonstrated greater effectiveness in treating priapism, especially in prolonged cases.13 This method allows for more efficient evacuation of blood and can be performed more efficiently in emergencies.13 While Winter's shunt is often used as a first-line approach due to its simplicity, it has a lower success rate and is associated with a higher risk of complications, such as urethral fistulas and erectile dysfunction.13 Studies have shown that the T-shunt achieves 100 % detumescence during the procedure and offers better long-term outcomes, making it the preferred option when initial management fails.13

When considering the next steps for treating priapism after stenting, the timing of intervention is crucial to prevent long-term complications such as erectile dysfunction, regardless of the treatment method used.13 In our patient's case, the initial approach during follow-up included medical management, specifically prescribing Tadalafil to support erectile function recovery and possibly prevent recurrent priapism. Tadalafil, a PDE-5 inhibitor, enhances nitric oxide signaling, promotes vasodilation, and improves blood flow to the corpora cavernosa.16 Research indicates that low-dose, long-term use of PDE-5 inhibitors may help restore balance in the nitric oxide pathway, reducing the likelihood of recurrent priapism while also improving erectile function in some patients.16

Furthermore, the patient was prescribed a vacuum erection device (VED). A VED works by creating negative pressure around the penis, which draws blood into the corpora cavernosa and expands the sinusoidal spaces, thereby improving oxygenation.17 After an episode of priapism, the VED can assist in penile rehabilitation by increasing arterial blood flow, potentially reducing fibrosis and preserving erectile function.17

In cases where shunts fail to provide relief, penile prosthesis implantation may be necessary to preserve erectile function, according to AUA guidelines.1 The best outcomes are achieved when the implantation is performed within seven days of a priapism episode.18 Given the child's young age, the decision was made to defer the placement of a prosthesis and instead pursue penile rehabilitation.

Conclusion

This case highlights the need for early recognition and a structured approach to managing ischemic priapism in pediatric patients. First-line treatments like aspiration and intracavernosal phenylephrine injections are standard, but timely surgical intervention is vital when conservative measures fail to prevent complications such as erectile dysfunction. Given the rarity of idiopathic pediatric priapism, further research is necessary to understand its mechanisms and improve treatment strategies. Postoperative penile rehabilitation, including PDE-5 inhibitors and vacuum erection devices, may help preserve erectile function following surgery. The possible link between chronic constipation and priapism raises important questions, although its clinical significance is unclear. Ultimately, a multidisciplinary approach involving urology, hematology, and other specialists is essential for adequately managing priapism in children.
