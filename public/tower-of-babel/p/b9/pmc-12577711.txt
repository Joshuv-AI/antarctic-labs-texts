Cascade-targeting pH/ROS microneedles promote scarless diabetic wound healing by macrophage metaboimmune reprogramming
Authors: Ganghua Yang, Jianqiu Yang, Zhaoping Diao, Jiajun Long, Zhiwen Shu, Chengkang Liu, Wenbing Wan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Precise macrophage modulation is essential for diabetic wound treatment, yet mitochondrial dysfunction often sustains proinflammatory states. We developed cascade-targeting nanoparticles [epigallocatechin-3-gallate and metformin nanoparticles modified with mannose (EM/Man NPs)] to regulate macrophage mitochondria, integrated into a detachable core-shell microneedle patch (EM/Man MNs) made of quaternary ammonium chitosan and reactive oxygen species (ROS)–degradable polymer. The patch offered high penetration and antibacterial activity, while its ROS-sensitive core released EM/Man NPs to scavenge ROS, restore adenosine 5′-triphosphate production, and reestablish redox balance. The NPs further activated the adenosine 5′-monophosphate–activated protein kinase/Sirtuin 1/peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor gamma coactivator 1α axis to promote mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative phosphorylation, repolarizing macrophages to an anti-inflammatory phenotype. In diabetic mice, EM/Man MNs accelerated healing via bacterial clearance, immune reprogramming, angiogenesis, and collagen deposition while inhibiting scar formation through interleukin-17 and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase–Akt suppression. This cascade-targeting strategy for modulating macrophage mitochondria to regulate immunity and redox homeostasis provides a previously unidentified approach for designing tissue engineering materials.

INTRODUCTION

In recent decades, patients with diabetes have increased year by year, affecting approximately 537 million adults worldwide between the ages of 20 and 79 (1). A notable complication of diabetes is the occurrence of chronic nonhealing wounds, often characterized by hyperglycemia, bacterial infection, and persistent inflammation, leading to wound migration without healing (2, 3). Even after aggressive long-term treatment, the disordered immune microenvironment can lead to pathological scars such as hypertrophic scars (HSs) in wound healing (4, 5). This leads to considerable patient suffering and imposes a substantial financial burden (6). Immunomodulatory dysfunction and elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels are key factors leading to delayed or nonhealing of diabetic wounds (7). Exploring their underlying mechanisms is essential for designing appropriate therapeutic strategies.

Recent studies have highlighted the central role of mitochondria in immunomodulation and redox homeostasis (8). The polarization of macrophage phenotype from M1 (proinflammatory) to M2 (anti-inflammatory) is considered to be a key factor driving the inflammatory phase to the healing proliferative phase (9). However, in diabetic wounds, impaired macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype leads to a sustained accumulation of proinflammatory M1 macrophages, thereby aggravating oxidative stress and perpetuating chronic inflammatory responses (10). Recently, more and more studies have revealed the key role of mitochondrial metabolism in reprogramming the polarization state of macrophages, in which M1 macrophages mainly use glycolysis, while M2 macrophages mainly rely on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) (8, 11). In addition, high-glucose conditions lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, particularly affecting the respiratory chain complexes, which drives an overproduction of ROS (12, 13). The resulting oxidative stress damages cellular and tissue integrity, substantially compromising the wound healing process.

Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), a natural polyphenol, exhibits notable biocompatibility alongside anti-inflammatory and antibacterial properties (14). Its polyphenolic hydroxyl structure enables interaction with bacterial cell membranes, disrupting their integrity and inhibiting bacterial proliferation (15). Moreover, EGCG demonstrates a strong affinity for mitochondrial outer membrane proteins through hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and π-π stacking, indicating its potential for mitochondrial targeting (16–18). Despite these advantages, EGCG’s intracellular efficacy is hampered by its low bioavailability and chemical instability (19). Metformin, primarily known for its antidiabetic effects, also shows great potential in anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory aspects due to its unique ability to regulate energy metabolism (20). It enhances mitochondriogenesis and aids in normalizing mitochondrial function, which is crucial for maintaining intracellular redox balance and regulating the immune microenvironment (21, 22). However, metformin’s therapeutic potential is limited by its short half-life and low bioavailability upon direct delivery. To overcome these limitations, nanoparticle (NP)–based delivery systems present a innovative approach for enhancing the intracellular functionality of both EGCG and metformin (23). While various delivery systems have been developed to improve bioavailability, most are confined to conventional topical delivery methods and lack specificity toward inflammatory sites. Mannose (Man), containing d-mannose residues, emerges as a promising candidate for targeted delivery (24). It is recognized by Man receptors, which are abundantly expressed on antigen-presenting cells, particularly macrophages at inflammatory sites (25). This specificity positions Man as an effective vehicle for targeting inflammatory macrophages. Recognizing the pivotal role of mitochondrial metabolism in macrophages in regulating the inflammatory microenvironment, we propose a distinct strategy that synergistically combines Man and EGCG for cascade targeting of mitochondria within macrophages at inflammatory sites, complemented by metformin to enhance mitochondrial function in coordination with EGCG. This approach aims to precisely reprogram macrophage metabolism through cascade targeting to promote M2 polarization, and this strategy offers a promising therapeutic avenue for modulating chronic wound healing.

In the past decade, several strategies have been explored to treat chronic wounds by targeting the inflammatory microenvironment surrounding the wound area, such as the delivery of cytokines (26), implantation of multifunctional hydrogels or scaffolds (27–29), and cell therapies (30). However, skin barrier systems, including physical, chemical, immune, and microbiome barriers, substantially hinder the delivery of active ingredients, thereby rendering treatment less effective (31). In addition, chronic wounds are often complicated by infections. Although topical antibiotics can help alleviate infections at the surface level, they may be ineffective against deeper infections due to limited penetration through the skin barrier. At present, a large number of studies have been conducted on the use of microneedles (MNs) to treat wounds (32, 33). The MN patch has excellent surface contact, high efficiency, and good mechanical strength, which can effectively penetrate the surface layer of skin and deliver drugs to deep skin tissues (34, 35). In addition, polymer MNs also show great application potential, which can achieve multidrug intelligent delivery by integrating therapeutic molecules, nanoparticles, and microparticles, thereby promoting the design and clinical application of multifunctional therapeutic strategies.

In this study, we developed a one-pot reaction to assemble EGCG and metformin into NPs (EM NPs) (23), which were subsequently surface modified with mannan to incorporate Man, resulting in NPs (EM/Man NPs) capable of cascade targeting mitochondria within macrophages (Fig. 1A). These NPs were then loaded into the needle tips of an MN patch composed of a core-shell structure of quaternary ammonium salt chitosan (QACS) and ROS-degradable polyvinyl alcohol (PVA-TS-PBA) (36), with a 3M removable medical tape serving as the backing substrate. The 3M tape will stick the needle tip out of the dry template but will separate from the needle tip when it comes into contact with body fluids. This entire construct, the detachable microneedles loaded with EM/Man NPs, is termed EM/Man MNs. The detachable EM/Man MNs promote diabetic chronic wound healing and reduce scar formation through antibacterial and anti-inflammatory modulation. QACS, a material renowned for its potent antibacterial properties, attracts negatively charged bacterial surfaces and disrupts bacterial membranes, thereby capturing and killing bacteria (37). Upon application to wounds, the MN structure effectively penetrates deep into the tissue, with the QACS shell capturing deeply embedded bacteria, while the synergistic action of EM/Man NPs in the MN core eradicates the underlying bacteria that are barely controllable by antibiotics. Concurrently, the MN core structure gradually degrades in response to oxidative stress, as the inflammatory phase progresses, leading to the release of the encapsulated EM/Man NPs. These NPs cascade target mitochondria within macrophages at the inflammatory site, modulating mitochondrial metabolism to induce macrophage polarization from the M1 (proinflammatory) to the M2 (anti-inflammatory) phenotype, thereby transitioning the wound from the inflammatory to the proliferative phase (Fig. 1B).

Previous studies have established that adenosine 5′-monophosphate–activated protein kinase (AMPK) plays a key role in oxidative stress, energy metabolism, and mitochondrial homeostasis and can activate Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) by regulating the level of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (oxidized form) (38–41). SIRT1 activation improves mitochondrial function by increasing the level of peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor (PPAR) gamma coactivator 1α (PGC-1α) (42). PGC-1α is a transcriptional coactivator that promotes mitochondrial function, enhances respiratory capacity, and OXPHOS (43). These findings suggest that regulation of the AMPK/SIRT1/PGC-1α axis represents an effective strategy for modulating macrophage polarization through mitochondrial metabolic regulation. Our findings demonstrate that EM/Man MNs activate the AMPK/SIRT1/PGC-1α pathway, promoting mitochondrial biogenesis, enhancing respiratory capacity, and up-regulating OXPHOS, thereby regulating macrophage immune status. In addition, the modulation of the immune microenvironment fosters angiogenesis and extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, ultimately reducing scar formation. In summary, this design accelerates the scarless wound healing process in both diabetic chronic wound models and rabbit ear scar models.

RESULTS

Characteristics of metabolism and inflammation in diabetic wounds

To explore the potential mechanism of the wound healing process of diabetic infection, we created an infected wound group and a noninfected group (blank group) with a diameter of 0.8 cm on the back of diabetic mice to observe the wound healing. As shown in Fig. 2A, wound size was measured on days 0, 5, 10, and 15, and wound healing was significantly slower in the diabetic-infected group than in the blank group. We used hematoxylin & eosin (H&E)–stained panoramic views to assess the wound width on days 5 and 10 in the diabetic-infected wound group and the control group (Fig. 2, B and C), and the results were similar to the gross wound observations, with the diabetic-infected wound group showing a lower wound healing rate than the control group (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, the results of Masson staining showed that the diabetic-infected wound group showed less collagen deposition and fiber rearrangement than the control group (Fig. 2, B and E).

The whole-transcriptome sequencing of diabetic-infected wound and uninfected wound tissues was carried out to study their mechanism. The volcano map showed that diabetic wounds had 1737 significantly altered genes after Staphylococcus aureus treatment (fig. S1A). The top 20 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) are highlighted in the heatmap (fig. S1B). The results of Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed that bacterial infection mainly affected biological processes (BP), such as mitochondrial function (repair of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex), oxidative stress (oxidoreductase activity), and so on (Fig. 2F). In addition, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) found that the bacterial infection group not only showed up-regulation of inflammatory signaling pathways, such as interleukin-17 (IL-17) signaling pathway and tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α) signaling pathway (Fig. 2, G and I), but also showed higher levels of glycolysis and lower levels of OXPHOS (Fig. 2, G and H). To investigate the potential mechanisms of chronic wound infections and elucidate the impact of macrophage oxidative stress and M2 polarization on diabetic wound healing, we quantified ROS levels via dihydroethidium immunofluorescence staining in diabetic wound tissues and evaluated M2 polarization using CD206 and inducible nitric oxide synthase

(iNOS) immunofluorescence. Semiquantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity revealed significantly elevated ROS levels in the S. aureus–infected group compared to the control group (fig. S2, A and B). Further assessment of M2 polarization status in the wound bed demonstrated a distinct shift in macrophage phenotypes. Immunofluorescence analysis showed markedly intensified iNOS signals (indicative of M1 macrophages) coupled with attenuated CD206 signals (indicative of M2 macrophages) in the infected group relative to controls (fig. S2, C and D). This suggests that bacterial infection may bring macrophages into a proinflammatory state by affecting the energy metabolism of cellular mitochondria. Mitochondrial metabolic function is an important source of regulating macrophage biological function. Therefore, precise regulation of macrophage mitochondrial function may be a potential target for the treatment of diabetic-infected wounds.

Characteristics of metabolism and inflammation in diabetic wounds

As shown in Fig. 3A, the natural polyphenol EGCG and metformin were integrated into EM NPs by one-pot reaction and subsequently modified with mannan on their surface, synthesizing multifunctional core-shell NPs (EM/Man NPs) with Man exposed on the surface and EGCG and metformin in the inner core. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images revealed that both EM NPs and EM/Man NPs presented uniform spherical particles (Fig. 3B). Notably, the surface of EM/Man NPs is smoother than that of EM NPs (Fig. 3B). Transmission electron microscopy images show that compared with EM NPs, EM/Man NPs show obvious core-shell structure, and energy-dispersive x-ray (EDX) spectroscopy shows that EM/Man NPs are composed of C, N, and O elements (Fig. 3C). Next, the reaction mechanism of NP formation was analyzed by electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) and x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to investigate the synthesis mechanism of EM/Man NPs (Fig. 3, D and E). Similar to previous studies, the results show that EGCG and metformin conjugated structures through multiple Schiff base and Michael addition reactions. The results of XPS, N spectrum, and O spectrum are shown in the figure. There is an obvious C = N peak at 399.8 eV in the N spectrum, which also confirms the above results (Fig. 3, F and G). The synthesis of EM/Man NPs was further confirmed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and the EM/Man NPs not only retained all the characteristic peaks belonging to the EM NPs but also showed the characteristic peak of Man at 1500 cm−1 (Fig. 3H). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis was used to evaluate the size and stability of NPs. The results showed that the zeta potentials of EM NPs and EM/Man NPs were −21.07 ± 0.35 and −20.90 ± 0.49 mV, respectively, and the average particle size of EM NPs was 274.2 nm, while after mannan modification, the average particle size increased to 317.5 nm (Fig. 3, I and J). Then, we explored whether EM NPs and EM/Man NPs would affect the viability of cells, and Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) experimental results showed that after 48 hours of coincubation with EM NPs and EM/Man NPs, cells showed the highest relative growth rate (RGR) at concentrations of about 200 μg/ml, while at concentrations below 400 μg/ml, cells showed no apparent cytotoxicity (Fig. 3K).

The ability of NPs to target macrophage mitochondria in vitro

We further investigated the macrophage mitochondria–targeting properties of NPs based on the interaction of Man with the macrophage surface and the interaction of natural polyphenols on mitochondria (Fig. 4A). First, we used DiD and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) to label NPs (EM NPs or EM/Man NPs) and nuclei, respectively. After incubation with DiD-labeled NPs for 30 or 120 min, significantly enhanced uptake of EM/Man NPs was observed in macrophages compared to EM NPs (fig. S3A). Furthermore, comparing the control group and the EM NPs group, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was significantly increased in the EM/Man NP group (Fig. 4C), indicating that Man component-mediated NP targeting was significantly enhanced compared to EM NPs. To further evaluate the selective targeting ability of EM/Man NPs to macrophages, we also incubated EM/Man NPs with human umbilical cord endothelial cells (HUVECs) and RAW cells, respectively, and the results showed that the red fluorescence intensity around the nucleus of HUVECs was significantly lower than that of RAW cells (fig. S3A), and the MFI of EM/Man NPs (RAW group) was significantly higher than that of EM/Man NPs (HUVEC group) regardless of 30 or 120 min (Fig. 4D). We further investigated the potential of these endocytosed NPs to localize around mitochondria via colocalization analysis of MitoTracker Green–labeled mitochondria and 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindocarbocyanine perchlorate (DiI)–labeled NPs. As shown in Fig. 4B, treatment with EM/Man NPs resulted in good overlap between red fluorescence (from DiI-labeled NPs) and green fluorescence of mitochondria, with well-coinciding colocalization profiles. In notable contrast, macrophages treated with DiI and DiI-labeled EM NPs showed very limited colocalization, and the same pattern was observed in HUVECs treated with EM/Man NPs. These findings further confirm the excellent cascade targeting ability of EM/Man NPs to mitochondria within macrophages.

Next, we further observed the selective targeting ability of EM NPs and EM/Man NPs to mitochondria in macrophages through biological TEM. As shown in Fig. 4E, EM NPs are mostly distributed between macrophages spatially, while EM/Man NPs are not only in close contact with macrophages but are also mostly endocytosed into cells and distributed around mitochondria and in contact with them (Fig. 4E). This targeting ability may be attributed to the phenolic hydroxyl moiety of natural polyphenols, which is beneficial to prevent the diffusion of mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) and scavenge it efficiently, thus providing a basis for regulating mitochondrial metabolism.

Fabrication and characterization of separated core-shell MN patches

Simply put, the separated core-shell MN patch is prepared through the mold drying process. First, QACS is used as the shell structure of the MNs, and TS-PBA and PVA are cross-linked to form the core component with ROS response (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, 3M medical adhesive tape was pressed on the mold as a supporting substrate, and the needle tip was pulled out to lastly obtain separable core-shell MNs. Furthermore, EM NPs or EM/Man NPs can be premixed with PVA solution by physical encapsulation and thus loaded into MNs, thereby preparing EM MNs, EM/Man MNs, and core-shell MNs without adding any NPs to the MNs. A representative EM/Man MN patch picture shows that it can be easily bent, indicating that the patch has good flexibility, and the needle tips are arranged in a 10 by 10 array (Fig. 5B). SEM can observe that each needle has a height of 600 μm and a base diameter of 200 μm (Fig. 5C). To achieve enhanced visualization, we loaded fluorescein sodium and rhodamine into the shell and core of MNs, respectively. To evaluate the core-shell structure of MNs, confocal fluorescence microscopy three-dimensional imaging revealed a central core labeled with rhodamine and fluorescing red, and the outer layer was wrapped by fluorescein sodium, showing green fluorescence (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, horizontal cross-sectional imaging of MNs also confirmed the existence of a core-shell structure (Fig. 5D).

To evaluate the skin penetration ability of EM/Man MNs, we used skin penetration experiments and force measurement experiments to evaluate their mechanical strength. The results of the mechanical properties of MNs confirmed that each needle can withstand forces up to 0.6 N, which greatly exceeds the 0.1 N force per needle required to pierce the skin (Fig. 5E). Applying the MN patch to rat dorsal skin, it could be observed that the skin surface produced an array of 10 by 10 punctures and remained completely embedded within the skin (Fig. 5F). In H&E section results, it can also be observed that MNs pierced the rat dorsal skin, which shows that the drug can be efficiently delivered into the rat skin through the MN patch (Fig. 5G). To track the intradermal release of EM/Man NPs from MNs, we encapsulated DiD-labeled EM/Man NPs within EM/Man MNs. Fluorescence imaging of skin cryosections revealed uniform distribution of EM/Man NPs in deep subcutaneous tissues (fig. S4A). Further magnification revealed discernible MN penetration tracks, with red fluorescent signals predominantly localized around the punctured tissue periphery (fig. S4, B and C).

Hydrogen nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy confirmed the synthesis of TS-PBA (fig. S5A), followed by FTIR spectroscopy, which confirmed the cross-linking of PVA and TS-PBA and the incorporation of EM NPs and EM/Man MNs into MN patches (EM MNs and EM/Man MNs) (Fig. 5H). We determined the ultraviolet (UV) absorption spectrum of EM/Man NPs (fig. S6A). To evaluate the drug release kinetics of MNs under simulated inflammatory conditions, we exposed EM/Man MNs to varying concentrations of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and different pH values to mimic the chronic wound microenvironment. The results showed that NP release from EM/Man MNs was accelerated with increasing H2O2 concentrations. In addition, pH-responsive release enhancement was observed, with accelerated NP release from EM/Man MNs as pH decreased (Fig. 5I and fig. S7A). The fluid extracted from each group of MN patches did not alter the proliferation of L929 cells (Fig. 5J), and the RGR of all MN patches was above 80% at each time point (Fig. 5K), indicating no apparent in vitro cytotoxicity of each group of MN patches according to the cytotoxicity grading criteria (ISO 10993.5-2009).

EM/Man MNs promote the metabolic transition of macrophages to mitochondrial OXPHOS and promote M2 polarization

First, we evaluated the ability of various MN patches to remove ROS. As shown in Fig. 6A, RAW 264.7 cells after H2O2 pretreatment significantly expressed mtROS, followed by treatment of cells with MN, EM MN, and EM/Man MN extracts, respectively. The expression of mtROS was significantly reduced in EMM MN–treated RAW264.7 cells compared to the H2O2 group, MN group, and EM MN group (Fig. 6, A and D). Furthermore, the inhibitory effect of EM/Man MNs and EM MNs on free radicals was significantly higher than that of the MN group, while the antioxidant capacity of EM/Man MNs was superior to that of EM MNs (Fig. 6C). Subsequently, changes in mitochondrial status were further evaluated. Reduced JC-1 aggregate/monomer ratio under ROS-induced oxidative stress indicated mitochondrial depolarization and dysfunction, whereas EM MNs and EM/Man MNs were able to restore the aggregate/monomer ratio (Fig. 6, B and E). We also evaluated the levels of adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) metabolites, and the results showed a significant increase in ATP expression in EMM MN–treated RAW264.7 cells compared to the H2O2 group, MN group, and EM MN group (Fig. 6F).

The real-time extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) was used to calculate the glycolytic parameters of macrophages. The results showed that the glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve of cells in the EM/Man MN group were significantly lower than those in the lipopolysaccharide (LPS), MN, and EM MN groups. In contrast, in the EM MN group, only glycolytic and glycolytic capacities were significantly lower than in the LPS group (Fig. 6G and fig. S8A). Meanwhile, the real-time oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was used to calculate the OXPHOS parameters of macrophages. Although the maximal respiration and ATP production of cells in the EM MNs + LPS and EM/Man MNs + LPS groups were higher than those in the LPS and MN + LPS groups, basal respiration, maximal respiration, and ATP production of cells in the EM/Man MNs + LPS group were significantly higher than those in the other groups (Fig. 6H and fig. S8B). Therefore, EM/Man MNs showed the most effective role in promoting OXPHOS metabolism of macrophages (Fig. 6H and fig. S8B). Subsequently, on the basis of the regulation of EM/Man MNs on macrophage mitochondrial metabolism, we further investigated the effect of EM/Man MNs on macrophage polarization. Fluorescence staining images of CD206 (M2 macrophage marker) and iNOS (M1 macrophage marker) and corresponding expression heatmaps showed that EM MN and EM/Man MN extracts promoted macrophage polarization from a proinflammatory state to a proregenerative phenotype (Fig. 6, I and J). In addition, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis of macrophages treated with EM/Man MNs demonstrated significant down-regulation of proinflammatory mediators (iNOS and TNF-α) concomitant with up-regulation of anti-inflammatory effectors (CD206 and IL-10) (fig. S9, A to D), indicating reprogramming toward an M2-like anti-inflammatory phenotype. The above results indicate that EM/Man MNs may be able to remove ROS, maintain mitochondrial homeostasis, increase basal respiration, maximal respiration, and ATP production of macrophages, promote the reprogramming of macrophage cell metabolism from glycolysis to mitochondrial OXPHOS, and promote M2 polarization.

In vitro angiogenesis of MN patches

To evaluate the in vitro angiogenic effects of MNs, in this study, we incubated HUVECs with conditioned medium harvested from RAW cultured on MN extracts to verify whether relevant angiogenic factors secreted by macrophages could effectively stimulate in vitro angiogenesis (fig. S10A). First, in the scratch experiment, the EM MN and EM/Man MN groups showed a smaller scratch area than the control group and MN group after 24 and 48 hours (fig. S10B). Quantitative analysis of scratch closure rates confirmed these findings. The EM/Man MN group showed better performance in wound healing compared to the EM MN group (fig. S10C). Furthermore, in the transwell experiment of HUVECs after coculture, the EM MN and EM/Man MN groups showed a more significant number of migrated cells and denser cell density compared to the control group and MN group (fig. S10D). Quantitative analysis of the number of migrating cells confirmed these results (fig. S10E). Moreover, in the HUVEC tube formation experiment, fluorescence images showed that the EM MN and EM/Man MN groups showed more dense branching points than the control group and MN group (fig. S10F). Quantitative analysis of the number of connected meshes also confirms these results (fig. S10G). In addition, in the present study, the angiogenesis marker CD31 was used to determine the effect of MN patches on the angiogenesis of HUVECs. The fluorescence images showed that cells in the EM/Man MN group expressed stronger CD31 fluorescence intensity compared with other groups, and the quantitative analysis of CD31 fluorescence intensity also confirmed these results (fig. S10, H and I), indicating excellent performance in inducing angiogenesis. Overall, these results suggest that the EM/Man MN patch has excellent proangiogenic potential.

In vitro antibacterial properties of MN patches

We also examined the antibacterial ability of different MN patches. Plate clone images of S. aureus and Escherichia coli after coculture with MNs showed that the MN, EM MN, and EM/Man MN groups all showed significant antibacterial performance and lower bacterial viability compared with the control group (fig. S11, A and B), in which the EM MN and EM/Man MN groups showed better antibacterial performance compared with the MN group (fig. S11, C and D). SEM images showed that the bacteria on the surface of MN, EM MN, and EM/Man MN groups all showed obvious abnormal morphology, such as depression and shrinkage (red part), compared with the control group (fig. S11, A and B). This indicates that they have substantial antibacterial properties, and the addition of NPs further enhances the antibacterial properties. Similarly, live/dead staining images of bacteria coincubated with MNs showed a large amount of red fluorescence (dead bacteria) and a small amount of green fluorescence (surviving bacteria) in the MN, EM MN, and EM/Man MN groups, with the most significant in the EM/Man MN group (fig. S11, F and G). The fluorescence semiquantitative analysis results showed similar results (fig. S11E).

In vivo therapeutic effect of MN patch on diabetic infection wounds

To evaluate the antibacterial and prohealing abilities of EM/Man MNs, we introduced S. aureus into streptozotocin-induced dorsal wounds in diabetic mice for 2 days to form a diabetic chronic wound model. The mice were randomly divided into four groups—control group, MN group, EM MN group, and EM/Man MN group. Different groups of wounds were measured and photographed on days 0, 5, 10, and 15 (Fig. 7A). As shown in the Fig. 7 (B and C), wounds treated in the EM/Man MN group showed accelerated wound closure, and wound area heatmaps showed that the EM/Man MN group showed the fastest wound contraction at each indicated time point (Fig. 7D). Quantitatively, the average residual wound area of the EM/Man MN group was 38.53 and 1% on days 10 and 15 after treatment, which was significantly lower than that of the control group (71.70 and 37.33%, respectively), the MN group (65.65 and 28.67%, respectively), and the EM/Man MN group (49.16 and 11%, respectively) (Fig. 7E). DISCUSSION

Diabetic chronic wound healing presents a major challenge to global healthcare systems, with substantial evidence indicating that diabetes adversely affects wound healing through prolonged inflammation, impaired angiogenesis, delayed healing, and increased susceptibility to infection. Persistent inflammation is recognized as a critical factor contributing to stalled or impaired wound healing (44). Modulating the excessive inflammatory phase to transition into a normal healing phase may facilitate optimal wound recovery (7). Polarization of macrophages from M1 (proinflammatory) to M2 (anti-inflammatory) is essential to advance the wound healing phase from inflammation to regeneration (45). Several recent studies have highlighted that proinflammatory macrophage-derived iNOS-induced mitochondrial dysfunction impairs reprogramming plasticity, suggesting that mitochondrial energy metabolism plays a key role in immunomodulation and redox homeostasis (46, 47). To achieve ideal macrophage reprogramming, anti-inflammatory induction alone is not enough, and the key is to restore normal mitochondrial energy metabolism. In this study, we report the design, synthesis, and characterization of EM/Man MNs, an MN patch engineered with cascade-targeting capabilities for macrophage mitochondria, which reprograms macrophage metabolism to promote M2 polarization. The modified MN patch also exhibits rapid antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, proangiogenic, and scar-inhibiting properties, providing a promising strategy for advancing the management of chronic wounds in diabetes.

Recent studies have explored several strategies to treat chronic wounds by targeting the inflammatory microenvironment (48–50); however, research focusing on sequentially targeting macrophages and intracellular mitochondria remains in its infancy. Over the past few decades, numerous studies have leveraged cell membrane engineering, vesicles, peptide modifications, and metal ions to achieve targeted therapeutic effects with enhanced efficiency (51–54). However, these cellular engineering approaches are often limited by issues such as immunogenicity, complex fabrication processes, and susceptibility to enzymatic degradation, which hinder their practical application. Furthermore, the inherent biotoxicity of metal ions restricts their broader utilization in biomedical fields. EGCG, a natural polyphenol with excellent biocompatibility, has a polyphenolic hydroxyl structure that confers mitochondria-targeting potential, making it a promising candidate for tissue repair (55). Similarly, metformin has shown considerable potential in anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory applications (20), and multiple studies have demonstrated metformin’s potential in wound repair by maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, reprogramming metabolism, and improving the inflammatory microenvironment (21, 56, 57). In addition, Man, which contains d-mannose residues, can specifically target Man receptors abundantly expressed on macrophages in inflammatory sites, making it an attractive candidate for targeted delivery (24). We form a cascade targeting effect through the combination of mannan and EGCG, which can accurately deliver NPs to mitochondria in macrophages. As a targeting ligand, mannan can specifically recognize Man receptors on the surface of macrophages, thus achieving cellular-level targeting. EGCG further targets mitochondria to ensure that drugs can directly act on the metabolic centers of macrophages. This cascade targeting strategy not only improves the accuracy of drug delivery but also reduces the potential side effects caused by nonspecific distribution. Our results suggest that this targeting cascade strategy improves the efficiency of targeting macrophages by about 2.5 times. This cascade-based approach, which precisely targets mitochondria within host cells, holds great potential for regulating cellular metabolism and effectively modulating immune function.

Given the close relationship between mitochondrial function and macrophage phenotype and function (8), we hypothesize that EM/Man NPs, composed of EGCG, metformin, and mannan, can serve as an immunomodulatory biomaterial for diabetic chronic wounds. This study aims to develop a innovative therapeutic strategy that leverages the synergistic effects of these components to precisely regulate mitochondrial function and macrophage polarization, thereby promoting effective wound healing.

The experimental findings demonstrate that we successfully synthesized EM NPs through a one-pot reaction using EGCG and metformin, followed by surface modification with mannan to form EM/Man NPs. These NPs were uniformly incorporated into the core structure of core-shell MNs to fabricate EM/Man MNs. For the sake of biosafety, we selected QACS and EGCG as the main components of antibacterial. On the basis of their powerful antibacterial properties, a large number of research reports have been designed as multifunctional hydrogel dressings, implant scaffolds, nanopreparations, etc. (58, 59). However, their antibacterial efficiency is mostly removed too quickly, and limited drug penetration is restricted, which makes their antibacterial effect greatly reduced. In this study, the physical penetration ability of MNs combined with the QACS to attract bacteria and the antibacterial ability of EGCG can eradicate the bacteria hidden deep in the wound and achieve the effect of eradicating bacteria. Our study is consistent with previous studies. The results of in vitro antibacterial experiments and in vivo antibacterial experiments on chronic infection in diabetic mice showed that EM/Man MNs achieved about 90% antibacterial effect.

The successful synthesis of EM/Man NPs was confirmed by TEM, SEM, and FTIR in this study, while live/dead staining and CCK-8 assays demonstrated the excellent cytocompatibility of the MNs. To further investigate the cascade-targeting capability toward macrophages, our data revealed that the internalization of EM/Man NPs by macrophages was approximately 2.5 times higher than that of EM NPs. Bioimaging further indicated that EM/Man NPs were more readily internalized by macrophages, with the internalized NPs predominantly localized around mitochondria, demonstrating their cascade-targeting ability to mitochondria within macrophages. Subsequently, we found that incubation with EM/Man MN extract significantly reduced ROS production, stabilized mitochondrial membrane potential, and increased ATP generation in macrophages, suggesting that EM/Man MNs enhance mitochondrial function. Moreover, the in vitro experimental results demonstrate that incubation with EM/Man MN extract substantially enhanced the levels of tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle metabolites in macrophages while concurrently reducing glycolytic metabolic activity, indicating a shift toward OXPHOS and improved mitochondrial function. Recent studies have highlighted mitochondria as signaling organelles that dynamically regulate cell-specific phenotypic processes (60–62). Consistent with previous findings, immunofluorescence results showed increased CD206 expression and decreased iNOS expression after incubation with EM/Man MN extract, promoting the polarization of macrophages from the M1 to the M2 phenotype. In summary, our findings demonstrate that EM/Man MNs cascade target mitochondria within macrophages, modulate glycolysis and TCA cycle metabolism, and potentially promote M2 macrophage polarization.

Furthermore, previous studies have shown that both metformin and EGCG can modulate these signaling pathways, including the AMPK, NF-κB, and IL-17 pathways (63–67), to promote tissue repair. Notably, PGC-1α, a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, can be induced by AMPK (68, 69). AMPK enhances mitochondrial biogenesis by activating SIRT1, which subsequently deacetylates PGC-1α (70, 71). Among the downstream transcription factors of PGC-1α, mitochondrial transcription factor A (mtTFA) has been identified as a critical regulator of mitochondrial DNA transcription and replication (72). To validate the therapeutic efficacy of EM/Man MNs on chronic wound healing and elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we established a diabetic infectious skin defect model. Treatment with EM/Man MNs significantly accelerated wound closure, with the mean residual wound area on day 10 and day 15 posttreatment reduced to 38.53 and 1%, respectively, in the EM/Man MN group, markedly lower than that of the control group (71.70 and 37.33%, respectively). Immunofluorescence staining for AMPK, SIRT1, PGC-1α, and mtTFA revealed that the average fluorescence intensity in the EM/Man MN group was significantly higher than in other groups. Consistent with in vitro findings, immunofluorescence staining for CD206 and iNOS on day 10 revealed a pronounced increase in M2 marker expression and a decrease in M1 marker expression in the EM/Man MN group compared to other groups. Transcriptome sequencing further uncovered the potential mechanism, suggesting that EM/Man MNs may enhance mitochondrial electron transport, up-regulate mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV, and augment glucose transmembrane transporter activity, thereby promoting OXPHOS and metabolic reprogramming in macrophages, which, in turn, regulates macrophage polarization. In addition, KEGG enrichment analysis demonstrated up-regulation of the AMPK pathway and down-regulation of inflammation-related signaling pathways, including IL-17, NF-κB, and TNF signaling pathways, in the EM/Man MN–treated diabetic chronic wounds. Accumulating evidence confirms that the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway is closely associated with macrophage polarization, immune regulation, and the reduction of scar formation (73–75). Macrophages play a pivotal role in regulating IL-17–mediated redox dynamics: M1 macrophages amplify T helper 17 cell (TH17 cell) responses through the secretion of IL-23/IL-1β, thereby disrupting redox balance, exacerbating inflammation, and establishing a self-sustaining cycle (76–78). In summary, EM/Man MNs are likely to promote mitochondrial biogenesis via the AMPK/SIRT1/PGC-1α signaling pathway, drive OXPHOS-mediated metabolic reprogramming in macrophages, synergistically inhibit the IL-17 and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways, and induce M2 phenotypic polarization of macrophages. Ultimately, this reverses the inflammatory microenvironment and accelerates the healing of chronic wounds.

In addition, persistent inflammation and dysregulated immune modulation not only contribute to delayed wound healing but are also closely associated with scar formation, impairing functional recovery (79, 80). Recent studies have highlighted various strategies to inhibit scar formation, including reducing sustained inflammatory responses, preventing infection, and leveraging the mechanical regulation of MNs, which have shown promising results in treating HSs (81–84). In our study, EM/Man MNs effectively delivered EM/Man NPs into deeper tissues, modulating the inflammatory microenvironment and preventing bacterial infection. Wounds treated with this approach exhibited a substantial reduction in scar thickness and a decreased ratio of type I/III collagen. Previous studies have demonstrated that inflammatory pathways, such as the IL-17 and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways, play critical roles in minimizing scar formation (85–87). Consistent with these findings, transcriptome sequencing results revealed that EM/Man MNs down-regulated the IL-17 and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways, thereby suppressing scar formation.

In this study, we successfully developed a detachable core-shell MN patch, termed EM/Man MNs, which integrates rapid antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and proangiogenic properties to effectively promote the healing of diabetic chronic wounds while minimizing scar formation. During the early inflammatory phase, the EM/Man MNs rapidly eradicated bacteria upon application to the wound. Subsequently, the core structure of the MNs responsively degraded in the highly oxidative wound environment, releasing EM/Man NPs that cascadedly targeted mitochondrial metabolism. This process facilitated a metabolic shift in macrophages from glycolysis to mitochondrial OXPHOS, thereby reprogramming macrophages toward an M2 phenotype. The EM/Man MN patch not only accelerated the healing of chronically infected wounds in diabetic mice but also reduced hypertrophic scarring in a rabbit ear scar model. Overall, our work presents a innovative strategy for cascade-targeting macrophage mitochondria to exert immunomodulatory functions through AMPK-mediated mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming, thereby accelerating all phases of wound healing. This study provides insights into the design of advanced bioenergetic materials for tissue engineering and regenerative medicine.

Synthesis of the EM/Man NPs

Synthesis of NPs: Briefly, 75 mg of EGCG was stirred sufficiently to dissolve in 25 ml of aqueous solution at room temperature, then 55 mg of Met was added and stirred for 10 min, and then 250 μl of sodium hydroxide (1 mM) was added to the reaction solution. After stirring the reaction overnight, EM NPs were obtained by centrifugation (12,000 rpm for 5 min). For further preparation of EM/Man NPs, the obtained EM NP particles were added to aqueous Man solution (2.5 mg/ml and 20 ml) and further stirred for 1 hour, followed by centrifugation to collect EM/Man NPs. All particles were washed three times with ultrapure water. Last, EM NPs or EM/Man NPs were obtained by vacuum drying.

Characterization of the physical and chemical properties of EM/Man NPs

The surface structure of EM/Man, EM/Man NPs, was observed by field-emission SEM (FEI Nova NanoSEM 450), and the internal structure and elemental composition of EM/Man, EM/Man NPs, were observed by projection electron microscope and energy analysis. The zeta potential and particle size of the EM/Man, EM/Man NPs, were measured using an NP size and zeta potential analyzer (DLS, Malvern Ceramics Co. Ltd., Nano ZS90). XPS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, K-Alpha, USA) was used for the analysis. The synthesis mechanism of NPs was analyzed by ESI-MS. FTIR (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 6700) was used to determine the functional groups of the NPs in the range of 500 to 4000 cm−1.

Selective cellular uptake and mitochondrial targeting experiments of EM/Man NPs

RAW264.7 cells were cultured and incubated with DiD-labeled EM NPs and EM/Man NPs (100 μg/ml), whereas HUVECs were incubated with DiD-labeled EM/Man NPs, both at 37°C for 0.5 and 2 hours. Subsequently, the nuclei of RAW264.7 cells and HUVECs were stained with DAPI (Solarbio Science & Technology Co. Ltd., China) for 10 min at room temperature. Fluorescence microscopy was then used (Olympus, IX73, Japan) to capture fluorescence images. Fluorescence quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ. The colocalization of NPs and macrophage mitochondria was investigated using TEM. Initially, NPs were coincubated with macrophages at a concentration of 100 μg/ml for 6 hours. After the coincubation period, the cells were centrifuged and subsequently fixed overnight with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4°C in preparation for the use of TEM analysis, which was performed using a JEOL JEM-1400Plus instrument from Japan. For mitochondrial-targeted localization, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was used to evaluate mitochondrial colocalization of different DiI-labeled NPs in macrophages and HUVECs. Macrophages were seeded in confocal glass-bottomed dishes and treated with free DiI, DiI-labeled EM NPs, and DiI-labeled EM/Man NPs for 2 hours. After washing three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the cells were stained with MitoTracker Green and DAPI dye at 37°C for 30 min. Last, the stained cells were observed with CLSM.

Preparation of EM/Man MN patch

A total of 1.5 g of 4-(bromomethyl)phenylboronic acid was dissolved in 60 ml of DMF at 60°C, followed by 0.15 g of N,N,N′,N′-tetramethyl-1,3-propanediamine added to the mixture and stirred for 24 hours. Last, the pale yellow solution was precipitated in THF (150 ml), and the resulting precipitate was washed three times with THF and then freeze dried to obtain pure TS-PBA. To prepare EM/Man MN patches with a core-shell structure, QACS (2%) was first dissolved in deionized water, and then 2 ml of the solution was poured into the polydimethylsiloxane mold with vacuum to allow the solution to fill to the MN tip. Then, shells of EM/Man MNs were obtained after air drying overnight at room temperature. PVA (2%) and TS-PBA (0.5%) were first dissolved in deionized water, and then EM/Man NPs or EM NPs were added to prepare a mixture solution with a concentration of 500 μg/ml. A total of 1.5 ml of the mixture was poured into the MN shell structure described above and placed under vacuum to fill the cavity to form the core structure. After drying at room temperature overnight, the bottom of the MNs was adhered with 3M tape, and the MNs were demolded as a backing to lastly obtain EM/Man MNs.

Characterization of the physicochemical properties of the EM/Man MN patch

The morphological structure of EM/Man MNs was photographed by SEM (Nova NanoSEM 450) and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, Olympus, FV1000). To evaluate the mechanical properties of the MNs, a square patch with a substrate of 8.5 mm and a height of 0.6 mm was prepared and tested, and the sample was compressed at a moving speed of 0.2 mm/min by an electronic general testing machine (Zhongshan Mester Industrial Co. Ltd, CMT6104). In a skin puncture experiment, the skin of normal rats was punctured with an MN, local photographs of the skin were taken, and the tissue was stained with H&E. DiD-labeled MNs were inserted into full-thickness mouse skin; the tissues were cryosectioned and examined via fluorescence microscopy to evaluate the distribution of NPs within the tissue. FTIR was used to determine the functional groups of each group of MNs in the range of 500 to 4000 cm−1.

Release of NPs in MNs: Two MNs were placed in 10 ml of PBS containing H2O2 solution of different concentrations (0.5 and 1 mM) and incubated at 37°C with constant temperature stirring. At predetermined time points, 1 ml of supernatant was collected, and 1 ml of PBS was added to maintain the volume. In addition, the absorbance of the supernatant at different time points was determined using a UV spectrometer (Shanghai Mipute Instrument Co. Ltd., UV-3100 instrument). From the calibration curves derived from the known concentrations of EM/Man NPs and their corresponding absorbance values, the release of NPs by MNs under different conditions was calculated.

Biocompatibility of EM/Man MN patch

The MN extract was prepared by soaking one MN patch in 2 ml of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) and stirring at a constant temperature (37°C) for 24 hours. Then, the extracts were filtered, disinfected, and lastly prepared into MN extracts for cell experiments. The in vitro biocompatibility of this biomaterial was assessed by the CCK-8 method and live/dead cell staining. A 96-well plate was seeded at a density of 2 × 103 L929 cells per well. Cell cultures were performed using standard complete DMEM medium, as well as conditioned medium of MN extracts. At 1, 3, and 5 days, the RGR of cells was calculated by measuring the absorbance [optical density (OD) value] of the cell supernatant, while staining was performed with a live & dead cell staining reagent.

Assessment of antioxidant capacity

Ability of NPs to scavenge ROS: Using the DPPH method, 500 μl of DPPH free radical working solution (0.1 mM) was mixed with 100 μl of extracts of MNs, EM MNs, and EM/Man MNs, respectively (the same method as above). The mixture was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. The absorbance was measured with a UV spectrophotometer at 517 nm. The formula for determining the free radical inhibition rate is as follows: inhibition rate (%) = (1 – OD value of sample group/OD value of control group) × 100%.

Mitochondrial function

mtROS was simultaneously detected using the MitoSOX assay kit. Macrophages were incubated with MN, EM MN, and EMM MN extracts for 24 hours and then replaced with H2O2 (0.3 mM) for 30 min. After washing the cells with PBS, the staining working solution was added, and the cells were incubated for 20 min in the dark and then observed by a microscope. The ImageJ software was used to quantitatively analyze the fluorescence intensity.

Macrophage ATP production was measured using an ATP assay kit (Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology). The incubated cells were lysed at 12,000g at 4°C. Cell lysates were extracted by centrifugation for 4 min. A total of 30 μl of the supernatant was added to a 96-well plate containing 100 μl of ATP working solution. The absorbance of the resulting solution was then measured using a microplate reader. Last, ATP concentration was calculated from the OD value.

Meanwhile, 264.7 cells were incubated with DMEM complete medium containing the extract of MNs, EM MNs, and EM/Man MNs for 24 hours and then with 0.3 mM H2O2 for 30 min. After the supernatant was aspirated and the incubated cells were washed with PBS, they were incubated with the JC-1 staining working solution at 37°C for 20 min. Subsequently, the cells were washed twice with JC-1 dilution buffer and observed with a fluorescence microscope. The ratio of red/green fluorescence intensity was calculated using the ImageJ software.

Metabolic status of macrophage mitochondria

To assess the mitochondrial respiratory and glycolytic activity of macrophages, the mitochondrial OCRs and ECARs were measured using the XF24 Seahorse device (Agilent Technologies). First, cells were incubated in Seahorse XF assay medium for 60 min. For OCR analysis, OM, FCCP, and rotenone/antimycin (R/A) were sequentially injected during real-time OCR measurements. The respiratory parameters were calculated as follows: basal respiration = last rate measurement before first injection − minimum rate measurement after rotenone/antimycin A (R/AA) injection; maximum respiration = maximum rate measurement after FCCP injection − minimum rate measurement after R/A injection; and ATP production = final rate measurement before OM injection − minimum rate measurement after OM injection. For ECAR analysis, glucose, OM, and 2-DG were sequentially injected during real-time measurement of ECAR. Glycolytic respiration parameters were calculated as follows: glycolysis = maximum ECAR value before OM treatment − last ECAR value before glucose injection; glycolytic capacity = maximum ECAR value after OM injection − last ECAR value before glucose injection; and glycolytic reserve = glycolytic capacity − glycolysis.

In vitro macrophage polarization experiments

The original 264.7 cells were cultured in DMEM complete medium supplemented with LPS (100 ng/ml) for 24 hours to induce an inflammatory microenvironment. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with conditioned medium containing the biomaterial extract, and the cells were incubated for an additional 48 hours. After treatment, RAW264.7 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 10 min, permeated with 0.5% Triton X-100, and blocked with 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Cells were then incubated overnight with anti-CD206 polyclonal antibody, followed by 1 hour in the dark with the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibody. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (catalog number: C1005, Shanghai Biotechnology, China). Fluorescence microscopy was used for cell visualization and imaging. Similarly, immunofluorescence staining of iNOS was performed using the iNOS polyclonal antibody. Last, the fluorescence intensity of CD206 and iNOS was quantified using the ImageJ software. The expression level of related genes in macrophage cells was detected by qPCR. The RNA of each group was extracted by the HiPure Total RNA Trace Kit of Megan Company (China), and the expression of iNOS, CD206, IL-10, and TNF-α genes was analyzed by real-time qPCR. All results were standardized with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) gene as internal reference. The primer sequences used in this experiment are as follows: GAPDH, TGTGTCCGTCGTGGATCTGA (forward) and TTGCTGTTGAAGTCGCAGGAG (reverse); iNOS, GTTCTCAGCCCAACAATACAAGA (forward) and GTGGACGGGTCGATGTCAC (reverse); CD206, CTCTGTTCAGCTATTGGACGC (forward) and CGGAATTTCTGGGATTCAGCTTC (reverse); TNF-α, GACGTGGAACTGGCAGAAGAG (forward) and TTGGTGGTTTGTGAGTGTGAG (reverse); IL-10, GAGAAGCATGGCCCAGAAATC (forward) and GAGAAATCGATGACAGCGCC (reverse).

Proangiogenic properties of EM/Man MN patch

In the cell scratch assay, HUVECs were seeded into 6-well plates. When the cell density reached ~90%, a straight line was drawn in the center of each well with a 1-ml pipette tip to create a scratch. Subsequently, the medium in each well was replaced with fresh DMEM complete medium containing the biomaterial extract, and then the coculture was continued for 24 and 48 hours. Cell migration and proliferation were recorded and photographed at 0, 24, and 48 hours, respectively, and the closure of the scratch gap was quantified using the ImageJ software.

In the transwell migration assay, 100 μl of a suspension of HUVECs (containing 1 × 104 cells) was added to DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (P/S), followed by the upper lumen of a transwell inserts (Corning, USA). The transwell chambers were then placed into the wells of a 24-well plate containing 500 μl of each set of MN extracts. Standard complete medium served as a control group. After incubation at 5% CO2 and 37°C for 24 hours, cells were fixed, stained with crystal violet solution (Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology, China), and photographed to quantify the number of migrating cells.

In vitro tube formation assay: Matrigel containing growth factors (BD Biosciences, USA) was portioned at 100 μl per well into each well of a 24-well plate and set at 37°C for 30 min. Subsequently, HUVECs were seeded onto the Matrigel at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well, and extracts of MNs were separately added in serum-free DMEM medium. Serum-free medium served as the control group. After 6 hours of culture and after staining the cells with a live & dead cell staining, the cell morphology was observed using a fluorescence microscope, and the number of branching points in the Matrigel was quantified using the ImageJ software.

The HUVECs were coincubated with conditioned medium containing the MN extracts of each group for 48 hours. After treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and blocked with 10% BSA. The cells were then incubated overnight with anti-31 polyclonal antibody and then with the corresponding fluorescent secondary antibody in the dark for 1 hour. Nuclei were stained with DAPI and imaged by fluorescence microscopy. Last, the fluorescence intensity of CD31 was quantitatively analyzed using the ImageJ software, and then the relative fluorescence intensity was normalized on the basis of the control group.

In vitro antibacterial properties of EM/Man MN patch

To assess antibacterial efficacy, the UV-sterilized MN patch was immersed in centrifuge tubes containing 3 ml of bacterial culture medium and inoculated with 100 μl of S. aureus or E. coli [106 colony-forming units (CFU)/ml], and then the cultures were continuously incubated for 6 hours. Medium alone was used as the control group. After incubation, the bacterial medium was diluted to a concentration of 1 × 10−5. Subsequently, diluted bacterial medium was inoculated onto agar plates, and after 20 hours of incubation, agar plates were photographed, and colonies were counted. SEM was used to observe the morphology of bacteria after intervention. For SEM observations, bacteria from each group underwent gradient alcohol dehydration (50, 70, 80, 90, 95, and 100%), followed by freeze drying. The bacteria were stained with live and dead bacteria staining, and the survival status of the bacteria was observed. The red and green fluorescence intensity was quantified by the ImageJ software.

In vivo wound healing test of diabetic chronic infection with EM/Man MN patch

Chronic wound model of diabetic S. aureus infection: Forty 10-week-old male BKS-DB mice (45 g) were ordered and kept in our animal center for 3 to 5 days. Each mouse was then anesthetized to create a full thickness skin defect (8 mm in diameter), and each wound was injected with 10 μl of S. aureus (1 × 107 CFU/ml) and left for an additional 2 days. After that, the mice were randomly divided into four groups, namely, control, MN, EM MN, and EM/Man MN groups; the wounds were treated with different MNs; and the group without any treatment was the control group. After surgery, each mouse was individually housed in a cage and photographed at each indicated time point to document changes during wound contraction. After 5 days of treatment, the wound exudates of each group were collected with inoculation loops and cultured on agar plates. After 24 hours, the number of colonies was recorded by photographs. Wound tissue samples were taken at 5, 10, and 15 days and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde.

In vivo EM/Man MN patches inhibit hypertrophic scarring

New Zealand white male rabbits (3 to 4 months) were anesthetized by intravenous injection of pentobarbital (30 mg/kg). Four wounds per ear were then made using an 8-mm biopsy punch. Then, the wounds were treated with MNs, EM MNs, and EM/Man MNs, respectively. The group that does not receive any treatment is the control group. Wounds were photographed on days 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25, and scars were collected for H&E staining, Masson trichrome staining, and sirius red staining on day 25. Last, SEI, collagen percentage, and type I/III collagen ratio were analyzed by ImageJ.

Histology and immunofluorescence staining

For the healing of diabetic-infected wounds, wound tissues from mice on days 5, 10, and 15 were collected, respectively. First, it was dehydrated with an alcohol gradient, then embedded with paraffin, and lastly cut into 5-μm sections for H&E staining and Masson trichrome staining. For immunofluorescence staining, all slides were dewaxed using day-10 specimens and rehydrated in a gradient, followed by 5 min of antigen recovery in heated citrate buffer [citrate buffer (pH 6.0)]. Second, 5% BSA solution was used to block nonspecific antibody binding. Third, the slides were incubated with the primary antibody at 4°C overnight and with the secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 hour. Last, the slides were stained with DAPI for 5 min and imaged by fluorescence microscopy. The relative fluorescence intensities of CD206, iNOS, AMPK, PGC1α, TFAM, CD31, and SIRT1 were analyzed using ImageJ. The relative fluorescence intensity was then normalized on the basis of the control group. Wound tissues were collected and treated with enzymes to produce single cell suspensions, and the percentages of M1 and M2 were detected by flow cytometry after fluorescent antibody staining. Wound tissues were harvested, and intracellular levels of cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-10, IL-6, and IL-1β were detected using ELISA kits purchased from Solarbio Science & Technology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

For the inhibition of rabbit ear scar formation experiment, rabbit ear scar tissue was collected on day 25. Similar to the appeal method, they were dehydrated with an alcohol gradient, then embedded with paraffin, and lastly cut into sections for H&E staining, Masson trichrome staining, and sirius red staining. SEI, collagen percentage, and type I/III collagen ratio were analyzed by ImageJ.

Transcriptome sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA purity and quantification were evaluated using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). RNA integrity was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Then, the libraries were constructed using VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The transcriptome sequencing and analysis were conducted by OE Biotech Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, and 150–base pair paired-end reads were generated. Raw reads of fastq format were firstly processed using fastp, and the low-quality reads were removed to obtain the clean reads. The clean reads were mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2. Fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped fragments (FPKM) of each gene was calculated, and the read counts of each gene were obtained by HTSeq-count. Principal components analysis was performed using R (v 3.2.0) to evaluate the biological duplication of samples.

On the basis of the hypergeometric distribution, GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs were performed to screen the significant enriched term using R (v 3.2.0), respectively. R (v 3.2.0) was used to draw the column diagram, the chord diagram, and bubble diagram of the significant enrichment term.

GSEA was performed using GSEA software. The analysis was used a predefined gene set, and the genes were ranked according to the degree of differential expression in the two types of samples. Then, it is tested whether the predefined gene set was enriched at the top or bottom of the ranking list.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was conducted using the GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. The t test was used to assess differences between two independent sample groups. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed for comparisons among multiple groups, followed by the Tukey’s multiple comparison test. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
