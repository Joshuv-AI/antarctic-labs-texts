Immortal time bias from conditioning on future events in Parkinson’s disease survival analysis
Authors: Jakob Hansen Viuff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis comment highlights potential immortal time bias in Brakedal et al.’s study of Parkinson’s disease (PD) survival. Because PD status and exclusion criteria rely on future prescription patterns, individuals classified as having PD are, by design, unable to die during early follow-up. This likely inflates survival estimates, particularly among the oldest patients. Aligning eligibility criteria with the start of follow-up and avoiding conditioning on future events would reduce this bias in future research.Subject terms: Diseases, Neurology, Neuroscience

arising from B. Brakedal et al. npj Parkinson’s Disease 10.1038/s41531-022-00280-4 (2022)

I read the paper by Brakedal et al.1 with great interest. The study provides valuable insights into the epidemiology of Parkinson’s Disease (PD) and has been widely cited. However, several methodological concerns related to immortal time bias warrant attention.

Immortal time bias occurs when, by design, individuals cannot experience the event of interest during a specific period of follow-up. For example, if exposure status at baseline (e.g., at hospital discharge) is defined using future information (e.g., a prescription filled one week after discharge), the individual must have survived until this information is recorded (e.g., at least one week since discharge). Consequently, those classified as exposed cannot die during the interval between baseline and exposure ascertainment2.

In the study by Brakedal et al., PD cases are defined as individuals with at least three consecutive prescriptions of anti-PD medication, each at least 30 days apart, with the period from the first to the last prescription being at least 180 days. However, the time of PD diagnosis (i.e., start of follow-up) is set to the time of the first prescription. This may introduce immortal time bias, as individuals classified as having PD, by definition, cannot die within the first 180 days. In an extreme scenario, one could imagine an individual meeting the inclusion criteria with three prescriptions spaced one year apart, making them immortal for two years.

Additional exclusion criteria are applied for individuals younger than 80 years (the “>80 years” stated in the paper appears to be a typographical error and should read “<80 years”) at the time of the first anti-PD prescription. These individuals are excluded if they have less than one year of treatment duration and an off-treatment period exceeding two years (defined as the time between the latest prescription and death or end of follow-up). This exclusion again conditions on future information.

To illustrate its potential implications, consider two otherwise identical individuals who both receive three consecutive prescriptions and both have an off‑treatment period longer than two years. One individual is excluded because the treatment duration is shorter than one year, whereas the other is included because the treatment duration exceeds one year. Excluding individuals with shorter treatment durations could inflate survival estimates. Conversely, consider two otherwise identical individuals who differ only in their off‑treatment interval: one has an off‑treatment interval shorter than two years, while the other exceeds two years. In this case, excluding individuals with longer survival could deflate survival estimates. It is therefore possible that, in practice, this exclusion criterion does not introduce additional net bias. Nevertheless, these examples illustrate the broader methodological concern of conditioning on future events.

The immortal time bias is also visible in the Kaplan–Meier curves presented in the study (Brakedal et al., Fig. 3). During the first 180 days, there appeared to be no deaths in the PD group. The absence of deaths within six months among individuals aged over 90 is particularly striking. This is concerning, given the authors’ conclusion that survival among individuals aged 85 years and older with PD is comparable to that of the general population.

The authors briefly address one of these issues in the discussion, noting that excluding individuals with less than 180 days of treatment removes those who died shortly after beginning treatment, which is more likely to occur among the elderly. While this seems plausible, the impact of this restriction on those included as PD cases is not discussed.

Several approaches can be used to address immortal time bias. One option is to define the first prescription as the start of follow-up without relying on future treatment information, although this may increase exposure misclassification. Alternatively, conditional survival can be estimated using a landmark approach3. For example, one can define a landmark at 180 days after the first prescription and assess exposure status at that time point (i.e., fulfillment of the three‑prescription criterion). Survival can then be estimated conditional on having survived the first six months. The same conditional survival can be estimated in the general population, enabling a valid comparison. Each strategy involves trade-offs and targets a slightly different research question, but all avoid conditioning on future events.

While immortal time bias is present by design, its impact is likely to be modest at younger ages with low mortality. In contrast, among the oldest individuals, with a much higher mortality, the same immortal period may meaningfully inflate survival estimates, making this bias particularly relevant for conclusions drawn in these age groups. In this sense, being “immortal” for six months represents a considerably larger advantage at older ages.

In summary, the case definition and exclusion criteria applied in Brakedal et al. likely introduce immortal time bias, which may inflate survival estimates among individuals with PD. Aligning eligibility criteria with the start of follow-up is crucial to reduce this bias. Future studies should avoid conditioning on future events and consider alternative approaches.
