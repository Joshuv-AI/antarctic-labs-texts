Biomechanical comparison of suture and screw fixation in PCL avulsion with variable bone quality
Authors: Sorawut Thamyongkit, Pasit Komonsuwan, Benrita Jitaree, Panya Aroonjarattham, Nattawat Rattanapan, Chompunut Somtua, Nadhaporn Saengpetch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVarious fixation techniques, including screws and suture-based methods, are used for posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) avulsion fractures. This study compared the biomechanical effects of different suture configurations in simulated PCL avulsion fractures. Twenty cadaveric knees were assigned to four fixation groups: antegrade screw (A), 1 suture tape with 2 tunnels (B), 2 suture tapes with 2 tunnels (C), and 2 suture tapes with 3 tunnels (D). Biomechanical testing included cyclic loading and load-to-failure tests, with elongation, stiffness, yield load, and failure modes recorded. No significant differences were found between groups (p > 0.05). Mean elongation ranged from 10.1 ± 2.9 mm (B) to 21.9 ± 15.9 mm (D), while mean yield load varied from 375.6 ± 110.9 N (C) to 428.6 ± 197.6 N (B). Fragment breakage was common in Groups A and D, while suture cut-through occurred in Groups B and C. Across all groups, bone mineral density (BMD) significantly correlated with yield load (r = 0.511, p = 0.021) and elongation (r = -0.553, p = 0.0110.Suture-based techniques provided comparable biomechanical performance to screws, and increasing suture tapes or tunnels did not enhance fixation strength. Bone quality should be considered when planning fixation and rehabilitation.Keywords: PCL avulsion fracture, Suture fixation, Screw fixation, Biomechanics, Bone mineral density, Cadaveric kneesSubject terms: Anatomy, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

The posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) plays a crucial role in knee stability1. Displaced PCL avulsion fractures have been conventionally treated with open surgery using screw fixation, a technique that is well-supported in the literature for providing reliable mechanical strength2–4. With advancement in arthroscopic surgery, this minimally invasive approach has been increasingly applied to PCL surgery, reducing complications and promoting faster recovery for patients. The size of the avulsion fragment is a key factor in selecting the fixation method. While screw fixation provides strong fixation for larger fragments, it may risk fragmenting smaller pieces and thereby reduce overall fixation strength. In contrast, the suture cerclage technique can be applied to both large and small fragments, as well as comminuted fragments, by grasping the ligament directly. When applied correctly, this technique also provides compressive pressure between the fragment and the tibial bone bed, promoting healing and stable fixation5,6.

Various fixation techniques have been reported, including antegrade/retrograde screws, suture anchors, and high-strength sutures with cortical suspension buttons7–9. The lack of consensus on the optimal technique underscores the need for more biomechanical data to guide clinical decision-making. Previous biomechanical studies have investigated suture-based techniques. Comparative studies have shown that cortical suspension buttons and retrograde screw fixation offer structural properties comparable to conventional antegrade screw fixation, with the benefit of being minimally invasive10,11. Additionally, Eggers et al. demonstrated that the transtibial pullout suture technique is comparable to screw fixation, with the effectiveness of suture fixation strongly dependent on the suture material used12. However, the impact of the number of sutures and their configuration on biomechanical performance has not yet been explored.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the biomechanical properties of four fixation techniques: screw fixation, one suture tape with two tunnels, two suture tapes with two tunnels, and two suture tapes with three tunnels, in simulated posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) avulsion fractures using human cadaveric knee specimens. Additionally, we aimed to assess the influence of bone mineral density (BMD) on fixation strength and construct elongation. We hypothesized that the three suture-based fixation techniques would show no statistically significant difference in biomechanical properties compared to conventional antegrade screw fixation and that higher BMD would be associated with improved fixation performance.

Methods

Specimens

The study was a biomechanical analysis using human cadaveric knee specimens. Twenty fresh-frozen cadaveric knees were prepared for the testing. All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for the use of human cadaveric specimens. The average knee cadaveric specimen age was 68 ± 17.8 years. All specimens had been transected, approximately 20 cm proximal and distal to the joint line of the knee and stored at 20 °C. Prior to dissection, they were thawed for 24 h at room temperature. The knees were grossly inspected to ensure that the bone, soft tissue and all ligaments were intact before dissection. To isolate the biomechanical effect of the fixation technique, all surrounding soft tissues, including the joint capsule, ligaments, and menisci, were carefully removed, as these structures may influence joint stability and potentially confound the study results. The posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) was preserved. The specimens were consistently kept moist with 0.9% normalsaline to maintain tissue integrity.

To simulate a PCL avulsion fracture, a standardized bone fragment measuring 18 mm (length) × 21.5 mm (width) × 9.5 mm (depth) was created using an oscillating saw and osteotome13, encompassing the entire tibial PCL insertion area. This uniform fracture model ensured consistency across specimens and enabled reliable comparisons between fixation techniques.

Surgical technique

Twenty cadaveric knees were randomly assigned to four groups, each subjected to a different fixation technique (Fig. 1). The conventional antegrade screw technique was used as control group. The suture-based techniques for PCL avulsion fixation were modified to evaluated in the different of suture numbers and configurations. All surgical procedures were performed by a single experienced surgeon to ensure consistency in technique. All knots were tied using the same knot configuration. Although no specific force gauge was used, care was taken to replicate consistent hand-tension across specimens.

Four different fixation techniques: (a) Antegrade screw fixation, (b) Suture fixation with 1 suture tape and 2 tunnels, (c) Suture fixation with 2 suture tapes and 2 tunnels, and (d) Suture fixation with 2 suture tapes and 3 tunnels.

Group A: antegrade cannulated screw fixation

The PCL bone fragment was anatomically reduced and temporarily stabilized using a 1.25 mm guide wire positioned at a 45-degree angle from posterior to anterior. The guide wire was over-drilled with a 2.7 mm cannulated drill bit. Finally, the fragment was secured bi-cortically using a 4.0 mm long-threaded cannulated screw with a washer, which was inserted over the guide wire14. This method providing stable compressing fixation through the screw and washer construct.

Group B: suture fixation with 1 suture tape with 2 tunnels

In this technique, a PCL tibial drill guide and two 3.0 mm Kirschner pins were used to create two bone tunnels, starting from the anterior tibial cortex just medial to the tibial tubercle, extending to the medial and lateral borders of the PCL avulsion site (Fig. 2). The PCL was secured using a suture tape (FiberTape: Arthrex, Naples, FL) with a double half-hitch knot placed just above the avulsion fragment. The free ends of the sutures were then shuttled through the two bone tunnels to the anterior tibial cortex15. Once both sutures were passed through the tunnels, they were fixed to the anterior cortex of the tibia using a suture button (Dog Bone Button: Arthrex, Naples, FL), secured with six knots. Sutures were manually tensioned by a single experienced surgeon using direct traction, guided by visual and tactile assessment, to achieve anatomic reduction of the fragment. A standardized technique was followed to ensure consistent tension across all specimens.

(a) Tibial tunnels were created using a PCL tibial drill guide with two 3.0-mm Kirschner pins. (b) The PCL was secured with suture tape using a double half-hitch knot, and the free sutures were passed through the bone tunnels. (c) The sutures were secured at the anterior tibial cortex using a suture button.

Group C: suture fixation with 2 suture tapes with 2 tunnels

This technique is similar to that used in Group B but utilizes two suture tapes. Both pairs of free suture ends were passed through two bone tunnels, positioned at the medial and lateral borders of the PCL avulsion site. The sutures were then secured to the anterior tibial cortex using a suture button, secured with six knots.

Group D: suture fixation with 2 suture tapes with 3 tunnels

Similar to the technique in Group C, an additional bone tunnel was created from the anterior tibial cortex to the superior border of the PCL avulsion site. The PCL was grasped using two suture tapes. One pair of suture ends was passed through the additional bone tunnel, while the other pair was passed through the medial and lateral bone tunnels at the inferior borders of the PCL avulsion site. The sutures were then secured to the anterior tibial cortex using a suture button.

Demographic data

Bone mineral density (BMD)

The demographics characteristics including age, gender and BMD were collected for all specimens (Table 1). BMD was measure for all specimens in this study using Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). The DXA measurements were performed with a Hologic Horizon A device (Hologic Inc., Marlborough, MA, USA), and all scans were conducted by the same technician to ensure consistency. BMD values of proximal tibia regions were calculated using lumbar vertebrae acquisition protocol. Four rectangular regions of interest, each 25 pixels wide, were measured distally from the intercondylar eminence (Fig. 3). The average BMD for each specimen was calculated from these four regions and expressed in g/cm²16.

Biomechanical measurement

Biomechanical testing was conducted at room temperature, with moisture maintained using saline throughout specimen preparation and testing to prevent desiccation. Each specimen was mounted on a uniaxial testing machine (UTM - Cometech) equipped with a load cell (NTS LRK-20KN). The load cell used in this study has an accuracy of ± 0.5%, and the system was calibrated prior to testing to ensure measurement reliability.The tibia was positioned horizontally and secured with a custom-made metal clamp at the base of the apparatus, while the femur was oriented vertically and mounted in a custom fixture attached to the moving crosshead of the machine. Knee flexion was maintained at 90° using a goniometer, and the femur was secured in a custom-made clamp to ensure consistent alignment along the y-axis during potting. A vertical load was applied to the femur to simulate a posterior drawer force at 90° of knee flexion (Fig. 4). All specimens underwent cyclic loading followed by load-to-failure testing to assess the biomechanical properties of the fixation methods. The biomechanical parameters including stiffness, elongation, yield load, maximum load and mode of failure were recorded.

Setup showing a cadaveric knee mounted in a materials testing machine, positioned at 90 degrees of flexion. The tibia (T) and femur (F) are secured, with the arrow indicating the direction of the applied force.

Cyclic loading

Prior to the main cyclic loading test, each specimen was preconditioned with 10 cycles of loading, ranging from 0 to 10 N, at an elongation rate of 200 mm/min. After the preconditioning phase, the specimens were subjected to 500 cycles of loading between 10 N and 100 N, at a frequency of 1 Hz, maintaining the same elongation rate of 200 mm/min. This cyclic loading protocol was consistent with methods reported in previous studies10,12. During the cyclic testing, the number of survived cycles and the maximum elongation at 2, 20, 100, and 500 cycles were recorded and compared across all group. Elongation was measured as the tibial translation relative to the femur, and the value was reset to zero at the beginning of the load-to-failure test.

Load-to-failure

After completing the cyclic loading tests, the specimens were subjected to a load-to-failure test. This involved applying a continuous load at a displacement rate of 200 mm/min until the fixation construct failed. During this phase, key parameters including elongation, stiffness, yield load and maximum load were recorded. The failure mode for each specimen was also documented. Failure was defined as tibial translation relative to the femur exceeding 5 mmThis elongation threshold was used as a clinical indicator of potential construct laxity. Failure modes whether due to bone fragment breakage, suture elongation, suture rupture, or suture cut-through were observed and recorded during load-to-failure testing at the point of ultimate load. Failure mode was determined through direct visual observation. In cases with complex or combined failure characteristics, the predominant failure mechanism was recorded based on the initial event observed during construct failure.

Statistical analysis

The sample size of at least 5 specimens per group was determined based on a previous study10, providing 86% power to detect differences with a significance level of P < 0.05. Normality of continuous variables, including age, BMD, loading forces, stiffness, and elongation, was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As all variables were found to be normally distributed, one-way ANOVA was used to evaluate differences between the fixation techniques. Post hoc comparisons were performed using the Holm–Sídák test to identify significant differences between groups. The null hypothesis for each pairwise comparison was that there was no statistically significant difference between the fixation groups. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to assess the relationship between BMD and biomechanical parameters. Categorical data, such as mode of failure, were analyzed using the Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All analyses were conducted using STATA version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Cyclic loading test

During cyclic loading, elongation was measured at 2, 20, 100, and 500 cycles (Table 2; Fig. 5). No specimens failed during cyclic testing. The overall mean elongation across all groups was 14.1 ± 9.2 mm, and the overall mean stiffness was 27.7 ± 10.7 N/mm. No significant differences were observed between the fixation techniques (p > 0.05).

Load-to-failure test

Following the cyclic loading test, the specimens were subjected to a load-to-failure test. Stiffness, yield load, and maximum load were recorded. The overall mean yield load was 400.5 ± 130.0 N, while the mean maximum load was 427.4 ± 141.9 N. Statistical analysis revealed no significant differences between the groups (p > 0.05) (Fig. 6).

Comparison of (a) yield load and (b) maximum load across all fixation techniques.

Modes of failure

Two failure modes were identified during load-to-failure testing: (1) fragment breakage, defined as fracture of the bone fragment under stress, and (2) suture cut-through, defined as suture material slicing through the tibial tunnel exit site (Fig. 7). Fragment breakage was more common in Groups A (n = 5) and D (n = 4), while suture cut-through occurred more frequently in Groups B (n = 4) and C (n = 3). Overall, there were 12 instances of fragment breakage and 8 instances of suture cut-through recorded. Among the suture fixation groups, the three-tunnel configuration (Group D) showed a tendency toward fragment breakage, indicating a shift in failure mode. However, no significant differences were found between Groups B, C, and D (p = 0.301). Pairwise comparisons also revealed no significant differences in failure modes between these groups, with p-values of 0.206 between Group B and Group C, 0.523 between Group C and Group D, and 1.000 between Group B and Group D (Fig. 8).

Correlation between BMD and Biomechanical properties

The study demonstrates a statistically significant positive correlation between bone mineral density (BMD) and yield load (r = 0.511, p = 0.021), indicating that higher BMD is associated with greater yield load. Additionally, there is a statistically significant negative correlation between BMD and elongation (r = − 0.554, p = 0.011), suggesting that higher BMD is linked to reduced elongation. However, no significant correlation was observed between BMD and stiffness (r = 0.302, p = 0.195) (Fig. 9).

Discussion

Several studies have investigated the effectiveness of different fixation techniques for posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) avulsion fractures, highlighting the ongoing debate among various methods. Numerous fixation techniques and devices have been developed to secure PCL avulsion fragments, promoting proper healing and preventing further displacement17–19. A previous biomechanical study using porcine knees demonstrated that cortical suspension button and retrograde screw fixation techniques offer biomechanical properties like conventional antegrade screw fixation, while suture cerclage techniques showed lower stiffness and yield load in comparison10. Clinically, arthroscopic suture fixation for PCL avulsion has been extensively studied, consistently showing positive outcomes in terms of knee stability and function. Studies report reductions in knee laxity to less than 1.5 mm in side-to-side difference, along with improvements in functional scores9,19–21. One of the key aspects of successful fixation is securely holding the PCL fragment and ensuring accurate placement of postero-inferior tibial tunnels to achieve proper anatomical reduction of the bone fragment11,22.

This study found no statistically significant differences in biomechanical properties among the fixation techniques, supporting our hypothesis that suture-based methods provide fixation strength comparable to screw fixation. Among the suture-based techniques, the three-tunnel configuration (Group D) predominantly failed due to fragment breakage rather than suture cut-through. Theoretically, this suggests that the three-tunnel configuration distributes load more evenly across the fragment. This shift in failure mode indicates that in cases of good bone quality, the increased compressive force on the fragment may enhance fixation stability and promote bone healing. However, in patients with compromised bone quality, this compressive force could lead to fragment breakage, resulting in no significant advantage over other suture configurations.

Clinically, a 5 mm elongation in the fixation construct can be significant, as it may result in joint laxity and functional impairment, potentially compromising knee stability and mobility23–25. The results from this study show that none of the fixation techniques were able to withstand an average load greater than 137.3 ± 89.5 N before the construct elongated 5 mm. This highlights the need for cautious, low-impact postoperative rehabilitation, regardless of the fixation method used. These findings align with those of Forkel et al.8, who reported that neither the suture-bridge with suture anchor technique nor the two-tunnel pullout suture technique provided sufficient fixation strength to resist physiological loads, highlighting similar concerns about fixation stability under stress. The use of protective bracing and limitations in knee motion may be necessary during the early postoperative period to prevent excessive loading and potential construct failure. Furthermore, although arthroscopic suture-based techniques offer the advantage of minimal invasiveness, they may involve longer operative times and higher surgical costs due to the need for specialized equipment.

Another important finding of this study is the strong link between BMD and both yield load and elongation. Higher BMD was associated with increased yield load and reduced elongation, emphasizing the importance of bone quality in the biomechanical performance of fixation techniques. This relationship was consistent across all fixation methods, suggesting that patients with higher BMD may achieve better fixation outcomes regardless of the technique used. Therefore, it is crucial to consider patient-specific factors such as BMD when selecting postoperative protocols to optimize results.

This study has several strengths. First, we utilized human cadaveric knees, which provide anatomical and biomechanical characteristics that closely resemble real clinical conditions. Although certain soft tissue structures such as the meniscus were removed to standardize the experimental setup and isolate the mechanical performance of the fixation constructs, the model remains relevant for evaluating biomechanical outcomes. Second, to our knowledge, this is the first study to compare different suture tunnel configurations and the number of sutures used to secure the PCL fragment. Third, whereas previous studies primarily used No. 2 high-strength sutures (FiberWire; Arthrex, Naples, FL), which often failed due to suture tearing, we opted for suture tape. This choice offers a broader surface area for improved pressure distribution, enhanced soft tissue grip, and may reduce the risk of tissue cut-through. Additionally, the inclusion of BMD data in this study allowed us to control for variations in bone quality across specimens, leading to more reliable comparisons. Clinically, these findings suggest that suture-based fixation methods can be considered viable alternatives to screw fixation in PCL avulsion fractures. Moreover, assessing bone quality preoperatively could guide both surgical technique selection and the development of patient-specific rehabilitation protocols.

However, this study has some limitations. First, the use of human cadaveric knees may not fully replicate in vivo conditions due to the absence of surrounding structures such as the capsule, meniscus, ligaments, and muscles. Second, as a time-zero biomechanical study, it does not account for biological healing processes or postoperative muscle forces that occur during recovery. The results reflect only the initial fixation strength and may not predict the mid- or long-term performance of these constructs during biological healing. Third, the relatively high average donor age (68 ± 17.8 years) may have affected bone quality. Although no significant differences in BMD were observed between the groups, specimens with higher BMD might exhibit different biomechanical properties and failure modes. BMD was measured at the proximal tibia to directly assess local bone quality and minimize intergroup variability. The values were reported in g/cm², which is standard in biomechanical studies. While this limits direct comparison with WHO classifications, the donor age likely corresponds to an osteoporotic population. Further biomechanical studies using specimens with higher BMD and incorporating surface pressure measurements at the fragment may be necessary. Although cadaveric models closely replicate anatomical and biomechanical conditions, future studies using synthetic bones with controlled material properties, along with an intact control group, could better isolate the role of BMD in fixation strength and provide a baseline for evaluating construct behavior. Additionally, future randomized controlled trials are needed to evaluate the clinical effects of different fixation techniques and validate the findings of this study.

Conclusion

All suture fixation techniques demonstrated biomechanical properties comparable to conventional antegrade screw fixation in PCL avulsion fractures. Increasing the number of suture tapes or tunnels did not significantly improve load-to-failure resistance or prevent construct elongation. Moreover, the positive correlation between BMD and yield load emphasizes the crucial role of bone quality in fixation strength. These findings suggest that BMD should be considered in planning postoperative rehabilitation protocols to optimize clinical outcomes.
