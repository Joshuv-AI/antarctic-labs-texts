Intracoronary Imaging in Brachytherapy
Authors: Steven Pfau, Kenneth Roberts, Daniel Chamié
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: coronary brachytherapy, coronary intervention, instent restenosis, intravascular ultrasound

Coronary in-stent restenosis (ISR) after drug-eluting stent (DES) percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) remains an important clinical entity. In fact, ISR PCI accounts for more than 10% of all PCI in the United States, and the numbers may still be increasing.1 Most importantly, DES ISR lesions are a challenging lesion subset, procedurally complex with a higher incidence of all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction, target vessel revascularization, and stent thrombosis compared to treatment of de novo lesions.2 The biologic and anatomic mechanisms of DES restenosis are varied and include neointimal hyperplasia, neoatherosclerosis (with and without calcium), calcium nodules, and stent underexpansion. An understanding of the specific mechanisms in a given lesion necessarily impact the interventional approach to treatment, such that intravascular imaging (IVI) is recommended in both European and U.S. guidelines at the time of ISR reintervention.3 The overwhelming evidence indicates IVI is the standard for best PCI outcomes, and indeed available data confirm lower major adverse cardiac events in IVI-guided PCI for restenosis, compared to angiography alone.4 Unfortunately, repeat restenosis (RISR) occurs in up to 40% of ISR lesions, creating a distinct lesion subset with limited data regarding effective treatment options.

Coronary intravascular brachytherapy (IVBT) entered clinical use 30 years ago, specifically for the treatment of bare-metal stent (BMS) restenosis. IVBT was based on careful study of the underlying biologic mechanisms, and robust randomized trial evidence confirmed efficacy.5 Displaced by the advent of DES in 2003, IVBT has now reemerged as a therapeutic option for RISR; contemporary data indicate a repeat revascularization rate of 29% at 2 years.6 However, despite society recommendations for IVI use in ISR, a quick review of 8 contemporary case series of IVBT indicates that only 4 reported IVI imaging, and in those series, IVI was used in only 32% of cases, with no discussion of how IVI findings were utilized.

In this issue of JACC: Advances, Kharsa et al7 attempt to answer an important and previously unaddressed question: can best practices for stent failure, in this case intracoronary imaging, improve the outcome of coronary brachytherapy procedures? In this single-center, retrospective sample of 221 coronary brachytherapy cases at a major academic center over 8 years, the conclusion is a tepid “no”. In fact, Kaplan-Meier estimates indicated a higher incidence of target lesion revascularization in the imaging group. Although there was some suggestion that use of intracoronary imaging influenced decisions regarding interventional device use (lithotripsy, cutting balloons), target vessel revascularization and cardiac mortality were not different between the IVI and angiography groups. After multivariate adjustment, IVI was independently associated with target lesion revascularization and all-cause mortality.

The authors should be commended for trying to answer this important question. Unfortunately, critical limitations of the study (observational design, phased-array IVI platform, and limited angiographic follow-up) minimize the significance of the results. Most notably, selection bias is a major caveat in interpreting these results. IVI is not a therapeutic device; its value lies in allowing operators to identify the mechanisms of stent failure and implement appropriate corrective measures. Because only cases achieving <30% residual stenosis after lesion preparation were included, the analysis cannot address whether IVI helped operators identify and correct the mechanical substrates of stent failure in the first place, nor whether IVI-guided lesion preparation would have produced better results than angiography alone. Finally, IVI was selectively used in the most complex lesions, as the authors note that focal ISR with satisfactory angiographic appearance after lesion preparation were often not imaged. This confounding by indication means that worse outcomes in the IVI group may simply reflect a higher underlying risk of more complex lesions rather than a lack of utility of imaging.

With these reservations in mind, what can we take away from this current data set regarding the use of intracoronary imaging in brachytherapy? In recognizing the limitations of their study, the paper’s discussion section provides a blueprint of important directions for future research in this area.

IVI is underutilized, particularly in this most challenging scenario of RISR

Poor adoption of IVI in U.S. interventional practice is well documented, and this study is another surprising example of IVI underutilization even in the most challenging cases at an academic medical center. Outcome improvements will necessarily require routine use high-definition IVI; the advantages of optical coherence tomography (OCT), particularly quantifying calcium and visualization of stent layers, are ideal in this setting.

Not all DES ISR is created equal

Although IVBT was developed for BMS restenosis, DES ISR is biologically different.8 DES ISR presents later, with a hypocellular proteoglycan rich neointima. Neoatherosclerosis, with or without calcification, is not uncommon in DES ISR, particularly beyond 1 year. Both eruptive and noneruptive calcium nodules may also occur within stents, particularly in older patients, those with chronic kidney disease, diabetes, severe underlying coronary calcification, and at certain anatomic locations (ostial and mid-right coronary artery). Characterization of the ISR subtype can only be reliably accomplished with high resolution imaging, preferably OCT.

Should IVBT be applied to every case of RISR?

Given that the science behind IVBT was based on the biology of BMS ISR, are there subtypes of DES ISR that respond better (or not at all) to radiation? Certainly some of IVBT failure in the current era may be related to specific subtypes being unresponsive (ie eruptive calcium nodules?), and a blanket approach may be neutral or even counterproductive.

Can IVI improve dose prescription?

Data to define the optimal IVBT dose in human coronary arteries is limited. Animal models, as well as clinical experience in the earliest brachytherapy trials, demonstrated that there was a lower boundary of effective dose in the treatment of coronary ISR. Contemporary IVBT dose prescriptions are based on dose calculation parameters from American Association of Physicists in Medicine task group consensus documents from 1999,9 where long-term risk was a significant concern. The evidence for long-term deleterious effects, however, has been absent in the more than 25 years of clinical follow-up. Due to the heterogeneous dose distribution related to vessel curvature, eccentric plaque and noncentered sources, detailed analysis of delivered dose indicates that segments of treated coronary arteries have tolerated very high, even extreme, doses without clear negative consequences. Importantly, data from the original era of brachytherapy indicate that clinical effectiveness of brachytherapy improves with doses higher than current prescriptions. Finally, individualized dose prescriptions based on IVI that account for vessel size, distance to the target media, attenuation by calcium and the metal of multiple stent layers may improve short and long-term outcomes. Software for individualized dose prescriptions based on IVI findings (necessarily OCT) are beginning to emerge10 and may represent significant forward progress in IVBT effectiveness.

At present, there is no consensus regarding the best approach to treatment of challenging RISR lesions. The study by Kharsa begins to frame the discussion around intracoronary imaging findings, an approach that has led to significant improvement in PCI outcomes in other complex lesion types. As we have embraced the use of IVBT in the DES ISR era, the opportunity to understand how best to use it—improved dose prescriptions for the most responsive ISR subtypes guided by imaging—is in front of us. We should open the door.
