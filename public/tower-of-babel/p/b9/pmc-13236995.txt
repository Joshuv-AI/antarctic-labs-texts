Chitinase-like proteins de-N-glycosylating CD36 modify cholesterol metabolism in atherosclerotic macrophages
Authors: Yu Wang, Jing Zhang, Meiyang Fan, Xianbin Hou, Xiaomeng Li, Wentao Zhang, Wenbin Liu, Yue Li, Yaqi Lu, Fei Li, Yanglong Guan, Yudan Wang, Bingyu Yuan, Xiaowei Li, Huilin Gong, Feng Ning, Xiaozhen Zhuo, Shemin Lu, Liesu Meng, Rikard Holmdahl, Wenhua Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolymorphisms of mouse chitinase-like protein 3 (Chil3), a member of the mammalian chitinase-like protein (CLP) family, have been demonstrated to be associated with inflammatory diseases by regulating lipid metabolism. However, the specific immunomodulatory impacts of CLPs, mainly mouse CHIL3 and its human functional homologue chitinase-3-like 2 (CHI3L2), on macrophage cholesterol metabolism and atherosclerosis have remained unclear. Here, we find CLPs (CHIL3 and CHI3L2) accelerate atherogenesis in a macrophage-dependent manner. Mechanistically, we identify an autocrine mechanism through which CLPs regulate cholesterol metabolism in macrophages. Macrophage-secreted CLPs exacerbate lipid uptake by binding to CD36. CLPs exhibit glycosidase activity, targeting and hydrolyzing N-glycosylated glycans on CD36, predominantly at sites N220 and N321, thereby enhancing lipid uptake. Increased lipid influx activates mTOR in macrophages, driving their transition to a pro-inflammatory phenotype while simultaneously suppressing peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) expression and thus impairing ABCG1-mediated cholesterol efflux. Single-cell sequencing reveals that CLPs increase atherosclerotic foamy macrophages, favoring vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMC) transformation into foam and osteoblast-like cells. Additionally, neutralizing antibodies targeting CHI3L2 prevent and treat atherosclerosis. These findings highlight the potential of CLPs as targets for disease diagnosis and therapy.Subject terms: Glycosylation, Atherosclerosis, Foam cells, Cardiovascular diseases, Innate immunity

Introduction

Identification of key genes regulating metabolic diseases offers valuable insights into disease pathogenesis. In our previous study, we positionally cloned Chil3, encoding chitinase-like protein 3 (CHIL3/Ym1), as a susceptibility gene for inflammatory diseases through its modulation of macrophage lipid metabolism and alternative activation1. CHIL3 belongs to a family of chitinase-like proteins (CLPs), which lack chitinase activity due to mutations in putative enzyme sites2. Mammals possess several identified CLPs, including chitinase-3-like 1 (CHI3L1), CHI3L2, CHIL3, CHIL4, chitinase domain containing 1 (CHID1), and oviductal glycoprotein 1 (OVGP1)3. Notably, CHI3L2 is exclusive to humans and absent in rodents, whereas CHIL3 and CHIL4 are uniquely found in rodents4. Macrophages represent a significant source of both mouse and human CLPs. CHIL3 and CHI3L2 have emerged as markers of alternatively activated macrophages (AAMs)1,5, whereas CHI3L1 is prominently induced in classically activated macrophages (CAMs)6. CLPs, which contain a conserved glycosyl hydrolase family 18 (GH18) domain, participate in biological processes that contribute to chronic inflammatory and metabolic diseases through shared or molecule-specific immunometabolic functions3.

Atherosclerosis is a chronic, lipid-driven vascular inflammatory disease and a pivotal pathological precursor to cardiovascular diseases (CVD), involving the pathophysiological activation of macrophages, vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), and endothelial cells (ECs)7,8. Dysregulation of cholesterol homeostasis in macrophages, mediated by the interplay of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) uptake through scavenger receptors and cholesterol efflux, leads to macrophage dysfunction9,10. Furthermore, VSMCs are the main cellular component of advanced atherosclerotic aortas and are highly plastic cells that can undergo various phenotypic transformations, such as those into mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), macrophage-like and osteochondrocyte-like cells11. These phenotypic transitions, which can be initiated by lipid uptake, enable VSMCs to contribute significantly to plaque development through enhanced lipid accumulation and calcification11. Identifying the molecular mechanisms controlling foam cell formation and retention within plaques holds promise for discovering potential clinical diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets for atherosclerosis. However, CLPs lacking chitinase activity have been poorly studied in atherosclerosis. Clinical studies have demonstrated a positive correlation between elevated serum CHI3L1/CHI3L2 levels and coronary artery disease development, plasma triglyceride (TG), and total cholesterol (TC) levels12–16. Only a few experimental studies have reported that CHI3L1 is associated with macrophage apoptosis, EC inflammation, and activation of VSMCs, thereby exacerbating atherosclerosis17,18. Overall, there remains an absence of experimental evidence linking CLPs to immunometabolic regulation in chronic inflammatory diseases such as atherosclerosis. Our study aims to explore the involvement of CHIL3 and its human functional homologue CLP in the modulation of atherosclerosis.

Herein, we reveal that CLPs, specifically human-specific CHI3L2 and mouse-specific CHIL3, are markedly induced in atherosclerotic macrophages, and in particular alter the phenotypic transition of macrophages and VSMCs, skewing them towards a disease-promoting phenotype. Interventions targeting these CLPs can influence atherogenesis. Mechanistically, CLPs exhibit glycosidase activity, leading to scavenger receptor cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36) deglycosylation, thereby regulating lipid uptake. Additionally, CLPs play a crucial role in modulating cholesterol efflux and the immune-inflammatory state of macrophages. Moreover, the significant efficacy of neutralizing antibodies against CHI3L2 in treating atherosclerosis underscores the potential of CLPs as promising targets for precise therapeutic strategies. Together, these findings highlight the profound impact and intricate mechanisms of CLPs in atherosclerosis.

Results

Human CHI3L2 and mouse CHIL3 homologously exhibit parallel expression patterns in macrophage polarization and atherosclerosis

Our previous studies linked CHIL3 to lipid metabolism, suggesting a potential role in metabolic disorders such as atherosclerosis1. Here, we used atherosclerosis models to investigate the role of CHIL3, a mouse-specific CLP with no known human ortholog, and its human functional homologue, in lipid metabolism-related diseases. Integrated RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) database (GSE237219 and GSE20741420) showed significantly elevated expression levels of Chil1, Chil3 and Chid1 in the aortas of apolipoprotein E null (ApoE−/−) mice aged 32-40 weeks compared to wild-type (WT) mice (Supplementary Fig. 1a). The RNA-seq database (GSE28829)21 revealed that human CHI3L1 and CHI3L2 levels were significantly upregulated in advanced atherosclerotic plaque (AAP) compared with early atherosclerotic plaque (EAP) among human CLPs (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Based on the commonality of CLP expression in the human and mouse datasets, we prioritized Chil1/CHI3L1, Chil3, and CHI3L2 for further investigation. In the single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset of the ApoE−/− and low density lipoprotein receptor null (Ldlr−/−) mouse models of atherosclerosis (GSE155513)11, Chil3 but not Chil1 expression was upregulated during atherosclerosis progression, particularly in atherosclerotic macrophages (Fig. 1a-f). Additionally, in the scRNA-seq dataset (GSE159677)22, CHI3L2 and CHI3L1 were abundant in the atherosclerotic core (AC) compared to proximally adjacent (PA) regions of human samples, and their expression in macrophages increased with disease progression (Fig. 1g-i). Based on these sequencing data, Chil3 and CHI3L2 demonstrated disease-associated induced expression.

Meanwhile, we also conducted a comparative analysis of CLPs between humans and mice from genetic and immunophysiological characteristics. The GH18 family includes chitinases and CLPs, with homologues loci between mice and humans identified, highlighting synteny between the mouse 1F4 and human 1q32 loci, and between mouse 3F3 and human 1p13 loci (Supplementary Fig. 1c)4,23. Notable orthologs in this family include CHIT1/Chit1, CHIA/Chia, CHI3L1/Chil1, and OVGP1/Ovgp1 (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Although human CHI3L2 and mouse Chil3 are not sequence homologues, their closely matching chromosomal locations indicate that the GH18 family is widely distributed among mammals and retains conserved structural features, despite variations in expression across species. To further identify which human CLP share functions similar to CHIL3, we performed macrophage polarization experiments, leveraging the fact that CHIL3 is a typical marker of AAMs. Polarization of mouse peritoneal macrophages (PMφ) into CAMs using lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) or AAMs using interleukin (IL)-4 and IL-13 was conducted. CHIL3 levels were increased in AAMs but not in CAMs, indicating CHIL3 serves as a signature marker for AAMs (Supplementary Fig. 1d, e). Particularly, CHI3L2 rather than CHI3L1 was highly expressed in THP-1-derived AAMs, suggesting a consistent effect of CHI3L2 on macrophages akin to CHIL3 (Supplementary Fig. 1f, g). It was clear that CHIL3 and CHI3L2 showed consistent expression pattern during macrophage polarization.

Given the parallel expression pattern of human CHI3L2 and mouse CHIL3 in macrophage polarization and atherosclerosis, our prior focus was directed solely on these two among CLP family. Therefore, “CLPs” in this study specifically refer to mouse CHIL3 and human CHI3L2. The expression of CHIL3 and CHI3L2 was further confirmed in mouse models or human patients. We found that ApoE−/− mice on a Western diet (WD) for 3 months exhibited markedly higher circulating levels of CHIL3 protein compared to those on a normal diet (ND) (Fig. 1j). Additionally, CHI3L2 protein levels were markedly elevated in human atherosclerotic plasma (Fig. 1k), and immunofluorescence of human carotid endarterectomy specimens showed higher CHI3L2 in severe (atheromatous plaque stage, APS) versus fibrous plaque stage (FPS) (Fig. 1l). Furthermore, CHI3L2 levels were similarly increased in CHI3L2 transgenic mice (ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg) under WD conditions (Supplementary Fig. 1h), supporting its role in plaque formation.

Nature polymorphism of Chil3 and human functional homologue CHI3L2 influence atherogenesis in mice

Then the role of CLPs (CHIL3 and CHI3L2) on atherosclerosis was investigated. We used a Chil3 congenic deficient mouse strain (ApoE−/−.Chil3∆) which has a deletion of CHIL3 protein due to the nature polymorphism of Chil3 gene1, and also created a humanized CHI3L2 transgenic mouse strain (ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg) crossed with ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ to exclude endogenous Chil3 interference. All male and female littermates were then subjected to a WD diet for 3 months. Metabolic parameters including serum TC, TG and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) were comparable, while the levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and ox-LDL were significantly reduced in male and female ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ mice compared to ApoE−/− and ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg mice (Fig. 2a, b). En-face Oil red O (ORO) staining of aortas and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and ORO staining of aortic roots revealed that the atherosclerotic lesion area of both male and female ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ mice was significantly less than that of ApoE−/− and ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg mice (Fig. 2c-e). Compared with the male or female ApoE−/− and ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg mice, in ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ mice, morphological analyses of the cross-sectional lesions showed an increase in collagen content by using Sirius red and contractile smooth muscle cell marker alpha smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) immunofluorescence staining, and a decrease in macrophages by using the macrophage marker CD68 immunofluorescence staining, respectively (Fig. 2d-g). Consistently, both male and female ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ mice exhibited reduced VCAM-1 (vascular cell adhesion molecule-1) expression in aortic root lesions compared to ApoE−/− and ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg mice, while ICAM-1 (intercellular adhesion molecule-1) expression showed no significant difference (Fig. 2f, g), indicating that CLPs may preferentially regulate VCAM-1-associated leukocyte recruitment pathways, without broadly affecting ICAM-1-mediated endothelial activation.

In addition to genetic manipulation, we also administered recombinant human CHI3L2 (rhCHI3L2) protein supplementation to mice and observed its effects on the atherosclerosis model. We first placed 2-month-old ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ mice on a WD diet for 1 month and then injected the rhCHI3L2 or control protein intraperitoneally every three days for 2 months, while continuing the WD diet (Supplementary Fig. 2a). ELISA analysis demonstrated that exogenous rhCHI3L2 was successfully delivered and could be detected in the mouse circulation (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Our results showed that treatment with rhCHI3L2 significantly increased the lesion area in both the aorta and aortic root, while collagen content in the aortic root was significantly reduced (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). Collectively, these findings highlight CHI3L2 can accelerate atherosclerosis progression.

CLPs alter the cell-specific phenotypic transition of macrophages and VSMCs

To delineate the role of CLPs in atherosclerotic cell fate, we performed scRNA-seq on aortic tissues from WD-fed littermate mice (ApoE−/−, ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ and ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg). The analysis identified a diverse array of cell types, including T cells, ECs, macrophages, fibroblasts and VSMCs (Fig. 3a), with cell type annotation based on established markers (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Localization analysis of CLPs showed predominant localization of Chil3 in macrophages of ApoE−/− mice, contrasting with its absence in ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ and ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg mice (Fig. 3a, b). CHI3L2 also exhibited predominant expression in macrophages of ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg mice, distinguishing them from the other groups (Fig. 3a, b). Differences in cell type composition were observed in these three groups, with macrophages aggregating less in the aorta of CLP-deficient mice, consistent with our previous immunofluorescence results for CD68-positive macrophages (Figs. 2f, g, 3c).

We further classified macrophage subpopulations and performed pseudotime trajectory analysis, revealing seven distinct states based on gene expression dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c). The trajectory originated from Ly6C2+ monocyte-like, MHC-II, and resident macrophages, progressed through foamy and inflammatory macrophages, and later transitioned into VSMC-like and fibrochondrocyte (FC)-like macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Differential gene expression supported this trajectory: foam cell-related genes Lpl and Trem2 were upregulated in foamy macrophages, whereas Myh11 increased in later states (Supplementary Fig. 3d). Harmony integration of macrophages across the three experimental groups showed seven distinct cellular subpopulations aligned with the pseudotime trajectory (Fig. 3d). Notably, Chil3 was specifically enriched in Ly6C2+ monocyte-like macrophages in ApoE−/− mice, whereas CHI3L2 was mainly expressed in foamy, VSMC-like and FC-like macrophages in ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg mice (Fig. 3e). Functionally, Chil3 increased inflammatory macrophages and reduced resident macrophages, while CHI3L2 expanded foam cell/FC-like clusters at the expense of resident and MHC-II macrophages (Fig. 3f). These findings suggest that CLPs not only influence macrophage phenotypic transition but also enhance macrophage infiltration during atherosclerosis progression.

The pseudotime trajectory analysis of VSMCs subpopulations derived from annotation of known markers revealed that the trajectory originated from contractile VSMCs, progressed through an intermediate cell state (ICS), and bifurcated into macrophage-like and FC-like VSMCs (Supplementary Fig. 3e, g). Genes associated with pseudotime progression were identified along these developmental trajectories. For instance, terminal VSMCs showed regulation of macrophage (Lgals3), osteogenic (Ibsp), and chondrogenic (Acan) genes (Supplementary Fig. 3f). Integration analysis demonstrated effective integration of VSMCs across the three experimental groups (Supplementary Fig. 3h). ICS cells exhibited elevated stem cell markers (Ly6a, Cd34) and reduced canonical SMC markers, indicating a transitional, multi-lineage phenotype with loss of the typical SMC phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 3g, i). Along the trajectory, increasing expression of FC-related (Col2a1, Ibsp, Acan) and macrophage-related (Lgals3, Tyrobp, Fcer1g) genes indicated the differentiation potential of ICS toward FC- or macrophage-like VSMCs (Supplementary Fig. 3g, i). Moreover, the presence of CLPs notably expanded the macrophage-like VSMCs and FC-like VSMCs, indicating that CLPs promote VSMC foam cell formation and calcification (Fig. 3g, h). To further validate the CLP-associated interaction between macrophages and VSMCs, we applied BMDM supernatants from ApoE−/−, ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ and ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg mice on VSMCs to assess foam cell formation and calcification (Fig. 3i). The results were consistent with the scRNA-seq data, indicating that macrophage supernatants containing CLPs significantly promote lipid deposition and calcium deposition in VSMCs (Fig. 3j, k).

We subsequently examined fibroblast subpopulations and observed their differentiation into myofibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. 3j). Fibroblasts exhibited high expression of Ly6a and Bmp4, indicative of differentiation from MSCs or adipocytes (Supplementary Fig. 3k, i). Minor differences were detected between fibroblast subtypes across the three groups (Supplementary Fig. 3m, n). Overall, these findings suggest that CLPs can influence the phenotypic transition of atherosclerosis-associated cells, particularly macrophages and VSMCs.

CLPs accelerate atherogenesis in a macrophage-dependent manner

ScRNA-seq highlights Chil3 and CHI3L2 expression predominantly in macrophages. This finding was further corroborated by co-localization experiments, which demonstrated the overlap of CHIL3 or CHI3L2 with CD68-positive macrophages in mouse atherosclerotic aortas (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 4a). Immunofluorescence staining of human atherosclerotic plaques demonstrated that CHI3L2 was highly expressed in plaque macrophages of APS compared to FPS (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 4b). Further investigation using ox-LDL-stimulated macrophages to induce foam cell formation showed markedly increased CHIL3 expression in foam cells derived from PMφ, bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs), as well as high CHI3L2 expression in foam cells derived from PMφ, BMDMs in transgenic mice, and the human THP-1 cell line (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). Additionally, analysis of the publicly available human RNA-seq dataset (GSE125126)24 revealed a significant upregulation of CHI3L2 expression in atherosclerotic macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 4e). These findings are consistent with public scRNA-seq data, which also demonstrate elevated expression of CHIL3 and CHI3L2 in macrophages within atherosclerotic lesions (Fig. 1c, f, i).

Then a bone marrow transplantation (BMT) model was used to assess the role of myeloid CLPs in atherosclerosis. Bone marrow (BM) cells from ApoE−/−, ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ and ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg mice, all littermates expressing the CD45.1 alloantigen, were transplanted into lethally irradiated ApoE−/− recipients expressing the CD45.2 alloantigen (Fig. 4c), and the successful hematopoietic reconstitution was confirmed by flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. 4f). After 12 weeks of WD feeding, H&E and ORO staining revealed reduced aortic lesion areas in mice receiving Chil3-deficient BM compared to those receiving CLP-expressing BM (Fig. 4d, e). Additionally, Chil3 deficiency also increased collagen deposition, with CHI3L2 overexpression reversing these effects (Fig. 4e). Given the macrophage-specific expression of Chil3, our animal model effectively demonstrates its impact on atherosclerosis. In contrast, CHI3L2 is broadly expressed; therefore, we generated a humanized CHI3L2 conditional knock-in mouse strain crossed with LysM-Cre mice in order to achieve macrophage-specific CHI3L2 expression (denoted as M-CHI3L2ki/wt). An additional model was established by injecting an adeno-associated virus-8 carrying a mutant PCSK9 gene (AAV8-PCSK9) into M-CHI3L2ki/wt and littermate control mice (M-CHI3L2wt/wt), followed by 3 months of WD feeding (Fig. 4f). Compared to controls, M-CHI3L2ki/wt mice developed more extensive lesions throughout the entire aortas and aortic roots, along with increased plaque burden and greater plaque instability (Fig. 4g, h). These findings further support a macrophage-centered role for CLPs in regulating lipid metabolism and atherosclerosis progression.

CLPs cause disequilibrium in cholesterol metabolism of macrophages, though facilitating ox-LDL uptake and lipid deposition

The role of CLPs in macrophage cholesterol metabolism was then examined. A significant decrease in lipid deposition within PMφ and BMDMs with CLP deficiency, but a significant increase in CLP-enriched macrophages, was revealed by ORO and BODIPY493/503 staining, suggesting an enhancement of foam cell formation by CLPs (Supplementary Fig. 5a-c and Fig. 5a-c). Moreover, administration of recombinant CHIL3 protein (rCHIL3) or rhCHI3L2 in macrophages significantly intensified lipid deposition (Fig. 5d, e).

Using flow cytometry and a multiphoton microscope employing Dil-labeled ox-LDL (Dil-ox-LDL), we provided additional evidence supporting the involvement of CHIL3 and CHI3L2 in lipid uptake. Chil3 deficiency markedly impaired ox-LDL uptake in PMφ and BMDMs, whereas CHI3L2 overexpression heightened ox-LDL uptake compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. 5d, e and Fig. 5f, g). Additionally, lipid uptake in PMφ and BMDMs exhibited a gradient increase with escalating concentrations of rCHIL3 or rhCHI3L2, consistent with findings observed in THP-1-derived macrophages treated with rhCHI3L2 (Supplementary Fig. 5f and Fig. 5h, i). These collective observations suggest CLPs promote ox-LDL uptake and foam cell formation, disrupting macrophage cholesterol homeostasis.

CLPs interact with CD36 and promote CD36 endocytosis to enhance lipid uptake

Given their secretory nature, CLPs may affect macrophage cholesterol metabolism via interactions with membrane proteins. Utilizing immunoprecipitation (IP) and mass spectrometry (MS) analyses, we identified the top five candidate CHIL3-interacting cell-membrane proteins in PMφ, including CD36, a pivotal scavenger receptor managing ox-LDL internalization in macrophages (Fig. 6a). Co-IP with His-tagged rCHIL3/CHI3L2 in BMDMs confirmed interaction with CD36, but not with other scavenger receptors such as oxidized low-density lipoprotein receptor 1 (OLR1) and macrophage scavenger receptor 1 (MSR1) (Fig. 6b). Further validation through Co-IP with antibodies against CD36 confirmed the efficient interaction of these proteins (Fig. 6c). Establishment of an HEK293T cell line overexpressing His-tagged CHIL3/hCHI3L2 and Flag-tagged CD36 enabled detection of the association between His-CHIL3/hCHI3L2 and Flag-CD36 in these cells (Supplementary Fig. 6a and Fig. 6d). Moreover, immunofluorescence staining directly showed the interaction of CHIL3/CHI3L2 with CD36 in BMDMs or THP-1-derived macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Notably, interaction studies excluded the directly binding of CHIL3 or CHI3L2 with ox-LDL (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d).

Given the demonstrated interaction between CLPs and CD36, we hypothesized that CLPs facilitate CD36 internalization, and thus, lipid uptake. Notably, while total CD36 protein expression remained comparable in macrophages from ApoE−/−, ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ and ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg mice (Supplementary Fig. 6e), surface CD36 was decreased upon ox-LDL treatment in ApoE−/− and ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg macrophages, a phenomenon not observed in ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ macrophages, indicating that CLPs promote CD36 internalization (Fig. 6e, f). Furthermore, exogenous rCHIL3 or rhCHI3L2 treatment promoted CD36 internalization in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 6g, h). Immunofluorescence staining unveiled ox-LDL-induced trafficking of CD36 to early endosomes, with observed co-localization with early endosome antigen 1 (EEA1) and Ras-related protein Rab11 (a recycling endosome marker) exclusively in ApoE−/− macrophages following ox-LDL treatment, contrasting with the absence of such co-localization in ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 6f).

Then we conducted rescue experiments based on the hypothesis that exogenous soluble CD36 protein would compete with cell surface CD36 for binding to CLPs and subsequently reduce the lipid uptake induced by CLPs. We utilized recombinant extracellular domain (ECD) of mouse or human CD36 proteins (Gly30-Lys439, rmCD36ECD or rhCD36ECD), to explore the potential competitive binding of exogenous CD36 to CLPs. Notably, administration of rCHIL3 protein significantly augmented cellular lipid uptake; however, pre-incubation of rCHIL3 protein with rmCD36ECD led to a marked reduction in cellular lipid uptake compared to the sole administration of rCHIL3 protein (Fig. 6i). Similarly, lentivirus-mediated knockdown of CD36 in BMDMs yielded comparable effects to those observed with rmCD36ECD treatment (Fig. 6j), with knockdown efficiency validated via Western blotting (Supplementary Fig. 6g). Furthermore, administration of rhCD36ECD resulted in competitive binding to CHI3L2, consequently attenuating lipid uptake in BMDMs relative to treatment with rhCHI3L2 protein alone (Fig. 6k). Collectively, these findings underscore the critical role of CLPs interaction with CD36 in mediating lipid uptake and cholesterol metabolism.

CLPs hydrolyze N-glycan of CD36 to promote lipid uptake

To delineate the mCD36 ECD responsible for the interaction with CHIL3, we generated several Flag-tagged mCD36 constructs, including full-length wild-type construct and truncated constructs with ECD partial deletion (designated as Flag-mCD36, Flag-mCD36∆1, Flag-mCD36∆2, and Flag-mCD36∆3; see Fig. 7a). Each of these constructs was transfected into 293T cells along with His-tagged CHIL3, and subsequent Co-IP analyses were performed. Interestingly, no matter which segment of CD36ECD was truncated, the interaction between these truncated CD36ECD constructs and CHIL3 always remained similar to that of the full-length protein, suggesting that CHIL3 binding might be independent on the protein structure of CD36 (Fig. 7a). Considering the lectin-like properties of the CLP family, we hypothesized its potential binding to extracellular oligosaccharide moieties of CD36. Glycosylation serves as a crucial modifier of membrane proteins, and CD36 is a highly N-glycosylated integral membrane protein (Fig. 7a)25. To further confirm the dependence of CD36 N-glycosylation on CHIL3 interaction, the binding of rCLPs to PNGase F (an N-glycan endoglycosidase) treated CD36 protein was scrutinized, with untreated CD36 protein serving as a control. A remarkable gel shift of CD36 protein in SDS-PAGE indicated a change of glycosylation upon PNGase F treatment, accompanied by diminished interaction between CD36 and rCLP protein (Fig. 7b). Considering the extensively conserved nature of CLPs and the previously documented weak β-N-acetylglucosaminidase activity of CHIL3, alongside the interaction of CHI3L2 with the N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) units26,27, the proteoglycan content was assessed by lectin blotting using wheat germ agglutinin (WGA), a GlcNAc-specific lectin, revealing a significant reduction in rmCD36ECD/rhCD36ECD upon rCHIL3/rhCHI3L2 treatment (Fig. 7c). This diminution in CD36 glycan content caused by CLPs markedly enhanced the binding of CD36ECD to ox-LDL (Fig. 7d).

Label-free quantitation of intact glycopeptides and modified glycans by MS was employed to assess CD36 N-glycosylation levels with or without rCLP treatment. Glycans and three N-glycosylation sites (N220, N320, and N321) were identified in mouse CD36, and two sites (N220 and N321) were identified in human CD36 (Fig. 7e). Interestingly, treatment with rCHIL3 decreased N-glycosylation levels on N220 and N321 in rmCD36ECD, while treatment with rhCHI3L2 reduced N-glycosylation levels on N220 in rhCD36ECD (Fig. 7e). Meanwhile, most of the glycoforms at these sites were reduced using rCLP treatment compared to the control, confirming the ability of CLPs to diminish CD36 N-glycosylation (Fig. 7f).

We next established CD36 constructs with mutations at glycosylation sites and transfected them into the 293T cell line. Both the single mutation at N220 of mouse CD36 (CD36N220Q) and the double mutations at N220 and N321 (CD36N220Q-N321Q) did not affect the membrane translocation of CD36 (Fig. 7g). However, these mutations led to an increase in ox-LDL uptake compared to WT construct (Fig. 7h, i). Meanwhile, rCLP protein treatment could only increase lipid uptake in WT constructs, and not in mutant constructs without glycosylation on N220 and N321, suggesting that CLPs target glycosylation sites to regulate CD36 function (Fig. 7h, i). In conclusion, these results demonstrate that CLPs with glycosidase hydrolytic activity bind and partially hydrolyze the N-glycosylated glycans of CD36, thereby promoting lipid uptake.

CLPs inhibit PPARγ-mediated cholesterol efflux through activating mTOR signaling pathway

In addition to the mechanism by which CLP targets CD36 to induce lipid intake, the secondary effects triggered by lipid overload in macrophages were further investigated based on our sequencing data. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of differentially expressed genes in macrophages from scRNA-seq revealed that CLP deficiency downregulated inflammation-related pathways, including the NOD-like receptor, chemokine, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction signaling pathways, while upregulating metabolism-related pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation, PPAR signaling, and glutathione metabolism (Supplementary Fig. 7a). This suggests that CLPs may be involved in regulating macrophage inflammatory responses, energy metabolism, and antioxidant capacity. In addition, the lysosome pathway was also markedly enhanced, reflecting increased cellular degradation and recycling capacity in CLP-deficient macrophages, which may indirectly support metabolic reprogramming and energy supply (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Further analysis showed that the core component of the NOD-like receptor family, the pyrin domain-containing 3 (Nlrp3) inflammasome, was significantly downregulated in macrophages from ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ mice, accompanied by markedly reduced expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including Il1b, Il6 and Tnf (Supplementary Fig. 7b), suggesting that the inflammasome may serve as a critical mediator through which CLPs regulate inflammatory cytokine expression in macrophages.

We subsequently performed bulk RNA-seq analysis of ox-LDL stimulated BMDMs isolated from ApoE−/− and ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ mice, revealing significant alterations in the expression of 1081 genes, with 463 downregulated and 618 upregulated genes in Chil3-deficient macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 7c). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway was the most significantly altered (Supplementary Fig. 7d). Notably, enrichment analysis of cholesterol metabolism genes suggested that CHIL3 may negatively regulate cholesterol transport and efflux. In Chil3-deficient macrophages, genes involved in cholesterol transport and hydrolysis, including Npc1/2 (Niemann-Pick C1/2), Stard3 (Star-related lipid transfer domain protein 3), Lipa (lysosomal acid lipase), and Nceh1 (neutral cholesterol ester hydrolase 1), as well as the efflux gene Abcg1 (ATP-binding cassette subfamily G member 1) were significantly upregulated, whereas cholesterol synthesis and uptake genes showed little change, with only Mylip (myosin regulatory light chain interacting protein, an E3 ubiquitin ligase promoting LDLR degradation) also being upregulated (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Upregulation of Soat1 (sterol O-acyltransferase 1/ACAT1) reflects coordinated cholesterol storage to maintain cellular homeostasis (Supplementary Fig. 8a). RT-qPCR further confirmed that Chil3 deficiency significantly upregulated Abcg1 and Mylip, and Western blotting showed that CLP deficiency significantly increased ABCG1 expression but not ABCA1 expression, suggesting a liver X receptor α (LXRα)-independent efflux pathway (Fig. 8a, Supplementary Fig. 8b-d). Previous study has shown that ABCG1 could, in certain contexts, occur independently of LXR activation28. This supports a role for CLPs in regulating ABCG1-mediated, LXRα-independent cholesterol efflux.

It has been reported that ABCG1 not only promotes cholesterol efflux but also contributes to lipid reprogramming and functional regulation29. Guided by this, we performed untargeted lipidomic profiling of macrophages and the results revealed that the loss of CLPs led to a marked reduction in lipid species associated with lipid storage, particularly cholesteryl esters (CE), accompanied by decreased levels of total cholesterol (Chol). These changes were consistent with the reduced foam cell propensity observed in our model (Fig. 8b, c). In contrast, membrane associated lipids, including phospholipids (phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylglycerol (PG), phosphatidylserine (PS), phosphatidylinositol (PI)), lysophospholipids (lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE)), and sphingolipids (ceramides (Cer)), were increased (Fig. 8b, c). This indicates that the loss of CLPs leads to membrane-centric lipid remodeling, altering the composition and biophysical properties of the bilayer membrane. Correspondingly, cholesterol efflux to HDL (ApoB-depleted serum) was markedly enhanced in Chil3-deficient macrophages, further emphasizing the ABCG1-mediated cholesterol efflux (Fig. 8d).

Our previous research has shown that CHIL3 regulates PPARγ to coordinate macrophage polarization1, and PPARγ is involved in the cholesterol efflux30. Using Western blotting, we observed that the absence of CLPs led to elevated levels of PPARγ and ABCG1 in macrophages with ox-LDL stimulation (Fig. 8e). In addition, T0070907, an inhibitor of PPARγ, reduced ABCG1 expression in ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ macrophages but not ApoE−/− or ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg macrophages (Fig. 8e). Macrophages respond to complex environmental cues by polarizing into distinct phenotypes. The nuclear receptor PPARγ drives robust phenotypic changes in macrophages following repeated stimulation with IL-4. In this context, the functional role of PPARγ is independent of ligand binding, and its upregulation under AAMs conditions is a common and conserved phenomenon31–34. We found that CLP deficiency significantly reduced the expression of inflammatory cytokines such as Il1b, Il6 and Tnf in foam cells, indicating an increased proportion of anti-inflammatory macrophages (Fig. 8f). In fact, as a negative regulator of AAMs, Chil3 deficiency enhances the AAM phenotype in mice1. Thus, we propose that enhanced PPARγ expression in CLP-deficient macrophages stems from increased anti-inflammatory macrophages rather than ligand-dependent mechanisms.

Lysosome-derived nutrients, including carbohydrates, lipids, and amino acids, are known to activate mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR)35–37. In macrophage foam cells, lipids internalized via CD36 dissociate from the receptor in the acidic endosomal environment; CD36 is recycled to the plasma membrane, whereas ox-LDL is delivered to lysosomes for degradation38. The resulting free lipids, particularly cholesterol, are sensed within lysosomes and act as major inducers of mTOR activation. Our data provide functional validation for this lysosomal core model. Compared to ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ BMDMs, excessive ox-LDL strongly induced mTOR activation in ApoE−/− and ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg BMDMs (Supplementary Fig. 8e). This mTOR activation was completely eliminated when lysosomal acidification and function were specifically inhibited using Baflomycin A1 (Baf-A1), underscoring the essential role of functional lysosomes in ox-LDL-driven mTOR signaling (Supplementary Fig. 8e). Recent studies have reported the critical role of mTOR nutrient sensing in regulating macrophage polarization, indicating that the mTOR pathway significantly promotes CAMs activation39–41. We found that the presence of mTOR inhibitor rapamycin significantly reversed the CLP-induced upregulation of inflammatory cytokines such as Il1b, Il6 and Tnf in foam cells, indicating that mTOR inhibits AAMs activation and may affect PPARγ (Fig. 8f). In fact, administration of rapamycin led to an upregulation in PPARγ and ABCG1 levels in ApoE−/− and ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg macrophages (Fig. 8g). Furthermore, functional experiments demonstrated that treatment with rapamycin and PPARγ inhibitors reversed the effects of CLPs on macrophage lipid deposition (Supplementary Fig. 8f, g). These findings elucidate that CLPs reduce the proportion of anti-inflammatory macrophages by promoting mTOR signaling, thereby inhibiting PPARγ-mediated cholesterol efflux.

Neutralization of CHI3L2 protects against atherosclerosis and promotes atheroma resolution

To evaluate the impact of eliminating CHI3L2 expression on atherosclerosis, we tested the effects of a CHI3L2-neutralizing antibody both in vitro and in vivo. First, we assessed the in vitro efficacy of the antibody and found that rhCHI3L2 administration to THP-1-derived macrophages significantly increased cellular lipid deposition and lipid uptake (Fig. 9a, b). In contrast, co-administration of rhCHI3L2 and the CHI3L2-neutralizing antibody significantly reduced both lipid deposition and uptake compared to isotype control IgG antibody (Fig. 9a, b). In vivo, injection of the CHI3L2-neutralizing antibody via tail vein into ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg mice on a WD (Fig. 9c) significantly reduced overall plaque burden in the aorta, as assessed by en-face ORO staining, and decreased lesion area and plaque area in the aortic root, as shown by H&E and ORO staining (Fig. 9d, e). However, there was no effect on the collagen content of the aortic root, as determined by Sirius red staining, compared to isotype control IgG antibody injection (Fig. 9d, e). Given the macrophage cholesterol efflux can promote atherosclerosis regression, we also evaluated the therapeutic effect of CHI3L2 neutralizing antibody on atherosclerosis regression. ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg mice were subjected to 3 months of WD followed by 1 month of ND feeding combined with CHI3L2 neutralizing antibody or IgG control treatment (Fig. 9f). Histological staining revealed that the presence of CHI3L2 antibody significantly reduced plaque size compared to IgG, although it had no significant effect on plaque stability (Fig. 9g, h). These results suggest the potential clinical therapeutic role of CHI3L2-neutralizing antibodies in atherosclerotic disease.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that the natural polymorphism of the mouse CLP family member Chil3 and its human functional homologue CHI3L2 contribute to atherosclerosis. Analysis of public datasets and our scRNA-seq data indicates that CLPs, particularly Chil3, CHI3L1 (Chil1 in mice), and CHI3L2 are predominantly expressed in macrophages42,43. Using chromosomal synteny and macrophage polarization profiles, we identified CHI3L2 as the human CLP most functionally aligned with CHIL3. We also found that CHI3L2 expression was markedly upregulated in THP-1-derived AAMs, closely mirroring the high levels of CHIL3 observed in mouse AAMs. These findings are consistent with previous reports5 and support the use of THP-1-derived macrophages as an initial model for functional studies. Subsequent functional experiments demonstrated that CHI3L2 can substitute for key CHIL3 functions, including promoting dyslipidemia, vascular inflammation, plaque expansion, and plaque instability. However, THP-1 cells do not fully capture the differentiation and activation features of primary human monocyte-derived macrophages, so further validation is needed in future work. Additionally, it is important to emphasize that although this study focused on CHI3L2, the expression of other CLP family members such as CHI3L1 or CHID1 in atherosclerotic tissues suggests that CLP family members may exert cooperative or context dependent effects that require further investigation.

We observed that CLPs significantly altered LDL-C levels, whereas TC remained unchanged, which is not contradictory. LDL-C reflects cholesterol in a specific lipoprotein fraction, whereas TC encompasses LDL, HDL, VLDL and remnants. Consequently, redistribution among different lipoprotein fractions may mask changes in LDL-C. In particular, in the ApoE−/− mouse model, cholesterol is mainly distributed in VLDL and remnant lipoproteins, and LDL constitutes only a portion of TC. Therefore, changes in LDL-C are more likely to reflect remodeling of lipoprotein distribution rather than alterations in overall cholesterol burden. It should be noted that in this study, LDL-C was measured using a homogeneous assay, which may exhibit some cross reactivity with VLDL and remnant lipoproteins under conditions of severe dyslipidemia, particularly in the ApoE−/− model. However, because the same assay was applied consistently across all experimental groups, the relative differences in LDL-C remain comparable. In addition, the changes in ox-LDL levels detected by ELISA further support this conclusion.

Single-cell analysis reveals distinct cell-type specificity for these CLPs: Chil3 is enriched in Ly6C2+ monocyte-like macrophages, whereas CHI3L2 is primarily expressed in foam cells, VSMC-like and FC-like macrophages. Functionally, Chil3 promotes inflammatory macrophage populations, while CHI3L2 expands foam cell/FC-like clusters, reflecting their non-orthologous relationship and subtype-specific effects. Consistent with previous evidence of macrophage plasticity in atherosclerosis44–47, we identified Trem2+ lipid-laden, low-inflammatory macrophages as a late-stage population linked to advanced lesions, with CHI3L2 predominantly expressed in these cells. Moreover, VSMCs exhibit phenotypic plasticity, including macrophage/foam cell-like, FC-like, and myofibroblastic-like states, confirming their contribution as a source of osteochondrogenic cells in plaques11,44. CLPs facilitate the transition of contractile VSMCs toward FC- and macrophage/foam cell-like phenotypes, thereby exacerbating lesion progression and highlighting their broad regulatory role in atherosclerosis-associated cell fate.

Our data indicate that CLPs regulate cholesterol transport in atherosclerotic macrophages by binding to CD36 and promoting CD36-mediated lipid uptake. Although no interactions with OLR1 or MSR1 were detected, their potential involvement cannot be excluded. Interestingly, partial deletions of the CD36 ECD do not affect CLP binding, suggesting that this interaction may rely on the lectin properties of CLPs rather than a specific protein motif42. Consistent with this, members of the GH18 family, including CHIL3 and CHI3L2, contain a β/α triose-phosphate isomerase (TIM) barrel domain with conserved sugar binding sites and a small α + β folding domain27,42,48. Accordingly, we found that CHIL3 and CHI3L2 bind N-linked glycans on CD36, as removal of N-glycosylation by PNGaseF eliminated the interaction. Interesting, in the study of CHI3L1-IL13Rα2 interaction, the mutation of N-linked glycosylation within the extracellular domain of interleukin-13 receptor α2 chain (IL-13Rα2) does not abrogate CHI3L1-IL13Rα2 binding49, however, another study with tunicamycin and PNGaseF demonstrates that their binding and signaling are increased when N-glycosylation is diminished50. Although these findings are sometimes contradictory, they suggest the possibility of glycans involvement in the interaction between CLP and membrane proteins.

The ECD of CD36 is highly N-glycosylated, which is essential for its trafficking to the cell surface25. Although glycosylation is not strictly required for ligand recognition, reduced glycosylation tends to increase binding to acetylated LDL (ac-LDL), while mutants with fewer glycosylation sites retain folding, surface trafficking, and ligand binding, indicating redundancy in glycosylation25. CHIL3 exhibits weak β-N-acetylglucosaminidase activity, suggesting a potential role in glycosaminoglycan digestion26. We found that CLPs reduce N-glycosylation at specific sites (N220, N321) on mouse CD36, suggesting that CLPs enhance ox-LDL binding and internalization by interacting with N-linked glycans on CD36 and reducing their glycosylation. However, our in vitro assessment of rmCD36ECD may not fully reflect physiological conditions, as polysaccharide heterogeneity and dynamic Golgi-mediated glycosylation complicate precise in vivo characterization.

Furthermore, our study demonstrates that CLP deficiency markedly enhances cholesterol efflux mediated by the PPARγ-ABCG1 signaling axis, whereas the expression levels of ABCA1 and its canonical transcriptional regulator LXRα remain unchanged. These findings indicate that CLPs selectively regulate cholesterol efflux pathways. Although ABCG1 and ABCA1 are both generally considered LXR target genes, ABCG1 can be selectively regulated and function independently of classical LXR activation under specific cellular contexts or pathological conditions28. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying this selective regulation of ABCG1 remain unclear and warrant further investigation. Previous studies have reported that ABCG1 deficiency can promote, have no effect on, or attenuate atherosclerotic lesion development in mice, highlighting a strong context dependent role for ABCG1 in atherogenesis51–53. In our model, CLPs promote atherosclerosis by simultaneously enhancing CD36 mediated lipid uptake and suppressing ABCG1 mediated cholesterol efflux. This dual regulatory mechanism is distinct from simple ABCG1 deficiency and may account for the discrepancies between our findings and previous reports. Nevertheless, in the absence of direct comparative experiments, these differences cannot be fully reconciled. Future studies are still needed to clarify how cholesterol efflux transporters such as ABCG1 affect atherosclerosis in different situations.

Importantly, we identify a ligand-independent PPARγ activation pathway. While ox-LDL can act as a PPARγ ligand30,54, CLP deficiency increases PPARγ expression, indicating that macrophage polarization, rather than ligand availability, governs its activation. PPARγ drives phenotypic changes in macrophages following repeated IL-4 stimulation, and its upregulation in AAMs is a conserved phenomenon31,32,34,55. Our previous studies also link macrophage polarization to PPARγ1, explaining why CLP deficiency induces a ligand-independent PPARγ pathway consistent with AAMs. mTOR, a key regulator of lipid metabolism, also participates in macrophage polarization by promoting the production of pro-inflammatory factors39,40. Our findings indicate that ox-LDL internalized by cells activates mTOR upon lysosomal entry, enhancing inflammatory cytokine secretion while reducing anti-inflammatory cytokines. Under these conditions, PPARγ expression decreases, suggesting that CLPs influence macrophage PPARγ expression in a ligand-independent manner, depending on macrophage state. Additionally, CD36-mediated lipid uptake may trigger intracellular cholesterol crystallization and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, enhancing IL-1β maturation56. Our scRNA-seq analysis indicates that the reduced inflammatory cytokine levels in CLP-deficient macrophages partly reflect reduced NLRP3 activity, as shown by decreased NOD-like receptor signaling and Nlrp3 expression in Chil3-deficient foam cells.

Finally, we examined the association between CHI3L2 levels and atherosclerosis in patients and evaluated the therapeutic potential of a CHI3L2-neutralizing antibody in mouse models. Our cohort excluded patients with common comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension, which helped clarify the specific role of CHI3L2 but may limit generalizability. Future studies with larger, more diverse cohorts are needed to validate these findings. Despite this limitation, our work on CHIL3 and CHI3L2 reveals a key paradigm in which mammalian CLPs regulate biological functions via glycosylation of membrane proteins and provides a foundation for developing clinical markers for atherosclerosis.

In conclusion, our study implicates a role for CLPs (CHIL3 and CHI3L2), in the regulation of atherosclerotic progression. Specifically, these proteins alter the fate of vascular-related cells, particularly macrophages and VSMC in disease process. Mechanistically, CLPs act in an autocrine manner to bind and hydrolyze N-glycans on CD36 through their glycosidase activity, thereby promoting lipid uptake. The lipid accumulation activates mTOR, skews macrophage populations toward a proinflammatory state, and suppresses PPARγ-ABCG1-mediated cholesterol efflux. Dual effects on both lipid uptake and cholesterol efflux synergistically amplify foam cell formation and inflammatory responses, consequently exacerbating atherosclerosis (illustrated in Fig. 10).

Mechanisms by which CLPs promote atherosclerosis.

Macrophage derived chitinase-like proteins (CLPs), including mouse CHIL3 and its human homologue CHI3L2, promote the accumulation of foam cell macrophages and drive vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) toward foam cell and fibrochondrocyte (FC)-like phenotypes, thereby exacerbating atherosclerosis. Mechanistically, CLPs act in an autocrine manner by binding to CD36 and hydrolyzing N-linked glycans, predominantly at the N220 and N321 sites, which enhances lipid uptake. Excessive lipid influx activates mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), promotes proinflammatory macrophage polarization, and suppresses peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ)-ATP-binding cassette subfamily G member 1 (ABCG1)-mediated cholesterol efflux.

Methods

Materials

The reagents and antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 4 (Antibodies and manufacturers) and Supplementary Table 5 (Reagents and manufacturers). All chemicals were of analytical grade and used without further purification unless otherwise specified.

Human plasma and aortic tissue samples

Human plasma and aortic tissue samples were collected with approval from the Ethics Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University (No. LLSPJ-2023-304), and all participants provided written informed consent. Identifying information was removed to ensure privacy. Experiments complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Plasma samples were obtained from healthy individuals (n = 34, all healthy individuals underwent checkups that excluded cardiovascular diseases, autoimmune disorders, acute and chronic infections, malignant tumors, and liver or kidney insufficiency) and specimens with atherosclerosis (n = 38), which were diagnosed by ultrasound. Human carotid endarterectomy specimens, including atheromatous plaque stage (APS, n = 19) and fibrous plaque stage (FPS, n = 19), were evaluated blindly by experienced pathologists according to the atherosclerosis staging system, were collected from the Department of Pathology, The First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. Persons with diabetes or hypertension were excluded. The human sample collection was not restricted to a single sex, enhancing the generalizability of the study. Baseline characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2.

Mouse strains

Chil3-deficient congenic mice with B10.RIII background (C57Bl/10 H-2r (BR)) was generated in our previous positionally cloning studies1,57,58, and backcrossed with C57BL/10 H-2q.ApoE−/− mice (denoted as BQ.ApoE−/−) to replace the background to C57BL/10 H-2q (BQ), with ApoE deficiency to accelerate spontaneous atherosclerosis (denoted as BQ.ApoE−/−.Chil3∆, or ApoE−/−.Chil3∆). Human CHI3L2 transgenic mice were created by using a PiggyBac-on-BAC transgenic strategy (Cyagen, China). Human CHI3L2 coding sequence with its regulatory sequence was introduced into the genome of C57BL/6 N mice. The transgenic mice were then backcrossed with BQ.ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ mice to generate BQ.ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg (ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg) mice, for the study of human CHI3L2 gene in mouse with a Chil3 deficient context. C57BL/6-Ly5.1-Pep3b (B6.CD45.1) mice, gifted by Prof. Baojun Zhang from Xi’an Jiaotong University, were crossed with BQ.ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg mice to generate BQ.ApoE−/−.CD45.1 mice, BQ.ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.CD45.1 mice and BQ.ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg.CD45.1 littermate mice. To achieve myeloid-specific overexpression of CHI3L2, we utilized a Cre-loxP-based conditional knock-in strategy (C57BL/6 N genetic background). Briefly, mice harboring a loxP-flanked STOP (LSL) cassette upstream of the human CHI3L2 gene (Cyagen, China) were crossed with myeloid Lyz2 (also known as LysM)-Cre mice. For breeding, heterozygous floxed CHI3L2 knock-in mice (CHI3L2ki/wt) were paired with LysM-Cre mice to obtain offspring carrying both the knock-in allele and the Cre transgene (M-CHI3L2ki/wt). Littermates carrying the wild-type CHI3L2 allele together with LysM-Cre (M-CHI3L2wt/wt) served as controls.

In the initial phenotypic experiments, both male and female mice were used, but no significant sex differences were observed (Fig. 2). For subsequent experiments, only male mice were chosen to reduce variability and simplify result interpretation. We also ensured that the number of mice in each group was balanced. All these mouse strains were maintained in specific-pathogen free conditions in the Experimental Animal Center in Xi’an Jiaotong University. All mice were housed in a temperature-controlled environment at 22 °C under 12 h light/dark cycles. All animal experiments were performed according to the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Use and approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Xi’an Jiaotong University (No.2023-1244).

Animal diet feeding and atherosclerotic lesions analysis

All male and female mice were fed a high-fat Western diet (WD; 41% kcal from fat, 0.15% kcal from cholesterol; Medicience, MD12015, China) starting at 8 weeks of age and maintained on the diet for 3 months. Our study examined male and female animals, and similar findings are reported for both sexes. To assess the effect of myeloid-specific CHI3L2 overexpression on atherosclerosis, each 8-week-old male mouse (M-CHI3L2ki/wt and M-CHI3L2wt/wt) was intravenously injected with 5 × 10¹¹ viral genomes of rAAV8-mPCSK9 (WZ Biosciences, China) and continued on WD for 3 months. CO2 was used to euthanize mice. After sacrifice, whole aortas were isolated and stained with Oil Red O (ORO), and en-face lesion area was measured by morphometry using Image J software (National Institutes of Health Software). Heart tissues containing aortic roots were carefully dissected and cryosectioned frozen sectioning. Serial cross-sections of 10 μm were cut through the aorta beginning at the origin of the aortic valve leaflets and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), ORO, Sirius Red staining and immunostained for α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), CD68, intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), or vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1) to quantify lesions in the aortic sinus. Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics) was used for quantification.

Quantification of lipid levels

Serum TC, TG, LDL-C and HDL-C were measured using an automatic biochemical analyzer (Beckman Coulter AU5431) with corresponding enzymatic colorimetric assay kits. Briefly, TC was determined by hydrolysis of CE with cholesterol esterase, followed by oxidation of free cholesterol by cholesterol oxidase to generate hydrogen peroxide, which reacts with a chromogenic substrate to produce a color proportional to TC concentration. TG was measured via a glycerol phosphate oxidase-peroxidase method, in which TG are hydrolyzed to glycerol and fatty acids, and glycerol is oxidized to produce hydrogen peroxide for colorimetric detection. LDL-C and HDL-C were quantified using homogeneous enzymatic colorimetric assays, in which specific surfactants and masking agents selectively solubilize non-target lipoproteins, allowing enzymatic oxidation of LDL-C or HDL-C. The resulting color intensity is proportional to LDL-C or HDL-C concentration.

Bone marrow transplantation (BMT)

Recipient CD45.2 male ApoE−/− mice at 8 weeks of age were irradiated with 2 doses of 4.25 Gy X-rays, separated by a 4-hour interval (Elekta Synergy, Sweden). BM cells were harvested from femurs and tibias of euthanized donor CD45.1 ApoE−/−, ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ or littermate ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg mice under sterile conditions. Briefly, bones were flushed with Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and passed through a 40 µm filter. BM cells (5 × 106) were injected via the tail vein into each recipient ApoE−/− mouse 6 h post radiation exposure to replenish the hematopoietic system. Successful BMT was confirmed after 3 weeks by detecting CD45.1 from transplanted mice using flow cytometry. BMT mice could then undergo 12 weeks of WD.

Administration of rhCHI3L2 protein in vivo

The recombinant human CHI3L2 (rhCHI3L2) protein, labeled with hIgG1-Fc, was produced by Tsingke (Shanghai, China, endotoxin levels below 5 EU/mg). For these experiments, 2-month-old ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ male mice were initially fed a WD for 1 month. Subsequently, the mice received intraperitoneal injections of either hFc-rhCHI3L2 protein (0.25 µg/g) or hFc-IgG1 controls (0.25 µg/g) every 3 days during the final 2 months of WD feeding.

Evaluation of CHI3L2 antibody efficacy in vitro and in vivo

In in vitro studies, THP-1-derived macrophages were stimulated with rhCHI3L2 in the presence or absence of a CHI3L2-neutralizing antibody (Sino Biological, Beijing, China; endotoxin <1 EU/mg). Cellular lipid deposition and uptake were assessed by flow cytometry. For in vivo experiments, 2-month-old ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg male mice were fed a WD for 1 month, then received monthly tail vein injections of CHI3L2-neutralizing antibody (5 μg/g) or IgG control (5 μg/g) for 2 months. To evaluate plaque regression, 2-month-old ApoE−/−.Chil3∆.hCHI3L2tg male mice were fed a WD for 3 months, then given a single tail vein injection of CHI3L2 neutralizing antibody or IgG, followed by 1 month on an ND.

Aortic cell isolation and flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from the mouse aorta (whole aortic arch and thoracic aorta) using enzymatic digestion59. Briefly, aortas were cut into small pieces and incubated in 1 mL enzyme cocktail (450 U/mL collagenase I, 125 U/mL collagenase XI, 60 U/mL hyaluronidase I-S, 60 U/mL DNase I; all in DMEM for 1 h at 37 °C. Digested tissues were filtered through a 40 µm strainer, and cells were centrifuged at 1200 × g, 4 °C for 10 min after erythrocyte lysis with ACK buffer. Cell pellets were used for flow cytometry. Anticoagulated blood was treated with ACK lysis and directly analyzed. BMT efficiency was confirmed by staining with PerCP anti-mouse CD45.1 and BV785 anti-mouse CD45.2 antibodies. Samples were acquired on a NovoCyte 3005 flow cytometer (Agilent) and analyzed using NovoSampler Pro software (Agilent).

scRNA-seq analysis

Aortic tissues from WD-fed male littermates were collected, and six aortas were pooled for scRNA-seq analysis at LC Bio Technology Co., Ltd (Hangzhou, China). Single-cell libraries were prepared using 10X Genomics protocols and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Reads were aligned to the mm10 reference genome with Cell Ranger (v6.1.1), including the human CHI3L2 gene. Cells were filtered based on mitochondrial gene content (<5%) and gene counts (200–2500) using Seurat 4.3.260. The top 2,000 variable genes (‘vst’ method) were used to compute principal components (PCs), and 33 significant PCs were selected via the ElbowPlot method. Batch effects were corrected with Harmony v1.0. Clustering and UMAP visualization were performed using Seurat functions, and cluster-specific marker genes were identified with FindAllMarkers. Pseudotime trajectory analysis was conducted with Monocle 3. Public scRNA-seq datasets (GSE155513, GSE159677) were analyzed similarly, using tSNE for visualization.

Immunofluorescence staining

In brief, after EDTA antigen retrieval, sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min and blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min at room temperature (paraffin sections were routinely dewaxed and hydrated). Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies in a humidified chamber. After three PBS washes, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 (AF488)- or Rhodamine-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. For directly labeled antibodies, AF488 anti-mouse CD68 was added after blocking. For co-staining, APC-CD36 or AF488 CD68 antibodies were added simultaneously with secondary antibodies. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Images were captured on a multiphoton microscope (SP8 DIVE, Leica) and acquired/analyzed with Leica LAS X 3.5.7. Quantification was performed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0.

Macrophage acquisition and cultivation

Mouse peritoneal macrophages (PMφ) were obtained from 6-to-8-week-old mice following intraperitoneal injection of 2 mL 3% thioglycolate broth. Three days later, mice were euthanized, and peritoneal cells were harvested by lavage with 5 mL ice-cold PBS/EDTA, centrifuged (1200 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), lysed with ACK buffer, washed, and cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS). After 6 h, non-adherent cells were washed off with pre-warmed PBS, and fresh medium was added for experiments.

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were generated by flushing femurs and tibias with sterile PBS, filtering through a 40 µm strainer, centrifuging, and lysing with ACK buffer. Cells were seeded at 2 ×106 cells/mL in DMEM with 10% FBS and macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF, 10 ng/mL) for 7 days.

THP-1 monocytes (human monocytic leukemia cell line) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% PS, then differentiated into macrophages using 10 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) for 48 h before harvesting for experiments.

Foam cell formation, lipid uptake and cholesterol efflux

Macrophages were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% PS, and treated with or without ox-LDL (30 μg/mL) for 48 h. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min at room temperature and washed with PBS. For ORO staining, cells were immersed in ORO solution for 20 min, rinsed with PBS, counterstained with hematoxylin, washed again with PBS, and visualized by light microscopy. Foam cells were identified by the presence of ORO-positive lipid droplets and quantified as a percentage of total cells. For neutral lipid staining, cells were incubated with BODIPY493/503 for 30 min at 37 °C and analyzed by flow cytometry or multiphoton microscopy. Foam cell formation was quantified based on the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of BODIPY staining. For lipid uptake assays, cells were incubated with DiI-ox-LDL (30 μg/mL) for 2 h at 37 °C, followed by analysis using flow cytometry or multiphoton microscopy. Where indicated, cells were pretreated with recombinant proteins or signaling pathway inhibitors for 4 h prior to the addition of ox-LDL or DiI-ox-LDL.

The cholesterol efflux assay performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, macrophages were seeded in 96-well plates (1 × 105 cells per well) and labeled with fluorescent cholesterol at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by equilibration for 16 h. Cells were then incubated with ApoB-depleted serum (HDL-containing) for an additional 4 h. Fluorescence intensities in the culture medium and cell lysates were measured using an Infinite M200 microplate reader (Ex/Em = 485/523 nm, Tecan), and cholesterol efflux was calculated based on the ratio of fluorescence in the medium to total fluorescence.

VSMC acquisition, cultivation and stimulation

Primary vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) were isolated from 6-8-week-old mice. Briefly, the entire aorta was excised, cleaned of connective tissue, and rinsed in cold PBS. After removal of the endothelial layer by gentle luminal scraping, aortas were minced and digested in DMEM containing collagenase II (1 mg/mL), elastase (0.5 mg/mL), and DNase-I (60 U/mL) at 37 °C for 1 h. The cell suspension was filtered, centrifuged, and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% PS. Cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2, and medium was replaced every 2–3 days. VSMCs between passages 3 and 6 were used for experiments.

To assess macrophage-VSMC crosstalk, BMDMs were treated with ox-LDL (50 μg/mL) for 48 h, and the conditioned media were applied to VSMCs for an additional 48 h. Lipid accumulation in VSMCs was evaluated by BODIPY staining and flow cytometry. For calcification assays, BMDMs were cultured for 10 days in the presence of ox-LDL (50 μg/mL) and calcification medium (CaP medium, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate and 1.5 mM CaCl2)61. The resulting conditioned media were used to stimulate VSMCs for 14 days, and calcification was assessed by Alizarin Red staining. For Alizarin red staining, VSMCs in 6-well plates were fixed in paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, incubated with 2% Alizarin red for 10 min and washed with double-distilled H2O. Positively stained cells appeared to have a reddish/purple color.

Macrophage polarization and signaling pathway studies

For macrophage polarization, lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/mL) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ, 10 ng/mL) were treated to macrophage to polarize the CAMs, while IL−4 (20 ng/mL) and IL-13 (50 ng/mL) were treated to macrophage to polarize the AAMs.

To study the cell signaling activation, macrophages were treated with or without PPARγ inhibitor T0070907 (1 μM), lysosomal function inhibitor Bafilomycin A1 (Baf-A1, 100 nM) or mTOR inhibitor rapamycin (100 nM) in the presence of ox-LDL for indicated times.

RNA extraction, quantitative real-time PCR and RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted from aortic tissues or macrophages by TRIzol reagent and reversely transcribed into cDNA using RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-qPCR was performed using FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master. All primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table 3. Relative mRNA expression was normalized to β-actin and calculated by the 2-ΔΔCt method.

For RNA-seq, ApoE−/− or ApoE−/−.Chil3∆ BMDMs treated with ox-LDL were dissolved in TRIzol and the RNA libraries were sequenced on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform by LC Bio Technology CO., Ltd (Hangzhou). Bioinformatic analysis was performed using the OmicStudio tools at https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool.

Protein extraction and Western blotting

Proteins were extracted by ice-cold radioim-munoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer containing proteinase inhibitor and/or phosphatase inhibitors, and quantified using a BCA kit. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 7.5% or 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 10% nonfat milk in TBST for 2 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. After washing with TBST three times for 10 min each, the blots were visualized and imaged using ECL Plus Kit and Genegenome XRQ NPC Chemiluminescent Imaging Analysis System (Syngene). The proteins were normalized with β-actin or β-Tubulin and respective total protein content. Densitometric analysis of the gels was done using ImageJ software. Antibody information is provided in the Supplementary Information, and uncropped blots are available as Source Data or Supplementary Information.

Plasmid construction and transfection

Recombinant lentiviruses targeting mouse CD36 (Lv-sh mCD36) or control were generated by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The shRNA target sequence is listed in Supplementary Table 3. BMDMs were infected at 70–80% confluency and analyzed 72 h post-transduction. Cells were then treated as indicated for subsequent experiments. Plasmids were transfected into 293T cells using Lipofectamine 8000, and cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for further analyses. Truncated and mutant Flag-mCD36 constructs were generated by Tsingke Biotech (Beijing, China) and sequence-verified.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assay

CHIL3 and CHI3L2 levels were quantified using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. CHI3L2 values were log-transformed to normalize data distribution. Mouse serum ox-LDL levels were measured using an ELISA kit. Absorbance was read at 405 nm using an Infinite M200 plate reader. In some experiments, chemiluminescent enzyme immunoassay was used.

For in vitro binding assays of CLPs to ox-LDL, ox-LDL (5 μg/mL) was encapsulated in a 96-well plate at 4 °C overnight. Then 5 μg/mL of His-rCHIL3 (positive control His & hFc-rmCD36) or His-rhCHI3L2 protein (positive control His & hFc-rmCD36) was added and incubated for 3 h at room temperature. Then, HRP-conjugated mouse anti-His antibody and HRP substrate were sequentially added to the wells and incubated for 1 h at room temperature.

To test the effect of CLPs on the binding of CD36ECD to ox-LDL, His & hFc-rmCD36 with/without His-rCHIL3, hFc-rhCD36 with/without His-rhCHI3L2, PNGase F-treated rmCD36 or rhCD36 were first incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. ox- LDL (5 μg/mL) was encapsulated on a 96-well plate at 4 °C overnight. Then approximately 5 μg/mL of CLP-treated or non-treated rCD36 was added and incubated at room temperature for 3 h after blocking using 1% BSA. PNGase F-treated rCD36 was used as a positive control. Peroxidase AffiniPure goat anti-human IgG, Fcγ fragment specific antibody and HRP substrate were then added to the wells and incubated for 1 h.

Immunoprecipitation and mass spectrometry (IP-MS)

Cell lysates were prepared by using ice-cold cell lysis buffer for Western and IP supplemented with proteinase inhibitor. To identify endogenous CLP-interacting proteins in macrophages, cell lysates were incubated with His-tagged CHIL3 for 1 h at room temperature. Control or specific antibodies (anti-IgG/anti-His) were added to the lysates and incubated for 12 h at 4 °C with constant rotation. Then, 30 µL of pre-washed Pierce Protein A/G beads were added and the mixture was incubated for an additional 4 h at 4 °C. The antigen-antibody-bead complexes were washed five times with PBS and sent for mass spectrometry (MS) analysis, analyzed by Shanghai Bioprofile Technology (China). Briefly, the samples (1 biological replicate for IgG and His groups) were enzymatically digested, and the resulting peptides were desalted using C18 StageTip and vacuum-dried. The peptides were separated using an Easy nLC 1200 system with solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (80% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid in water) for gradient elution. The separated peptides were analyzed using a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer in DDA mode, with a precursor ion scan range of 350-1800 m/z, a resolution of 60,000 for MS1, and a resolution of 15,000 for MS2. Data were analyzed using MaxQuant 2.0.1.0, and the UniProt-Reference proteome-Mus musculus (Mouse) [10090]-55286-20230104.fasta database was used for peptide identification. Filtering criteria included PSM FDR ≤ 0.01 and protein FDR ≤ 0.01. The IP-MS identification results are provided in the Source Data, with a focus on membrane proteins.

For the co-IP experiments used in Western blotting, proteins were extracted from 293T cells transfected with the relevant plasmids. Alternatively, recombinant CD36 proteins were deglycosylated and incubated with recombinant CHIL3 or CHI3L2 at 37 °C for 1 h prior to IP. After the immune complex formation, immunocomplexes were eluted with 2× loading buffer by boiling at 95 °C for 5 min, separated by SDS-PAGE, and analyzed by immunoblotting with appropriate antibodies.

N-glycosylation removal assay

Non-denaturing deglycosylation of rCD36 was performed. Briefly, rCD36 proteins were mixed with GlycoBuffer 2 (10×) and PNGase F, and the mixture was incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. The deglycosylated samples were then used for subsequent experiments.

Lectin blotting

rCD36 was incubated/not incubated with equal amounts of rCHIL3 or rhCHI3L2 for 24 h at 37 °C, with PNGase F-treated rCD36 as a positive control. Proteins were separated by SDS‑PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Glycan content on CD36 was assessed by lectin blotting using biotin‑conjugated wheat germ agglutinin (bio‑WGA). Incubation with 5 μg/mL bio-WGA was performed for 2 h, followed by 1 h of incubation with Streptavidin HRP. Signals were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence.

Glycomics and analysis

Label-free quantitation of intact glycopeptides and modified glycans by MS was performed by incubating rCD36 with/without rCHIL3 or rhCHI3L2 at 37 °C for 24 h. Glycan mass spectrometry was done and quantitatively analyzed by Shanghai Bioprofile Technology (China). The quantitative analysis results of glycosylation levels at each site after correcting for protein concentration were shown by column diagram, and the quantitative results of glycoforms at each site after correcting for protein concentration were shown by annular heat map. The glycoforms information is presented in the Source Data.

Non-targeted lipidomic analysis

Non-targeted lipidomic analysis was performed by LC Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). Briefly, cell samples were extracted using a modified methyl tert-butyl ether method. Lipids were separated on an ACQUITY UPLC CSH C18 column using a gradient of mobile phase A (acetonitrile/water, containing ammonium formate and formic acid) and mobile phase B (isopropanol/acetonitrile, containing ammonium formate and formic acid). The separated lipids were analyzed by high-resolution mass spectrometry in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes, with full-scan and data-dependent acquisition. Raw data were processed using XCMS and metaX for peak detection, alignment, and retention time correction, and metabolites were annotated by matching to KEGG and HMDB databases. During data processing, for each genotype, lipid abundances in ox-LDL treated samples were normalized to their corresponding untreated control groups to obtain relative lipid change values.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, while the equality of variances was determined using the F-test. The statistical significance of the difference between means was assessed using the two-tailed Student’s t-test (for normally distributed data) or Mann Whitney U test (for not normally distributed data). One-way ANOVA with appropriate post-hoc correction was utilized for comparing the effect of treatment within a group. Two-way ANOVA with appropriate post-hoc correction was used when two conditions were considered between the groups. Adjusted P values were used for the post-hoc tests. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism, version 9.0. Specific statistical information is provided in the Source Data.
