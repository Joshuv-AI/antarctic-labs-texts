Crosstalk between noncoding RNAs and autophagy in renal cell carcinoma: Deciphering molecular pathways and therapeutic prospects
Authors: Mina Alimohammadi, Mojgan Noroozi, Alireza Mafi, Fateme Zare Khormizi, Amirhosein Abbasi, Najma Farahani, Seyedeh Mahdieh Khoshnazar, Mehrdad Hashemi, Afshin Taheriazam, Kiavash Hushmandi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is one of the most common and aggressive forms of kidney cancer, accounting for over 90% of cases. Despite advances in diagnosis and treatment, RCC is often detected at advanced stages and demonstrates poor response to traditional therapies such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and hormonal treatment. Consequently, novel molecular targets are urgently needed. Autophagy, a tightly regulated catabolic mechanism that preserves cellular homeostasis via degradation of damaged organelles and proteins, plays a dual role in RCC—acting both as a tumor suppressor in early stages and a tumor promoter under stress conditions. Recent studies have revealed that non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), particularly long ncRNAs and microRNAs (miRNAs), are key regulators of autophagy in various cancers, including RCC. These ncRNAs influence the expression of autophagy-related genes and modulate critical signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT/mTOR, AMPK, p53, and KEAP1/NRF2. By acting as molecular sponges, scaffolds, and transcriptional regulators, ncRNAs either enhance or suppress autophagic activity, thereby affecting tumor progression, metastasis, and treatment resistance. This review synthesizes current knowledge on the crosstalk between ncRNAs and autophagy in RCC. We identify specific ncRNAs involved in RCC pathogenesis, describe their regulatory mechanisms, and evaluate their potential as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Understanding these complex molecular interactions may lead to more effective, personalized treatment strategies and improved clinical outcomes for RCC patients.

Background

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is one of the most frequent types of kidney cancer, accounting for over 90% of all cases [1]. It is a highly aggressive disorder with a high risk of metastasis that is frequently detected late due to its asymptomatic character in early development. The primary treatment for localized RCC is surgical resection; however, many patients develop recurrence, and metastatic RCC is difficult to treat due to resistance to traditional medications such as chemotherapy, radiation, and hormone therapy [2]. As a result, several investigations have identified novel molecular pathways and therapeutic targets that may enhance patient prognosis and treatment outcomes.

One major topic of interest in RCC research is the function of autophagy, a closely regulated cellular process that degrades and recycles damaged organelles, misfolded proteins, and other cellular detritus [3]. Autophagy is a critical homeostatic system that protects cellular integrity and responds to metabolic stress. Autophagy plays a complex and dual-faceted role in cancer—it can act as a tumor suppressor in the early stages by preventing the accumulation of damaged cellular components, but it can also promote tumor survival in the later stages by providing cancer cells with metabolic flexibility under stressful conditions [4]. Given this paradoxical character, using autophagy as a treatment option necessitates a thorough understanding of its regulatory mechanisms in RCC.

Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), which include microRNAs (miRNAs) and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), have lately emerged as important regulators of autophagy in a variety of malignancies [5]. These RNA molecules do not encode proteins, but they are vital for gene expression, cellular signaling, and epigenetic regulation. Specifically, ncRNAs regulate essential autophagy system components, including autophagy-related genes (ARGs) and associated signaling cascades like the PI3K/Akt/mTOR and AMPK pathways [6]. The dysregulation of ncRNAs can have a major impact on autophagy activity, either boosting or decreasing it, influencing RCC development, metastasis, and treatment resistance [7]. Among ncRNAs, lncRNAs have been singled out for their function in RCC advancement via autophagy control. LncRNAs act as molecular sponges, scaffolds, or transcriptional regulators, influencing the expression of autophagy genes and pathways. Several autophagy-related lncRNAs (ARlncRNAs) have been discovered as having an effect on RCC cell proliferation, apoptosis, and treatment resistance [8]. However, the specific processes by which these lncRNAs contribute to autophagy and RCC pathogenesis are still unknown. The use of ARLNRs as possible biomarkers for RCC prognosis and therapy response is a promising path in customized cancer treatment.

This paper will provide a detailed summary of the role of non-coding RNA-mediated autophagy regulation in RCC. It will investigate the complex link between ncRNAs and autophagy, highlighting major regulatory processes and discussing the implications for RCC treatment methods. Understanding these molecular interactions could open the way for new therapeutic approaches that use ncRNAs to influence autophagy, thereby improving RCC patient outcomes.

Autophagy pathways and molecular mechanisms

The nature of life is reflected in the equilibrium between the synthesis and degradation of biological materials. By eliminating harmful protein aggregates and aging or damaged organelles, autophagy, one of the primary catabolic processes in eukaryotic cells, helps to preserve this equilibrium. Autophagy supplies essential elements for the production of biomolecules by recycling macromolecules back into the cytosol. Consequently, autophagy plays a crucial role in the response to microenvironmental stress and is necessary for preserving bioenergetics in cell equilibrium. Evolution has maintained autophagy, also known as “self-eating” in Greek, as a controlled, energy-dependent mechanism. Autophagy contributes to sorting and recycling pathogens, long-lived proteins, and redundant or damaged cellular organelles. Most cell types require a basal level of autophagy to keep up cellular equilibrium under normal physiological conditions, but during formation and in response to several stressors, including food deprivation, damaged DNA, a lack of oxygen or hypoxia, and metabolic or oxidative stress, autophagy can be induced [9]. In mammalian cells, three different kinds of autophagy have been discovered: macro-autophagy, chaperone-mediated autophagy (also known as CMA and microautophagy. These types of autophagy are distinguished by the initial signals, the sort of cargo they carry, and the mechanism’s level of specificity [10].

As shown in Fig. 1, in micro-autophagy, the lysosomal membrane is invaded again, resulting in the development of an autophagic body and the subsequent destruction of the separated cytoplasm [11]. HSP70 and a group of different chaperones that are specific to cytosolic target proteins with a KFERQ-like motif and interact with LAMP2A (referred to as lysosome-associated membrane protein type 2a) to transport cargo directly to the lysosomal membrane are involved in CMA, which is only found in mammalian cells [12]. The main autophagic breakdown form, known as macro-autophagy (from this point on autophagy), is a multi-step process that starts with the cytoplasmic components being engulfed into a structure with dual membranes shaped like crescents called a phagophore. A group of evolutionarily conserved autophagy-related genes (Atg) and the proteins they encode, Atg proteins, control this process. These proteins are attracted to the hierarchy of the pre-autophagosomal structure (also known as PAS), the site of phagophore formation. Therefore, autophagy provides the elements required for retaining cellular homeostasis, which is vital to life and proliferation [13].

The primary self-degradative process, called macroautophagy, or autophagy, is demonstrated by the production of double-membrane autophagosomes. In the field of anticancer therapy, autophagy has two roles: it can act both cytoprotective and cytostatic, known as cytoprotective and cytostatic autophagy, respectively. Pharmacological small molecules that either stimulate or inhibit autophagy have shown encouraging outcomes in the therapy of cancer [14]. The mTOR/PI3K/Akt, AMPK, and MAPK signaling pathways and their upstream and downstream factors all serve important roles in cancer and autophagy [15].

Different causes, including amino acid depletion, reduced insulin and ATP levels, and lack of oxygen, can induce autophagy in organisms (Fig. 2) [16]. An important regulator of the autophagy lysosomal process, transcription factor EB (TFEB) is a member of the microphthalmia group of the MiT family (basic helix-loop-helix leucine-zipper (bHLH-Zip) transcription factors). Starvation-induced autophagy is controlled by TFEB, which raises the expression of lysosomal and autophagy genes. When mTORC1 (refer to mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1) kinase complex is deactivated, as happens when a cell is starved, transcription factor EB phosphorylation is inhibited, enabling TFEB to transfer into the nucleus. Genes related to autophagy and lysosomal exocytosis are induced when TFEB interacts with the coordinated lysosomal expression and regulation (known as CLEAR) element of genes once they arrive in the nucleus [16, 17].

The PI3K (class III phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase) -associated kinase family consists of the mTORC1 kinase complex, which inhibits autophagy [18]. Phosphorylating ULK1 and Atg13 specifically causes mTORC1 to reduce autophagy via adversely controlling the activity of the ULK1 complex, which comprises Atg13, focal adhesion kinase family interacting protein (FIP) 200, ULK1, and Atg101. Furthermore, via regulating TFEB’s location within lysosomes, mTORC1 governs autophagy [16]. The active (GTP-binding) form of the brain-enriched low-molecular-weight G protein Ras homolog (known as Rheb) promotes mTORC1 on the surface of lysosomes [19]. Rag, a low-molecular-weight G protein, further controls the subcellular location of mTORC1. Rag is formed by heterodimers of RagA/B and RagC/D. When amino acids are scarce, Rag modulates mTORC1 activity by altering the protein’s conformation through its interaction with GDP/GTP [20]. The phagophore is formed via a PI3K complex, which is mobilized downstream of ULK1 upon mTORC1 deactivation. The class III PI3K complex, which consists of VPS34, VPS15, Beclin-1, and Atg14, produces phosphatidylinositol-3-phosphate (PI3P) and forms omegasomes during the early phases of phagophore development [21]. Furthermore, phosphoinositide-interacting protein (known as WIPI), which has a WD repeat domain, functions as a PI3P effector protein, and WIPI2 interacts with Atg2. For autophagosome production to happen, Atg2 contributes to the transport of lipids, and the membrane is related to the phagophore and endoplasmic reticulum [22]. Autophagosome formation is mediated via two ubiquitin-like binding reaction systems, the Atg12-Atg5 binding reaction system and the LC3 or the microtubule-related protein light chain 3 binding reaction system. The ubiquitin-like protein LC3 in the LC3-binding system interacts with phosphatidylcholine (PE) to contribute to the elongation and isolation of the autophagosome membrane. The ubiquitin-like protein Atg12, by covalent interaction, forms with Atg5 through Atg7 and Atg10 in the Atg12-Atg5 binding system. Next, Atg16L1 is attached and a link to the Atg12-Atg5-binding system [23]. The phagophore attracts this conjugate, generating E3-like activity toward the LC3-PE complex. The lipidized LC3 binds to several selective autophagy receptors to preferentially degrade certain substrates through its E3-like action. The phagophore must extend and close to complete autophagosome development. Autophagosome closure is aided by the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (refer to ESCRT) complex. Two types of soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive protein (NSF) attachment protein receptor (SNARE) complexes, formed by a synaptosomal-associated protein (SNAP) 29 and lysosomal vesicle-associated membrane protein (VAMP)7/VAMP8, facilitate the fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes. Autolysosomes, which degrade the cargo, develop when lysosomes and autophagosomes fuse [24, 25].

The process of controlling autophagy is very intricate. Growth factors, nutrition, and energy signals are significantly associated with the autophagy inhibitor mammalian target of rapamycin (refer to mTOR) [26]. As a key effector of mTOR phosphorylation in mammalian cells, p70S6K (p70 ribosomal S6 protein kinase) is a potential substrate of mTOR [27]. The signaling pathways for phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) are vital for the initiation and progression of certain human malignancies. However, the precise roles of these subfamilies in BBR-induced autophagy in gastric cancer cells remain to be determined [26].

Molecular mechanisms of macroautophagy

Starvation and stress strongly activate macroautophagy. During the initiation phase, the ULK complex—composed of ATG13, ATG101, FIP200 (also known as RB1CC1), and ULK1 (or ULK2)—assembles near the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane, providing a scaffold for autophagosome formation. Recent studies suggest that the assembly and function of the ULK complex, particularly the organization of FIP200, may involve the formation of biomolecular condensates through liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), which facilitates the nucleation of autophagosomes [28]. Similarly, in yeast, the Atg1 complex acts as a scaffold for the pre-autophagosomal structure (PAS), and its assembly is also mediated by LLPS through interactions among Atg1, Atg13, and the Atg17-Atg29-Atg31 subcomplex [29]. Consequently, it is thought that the starvation-induced ULK/Atg1 complex assembly, which LLPS facilitates, is required for autophagosome formation. Recent research suggests that cargo forces can also trigger the construction of the ULK complex, in addition to the starvation-induced process. Ubiquitin is often utilized to designate autophagic cargo, and the ubiquitin-binding macroautophagy adaptor SQSTM1 (also referred to as p62) can recognize this ubiquitin. Through its interaction with FIP200 in the ULK complex, SQSTM1 recruits the ULK complex and creates a liquid-like biomolecular condensate that contains ubiquitinated cargos [30]. This facilitates the SQSTM1 condensate to selectively degrade ubiquitinated cargo proteins. The SQSTM1 condensate also has other ubiquitin-binding macroautophagy adaptors that make it easier for the ULK complex to join with FIP200These adaptors include TAX1BP1 and NDP52 (sometimes referred to as CALCOCO2) [31].

NBR1, a ubiquitin-binding macroautophagy adaptor, connects with SQSTM1 to control how SQSTM1 condenses and binds to TAX1BP1 to recruit the ULK complex (Fig. 3) [32, 33]. The ULK complex recruits the class III phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase complex I and ATG9 vesicles after assembly. ATG9 vesicles in yeast form the foundation of autophagosomal membranes, whereas at the site of autophagosome formation, the class III phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase complex I produces PI3P [34], which in turn recruits PI3P-binding effectors, including in mammals WIPI1, WIPI2, WIPI3, and WIPI4, and the last phase the lipid transfer protein ATG2. Generally, ATG9 vesicles take part in the ATG13-ATG101 subcomplex [35–37] inside the ULK complex to localize them to the site of autophagosome formation. The ubiquitin-binding macroautophagy adaptor OPTN can recruit these vesicles to ubiquitinated mitochondria during macromitophagy [38]. Furthermore, an increase in ATG9 vesicles is observed as a consequence of NBR1 overexpression, indicating that NBR1 is also involved in recruiting ATG9 vesicles [39]. Thus, like the previously discussed cargo-driven assembly of the ULK complex, Cargo triggers the initiation of this ATG9 recruitment. Consequently, a common mechanism to induce selective macroautophagy may involve cargo-driven assembly of the first ATG components. The next step is the elongation of the phagophore membrane, the precursor of the autophagosome. Though the molecular mechanisms behind this process have remained unclear, new findings provided clarity.

One of the special ATG proteins, ATG9, assembles into a homo-trimeric complex within the phagophore membrane. Acting as a scramblase, it interacts with the lipid transfer protein ATG2 [40, 41] and facilitates the transfer of phospholipids between the outer and inner layers of the phagophore membrane [42, 43]. The WIPI3/4 family proteins directly and the WIPI1/2 family proteins indirectly bind ATG2 to the autophagosome development site. Furthermore, VMP1 and TMEM41B, two ER membrane proteins with scramblase activity, interact with ATG2 [42, 44, 45]. ATG2 functions as a bridge in the membrane elongation process, transferring phospholipids from the outer layer of the ER membrane to the outer layer of the phagophore membrane. The ATG9 scramblase complex then repositions these ER-derived phospholipids to the inner layer of the phagophore membrane, facilitating its elongation. The process also involves two ubiquitin-like conjugation systems, the ATG8 and ATG12 systems, which play a role in this elongation. In mammals, ATG8 family proteins (LC3-PE and GABARAP-PE) are formed when the E1-like enzyme ATG7 and the E2-like enzyme ATG3 conjugate ATG8 family proteins to PE. Similarly, ATG7 and ATG10 work together to conjugate the ubiquitin-like protein ATG12 to its substrate ATG5. In the LC3/GABARAP conjugation process, the ATG12-ATG5 conjugate interacts with ATG16L1 to form the ATG12-ATG5-ATG16L1 complex, which acts as an E3-like enzyme. Both GABARAP and LC3 are present on the phagophore membrane, with GABARAP playing a role in the early phase and LC3 in the later stages of autophagosome formation. GABARAP is likely found in its lipidated form. These proteins interact through the GABARAP-interacting motif (GIM), ATG8-interacting motif (AIM), and LC3-interacting region (LIR), commonly referred to as LIR motifs. Several key ATG proteins, such as FIP200, ULK1, ATG13, ATG14, and ATG2, contain a shared tetrapeptide motif (W/F/Y-x-x-L/I/V) that allows interaction with ATG8 family proteins. Recent studies suggest that ATG16L1 and the PI3P effector WIPI2 cooperate to enhance ATG8 family protein lipidation at the autophagosome formation site, thereby promoting LIR-mediated recruitment of ATG2 [46]. It seems that these interactions among core ATG proteins and the ATG8 family members are important for autophagosome formation [47]. ATG8 family members play an essential role in cargo identification during ubiquitin-dependent and ubiquitin-independent forms of selective macroautophagy. Ubiquitin-binding adaptors, including SQSTM1, NBR1, OPTN, NDP52, TAX1BP1, and TOLLIP, interact with ubiquitinated cargo proteins or organelles in ubiquitin-dependent selective macroautophagy. These adaptors have LIR motifs, which cause phagophores to detect ATG8 family proteins and selectively degrade these cargos [48].

Recent investigation suggests that the specific engulfment of SQSTM1 condensates via the autophagosomal membrane is facilitated by the binding among LC3/GABARAP and the LIR motif of SQSTM1 [49]. During this process, which is affected by “membrane wetting,” the autophagosomal membrane gets distorted, and membrane elongation is encouraged at the condensate surface mediated by the SQSTM1 condensates [48]. In addition to SQSTM1 condensates, several instances of cargo receptor condensates released via this type of macroautophagy, also called “fluidophagy,” include ferritin-NCOA4 condensates in humans and Ape1 condensates in yeast. Cargo-resident macroautophagy receptors recognized via LC3 in ubiquitin-independent selective macroautophagy include CCPG1, TEX264, FAM134A/B/C (RETREG2/1/3), Sect. 62, RTN3L, and ATL3 during macroERphagy and TRIM5, BNIP3, NIX (BNIP3L), BCL2L13, FUNDC1, FKBP8 during macromitophagy as well as CALCOCO1 during macroGolgiphagy; and TAX1BP1 for macroferritinophagy [37].

The phagophore membrane blocks post-elongation, forming a double-membraned autophagosome. The endosomal sorting complex required for transport (also known as ESCRT) coordinates membrane scission, which is necessary for the closure [50, 51]. The isolated material then breaks down in an autolysosome, and this is produced when the autophagosome joins forces with lysosomes. Two SNARE (refer to soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor) complexes, YKT6-SNAP29-STX7 and STX17-SNAP29-VAMP7/8, mediate this fusion process [52]. Furthermore, autophagosome-lysosome fusion involves membrane tethering elements such as PLEKHM1, EPG5, RAB7, homotypic fusion, and vacuole protein-sorting complex [53].

Molecular mechanisms of microautophagy

Early in the 1980 s, electron microscopy was utilized to investigate microautophagy, the process by which lysosomes devour cytosolic proteins by invaginating lysosomal membranes in animal cells [54]. Lysosomal membrane invagination (such as endosomal membranes in endosomal microautophagy) and lysosomal membrane protruding are the two forms of microautophagy [55].

The ESCRT complex, which facilitates the inner distortion and subsequently fission of lysosomal or endosomal membranes to produce intraluminal vesicles, seems to be involved in the invagination type. Except for the ATG8/ATG12 conjugation systems, which are essential for cargo recognition, this mechanism doesn’t need the core ATG proteins. On the other hand, core ATG and SNARE proteins are necessary for the protruding type of microautophagy. The yeast Komagataella phaffii’s micropexophagy is the most well-studied protrusion-type microautophagy process. This mechanism includes de novo membrane development at the membrane closure point, which may be mediated via macroautophagy machinery, such as core ATG and SNARE proteins, and membrane deformation that is reliant on the ATG8/ATG12 conjugation systems [56].

Selective microautophagy occurs in several forms. A major instance is the direct integration of ER segments into lysosomes, involving the ATG8/ATG12 conjugation systems and the macroER-phagy receptor Sect. 62. Another instance is the ER’s procollagen-containing subdomains being knocked down via microER-phagy, which is dependent on the ESCRT complex, the ATG8/ATG12 conjugation systems, and the ubiquitin-binding macroautophagy adaptor SQSTM1. These instances suggest that macroautophagy and microautophagy share some cargo identification mechanisms, if not each of them. Additionally, subdomains of different cellular components are specifically destroyed via microautophagy: photodamaged chloroplasts (microchlorophagy), lipid droplets (microlipophagy), lysosomal membranes (microlysophagy), and the nucleus (micronucleophagy) [57]. Furthermore, endosomal microautophagy is the term used to describe membrane invagination at the endosomal membranes [55, 58].

The process very similar to ESCRT-dependent multivesicular body production, is endosomal microautophagy. The cytosolic molecular chaperone HSP70 recognizes the KFERQ-like pentapeptide motif found in cytosolic cargo proteins, that this kind of microautophagy can specifically break down [59]. Following their interaction with phosphatidylserine, HSP70 brings these cargo proteins to the lysosomal membrane, where they are internalized into lysosomes. NCOA4, NDP52, NBR1, TAX1BP1, SQSTM1, and ferritin are among the macroautophagy adaptors/receptors and cargos that are specifically degraded with starvation-induced endosomal microautophagy. ATG8/ATG12 conjugation systems play a role in this process, which leads to endosomal microautophagy-mediated destruction of ATG8 family proteins. Related ESCRT complex-dependent phenomena, such as the Nbr1-mediated vacuolar targeting route in the yeast Schizosaccharomyces pombe, function without ATG proteins and may be related to endosomal microautophagy in mammals [58].

Furthermore, LC3-dependent extracellular vesicle loading and secretion (known as LDELS) has been observed [60]. nSMase2, sometimes referred to as SMPD3, is necessary for this process but does not comprise the ESCRT complex. RNA-binding proteins, including HNRNPK and SAFB, are specifically integrated into endosomes by LC3 via the LIR motif in LDELS, and exosomes are finally released from the cell. The deep connection between microautophagy and macroautophagy at the time of ferritin degradation has been brought to light by recent research.

The intimate connection between microautophagy and macroautophagy at the time of ferritin breakdown, NCOA4, has been emphasized in recent research [61]. Both endosomal microautophagy (microferritinophagy) and macroautophagy (macroferritinophagy) target these ferritin-NCOA4 condensates, and both mechanisms need TAX1BP1 as an adapter [61]. Since ferritin is known to be released via exosomes, some of the ferritin taken via microferritinophagy may be intended for secretion, in contrast to the destruction of ferritin through macroferritinophagy.

Molecular mechanisms of Chaperone-Mediated autophagy

The concept of Chaperone-Mediated Autophagy (or CMA) was first introduced in the late 1980 s when it was found that the molecular chaperone HSP70 detects the KFERQ-like motif, which is essential for selective integration into lysosomes [62]. Contrary to endosomal microautophagy, which is dependent on HSP70, CMA does not require membrane deformation. Rather, LAMP2A protein (a lysosomal membrane protein) facilitates the direct transport of proteins carrying the KFERQ-like motif into lysosomes. HSP70, HSP40 (also called DNAJB1), and CHIP are the cofactors that bind to the KFERQ-like motif during CMA. Across the LAMP2A complex in the lysosomal membrane and into the lysosomal lumen. The quantity of LAMP2A in the environment, at least partially, controls CMA activity [63]. Additionally, it has been discovered that there is a correlation between the activities of macroautophagy and CMA, suggesting that there is communication between the two. There has also been evidence of direct ingestion of cytosolic DNA and RNA into lysosomes; this process is referred to as DNautophagy and RNautophagy, respectively. The cytosolic arginine-rich regions in LAMP2C and SIDT directly recognize RNA and DNA in this ATP-dependent mechanism [37, 64]. While the full molecular process behind RN/DNautophagy is still unclear, it is occasionally categorized as a form of selective microautophagy.

Autophagy in maintaining kidney health and disease

Normal autophagic reactions are essential for controlling native kidney cells’ fundamental operations and shielding the kidneys from acute or chronic injury [65, 66]. The condition in which important autophagic genes are eliminated during the embryonic phase has a noticeable impact on the formation of normal kidneys. For instance, inducing Cre-combinase expression in Atg5fl/fl or Atg7fl/fl animals through the Six2 (also known as sine oculis-related homeobox 2) promoter causes autophagy disruption throughout the entire nephron, which in turn causes severe reorganization of the tubular and glomerular structures, ultimately resulting in permanent renal failure [67]. Likewise, progressive kidney injury and tubulointerstitial fibrosis result from the loss of atg5 in both proximal and distal tubular epithelial cells (or TECs). However, autophagy deficiency is restricted to distal TECs; this result does not occur, suggesting that proximal TECs depend more on basal autophagy than their distal counterparts. Crucially, cathepsin D (short known as Ctsd) or Pik3c3/Vps34, as well as podocyte-specific deletion of Atg5, leads to a disruption of autophagic flux, which in turn causes proteinuria and glomerulosclerosis, ultimately inducing severe glomerulopathy and renal failure. More specifically, phenotypic alterations linked to autophagy suppression across many renal components either become clinically apparent or worsen with age, demonstrating that impaired autophagy is the main cause of renal aging [66].This finding corresponds to results showing an increase in the expression level of the autophagy suppressor protein RUBCN over time, which is linked to worsening symptoms of lysosomal dysfunction [68].

Inflammation is frequently involved in renal diseases, including responses to various types of acute kidney injury and chronic kidney disease (CKD) [69]. Genetic variations in autophagy-related genes (such as Atg5 and Ulk1) have been correlated with a higher risk of developing inflammatory disorders [65, 70]. Consistent with these results, autophagy is predicted to contribute to context-specific regulation of inflammation linked to renal disease. Dysfunctional autophagy plays a significant role in the progression of diabetic kidney disease [71]. The importance of autophagy in the change from acute to chronic kidney disease, which results in abnormal tissue repair and fibrosis, has not yet been identified, in sharp contrast to cases of acute renal injury. Since the proliferation surge of resident renal tubular cells is essential for the regeneration of kidney structure, regenerative repair may benefit from the reduction of autophagy responses that result in acute injury [72].

Autophagy in cancer: both a tumor suppressor and a promoter

Autophagy is a cellular mechanism that plays a dual role in cancer. It can either promote tumor cell growth or suppress tumors by inducing the death of cancer cells. Autophagy acts as a natural tumor suppressor, particularly in the early stages of tumorigenesis. However, in advanced stages of cancer, it supports tumor progression by increasing nutrient availability and improving cancer cell survival.

Autophagy as a tumor suppressor

In many types of tumors, autophagy is initially reduced or inactivated. Oncogenic alterations, such as mutations or loss of function in PTEN and hyperactivation of PI3K and AKT—key inhibitors of autophagy—lead to autophagy suppression via the mTOR pathway [73].

Studies on Beclin-1, ATG5, and ATG7 have confirmed the tumor-suppressive role of autophagy, showing its involvement in spontaneous tumor inhibition [74–76]. In breast cancer, Beclin-1, with the help of UVRAG, is recruited from the PI3KC3 complex to the cellular membrane, enhancing cell adhesion and inhibiting β-catenin pathway genes [74, 77].

In human synovial sarcoma, overexpression of Beclin-1 correlates with reduced cell proliferation and the induction of apoptosis [78]. Similarly, in gastric cancer, Beclin-1 expression is upregulated via miR-140-3p, leading to suppression of cell viability and invasion through autophagy activation [79].

Autophagy also contributes to genomic stability by repairing DNA damage through the NER and DDR pathways, as well as by inhibiting p38 MAPK activation, thereby preventing tumor development [80–82]. Elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) can damage the genome, impair mitochondrial function, and trigger tumorigenesis; however, excessive ROS levels are harmful to both normal and cancer cells [83]. Certain compounds, such as daphnetin, induce both autophagy and apoptosis in ovarian cancer cells via the PI3K/Akt/mTOR or AMPK/Akt/mTOR signaling pathways [84, 85].

Autophagy as a tumor promoter

Autophagy may enhance the resistance of cancer cells to stressful conditions like chemotherapy, radiotherapy, hypoxia, and hypothermia, thus contributing to prolonged cancer cell survival [86].

Under stress, autophagy regulates tumor metabolism, supporting cancer cell adaptation and persistence. In renal cancer, mitochondrial dysfunction leads to the activation of autophagy, which in turn promotes cell proliferation and migration [87]. Reduced expression of Beclin-1 in gastric and colorectal cancers has been associated with increased cell invasion and metastasis [88, 89].

In KRAS-mutant mouse models, the inhibition of ATG7, ATG13, or ULK1 suppressed tumor growth, suggesting that autophagy is involved in glioma initiation and development [90].

In colorectal cancer, the overexpression of NTF4 is associated with poor survival, while its inhibition promotes EMT suppression and activates autophagy [91]. Recent studies indicate that autophagy activation contributes to chemoresistance, including resistance to cisplatin [92]. In glioblastoma cells, glucose deprivation triggers increased autophagy, leading to a quiescent state that allows cells to evade cell death [93].

Formation and characteristics of NcRNAs

The core concept of molecular biology suggests that genetic information is transmitted from DNA to RNA and ultimately to protein, which functions as the cell’s operational unit. After the Human Genome Project was completed at the turn of the century, coupled with breakthroughs in high-throughput sequencing and transcriptome technologies, scientists were astonished to find that only 2% of the 3 billion base pairs in the human genome encode protein-coding instructions. Research reveals that transcription accounts for approximately 98% of the genome. Nevertheless, most of these transcripts lack protein-coding potential, classifying them as ncRNAs [94–96]. The most important ncRNAs are regulatory ncRNAs, which are crucial in regulating key biological processes in organisms. In eukaryotes, including mammals, a major section of the genome is transcribed to generate various ncRNAs. These ncRNAs perform diverse acts in biological mechanisms at both the transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels [94]. For instance, they facilitate genome rearrangement and DNA replication, thereby safeguarding genomes from exogenous nucleic acids [97]. These ncRNAs come in different forms, including miRNAs or miRs, circRNAs, small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs), and lncRNAs [96, 98–100]. MiRNAs are small ncRNAs approximately 22 nucleotides in length, primarily regulating gene expression post-transcriptionally [94, 101]. MiRNAs, being highly conserved across species, are transcribed from mammalian genomic DNA into primary miRNAs, which are then processed into precursor miRNAs and mature miRNAs. MiRNAs mostly stop genes from being expressed after they have been translated. On the other hand, the biogenesis of lncRNAs shares similarities with messenger RNAs (mRNAs), as both are transcribed by RNA polymerase II [102, 103]. Transcription of various DNA segments produces lncRNAs, which possess a 5’ cap and a 3’ poly-A tail for stability [103, 104]. Most lncRNAs undergo splicing, and a significant portion experiences alternative splicing due to intronic AG and GU nucleotides, resulting in specific cleavage by the spliceosome [104]. Additionally, the relative amount of lncRNAs is regulated by Dicer1, an RNase involved in producing mature miRNAs, which controls lncRNA expression levels [103, 105]. LncRNAs play a wider range of regulatory roles. They can change the expression of protein-coding genes in both positive and negative ways by interacting with RNA, proteins, and chromatin structures in different ways. Although some lncRNAs are conserved through evolution, their degree of conservation is lower than that of protein-coding genes. Remarkably, the promoter regions of lncRNAs show conservation levels akin to those of various protein-coding genes [94].

NCRNAs in autophagy: exploring the role and interplay between non-coding RNAs and autophagy

Various mechanisms regulate autophagy, and recent studies have highlighted the involvement of ncRNAs in this process [94, 106]. Studies reveal that ncRNAs regulate autophagy and contribute to disease phenotypes [97]. Accumulating evidence points to the significant contributions of ncRNAs in regulating autophagy across different physiological and pathological contexts, from embryonic development to diseases such as cancer [98, 107]. Particularly, lncRNAs and miRNAs have been implicated in modulating autophagy by interacting with each other and forming regulatory networks. MiRNAs are pivotal regulators of autophagy. These molecules bind to complementary mRNA sequences, leading to mRNA degradation or translational repression, thereby modulating gene expression. miRNAs can influence various stages of autophagy. For instance, miR-199a inhibits autophagy by promoting mTOR signaling, contributing to cardiac hypertrophy [97, 108]. Additionally, miR-26b mediates autophagy suppression by targeting ULK2 in prostate cancer cells [109]. Research has also identified miR-30a as a key regulator of autophagy via its interaction with the beclin1 gene in multiple cancer cell types [98].

An increasing number of studies have suggested that lncRNAs can act as either oncogenes or tumor inhibitors in cancer [103]. Additionally, lncRNAs that regulate autophagy can serve as pro-survival or pro-death factors in cancer [103, 110]. They modulate autophagy by affecting ATGs and their pathways, influencing different cancer stages, especially metastasis [103, 111]. Many lncRNAs negatively control autophagy by interacting with RNA-binding proteins, while some lncRNAs can promote autophagy by directly interacting with other autophagy regulators. lncRNAs function as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs), sequestering miRNAs and influencing autophagy-related pathways. An example is maternally expressed gene 3 (MEG3), a tumor suppressor, which is downregulated in certain cancers, highlighting its potential role in tumor development and progression [112].

Both computer analyses and experiments show that a lot of autophagy-related miRNAs and lncRNAs are found in CpG islands and areas that are changing their histones, which is how they get epigenetic signatures. Importantly, epigenetic mechanisms can influence the expression of these non-coding RNAs. Conversely, miRNAs and lncRNAs can regulate multiple target genes involved in various intracellular signaling pathways of autophagy downstream of themselves. This intricate interplay between epigenetic modifications and ncRNAs adds another layer of complexity to the regulation of autophagic responses in cells and tissues [97].

Given autophagy’s contribution to the pathogenesis of numerous human disorders, these lncRNAs are implicated in a wide range of conditions, spanning from cancer to age-related pathologies [94]. In nonmalignant conditions, various lncRNAs have significant roles in the regulation of autophagy. HOTAIR (HOX transcript antisense RNA) is involved in the pathogenesis of intervertebral disc degeneration by modulating the AMPK-MTOR-ULK1 pathway. It enhances autophagy, apoptosis, and senescence in nucleus pulposus cells [94]. MALAT1 (metastasis-associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript 1) acts as a molecular sponge for miR-26b and miR-200c-3p in cerebral ischemic stroke. This interaction upregulates ULK2 and SIRT1, leading to elevated autophagy and shielding of brain microvascular under oxygen-glucose deprivation conditions [94, 113]. Other lncRNAs implicated in autophagy regulation in specific contexts include SPAG5-AS1, Gm5524, Gm15645, and SOX2-OT, which are implicated in regulating autophagy in diabetic nephropathy [94, 114, 115]. Elevated H19 levels in pre-eclampsia facilitate trophoblast cell invasion and autophagy, likely via the PI3K-AKT-MTOR signaling pathway [94, 116].

MiRNAs

Small ncRNAs with a length of 19–25 nucleotides are called miRNAs. They are primarily demonstrated to suppress gene expression through the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC). Recent studies have notably shown that miRNAs can upregulate gene expression in particular situations, including initiating the formation of the ribosomal complex at the 5’ UTR of mRNA transcripts to facilitate protein translation [117]. MiRNAs have recently been demonstrated to contribute to cancer progression and may serve as predictive biomarkers for the disease. Numerous cancer types have been the Subject of in-depth research on the use of miRNAs as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets [118]. It has been shown that miR-204 directly regulates TRPM3 expression by blocking TRPM3 translation, and indirectly by blocking the translation of caveolin 1 (CAV1), which is required for TRPM3 expression [121]. By attaching itself to a miR-204 location in the 3´ untranslated region (3´UTR), miR-204 directly prevents TRPM3 from translating. Likewise, through a miR-204 location in the 3´ UTR of CAV1, miR-204 suppresses the expression of CAV1.Interestingly, TRPM3 controls autophagy in the opposite way from miR-204. TRPM3 promotes LC3A and LC3B autophagy via Ca2 + influx and calmodulin-regulated signaling pathways on several levels, while miR-204 suppresses LC3B-dependent autophagy. First, it promotes the formation of autophagosomes by activating calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase kinase 2 β (CAMKK2) and AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK, also called PRKA) in a Ca2+-dependent manner. This leads to the phosphorylation of unc-51, like autophagy activating kinase 1 (ULK1) [136]. It has also been found that TRPM3 activity suppresses the expression of miR-204, a microRNA that is expressed less in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) than in normal kidneys [123]. The function of miR-30a in sorafenib-induced activity in RCC cells was described in a study. Zheng et al.. discovered that in several human RCC tissues and RCC cell lines, miR-30a expression was markedly downregulated. Consequently, its target gene, Beclin-1, was upregulated. Indicators such as LC 3B-I/-II conversion, Beclin-1/ATG-5 overexpression, and p62 degradation revealed that sorafenib triggers autophagy in RCC cells (786-0 and A489 lines). In 786-0 or A489 cells, exogenous expression of miR-30a increased sorafenib-induced cytotoxicity and suppressed Beclin-1 expression. On the other hand, adding antagomiR-30a to suppress miR-30a enhanced Beclin-1 expression and prevented the cytotoxic effects of sorafenib on RCC cells. Moreover, autophagy inhibitors, such as Bafilomycin A1, 3-methyladenine, or chloroquine, increased sorafenib activity and significantly accelerated cell death. Similarly, targeted siRNAs silencing Beclin-1 or ATG-5 enhanced sorafenib-induced cytotoxicity in RCC cells. These findings suggest that miR-30a deregulation in RCC may hinder sorafenib-induced apoptosis via an autophagy-dependent pathway, highlighting miR-30a as a potential therapeutic target for the disease [122]. According to a study, autophagy stimulates the growth of tumors by producing nutrients through the breakdown of intracellular components. TRPM3 modulates autophagy in a way that contrasts with miR-204. While miR-204 suppresses LC3B-dependent autophagy, TRPM3 promotes both LC3A and LC3B-mediated autophagy by facilitating Ca2 + influx and activating calmodulin-dependent signaling pathways at multiple levels [121]. According to Harteneck [137], Oberwinkel and Phillipp [138], and Wagner et al. [139]., MiR-204 is expressed from intron 6 of the TRPM3 gene, which encodes a transient receptor potential non-selective cation channel that conducts Ca2 + and Zn2 + ions. The human kidney exhibits enhanced expression of both miR-204 and TRPM3 [140]. It is triggered by exposure to the hormone pregnenolone sulphate (PS) outside cells [141, 142]. Mefenamic acid (MFA) selectively inhibits TRPM3 [143]. VHL-induced expression of two short TRPM3 transcripts, but not TRPM3 transcripts encoding the full-sized functional channel protein, corresponds with the VHL-mediated elevation of miR-204 expression [123]. Significantly, a concurrent drop in miR-204 and an increase in LC3B levels have been linked to greater tumor grade in human ccRCC, suggesting that LC3B-mediated macroautophagy is essential for RCC progression. VHL inhibits tumor growth by triggering the production of LC3C, and HIF-regulated paralog of LC3B, in addition to activating endogenous miR-204. These findings demonstrate that VHL functions as a regulator of autophagic programs that suppress tumor progression [144]. Additionally, to create tumorigenic models, A498 cells have been injected into nude mice. The model mice were then administered curcumin at varying dosages (100, 200, and 400 mg/kg) once daily for 30 days. LncRNA

Another class of RNA species is lncRNAs. They are functionally varied and range in sequence length from 200 bp to 10 kb. LncRNAs can be classified in two main ways: (1) based on their synthesis, whether they are transcribed from intronic or intergenic regions of the genome in a sense or anti-sense manner, and (2) based on their well-established opposing roles in different types of cancer [145]. An increasing amount of research has shown that lncRNAs influence the various phases of autophagy (initiation, phagophore nucleation, elongation, closure, and fusion) and hence influence the chemoresistance, metastasis, and proliferation of cancer cells [146]. LncRNAs and ABC transporters: A missing link in RCC chemoresistance

ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, such as P-glycoprotein (P-gp/ABCB1), MRP1 (ABCC1), and BCRP (ABCG2), are well-known mediators of multidrug resistance (MDR) by actively effluxing chemotherapeutic drugs from cancer cells, reducing their intracellular concentration and efficacy [219]. In a study by Ebrahimnezhad, a critical gap in understanding the role of lncRNAs in regulating ABC transporters, particularly in RCC, was identified [220]. The study emphasizes that lncRNAs can influence the expression and function of ABC transporters, thereby contributing to chemoresistance, and lncRNAs can modulate ABC Transporters. LncRNAs can act as ceRNAs, sequestering microRNAs that would otherwise suppress ABC transporter genes. LncRNAs recruit chromatin-modifying enzymes (e.g., EZH2) to alter the transcriptional activity of ABC transporter genes. Some lncRNAs directly interact with transcription factors or RNA-binding proteins to stabilize ABC transporter mRNAs [220]. Targeting lncRNAs that regulate ABC transporters could reverse drug resistance and improve chemotherapy efficacy in RCC and other cancers. RCC is notoriously resistant to conventional chemotherapy, partly due to ABC transporter overexpression. Incorporating lncRNA-ABC transporter interactions into RCC research could uncover novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Clinical application in RCC

Since the 1970 s, nucleic acids have gained interest in the field of therapeutics [221]. They treat illnesses by introducing synthetic oligonucleotides into cells, either permanently or temporarily. The progression of ncRNA-based therapies has enabled the treatment of diseases that were previously difficult to manage with conventional medications. Their distinct expression profile and stability make them good biomarkers for use [222]. Comparative expression profiling has been used in early research to determine the functions of ncRNA in the development of tumors in both malignant and healthy tissue. For inaccessible tumors, progress was not made, in contrast to tissue biopsies, which are more comprehensive and advanced. Furthermore, neither the genetic condition of the tumor nor the variability of treatment resistance can be disclosed by tissue biopsies [223–226]. Some common cancer signatures, or ncRNAs, have been discovered when examining the blood, urine, and saliva of cancer patients [227]. Liquid biopsy utilizes ncRNAs from both cancerous and healthy cells to monitor tumor status and treatment response. However, naked ncRNAs circulating in biofluids are highly susceptible to degradation by RNases [228]. NcRNAs are useful biomarkers, therapeutic targets, and potential therapeutic agents because dysregulation of ncRNA expression is commonly associated with human disorders like cancer [229, 230].

NcRNAs are promising biomarkers and therapeutic targets for RCC, according to a number of recent studies. These ncRNAs may more effectively differentiate between various clinical stages than standard classification methods predict. Identifying the ncRNAs associated with autophagy is essential. In addition to helping with autophagy-therapy decisions, ncRNAs may offer new treatment alternatives. Chemotherapy is a critical treatment for RCC, but many patients exhibit insensitivity to it. Consequently, these patients will not only experience numerous side effects but also face poor therapeutic outcomes. Determining the sensitive and insensitive groups for targeted treatment and evaluating chemotherapy responses is crucial. Targeting these ncRNAs may yield unexpectedly positive outcomes, as numerous studies have shown their role in resistance to conventional chemotherapy [231–233]. However, therapeutic targeting of specific ncRNAs is thought to enhance the effectiveness of immunotherapy. Characterizing ncRNAs will help identify appropriate patient populations for tailored treatment approaches. As a result, this can provide a framework for designing accurate, personalized care protocols. Furthermore, PCR, a widely used technique in medical testing, is predominantly employed for lncRNA detection. It is simple to use, cost-effective, and offers speed, high sensitivity, and specificity. This makes lncRNA detection an ideal tool for tumor diagnosis and prognosis. Targeted treatments for lncRNAs can be achieved through siRNA vector systems, which have been extensively explored in clinical applications [231–233]. Intervention design for lncRNAs is very straightforward. Targeting lncRNAs can be utilized for targeted therapy against malignancies as they regulate key oncogenes or tumor signaling pathways from upstream. Therefore, promising targets for RCC precise therapy would include an intervention in lncRNAs or lncRNA-related signaling networks. However, it is crucial and urgent to thoroughly understand the distinct functional mechanisms of lncRNAs in cancer, as this remains a significant challenge to their clinical application [234]. Therefore, ARlncRs, which have been verified in cancer, may be useful markers to build prognostic prediction techniques [235, 236]. According to Song et al.., lncRNA ADAMTS9-AS2 can stop the progression of ccRCC by regulating FOXO1 through miR-27a-3p [237]. Conversely, Zhai et al.. reported that lncRNA URRCC stimulates ccRCC growth [238]. Additionally, a group of researchers has developed a prognosis model that incorporates various immune-related ncRNAs [239]. In one study, a risk score was developed and evaluated that included nine ARlncRs, which significantly improved the prognostic prediction accuracy for ccRCC. A nomogram was constructed and validated as an effective tool for forecasting prognosis and guiding clinical decision-making when combined with factors such as age, gender, and risk score. These discoveries enhance our understanding of the fundamental mechanisms through which ARlncRs modulate the progression and metastasis of ccRCC [159]. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that certain medications, by targeting ncRNAs, can effectively treat kidney cancers while potentially inhibiting the autophagic processes triggered by these ncRNAs. Research by Kimura T’s group revealed that autophagy can protect kidneys by reducing inflammation [240].

Investigating biomarkers associated with autophagy is, therefore, critical for forecasting disease advancement and evaluating RCC patient outcomes [158, 170]. Over the past decades, remarkable advancements have been achieved in the development of novel immunotherapeutic agents, such as checkpoint inhibitors and vaccines, which are now increasingly utilized to treat mRCC. Even with elevated response rates to PD-1 and/or CTLA-4 axis inhibition combinations, the current standard of care for RCC research suggests that a significant number of RCC patients fail to experience long-lasting clinical benefits from these therapies. Additionally, HOTAIRM1 has been shown to inhibit RCC invasion and growth. A newly developed autophagy-lncRNA prognostic model, based on factors such as age, sex, tumor grade, T, M, N, and AJCC stages, may effectively stratify RCC patients into low-risk and high-risk groups. This model also has the potential to predict 3- to 5-year survival outcomes for RCC patients in both groups. Additionally, they discovered possible RCC treatment targets using GSEA analysis. According to their findings, the risk forecasting scores derived from the sARLNRs can help distinguish high-risk individuals from ccRCC patients who share similar clinical or molecular traits, allowing for the selection of a suitable and customized treatment plan [157]. Subsequent analysis has revealed that ARlncRNA expression patterns may provide insights into ccRCC progression on its own. This implies that the elevated risk values correlate with a poorer prognosis and greater malignancy. According to Jiang et al. [239], MELTF-AS1 is linked to immunological processes and can function as a prognostic indicator.

Zhou et al.. have determined that risk score, grade, and AJCC stage are independent predictive markers after evaluating the association between risk score from the prognostic signature and clinicopathological features. According to reports, lncRNA AC099850.3 is a part of the lncRNA-miRNA‐mRNA ceRNA network, which may help with the diagnosis and management of tongue squamous cell carcinoma [241]. In the meantime, lncRNA AC099850.3 is a part of the ARlncRNAs prognostic model and might be used as a marker for the diagnosis and management of hepatocellular cancer [242]. The survival of patients with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) has been adversely connected with SPINT1-AS1 expression (p < 0.05), indicating that it may function as a tumor suppressor in ESCC [243]. Additionally, lncRNA AC108449.2 may be included in the immune-related predictive model and the ARlncRNAs prognostic model for the diagnosis and treatment of kidney carcinoma and bladder cancer. Furthermore, Li et al.. discovered that lncRNA SPINT1-AS1 has been downregulated in the tissues of esophageal colorectal cancer, indicating that it could potentially be a tumor prognostic indicator [244]. In addition, lncRNA SPINT1-AS1 was able to control pan-cancer lapatinib sensitivity. In the study, the 8-lncRNAs signature is effective in differentiating between OS in low- and high-risk patient groups (AUCICGC = 0.749, p < 0.01; AUCSYSU = 0.714, p < 0.01) and PFS (AUCSYSU = 0.705, p < 0.01). Additionally, compared to other established predictive biomarkers, the 8-lncRNAs signature performed better. Reduced SPINT1-AS1 expression has recently been proposed as a potential independent predictor of poor prognosis in colorectal and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [243, 244]. By epigenetically suppressing EphB3 through EZH2 and LSD1, the lncRNA FOXD2-AS1 predicts a poor prognosis in gastric cancer and encourages the cell-cycle progression and proliferation of glioma cells through [245]. Although the aforementioned research has demonstrated a connection between these three lncRNAs and the development and spread of tumor cells, it is unknown if they have an impact on the autophagy activity of tumor cells. They conducted tests to confirm the connection between autophagy and the eight lncRNAs. Since only a few patients respond well to chemotherapy, treatment options for patients with stage RCC following major surgery are highly debatable. Thus, stricter biomarkers are desperately needed to assist in screening these patient categories [156].

Recent research has demonstrated the roles of ncRNAs—including miRNAs, circRNAs, and lncRNAs—in the progression, drug resistance, and metastasis of RCC. However, translating these findings into clinical practice requires addressing several challenges, such as developing reliable diagnostic methods, targeted delivery systems, and integration with existing therapies. One of the most promising approaches is the use of liquid biopsies to detect circulating ncRNAs like miR-141-5p, hsa_circ_0035483, and lncRNAs such as GAS5 in patient blood or urine. This non-invasive method could serve as a viable alternative to tissue biopsies, enabling early diagnosis and treatment response monitoring through sensitive techniques like qPCR, NanoString, or next-generation sequencing. From a therapeutic perspective, targeting specific ncRNAs using antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), siRNAs, or CRISPR/Cas9 holds significant potential. For example, inhibiting circFTO with ASOs could restore miR-514b-3p activity and suppress tumor progression, while synthetic miR-141-5p mimics could counteract the effects of hsa_circ_0067301. However, the primary challenge lies in delivering these molecules specifically to kidney cancer cells, which could be addressed using lipid nanoparticles or engineered exosomes. Additionally, since certain lncRNAs like MALAT1 contribute to drug resistance by upregulating ABC transporters (e.g., P-gp), silencing them with siRNAs could enhance chemotherapy efficacy. Another promising avenue is combining ncRNA-based therapies with immunotherapy. For instance, suppressing lncRNAs like GAS5, which play a role in immune evasion, could improve responses to PD-1 inhibitors. Prognostic models based on multi-ncRNA signatures (e.g., the 8-lncRNA model) could also help stratify patients by risk and guide personalized treatment decisions. However, before these approaches can be widely adopted in clinical settings, further clinical trials are needed to confirm their efficacy and safety. Ultimately, integrating these findings into precision medicine paradigms could revolutionize RCC management and improve outcomes for treatment-resistant patients. lncRNAs hold great potential as prognostic biomarkers. A sequencing analysis of 483 cancer patients identified nine lncRNAs (AC098484.1, AC005104.1, CCDC18-AS1, ANKRD10-IT1, SH3BP5-AS1, GARS1-DT, AP000692.1, AC048382.2, and MHENCR) that were associated with immune and inflammatory pathways. These lncRNAs are believed to play significant roles in RCC progression and may serve as valuable biomarkers for patient stratification and prognosis [246].

Biomarkers offer crucial insights into determining the optimal therapeutic strategies for patients with RCC. In 2021, Gui et al. evaluated the expression of eight lncRNAs (AC156455.1, AC107021.2, AC073611.1, AC105446.1, FOXD2-AS1, MELTF-AS1, SPINT1-AS1, and WDFY3-AS2) in RCC patients. Their findings indicated that these ARlncRNAs are significantly associated with overall survival, highlighting their prognostic value in RCC [156].

Conclusion

RCC continues to be a significant problem in oncology due to its high recurrence rate, metastatic potential, and resistance to traditional treatment options. As the limitations of chemotherapy, radiation, and hormone therapy become more apparent, the quest for new molecular targets has accelerated. Autophagy, a cellular function required for homeostasis, has emerged as a key player in RCC progression and therapy response. While autophagy can shrink tumors in their early stages, it also gives cancer cells a survival edge in advanced disease, confounding its potential as a therapeutic target. NcRNAs, notably microRNAs and lncRNAs, have been identified as important regulators of autophagy in RCC. These ncRNAs have an impact on several signaling pathways, including PI3K/Akt/mTOR and AMPK, all of which play important roles in tumor development, apoptosis, and therapeutic resistance. ARLNRs have received increased interest due to their capacity to regulate autophagy-related genes and influence RCC progression. However, despite their potential as prognostic indicators and therapeutic targets, the precise molecular pathways underpinning ARLNR activity in RCC remain unknown. Future studies should concentrate on understanding the intricate relationship that exists between autophagy, ncRNAs, and the pathophysiology of RCC. To confirm the prognostic importance of ARLNRs and investigate their potential as therapeutic targets, further clinical trials and functional investigations are necessary. Furthermore, creating targeted treatments that alter ncRNA-mediated autophagy may offer a fresh and more efficient method of treating RCC. Personalized medicine for RCC patients may become a reality by incorporating ncRNA-based techniques into clinical practice, which would ultimately enhance treatment results and survival rates.
