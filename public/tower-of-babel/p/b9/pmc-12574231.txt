Lupus enteritis and peritonitis as a first presentation of systemic lupus erythematosus: a case report

Background

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by multisystem involvement, with manifestations ranging from mild cutaneous and musculoskeletal symptoms to severe, life-threatening complications. Gastrointestinal involvement is known to occur in SLE patients, although there is no exact prevalence or incidence. Gastrointestinal involvement is often underdiagnosed due to its nonspecific presentation. Among the diverse GI manifestations, lupus enteritis and peritonitis are rare but significant complications that can lead to severe morbidity if not promptly recognized and managed [1–3].

Lupus enteritis (LE), defined as inflammation of the small intestine secondary to SLE, presents symptoms such as abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea. It is often associated with intestinal vasculitis, leading to bowel wall thickening, edema, and, in severe cases, ischemia or perforation. The reported incidence of lupus enteritis varies, with studies suggesting it affects between 0.2% and 9.7% of SLE patients. Lupus peritonitis (LP) is considered even rarer, though precise incidence rates are not well-defined in the literature [4]. Distinguishing these conditions from other causes of abdominal pain in SLE patients, such as infections, mesenteric thrombosis, or medication-related side effects, is crucial for appropriate management [5, 6].

Given the rarity of concurrent lupus enteritis and peritonitis, we present a case highlighting this unique presentation, emphasizing the diagnostic challenges, imaging findings, and therapeutic strategies. This report aims to contribute to the growing body of literature on GI involvement in SLE and underscore the importance of early recognition and targeted treatment.

Case presentation

A 38-year-old married female from Hebron, Palestine, presented to our emergency room with abdominal pain for 3 days. The patient was in her usual state of health until she developed abdominal pain 3 days ago. The pain was gradual, progressive, and colicky. When the patient was asked more thoroughly, she had five episodes of bloody coffee ground vomiting since the start and passed black stools one time. The patient took analgesics by herself, but without improvement. She had no fever, chills, jaundice, change in urine color, chest pain, or any other symptoms. On past medical history, our patient had a history of migratory polyarthralgia for 3 years. She visited multiple doctors regarding her joint pain, for which she was prescribed various medications that she doesn’t remember, with mild improvement. She stopped taking the medicines 5 months ago and started taking non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) on her own when she complained of joint pain. The patient also had a history of alopecia and photosensitivity for 2 years and two abortions in the first trimester without a known cause. The patient had a family history of rheumatoid arthritis in one of her cousins, diagnosed at the age of 37, and a history of hypothyroidism in another cousin, diagnosed at the age of 41. The patient stated that she smokes sometimes, but not often.

On physical examination, the patient looked ill, fatigued, and in pain, and she was pale. Her vital signs were within normal limits. Upon admission, the patient exhibited moderate distress characterized by vomiting and abdominal pain, alongside symptoms of anemia and dehydration; however, there were no signs of hemodynamic instability or altered mental status. Blood pressure remained stable, oxygen saturation was sustained on room air, and there were no indications of septic or hypovolemic shock. On the chest examination, she had scattered wheezes and right basal crepitations. The abdominal exam showed a distended abdomen, with tenderness in the right upper quadrant and the epigastric regions. The rest of the exam was normal. Given that the patient exhibited hemodynamic stability, normal vital signs, and no respiratory distress, along with her unique presentation. She was admitted to our medical wards for further evaluation and treatment.

Extensive laboratory workup was done, including hematologic, immunologic, thyroid, kidney function test, liver function test, electrolytes, inflammatory markers, and many others, which showed hypokalemia and hypophosphatemia. She also had microcytic anemia with high reticulocyte count, lymphopenia, and eosinopenia. She had a positive Coombs test. In addition, she had elevated ESR and CRP (Table 1).

The patient finally received the diagnosis of SLE with lupus enteritis and peritonitis. The consent form was taken, and the patient was given cyclophosphamide 500 mg with mesna. The patient started developing improvement in her symptoms, with regression of her ascites and resolution of her anemia. The patient was discharged on prednisolone 40 mg, proton pump inhibitor (PPI) 40 mg, and hydroxychloroquine 200 mg. the patient had multiple follow ups since her remission for one month, the patient was satisfied with her results. She denied any further investigations. From that time, she didn’t attend her regular follow ups.

Discussion

Gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms are well established to occur among patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), with prevalence and incidence not exactly known. Most of these symptoms are nonspecific, including nausea, vomiting, weight loss, and abdominal pain [3]. In most instances, they are due to medication side effects or secondary to infections. However, in rare cases, lupus enteritis (LE) and peritonitis can be the underlying cause of these symptoms [2, 4]. Here, we present a patient with severe gastrointestinal symptoms who was ultimately diagnosed with lupus enteritis and peritonitis as the underlying cause of her manifestations.

In 2004, the British Isles Lupus Assessment Group (BILAG) defined lupus enteritis as either vasculitis or inflammation of the small bowel with supportive imaging or biopsy findings [7]. The prevalence of LE in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) patients varies by region, with reported rates ranging from 0.2% to 9.7%. Notably, Asian populations have a higher incidence of LE compared to other groups. Moreover, LE predominantly affects women [8]. While serositis in SLE patients is relatively common, lupus peritonitis, characterized by inflammation of the peritoneal serous membrane, is very rare [2]. Although an autopsy study of patients with SLE revealed peritoneal involvement in up to 70% of cases, only 10% exhibited clinical symptoms during their lifetime (1 [1]. The pathophysiology for both lupus enteritis and peritonitis is primarily attributed to two mechanisms: immune complex deposition and thrombosis of intestinal or peritoneal vessels [9].

The likelihood of developing LE increases with a higher SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) [8]. LE can affect the entire gastrointestinal tract. Nevertheless, the jejunum (83%) and ileum (84%) are the most involved segments [4]. The cardinal symptoms for a patient with LE are abdominal discomfort (97%), ascites (78%), nausea and vomiting (45%), diarrhea (42%), and fever (20%) [10]. Lupus peritonitis also shares overlapping symptoms with LE, particularly abdominal pain and ascites [2]. Except for fever and diarrhea, all the symptoms listed above were present in our case. Additionally, our patient had other infrequent symptoms, including melena and hematemesis [8].

Excluding lupus nephritis (LN) is crucial, as it is a well-documented comorbidity of lupus enteritis (LE), with a reported prevalence of up to 54% in Asian populations. Additionally, lupus cystitis and hydronephrosis have been reported in LE patients. In addition to renal involvement, hematologic abnormalities such as anemia (52%) and lymphopenia (40%) are also common in LE, as demonstrated in our case [8]. Lupus patients have a threefold increased risk of pulmonary embolism compared to the general population [11]. Our patient was incidentally diagnosed with pulmonary embolism. However, to our knowledge, there is no existing literature addressing the coexistence or potential association between pulmonary embolism and LE, suggesting a gap in understanding that warrants further investigation.

The most frequent laboratory findings in lupus enteritis include hematologic abnormalities, such as leukopenia, lymphopenia, and anemia, in addition to positive ANA (92%), anti-dsDNA (74%), low complement levels (88%), anti-RNP (28%), anti-SSA (26%), and anti-Sm (24%) [4]. However, the diagnosis of lupus enteritis lacks a specific biological marker. Ascitic fluid analysis and imaging are essential in diagnosing lupus peritonitis. The characteristic ascitic fluid in lupus peritonitis typically presents as an exudative fluid with a Serum Ascites Albumin Gradient (SAAG) of less than 1.1. Additionally, the fluid may show a wide range of white blood cell (WBC) counts, varying from 10 to 1630/mm³, and a protein concentration ranging from 34 to 47 mg/dl [12].

An abdominal CT scan is the primary imaging modality to detect lupus enteritis (LE). It typically shows [1] bowel wall edema and enhancement (target sign) [2], engorgement of mesenteric vessels (comb sign), and [3] increased attenuation of mesenteric fat [4].Our patient’s CT findings were consistent with the diagnosis of lupus enteritis and peritonitis and were instrumental in confirming the correct diagnosis. The abdominal CT scan revealed diffuse circumferential wall thickening and mural stratification, presenting the ‘target sign,’ along with surrounding fat stranding and interloop free fluid. Abdominal ultrasound can serve as a quick and simple method for detecting bowel edema, indicative of LE, and may be utilized when a CT scan is unavailable [13]. The results of endoscopic biopsies in LE are typically nonspecific and involve only superficial layers; a small percentage of patients show vasculitis or necrosis. Although not considered helpful for diagnosis, endoscopic tests aid in ruling out alternative causes. Nonspecific findings such as ulcers, erosions, and mucosal edema have been reported to be associated with LE [4]. In contrast, while ascitic fluid analysis and imaging are helpful for the diagnosis of lupus peritonitis, it should be regarded as a diagnosis of exclusion, requiring a thorough evaluation to rule out more common causes of exudative ascites. These include conditions such as peritoneal carcinomatosis, primary mesothelioma, peritoneal pseudomyxoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, peritoneal tuberculosis, nephrotic syndrome, protein-losing enteropathy, and severe malnutrition [14]. The ascitic fluid analysis in our patient ruled out secondary (infectious) peritonitis, as evidenced by a normal glucose level, normal lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and a low neutrophil count (< 250 cells/mm³). No evidence of intra-abdominal malignancy, abscess, or visceral perforation was observed on contrast-enhanced CT (no focal mass, no free air). The absence of pancreatitis was confirmed by the presence of normal serum lipase levels. Despite the absence of cytological examination of ascitic fluid, the absence of radiologic indicators of malignancy, and the patient’s clinical context rendered neoplastic peritonitis was highly improbable. Tuberculous peritonitis was not specifically investigated due to the absence of epidemiologic risk factors or clinical features indicative of tuberculosis. The peritonitis was ascribed to SLE-associated serositis by systematically excluding infectious, neoplastic, and other inflammatory causes.

The first line of treatment for lupus enteritis involves complete bowel rest along with high-dose intravenous methylprednisolone. Immunosuppressive medications such as cyclophosphamide, cyclosporine A, and azathioprine may be used as part of a maintenance treatment plan, often in combination with oral corticosteroids [9]. In the case of lupus peritonitis, it shares a similar management as lupus enteritis which typically involves the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and corticosteroids. In refractory cases, characterized by ongoing fluid accumulation in the serosal cavity, immunomodulators or immunosuppressants may be considered. Notably, pulse therapy with cyclophosphamide has proven to be an effective approach in these situations [1]. Our patient was treated with 1 gram of intravenous methylprednisolone pulse therapy, 500 mg of cyclophosphamide in combination with mesna, and 100 IU of Low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) (enoxaparin). Following this treatment, her condition improved significantly. Relapses can occur in lupus enteritis, with recurrence rates up to 30%, often within the first two years [8]. In cases of lupus enteritis, an intestinal wall thickness greater than 9 mm is a marker for increased recurrence risk. For these patients, extended corticosteroid therapy and/or the addition of immunosuppressive medications may be required [15].

The selection of an agent is contingent upon the existing illness manifestations, the patient’s age and reproductive potential, safety considerations, and cost. Methotrexate and azathioprine (AZA) should be contemplated for patients exhibiting inadequate symptom management following a trial of glucocorticoids and hydroxychloroquine, or when hydroxychloroquine alone is deemed insufficient, owing to the extensive experience with their application and their comparatively safe profile. Cyclophosphamide may be utilized in organ-threatening conditions (particularly renal, cardiopulmonary, or neuropsychiatric) and only as a salvage therapy for resistant non-major organ manifestations; owing to its gonadotoxic properties, it should be administered with caution in individuals of reproductive age, both women and men [16]. In our case, the patient had lupus enteritis, peritonitis, as well as worsening in her ascites despite the use of glucocorticoids. We recommended using cyclophosphamide, which showed tremendous improvement in her state of health. After the improvement, the patient was switched to regular lupus therapy.

Bowel wall ischemia and perforation are the most serious and potentially life-threatening complications. Systemic corticosteroids can mask symptoms of peritonitis, so a high index of suspicion for perforation is essential, along with careful clinical and radiological monitoring [4].

Conclusion

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune disorder, that presents multiple manifestations, including musculoskeletal, cardiac, renal, and many other manifestations. We highlight in our presentation, a case of SLE With mostly gastrointestinal manifestations, specifically lupus enteritis and peritonitis. These gastric manifestations hold many differential diagnoses and could be interpreted through different approaches. So, it is important to consider SLE during the workup of gastrointestinal symptoms in differential diagnosis. Gastrointestinal manifestations in SLE are quite rare, especially lupus enteritis and peritonitis. These two gastrointestinal manifestations, even though they are rare to occur, it is even more rare and unique when they occur together. This case highlights the importance of knowing how gastrointestinal manifestations arise in SLE patients. Laboratory findings, imaging, and interventions such as gastroscopy, are important in the diagnosis of SLE. Management is very crucial since it influences the outcomes of the disease. This case is unique, it holds importance for the physicians on how to deal with gastrointestinal manifestations in SLE patients, and how they could be managed correctly.
