Comparative efficacy of cervical erector spinae plane block and superficial cervical plexus block for postoperative analgesia in anterior cervical discectomy and fusion: A prospective randomized study
Authors: Ergun Mendes, Ozal Adiyeke, Onur Sarban, Ilhan Yilmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is a commonly performed procedure for cervical disc disease; however, significant postoperative pain may hinder recovery and increase opioid consumption. This study compared the efficacy of cervical erector spinae plane block (cESPB) and superficial cervical plexus block (sCPB) techniques in ACDF.Methods:This prospective, randomized study included American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class I–II patients aged 18 to 65 years who underwent ACDF surgery for cervical (C)5–C6 or C6–C7 disc disease. Patients were randomly assigned to 1 of 2 groups according to the regional block technique administered: cESPB (n = 20) and sCPB (n = 20). Perioperative propofol and remifentanil consumption were recorded. While the primary outcomes were the Quality of Recovery-15 (QoR-15) and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores, the secondary outcomes included analgesic consumption, the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), and the presence of shoulder and neck myofascial pain.Results:Total intraoperative propofol and remifentanil requirements were similar between the groups (P = .362 and P = .058, respectively). Intraoperative remifentanil consumption was higher in the sCPB group during T2, T3, and T4 (P = .004, P < .001, and P = .018, respectively). QoR-15 scores were significantly higher in the cESPB group than in the sCPB group (121.45 ± 6.48 vs 114.15 ± 8.19; P = .003; MD: 7.30; 95% CI: 2.57–12.03; Cohen d = 0.99). PCA infusion volumes at PCA6, PCA12, and PCA24 were significantly lower in the cESPB group (18.32 ± 8.68 vs 26.10 ± 11.12 mL, 33.35 ± 13.83 vs 43.70 ± 14.78 mL, and 53.30 ± 16.45 vs 65.05 ± 19.09 mL, respectively; P = .019, P = .028, and P = .044; Cohen d = -0.78, −0.72, and −0.66, respectively). VAS scores, PONV incidence, and shoulder/neck myofascial pain were similar between the groups (P > .05), with small effect sizes observed across all VAS measurements.Conclusions:In patients undergoing ACDF surgery, cESPB was associated with higher postoperative QoR-15 scores and lower postoperative PCA consumption compared with sCPB. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as the compared interventions differed in laterality, total local anesthetic volume, and potential dermatomal coverage. Therefore, the observed associations may not be attributable solely to the block technique itself.

1. Introduction

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is a commonly performed surgical procedure for the treatment of cervical spine disorders, including disc herniation, cervical spondylosis, and degenerative disc disease. Although effective in alleviating neurological symptoms and restoring spinal stability, ACDF is frequently associated with significant postoperative pain, which may hinder early mobilization, delay recovery, and reduce patient satisfaction. Therefore, effective perioperative pain management is a key component of optimizing postoperative outcomes and facilitating a faster return to function.[1]

Traditionally, postoperative pain following ACDF has been managed with systemic analgesics, including opioids and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.[2] While these medications can be effective, their use is often limited by side effects, including sedation, nausea, constipation, and the risk of opioid dependency, especially in the early postoperative period.

In recent years, regional anesthesia techniques have gained increasing attention as components of multimodal analgesia strategies aimed at improving pain control while minimizing systemic drug-related adverse effects. Among these techniques, cervical erector spinae plane block (cESPB) and superficial cervical plexus block (sCPB) have emerged as promising approaches for perioperative analgesia in anterior neck and cervical spine surgery.[3,4] These techniques offer the potential for targeted sensory blockade, thereby reducing opioid requirements and improving patient comfort. However, despite their growing clinical use, the comparative effects of cESPB and sCPB on postoperative analgesic requirements, patient-reported pain, and quality of recovery remain inadequately characterized.[5]

Therefore, this study aimed to compare the effectiveness of cESPB and sCPB in patients undergoing ACDF, with a particular focus on perioperative analgesia and postoperative recovery outcomes. By evaluating these 2 regional anesthesia techniques, we sought to contribute to the development of more effective and individualized pain management strategies for cervical spine surgery.

2. Materials and methods

This prospective study was approved by the local ethics committee (KAEK/2023.06.232) and registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: NCT05680142). The clinical trial was prospectively registered on July 7, 2023, and completed on May 15, 2024. Informed consent was obtained from all patients. This manuscript adheres to the applicable CONSORT guidelines.

2.1. Study design and data collection

The study included American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) I–II patients aged 18 to 65 years scheduled for elective ACDF surgery at the cervical (C)5–C6 or C6–C7 levels under general anesthesia. Exclusion criteria included bleeding disorders, neurological diseases, a Body Mass Index (BMI) outside 19 to 30 kg/m2, neck infection, and refusal of the procedure.

Eligible patients were randomly assigned to either the cESPB or sCPB group using a computer-generated random number table. Randomization codes were generated before patient enrollment. Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by an investigator who was not involved in patient recruitment, block performance, perioperative management, outcome assessment, or statistical analysis. The envelopes were opened immediately before block administration.

All patients received midazolam (0.02 mg/kg) as premedication. All block procedures, including both cESPB and sCPB, were performed preoperatively by the same anesthesiologist (EM), who had approximately 10 years of clinical experience in ultrasound-guided peripheral nerve blocks. The clinicians responsible for evaluating perioperative management and assessing postoperative outcomes were blinded to group allocation and had no involvement in the block procedures. Blocks were performed under ultrasound guidance using an 8 to 12 MHz linear transducer with an in-plane approach after negative aspiration.

The sCPB was performed on the right side of the neck at the level of the C4 vertebra. A transducer was placed transversely over the lateral border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle (SCM). A 22‑gauge block needle was advanced into the fascial plane deep to the SCM from lateral to medial, but superficial to the prevertebral fascia. Ten mL of 0.25% bupivacaine was injected unilaterally, visualizing its spread between the fasciae under the SCM.[6] The cESPB was applied bilaterally to the fascia between the semispinalis capitis muscle and the posterior scalene muscle at the transverse process of C6 or C7.[7] The transverse process of C7 was identified by its narrow tip and the absence of the anterior tubercle between C6 and C7. The ultrasound probe was placed posteriorly, lateral to the spinous process. A 22-gauge, 80 mm block needle was inserted lateral to the ultrasound in a posterolateral direction until it contacted the posterior tip of the posterior tubercle of C6 or C7. A 0.25% solution of the local anesthetic (LA) bupivacaine was injected bilaterally at a volume of 10 mL per side.

Anesthesia was managed by a blinded anesthesiologist. In the operating room, the electrocardiogram, noninvasive blood pressure, bispectral index (BIS), and peripheral oxygen saturation were monitored. Anesthesia was induced with fentanyl at 1 mcg/kg, propofol at 2 mg/kg, and rocuronium at 0.5 mg/kg. All patients underwent left radial catheterization. Ventilation was maintained with 40% oxygen and 60% air at 2 L/min.

Total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) was initiated with propofol at 5 mg/kg/h, adjusted based on BIS values between 40 and 60. Remifentanil at 5 mcg/kg/h, adjusted based on mean arterial pressure (MAP) above 60 mm Hg, was targeted to ensure adequate organ perfusion, with adjustments of 0.5 levels every 5 minutes. Diltiazem 5 mg was administered if MAP exceeded 90 mm Hg, ephedrine 5 mg IV if MAP dropped below 50 mm Hg, and atropine 0.5 mg IV if the heart rate fell below 45 bpm. Medication usage and operative time were recorded from induction to awakening.

The perioperative timeline was divided into 4 distinct phases: Time (T)1 (from induction to the first surgical incision), T2 (from the first incision to vertebral body exposure), T3 (from disc removal and graft placement to screw application), and T4 (from the closing phase to patient awakening). All procedures were performed by the same surgeon (IY). Operative time was documented as the duration from induction to awakening.

2.2. Definition of outcome

Postoperative pain control included paracetamol (10 mg/kg), tenoxicam (20 mg), and tramadol (1 mg/kg) administered before awakening. Pain was assessed using a 0 to 10 Visual Analog Scale (VAS) during the first 24 hours, with 0 indicating no pain and 10 indicating the worst imaginable pain. Postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) were rated on a Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) from 0 to 3, with ondansetron 8 mg given for NRS scores of 2 or 3. Shoulder and neck myofascial pain was evaluated 1 month postoperatively using VAS, with a score of 3 or higher indicating myofascial pain in the neck or shoulder.

Patient analgesic requirements, frequency of PONV, and VAS scores were monitored by a nurse blinded to the study in the postanesthesia care unit (PACU). After 1 hour, patients were transferred to the ward. Postoperative analgesia was maintained using a patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) device with tramadol (5 mg/mL), set to deliver 5 mg/h with a 15 mg bolus and a 20-minute lockout time. VAS scores and tramadol consumption were recorded at 6, 12, and 24 hours postoperatively.

The Quality of Recovery-15 (QoR-15) scale, which measures recovery across 5 domains (physical comfort, pain, physical independence, psychological support, and emotional state), was used to compare patients’ QoR-15 scores at 24 hours postoperatively. The primary endpoints of this study were improved postoperative QoR-15 scores and VAS scores. Secondary endpoints included reduced postoperative analgesic consumption, a lower incidence of PONV, and a decreased incidence of myofascial pain in the shoulder or neck.

2.3. Statistical analysis

Based on the published QoR-15 values (group 1: 113.9 ± 17.83 and group 3: 137.77 ± 9.64),[8] the calculated effect size (Cohen d) was approximately 1.66. However, to avoid overestimation, a more conservative effect size (Cohen d = 1.1) was assumed for the sample size calculation. Using a 2-tailed t test with an alpha of 0.05 and 90% power, the required minimum sample size was determined using G*Power (version 3.1; Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf) to be 17 participants per group (34 in total). To further ensure statistical robustness and account for potential dropouts, 20 participants per group (40 in total) were ultimately recruited.

Data are presented as mean ± SD, median [IQR], or n (%). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Independent group comparisons were performed using Student t test or the Mann–Whitney U test according to data distribution. Repeated measures ANOVA with Bonferroni correction was applied for parametric repeated measurements, while Friedman test was used for nonparametric repeated analyses. Within-group comparisons were assessed using the paired t test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test, as appropriate. In addition, effect sizes (Cohen d) and 95% confidence intervals were calculated where applicable. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Mac OS, version 27.0; IBM Corp.), and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

A total of 47 patients were assessed for eligibility. Seven patients were excluded before randomization, including 5 who did not meet the inclusion criteria and 2 whose surgeries were canceled. The remaining 40 patients underwent randomization and were allocated to either the cESPB group (n = 20) or the sCPB group (n = 20). All randomized patients received the allocated intervention, completed follow-up, and were included in the final analysis. No patients were lost to follow-up, resulting in a dropout rate of 0%. 3.2. Intraoperative drug consumption

The total intraoperative propofol consumption (mg) and remifentanil consumption (mcg) were similar between the sCPB and cESPB groups (P > .05). Propofol consumption (mg) was 2032.50 ± 718.24 in the sCPB group and 1807.50 ± 819.06 in the cESPB group (P = .362). Remifentanil consumption (mcg) was 3165.00 ± 1124.73 in the sCPB group and 2416.50 ± 1292.86 in the cESPB group (P = .058).

Propofol (mg/kg/h) consumption was similar across all time periods (P > .05). Propofol (mg/kg/h) consumption at T1, T2, T3, and T4 was 4.45 ± 0.96, 5.45 ± 1.20, 8.47 ± 1.71, and 6.03 ± 0.83 in the sCPB group, and 4.50 ± 1.09, 5.90 ± 1.25, 7.80 ± 2.79, and 5.33 ± 1.62 in the cESPB group, respectively (P = .878, P = .254, P = .363, and P = .096, respectively).

While remifentanil consumption (mcg/kg/h) was similar between the sCPB and cESPB groups at T1 (P = .943), significant between-group differences were observed at T2, T3, and T4 (P = .004, P < .001, and P = .018, respectively). Remifentanil (mcg/kg/h) consumption at T1, T2, T3, and T4 was 4.53 ± 1.03, 5.22 ± 0.94, 15.28 ± 4.63, and 6.90 ± 2.17 in the sCPB group, and 4.55 ± 1.17, 6.38 ± 1.35, 9.40 ± 4.14, and 5.33 ± 1.85 in the cESPB group, respectively.

When evaluating the groups within themselves, the time periods T1, T2, T3, and T4 were found to differ significantly in terms of propofol and remifentanil consumption (P < .001). In the sCPB group, propofol consumption showed statistical similarity between T2 and T4 (P = .110) but differed significantly between other periods. In the cESPB group, propofol consumption was statistically similar between T1 and T4 (P = .057) and T2 and T4 (P = .241), but differed significantly between other periods. Remifentanil consumption in the sCPB group differed significantly across all time periods. 3.3. Primary outcomes

QoR-15 scores were significantly higher in the cESPB group than in the sCPB group (121.45 ± 6.48 vs 114.15 ± 8.19; P = .003; MD: 7.30; 95% CI: 2.57–12.03; Cohen d = 0.99).

VAS pain scores at VAS1, VAS6, VAS12, and VAS24 were 1.55 ± 1.47 vs 2.20 ± 1.61, 1.10 ± 1.02 vs 1.20 ± 1.06, 1.50 ± 1.24 vs 1.40 ± 1.14, and 1.25 ± 0.97 vs 1.20 ± 1.01 in the cESPB and sCPB groups, respectively (P = .190, P = .762, P = .791, and P = .873, respectively). The corresponding mean differences, 95% confidence intervals, and effect sizes were: VAS1 (MD: −0.65; 95% CI: −1.64 to 0.34; Cohen d = -0.42), VAS6 (MD: −0.10; 95% CI: −0.76 to 0.56; Cohen d = −0.10), VAS12 (MD: 0.10; 95% CI: −0.66 to 0.86; Cohen d = 0.08), and VAS24 (MD: 0.05; 95% CI: −0.58 to 0.68; Cohen d = 0.05).

3.4. Secondary outcomes

Rescue analgesia was required in 7 patients (35%) in the sCPB group and 5 patients (25%) in the cESPB group (P = .490). Among patients who required rescue analgesia, the mean analgesic consumption (mg) was 95.14 ± 35.15 in the sCPB group (n = 7) and 105.00 ± 37.41 in the cESPB group (n = 5) (P = .653).

PCA infusion volumes at PCA6, PCA12, and PCA24 were significantly lower in the cESPB group than in the sCPB group (18.32 ± 8.68 vs 26.10 ± 11.12 mL, 33.35 ± 13.83 vs 43.70 ± 14.78 mL, and 53.30 ± 16.45 vs 65.05 ± 19.09 mL, respectively; P = .019, P = .028, and P = .044, respectively). 3.5. Myofascial pain and hemodynamic support

The presence of shoulder and neck myofascial pain at preoperative, postoperative, and 1-month follow-up was recorded as 9 (45%), 5 (25%), and 4 (20%) in the sCPB group, and 8 (40%), 4 (20%), and 2 (10%) in the cESPB group (P = .749, P = .705, P = .633, and P = .376, respectively).

The intraoperative requirements for diltiazem, atropine, and ephedrine were 2 (10%), 3 (15%), and 4 (20%) in the sCPB group, and 3 (15%), 2 (10%), and 2 (10%) in the cESPB group (P = .633, P = .254, P = .633, and P = .376, respectively). No complications were observed in either group (P > .05).

4. Discussion

In this prospective randomized study, cESPB was associated with higher postoperative QoR-15 scores than sCPB in patients undergoing ACDF surgery. The observed between-group difference in QoR-15 scores was 7.30 points, with a large effect size (Cohen d = 0.99). Previous studies have reported a minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of approximately 8 points for the QoR-15 scale.[9] Therefore, although the observed difference was statistically significant, it approached rather than exceeded the established MCID threshold. Consequently, the clinical relevance of this finding should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Given that QoR-15 was the primary outcome of our study, these findings suggest that cESPB may be associated with improved postoperative recovery quality under the conditions of the present study. In addition, postoperative PCA consumption was consistently lower in the cESPB group, with moderate-to-large effect sizes observed at PCA6, PCA12, and PCA24 (Cohen d = −0.78, −0.72, and −0.66, respectively), supporting a potential opioid-sparing effect.

ACDF causes significant pain, often leading to long-term opioid use.[10] Effective perioperative analgesia is crucial for pain management and outcomes.[11] Techniques like cESPB and sCPB are increasingly used in neurosurgery.[3,12] The sCPB, originating from the C2-C4 nerve roots, is frequently preferred in many neck surgeries due to its anesthetic and analgesic effects. This dermatomal distribution covers a wide area of superficial skin and soft tissue, extending from the infra-auricular region down to the upper thoracic cage and the shoulder–acromioclavicular joint.[13] For deeper tissue anesthesia, the addition of bilateral deep cervical plexus block (dCPB) is usually required; however, due to the higher associated risks compared to SCPB, it is generally avoided.[14] Wang et al reported that combining sCPB or dCPB with general anesthesia in patients undergoing single-level ACDF (C3–C7) reduced operative time, postoperative analgesic requirements, and recovery time, although general anesthesia alone provided better intraoperative stability and patient satisfaction.[15] Nevertheless, the routine use of a right-sided surgical approach in ACDF surgeries makes unilateral sCPB a safer option.[16]

Today, high thoracic and lower cervical erector spinae plane blocks (cESPB) are emerging as alternative approaches for surgeries in these regions due to their cranial spread and safer profile.[11,17] To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first prospective trial to compare 2 distinct techniques – each shown in the literature to contribute to postoperative pain management – for ACDF surgeries.

Previous cadaveric and clinical studies demonstrated that cESPB may spread to the cervical nerve roots and surrounding fascial compartments,[7] potentially contributing to effective perioperative analgesia but also raising concerns regarding phrenic nerve involvement.[18] While bilateral phrenic nerve paralysis (PhNP) rates reached up to 19% with partial dye spread, this probability decreased to nearly 1% with intense staining patterns.[19] PhNP is defined as a reduction of 75% or more in diaphragmatic movement, though no clinical symptoms were observed.[20,21] Previous studies evaluating cervical and high-thoracic ESPB (HT-ESPB) techniques have demonstrated that both the injection level and local anesthetic volume may substantially influence cranio-caudal spread, dermatomal coverage, and anterior/paravertebral diffusion.[22-28] In our study, no clinically significant respiratory complications were observed, possibly due to the preoperative administration of cESPB or the relatively lower local anesthetic volume used for bilateral cESPB administration.

Bilateral thoracic (Th)1 or Th3 ESPB has been investigated for postoperative analgesia in posterior cervical fusion surgery. Administration of 20 mL of local anesthetic mixed with contrast demonstrated variable spread, with extension to the C2 paravertebral region in some cases. In contrast, reports from shoulder surgery have shown inconsistent results, suggesting that unilateral Th2–Th3 ESPB with 30 mL of local anesthetic may not provide adequate analgesia of the shoulder capsule (C3–C4). Taken together, these findings suggest that thoracic-level ESPB may not consistently provide sufficient coverage for cervical spine procedures.[29]

Elsharkawy et al.[7] found that unilateral 20 mL C6 or C7 ESPB with Indian ink consistently reached the C5-Th1 nerve roots. A similar injection with radiographic contrast extended to C2–C3.[30] In a patient who underwent ACDF surgery between C6-Th1, bilateral Th1 ESPB with 20 mL LA caused sensory loss between C3-Th3.[31] Case-specific cESPB applications at the transverse process in C5–C7 disc surgeries provided effective analgesia, stable hemodynamics, and reduced opioid use with bilateral 15 mL 0.25% bupivacaine per side.[17] In 6 cases of shoulder arthroscopy, unilateral C6 or C7 ESPB with 20 to 30 mL LA consistently spread to C3–C4, with remifentanil requirements ranging from 0.06 to 0.13 mcg/kg/min, averaging 0.10 mcg/kg/min.[32] Significant between-group differences in remifentanil consumption were observed during T2, T3, and T4. These findings may suggest that sCPB provides more effective analgesia for superficial cervical tissues, whereas cESPB may offer broader coverage of deeper cervical structures.

Episodes of hypotension and bradycardia were observed during the intraoperative period, specifically during the administration of TIVA. These events occurred after anesthesia induction and not immediately following the regional block procedures. Therefore, they were considered to be related to anesthetic management rather than the block techniques themselves.

Positioning the ESPB catheter at C5-C6 enhanced analgesia via brachial plexus access, improving postoperative recovery with a 10 mL LA mixture.[33] Hamadnalla et al[34] demonstrated effective craniocaudal and anterior spread between C4-Th4 by positioning the ESPB catheter at C7 during shoulder disarticulation surgery.

Kamel et al[35] compared 15 mL C6 ESPB with an intermediate cervical plexus block – below the superficial cervical fascia – (iCPB) in anterior cervical discectomy (without fusion), finding cESPB superior in reducing nalbuphine use and preventing PhNP, which occurred in 1 patient in the iCPB group. VAS scores were higher at the eighth hour with iCPB and at the twelfth hour with cESPB. Unlike our study, postoperative recovery quality was not evaluated using the QoR-15 questionnaire. The absence of significant differences in VAS scores may be related to the routine use of multimodal postoperative analgesia and intravenous PCA in both groups.

An apparent discrepancy was observed between the similar postoperative VAS scores and the lower PCA consumption and higher QoR-15 scores in the cESPB group. However, QoR-15 is a multidimensional outcome measure that evaluates several aspects of postoperative recovery beyond pain intensity alone, including physical comfort, physical independence, emotional well-being, and psychological support. Therefore, improvements in overall recovery quality may occur despite comparable pain scores. In addition, the routine use of multimodal analgesia and intravenous PCA in both groups may have reduced differences in reported pain intensity while still allowing differences in opioid consumption and recovery quality to become evident.

Mariappan et al[16] demonstrated improved QoR-40 with unilateral sCPB using 10 mL 0.25% bupivacaine for single or 2-stage ACDF surgery. In our study, the quality of recovery was assessed with QoR-15 and this improvement was found to be higher in the cESPB group. The sCPB was performed at the midpoint of the SCM with 10 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine. They used a fan-shaped injection technique without ultrasound and reported similar opioid use (Morphine-ME 20–25 mg) to the control group. In our study, opioid consumption was lower in the cESPB group. Their VAS scores were higher than ours, with medians of 4 to 6 in the first 12 hours. This difference in VAS scores may be attributable to differences in postoperative analgesic management between the studies. In our study, there was no difference in the incidence of PONV between the 2 approaches.

Previous studies have suggested that ESPB may contribute to chronic pain reduction through extensive fascial and dorsal ramus spread involving multiple cervical dermatomes.[26,36-39] In our study, although postoperative shoulder and neck pain scores were lower in the cESPB group, the difference was not statistically significant at 1-month follow-up.

An important consideration when interpreting our findings is that the 2 investigated techniques differed not only in block type but also in laterality, total local anesthetic volume, and potential dermatomal coverage. Therefore, the observed advantages associated with cESPB cannot be attributed solely to the block technique itself. Bilateral administration and the larger total volume of local anesthetic may have contributed to the wider sensory coverage and improved recovery outcomes observed in the cESPB group. Future studies using standardized local anesthetic volumes and comparable unilateral or bilateral block protocols are needed to better isolate the specific effects of each technique.

5. Limitations

The differences in the total volume of the local anesthetic, the absence of a control group, and the differences in bilaterality and dermatomal coverage between the protocols can be considered important limitations of this study, as these factors prohibit a pure head-to-head comparison of the block techniques. In addition, differences in dermatomal coverage and block levels between sCPB and cESPB may have influenced analgesic outcomes independently of the technique itself. The sample size calculation in this study was based on QoR-15 scores from a different surgical context due to the lack of direct comparative data for ACDF procedures, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, the relatively small sample size may have reduced the statistical power for detecting differences in secondary outcomes. In addition, multiple secondary outcomes and repeated between-group comparisons were evaluated, which may increase the risk of a type I error. Although Bonferroni correction was applied for repeated measures analyses where appropriate, adjustment for all between-group comparisons was not performed. Therefore, some statistically significant findings, particularly among secondary outcomes, should be interpreted with appropriate caution until confirmed in larger studies.

Additionally, the lack of objective evaluation for potential complications, particularly PhNP using ultrasonographic assessment according to established guidelines, represents an important limitation regarding the safety evaluation of the investigated techniques. Postoperative pain scores may therefore have been confounded by neuropathic pain components, potentially limiting the accuracy of pain assessment. Postoperative pain assessment may also have been influenced by multiple factors, including concomitant systemic analgesic administration, which could not be fully controlled despite standardized analgesic protocols. Hemodynamic events such as hypotension and bradycardia occurred during TIVA and may also have acted as confounding factors when evaluating the safety profile of the regional blocks.

Finally, the absence of a control group receiving standard analgesia without a regional block limits the ability to fully evaluate the absolute clinical benefit of the investigated regional anesthesia techniques. Further studies with longer follow-up periods, larger sample sizes, and more standardized protocols are necessary to obtain more reliable conclusions regarding the incidence and characteristics of chronic pain.

6. Conclusions

In ACDF surgery, cESPB was associated with higher postoperative QoR-15 scores and lower postoperative PCA consumption compared with sCPB under the conditions of the present study. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the absence of a no-block control group, the relatively small sample size, and differences between the compared interventions, including laterality, total local anesthetic volume, and potential dermatomal coverage. Therefore, the observed associations may not be attributable solely to the block technique itself and do not establish definitive superiority of 1 technique over the other. Further prospective studies with larger sample sizes and standardized block protocols are required to better define the comparative effectiveness of these regional anesthesia techniques.
