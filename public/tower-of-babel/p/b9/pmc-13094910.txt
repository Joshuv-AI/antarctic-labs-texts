Anesthesia technology students’ perspectives on multidisciplinary preoperative education: Implications for interprofessional training
Authors: Ellen Safadi, Murtada Idris, Maryam Hadi, Ihab Khatib, Mohammad Rawashdeh, Magdi A. Ali, Mohamed Zakaria El-Sayed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Multidisciplinary preoperative education enhances patient comprehension, reduces anxiety, and improves safety outcomes. However, the perspectives of anesthesia technology students regarding their preparedness for such roles remain underexplored. This study assessed anesthesia technology students’ perceptions of multidisciplinary collaboration in preoperative education and evaluated perceived curricular readiness.MATERIALS AND METHODS:An institution-based descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted at Gulf Medical University, Ajman, UAE, between March 1st and May 1st, 2025. A structured, pilot-tested questionnaire was distributed electronically to students across four academic years. Of 120 eligible students, 100 completed the survey (response rate: 83.3%). Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, and free-text responses underwent thematic analysis.RESULTS:Most respondents (93%) recognized multidisciplinary teamwork as essential to effective preoperative education, yet only 17% had observed consistent collaboration in clinical settings. Confidence in contributing to multidisciplinary education was reported by 37%, while 35% believed the curriculum had prepared them adequately. Thematic analysis revealed calls for enhanced simulation-based training, structured interprofessional opportunities, more precise role delineation, digital tools, and strategies to overcome language barriers.CONCLUSION:While anesthesia technology students strongly value multidisciplinary collaboration, they report limited confidence and exposure during training. Targeted curricular enhancements, particularly simulation and structured interprofessional education, are needed to strengthen student readiness for preoperative patient education. These findings illustrate the value of integrating comprehensive communication and teamwork skills into anesthesia technology programs to optimize patient outcomes and interprofessional collaboration.

Introduction

A multidisciplinary approach is central to preparing patients for surgery. Surgical outcomes are influenced not only by technical skill but also by how patients are educated and supported before the procedure. Over the past decades, preoperative education has evolved from providing basic instructions to structured interventions that address safety, satisfaction, and recovery.[123] Patient safety remains a priority because communication failures and human error are major contributors to morbidity and mortality in perioperative care.[456] To address this, multidisciplinary education has been promoted as a strategy to improve care quality, teamwork, and cost-effectiveness.[78]

Despite progress, preoperative education about anesthesia shows persistent gaps. Patients frequently report feelings of fear, uncertainty, and a lack of understanding regarding anesthesia risks and the roles of healthcare providers.[19] Studies show that up to 60% of patients have misconceptions about anesthesia, and more than one-third experience high levels of preoperative anxiety linked to a lack of information.[57] Research from multiple regions highlights that informed consent processes are often incomplete, leaving patients underinformed about the benefits, risks, and alternatives associated with anesthesia.[810] Such deficits undermine patient autonomy and may contribute to dissatisfaction and adverse outcomes.[10]

Patient-centered approaches, including shared decision-making, have been recommended to address these gaps. Involving patients in discussions improves satisfaction, reduces decisional conflict, and enhances adherence to care plans.[1112] Structured education programs led by multidisciplinary teams have demonstrated measurable benefits, including reduced preoperative anxiety, improved pain control, shorter hospital stays, and a faster return to daily activities.[131415] Some programs also incorporate prehabilitation strategies, such as nutrition counseling and exercise, which further enhance recovery.[1617] However, patient surveys show that unmet needs persist in areas such as pain management, recovery expectations, and clarity of anesthesia-related information, with as many as 25% of patients reporting they felt underprepared despite education efforts.[1819]

Barriers to effective preoperative education are especially evident in low-resource settings. Studies from Ethiopia and other regions have documented poor educational practices among nurses and surgical staff, with only 38–40% demonstrating good practices. Contributing factors include limited training, lack of teaching materials, language barriers, and heavy workloads.[172021] These findings highlight the need for systematic interventions, staff development, and enhanced institutional support.

Within this landscape, anesthesia technologists play a critical role. They are directly involved in perioperative care, patient monitoring, and communication about anesthesia. Their role extends to supporting informed consent, alleviating patient anxiety, and ensuring that patients receive accurate and comprehensible information. Previous research has highlighted that patient awareness of anesthesia as a distinct specialty remains limited, with fewer than 30% of patients correctly identifying the responsibilities of anesthesiologists.[1522] This gap underscores the importance of anesthesia technologists and students as educators within multidisciplinary teams.

However, limited research has explored the perspectives of anesthesia technology students on preoperative education. Students represent the next generation of providers, and their views reflect both the current strengths of their training and areas in need of improvement. Understanding their perceptions is crucial for strengthening curricula, aligning educational approaches with international standards, and developing strategies that enhance patient outcomes. Incorporating their insights may also foster a stronger culture of patient-centered care within anesthesia practice.

This study, therefore, examines the perceptions of anesthesia technology students regarding multidisciplinary approaches to preoperative patient education. The objective is to identify strategies that enhance patient understanding, satisfaction, and recovery, while also informing future training and policy development to support anesthesia providers in delivering effective education.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

An institution-based descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted at the Anesthesia Technology Department of Gulf Medical University (GMU), Ajman, UAE, over 2 months. The survey, distributed via e-mail to students across all four academic years, was collected from March 1st to May 1st, 2025 (62 days).

Study participants and sampling

The target population comprised 137 anesthesia technology students enrolled across four academic years: first year (43), second year (33), third year (24), and fourth year (37). We invited 120 out of 137 students, excluding 17 students who had previously participated in the pilot testing, to avoid duplication of responses and minimize bias. The survey was emailed to the students’ official emails and participation was voluntary. Completion of the questionnaire was considered as informed consent. By the end of the data collection period, 100 students had completed the survey, yielding an 83.3% response rate and representing a broad cross-section of all academic years.

Data collection tools and techniques

A structured questionnaire was developed de novo, guided by a review of relevant literature[127] and an expert anesthesia consultant. Items were newly constructed to capture the perspectives of anesthesia technology students specifically. The questionnaire, constructed with Likert-scale items, typically ranged from “strongly disagree” (1) to “strongly agree” (5), and included qualitative responses, such as suggestions for additional educational topics or approaches. It was developed based on a literature review and expert input. The questionnaire assessed students’ perceptions of multidisciplinary preoperative education, their exposure to it, and confidence in participating in team-based patient education. The tool underwent pilot testing with 17 students to evaluate clarity and internal consistency, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.81.

The questionnaire was distributed electronically to all anesthesia technology students (120 students), excluding the 17 who participated in the pilot study. The response rate was 83.3% with 100 students completing the survey. Participation was voluntary, with informed consent obtained electronically.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the GMU Institutional Review Board (RB-COHS-STD-64-Jan-2025). Participants received detailed information about the research and provided digital informed consent before participation. Data were collected using Google Forms (Google Inc., Mountain View, CA). Technical safeguards—IP address filtering and response review prompts—were employed to limit duplicate submissions and maintain data integrity. No personal identifiers were collected, and all responses were anonymized. Data were stored securely in an encrypted repository with access limited to the research team.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed descriptively using frequencies and percentages. Qualitative responses were thematically analyzed.

Results

The study assessed anesthesia technology students’ perceptions of the effectiveness of a multidisciplinary approach to preoperative patient education, using both quantitative and qualitative data [Tables 1-3]. One hundred of the 120 invited students participated, yielding an 83.3% response rate. Participants included 30 first-year, 22 second-year, 15 third-year, and 33 fourth-year students. Most respondents were female (89%) and 11% were male [Table 1]. Qualitative responses were thematically analyzed. Responses were reviewed repeatedly, and key phrases were coded into concepts such as communication, time allocation, digital tools, and evidence-based education. Related codes were grouped into seven themes: effective communication, adequate time allocation, use of digital tools, community engagement, postoperative feedback, evidence-based education, and teaching methods. Theme frequencies are shown in Table 3. Two researchers reviewed the coding to ensure consistency and reduce bias.

Table 2 shows that most students recognize the importance of multidisciplinary teamwork in preoperative education, with 93% agreeing or strongly agreeing. Seventy-six percent acknowledged the key role of anesthesia professionals. Fewer students felt confident in their own contribution, with only 37% reporting self-confidence in team-based education. Observing effective multidisciplinary communication during clinical training was rare (17%), and only 35% felt the curriculum adequately prepared them for team-based education, indicating areas for improvement. Simulation-based learning was well received, with 75% recognizing its value in understanding team-based approaches.

Discussion

Anesthesia technology students strongly recognized the value of multidisciplinary teamwork in preoperative education, with 93% acknowledging its importance. This finding aligns with evidence that coordinated group teaching improves patient comprehension and satisfaction.[1] Students also highlighted the role of anesthetists in preoperative education, consistent with literature emphasizing the expanding responsibilities of anesthesia professionals in perioperative counseling and patient communication.[59]

Despite widespread acknowledgment of the importance of teamwork, self-reported confidence and curriculum preparedness were low. Only 37% of students felt confident contributing to team-based education, and 35% indicated that the curriculum adequately prepared them for this role. This discrepancy suggests a gap between theoretical understanding and practical readiness. Limited interprofessional exposure during clinical placements, with only 17% of students observing effective multidisciplinary communication, may contribute to this gap. Similar deficiencies have been documented in other professional health training programs.[1415] Enhancing interprofessional education by incorporating simulation-based exercises and structuring multidisciplinary clinical rotations may improve preparedness and confidence.

The sample exhibited a pronounced gender imbalance, with 89% female respondents. While representative of the cohort, this limitation restricts the generalizability of the results. It may influence the observed confidence levels, as gender differences have been reported in communication styles and perceived self-efficacy during clinical training. Future studies with more balanced cohorts are needed to clarify potential gender-related effects on perceptions of teamwork and readiness.

Simulation-based learning was identified as a key facilitator of team-based education, with 75% of students recognizing its value in bridging the gap between theoretical knowledge and clinical practice. Qualitative responses further highlighted strategies for optimizing preoperative education, including clear communication, integration of digital resources, and patient feedback mechanisms. These findings suggest that students appreciate both institutional and individual factors that influence educational effectiveness.[23]

Overall, the findings demonstrate strong conceptual support for multidisciplinary collaboration but reveal persistent gaps in confidence, clinical exposure, and curricular preparation. Targeted curriculum enhancements, expanded interprofessional opportunities, and structured support mechanisms, including translation services, are recommended to strengthen student readiness and improve patient outcomes.

This study was limited to a single institution, relied on self-reported data, and included a predominantly female sample, which may restrict generalizability. Although the questionnaire was validated through content review and pilot testing, its scope was limited. Varying clinical exposure across academic years could have influenced students’ preparedness. Future multicenter studies with more diverse cohorts and broader representation are needed to provide a more comprehensive assessment of student readiness for multidisciplinary preoperative education.

Implications and recommendations

The observed gaps highlight the need for curricular innovation that goes beyond technical training.

Educational implications: Embedding structured simulation-based and interprofessional training can bridge the divide between awareness and practice. Addressing communication and cultural competence is essential, particularly given the importance students place on overcoming language and literacy barriers. Inclusive strategies should also support the empowerment of all learners.

Programs should expand simulation experiences into interprofessional OSCEs, structured preoperative huddles, and the use of standardized counseling scripts. Student-led strategies such as simplified communication, adequate time allocation, digital platforms, and community engagement should be adopted to enrich practice. Formal feedback loops, such as postoperative patient surveys, can be utilized to strengthen reflective learning and quality improvement.

Conclusion

Anesthesia technology students recognize the importance of multidisciplinary preoperative education but demonstrate limited practical competence in collaborative skills. Integrating structured, experiential learning modules that emphasize interdisciplinary teamwork into the curriculum can enhance clinical preparedness and bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and real-world practice.
