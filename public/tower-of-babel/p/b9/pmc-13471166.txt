Effects of different exercise programmes on ankle stiffness, muscle strength, and plantar pressure distribution in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus and peripheral neuropathy: a single-blind randomised controlled trial
Authors: Cansu Koltak, Yasin Yurt, Ayşe Nur Tunalı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic peripheral neuropathy impairs plantar sensation and disrupts foot and ankle biomechanics, thereby increasing the risk of foot ulceration. This study aimed to compare the effects of different exercise interventions on ankle stiffness, ankle muscle strength, plantar pressure distribution and vibration perception threshold (VPT) in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D) and diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN).MethodsSeventy-four participants with T2D and DPN were randomised into three groups: a control group (CG) receiving standard medical treatment; an aerobic exercise group (AEG) receiving medical treatment plus supervised treadmill training three times per week; and a combined exercise group (AE+FAEG) performing additional targeted foot and ankle exercises following each aerobic session. Custom-made foot orthoses were provided to all participants. Ankle stiffness, isokinetic muscle strength, plantar pressure distribution, vibration perception threshold (VPT), and glycaemic parameters were assessed at baseline, Week 12, and Week 24. Data were analysed using two-way repeated measures ANOVA to evaluate Group × Time interactions.ResultsSignificant Group × Time interactions were observed for ankle stiffness (p < 0.001, η²p = 0.35), muscle strength (p < 0.001, η²p = 0.19–0.48), forefoot-to-rearfoot pressure ratio (p < 0.001, η²p = 0.31), VPT (p < 0.001, η²p = 0.51–0.52), and glycaemic parameters (p < 0.05, η²p = 0.10–0.24). Compared with the CG, both exercise groups improved over time, with greater gains in the AE+FAEG. While within-group reductions in plantar pressure were observed across all groups (p < 0.001), between-group differences were not consistently detected.ConclusionsThe integration of targeted foot and ankle exercises into a supervised aerobic programme was associated with meaningful improvements in ankle stiffness and muscle strength in individuals with T2D and DPN. Both exercise modalities were associated with improvements in glycaemic parameters, with the combined intervention demonstrating superior functional benefits compared with standard care. These findings support the inclusion of combined exercise strategies in rehabilitation programmes targeting biomechanical and metabolic impairments associated with diabetic foot complications.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, NCT05422235. Background

Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN), a prevalent microvascular complication of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D), is characterized by structural and functional impairments of the peripheral nervous system [1]. It affects 30–50% of patients with T2D, with disease duration identified as a significant independent risk factor for the development of DPN [2]. DPN primarily impacts distal sensorimotor nerves, causing muscle weakness, loss of proprioception, paraesthesia, and balance deficits [3]. Furthermore, microvascular dysfunction and soft tissue glycosylation reduce ankle strength and joint mobility while increasing tissue stiffness [4]. Notably, limited ankle joint range of motion and associated biomechanical changes have been identified as significant contributing factors to elevated plantar pressure and increased ulceration risk [5]. These alterations shift the pressure distribution under the foot, thereby increasing the risk for diabetic foot ulcers.”

In T2D management, lifestyle modifications and exercise are vital adjuncts to pharmacological therapy [6]. Meta-analyses demonstrate that aerobic, resistance, and combined training significantly improve glycaemic control [7]. Exercise mitigates neuropathy through several mechanisms, including enhanced muscle mass and oxidative capacity, which improve insulin sensitivity and help preserve nerve health [8]. By increasing microvascular perfusion, exercise enhances oxygen and nutrient delivery to nerves, reduces advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), and modulates nerve growth factor (NGF) receptors to support regeneration [1]. Furthermore, myokines and cytokines secreted during exercise regulate metabolic functions and anti-inflammatory responses [9]. Moderate-intensity aerobic exercise increases sensory nerve conduction velocity and improves ankle strength and mobility [1, 10, 11].Targeted foot and ankle exercises may also normalise plantar pressure and gait patterns by enhancing joint mobility and intrinsic muscle strength [12].

Although previous studies have suggested that ankle stiffness may influence plantar loading patterns [13], no previous randomised controlled trial has specifically examined the effects of combined aerobic and foot and ankle exercise programme on ankle stiffness as a primary outcome in individuals with DPN. Previous studies have confirmed the benefits of exercise on plantar pressure, lower extremity tissue mechanics, muscle strength, and neuropathy symptoms in individuals with DPN. However, while research has examined aerobic training or foot and ankle stretching and strengthening individually, their combined effects remain under-investigated [1, 6, 10, 11]. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the efficacy of adding specific foot and ankle exercises to treadmill-based aerobic training. We hypothesised that this combined intervention would produce greater improvements in plantar pressure distribution, ankle muscle strength, and ankle stiffness compared to aerobic training alone.

Methods

This study was designed as a single-blind (assessor-blinded) randomised controlled trial conducted in accordance with the CONSORT statement. The study protocol was approved by the Eastern Mediterranean University Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee (Approval No: ETK00-2021-0195). The trial was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT05422235), with registration completed on 13 June 2022, prior to the commencement of participant enrolment. The research was carried out between June 2022 and March 2025 at the outpatient clinic of Eastern Mediterranean University, involving participants aged 50–65 years diagnosed with T2D and confirmed DPN. All procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to enrolment, all participants received a detailed explanation of the study’s purpose and procedures, after which written informed consent was obtained.

Participants

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) ability to walk independently (2), cognitive competence (Mini-Mental State Examination score ≥ 23), and (3) a confirmed diagnosis of DPN according to the American Diabetes Association (ADA) guidelines. In accordance with ADA criteria, DPN was confirmed by abnormal results in at least two of the following assessments: inability to detect the 10-g Semmes-Weinstein monofilament, reduced vibration sensation (128-Hz tuning fork), a vibration perception threshold (VPT) > 25 V, sensory loss in the pin-prick test, or diminished/absent ankle reflexes [14]. In the present study, all participants met these criteria based on abnormal 10-g Semmes-Weinstein monofilament findings and VPT exceeding 25 V assessed at the hallux. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) active foot ulcers (2), a history of lower extremity amputation or surgery (3), Charcot foot deformity (4), major vascular complications. Furthermore, individuals who had engaged in a regular exercise programme within the preceding three months were excluded to minimise baseline variability.

Sample size calculation

The required sample size was determined using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7). For a repeated-measures ANOVA (within-between interaction) comparing three groups across three time points, the parameters were set at an effect size of f = 0.25, α = 0.05, and a power (1-β) of 0.90. The analysis yielded a minimum required sample size of 72 participants (n = 24 per group). To account for an anticipated 20% attrition rate, the target sample size was set at 87 participants.

Protocol amendments

The sample size specified in the trial registry (NCT05422235) reflected the initial planning-stage calculation based on a preliminary nonparametric ANOVA framework (Cohen’s d = 0.40, α = 0.05, power = 80%), which targeted 111 participants including an anticipated 20% attrition rate. During the finalization of the study protocol, the statistical analysis plan was revised to a repeated-measures ANOVA design (three groups across three time points). This revision was methodological in nature, based on the finalized statistical analysis plan, rather than interim study findings. No other amendments were made to the study protocol following initial trial registration.

Randomization

A total of 118 individuals with T2D were screened for eligibility, of whom 87 met the inclusion criteria and were successfully enrolled. Participants were allocated to one of three study groups using a random number table generated by Random Allocation Software (Version 1.0.0, Isfahan University, Iran) [15]. Allocation concealment was maintained, with assignments revealed sequentially upon enrolment. Throughout the 24-week study period, 13 participants (14.9%) withdrew from the trial. In the CG, five participants were lost to follow-up: three relocated and two initiated dialysis. In the AEG, five participants discontinued due to transportation issues (n = 3) and time constraints (n = 2). In the AE+FAEG, three participants withdrew due to transportation difficulties. In total, 74 participants completed the trial and were included in the final analysis: CG (n = 24), AEG (n = 24), and AE+FAEG (n = 26). Blinding

To maintain methodological rigour and minimise assessment bias, clinical assessments and exercise interventions were conducted by independent physiotherapists, with those responsible for assessments remaining blinded to group allocations throughout the study. Participants were also instructed to refrain from disclosing their assigned intervention to the assessor at any point during the study. As described above, clinical assessments were conducted at three time points: baseline (Week 0), immediately post-intervention (Week 12), and at the end of the follow-up period (Week 24).

Outcome measures

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were recorded for all participants, including age, height, body mass, body mass index (BMI), and time since T2D diagnosis. Outcome measures were assessed at three time points: baseline (Week 0), immediately post-intervention (Week 12), and at the end of the follow-up period (Week 24). The primary outcome was ankle stiffness. Secondary outcomes included plantar pressure distribution, ankle muscle strength, VPT, and glycaemic parameters. This article represents a partial analysis of the registered clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT05422235). While additional clinical and patient-reported outcomes across neuropathic pain and quality-of-life domains were collected as part of the broader registered trial protocol, this manuscript focuses specifically on biomechanical and functional outcomes relevant to diabetic foot management. These remaining outcomes will be reported in future publications. Unless otherwise specified, all unilateral assessments were performed on the dominant lower extremity to ensure methodological consistency, minimise measurement variability, and reduce participant burden, consistent with established protocols in diabetic neuropathy research [16]. All equipment was calibrated prior to each session according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Primary outcome measure

Ankle joint stiffness

The primary outcome was ankle joint stiffness, evaluated using an isokinetic dynamometer (Cybex Humac Norm, Model 502140, Computer Sports Medicine Inc., USA) operating in continuous passive motion (CPM) mode. An angular velocity of 60°/s was applied to represent functionally relevant daily activity and approximate ankle speed during normal gait [17]. During the procedure, participants were positioned prone with the knee in full extension. The thigh was stabilized, and the foot, maintained in a neutral subtalar position, was firmly secured using Velcro straps to minimize compensatory movements. Each participant’s maximum passive dorsiflexion range was recorded to establish individual safety limits. Gravity correction was performed via the device software to negate the influence of limb weight on the torque measurements.

To ensure the validity of the passive resistance measurements, surface electromyography (EMG) was used concurrently to monitor the electrical activity of the plantar flexor muscles. This confirmed that participants remained relaxed throughout the motion, ensuring that the recorded torque represented true passive stiffness without interference from active muscle contractions. The range of motion was standardized from 5° of plantar flexion to each individual’s maximum passive dorsiflexion limit. Ankle stiffness was subsequently calculated as the change in torque relative to angular displacement from the neutral position toward dorsiflexion (Nm/degree) [18].

Secondary outcomes

Glycaemic control

To evaluate the metabolic status of the participants, HbA1c and fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels were measured at all three assessment time points. Glycaemic control was assessed via glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) and fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels. HbA1c, reflecting average glycaemia over the preceding three months, was determined using the high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) method. FPG measurements were obtained from venous blood samples following a minimum 8-hour overnight fast and analysed using the enzymatic colorimetric method. Blood samples were collected at all three assessment time points (Week 0, Week 12, and Week 24). All biochemical analyses were conducted in a single accredited laboratory under standardised quality control procedures to ensure data reliability and minimise inter-assay variability.

Ankle muscle strength

Concentric muscle strength of the ankle plantar flexors and dorsiflexors was assessed using an isokinetic dynamometer (Cybex Humac Norm, Model 502140, Computer Sports Medicine Inc., USA). Prior to the assessment, participants performed a standardised warm-up consisting of 3 min of unloaded cycling on a bicycle ergometer, after which they were positioned prone for testing. Muscle strength was evaluated at two angular velocities: 60°/s (to measure maximal torque) and 180°/s (to assess muscle power). To minimise familiarisation effects and ensure data quality, participants performed three familiarisation trials before each test speed, followed by a 10-second rest interval. A 60-second rest interval was allowed between the two test velocities. A total of 10 maximal repetitions were performed at each velocity. The highest peak torque value was recorded, normalised to body mass, and expressed as relative peak torque (Nm/kg).

Plantar pressure distribution

Dynamic plantar pressure distribution was assessed during walking using a pedobarographic platform (Medilogic Platform Basic, Schein & Meyer GmbH, Germany), following a standardised two-step protocol. To minimise measurement error and ensure reliability, participants performed one familiarisation trial prior to the actual assessment [16]. For regional analysis, the plantar surface was divided longitudinally into three anatomical regions: the forefoot (metatarsal region), midfoot, and rearfoot (calcaneal region). Peak pressure (PP) values (N/cm²) were recorded for each region. Elevated forefoot peak pressure and an increased forefoot-to-rearfoot (F/R) pressure ratio are established clinical indicators of ulceration risk in DPN [20, 21]; therefore, these parameters were calculated for each participant. Plantar pressure was assessed bilaterally; however, only the dominant foot was included in the statistical analysis, consistent with previous methodology [16].

Vibration perception threshold

VPT was measured using a biothesiometer (Vibrometer VPT®, Madras Engineering Service, India). Prior to the assessment, participants were familiarised with the vibration sensation through application of the probe to the distal surface of the palm. Measurements were obtained from both the plantar and dorsal surfaces of the foot.

For the plantar region, VPT was assessed at six sites: the hallux, the heads of the 1st, 3rd, and 5th metatarsals, the instep, and the heel. The mean of these six measurements was used for analysis [22]. For the dorsal region, four locations were assessed: the medial and lateral malleoli, the dorsum of the foot, and the first dorsal web space; the mean of these four measurements was calculated.

During the procedure, the probe was applied perpendicularly to each test site with constant pressure. The voltage was increased gradually from 0 V at a rate of 1 V/s. The threshold at which the participant first perceived the vibration was recorded as the VPT value in Volts (V) [23]. Each site was tested three times, with a 10-second rest interval between consecutive measurements, and the mean value was recorded for analysis.

Interventions

The study comprised three intervention arms: a control group (CG), an aerobic exercise group (AEG), and a combined aerobic and foot and ankle exercise group (AE+FAEG). Participants in the CG continued their routine pharmacological treatment and diabetes management under the supervision of their physicians, without engaging in any structured exercise programme. All exercise interventions were administered over a 12-week period at a frequency of three sessions per week.

Custom foot orthoses

To standardize the walking environment and mitigate potential confounding variables related to footwear, custom-made foot orthoses were fabricated for all participants across all three groups. The orthoses were developed using computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technology (PadCad, Germany), with designs specifically tailored to each participant’s individual plantar pressure distribution. The orthoses were fabricated using a computer numerical control (CNC) milling machine and made of ethylene-vinyl acetate (EVA) with a Shore A hardness of 30, selected for its cushioning properties and durability. All participants were instructed to wear the orthoses with their athletic footwear throughout the 24-week study period, comprising 12 weeks of intervention followed by a 12-week follow-up.

Aerobic Exercise Group (AEG)

The AEG underwent supervised treadmill training three times per week over 12 weeks, comprising 36 sessions in total. Exercise intensity was individualised using the Karvonen formula, calculated based on each participant’s submaximal treadmill exercise test results obtained via the Modified Bruce Protocol, to determine the target heart rate [24, 25]. Training intensity commenced at 50% of the heart rate reserve (HRR) and was progressively increased to 70% HRR based on individual adaptation and tolerance [26]. Each session consisted of a 5-minute low-intensity warm-up, followed by a target heart rate walking phase beginning at 20 min and progressively increased according to each participant’s cardiovascular response (Table 1), and concluded with a 5-minute cool-down comprising slow walking and stretching. All sessions were supervised by a physiotherapist to ensure adherence to the prescribed intensity and to monitor for adverse events, including hypoglycaemia, dizziness, or excessive fatigue. Standard safety precautions, including the use of treadmill safety clips and continuous heart rate monitoring, were consistently maintained throughout the intervention period.

Aerobic Exercise + Foot and Ankle Exercise Group (AE+FAEG)

Participants in the AE+FAEG completed the identical supervised treadmill training protocol as the AEG. Immediately following each treadmill session, participants engaged in a structured therapeutic foot and ankle exercise programme (see Supplement 1 for the complete exercise list). The exercise programme incorporated three key components: flexibility training, including stretching of the posterior compartment muscles (gastrocnemius and soleus) and the plantar fascia; progressive strength training, comprising toe curls, marble pickups, and resistance band exercises; with particular emphasis on intrinsic foot muscle strengthening to address the muscle imbalances characteristic of DPN; and neuromuscular control training, involving sensory-based balance and proprioceptive exercises to enhance lower extremity stability [27]. The intensity and complexity of all exercises were individually progressed based on each participant’s performance, tolerance, and functional improvement throughout the 12-week intervention period.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables, with continuous data presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables expressed as frequencies and percentages. 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for the mean of each outcome at each timepoint and group were calculated using the t-distribution method (Mean ± t (0.975, n–1) × (SD / √n)), where n represents the number of non-missing observations. Data normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. Baseline comparability between the three groups (CG, AEG, and AE+FAEG) was evaluated using one-way analysis of variance ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test for normally distributed data, and the Kruskal–Wallis test with Mann–Whitney U pairwise comparisons for non-normally distributed data.

To evaluate primary and secondary outcomes across the three time points (baseline, Week 12, and Week 24), a two-way repeated measures ANOVA (Group × Time) was performed. Where the assumption of sphericity was violated, degrees of freedom were corrected using the Greenhouse–Geisser adjustment. In the presence of significant interactions or main effects, Tukey’s HSD post-hoc tests were applied for between-group comparisons, while Bonferroni-corrected paired t-tests were used for within-group temporal comparisons. For between-group comparisons, mean differences with corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated and presented in all summary tables. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared (η²p).

All analyses were conducted using R (v.4.3.1) and GraphPad Prism (v.9.5.0) based on complete-case data (n = 74). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. To ensure the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were performed using three ANCOVA models: (i) unadjusted (group only), (ii) adjusted for diabetes duration, and (iii) adjusted for both diabetes duration and baseline outcome values. The group effect was reported for each model using Type II sums of squares.

Ankle muscular strength and stiffness

At baseline, significant between-group differences were observed for PF strength at 180°/s (p = 0.015) and DF strength at 180°/s (p = 0.029). A statistically significant Group × Time interaction was observed for passive ankle stiffness (F (3.92, 139.06) = 18.85, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.35). Within-group analyses revealed a progressive increase in stiffness in the CG at the immediate post-intervention assessment (p = 0.030). In contrast, both exercise groups demonstrated significant reductions in stiffness at the 24-week follow-up compared to baseline (AEG: p = 0.001; AE+FAEG: p < 0.001). Notably, only the AE+FAEG achieved a significant reduction as early as the immediate post-intervention phase (p < 0.001). Between-group comparisons revealed that the AE+FAEG demonstrated significantly lower stiffness values compared with the CG at both post-intervention (p = 0.025) and 24-week follow-up (p = 0.038).

Significant Group × Time interactions were also observed for all isokinetic strength variables: PF strength at 60°/s (F(3.71, 131.85) = 30.36, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.46), DF strength at 60°/s (F(3.37, 119.69) = 32.71, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.48), PF strength at 180°/s (F(3.96, 140.57) = 12.27, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.26), and DF strength at 180°/s (F(3.86, 136.98) = 8.36, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.19). The CG demonstrated significant declines in both PF and DF strength over the 24-week period (all p < 0.05). The AEG preserved baseline strength levels with no significant within-group decline, whereas the AE+FAEG demonstrated significant improvements in PF and DF strength at 60°/s from post-intervention onwards (all p < 0.05). For PF strength at both angular velocities (60°/s and 180°/s), both exercise groups achieved significantly higher values than the CG at Weeks 12 and 24 (all p < 0.05), with no significant difference between the two exercise groups (all p > 0.05). For DF strength at 60°/s, both exercise groups were significantly stronger than the CG at both post-intervention and 24-week follow-up (AEG vs. CG: p = 0.020; AE+FAEG vs. CG: p < 0.001), with no significant difference between exercise groups (p = 0.090). For DF strength at 180°/s, only the AE+FAEG demonstrated significantly higher values than the CG at all timepoints (Post: p = 0.003; 24 wk: p = 0.001), while the AEG did not differ significantly from the CG at either assessment point. Glycaemic control

Significant Group × Time interactions were observed for both fasting plasma glucose (FPG) (F (3.64, 129.26) = 4.12, p = 0.005, η²p = 0.10) and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) (F (3.72, 132.23) = 10.98, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.24). All metabolic parameters were comparable across the three groups at baseline (p > 0.05).

Within-group analyses revealed that FPG decreased significantly only in the AE+FAEG from the immediate post-intervention phase, with reductions maintained at the 24-week follow-up (both p < 0.05). In the AEG, a significant reduction in FPG was observed at the 24-week follow-up compared to both baseline and the immediate post-intervention assessment (both p < 0.05). No significant within-group changes in FPG were observed in the CG.

HbA1c levels decreased significantly in both exercise groups from baseline to post-intervention and remained significantly lower at the 24-week follow-up (all p < 0.001). No significant changes in HbA1c were observed in the CG. Detailed descriptive and inferential statistics for all metabolic outcomes are presented in Table 4.

Plantar pressure distribution and vibration perception threshold

At baseline, significant between-group differences were observed for rearfoot pressure (p < 0.001), fore/rear pressure ratio (p < 0.001), dorsal vibration threshold (p = 0.025), and plantar vibration threshold (p = 0.026). Significant Group × Time interactions were observed for forefoot peak pressure (F(3.54, 125.53) = 12.44, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.26), the F/R pressure ratio (F(3.48, 123.72) = 16.22, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.31), dorsal VPT (F(2.71, 96.33) = 36.63, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.51), and plantar VPT (F(2.81, 99.68) = 38.66, p < 0.001, η²p = 0.52).

Within-group analyses demonstrated significant reductions in forefoot peak pressure across all three groups over the 24-week period (all p < 0.05). However, between-group comparisons revealed no statistically significant differences at any time point (p > 0.05).

No significant Group × Time interaction was detected for rearfoot pressure (p = 0.442). Between-group differences in rearfoot pressure were statistically significant at all timepoints (all p < 0.001); however, these differences reflect pre-existing baseline imbalances rather than differential treatment effects. This interpretation was further supported by the sensitivity analysis, in which between-group differences were no longer significant after adjusting for baseline values (Additional File 1).

Significant longitudinal reductions in the F/R ratio were observed in both exercise groups from baseline to post-intervention and through the 24-week follow-up (all p < 0.05). Notably, the AE+FAEG exhibited significantly higher F/R ratios compared to the CG at baseline (p < 0.001); however, this disparity progressively diminished over time, although it remained statistically significant at both post-intervention (p = 0.026) and 24-week assessments (p = 0.024).

Although significant within-group reductions in dorsal and plantar VPT were observed in both exercise groups (all p < 0.05), the overall between-group effect did not reach statistical significance for either dorsal VPT (p = 0.284) or plantar VPT (p = 0.162). No significant changes in dorsal VPT were observed in the CG throughout the study period. For plantar VPT, a statistically significant increase was observed in the CG at the 24-week follow-up (p < 0.05), suggesting a trend toward progressive sensory decline, though the clinical significance of this change warrants cautious interpretation given the modest magnitude of increase. In contrast, both exercise groups demonstrated significant threshold reductions at Weeks 12 and 24 (all p < 0.05). Detailed descriptive and inferential statistics for all plantar pressure distribution and vibration perception threshold outcomes are presented in Table 5.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that the integration of targeted foot and ankle exercises into a supervised treadmill-based aerobic exercise programme produces superior improvements in ankle stiffness and muscle strength compared with aerobic training alone. Regarding plantar pressure distribution, although significant within-group reductions in forefoot peak pressure were observed across all groups, between-group differences in the forefoot-to-rearfoot pressure ratio should be interpreted in light of baseline disparities, as AE+FAEG maintained higher values than CG across all time points. The significant Group × Time interaction indicates a distinct pattern of change between groups. To our knowledge, this is the first randomised controlled trial to examine the combined effects of treadmill-based aerobic training and targeted foot and ankle exercises on ankle stiffness as a primary outcome in this population.

Aerobic exercise is well established to confer beneficial effects on metabolic control and muscle function in individuals with T2D [28–30]. Consistent with this, aerobic training in the present study was associated with preserved muscle strength and significant within-group improvements in glycaemic parameters. Notably, the addition of foot and ankle-specific exercises was accompanied by superior improvements in ankle stiffness and muscle strength, while also demonstrating a greater absolute reduction in the fore/rear pressure ratio compared to the control group. While improvements in plantar pressure distribution were also observed, these were consistent across all intervention groups, reflecting the shared contribution of orthotic support.

Increased ankle stiffness is a clinically relevant concern, as it restricts ankle range of motion and elevates peak plantar pressures, thereby increasing the risk of ulceration [31]. In the present study, the CG demonstrated a significant increase in ankle stiffness at post-intervention (p = 0.030), whereas at post-intervention only the AE+FAEG demonstrated a significant reduction, and at the 24-week follow-up significant reductions were observed in both exercise groups. The significant Group × Time interaction for ankle stiffness, together with the reduction from 0.52 to 0.42 Nm/rad observed in the AE+FAEG, supports the therapeutic contribution of targeted distal training. These findings suggest that integrating foot and ankle-specific exercises into an aerobic training regimen may more effectively attenuate the deterioration of tendon-muscle viscoelasticity associated with T2D [5, 32]. This interpretation is consistent with previous evidence indicating that targeted interventions can mitigate pathological loading by reducing ankle stiffness and promoting a more normalised plantar pressure distribution [33–35]. However, in the present study, all participants were provided with custom-made orthoses as part of standard ethical care for individuals with high-risk DPN. While this was essential for patient safety, the mechanical effect of the orthoses likely overshadowed any additional incremental benefits of the exercise intervention on plantar pressure redistribution.

Although no significant change in ankle stiffness was observed in the AEG at post-intervention, a significant reduction emerged at the 24-week follow-up. This pattern may reflect a delayed adaptive response in tendon-muscle viscoelasticity when aerobic exercise is performed in isolation. Given that the aerobic component was implemented as treadmill walking, the late improvement may also be partially attributable to the gradual adoption of a sustained daily walking habit, which could have generated cumulative mechanical loading over time, thereby contributing to progressive improvements in biomechanical properties.

Three distinct trajectories in ankle muscle strength were observed across the study groups following the intervention. The CG exhibited a progressive decline in strength, while the AEG maintained baseline levels, suggesting that aerobic exercise may exert a protective effect on distal muscle function and decelerate neuromuscular deterioration.

The superior outcomes observed in the AE+FAEG are consistent with previous evidence suggesting that multimodal interventions may be more effective than single-component aerobic training alone [18, 36]. With regard to muscle strength, both exercise groups demonstrated significantly higher dorsiflexion strength at 60°/s compared with the CG at the 24-week follow-up, with the AE+FAEG showing a particularly pronounced difference relative to the more modest, yet significant, difference observed in the AEG. Although the difference between the two exercise groups did not reach statistical significance, this pattern may suggest a potential additional benefit of localised resistance training, which warrants further investigation. The absence of significant between-group differences for certain parameters may reflect inter-individual variability in tendinous adaptation, as prolonged hyperglycaemia and extensive collagen cross-linking can attenuate the adaptive response to exercise in some individuals [37]. Although targeted foot and ankle training may help alleviate plantar tissue loading by reducing stiffness, longer-term studies are needed to clarify its role in ulcer prevention.

In DPN, sensory deficits, restricted joint range of motion, and intrinsic muscle imbalances commonly contribute to a pathological shift in loading from the rearfoot toward the forefoot, thereby elevating peak plantar pressures [5]. In the present study, significant longitudinal reductions in both the F/R ratio and forefoot peak pressure were observed across all groups. Although the AE+FAEG demonstrated the greatest absolute reduction in the F/R ratio (Δ = −0.39 vs. Δ = −0.12 in CG), post-intervention comparisons revealed that the AE+FAEG continued to exhibit a significantly higher F/R ratio than the CG at both post-intervention and 24-week follow-up. This persistent difference should be interpreted in light of the substantially elevated baseline F/R ratio in the AE+FAEG, which entered the study with a greater degree of forefoot loading imbalance compared to the CG. The significant Group × Time interaction suggests a distinct pattern of change across groups rather than indicating a simple superiority of the combined intervention, and between-group comparisons should be interpreted in light of these baseline disparities. Although between-group differences in forefoot peak pressure were not significant in the unadjusted analyses, sensitivity analyses indicated that group differences emerged after adjustment for baseline values, reflecting the influence of pre-existing between-group variability on the primary analysis. In the CG, reductions in forefoot peak pressure were observed, accompanied by a decline in the F/R ratio. However, this change appears to be primarily driven by decreased forefoot loading, as rearfoot pressure remained unchanged throughout the follow-up period, suggesting limited evidence of active redistribution toward the rearfoot. Given that all participants received custom-made foot orthoses, this finding indicates that orthotic intervention may contribute to partial normalization of plantar loading in high-risk individuals. However, as no group received exercise without orthoses, the independent contribution of orthotic support cannot be formally isolated within the present design.

The progressive strength loss and concurrent deterioration in plantar vibration perception observed in the CG are consistent with the motor unit depletion, muscle atrophy, and impaired torque generation characteristic of DPN [38, 39]. In contrast, the superior gains in the AE+FAEG suggest that incorporating targeted ankle resistance exercises is necessary to surpass the functional threshold required for meaningful clinical improvement. The strength gains observed at low angular velocities likely reflect type I muscle fibre hypertrophy and neural adaptations, both of which are critical for balance and gait stability. Collectively, these findings support the notion that localised, targeted loading is more effective than aerobic exercise alone in counteracting muscle deterioration and reversing strength deficits in individuals with T2D.

Although significant within-group reductions in both dorsal and plantar VPT were observed in both exercise groups, between-group differences did not reach statistical significance for either measure, and these findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. The large Group × Time interaction effect sizes (η²p = 0.51–0.52) reflect divergent trajectories of change across groups rather than confirmed between-group superiority. This pattern may reflect exercise-facilitated neural plasticity and enhanced endoneurial blood flow, thereby attenuating baseline sensory deficits. While aerobic exercise alone may stabilise muscle strength in the short term, the present findings suggest that targeted regional resistance training is necessary to achieve clinically meaningful gains. This is consistent with evidence demonstrating that functional and progressive resistance protocols preserve muscle mass and delay the progression of neuropathic symptoms [3, 18, 36, 40].

These findings are consistent with evidence that exercise enhances nerve conduction velocity and promotes axonal regeneration through upregulation of neurotrophic factors, including nerve growth factor (NGF) and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) [37, 38]. The comparable improvements observed across exercise groups should be interpreted in light of the the higher baseline VPT values in the exercise groups, which may have influenced the detection of between-group differences in the unadjusted analyses.

Both exercise groups demonstrated significant reductions in fasting plasma glucose and HbA1c levels, consistent with previous evidence indicating that aerobic or combined exercise interventions of 8–16 weeks duration improve glycaemic control [12, 30, 41, 42]. Although no statistically significant between-group differences were observed in the primary analysis, the sensitivity analyses indicated that group differences emerged after adjustment for baseline values, reflecting the influence of pre-existing between-group variability on the primary analysis rather than confirming the presence of a true intervention effect. This absence of divergence may reflect inter-individual variability in pharmacological management, dietary habits, and baseline insulin sensitivity. Furthermore, although 24 weeks represents a substantial intervention period, it may be insufficient to produce a clinically meaningful separation between exercise modalities. Given the complexity of metabolic regulation in T2D, larger and more rigorously controlled trials may be necessary to detect subtle between-group differences in glycaemic outcomes.

A significant baseline difference in diabetes duration was observed between groups, with longer disease duration in the exercise groups. Given that disease duration is closely associated with metabolic and neuromuscular impairment, this could represent a potential confounding factor. However, the results of the ANCOVA sensitivity analysis demonstrated that all primary findings - particularly those related to ankle stiffness and muscle strength - remained robust after adjustment for diabetes duration and baseline values. Importantly, no outcome lost statistical significance following adjustment, indicating that diabetes duration did not confound the observed intervention effects. Notably, for several outcomes, statistical significance emerged only after adjustment, reflecting the influence of pre-existing between-group variability on the unadjusted analyses rather than confirming the presence of true intervention effects. Taken together, these findings strengthen the internal validity of the study and support the conclusion that the observed improvements are attributable to the intervention rather than baseline imbalances.

The present findings carry direct clinical implications for the rehabilitation management of individuals with T2D and DPN. The superior outcomes observed in the AE+FAEG suggest that physiotherapists and clinicians should consider incorporating structured foot and ankle exercises alongside supervised aerobic training as a standard component of DPN rehabilitation programmes. Given the progressive nature of DPN and its associated biomechanical consequences, early implementation of such combined interventions may be particularly beneficial in delaying the onset of diabetic foot complications. Furthermore, the use of custom foot orthoses as an adjunct to exercise may provide additional benefits in redistributing plantar loading, thereby reducing ulceration risk in high-risk individuals.

The present findings suggest that while custom foot orthoses serve as an essential passive intervention for modifying plantar loading patterns, they primarily target mechanical pressure redistribution rather than the underlying pathophysiological processes of DPN, such as neural degeneration and progressive tissue stiffening. These observations indicate that orthotic support alone may be insufficient to halt the progression of functional decline. Therefore, additional active interventions, such as the exercise programme evaluated in this study, should be considered to address the multifactorial nature of the condition, specifically targeting neuromuscular and biomechanical adaptations that passive devices cannot reach. In contrast, the integration of targeted foot and ankle exercises with aerobic training resulted in more pronounced improvements in ankle stiffness and muscle strength compared to aerobic training alone. While total peak plantar pressures were primarily managed by orthotic support across all groups, the combined intervention was associated with differences in dynamic loading patterns, as reflected by the forefoot-to-rearfoot pressure ratio, although these findings should be interpreted in light of baseline disparities.

Taken together, these findings underscore the importance of a multimodal rehabilitation approach in the clinical management of DPN, wherein aerobic training, targeted resistance exercises, and orthotic support each contribute distinct and complementary therapeutic benefits. Future research should investigate the long-term sustainability of these improvements, the optimal exercise dosage, and the potential of this combined approach in preventing diabetic foot ulceration in high-risk populations.

Limitations

Despite the controlled nature of the study, certain limitations must be acknowledged. First, there was no direct control over the participants’ pharmacological management, dietary habits, or spontaneous daily physical activity, which may have introduced confounding variables. Second, the significantly shorter duration of diabetes in the control group compared to the exercise intervention groups is a notable limitation, as longer disease duration is intrinsically linked to more advanced metabolic and neuromuscular impairment. Additionally, baseline imbalances may have influenced the interpretation of between-group outcomes for the related parameters. However, sensitivity analyses adjusting for diabetes duration and baseline outcome values demonstrated that this imbalance did not materially affect the study findings, and no between-group effect was explained by diabetes duration. Third, although a formal clinical staging system was not applied, VPT > 25 V was used as an objective inclusion criterion corresponding to established thresholds for advanced neuropathy and elevated ulceration risk. Nevertheless, the absence of further stratification by neuropathy severity represents a limitation, as varying degrees of neuronal damage within this threshold may differentially influence the capacity for neuromuscular adaptation and functional recovery. This is further evidenced by the withdrawal of two participants due to dialysis initiation during the study period, highlighting the heterogeneity of systemic complication burden even within a narrowly defined neuropathy threshold. These factors may have influenced the comparative outcomes and potentially limited the emergence of more distinct between-group differences. Fourth, all unilateral outcome measures were assessed on the dominant lower extremity, consistent with previously established methodology in this field. While diabetic peripheral neuropathy typically presents as a symmetrical, bilateral condition, potential side-to-side asymmetries were not accounted for, which may influence the generalisability of the biomechanical findings. Fifth, the use of custom foot orthoses across all groups as part of standard ethical care may have independently influenced plantar pressure and biomechanical outcomes. This may have attenuated the ability to detect exercise-specific effects on plantar pressure outcomes, as the mechanical benefits of orthotic support may have been present across all groups simultaneously. Accordingly, the observed findings may partly reflect the combined influence of orthotic support and exercise rather than the isolated effects of exercise alone.

Conclusion

In individuals with T2D and DPN, the integration of targeted foot and ankle exercises into a supervised aerobic training programme resulted in significant improvements in ankle stiffness and muscle strength. While changes in plantar loading parameters were observed across all groups, differences in the forefoot-to-rearfoot pressure ratio suggest distinct patterns of change over time rather than a uniform intervention effect. Overall, the results support a beneficial role of the combined intervention for ankle stiffness and muscle strength outcomes, although findings related to plantar pressure distribution and glycaemic parameters should be interpreted with consideration of baseline differences and the uniform use of orthotic support across groups. The combination of aerobic training, targeted exercise, and orthotic support appears to offer a comprehensive and clinically relevant approach for improving biomechanical and functional outcomes in individuals with DPN. Future studies should further explore the long-term effects of this multimodal strategy and its role in preventing diabetic foot complications.
