The Validity and Reliability Study of the Turkish Version of Cognitive Complaints in Bipolar Disorder Rating Assessment
Authors: Ezgi Ince Guliyev, Gulcan Karaca Ozkutuk, Sema Cagal, Ece Ozlem Ozturk, Tugay Elik, Nupelda Akdas, Cem Horasan, Sibel Cakır, Nese Direk, Omer Aydemir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThe Cognitive Complaints Rating Scale in Bipolar Disorder (COBRA) is a brief self‐report measure designed to capture Subjective Cognitive Complaints (SCC) experienced by patients with Bipolar Disorder (BD). This study aimed to translate the 16‐item COBRA into Turkish (COBRA‐TR) and comprehensively assess its psychometric properties.MethodsWe recruited 160 participants, consisting of 80 BD patients in remission and 80 controls. The psychometric evaluation included confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), calculation of internal consistency, and examination of divergent and convergent validity through Pearson's correlations with related scales. Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSSv29 and the Jamovi software of R Statistical Package.ResultsThe COBRA‐TR showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach α = 0.898) and robust convergent validity, strongly correlating with the Cognitive Failures Questionnaire (r = 0.784; p < 0.001). It demonstrated moderate‐to‐high correlations with cognitive (r = 0.574; p < 0.001) and global functioning (r = 0.624; p < 0.001), as assessed by the Functioning Assessment Short Test. CFA provided strong support for a unidimensional factor structure. Furthermore, the COBRA‐TR successfully differentiated patients from controls. Clinical variables minimally influenced the scale, though it showed weak correlations with subclinical depressive symptoms (r = 0.325; p = 0.003) and working memory performance (r = −0.250; p = 0.002).ConclusionOur findings highlight that the COBRA‐TR is a highly reliable and valid tool, effectively capturing SCC among BD patients. Its brevity, ease of administration, and strong psychometric properties suggest its potential utility as a valuable instrument in both clinical practice and research settings.

Introduction

Cognitive impairment in Bipolar Disorder (BD) has become increasingly relevant, affecting patients not only during the acute phases of illness but also during remission (Bourne et al. 2013; Martínez‐Arán et al. 2004Martínez‐Arán et al. 2004; Miskowiak et al. 2018; Solé et al. 2017; Van Rheenen et al. 2020). These deficits, which emerge from the earliest stages of the illness (Bora and Pantelis 2015), persist throughout the lifespan and encompass multiple domains, including memory, attention, processing speed, and executive functioning (Bora et al. 2009). Evidence suggests that although symptomatic remission can be attained with existing treatment modalities, many patients continue to experience impairments in several functional domains, such as employment and social engagement (Fagiolini et al. 2005; Rosa et al. 2010; Sengupta and Jena 2022). Several longitudinal studies have demonstrated that cognitive impairment is a key predictor of daily functioning (Li et al. 2020; Martino et al. 2009; Tabarés‐Seisdedos et al. 2008; Van Rheenen et al. 2020) and quality of life (Cotrena et al. 2016; Mackala et al. 2014; Toyoshima et al. 2019) in patients with BD, ultimately contributing to long‐term disability. Therefore, it is crucial to assess cognitive functions from the early stages of the illness using appropriate measurement tools to inform clinical management and guide intervention strategies.

Although comprehensive neurocognitive batteries provide an objective measure of cognitive performance, their administration is usually costly, time‐consuming, and necessitates trained professionals. So, their use may often be limited in many clinical settings and not achievable for most patients. On the other hand, evaluating subjective cognitive complaints is a relatively easier method for assessing cognitive functioning and can possibly help to decide who to administer comprehensive neurocognitive tests (Miskowiak et al. 2018). In addition, subjective measures provide additional insights by incorporating patients' perspectives and informing clinicians about the areas where patients are experiencing difficulties. Several self‐report tools are available for measuring subjective cognitive dysfunction in patients with psychiatric disorders, such as the Cognitive Difficulties Scale (CDS) and the Cognitive Failures Questionnaire (CFQ) (Broadbent et al. 1982; McNair and Kahn 1983). However, these tools lack specificity for BD. The “Cognitive Complaints in Bipolar Disorder Rating Assessment (COBRA),” a 16‐item self‐report tool, was designed to address cognitive difficulties specifically experienced by patients with BD, mostly related to areas of memory and executive functions (Rosa et al. 2013). COBRA scores have been associated with objective measures of memory and executive functioning (Rosa et al. 2013), though some studies report inconsistent findings (Lima et al. 2018). The International Society for Bipolar Disorders (ISBD) has recommended COBRA as a feasible tool for screening subjective cognitive impairment in BD (Miskowiak et al. 2017, 2018).

The COBRA is available in Spanish, English, Japanese, Chinese, Danish, Portuguese, Persian, and several regional languages (Jensen et al. 2015; Lima et al. 2018; Momeni et al. 2023; Rosa et al. 2013; Toyoshima et al. 2017; Xiao et al. 2015; Yoldi‐Negrete et al. 2018). However, there is currently no validated self‐report tool for assessing subjective cognitive dysfunction in BD in Turkish.

The primary aim of this study was to assess the reliability and validity of the Turkish version of COBRA to be used in BD patients in Türkiye. Additionally, we aimed to investigate the relationship between subjective cognitive complaints and psychiatric complaints, functioning, and other clinical parameters. Lastly, we sought to evaluate how subjective cognitive complaints, as measured by the Turkish version of COBRA, correlate with objective neurocognitive assessments.

Methods

Participants

This cross‐sectional study was conducted at the Mood Disorders Unit of the Department of Psychiatry of Istanbul University Faculty of Medicine and the Bipolar Disorder Unit of the Department of Psychiatry of Celal Bayar University Faculty of Medicine. Participants comprised 80 outpatients with Bipolar I Disorder and 80 age and gender‐matched healthy controls recruited from the general population within the same catchment area.

Patients with Bipolar I Disorder in remission for the minimum duration of 8 weeks, according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders fifth edition (DSM‐5) (American Psychiatric Association 2013), aged between 18 and 65 years, having at least 8 years of education with no history of electroconvulsive therapy in the past three months were included in the current study. Patients with a neurological disorder (dementia, epilepsy, etc.), an alcohol or substance use disorder, or an intellectual disability were excluded. All patients had a score of less than 8 on the Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) and a score of less than 7 on the 17‐item Hamilton Depression Scale (HDRS‐17). Healthy controls with no current or past psychiatric or cognitive disorder and a family history of chronic mental disorders such as schizophrenia or BD were included from the same catchment area. The Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Celal Bayar University Faculty of Medicine approved the study protocol (Date: 14/02/2024, No: 20.478.486/2265), and the work was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients and healthy controls gave written informed consent after a detailed explanation of the study, and anonymity was preserved.

Translation Process

The translation process of the Cognitive Complaints in Bipolar Disorder Rating Assessment (COBRA) included forward translation, back translation, and reconciliation of back‐translation with the original scale's developers. Firstly, the scale was translated from English to Turkish by two experts who are native speakers of Turkish and fluent in English using the parallel‐blind method. The two versions of the first translations were compared and merged into a single translated form. Then, the single merged form was back‐translated into English by a linguist to be approved by the scale's developers. The final version was shaped based on the developers' recommendations and then tested by experts and patients to evaluate its comprehensibility. All researchers approved the final version before collecting data for the study.

Tools

In addition to the COBRA, data collection tools comprised the Functioning Assessment Short Test (FAST), The Screen for Cognitive Impairment in Psychiatry (SCIP), and the Cognitive Failures Questionnaire (CFQ). We also used YMRS and HDRS‐17 to measure symptom severity and confirm remission. A data collection form developed by the authors was also used to collect socio‐demographic and clinical information from participants.

The Cognitive Complaints in Bipolar Disorder Rating Assessment (COBRA)

COBRA is a self‐report rating scale developed by Rosa et al. that measures the subjective cognitive complaints experienced by individuals who are diagnosed with BD (Rosa et al. 2013). It is a 4‐point, Likert‐type rating scale consisting of 16 items that are graded on 0–3 (0 = never, 1 = sometimes, 2 = often, 3 = always). The total score ranges between 0–48 points. A higher score indicates more severe subjective cognitive complaints. The cognitive domains of the final version of the COBRA are executive functioning, processing speed, working memory, verbal learning and memory, attention/concentration, and mental tracking. COBRA has very high internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha = 0.913) with a single‐factor solution in structural validity.

The Functioning Assessment Short Test (FAST)

FAST, developed by Rosa et al., is a 4‐point Likert‐type scale with 24 items. Administered by an interviewer, it assesses functional outcomes in people diagnosed with BD over the following six domains: autonomy, occupation, cognition, financial skills, interpersonal relationships, and leisurely activities (Rosa et al. 2007). Total score ranges between 0 and 72, with higher scores indicating worse functioning. The validity and reliability study of its Turkish version was conducted by Aydemir and Uykur (Aydemir and Uykur 2012).

The Screen for Cognitive Impairment in Psychiatry (SCIP)

SCIP is a short cognitive impairment screening tool developed to be used in psychiatric patients (Purdon 2005) and included among the International Society for Bipolar Disorders (ISBD) recommendations for cognitive assessment (Miskowiak et al. 2018). SCIP evaluated cognition over the following domains: Verbal Learning Test—Immediate (VLT‐I), Working Memory Test (WMT), Verbal Fluency Test (VFT), Verbal Learning Test—Delayed (VLT‐D), and Processing Speed Test (PST). In the instruction book, two alternatives are raised to calculate its score. The first one is to direct the summation of test scores to obtain a total score, and the second one involves a calculation of z‐scores according to norm data for that population. It was translated into Turkish after permission was obtained from its author. We did not calculate the z‐scores because the normative study was not performed in the Turkish population. Therefore, we used the first solution to calculate the score.

The Cognitive Failures Questionnaire (CFQ)

CFQ, originally developed by Broadbent et al., is a 25‐item self‐rating instrument that subjectively assesses cognitive functions on a 4‐point Likert‐type scale (Broadbent et al. 1982). The total score ranges between 0 and 80. Turkish adaptation study was carried out by Yapıcı‐Eser et al. (Yapıcı Eser et al. 2020).

Hamilton Depression Rating Scale 17 (HDRS‐17)

For assessing depressive symptoms, a 17‐item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS‐17), a clinician‐rated instrument was used. Scoring ranges from 0 to 52, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. The HDRS‐17 has demonstrated good inter‐rater reliability and internal consistency in both clinical and research settings (Hamilton 1960). The reliability and validity study for the Turkish version was performed by Akdemir et al. (Akdemir et al. 2001).

Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS)

Manic symptoms were assessed using the Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS), an 11‐item clinician‐rated scale designed to evaluate the severity of manic symptoms. Total scores range from 0 to 60, and higher scores reflect a greater symptom severity. The YMRS is widely used and has demonstrated robust psychometric properties across clinical populations (Young et al. 2000). The reliability and validity study for the Turkish version was performed by Karadag et al. (Karadağ et al. 2001).

The Turkish version was validated by Karadağ et al. (2001).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 29 (IBM Corporation, Chicago, IL, USA), and the Jamovi software of R Statistical Package. Descriptive statistics were used to evaluate the sociodemographic and clinical data of study groups. Cronbach's alpha coefficient was calculated to assess the reliability of COBRA in the internal consistency analyses. Correlations between COBRA and other scales, particularly with CFQ and FAST to test its concurrent validity, were computed via Pearson correlation. Since a single‐factor structure was obtained in the original study, we performed a Confirmatory Factor Analysis for the single factor instead of an Exploratory Factor Analysis. We calculated various fit indices to assess the goodness‐of‐fit of the CFA model. Fit indices such as the CFI (confirmatory fit index) and the TLI (Tucker‐Lewis index) are expected to range between 0 and 1, with values greater than 0.90 indicating a good fit. RMSEA (root mean square error of approximation) is interpreted as values of 0.01, 0.05, and 0.08, indicating excellent, good, and mediocre fit, respectively. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses, and a Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons.

Reliability and Validity Analyses

The Cronbach's alpha coefficient was 0.898 for the total scale, indicating high reliability. Item‐total correlations ranged between 0.363 and 0.679. Excluding any item did not increase Cronbach's alpha value. The Confirmatory Factor Analysis was performed to assess the construct validity of COBRA and showed that the single‐factor solution was a good fit, with CFI = 0.909, TLI = 0.896, and RMSEA = 0.07. The df and CMIN/df values required for the analysis were 104 and 1.76, respectively. Pearson correlation analysis of COBRA, performed to assess concurrent validity, resulted in a high positive correlation with CFQ (r = 0.784; p < 0.001) and a moderate positive correlation with FAST cognitive functioning subscale (r = 0.574; p < 0.001) in the total group. When dividing the participants according to their disease status, similar results were obtained in the BD group but not in HCs, where no association was observed between COBRA and FAST cognitive score after Bonferroni correction (Table 2). The total COBRA score was statistically significantly higher in the patient group compared to healthy controls (13.14 ± 7.97 vs. 10.64 ± 7.15; t = 2.089, p = 0.038), indicating the ability to discriminate between patient and healthy control groups.

Discussion

This study sought to assess the validity and reliability of the Turkish version of the COBRA scale (COBRA‐TR). The findings demonstrated excellent internal consistency and strong convergent validity with the Cognitive Failures Questionnaire (CFQ), as well as moderate correlations with the cognitive subdomain of the Functioning Assessment Short Test (FAST). Additionally, confirmatory factor analysis supported a one‐factor model, and the scale also effectively differentiated between patients and controls. The fact that COBRA‐TR was minimally affected by the clinical parameters, including sociodemographic variables, clinical course, and treatment choices, supported its divergent validity and suggested its usability in a wide range of patient groups with BD. Overall, the results suggest that the Turkish adaptation of the COBRA scale is both highly reliable and valid to be used in clinical settings with BD patients in Turkiye.

Our internal consistency tests yielded a Cronbach's alpha value of 0.898 for COBRA‐TR, indicating a fairly high reliability and consistent with previous studies reporting Cronbach's alpha values ranging from 0.870 to 0.913 (Jensen et al. 2015; Lima et al. 2018; Rosa et al. 2013; Toyoshima et al. 2017; Xiao et al. 2015; Yoldi‐Negrete et al. 2018). Results of the confirmatory factor analysis supported the single‐factor structure of the COBRA‐TR. This aligns with most previous validation studies (Rosa et al. 2013; Toyoshima et al. 2017; Xiao et al. 2015; Yoldi‐Negrete et al. 2018), including the original scale development study (Rosa et al. 2013). As expected, patients reported more severe cognitive complaints compared to healthy controls, which is in line with the literature (Jensen et al. 2015; Rosa et al. 2013; Toyoshima et al. 2017; Yoldi‐Negrete et al. 2018).

Previous works have yielded inconsistent results regarding the correlation between subjective cognitive complaints measured by COBRA and objective cognitive tests. In the original scale development study, Rosa et al. reported that the COBRA significantly correlated with executive function measured by Wisconsin Card Sorting Test perseverative errors, with verbal learning and memory measured by California Verbal Learning Test total list A, cued delayed recall, and Rey Figure for visual memory, and more strongly with working memory index only in the patient group (Rosa et al. 2013). Similarly, Jensen et al. utilized both an extensive neurocognitive battery and the SCIP, demonstrating a weak to moderate negative correlation between working memory and executive function domains of both objective measures and the COBRA (Jensen et al. 2015). A Japanese translation study of the COBRA identified a weak correlation between the total COBRA score and processing speed, as measured by the Trail Making Test‐A (Toyoshima et al. 2017). In contrast, while Chinese and Brazilian Portuguese translation studies did not find an overall association (Lima et al. 2018; Xiao et al. 2015), the Persian translation study found a strong negative correlation between COBRA scores and Mini‐Mental State Examination scores (Momeni et al. 2023). In the current study, no correlation was found between the COBRA‐TR score and objective cognitive scores, except for a mild negative correlation between the working memory subtest of the SCIP and the COBRA score, which lost significance when the study groups were analyzed separately. Although findings from COBRA validation studies suggest a weak relationship between working memory and executive functions, there is insufficient evidence in the literature to determine which specific cognitive domain corresponds to subjective cognitive complaints (Groenman et al. 2022).

Interestingly, a recent study with a limited sample size found that subjective cognitive complaints and objectively measured cognitive functions followed discrepant trajectories over a 6‐year period (Czepielewski et al. 2023). Indeed, weak or absent correlations between subjective complaints and objective cognitive performance have been frequently observed not only in patients with BD but also in various other psychiatric conditions, including depression, psychosis, and ADHD (Groenman et al. 2022), as well as in aging adults without any psychiatric diagnosis (Burmester et al. 2016). The underlying reasons have been investigated across several disorders. For instance, a study conducted among patients with BD categorized participants as either over‐reporters or under‐reporters based on their degree of mismatch between subjective complaints and objective measures (Miskowiak et al. 2016). This study concluded that patients with subclinical mood symptoms (both manic and depressive), those diagnosed with BD type 2, and male participants were more likely to over‐report cognitive impairment, whereas those with higher IQ scores tended toward under‐reporting. Employing a similar methodology, Serra‐Blasco et al. investigated this in major depressive disorder patients. They found that the concordance between the subjective complaints and objective measures was weaker in the subjects with residual mood symptoms (Serra‐Blasco et al. 2019). Additionally, higher IQ and better executive functioning appeared to contribute to underestimating cognitive problems. Certainly, other factors may also influence how people perceive their cognitive abilities, such as insight and metacognitive capacity, and these aspects merit further exploration. Nevertheless, subjective cognitive complaints represent an important dimension of cognitive processes, as they reflect patients' socio‐occupational functioning of patients both in the current study and in the broader literature on mood disorders (Demant et al. 2015; Jensen et al. 2015; Luo et al. 2020; Miskowiak et al. 2016; Ott et al. 2016; Vicent‐Gil et al. 2023) as well as their overall quality of life (Hill et al. 2017). Rosa et al. suggested that subjective cognitive complaints measured by the COBRA scale could serve as a proxy for psychosocial functioning (Rosa et al. 2013). Supporting this view, Czepielewski et al., in their longitudinal study, proposed that improvements in socio‐occupational functioning may influence subsequent reductions in cognitive complaints (Czepielewski et al. 2023). However, given the current level of evidence in the literature, it is not possible to draw definitive conclusions regarding the causal direction of the relationship between subjective cognitive complaints and functioning, highlighting the need for further investigation into this association.

In the current study, subclinical depressive symptoms severity was the only clinical parameter related to the COBRA. This finding aligns with the existing literature, where depressive symptoms consistently emerge as the most commonly correlated factor with subjective cognitive complaints, observed both in psychiatric patients and healthy aging adults (Burmester et al. 2016; Groenman et al. 2022; Hill et al. 2016). A similar pattern was reported in previous studies utilizing COBRA in remitted BD patients (Lima et al. 2018; Rosa et al. 2013; Toyoshima et al. 2017; Yoldi‐Negrete et al. 2018). However, several other clinical factors have occasionally been associated with higher COBRA scores, including subclinical manic symptoms (Lima et al. 2018; Yoldi‐Negrete et al. 2018), the total number of mood episodes (Lima et al. 2018; Rosa et al. 2013), number of hypomanic episodes (Rosa et al. 2013), number of depressive episodes (Lima et al. 2018), number of suicide attempts (Lima et al. 2018), treatment with antipsychotic medications (Yoldi‐Negrete et al. 2018), and a diagnosis of BD type II (Rosa et al. 2013; Yoldi‐Negrete et al. 2018). Similar to our results, Toyoshima et al. also did not find any significant relationship between clinical parameters and COBRA scores in their validation study conducted in a Japanese population (Toyoshima et al. 2017). Taken together, the available evidence suggests that COBRA scores are generally not consistently influenced by clinical course variables, except for subclinical depressive symptoms, which appear to have a clear impact on cognitive functioning.

A key strength of the current study is that, in addition to validity and reliability analyses, it also explored subjective cognitive complaints assessed by COBRA‐TR in relation to various clinically relevant parameters, including disease course, treatment, objective cognitive performance, symptom severity, and socio‐occupational functioning. This comprehensive approach provides a broader understanding of how subjective cognitive dysfunction interacts with key aspects of BD. However, despite these strengths, certain limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, SCIP was designed as a screening tool, although recommended by the ISBD Targeting Cognition Task Force for assessing cognition in BD patients (Miskowiak et al. 2018). A more comprehensive battery would give better insights into the nature of the relationship between subjective cognitive complaints and objective cognitive measures in the Turkish context. Second, our control group was slightly more educated than the patient group, which might create a bias. Third, we did not evaluate test‐retest reliability. Although previous studies reported high retest reliability, we could not provide information regarding that in Turkish patients. Finally, our sample was selected from specialized BD units. Therefore, it consisted of patients whose symptoms were well controlled and who received specialized care such as well‐structured psychoeducation sessions, and it may not be representative of BD patients in the general population in Türkiye.

In conclusion, the Turkish version of the COBRA, COBRA‐TR, is a brief and easily administered screening tool with high internal consistency, strong concurrent validity, and a robust one‐factor structure. These psychometric properties indicate that COBRA‐TR is highly reliable and valid to be used for assessing subjective cognitive complaints in Turkish‐speaking patients with BD. Furthermore, as COBRA‐TR scores demonstrated strong correlations with socio‐occupational functioning, the scale may provide valuable insights for clinicians into patients' functional status. Nevertheless, the association between subjective complaints and objective cognitive abilities remains complex, warranting further investigation.
