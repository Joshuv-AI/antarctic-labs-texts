Seeing both sides of the story: creating patient/provider persona dyads for use in mental health service co-design
Authors: Amanda Dominello, Sami Lu, Robyn Clay-Williams, Colleen Cheek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTailored methods are needed to co-design services with consumers, in particular priority groups who experience access and outcome inequalities, but these methods are not well reported in the academic literature. Personas offer a creative human-centred approach and participatory tool for involving consumers in healthcare co-design, but without the provider perspective they tell one side of the story. In the context of value-based healthcare, which encompasses both patient experiences and outcomes and provider experiences, we designed patient/provider persona dyads for use in workshops held to redesign care for people who attend Emergency Departments with mental health concerns.MethodsThe study was conducted in three steps. First, clinical scenarios were constructed from analysis of data from non-participant observations and interviews with mental health providers, patients and epistemic experts. Second, semi-structured interviews with four patients and six providers were completed to co-design the personas. Third, persona dyad cards were created to represent the themes from the interview analysis.ResultsThree overarching themes were identified through inductive analysis of participant interviews: Humanise, acknowledging the human behind the patient or provider; Contextualise, situating the patient and provider in the healthcare system; and Illuminate, revealing thoughts, feelings and goals of the personas. Patient participants preferred personas portray positive, strengths-based goals; provider participants emphasised the complexity of their roles, including systemic challenges and frustrations.ConclusionIt is anticipated that use of the persona dyads in co-design workshops will generate more realistic, empathetic and actionable design solutions. The method used and persona profile cards may be applied in future research and service redesign projects, particularly in contexts involving priority populations and complex care environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40900-026-00910-8.Keywords: Co-design, Consumer involvement, Emergency department, Equity, Lived experience, Mental health, Personas, Priority populations, PROGRESS-Plus, Value-based healthcare

Background

Individuals with lived experience of health conditions have a fundamental human right to be involved in designing policies, programs and services that impact their health and wellbeing [1]. Their involvement offers rich insights and experiential knowledge that are essential for creating inclusive, equitable, and responsive health systems [1, 2]. Within the healthcare system, universal solutions are often applied to unequal realities, privileging institutional voices over those who have lived and living experience, whom we will refer to as consumers [3]. Meaningful involvement of consumers requires recognition of the diversity and complexity of consumer voices. Principles such as dignity, equity, inclusivity, and shared decision-making underpin effective engagement, particularly for groups who experience social disadvantage and inequalities, henceforth referred to as priority populations [1, 4].

The World Health Organization has called for new participatory approaches to consumer involvement that are respectful, dignified and value lived experience as expertise equal to traditional forms of evidence [1]. Meaningful patient and public engagement enhances the relevance, acceptability and effectiveness of policies and programs, while fostering shared ownership and accountability [1]. Co-design is one participatory approach that brings together stakeholders to identify complex system challenges and collaboratively develop solutions [5]. In health, co-design draws on the experiential knowledge of consumers and healthcare providers to inform the design of person-centred services [4, 6]. Tailoring co-design methods to meet participant needs is essential for engaging priority groups, who often face disparities in healthcare access and outcomes, and for creating inclusive, representative and safe environments that support meaningful consumer participation [1].

Co-design methods should also meaningfully engage healthcare providers and incorporate their lived experiences. This aligns with the principles of value-based healthcare, an increasingly adopted approach in healthcare systems that prioritises health outcomes over service volume [7, 8]. Value-based healthcare aims to improve patient outcomes and experiences, enhance provider experience, and increase system efficiency and effectiveness [8–10]. By focusing on the needs and experiences of both consumers and providers, co-design can foster shared goals and generate tailored solutions.

Personas, which are hypothetical and realistic representations of individuals [11], are one method used as a creative, human-centred approach to facilitate empathetic and reflective analysis of needs and design of responsive actions [12]. Using personas and scenarios as a participatory tool in co-design can help reduce power imbalances by distancing participants from their personal experiences and professional roles [13]. Consumer personas provide a medium through which consumers can drive open discussions, establishing the suitability of personas as a strengths-based co-design tool for priority populations [12]. Consumer personas can also be effective for healthcare providers, prompting them to consider and empathise with the diverse experiences of consumers. These strategies help to reduce reliance on assumptions and support more balanced and consumer-focussed outcomes [13], suggesting provider personas may also have value.

We aimed to design persona dyads to represent both consumer and healthcare provider perspectives for use in co-design workshops. We used a clinical scenario presented from two perspectives (Emergency Department (ED) patient and provider) because dyads offer a shared understanding of both patient and provider experiences, priorities and values. The personas were created to highlight the emotions, motivations and goals of consumers and healthcare providers, and to situate their experience in the ED. In this study, patient/provider persona dyads are positioned as a mechanism for supporting value-based healthcare by enabling shared understanding of patient experience, provider experiences and system constraints that shape care delivery. By making these elements visible, the personas are intended to support co-design discussions that are more likely to generate feasible, sustainable and experience-informed service improvements [14].

There is wide variation in how personas are created and by whom, as well as different visuals and various formats to present personas [12]. Participatory tools, such as personas, are not well documented and are published more often in grey than academic literature [15]. Our description of the method to create persona dyads as a participatory tool to involve both consumers and providers in healthcare co-design addresses this gap in the literature and responds to the WHO call for new participatory approaches.

Methods

This study is a component of the MyED project, a five-year study that aimed to improve ED care for five marginalised cohorts living in a health district in NSW, Australia [16]. The district is home to over 1 million people and is culturally and socioeconomically diverse [17]. MyED comprises three stages. In MyED Stage 1, the experience of ED care was explored in depth through non-participant observations of the ED [18] and interviews with consumers, providers and epistemic experts to understand and identify areas of common concern, henceforth referred to as touchpoints. In MyED Stage 2, strategies to improve ED care, experience and system performance will be developed in co-design workshops with consumers and ED providers. In MyED Stage 3, selected strategies will be implemented in EDs in the project health district.

This study involved one of the MyED study cohorts: adults who attend ED with mental health concerns, henceforth referred to as patients, and the ED providers who care for them. In Australia, the mental health system is failing to meet the needs of people experiencing mental health crises, as reflected in frequent ED presentations during crises, long wait times, and police involvement [19]. The overstimulating ED environment and delays in care contribute to poor patient outcomes, including increased agitation, use of restrictive interventions and the patient leaving the ED prior to completion of their care [20].

The study was conducted in three steps: clinical scenario construction; persona development interviews, and persona creation. The persona dyads designed in this study will be used in MyED Stage 2 co-design sessions in lieu of filming participant interviews, a practice we considered had moral and ethical implications that we wished to avoid [21]. We report on the three persona design steps in this paper using the terms personas and persona dyads. To improve readability and comprehension, we replaced the term ‘persona’ with ‘character’ in study participant interviews and resources [22]. We used the term ‘patient’ instead of ‘consumer’ to reflect the perspective of the study participants and type of dyad character developed.

Step 1: clinical scenario construction

Analysis of data from non-participant observations [18] and interviews with mental health providers (ED staff (n = 44)), patients presenting to ED with mental health crises (n = 29) and epistemic experts (academic/clinician (n = 16)) (unpublished results), revealed two touchpoints for the care of this patient group who attend EDs:

Touchpoint 1 - Earlier review and inhouse process improvements to support patients in the ED, for example, reassurance, communication, checking in, ways of finding quiet or distraction, design of spaces, and early appropriate referral to community services.

Touchpoint 2 - Integrated models of care, treatment and support for patients who are acutely intoxicated, presenting with/at risk of, acute, severe behavioural disturbance associated with substance use, psychiatric conditions, and other co-occurring health conditions, with clear roles and responsibilities and escalation processes.

We constructed a fictional clinical scenario featuring a patient/provider persona dyad for each touchpoint. The clinical scenarios were informed by prior observational and interview data (MyED Stage 1) and reviewed by two independent and experienced ED providers to ensure plausibility and fidelity to practice.

Step 2: persona development interviews

Participants and recruitment

We completed semi-structured interviews with patients who participated in MyED Stage 1 and had attended an ED in the hospital for low acuity mental health issues; and providers who worked in ED. A purposive strategy for recruitment was adopted.

Patients interviewed in MyED Stage 1 who consented at the time to be contacted about participating in the co-design process and provided contact details, were invited to participate in the interviews. These participants were low acuity patients aged 18 years or over and who were identified by mental health clinicians at that time as having capacity to participate. Of the 29 Stage 1 participants, 12 indicated interest in MyED Stage 2 and were contacted about opportunities to participate in the persona design interviews and co-design stage. To balance our desire for inclusion and to ensure ethical participation, we included two participation models: one-to-one face-to-face or online interview with individuals; or individuals who wished to participate in a supported model, for example with a family member or friend. Patients currently experiencing psychosis, who were acutely suicidal or who could not provide informed consent individually or with signing support were excluded from the study.

Providers who worked at participating EDs and cared for adults presenting with a mental health issue were invited to attend persona design interviews. Provider participants were invited if they: participated in a Stage 1 MyED interview; or were available to join an interview when the research team was onsite at a study hospital. We included two participant models: one-to-one online or face-to-face interview with individuals; or group interview session with two or more individuals.

Demographic details were collected from participants who had not already provided these in MyED Stage 1 interviews.

Data collection

In preparation for the persona development interviews, we created persona profile scaffolds. These scaffolds presented different persona card content and presentation style options. The clinical scenarios and persona profile scaffolds were put together in PowerPoint presentations and shown to participants during patient and provider interviews (Supplementary File 1: Interview guides and accompanying slides).

Participants were presented with the clinical scenarios and invited to suggest content to include on the relevant persona profile scaffold; patients were asked to comment on the two patient personas and providers were asked to comment on the patient and provider personas. Two different persona card styles were shown to participants to elicit preferences on format and image type (cartoon or photograph).

Patient participants were also asked: to anticipate the main concerns for the patient personas when they arrive in the ED; what things, for example staff interactions and physical environment, could best support the patient personas in the ED; and what the patient personas would value in the care they received in the ED. Provider participants were asked: to anticipate the main concerns for the patient personas when they arrived in the ED; to describe the typical patient journey for the patient personas while they were being treated in the ED; what things, for example staff interactions and physical environment, could best support the patient personas in the ED; and what ED clinicians would experience in delivering care to the patient personas.

During the interviews, we also asked patient and provider participants how we could deliver the Stage 2 MyED co-design workshops in a way that offered a good experience for them and what questions should be asked to evaluate their participation in the co-design sessions. These responses informed planning and evaluation of Stage 2 MyED co-design workshops.

Interviews were conducted between February and March 2025. As a PhD candidate, AD completed all interviews with a supervisor (either CC or RC-W). While we took care to minimise participant discomfort, at the conclusion of each interview and in a follow-up email, participants were provided with the process for engaging support services in the event the interview caused them to be upset in following days.

Interview analysis

We used descriptive statistics to report participant demographics. Interviews were audio recorded, transcribed verbatim and reviewed by the interviewing researcher to support familiarisation with the data and reflexive engagement throughout the analysis process. Data were organised in Microsoft Excel. We analysed the interview data about persona card content using an inductive reflexive thematic analysis informed by Braun and Clarke’s approach [23, 24], which conceptualises theme development as an active, interpretive and iterative process. First, we familiarised ourselves with the data through repeated readings of the transcripts, noting initial ideas and identifying extracts pertinent to the development of persona dyads. Second, we generated codes that captured features of the data relevant to the aim of designing persona dyads for use in co-design. Third, we engaged in an iterative process of collating and developing codes into potential sub-themes and themes across patient and provider interviews. Fourth, we reflexively reviewed and refined the developing sub-themes and themes to ensure they captured meaningful patterns relevant to persona design. Fifth, we continued theme refinement through ongoing engagement with the data, ensuring that codes generated from the patient interviews and those from the provider interviews were clearly captured in each theme. Sixth, we selected vivid, compelling examples to depict each sub-theme and theme and support the analytic narrative. Responses to questions about the presentation style of the persona cards and workshop delivery were summarised.

We used the PROGRESS-Plus tool to report the factors that limit opportunities for health that were either presented in the personas or reported by interview participants who contributed to persona development: Place of residence; Race/ethnicity/culture/language; Occupation; Gender or sex; Religion; Education; Socio-economic status; Social capital; and “Plus” factors that stratify health opportunities and outcomes such as age and disability [25, 26]. We used the GRIPP2 short form [27] (Supplementary File 2: GRIPP2 short form) to report consumer involvement in the study and were guided by the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) Guideline [28] (Supplementary File 3: SRQR checklist).

Step 3: persona creation

Persona content was derived directly from coded interview data, with themes and sub-themes from the analysis providing the organising structure for persona profiles. Persona attributes and statements were traced back to participant transcripts to ensure that content reflected participant accounts rather than researcher inference. Rather than reconciling or homogenising differing perspectives from patients and providers or prioritising one group’s views over another’s, persona card content decisions were guided by an intention to preserve these differences within paired patient/provider personas. Where patients emphasised strengths-based goals, empowerment and positive future orientation, these priorities were captured in patient personas. Where providers highlighted systemic constraints, safety concerns and role complexity, these elements were explicitly reflected in provider personas. This approach helped to ensure that the persona dyads presented the realworld experience of emergency mental health care from both perspectives.

The number of persona dyads was determined by the two priority touchpoints identified during earlier analysis (MyED Stage 1). Decisions regarding persona card information layout and visual format were informed by participant responses to relevant persona development interview questions, with emphasis on clarity, affinity with the touchpoints and suitability for use in timelimited co-design workshops. The image presented on each persona card was created using artificial intelligence programs DALL-E [29], Midjourney [30] and a character builder with experience in modelling and animation.

Reflexivity

The research team comprised: a PhD student who is a consumer researcher and has participated in and facilitated co-design workshops as both a consumer and researcher (AD); researchers with expertise in qualitative research methods, health services research and implementation science (CC, RC-W); a healthcare provider (CC); as well as a person with lived experience and a MyED study participant (SL). The impetus for this study is AD’s PhD research interest in how to involve priority populations in co-design and builds on a published scoping review on the use of personas to involve consumers in co-design [12].

Results

The scenarios were in the form of a brief narrative and introduced a patient/provider dyad (Box 1: Clinical scenarios). Scenario 1 featured Ana (patient) who presented to an ED feeling overwhelmed and who was experiencing a prolonged wait in a crowded ED waiting area; Darius was the ED nurse (provider) monitoring Ana. Scenario 2 featured Bill (patient) who presented to ED acutely intoxicated and who was at risk of severe behavioural disturbance; Gina was the ED doctor (provider) leading Bill’s care.

Clinical scenarios

Scenario 1

Ana is sitting the Emergency Department waiting area. She has been struggling with university, a new job, and recently her best friend died in a car accident. Ana has had little sleep for a week. She has told her mum she just wants it all to stop and has had thoughts about harming herself. Her mum rang the GP as she is worried about her, but they told her to take Ana to the ED. Her mum dropped her off but had to go home to look after her younger siblings. This is the first time Ana has been in an ED.

Ana feels like she has told the same information (her name, date of birth etc.) to lots of people in the Emergency Department but nobody has told her how they can help. The waiting room is crowded. A man sitting across from her is whispering to himself and staring. She doesn’t feel safe. Ana is so tired. Feeling alone and forgotten, she thinks she will leave the Emergency Department and go home to try to sleep.

Darius is a nurse in the Emergency Department. He feels exhausted by the busyness of the day. Darius looks over to Ana from the triage area and observes that she looks anxious. He knows that the wait to see a mental health specialist is very long. He knows Ana may want to leave before being seen.

Scenario 2

Bill arrived at the Emergency Department by ambulance. He has previously sought health care for anxiety and depression and usually takes antidepressants. Bill was drinking alcohol at home and was found on the floor by his partner. She called the ambulance as she was worried about him.

In the ambulance, Bill blew a blood alcohol reading of 0.25 (over 4 times the legal limit). Bill doesn’t remember much, but the paramedics have also shown the Emergency Department staff cuts on Bill’s wrist. The Emergency Department area is noisy and busy. Bill is too intoxicated to go to the waiting room so he is still on the ambulance stretcher. Bill tells the nurse that he can see his brother who is deceased. He admits that this often happens after he’s used ‘ice and grass’.

Bill doesn’t have anything with him. He has left his phone at home, his partner is at home with his young child, and he is worried that he can’t contact his family. Bill is becoming increasingly angry, calling out and threatening staff.

Gina is a senior doctor in the ED. Gina will need assistance from both ED staff and other care teams to manage Bill while he is in the ED. Gina is worried that Bill’s behaviour will deteriorate and is concerned about how that will impact her safety, and the safety of other staff and nearby patients.

Inductive analysis summary

Patient summary

The Humanise theme captures participants’ desire to be seen and understood as individuals beyond their clinical presentation. In the context of ED care for people experiencing mental health concerns, this theme reflects the importance of acknowledging personal identity, values, and traits to foster empathy, trust, and more responsive care.

Relevance to Persona Design

The Humanise theme encompasses the design of personas that reflect the complexity and individuality of patients with mental health concerns. Including elements of background and personality in persona profiles elucidates diverse patient experiences and counters stigmatising assumptions. This theme supports the development of rich characters that humanise the clinical encounter. Presenting personas with personal information acknowledges the human behind the patient.

Example from persona cards

Ana: Eldest of 4 children. Goes out with friends when they do something quiet, like watch a movie.

Bill: Coaches his child’s soccer team. Values family, they are his priority.

Provider summary

The Humanise theme explores how ED providers’ professional and personal experiences and values shape their interactions with patients presenting with mental health concerns. Participants wanted to be seen as empathetic and realistic individuals with intertwined personal and professional lives.

Relevance to Persona Design

The Humanise theme reflects the importance of creating provider personas that are realistic and relatable. Including personal background and skills, and professional experience in personas acknowledges the intersection between personal and professional lives, extending role-based descriptions to portray humanising elements that influence care delivery.

Example from persona cards

Darius: Regularly checks in on younger cousin who has anxiety and depression. Dedicated. Registered nurse.

Gina: Supports elderly father who lives alone and has complex health needs. Calm in tense situations. Has experienced verbal abuse from patients. Recently a patient tried to stab her with a butter knife.

Patient summary

The Contextualise theme highlights the importance of understanding the broader life circumstances that influence a patient’s presentation to the ED. For patients experiencing mental health concerns, contextual information, particularly medical history and support systems, can provide critical insight into their current needs, vulnerabilities, and potential pathways for care.

Relevance to Persona Design

The Contextualise theme signifies the importance of embedding personal history and social context into personas so that the characters are nuanced, empathetic and reflect the lived experience of people who present to ED with mental health concerns. Including details about medical history and support systems enables understanding of the complexity of each persona’s situation.

Bill: Drinks heavily at times, occasional drug use. Saw a psychologist a while ago to help with his anxiety and depression.

The Contextualise theme explores the role of ED providers in a complex system and the structural supports and limitations in the ED environment. It highlights the supports and role expectations that shape provider experience and impact patient care.

Relevance to Persona Design

Personas that reflect role diversity and the broader organisational environment in which providers operate represent the constraints providers encounter in the ED. Articulating the provider’s role in personas differentiates the clinical responsibilities and functions of ED staff.

The Illuminate theme reflects the emotional, psychological, and environmental intensity of the ED for individuals experiencing mental health crises. This theme also reflects patients’ desires to be seen, heard, and supported in ways that validate their experiences and guide them toward positive outcomes.

Relevance to Persona Design

The Illuminate theme provides insight into the patient experience and their wish to feel acknowledged, understood, validated and supported in ways that help them achieve outcomes beyond the ED. Incorporating thoughts, feelings, needs, and goals in personas generates empathy and illustrates patient strength and self-determination. The Illuminate theme captures the thoughts, feelings and challenges experienced by ED providers. It highlights the tensions, safety concerns, and fragmented workflows that shape clinical practice, often in ways that are invisible to patients.

Relevance to Persona Design

Demonstrating the complexity, operational pressures and dilemmas of ED mental health care in personas highlights the need to prioritise provider wellbeing alongside patient care. Representing the lived experience of providers in personas offers an understanding of the realities of ED care and generates empathy for both patients and providers.

Themes from patient interviews

Humanise

This theme signals the importance of seeing patients as individuals beyond their clinical presentation. It emphasises the value of understanding personal context to foster empathy, trust, and tailored care in the ED.

Background

Participants stated that even brief personal details, for example hobbies and social habits, can help inform a more complete picture of the patient. Including background information in the persona can humanise the patient, situate their experience and shift the perspective of a patient from a purely medical lens to a more holistic understanding of the individual.

It’s quite overwhelming, I guess, to get all this background information without seeing a sense of, like, who Bill is like. This is obviously not his average state because he’s in emergency so it’s, yeah, it’s quite confronting having a list of all these, maybe more negative views of Bill. So by having values, it can kind of help process and visualise like maybe the future goals and like more opens it up to like a better future for Bill - Patient 2

Personality

Personality traits were seen as influencing communication, empathetic engagement and the patient-provider dynamic. Traits such as being quiet, shy, or emotionally closed off were described as potentially influencing how patients engage with staff and how staff interpret their needs. Participants supported the inclusion of personality in the persona card to avoid harmful misjudgement and stereotyping based on appearance or behaviour.

… doctors can be quick to kind of … label someone … which can be very isolating and like it can just kind of shatter your self-confidence as a patient … I personally think the personality section’s very important … it’s helpful when they actually want to know you and they can see past you as a patient and can kind of gather information about your personality. – Patient 2

Contextualise

This theme reflects participants’ emphasis on the importance of understanding the broader context surrounding mental health presentations to the ED. This includes prior medical history and the presence or absence of support systems. Participants suggested that such contextual information can elucidate current needs, anticipate risks, and help to tailor care appropriately.

Medical history

Participants highlighted the relevance of knowing a patient’s mental health history, including previous diagnoses, treatment experiences, and engagement with mental health services. Including medical history information in the personas was seen as important for not only understanding the emotional and psychological context of the ED visit but also planning appropriate interventions that deliver safe, responsive and person-centred care.

Support systems

Participants discussed the role of informal and formal support systems in shaping mental health experiences and recovery. Participants described how the loss of key relationships, family dynamics, and prior engagement with mental health services could affect emotional wellbeing and help-seeking behaviour. Revealing fragile or disrupted support systems in personas is significant for understanding needs and planning follow-up care.

… depending on how much experience [Ana] has had with mental health departments … support systems would look a lot different. – Patient 3

“Bill will be concerned about not having his own partner.” – Patient 4

Illuminate

This theme captures the emotional, psychological, and environmental intensity of the ED for individuals experiencing mental health concerns. Participants described feelings of fear, vulnerability and self-doubt and an environment that compounded confusion and distress. This theme also reflects patients’ desires to be seen, heard, and supported in ways that validate their experiences and guide them toward meaningful outcomes.

Thoughts and feelings

Participants shared descriptions of their emotional states during ED visits including anxiety, sensory overwhelm, and feeling unsafe. These emotions were often intensified by an ED environment beset with bright lights, noise, long waits, and lack of privacy. Persona information that depicts the judgement, stress and overstimulation faced by patients provides insight to the difficulty they experience when trying to seek help or advocate for themselves in the ED.

… when you’re in that state, sometimes the feeling of judgement can just make you more stressed and increase the feelings of anxiety … just being listened to, not judged … might help [Bill] - Patient 1

And feeling, I guess, not important enough in the ED room … I guess that could be added to the thoughts and feelings … because mental health is very much on the inside … I just feel like it’s difficult to convince myself that I’m doing the right thing by getting help - Patient 3

“I want ED staff to …”

Participants emphasised the importance of small gestures of care, such as offering a blanket, cup of tea or checking in regularly, that can improve their ED experience. They also expressed a desire for staff to avoid judgment and to treat them as individuals rather than stereotypes. Patients want to be kept informed, treated with respect and supported in ways that acknowledge their mental health challenges. Articulating needs and preferences for ED staff engagement in the persona card with the statement, ‘I want ED staff to …’ was described as empowering for the patient character.

I actually see that heading [‘I want ED staff to …] is quite empowering for the patient … maybe if they are able to make decisions for themselves, it does give them a bit of empowerment … I kind of feel like I can bridge the gap between where I am now and where I want to be - Patient 2

Goals of ED experience

Participants outlined what they hoped to gain from their ED visit, including emotional relief and longer-term outcomes such as resources, discharge planning and follow-up care. Participants viewed the ED as a gateway to broader support systems, such as community mental health services. Participants supported the inclusion of information that empowered the personas to make decisions about their care and articulate goals beyond immediate clinical treatment.

… your problems also don’t just go away just because you got discharged from the ED … I felt more supported when I was linked with community services after discharge, so that could be a goal, that clinicians guide [Ana] in terms of … her experience as a patient - Patient 3

Themes from provider interviews

Humanise

This theme emphasises the importance of recognising ED providers as whole individuals whose professional experiences, personal backgrounds and interpersonal styles shape their engagement with patients experiencing mental health concerns. It highlights the significance of seeing clinicians not just as professionals, but as individuals whose personal and professional lives intersect in complex ways.

Professional experience

Participants explained that their professional experiences, for example formal training, years of experience and past patient encounters, affect their confidence, competence and approach to care. They described how junior staff may struggle with complex presentations if they have had limited exposure to mental health patients. Providers advocated representing these nuances in personas to reflect the diversity of provider perspectives.

I was a mature entrant and I would be quite happy dealing with a chap like [Bill] as an intern, whereas somebody who’s gone straight from high school to medical school would struggle with a chap like that – Provider 3

Personal background

Personal background was seen as a key determinant of how providers perceive and respond to patients. Participants described how their personal histories and life experiences influence their clinical judgment, communication style and interactions with patients. Providers also acknowledged the presence of biases and prejudices, despite efforts to remain professional.

… the fact that [Darius] has a personal background will, in effect, humanise him … I have family members with mental illness and often, you know, patients that I’ve seen I’ll go, ‘Oh fuck he’s like my [family member] … I have to really be cognisant of my own personal challenges with these patients. - Provider 6

Personal skills

Personal skills were identified as a critical factor shaping providers’ management of mental health presentations. Providers described a range of interpersonal and emotional skills essential to their roles. Participants acknowledged the emotional toll of care and importance of acknowledging provider’s own needs and vulnerabilities.

“I trusted you so many times and you’ve burnt me … I will empathise with you, but I’m not going to trust you. I’m waiting for you to click or flip on me and I’m ready to give you the mum voice.” – Provider 4

Contextualise

This theme explores how ED providers operate within a complex and often constrained system when caring for patients with mental health concerns. Participants described the impact of systemic factors such as staffing levels, physical space, and access to mental health-trained personnel on their ability to provide safe and effective care. Role-specific responsibilities were also discussed.

Supports/Enablers

Providers described the factors that influence their ability to care for patients with mental health concerns. Recurring concerns were the lack of dedicated space, adequately trained staff, workflows and infrastructure needed to deliver responsive patient care. Time pressure was another critical issue. Participants noted that delays in care could exacerbate patient distress, leading to agitation and reactive behaviour.

Do we need to also look at … how the system could support what [providers are] trying to do? … We don’t have the right staff who are properly trained … ED staff we see a bit of everything and not enough of one thing. – Provider 1

Role in ED

Providers reflected on their specific responsibilities within the ED. While acknowledging the limitations of their role, they also emphasised the importance of communication, reassurance, and basic comfort measures. Participants acknowledged that while patient interactions may be limited, small acts of kindness and clear communication can positively impact patient experience.

In the first couple of interactions … the interactions [are] really quite short and sweet and targeted. Sounds horrible, I know, but they won’t be spending much time with [Ana] in that first instance. Unless of course, there’s real cause for concern – Provider 1

… I also say to them, if you start to feel stressed, if you feel unsafe, if you feel like your everything inside is becoming too much, talk to us. I offer them food, tea, coffee, blankets, like those kind of things to make them more comfortable. But I think it’s really important to say to them that if it’s too much, talk to us - Provider 5

Illuminate

This theme captures the internal reflections of providers and explores the emotional and ethical challenges faced by ED providers when caring for patients with mental health concerns. The ED environment was characterised as chaotic and often unsafe, both for patients and providers. Participants shared experiences of verbal abuse, personal safety concerns, and expressed concern for patients experiencing long wait times and fragmented care.

Thoughts and feelings

Providers expressed concern for patient safety and wellbeing, and worried about the psychological impact of long wait times, overstimulation, and lack of support, especially for young or vulnerable individuals. They shared their emotional responses to patient presentations, including empathy, concern, frustration, and moral distress. Participants acknowledged the emotional burden of working in an overstretched system and the need for psychological safety and support for providers.

[Bill is] in an environment that’s going to exacerbate him. It’s noisy, he’s getting anxious with that and he’ll need a lot of, you know, reassurance and de-escalation. And I’m worried that we won’t be able to give him that where he is - Provider 2

For me in this setting, coming in at this from a clinician perspective, my biggest concern is actually not Bill, my biggest concern is me, right? Because I’ve been hit too many times for me to not want to make me the biggest concern. And, you know, there are plenty of clinicians in Australia in the last five years, that have lost their life because of Bills, right? So we, you know, for me that’s always going to be the number one concern and I kind of want that reflected in this [persona] card - Provider 6

Challenges in ED

Providers described a range of practical and systemic challenges that complicate care delivery. The ED environment was described as chaotic, overstimulating, and often unsafe for patients with mental health crises. Participants described the fragmented nature of care and disjointed workflow as barriers to building rapport and delivering empathetic care. The threat of violence and the presence of high-risk patients in the ED were described as routine but deeply unsettling, especially for junior staff.

“… that’s what we face every single day … unfortunately the patients are victims, victims is probably the wrong word, but that’s what they’re experiencing. So, we have to work under these challenges, under these roles, responsibilities, actual and perceived. And yeah, they unfortunately get the outcome of it - Provider 1

Presentation of persona cards: patient and provider responses

Participants expressed a preference for persona cards that are easy to read, structured and list pertinent information, but had differing perspectives on the image.

I like how it’s all like listed … It’s like very straight to the point, which is what I think a lot of people find easier reading than, rather than, having to read through like paragraphs of information- Patient 1

There were differing views about the use of an image, such as a cartoon-like character, or a photo on the persona cards.

Sometimes a photo just helps better visually, like, come up with like a picture in your head. I mean the cartoon isn’t bad, but I think just like proper photos just help sort of along the lines of imagining what [Ana] could be like - Patient 1

But something I don’t like is using someone’s photograph … it makes me feel a little uncomfortable that we’re creating his values, and challenges and goals. It feels too personal seeing someone’s face on the card rather than just the cartoon character. – Patient 3

Co-design workshop delivery and evaluation: patient and provider responses

In preparation for the Stage 2 MyED co-design workshops, we asked participants for strategies that could be implemented to prepare people for co-design and support them during the sessions. Patient participants supported the option to bring a support person to workshops, a program that was paced to counter sensory overload and information processing challenges. Receipt of information in advance of the workshops about what to expect during the sessions was also considered valuable. One patient participant was concerned about sharing negative experiences at a co-design workshop that involved providers.

I’m still a bit worried about coming to workshops because, like, my main concern would be because I’ve been in the ED quite a few times and I have quite a lot of negative experiences that … I don’t want to be like offending the staff … I just feel like it … wouldn’t feel as safe talking about my experiences so openly … so yeah, that’s something that I’m just a bit concerned about. - Patient 3

During the interviews, we also asked patient and provider participants for metrics that would be important for the research team to capture when evaluating participation in a co-design process. Participants suggested questions about participant safety, comfort with asking questions in the workshop, workshop structure and impact of the workshop discussion.

Certainly around the power gradient, did you feel safe? I suppose the impact that it had on, on the patient, as well, like, as much as you kind of distance yourself from it as a patient, how did it impact them? And again, nurses and doctors being able to kind of step out of their real clinical role and, like be in the trenches with the patients working this out. - Provider 2

Discussion

We used an innovative methodological approach to generate patient/provider persona dyads that humanise, contextualise and illuminate the lived experiences of ED mental health care. Personas have been reported to keep consumers in focus when discussing healthcare ideas and solutions [31–39], to generate empathy [11, 31, 35, 38, 40–43] and privilege experiences as stories to be shared [41]. Building on this, we have created persona dyads to elevate the emotions, motivations and goals of both patients and providers and to contextualise their experience. This is important as both perspectives must be considered in value-based healthcare.

Personas have been used across multiple design domains, ranging from data-driven representations to artefacts derived from qualitative insights [12, 44–47]. Amid criticisms of under-reporting of design and methods, we deliberately adopted a participatory, interview-based approach to persona creation to address the concerns in the literature regarding superficial or stereotyped personas [46, 47]. By co-designing the personas with patients and providers, we anticipate that the personas will be authentic, relatable and generate a sense of ownership in co-design sessions. Furthermore, persona dyads offer promise for co-design that more meaningfully engages both patients and providers as equal participants.

A growing body of literature has examined the involvement of service users and people with lived experience in co-design processes in mental health care, including several recent systematic and scoping reviews. While these reviews consistently highlight the promise of co-design, they also draw attention to limitations in how participation is sometimes operationalised, particularly when involvement is procedural, consultative or insufficiently attentive to issues of power and safety [48–50]. Evidence from these reviews suggests that co-design tends to be most effective when it is grounded in genuinely human-centred approaches that privilege experiential knowledge, foster trust and inclusivity, attend to empowerment and psychological safety, and support shared sense-making, rather than positioning lived experience as an adjunct to professional decision-making [48, 49, 51–54]. Reviews further emphasise the importance of methods that enable engagement with experiences, values and local contexts, and that use creative activities to facilitate meaningful participation in mental health co-design [49, 51, 52, 54, 55]. Our co-designed patient/provider persona dyads align closely with these insights. By featuring the human and contextual dimensions of emergency mental health care and developing personas to be an immersive participatory tool, this approach responds to calls for ethical, human-centred co-design methods in complex mental health service environments.

The three themes identified through analysis of participant interview data, humanise, contextualise and illuminate, informed the development of the persona cards. For patient participants, it was important that the personas were portrayed in a way that ensured they were seen “like a human, not just this person with a list of diagnoses” (Patient 3). Humanising patients aligns with the principles of dignity and respect promoted by the World Health Organization [1] for meaningful engagement. Provider participants valued persona cards that reflected professional experience, though views varied on the inclusion of personal information. Some providers appreciated personas that conveyed “a little bit of the clinician as a person as well as the clinician as a character” (Provider 6), while others felt such details were unnecessary. Humanising both patient and provider personas may help rebalance power dynamics in co-design by highlighting patients’ strengths beyond their mental health presentations, and portraying providers as individuals with their own strengths and vulnerabilities. This approach may enable a co-design environment where people, rather than their patient and provider roles, come together to share experiences and collaboratively explore solutions.

The inclusion of contextual information in personas was considered important for enabling a more holistic view of the individual’s situation with sufficient depth to elicit understanding. Facilitating this shared understanding during co-design sessions was considered important in effectively allowing participants to “see both sides of the story” and appreciate the complexity of each other’s experiences. Such insight into these challenges may strengthen the co-design process, increasing the likelihood of generating feasible and contextually relevant solutions.

Patient participants expressed a desire for personas to articulate strength-based goals and convey empowerment, particularly through the statement “I want ED staff to …”. In contrast, provider participants emphasised the importance of capturing the complexities of their roles, including challenges, concerns, and frustrations related to delivering patient care and the precarious nature of their professional environment. Illuminating such goals, thoughts and emotions in personas may help orient co-design discussions toward more empathetic solutions that support both patient-centred outcomes and improvements in the working conditions of healthcare providers.

We have previously reported that personas used in healthcare co-design have been represented through various image formats, including cartoons, photos and icons [12]. In this study, participants expressed differing preferences regarding the image format. For example, one participant described photographs as too personal and another as more relatable than a cartoon image. To accommodate these contrasting views, we developed rich sketches that aimed to humanise the personas by featuring emotive expressions and dynamic poses, and to contextualise them by depicting elements such as the ED environment and the professional roles of provider personas. The persona cards were designed with a consistent visual style to promote equity between patient and provider personas, although the content was tailored to ensure relevance to each group.

Valaitis et al. [37] distinguish personas from traditional co-design methods such as focus groups and interviews, which often position consumers as ‘consultants’ responding to researcher-driven agendas. In contrast, personas offer a person-centred approach that expose participant needs, ideas, and solutions, and challenge naïve, stereotyped, or outdated assumptions sometimes held by design teams or other stakeholders [37]. Compared to other tools such as vignettes, personas offer advantages through their visual appeal and tailored content, which can foster greater empathy and engagement. This is supported by insights from neuroscience and literary studies, that have identified key features to activate empathetic responses to fictional characters [56, 57]. The themes identified from participant interviews in this study reflect these key features: relatable narratives that provide contextual depth and reveal motivations and vulnerabilities (humanise and contextualise); access to internal thoughts and emotions that deepen emotional engagement (illuminate); and visual storytelling and immersive scenarios, evident in the rich sketches on persona cards and accompanying clinical scenarios.

Persona dyads are not presented as a replacement for other forms of consumer involvement, such as interviews, focus groups or project partner roles. Instead, they offer a complementary technique to collective sense-making in mixed stakeholder settings, particularly where direct sharing of personal experiences may be emotionally burdensome, ethically challenging or constrained by power dynamics. While this study focused on generating insight and shared understanding of ED experiences, the persona dyads are intended to support subsequent translation into design requirements during co-design workshops. During co-design, needs, constraints and goals articulated through the personas will promote discussion of service improvement strategies directly relevant to the scenario and support participants to engage with dual perspectives while maintaining emotional and ethical distance from personal experiences. The personas are not positioned as interventions with measurable effects, but as a participatory tool to facilitate engagement, foster empathy and stimulate person-centred solutions. Accordingly, their value will be examined through analysis of workshop transcripts and participant evaluation surveys.

Limitations and benefits

There are several limitations associated with the method used to co-design the persona dyads. First, the interview process was time-and resource-intensive. Second, we did not seek feedback from participants on the finalised personas or on their experience of the persona design process. For example, one consumer expressed discomfort with contributing content without sufficient background context: “I know she’s like a hypothetical character, but I don’t want to put, like, thoughts and feelings or like words in [Ana’s] mouth when I don’t know her full experience” (Patient 3). This limitation reflects a concern raised in the co-design literature, where studies often claim to use co-design despite limited end-user decision-making power and a lack of evaluation of participant experience or the perceived value of co-design outputs [48–50, 54]. Although we did not evaluate the personas immediately following their development, we will be obtaining participant feedback during the co-design workshops, including reflections on the personas’ authenticity, utility and presentation, as well as how effectively they supported involvement in the co-design process. Critical perspectives on participatory methods, such as the concept of participatory assimilation, further caution that even well-intentioned and human-centred approaches to co-design may inadvertently reproduce existing power imbalances when decision-making authority remains constrained and lived experience narratives are reshaped to align with institutional or professional goals [50, 58]. Related critiques highlight how the absence of sustained opportunities for participant influence and reflexive evaluation can lead to the selective incorporation or reinterpretation of experiential knowledge within participatory processes [50, 59]. Acknowledging these risks, we view the persona dyads not as neutral or definitive representations of lived experience, but as artefacts developed within specific ethical, methodological and system constraints, highlighting the importance of participant reflection on their use and impact in the subsequent co-design stage.

This study reports on the development of a co-design tool and conclusions are therefore limited to methodological contribution. Additional limitations relate to the study sample. Although purposive sampling enabled in-depth exploration of patient and provider perspectives relevant to emergency mental health care, the number of participants was relatively small. As such, the personas are not intended to be generalisable representations of all patients and providers, but to reflect a range of experiences that were salient within the study context.

A further consideration is that some participants involved in persona development may also participate in subsequent co-design workshops. While this continuity supports relationship building and sustained engagement, it may also influence how personas are perceived, discussed or evaluated during the workshops. We acknowledge that this dual role could shape participant responses and reflexive accounts of the personas’ usefulness. Conversely, the personas may have enabled key perspectives to be represented in ways that participants then do not have to disclose as personal experiences during the workshops. Despite these stated limitations, and recognising that actual use in subsequent co-design will be reported separately, sharing the method used to develop the persona dyads, and making them available for adaptation, may enable other research and design teams to tailor the personas for their own contexts and re-use in co-design.

There were also benefits to the approach we employed. First, participant interviews generated further valuable insights that informed the co-design workshop program. Second, participants shared practical considerations, such as the need for regular breaks and the availability of a support person for consumer participants, that helped shape the logistics of the co-design sessions. Importantly, the process cultivated relationships with participants, leading to sustained engagement in the MyED study, including the involvement of a consumer co-author on this paper.

Conclusion

Persona dyads were designed to present the core perspectives, experience goals, and humanity of patients and providers. It is anticipated that use of the persona dyads in co-design will support the creation of more realistic, empathetic, and actionable design solutions that are not only clinically informed but also emotionally responsive and reflect the lived experiences of ED patients with mental health concerns and the providers who care for them. The method used to create the persona dyads and resultant profile cards delivered in this study are resources that may be tailored for use in other research and service redesign projects.
