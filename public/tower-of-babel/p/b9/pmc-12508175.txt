Evolution of competitive systems in nature
Authors: Chubo Deng, Xian Sun, Rujie Guan, Liang Chang, Hongfeng Yu, Wanxuan Lu, Yingyan Hou, Kun Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Conflict between systems is ubiquitous in nature and throughout the universe, this study presents a novel field-theoretic framework for modeling competitive systems, which simplifies the modeling of interactions between similar objects by treating them as fields, and employs mathematical models to calculate and solve the evolutionary outcomes. The key contribution lies in developing and solving a novel class of nonlinear partial differential equations that incorporate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta$$\end{document}-source terms to characterize resource supply processes, and theoretical demonstrate that system evolution converges to three universal regimes(traveling waves, oscillations or stable equilibrium), with phase transition criteria determined by quantitative relationships between eigenvalues and supply parameters. Rigorous theoretical derivations and numerical results demonstrate that the model’s computations possess significant generality and applicability, offering a robust framework to explain various antagonistic phenomena observed in nature.

From the grand scale of galaxy mergers in the universe to the interactions between ants and anteaters, the natural world is replete with exchanges, conflicts, and evolution between competing systems1–5, and several typical examples are illustrated in Fig. 1, Similarly, in human society, confrontations between different systems are pervasive6. Various nations, religions, economic entities, and ideologies experience varying degrees of competition and antagonism. The presence of antagonism in complex systems is ubiquitous, both in the natural world and in human society7–9. Conflicts, strategic games, and resource competition among participants increase the complexity and disorder within a system, leading to an increase in entropy10,11. The essence of entropy increase lies in the internal uncertainty, dynamic changes, and chaotic flow of information within the system. On the other hand, unlike isolated systems, non-isolated systems can locally reduce entropy through ongoing energy input and replenishment. This allows them to sustain order or maintain a low-entropy state12,13. This process is commonly observed in various natural and engineered systems, such as living organisms, metabolism, and heat engines.

Although the forms of confrontation between different systems may vary, the underlying interaction mechanisms share commonalities, and their evolutionary outcomes exhibit similarities and universality. The relationship between confrontation and non-confrontation is not static or rigid; instead, there is potential for mutual transformation. Depending on the specific development of the systems, some originally non-confrontational dynamics may evolve into confrontational ones, while certain conflicts can transition from confrontational to non-confrontational. For example in predator-prey dynamics, normally non-confrontational resource competition can escalate into direct confrontations when prey density falls below critical thresholds, as modeled in literature14. Conversely, territorial conflicts among wolves may de-escalate to ritualized displays when population densities stabilize. In economic system, he transition from market competition (non-confrontational) to trade wars (confrontational) has been quantitatively modeled using game-theoretic frameworks, particularly when resource asymmetry exceeds 1.5:1 ratio15. The reverse transition occurs through institutional mediation, as demonstrated in fishery management conflicts.

The challenges in the evolution of complex systems primarily arise from their high non-linearity, dynamic nature, and multi-level interactions16,17. The presence of multiple, interrelated objectives in complex systems presents a significant challenge for the computational modeling of their evolution18. Even minor changes in individual components can lead to significant global impacts, making such changes difficult to predict. Additionally, the feedback mechanisms and adaptive behaviors among system components further complicate the evolution of the system over extended time scales.

When modeling dynamic systems, physicists typically employ two approaches: particle-based modeling19–23 and field-based modeling24–28. The core of particle-based modeling is to view physical processes as the result of interactions between particles with specific properties. This approach focuses on modeling the interactions between particles. In contrast, field-based modeling treats physical processes as changes in specific characteristics at each point in space. It involves modeling the continuous variation of these characteristics across space to study the physical system. In particle-based methods, physicists can simulate the motion of each particle using classical physical laws29. However, as the number of particles increases, the computational complexity rises sharply, placing greater demands on computational resources. Additionally, with the passage of time and the increase in iterations, errors accumulate at each step, eventually leading to system instability. These two challenges make long-term prediction tasks significantly more difficult.

Therefore, we choose to model the evolution of complex systems using the mean field approach. Mean field theory is a method for studying complex many-body problems30–35, where the interactions of a large number of particles are approximated by each particle experiencing an effective field, simplifying the problem to a single-field. The method provides valuable insight into the overall behavior of the system, such as phase transitions and collective phenomena, without requiring detailed information about each individual particle. Accordingly, this paper aims to explore the interaction mechanisms within systems that underlie natural patterns. Through this research, we hope to guide the discovery and explanation of new natural phenomena. The primary task of this paper is to model a competitive scenario where both parties have energy inputs, and its evolutionary calculations can be represented using mathematical model. Discussion

In this paper, we point out that any antagonistic systems will produce three types of evolutionary outcomes. The changes in its evolutionary state are caused by varying external energy inputs, which have a decisive impact on the system’s evolutionary results. The three distinct outcomes observed in this model illustrate how varying parameters influence the competitive dynamics between systems.The first outcome suggests the presence of a Nash equilibrium, where both systems, having optimized their strategies, reach a state where neither has an incentive to deviate from the current situation. In this case, the competition effectively ends in a stable, non-dynamic state.The second outcome highlights a dynamic game scenario, where the strategies of both systems remain in flux, leading to periodic shifts in power. This reflects the iterative nature of game-theoretic interactions, in which strategic adjustments are made continuously, and no permanent equilibrium is achieved. The third outcome aligns with zero-sum games or competitive exclusion, where the accumulation of advantage by one system leads to the complete marginalization or elimination of the other. This is often seen in systems with asymmetric resources or abilities, where the stronger party’s advantage becomes increasingly decisive over time.

These outcomes demonstrate the complexities of system interaction, ranging from equilibrium states to dynamic oscillations, or the dominance of one system over the other. Each outcome provides valuable insight into the nature of competition, stability, and strategic evolution, and offers a framework for further exploration in the contexts of game theory, ecological modeling, and social dynamics. Building upon the foundational theoretical framework presented in this manuscript, our future research will focus on empirical validation through application domains. In the next research phase, we will develop domain-adapted parameter mapping protocols to translate mathematical operators into physically measurable quantities and quantitatively validate the model’s predictive power through controlled comparisons between simulated outcomes and empirical observations.
