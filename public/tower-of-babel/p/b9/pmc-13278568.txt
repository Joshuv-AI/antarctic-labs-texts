Knowledge, attitude, and practice toward infection control precautions among medical students in Jordan: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Nader Alaridah, Tasneem Aloqaili, Maryam I. Abutouq, Moath Fateh, Ahmed Aljabali, Hamzeh Alshahwan, Sawsan Alomari, Mohammad Al Madadha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Infection control is an essential component of healthcare and vital for ensuring the safety of patients and healthcare professionals. This study aims to assess knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAPs) among medical students in Jordan.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A cross-sectional study was conducted from September to October 2024 among medical students enrolled in six Jordanian universities. Stratified random sampling was used to select participants across academic years (2nd to 6th year). Data were collected via a validated, self-administered online questionnaire adapted and translated for Jordanian students.RESULTS:Of 453 medical students, 58.1% demonstrated good knowledge, 75.5% positive attitudes, and 78.6% good practices toward infection control precautions. Females represented 67.5% of participants. Students with excellent and very good microbiology GPAs had higher odds of positive attitudes (OR = 3.89, 95% CI: 1.21-12.48; P = 0.022 and OR = 3.02, 95% CI: 1.05-8.69; P = 0.04, respectively). Male students were less likely to demonstrate good practices (OR = 0.518, 95% CI: 0.323-0.831; P = 0.006). Overall, students exhibited high (>70%) attitudes and practices toward infection control.CONCLUSION:Inclusion of these practices in clinical curricula is essential to improve infection control. The most significant gap was the ability to perform a risk assessment correctly, which is critical to national Infection Control and Prevention policies. This is the first and largest multi-institutional study assessing KAP toward infection control among medical students in Jordan, highlighting the need for policy-aligned curriculum improvements to strengthen future healthcare competencies.

Introduction

Healthcare-associated infections (HCAIs) are infections that emerge 48 hours or more after hospital admission or within 30 days of receiving healthcare and originate in a hospital or other healthcare facility during treatment.[1] Recent studies have highlighted HCAIs as a major public health concern. In Saudi Arabia, 34.5% of HCWs reported having encountered a needlestick injury, which is associated with a bloodborne infection. Furthermore, Jordan’s hospitals have substantial obstacles in infection control policy in terms of funds, training on how to handle personal protective equipment correctly, and heavily visited patients.[23]

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), in high-income countries, 7 out of 100 hospital patients are affected by HCAIs, compared to 15 out of 100 in low- and middle-income countries. Tragically, 1 in 10 affected patients succumbs to HCAIs, with higher risks observed in intensive care settings and among newborns.[4] HCAIs are particularly prevalent in intensive care units (ICUs), with 17% to 51.4% of ICU patients at risk of acquiring infections during hospitalization.[5]

Medical students represent an important subgroup of healthcare workers (HCWs) and are at increased risk of acquiring infection if they are not following Infection Control and Prevention (ICP) measures. Evidence indicates a considerable gap in understanding and implementation of ICP protocols. Nevertheless, targeted training has been shown to improve knowledge, awareness, and compliance, underscoring the critical need for structured ICP education within medical curricula.[678]

Infections frequently originate from patients’ own flora, which can change due to colonization by hospital-acquired pathogens through HCWs’ hands or contaminated environments. Identifying the precise source of infection is difficult as comprehensive investigations are typically limited to outbreaks or unusual cases in well-resourced hospitals. The estimated global cost of treating the five most common HCAIs ranges from $8.3 to $11.5 billion annually.[9]

Understanding and addressing HCAIs in Jordan is critical to public health as these infections often prolong hospital stays, raise healthcare costs, and contribute to mortality. They also add to the burden of antimicrobial resistance, complicating treatment efforts. Therefore, our study aims to assess the level of knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) among medical students in Jordan and identify key predictors. To our knowledge, this is the first study in Jordan to examine this topic using a large sample drawn from multiple universities, providing a comprehensive and representative perspective. Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional study among 453 randomly collected medical students’ responses in Jordan from September to October 2024.

Study participants and sampling

We used stratified random sampling, in which medical schools, as well as each academic year, were considered strata, and within the strata, students were randomly selected. The inclusion criteria were participants aged ≥18 years and currently enrolled in one of six Jordanian medical schools (University of Jordan, Hashemite University, Yarmouk University, Mut’ah University, Jordan University of Science and Technology, Al-Balqa Applied University), from the second year until the sixth year. We excluded first-year medical students, who had no microbiology courses in their curriculum.

Data collection tool and technique

An online self-administered structured English questionnaire was used, which was based on previously validated studies.[10] The questionnaire was translated via forward–backward translation, starting with translation from English to Arabic, followed by backward translation from Arabic to English to ensure valid translation, and then analyzed and approved by an expert committee. Based on a prior study conducted in Malaysia, a questionnaire was adapted and translated into the Arabic language and confirmed to be accurate for Jordanian students. Content and face validities were established through expert review, translation procedures, and public testing. We administered this questionnaire via online platforms to 45 randomly selected members of the public to demonstrate its suitability and readability and then made modifications based on the participants’ feedback. We subsequently conducted a pilot test to assess the questionnaire’s reliability based on 30 participants. The Cronbach’s alpha value was 0.811, indicating that the instrument maintained a high level of internal consistency, confirming its reliability. The questionnaire consists of 45 questions, which are divided into five sections. We used the Raosoft sample size calculator to calculate the minimum required sample size based on the total of 13,625 medical students reported by the Jordanian government. By assuming that 50% of the target population would have good knowledge, with a 5% margin of error and a confidence interval of 95%, the minimum sample size of 374 was determined. We added nearly 79 additional participants, considering a possible nonresponse rate, resulting in a total of 453 participants.

Ethical consideration

The current study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Jordan University Hospital, University of Jordan (IRB-JUH), in meeting No. 184/2022, which was held on June 9, 2022 [reference number: 10/2022/12851]. Electronic consent from each respondent was obtained before proceeding with the questionnaire. All information gathered during this study was confidential, and participants’ anonymity was always protected. No personal information that could reveal the participants’ identities was gathered. Data privacy was ensured by securely storing all collected data, accessible only to the research team. Any data shared or published were anonymized to protect participant identity, and data sharing will only occur with appropriate ethical approval.

Statistical analysis method

We used JAMOVI software for statistical analysis. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Categorical variables related to the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants are expressed as frequencies and percentages. For the statistical analysis, we categorized the levels of knowledge and practices into good and poor, and attitudes into positive and negative categories, based on two modified Bloom cutoff criteria: 70% of the total score per outcome.

To test for the normality of the distribution of the continuous variables, we used either the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (K–S) or the Shapiro–Wilk test (S–W), considering the relative sample size per group. Our data were found to be non-normally distributed. A multivariable logistic regression analysis was subsequently conducted to evaluate the odds ratios of the explanatory variables to predict KAP levels toward infectious hazards.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants

A collective total of 453 medical students from six public medical schools in Jordan participated in this study with a median age of 21 years. Of these, 67.5% were female, and 68.2% were urban. The number of participants from each medical school was matched, and the highest percentage of participants from Jordan University of Science and Technology (JUST) was 19.4%. In terms of academic year, 29.4% were third-year medical students. In total, 36.6% of the participants had a very good microbiology GPA [Table 1].

HSE, Health and Safety Executive

Most medical students thought it is important to prevent cross-infection in healthcare settings (99.3%) and correctly identified that standard precautions protect patients, health workers, and visitors (70%). High percentages correctly responded to statements on applying infection control measures to all patients regardless of diagnosis (89.6%), the role of hand washing (96%), the adequacy of tap water (93.8%), and the importance of plain soap and alcohol-based hand rubs (75.9%).

They also correctly understood that personal protective equipment reduces—but does not eliminate—the risk of infection (93.8%) and that all sharps must be appropriately disinfected or destroyed according to national guidelines (97.1%).

However, fewer participants correctly responded to statements related to how to perform a risk assessment (19.6%), the third step of risk assessment according to the health and safety executive (HSE) (36.6%), and the appropriate filling volume of a safety box, which should not exceed three-quarters of its capacity (31.8%) [Figure 2].

A logistic regression analysis identified several independent predictors. Younger age was significantly associated with better knowledge (OR = 0.879, 95% CI: 0.782-0.988, P = 0.03). Gender was a significant predictor of good practice, with females showing higher odds than males (OR = 0.518, 95% CI: 0.323-0.831, P = 0.006). Higher academic performance was significantly associated with positive attitudes, particularly among those with very good (OR = 3.02, 95% CI: 1.049-8.69, P = 0.04) and excellent GPAs (OR = 3.89, 95% CI: 1.212-12.48, P = 0.022).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this survey represents the first comprehensive examination of medical students’ KAP regarding standard precautions of infection control across Jordan’s six public medical schools, consistent with findings reported in similar settings across the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region. The objective was to assess their awareness levels and formulate new guidelines and regulations to minimize risks while maintaining good medical practices and a positive attitude.

Our findings revealed that while students demonstrated generally good practices and a reasonably positive attitude toward infection control, their knowledge was comparatively limited. This reflects a common challenge in healthcare education—the disconnect between theoretical understanding and practical application. Similar trends have been observed in studies from Saudi Arabia, Malaysia, and Iran, where positive attitudes did not consistently translate into effective behavior.[101112]

In contrast, studies from Lahore, Pakistan, reported relatively better knowledge and practice scores but low to moderate attitude levels toward infection control among healthcare workers.[1314] In our cohort, students in Jordan showed a favorable attitude (75.5%), though it remained slightly lower than their practice scores. This disparity may be due to limited clinical exposure, especially in the early years of medical school, and the predominance of theoretical instruction in some curricula—both of which can hinder the development of a strong, internalized attitude toward infection control.[67]

We discovered that the majority (78.6%) of the medical students had good practices for protection against infections. This rate is greater than that reported in studies from Saudi Arabia (73.3%)[11] and Vietnam (around 46%)[15] but lower than the rate reported among nurses in India (91%).[16] This discrepancy may reflect the influence of professional role, level of clinical exposure, and national healthcare training standards. Jordanian students likely benefited from refreshed microbiology content and foundational knowledge about blood-borne infections and prevention strategies.

Infection control proficiency appears to decline during the clinical years, which may be attributed to reduced structured instruction and increased academic and clinical pressures. Our findings suggest that continual exposure to foundational lectures and reinforcement of core practices helps sustain strong knowledge and attitudes. This is consistent with findings from a study in India, where a training program led to a significant immediate improvement in knowledge scores following the intervention; however, a notable decline was observed 1 month later, highlighting the need for ongoing and periodic infection control training to maintain knowledge retention over time.

To address the gap, an interventional study conducted on Egypt using infection control training course showed that the more the students know (knowledge score), the better they performed infection control practices (practice score)—and this relationship was both strong and reliable after the training (r = 0.8, P = 0.0001).[17] Likewise, a Saudi quasiexperimental study implemented simulationbased COVIDera IPC training and documented significant gains in KAP scores.

The COVID-19 pandemic significantly raised awareness of infection control among medical students, particularly highlighting the importance of hand hygiene as a primary strategy of infection control and prevention. Tragically, many healthcare workers were infected and lost their lives while serving on the front line during this crises in. This underscores the urgent need to reinforce adherence to standard precautions.[181920]

The comparison with other international studies reveals that Jordanian students outperformed some peers in knowledge and practice domains but lagged behind others, such as experienced nurses in India. This reflects the influence of systemic differences in healthcare education, cultural norms, and institutional enforcement of infection control policies.

Gender, urban residency, GPA, and source of information were significantly associated with better KAP scores. These findings align with previous research indicating that sociodemographic factors can affect infection control behavior. In contrast to a Malaysian study that found no significant differences across years of study,[10] our findings suggest that factors such as background, curriculum quality, and access to academic support resources can substantially influence outcomes.

Given these insights, there is a need to strengthen infection control education throughout medical training, especially in clinical years. Targeted strategies should include reinforcing theoretical knowledge, simulation-based training, practical workshops, and real-time assessments. Emphasis on key knowledge areas such as risk assessment, proper PPE use, and needle handling should be integrated more systematically into both lectures and hands-on activities.

We conclude that while Jordanian medical students generally demonstrate positive attitudes and good practices toward infection control, knowledge remains a critical area for improvement. Addressing these deficits requires revising education curricula, enhancing learning objectives, and verifying their achievement through practical assessments. Early and continuous training will not only improve student preparedness but also contribute to reducing occupational risks and limiting the spread of healthcare-associated infections.

Our research has the potential to make a real-world impact by informing policies and interventions aimed at improving infection control practices in healthcare settings. Since 67.5% of participants were female medical students, this accurately reflects the gender distribution in Jordan’s medical schools, where females outnumber males. The evidence gathered will help education systems and training program providers enhance students’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) by developing updated programs, curricula, and policies.

Limitations and recommendations

Given that this study is cross-sectional, our findings represent associations at a single point in time and do not establish causality between variables such as knowledge, attitude, and practice. Additionally, the study relies on self-reported data and its generalizability is limited to public universities. A major strength is the large, diverse national sample across all public medical schools in the region.

For future research, incorporating academic performance measures such as cumulative GPA could clarify links between academic success and KAP regarding infection control. Moreover, including curriculum details and teaching methods would strengthen understanding of factors influencing students’ KAP. Finally, deeper qualitative studies are recommended to explore why knowledge gaps persist, providing richer insights beyond quantitative associations.

Conclusion

Although medical students in Jordan showed positive attitudes and generally good practices when it comes to infection control, their knowledge in this area was still lacking. This gap highlights the importance of strengthening infection control education, especially during the early years of clinical training. By focusing more on practical, hands-on learning, we can better prepare students for real-world situations. Improving these parts of the curriculum will not only boost students’ confidence and skills but also help reduce infections in healthcare settings and keep patients safer in the long run.
