An energy saving perception method for industrial circulating cooling system
Authors: Xiaochen Zhu, Chenlin Wang, Xianan Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe circulating cooling system (CCS) is an auxiliary cooling system widely used in industrial processes, with substantial potential and significance for energy savings. However, in the field of energy-saving perception research for this system, issues such as incomplete evaluation systems and unreasonable indicator weighting still persist. Therefore, this paper establishes a new index system for industrial CCS by analyzing its operational mechanisms, covering three key aspects: heat exchange, cooling, and circulation. On this basis, by considering the subjective experience of experts and the objective characteristics of evaluation data, a comprehensive perception method based on the subjective assignment of the binomial coefficient method and the objective assignment of the CRITIC method is proposed to perform a thorough energy-saving evaluation of the system. Finally, the method’s applicability is validated through an accurate assessment of the energy-saving status of the CCS in a steel enterprise.Keywords: Circulating cooling system (CCS), Energy-saving perception, Index system, Binomial coefficient method, CRITIC methodSubject terms: Energy infrastructure, Renewable energy

Introduction

The circulating cooling system (CCS) is a simple and efficient auxiliary cooling system widely used across various industrial sectors, including petroleum, chemical engineering, iron and steel smelting, power generation, paper manufacturing, and food production. As an essential water conservation facility, CCS significantly reduces cooling water usage for industrial enterprises. However, in the steel industry, for instance, CCS water consumption can exceed 80% of the total water use, while its electrical load may account for 20%−30% of the total power consumption. Thus, CCS is also a major water-consuming and energy-intensive unit within the production system1. Due to the numerous production processes, extensive coverage area, and complex operational conditions, CCS often leads to energy and resource waste, highlighting a substantial potential for energy savings. With the intensifying global greenhouse effect in recent years, there is growing attention on low-carbon, energy-efficient, and refined production in the industrial sector. Conducting energy-saving research on industrial CCS can reduce energy and resource consumption, lower operational costs, and improve the overall energy efficiency of industrial systems.

Currently, researchers are focusing on energy-saving studies in CCS. Based on the types of research problems and energy-saving approaches, these studies can be classified into three categories: energy-saving design, operational optimization, and energy-saving retrofitting. Research on energy-saving design focuses on optimizing the equipment and structure of the system based on specific design objectives. Kim et al.2 viewed the operating equipment in CCS as a whole, taking into account the interactions among the equipment, and optimized the piping network design. Panjeshahi et al.3 expanded on Kim’s work more precisely by designing the IOTCS method based on pinch analysis and mathematical programming. Sun et al.4 and Gololo et al.5 both proposed stepwise optimization design methods. The former developed a thermodynamic-hydraulic analysis model, while the latter divided the network design into two steps: network topology analysis and system pressure drop calculation. For CCS comprising cooler and cooling tower networks, Rubio-Castro et al.6 proposed a superstructure approach that considers all possible configurations and interconnections. Ma et al.7 employed a series–parallel superstructure to reduce the cooling water flow rate and applied a main–auxiliary pumps structure in the pumping system. Zhu et al.8 described the complete structure information and operating status of CCS through the principle of operation and superstructure. Zeng et al.9 proposed a control-oriented thermal model framework that segregates the water circulation cycles. Zhang et al.10 performed sequential optimization of cooler and pump networks with different types of cooling. Liu et al.11 optimized the network configuration of CCS based on chance constrained programming. Research in energy-saving design is highly significant for improving the operating efficiency and energy-saving performance of CCS, and it provides valuable theoretical tools for related energy-saving studies.

The research on operation optimization aims to adjust and optimize the operating parameters of each device within the system to meet specific operational requirements. Hu et al.12 optimized the number of parallel pumps based on load demand and energy consumption. Qureshi et al.13 optimized the speed combination of cooling water pumps and cooling tower fans to minimize energy consumption. Leveraging the relationship between operating conditions and turbine power, Wang et al.14 optimized the operating points through an artificial neural network and a hybrid improved particle swarm optimization algorithm. Zhu et al.15 optimized the network structure, equipment operation modes, and conditions of the CCS through a superstructure approach. Niu et al.16 optimized the operation modes and states of energy-consuming components through an adaptive differential evolution algorithm. Lv et al.17 considered factors such as evaporation, leakage, blowdown, and heat transfer, proposing operational strategies for open-cycle cooling water systems under varying external conditions. Wang et al.18 analyzed and optimized the impact of ambient dry-bulb temperature, ambient wet-bulb temperature and working fluid flow rate on the operational costs of a CCS using a superstructure approach. Liang et al.19 considered fouling in pipelines, dynamic concentration cycling and variable frequency drives, and achieved fouling control, water conservation, and energy savings in CCS. Operational optimization research features low implementation costs and relatively low complexity, offering substantial application value. However, both energy savings and optimal operational state are constrained by the original design level of the system, resulting in relatively limited energy-saving capacity.

Energy-saving retrofitting research involves optimizing the system structure and equipment attributes within the existing resources and configuration conditions. For specific energy-saving improvements, Liang et al.20 converted constant speed pumps (CSP) to variable speed pumps (VSP) by installing variable frequency drives (VFDs) on the pumps, and optimized the configuration of VFDs, the number of CSPs and VSPs. Tang et al.21 retrofitted the cooling tower with turbine power generation and pump energy-saving water tank technology. Yang et al.22 used a lithium bromide absorption heat pump to recover waste heat and water. In the design of energy-saving retrofit schemes, Wang et al.23 tackled the retrofitting of heat exchanger networks using heat transfer enhancement methods. Hu et al.24 retrofitted a combined heat and power (CHP) unit by adding heat exchangers, enhancing power generation capacity. Sreepathi et al.25 proposed several heat exchanger network retrofitting strategies based on exchanger reassignment. Jahromi et al.26 presented an energy retrofit solution through thermal analysis of heat exchanger networks. Liu et al.27 introduced a hierarchical structure and applied the Poppe method to comprehensively evaluate water and air parameters within the cooling tower. Energy bottleneck diagnosis is the foundation of energy saving retrofit. Zhu et al. introduced a method for diagnosing energy-saving bottlenecks in industrial systems through multi-mode energy efficiency density decomposition28, and extended this approach by incorporating energy efficiency gap (EEG) analysis29. Energy-saving retrofitting reduces the need for new construction, lowering costs and environmental impact while improving economic efficiency.

Despite significant progress in energy-saving studies for industrial circulating cooling systems, limitations persist in energy-saving status perception. Existing methods lack objectivity, hindering a comprehensive analysis of the system’s energy-saving level. This shortfall creates a lack of scientific basis for energy-saving optimization, leading to randomness. This issue also affects the evaluation of energy-saving solutions, impacting the accuracy of decision-making.

While this research is not fully mature in industrial CCS, some relatively mature findings have been proposed for other industrial systems. For example, in the field of power systems, Wang et al.30 tackled real-time assessment in resilient power systems by creating a node voltage-based state matrix and characterizing the grid state based on the power network’s physical and operational characteristics. Liao et al.31 proposed a resilience evaluation method based on power support capability and network topology. Fan et al.32 proposed a multi-attribute node importance evaluation method for complex power grids based on the Gini coefficient, considering the impact of electrical characteristics and indicator weighting on the results. Liu et al.33 proposed a reliability evaluation method for power systems using Lagrange multipliers, eliminating extensive optimal power flow calculations. In terms of pipeline systems, Sun et al.34 implemented a Radial Basis Function Neural Network to enable rapid computation of safety factors for pipeline defects. These studies hold significant reference value for research on energy-saving perception methods in industrial CCS. Xie et al.35 developed an energy efficiency evaluation system for natural gas pipelines using the AHP. Wang et al.36 developed a risk assessment model for oil and gas pipelines by improving AHP and TOPSIS. Wu et al.37 combined AHP and grey clustering decision-making to assess pipeline safety under conditions of incomplete data. However, traditional evaluation methods such as AHP and TOPSIS often rely heavily on expert judgment or fixed distance metrics, which may lead to limited adaptability and reduced accuracy when dealing with complex, data-rich energy-saving perception scenarios.

Current evaluation methods for the energy efficiency of circulating cooling systems (CCS) often suffer from limited indicator coverage and inadequate integration of subjective and objective weighting strategies. To accurately perceive the energy-saving status of industrial circulating cooling systems, this paper designs a multi-indicator evaluation system for industrial CCS by analyzing the system’s operating mechanism and proposes a subjective-objective comprehensive energy-saving perception method based on the Binomial Coefficient-CRITIC approach. The second section introduces the operating mechanism of industrial CCS and analyzes the transfer and conversion processes of energy and materials. The third section establishes the system’s energy-saving evaluation index system. The fourth section presents the subjective-objective comprehensive energy-saving perception method. In the fifth section, the proposed method is applied to an actual pipeline network system for energy-saving analysis, verifying its practical effectiveness.

Mechanism analysis of CCS

Operating mechanism of CCS

The industrial CCS utilizes water as its cooling medium, which circulates through a network composed of heat exchangers to cool equipment, materials, devices, or process media within the industrial process system. The main operational process is illustrated in Fig. 1A water storage unit (such as a reservoir) stores and regulates the cooling medium and is typically regarded as the start of the cooling cycle for ease of calculation and analysis.

CCS Operating Mechanism.

Analyzing the input, output, conversion, and loss processes of various forms of energy in each operational stage of the system enables an effective assessment of the system’s energy utilization status, providing an essential foundation for energy-saving research. First, the circulating water pump partially converts the externally supplied electrical energy into the kinetic energy of the cooling water in the reservoir. As the cooling water flows through the pipes, it encounters frictional resistance and local resistance, resulting in both frictional head loss and local head loss, with a partial loss of kinetic energy. As the cooling water flows through the valve, it encounters local resistance, resulting in local head loss and a partial loss of kinetic energy. The (indirect) heat exchanger transfers the thermal energy from the process-side cooled medium into the cooling water. The cooling water flows through the piping network into the cooling tower for cooling, where all of the acquired kinetic energy is fully dissipated. The cooling tower uses part of the externally supplied electrical energy to remove thermal energy from the cooling water. Finally, the cooling water flows back to the storage unit by gravity, awaiting the next cooling cycle. The energy flow of the circulating cooling system is shown in Fig. 2.

Energy-Saving evaluation system

By analyzing the energy transfer and conversion processes in the operation of industrial circulating cooling systems, it is clear that the system’s energy flow primarily involves the kinetic energy and the thermal energy of the cooling water. In combination with established evaluation frameworks from other industrial fields, this paper proposes a new energy-saving evaluation index system. This system primarily analyzes three aspects: process heat exchange, waste heat cooling, and water circulation, providing a comprehensive, multi-dimensional evaluation of the system’s energy-saving status to achieve accurate perception of its energy efficiency.

Perception method based on combined subjective and objective weighting

Case application

To verify the effectiveness of the proposed energy-saving state perception method, a simulation experiment was designed. The experiment is based on the circulating cooling system of a steel enterprise, where the system’s operational data and the proposed method accurately quantify and describe the energy-saving state.

The case system includes three energy-saving retrofit schemes: (1) Adopting a variable frequency power-saving retrofit scheme. (2) Using a valve adjustment water-saving retrofit scheme. (3) Implementing an integrated energy-saving retrofit scheme. The preprocessing involves two main steps: the first is indicator standardization, where all indicators in the evaluation system are converted to positive indicators, ensuring that the evaluation results are quantified using a unified standard. The second step is dimensionless processing of the indicators, where each indicator value is normalized to retain evaluation and perception information while eliminating the influence of different units among indicators. Conclusion

This paper establishes a new energy-saving evaluation system for industrial circulating cooling systems by conducting an in-depth analysis of the operational mechanism and integrating the principles of energy transfer and conversion within the system. The system includes 3 primary indicators and 8 secondary indicators, allowing for comprehensive sensing of the system’s energy-saving status. Meanwhile, balancing subjective experience with objective data, this paper proposes a comprehensive energy-saving status sensing method based on the binomial coefficient method and the CRITIC method. The method is validated through practical case application. The results show that the proposed method can reasonably and accurately perceive the overall energy-saving status of industrial circulating cooling systems and achieve comprehensive quantification of the energy-saving status. It serves as an important basis and evaluation criterion for energy-saving-related research on the system.
