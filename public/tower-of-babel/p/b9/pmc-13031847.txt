Selecting suitable moss indicators for routine bioindication of roadside air pollution
Authors: Vítězslav Plášek, Katarzyna Łuczak, Grzegorz Kusza, Hana Režnarová, Arkadiusz Nowak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the context of accelerating urbanization, industrial development, and climate change, accurate and cost-effective methods for monitoring air pollution are increasingly needed. Among biological tools, cryptogams—particularly mosses—have attracted considerable attention due to their physiological traits and wide distribution. Seeking effective indicator species, this study evaluates the bioindication potential of five common pleurocarpous mosses (Plagiomnium affine, Climacium dendroides, Hypnum cupressiforme, Pseudoscleropodium purum, and Thuidium tamariscinum) for monitoring air pollution using moss bags and a rapid, non-invasive method of magnetic susceptibility measurements. We focused on traffic-related heavy metals, including zinc (Zn), lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), manganese (Mn), copper (Cu), chromium (Cr), nickel (Ni), and iron (Fe), quantified by Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry. The results revealed clear interspecific differences in pollutant accumulation, with Plagiomnium affine and Climacium dendroides performing best, likely due to favorable morphological and physiological traits such as high specific leaf area and open leaf architecture. All tested species are widespread and easily accessible, enabling efficient biomass collection for field applications. By linking functional traits with pollutant retention capacity, this study provides evidence-based recommendations for species selection in routine biomonitoring protocols across temperate habitats.Keywords: Airborne pollutants, Bioindication, Environmental monitoring, Magnetic susceptibility, Heavy metals, Moss bags, Specific leaf area, Species selectionSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

Mosses have become increasingly prominent as model organisms in the field of environmental biomonitoring, particularly for assessment of pollution indication along transportation corridors1–9. Road traffic, along with industrial zones and agricultural activities, represents a major anthropogenic source of environmental contamination by potentially toxic elements10,11. Motor vehicles contribute to 10–25% of total dust emissions, with their impact further amplified by secondary dust resuspension generated by tire movement12.

The distinctive physiology of mosses—characterized by the absence of a cuticle and true root system—enable them to absorb and accumulate pollutants directly from the atmosphere. This trait makes them particularly suitable as bioindicators of air quality6,13,14. Compared to vascular plants, mosses do not regulate pollutant uptake through stomatal closure, nor are they affected by root contamination, which makes their pollutant load more directly representative of atmospheric inputs15.

The application of mosses in environmental monitoring dates back to the pioneering study by Rühling and Tyler16, which established the foundational methodology for assessing atmospheric deposition of heavy metals, especially lead. Their work laid the groundwork for subsequent developments and refinements in bryophyte-based biomonitoring techniques.

Over recent decades, numerous studies have confirmed the effectiveness of mosses in tracing atmospheric deposition of various pollutants, including heavy metals, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), and persistent organic pollutants (POPs). Among the most frequently used taxa are Hypnum cupressiforme Hedw.9[,17,18, Pleurozium schreberi (Willd. ex Brid.) Mitt.19,20 and species of Sphagnum spp.21–23., valued for their wide distribution and high pollutant accumulation capacity. These species are commonly applied in passive biomonitoring strategies, in which naturally occurring mosses are collected across multiple sites to evaluate spatial and temporal trends in environmental pollution.

However, many other moss species are also widely distributed across different regions worldwide. They vary in morphological and anatomical traits that are crucial for pollutant adsorption and absorption—such as leaf size and arrangement, specific leaf area, presence of lamellae or awns, plant height, desiccation tolerance, and sensitivity to humidity changes6[,24. For metals and metalloids, it is generally accepted that the pollutant uptake capacity of mosses and lichens is related to their high surface-to-mass ratio, their ability to intercept and retain airborne particulate matter25,26 and the specific physicochemical properties of their cell walls27,28.

In addition to passive techniques, active biomonitoring methods—most notably the moss bag technique—have been developed and widely applied22,23,29,30. This approach involves placing moss samples into permeable containers (moss bags), which are then deployed in target environments for a defined exposure period. The accumulated pollutants are subsequently analyzed, offering a standardized and reproducible means to assess local pollutant loads. This technique has proven especially useful in urban and industrial areas, where naturally growing mosses maybe sparse or absent.

Recent developments in biomonitoring have also explored the potential of mosses in assessing emerging pollutants, such as microplastics and engineered nanoparticles31. While traditional contaminants, including heavy metals, remain a primary focus, the interactions between mosses and novel pollutants constitute a rapidly growing area of investigation. For instance, recent studies have demonstrated that mosses are capable of accumulating polystyrene nanoparticles, underscoring their potential utility in monitoring microplastic contamination9,]32.

Advances in methodology have encompassed improvements in analytical techniques, notably the implementation of inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), which enables highly sensitive and accurate quantification of pollutant concentrations within moss tissues. In parallel, the standardization of sampling protocols and data interpretation frameworks has significantly enhanced the reproducibility, comparability, and overall reliability of moss-based biomonitoring across diverse geographic regions and time frames. Active biomonitoring presumes the bioindicator is alive long enough to respond physiologically to pollution. Świsłowski et al.33 show that moss vitality (e.g., chlorophyll content and PSII efficiency) declines with exposure duration and environmental stressors, so procedures must explicitly manage the organism’s health state throughout the protocol. To keep moss as a living indicator (rather than a passive dust trap), standardize pre-exposure conditioning and homogenization, document baseline vitality, limit exposure to windows where PSII function is retained and vitality is maintained33.

Mosses thus represent an effective and adaptable tool for monitoring air pollution, both along traffic corridors and in other anthropogenically impacted environments5,6,34. Their ability to accumulate a broad spectrum of pollutants, combined with methodological advancements in both passive and active monitoring approaches, has firmly established their role in environmental assessment35. Ongoing research continues to extend the scope of their applications, particularly in relation to emerging contaminants, thereby reaffirming the relevance of bryophyte biomonitoring in addressing current and future environmental challenges.

Magnetic susceptibility analyses represent an important tool in environmental monitoring, enabling rapid and non-destructive determination of pollution levels of anthropogenic origin. Low-field and mass-specific magnetic susceptibility describe the ability of materials to become magnetized under the influence of an external magnetic field. This property depends on the concentration, mineralogical composition, and grain size of magnetic particles36. In environmental research, mass-specific magnetic susceptibility is commonly employed as a proxy for assessing the concentration of anthropogenic magnetic particles, including those with ferro-, ferri-, and antiferromagnetic characteristics. Field measurements of magnetic susceptibility (κ) are widely used to identify areas with elevated deposition of urban, industrial, and traffic-related dusts37–41.

As previously noted, mosses are globally distributed and characterized by a high surface area-to-weight ratio, which makes them particularly effective at intercepting airborne particulate matter42. Owing to the absence of a true root system, mosses obtain most of their nutrients through wet and dry atmospheric deposition. Their remarkable capacity to accumulate potentially toxic elements over prolonged periods – often at very high concentrations—renders them valuable organisms for long-term environmental monitoring35. However, this accumulation capacity is highly species-dependent7, and it remains unclear which moss taxa are most suitable for routine application as bioindicators under variable field conditions.

Accordingly, the present study aims to evaluate multiple moss species to identify those most suitable for effective and standardised biomonitoring of roadside air pollution. An additional objective is to determine which species exhibit the highest sensitivity to low pollutant concentrations, a factor particularly relevant to the early detection of subtle changes in air quality. Furthermore, the study incorporates an assessment of magnetic susceptibility in moss tissues as a proxy for the accumulation of anthropogenic magnetic particles, thereby providing an indirect indicator of metal emissions and particulate pollution associated with vehicular traffic. The analysis of this parameter was conducted to evaluate the usefulness of magnetic susceptibility as a rapid, non-destructive, and cost-effective tool complementing traditional chemical methods, enabling the estimation of heavy metal pollution levels in the environment43. We also hypothesised that moss species with characteristics such as large leaf size, high specific leaf area and loose leaf and stem arrangement, would be effective bioindicators of air pollution.

Materials and methods

Collection and deployment of moss samples

To evaluate the suitability of individual bryophyte species for routine air pollution monitoring, five pleurocarpous moss species were selected: Climacium dendroides (Hedw.) F. Weber & D. Mohr, Hypnum cupressiforme Hedw., Plagiomnium affine (Blandow) T.J. Kop., Pseudoscleropodium purum (Hedw.) M. Fleisch., and Thuidium tamariscinum (Hedw.) Schimp. These species were chosen based on their abundance, broad ecological range, and ease of collection in sufficient quantities. They differ in morphological traits such as plant size, growth form, leaf dimensions and shapes, and leaf surface characteristics – details of which are presented in Table 1.

The moss material was collected on April 25, 2024, at the foothills of the Opava Mountains (near Zlaté Hory), a region characterized by low levels of anthropogenic pollution. All procedures related to the plants were carried out in accordance with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and regulations. All relevant permissions were obtained for the collection of plant materials. The specimens were identified by the first author, Vítězslav Plášek, and the reference material was deposited in the OSTR herbarium (University of Ostrava) under numbers B-7456 to B-7471. Immediately after collection, samples were transported to experimental sites located in the city of Opole, where they were placed into permeable containers (so-called “moss bags”) and suspended on trees along roadside corridors in Ligota Prószkowska (50°32’41”N; 17°49’53”E) and the Bierkowice district of Opole (50°40’53”N; 17°51’39”E). Each bag contained only one moss species, and sets comprising all five species were deployed at both locations. The experimental design was duplicated for statistical robustness. The two road sites were comparable in terms of traffic load, with an average daily traffic volume of 10,952 vehicles—predominantly personal cars (9,791/day)44.

The moss bags were exposed to ambient environmental conditions for three durations: 1, 3, and 6 months. Following each exposure period, the samples were retrieved and immediately transported to the laboratory for further processing. In the laboratory, mosses were removed from the bags, air-dried at room temperature, and subsequently homogenized using a Retsch agate ball mill (model S1).

Table 1 summarizes the key morphological characteristics of the bryophyte species selected for comparison. The cushion size column indicates the average dimensions of moss cushions in the sampled populations. All selected species form sufficiently large cushions, allowing for consistent and reliable sampling in biomonitoring applications. The leaf area reflects the average surface of individual leaves. Larger leaf areas are suspected to be positively correlated with the capacity for pollutant adsorption and absorption. Leaf arrangement on the stem describes the spatial organization of leaves. Species with more widely spaced leaves may offer a greater interception surface, potentially enhancing their ability to capture airborne pollutants compared to species with tightly appressed foliage. The leaf surface character was also assessed, as the presence of papillae or other surface structures may reduce the efficiency of particle deposition and absorption. Smooth leaves generally offer fewer physical barriers to pollutant accumulation. Other traits, such as serrulate margins or awn-shaped leaf tips, may additionally influence particle retention by modifying surface complexity. The presence of paraphyllia or pseudoparaphyllia – which increases the photosynthetic and absorbing surface area – may further enhance pollutant accumulation. Finally, Specific Leaf Area (SLA; leaf area per unit dry mass) is highlighted as an ecophysiologically relevant trait. Species with higher SLA values typically exhibit thinner leaves with greater surface area relative to dry mass, which may result in slower water uptake but higher exposure per unit biomass. Conversely, mosses with lower SLA values tend to have thicker leaves, allowing for rapid hydration and efficient use of short-term moisture availability.

Magnetic measurements

Before the chemical analysis, the volumetric magnetic susceptibility (κ, expressed in SI units of 10− 5) was determined for each sample in order to assess the ability of each species to absorb magnetically active particles, which show a correlation with heavy metal pollution. Measurements were performed in the laboratory using a Bartington MS2B dual-frequency sensor operating at 0.47 kHz and 4.7 kHz. For each moss species and exposure period, five homogenized subsamples were weighed and placed into standardized 10 cm³ Bartington plastic containers. These containers were then inserted into the sensor coil for measurement. Mass-specific magnetic susceptibility (χ, 10− 8·m3·kg− 1) was calculated according to the formula: χ = κ/ρ, where ρ represents the density of the sample (kg·m− 3).

Chemical analysis

Approximately 0.5 g of moss from each homogenized sample was subjected to acid digestion in Teflon vessels using a microwave digestion system (Mars X, CEM, USA). A fixed-temperature program (180 °C for 10 min) was applied, with a mixture of concentrated nitric acid (HNO₃) and hydrochloric acid (HCl) in a volume ratio of 1:3. After cooling, the digested solutions were quantitatively transferred into volumetric flasks and diluted to a final volume of 25 ml with deionized water. The concentrations of eight elements – Zn, Pb, Cd, Mn, Cu, Cr, Ni, and Fe – were determined using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) with a PlasmaQuant 9100 spectrometer (Analytik Jena, Germany).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Statistica 13.3 (TIBCO Software Inc., USA) and R 4.3.1 (R Core Team, 2024). Prior to analysis, all variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test. Because most datasets did not meet the assumptions of normality or homoscedasticity, non-parametric tests were applied.

To examine differences in heavy metal concentrations and mass-specific magnetic susceptibility (χ) among moss species and exposure durations, the Kruskal–Wallis one-way analysis of variance by ranks was applied45. In cases where statistically significant effects were observed, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s multiple comparison test with Bonferroni correction to determine statistically significant differences between individual groups. A significance level of p < 0.05 was applied. Temporal changes in χ over the three exposure periods (1, 3, and 6 months) were analyzed separately for each species using the same procedure. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated to assess relationships between magnetic susceptibility and the concentrations of individual heavy metals (Cr, Ni, Mn, Zn, Cu, Pb, Cd, Fe). To explore multivariate relationships between morphological leaf traits (specific leaf area – SLA, leaf area) and metal accumulation patterns, a Canonical Correspondence Analysis (CCA) was performed using the vegan package in R. The analysis was based on centred and standardized data.

Descriptive statistics (median, interquartile range) were used to summarize concentration data due to their non-normal distribution. All statistical tests were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Temporal changes in magnetic susceptibility

For each moss species, mass magnetic susceptibility (χ) was measured after 1, 3, and 6 months of exposure. Kruskal–Wallis tests confirmed statistically significant temporal variation in susceptibility for all species studied (p < 0.05 in all cases). Mass-specific magnetic susceptibility (χ) increased over time in all moss species, but the intensity and linearity of this trend varied (Fig. 3). The time point “0” shown in Fig. 3 represents baseline (T0) measurements obtained prior to exposure. Subsequent values recorded after 1, 3, and 6 months therefore reflect cumulative magnetic susceptibility resulting from both initial background load and atmospheric deposition during exposure. Plagiomnium affine showed a clear, consistent increase in magnetic susceptibility across the three exposure periods (1, 3, and 6 months). Hypnum cupressiforme Pseudoscleropodium purum and Climacium dendroides also showed increasing susceptibility with time, though with slightly more variability and less pronounced differences between exposure periods. Thuidium tamariscinum, despite showing statistically significant variation, had the lowest absolute values and smallest changes in susceptibility.

Discussion

Species performance in biomonitoring applications

It is widely accepted that a perfect bryophyte bioindicator should be common, readily available, and occur predominantly in relatively unpolluted environments. Such species include Pleurozium schreberi, Sphagnum fallax, and Dicranum polysetum46,47. It is also generally assumed that the physiological state of the moss is of limited importance in bioindication, as passive mechanisms of particle interception and accumulation tend to dominate over active uptake processes. However, recent studies have highlighted that the organism’s vigour and physiological condition – particularly its state of cryptobiosis – may influence pollutant retention33. Therefore, selecting moss species that are well adapted to the specific habitat and microclimatic conditions of the sampling site is essential. Drought resistance, ecological tolerance, and morphological robustness all contribute to the moss’s ability to maintain its bioindicator function over extended exposure periods.

Given this, the search for suitable moss species should not be limited to a few traditionally used taxa. Instead, identifying species that perform well under specific climatic and habitat conditions is essential for improving the precision and applicability of biomonitoring protocols—particularly in ecologically sensitive areas intersected by roadways.

Among the species examined in this study, Plagiomnium affine demonstrated the highest overall suitability as a bioindicator. It is widely distributed in temperate regions of Europe and North America48 and typically inhabits relatively humid environments. In our biomonitoring experiments, P. affine showed high efficiency in accumulating heavy metals. Already at the second sampling point (after the initial exposure period), a marked increase was observed in elements characteristic of industrial and traffic-related emissions. Particularly high values were recorded for iron (Fe: 5718.3 mg·kg− 1) and manganese (Mn: 968.3 mg·kg− 1), indicating effective accumulation of Fe-rich mineral/technogenic particles. However, the weak Fe–χ correlation observed for C. dendroides suggests that this iron is not necessarily present in strongly magnetic forms.

Moreover, P. affine exhibited elevated concentrations of metals typically associated with road traffic and urban activities, including zinc (Zn: 148.7 mg·kg− 1), copper (Cu: 34.6 mg·kg− 1), and lead (Pb: 17.5 mg·kg− 1). The presence of these elements suggests a substantial contribution of anthropogenic aerosols to deposition on the gametophyte surface. The relatively high levels of cadmium (Cd: 1.6 mg·kg− 1) and nickel (Ni: 9.9 mg·kg− 1) further confirm that P. affine effectively adsorbs highly mobile elements typical of roadside and urban environments.

Studies conducted by Spagnuolo et al.26 also demonstrated the ability of P. affine to capture organic compounds from the aerosol phase, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) such as phenanthrene. The authors emphasized that these substances are mainly accumulated as solid particles deposited on the gametophyte surface, underscoring the key role of moss morphology in pollutant sorption. Together with our results, this finding supports the potential of P. affine as a versatile bioindicator in air pollution studies, particularly in areas dominated by traffic-related emissions.

Importantly, P. affine also has a broad ecological amplitude, occurring in grasslands, riparian woodlands, broadleaf forests, and coniferous stands. This ecological versatility enhances its utility in diverse landscape contexts.

The second most effective species was Climacium dendroides, which is also widespread across the temperate zones of Eurasia and North America. It typically inhabits moist habitats, such as Molinion meadows, peatlands, alder woods, and streambanks49. Its wide occurrence and morphological traits contribute to its suitability for pollution monitoring. However, other studies have indicated that under conditions of high humidity its ability to accumulate trace elements may be reduced. Continuous leaching of metal particles by water, together with the relatively smooth surface of its leaves, means that in highly waterlogged habitats C. dendroides does not accumulate pollutants as effectively. Thus, although Gorelova et al.50 recognized it as one of the more effective species in terms of overall occurrence and environmental tolerance, its usefulness in passive biomonitoring – especially in wet habitats – may be limited by leaching processes and the specific morphological features.

During the exposure period, C. dendroides exhibited the highest concentrations of iron (Fe: 10294.0 mg·kg− 1) and manganese (Mn: 1650.8 mg·kg− 1) among all the species studied. Such high levels indicate effective accumulation of industrial dust particles rich in minerals with ferromagnetic properties. This species also showed elevated levels of heavy metals associated with traffic emissions, such as copper (Cu: 39.3 mg·kg− 1), zinc (Zn: 100.1 mg·kg− 1), and lead (Pb: 14.6 mg·kg− 1).

The presence of these elements confirms that C. dendroides is susceptible to the deposition of anthropogenic aerosols, primarily from transportation and industrial sources. The content of cadmium (Cd: 0.5 mg·kg− 1) and nickel (Ni: 16.1 mg·kg− 1) further indicates effective sorption of highly mobile elements under conditions of increased anthropogenic pressure.

The species Thuidium tamariscinum showed a distinctly lower capacity for accumulating heavy metals compared with the other mosses examined. Already at the second exposure period, relatively low concentrations of elements characteristic of industrial and traffic-related pollution were recorded. The levels of iron (Fe: 2552.6 mg·kg− 1) and manganese (Mn: 311.0 mg·kg− 1) were more than twofold lower than those observed in the species with the highest sorption capacity, indicating a limited ability to bind ferromagnetic particles.

Similarly, the results for typical traffic-related metals confirmed only moderate sorption linked to road transport and urban activity: copper (Cu: 21.2 mg·kg− 1), lead (Pb: 8.5 mg·kg− 1), zinc (Zn: 80.3 mg·kg− 1), and cadmium (Cd: 1.0 mg·kg− 1). The concentrations of nickel (Ni: 5.3 mg·kg− 1) and chromium (Cr: 7.4 mg·kg− 1) were also lower than in the other species, further supporting the conclusion that T. tamariscinum has a limited capacity to adsorb mobile and trace elements.

The results suggest that the pleurocarpous morphology of T. tamariscinum—with tightly appressed leaves and a compact growth form - may hinder the effective retention of aerosols containing heavy metals. Nevertheless, its broad ecological tolerance, including its occurrence on dry and sandy soils and in coniferous forests, indicates that this species may still serve as a suitable background-level bioindicator in environments with lower anthropogenic pressure, such as young postglacial landscapes or forested areas with low traffic intensity.

Functional traits driving bioaccumulation efficiency

The two most effective moss species identified in this study, Plagiomnium affine and Climacium dendroides, are characterized by relatively large leaf surface areas. P. affine in particular reached individual leaf areas of up to 20 mm2, making it the largest among the species tested. As expected, a larger surface area enhances the potential for adsorption and, where applicable, active uptake of airborne pollutants.

In P. affine, not only the absolute leaf area but also the loose arrangement of leaves along the stem appears to be functionally important. This trait may promote more effective exposure to air currents and particulate deposition, even when accounting for potential structural deformation of mosses enclosed in experimental moss bags. By contrast, Thuidium tamariscinum, the least effective bioaccumulator, possesses strongly appressed leaves and a dense growth form. In addition, the presence of distinct papillae on Thuidium leaf surfaces may interfere with the adhesion of water droplets, which are the primary vector for atmospheric pollutants. Although papillae might superficially increase total surface area, their microstructure could reduce the stability and retention of the water film and thereby limit pollutant deposition and uptake51.

These findings are consistent with the well-established plant functional trait known as Specific Leaf Area (SLA), defined as the ratio of leaf area to dry weight. In vascular plants, higher SLA is associated with greater metabolic activity, rapid growth, and high resource uptake efficiency52–54. Among the bryophytes studied, P. affine and C. dendroides exhibited the highest SLA values (see Table 1), suggesting a similar ecological strategy.

The significance of these morphological traits was further supported by the canonical correspondence analysis (CCA) (Fig. 4). The vectors for SLA and leaf surface area were strongly aligned with the same sector of the plot as metals such as Zn, Pb, and Cd, indicating a clear relationship between leaf functional traits and the mosses capacity for pollutant bioaccumulation. The species P. affine and C. dendroides clustered within this same ordination space, confirming their strong functional congruence with the gradient of metal-bearing aerosols. In contrast, T. tamariscinum and Pseudoscleropodium purum were positioned far from this cluster of variables, which is consistent with their low contribution to metal accumulation.

In bryophytes, SLA influences water absorption and retention dynamics, which are key factors in their performance as bioindicators, especially when deployed in moss bags35. Mosses with high SLA, characterized by thinner leaves and larger surface areas relative to biomass, tend to absorb water more slowly because of lower tissue density. However, once hydrated, they retain water for longer periods and distribute it across a broader surface. This extended hydration may support prolonged metabolic activity and enhance the sustained accumulation of pollutants over time21,55.

In light of the observed association between leaf architecture and specific leaf area, we identify three tractable model species for follow-up experimentation: Atrichum undulatum Web.et. Mohr., Mnium hornum Hedw., and Plagiomnium undulatum (Hedw.) T.J. Kop. These taxa are widespread in temperate forests, form sufficiently large populations for replicated sampling, and are logistically straightforward to collect, which facilitates controlled comparisons across sites and seasons.

Atrichium undulatum also exhibits relatively high SLA owing to its efficient laminar construction. Well-developed lamellae on the costa substantially increase photosynthetic surface area without a commensurate increase in dry mass, and leaves may attain lengths of ≈ 10 mm, further amplifying area-based metrics56. Mnium hornum is likewise a promising candidate: its comparatively large leaves (typically ≈ 4 mm, reaching ≈ 8 mm toward shoot apices) and the presence of a distinct marginal border suggest a high area-to-mass ratio and potentially reduced mechanical costs per unit area57. Finally, Plagiomnium undulatum bears tongue-shaped, undulate leaves with decurrent bases that extend onto the stem; this architecture effectively increases exposed leaf area and is therefore expected to elevate SLA58.

Targeted experiments using these species could explicitly test how architectural features (e.g., number and height of costal lamellae, lamina thickness, degree of undulation, extent of leaf decurrency) predict physiological traits relevant for biomonitoring. For ensuring comparability, measurements should standardize hydration state, developmental stage, and microhabitat light and moisture, and incorporate within- and among-population replication. Coupling SLA with gas-exchange or chlorophyll-fluorescence assays, alongside quantification of water-holding capacity, would clarify whether architectural traits and high SLA also entail trade-offs in desiccation tolerance or carbon gain. Taken together, A. undulatum, M. hornum, and P. undulatum may provide a coherent test set for evaluating how leaf construction mediates resource-economy strategies in acrocarpous mosses.

Additionally, mosses with high SLA (e.g. Plagiomnium cuspidatum, Rhizomnium punctatum) may be particularly well-suited to biomonitoring in relatively stable environments, such as roadside or urban locations, where prolonged moisture availability allows for steady pollutant accumulation52.

Conversely, mosses with low SLA typically have thicker leaves and smaller surface area relative to mass. These species absorb water rapidly but also dry out quickly because water is concentrated in a smaller volume and more exposed to evaporation. In bioindication contexts, such mosses may be better suited to more variable environments, where intermittent moisture pulses can be rapidly exploited for short-term pollutant uptake.

A potential methodological constraint of active moss-bag biomonitoring is the physical compression of moss shoots during exposure. Such compression is inherent to bag-based systems and may theoretically influence surface area availability or microenvironmental conditions. However, in the present study all species were subjected to identical bag design and packing density, thereby standardising any compression effects across treatments. Although bryophytes with more complex morphology may experience relatively greater compression, our results demonstrate that these species nonetheless exhibited higher pollutant accumulation capacity. This indicates that species-specific functional traits outweigh any potential influence of morphological compression, and that interspecific comparisons remain valid under the applied experimental design.

Taken together, SLA emerges as a key functional trait for selecting optimal moss species for use in biomonitoring with moss bags. Species with high SLA values, such as Plagiomnium affine, appear particularly effective for long-term environmental monitoring programs conducted in habitats with stable moisture regimes.

The role of magnetic susceptibility in heavy metal biomonitoring

The results of this study confirm that mass magnetic susceptibility (χ) is not only an indicator of the accumulation of iron- and manganese-bearing particles, but also correlates with the concentrations of heavy metals in biomonitors, provided that species selection and exposure conditions are properly controlled. The interpretation of our results suggests that higher χ values are primarily associated with the deposition of magnetic particles, including magnetic forms of iron and manganese (e.g., ferromagnetic fractions), as well as other technogenic particles exhibiting magnetic properties. Similar conclusions have been drawn in previous research; for example, moss bags studies have shown that magnetic measurements can be used as a rapid screening tool for particulate pollution prior to full chemical analysis59.

Based on the comparative plot (Fig. 2), we observed that Plagiomnium affine and Climacium dendroides reached the highest χ values across exposure periods, reflecting their strong capacity to accumulate magnetically active particles. At the same time, the correlation analysis (Table 2) showed that in these species the coefficients between χ and heavy metal content (such as Cu, Ni, Zn, Cr) were statistically significant and relatively high (e.g., r ≈ 0.667 for Cu in C. dendroides).

These results underscore that the choice of biomonitoring species is crucial, in line with previous work indicating that bryophytes should not be treated as functionally interchangeable in biomonitoring applications60.

The boxplots (Fig. 3) show a statistically significant (Kruskal–Wallis test, p < 0.05) increase in χ across successive exposure periods (0 → I → II → III). This trend indicates that mosses accumulate particles cumulatively, and that exposure duration is a key factor in active biomonitoring. A similar time-dependent increase in metal concentrations has been reported in moss bag studies by Świsłowski et al.47.

The interpretation of our results suggests that higher χ values are mainly associated with the deposition of particles rich in iron and manganese – namely fine ferromagnetic fractions61. Notably, χ did not consistently track total Fe concentrations across species. In particular, despite very high Fe loads in Climacium dendroides, the Fe–χ relationship was weak (Table 2). This suggests that a considerable fraction of accumulated Fe may occur in weakly magnetic or non-magnetic forms (e.g., oxidized phases or ionic/complexed Fe) or that χ primarily reflects the magnetic mineral fraction and its grain-size/mineralogy rather than total iron content. Therefore, χ should be interpreted as a proxy of magnetic particle deposition rather than a universal surrogate for total Fe load, with species-specific performance62.

By contrast, the moderate correlations observed in P. affine imply that, in this species, metal accumulation may also proceed via more complex mechanisms—such as ionic sorption or complexation within the gametophyte structure—which weakens the direct χ-metal proportionality27.

Our results confirm that χ measurements can serve as an effective screening tool for assessing exposure to technogenic particles and heavy metals, especially in roadside and urban environments. The high spatial resolution of moss-bag sampling, combined with rapid magnetic measurement, offers strong potential for identifying pollution “hotspots”, as demonstrated by Vuković et al.60 in Belgrade.

However, the interpretation of χ data requires consideration of several factors: species-specific traits (morphology, leaf surface characteristics, accumulation strategy), exposure duration and environmental conditions (humidity, deposition rate), and the composition of deposited particles (ferromagnetic Fe/Mn particles vs. dissolved metal ions). Neglecting these factors may lead to misinterpretation of magnetic data.

Although χ measurements are promising, certain limitations must be acknowledged: not all metals and particulate forms exhibit a strong magnetic signal, as reflected by the weak correlations for Cd and Fe in some species. Additionally, physiological state of mosses, meteorological conditions, and variability in deposition may influence χ–metal relationships.

Future work should focus on combining magnetic analysis with multi-fraction particle characterization, standardizing exposure protocols, and developing predictive models for heavy-metal accumulation based on χ, as suggested by Salo and Mäkinen47[,63.

Limitations and perspectives

Although this study identified clear interspecific differences in the accumulation of magnetically active particles and heavy metals, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the experiment was conducted under specific environmental conditions, within a limited geographic region and over a defined time period. The performance of moss species may therefore differ under other climatic regimes, substrate types, or pollution sources. Second, although morphological and physiological traits such as SLA, leaf surface structure, and cushion architecture were linked to bioaccumulation efficiency, these relationships are largely correlative. Experimental verification of causal mechanisms – particularly those related to cellular uptake processes or molecular responses – is still needed. In addition, the influence of environmental stressors such as desiccation, UV radiation, and microbial interactions on pollutant retention has not yet been fully explored.

Importantly, all moss species included in this study are widespread and form abundant populations in their respective habitats. This greatly facilitates their practical application in biomonitoring, as large quantities of biomass can be collected in the field with minimal effort. Such accessibility enhances the feasibility of routine sampling and long-term monitoring across various habitat types.

Future studies should therefore aim to validate these findings across broader ecological gradients and with a wider array of moss species. Integrating physiological measurements, molecular analyses, and controlled exposure experiments would improve our mechanistic understanding of moss-based biomonitoring and support the development of standardized species-selection protocols tailored to specific habitat types and pollution contexts.

Conclusion

This study confirms that the appropriate selection of moss species and the duration of exposure are crucial for the effective biomonitoring of traffic-related air pollution. Among the five tested pleurocarpous species, Plagiomnium affine and Climacium dendroides proved to be the most efficient bioindicators of vehicular emissions, showing the highest and most consistent capacity to accumulate heavy metals and magnetically active particles. Their morphological traits – large leaf surface area, high specific leaf area (SLA), and loose leaf arrangement – substantially enhance their ability to intercept and retain traffic-derived airborne contaminants.

A clear increase in the concentrations of elements typical of road traffic emissions, such as manganese (Mn), zinc (Zn), copper (Cu), and nickel (Ni), was already observed after the first month of exposure. These results demonstrate that even a short, one-month exposure period in moss bags allows for a reliable assessment of the environmental status in areas affected by road traffic. Longer exposure durations (3–6 months), however, provide a more comprehensive picture of deposition dynamics and improve the sensitivity of pollutant detection.

Among the analyzed elements, the highest accumulation levels were found for Zn, Cu, Ni, and Cr, which are the main carriers of the magnetic signal, In contrast, Pb and Cd showed weaker correlations with magnetic susceptibility, suggesting that their accumulation in moss tissues is governed by mechanisms other than the deposition of ferromagnetic particles.

Magnetic susceptibility (χ) measurements proved to be an extremely valuable, rapid, and non-destructive indicator of anthropogenic particulate pollution, particularly that associated with vehicular traffic. The strong correlations between χ and the concentrations of Fe, Mn, Cu, Cr, and Ni confirm that magnetic susceptibility can serve as an indirect quantitative indicator of heavy-metal contamination in moss tissues. Moreover, χ measurements enable a preliminary spatial assessment of traffic-derived particulate deposition, providing an effective screening tool prior to detailed chemical analyses.

In summary, our results demonstrate that the combination of carefully selected bioindicator species (especially Plagiomnium affine and Climacium dendroides), an optimized exposure duration (minimum one month, preferably 3–6 months), and the application of magnetic susceptibility analysis significantly enhances the accuracy, reproducibility, and cost-effectiveness of biomonitoring of traffic-related air pollution. This integrated approach represents a reliable and economically viable tool for environmental quality assessment in roadside areas under temperate climate conditions.
