Genomic information contributing to the understanding of rheumatic diseases
Authors: Kazuhiko Yamamoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Genomic information related to rheumatic diseases is accumulating, but the connection between this information and the understanding of diseases and better medical care is not yet sufficient. This review aims to provide an overview of the current status of genomic information that can contribute to rheumatic diseases.

INTRODUCTION

Although there are several patterns of association between rheumatic diseases and the genome, this review focuses on disease-associated genetic loci in rheumatic diseases, which are predominantly multifactorial disorders. Genomic analyses of multifactorial diseases have been performed mainly using genome-wide association studies (GWAS), which have become prevalent after 2007 [1]. GWAS commonly analyses single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with a minor allele frequency of 1% or more, but recently, the comprehensive term ‘genetic variant’ is often used. Due to the high rate of recombination between homologous chromosomes during meiosis, our chromosomes exhibit a block-like structure. Although tens of millions of genetic variants are estimated to exist, GWAS typically examine approximately 500,000 to 1 million SNPs as selected tag markers to cover the entire genome. Regions showing statistically significant frequency differences, for example, between patients and healthy controls, are identified as potential susceptibility loci. The number of samples has a significant impact on the detection of statistically significant differences. Therefore, to date, GWAS reports tend to focus on identifying genetic regions associated with disease susceptibility, where large sample sizes are easier to obtain. In contrast, the analyses of genetic variants related to disease severity, drug response, and prognosis are challenging due to various factors, including the difficulty of collecting samples and the need to account for treatment interventions, resulting in fewer published studies [2].

RHEUMATIC DISEASES AND HUMAN LEUKOCYTE ANTIGEN

The human leukocyte antigen (HLA) region plays a prominent role in antigen-specific immune responses and has been reported to be associated with many rheumatic diseases even before GWAS. These associations can be broadly classified into those involving HLA class I and class II molecules. HLA class I-associated diseases include ankylosing spondylitis (AS) [3], reactive arthritis, and psoriatic arthritis, all of which show a strong association with HLA-B27. Additionally, Behçet’s disease and HLA-B51 have also been reported. These disorders are characterised by a high frequency of triggers such as enthesitis and intestinal and urinary tract infections [4]. HLA class II-associated diseases include rheumatoid arthritis (RA), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), Sjögren’s syndrome, polymyositis/dermatomyositis, and systemic sclerosis [5]. These are strongly associated with autoantibody production and have a higher incidence in women. However, the expression patterns of class II molecules differ between humans and mice, and thus the relationship between class II molecules and diseases may not necessarily be extrapolated from mouse findings [6].

Several hypotheses exist regarding HLA-related mechanisms, though none have been fully verified. Concerning the representative class I molecule HLA-B27, a hypothesis proposes that antigen presentation to CD8-positive T cells occurs via the generation of peptides binding to B27 through interaction with another genetic variant of aminopeptidase, such as ERAP1 [7]. Furthermore, a hypothesis concerning the abnormal folding protein response of HLA-B27 has been reported [8]. This is a cellular stress pathway activated when the endoplasmic reticulum detects abnormally folded HLA-B27 heavy chains. This stress is thought to induce the production of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-23, contributing to disease onset. Among class II molecules, RA susceptibility was previously characterised by the amino acid sequence at positions 70-74 of HLA-DRB1, termed the Shared Epitope [9]. However, recent detailed analyses suggest that positions 11 and 13 are crucial [10]. Further progress is anticipated regarding the relationship with the presentation of arthritis-inducing antigens. On the other side, detailed studies also reveal that multiple HLA regions are frequently implicated in the same disease. Notably, these regions may exert either disease-promoting or disease-suppressing effects, suggesting they may jointly regulate disease development [11].

GENETIC VARIANTS IN NON-HLA REGIONS AND RHEUMATIC DISEASES

GWAS is particularly effective for detecting genetic associations outside the HLA region. In rheumatic diseases, approximately 150 genetic loci have been reported to be associated with RA [12] and over 200 with SLE [13,14]. Regarding non-HLA factors, predicted target genes implicated in rheumatic diseases [2,15] include (1) immune receptors and their signalling pathways, (2) cytokine signalling pathways, (3) signal regulatory molecules [16], (4) cellular stress responses and metabolic systems [17], and (5) tissue remodelling and fibrosis [18]. While some of these are involved in the common pathophysiological basis of rheumatic diseases, others are disease-specific: for SLE, these include the interferon pathway and nucleic acid metabolism, and for RA, T cell and B cell signalling.

The term ‘gene locus’ refers to both a chromosomal position and its surrounding region. Due to chromosomal block structures, SNPs detected by GWAS may serve as proxies for the causal variants, but cannot by themselves pinpoint the precise pathogenic variant. Therefore, a more detailed analysis to estimate or identify the responsible variants that actually have a causal relationship with the phenotype is required and is called fine mapping or functional genetics [19].

FUNCTIONAL ROLES OF DISEASE RISK GENETIC VARIANTS

As GWAS analyses have progressed, it has become clear that many disease-associated genetic variants are located not in protein-coding regions but in non-coding regions. Approximately 80%-90% of disease-associated genetic variants are located in the regions related to enhancers, promoters, or long non-coding RNA and are thought to influence quantitative changes in cell type-specific gene expression. This is referred to as expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) [20]. This term was initially used to describe genetic regions influencing mRNA expression levels, but the concept has expanded to molecular quantitative trait locus (QTL) to encompass quantitative expression traits of other molecules. Examples include DNA methylation (methylation (me)QTLs), protein (protein (p)QTLs), and metabolite (metabolite (met)QTLs) [21]. It is also speculated that differences in RNA splicing may also be associated with diseases.

CONTRIBUTIONS OF GENOMIC INFORMATION TO THE UNDERSTANDING OF RHEUMATIC DISEASES

Genomic information contributes to the understanding of diseases in the following ways: (1) patient stratification for diagnosis and prediction of treatment response, (2) dissection of gene-environment interactions, (3) personalised medicine through polygenic risk scores (PRS), (4) causal inference for intermediate molecular traits such as transcriptomes and proteomes, (5) identification of new drug targets, and (6) mechanistic insight through functional genetics.

For example, regarding patient stratification, it has been revealed that the frequency of HLA-DRB1*04 differs markedly between RA patients with positive and negative anticitrullinated protein antibody [22]. However, as mentioned above, at present, GWAS data for stratified subsets are insufficiently reported. Thus, further GWAS analyses in disease-specific subsets are essentially required. Regarding gene-environment interactions, reports have shown that the synergistic effects of smoking and genetic risk variants in RA. In AS, the interaction between HLA-B27-positive individuals and gut microbiota has been suggested to contribute to disease onset, as reported in a twin cohort [23] and demonstrated in experimental animals [24].

PRS is calculated by multiplying the effect size of each risk variant by its variant number and summing the total for each individual. While PRS currently lacks sufficient power to predict disease status on its own, its usefulness as a tool to support clinical practice has been reported [25]. In drug discovery, 33 of the 50 new drugs approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2021 were found to have genes encoding targeted proteins or proteins known as ‘protein-protein interaction’ with these targets that were already associated as human genetic evidence with the approved disease or a closely related phenotype [26]. This suggests that genetic information revealed by GWAS is highly likely to be a factor in the underlying mechanisms of diseases or in the upstream factors of specific phenotypes.

DIRECTIONS FOR MECHANISTIC INSIGHT

Understanding individual immune function and population diversity from genomic information will become increasingly important. Generally, one effective approach to linking genetic variants associated with diseases to disease mechanisms is to identify the true responsible genetic variants on the disease-associated chromosomal block structure. For example, in the case of immune-related diseases, colocalisation of GWAS signal and eQTL in each cell type, including T cells, B cells, dendritic cells, and more detailed subsets, is the initial effective step, and candidates can be narrowed down [27]. Functional analyses are then conducted to confirm the causality. If multiple candidates remain, functional validation using cell type-specific genome editing technologies such as CRISPR-Cas9 may ultimately be necessary [28].

When the actual causative genetic variant is identified, pursuing its mechanism enables progress towards drug discovery by elucidating the detailed pathophysiological mechanisms. Taking eQTLs as an example, the current understanding is that the causal variants identified by GWAS frequently reside within enhancer, promoter, or long non-coding RNA regions and that they influence the expression levels of nearby or, in some cases, distant target genes in a cell-specific manner [29]. Therefore, the next step is to investigate the impact on epigenomic changes, such as whether genetic variation affects transcription factor binding [30] or whether loop structures exist between enhancer and promoter regions on chromosomes surrounding target genes.

We are currently constructing comprehensive multi-omics datasets encompassing genotypes, gene expression, epigenome, enhancer site identification, and eQTL analysis in resting and stimulated states, as well as proteome, lipidome, and gut microbiota, together with metabolome analysis for each immune cell subset separated from healthy individuals. These datasets are expected to provide foundational information for various studies, including detailed mechanistic research on human immune function and immune function estimation. In parallel with these analyses, we believe that integrating the multi-omics datasets with genotypes and gene expression data of immune cell subsets in diverse pathological conditions of rheumatic diseases can lead to a more detailed estimation of underlying mechanisms.

CONCLUSION

I have introduced the current status of how genomic information and gene expression data from rheumatic diseases contribute to understanding disease mechanisms.
