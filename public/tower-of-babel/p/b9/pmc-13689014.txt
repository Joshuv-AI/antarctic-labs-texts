Development of a rapid and simple Xeno Nucleic Acid (XNA) sensor-based microRNA detection platform for Parkinson’s disease diagnostics
Authors: Mi Young Byun, Moon Young Ryu, Seok Jong Chung, Bora Lee, Jong Ryeol Oh, Jin Kyu Woo, Jin Young Shin, Min Kyun Han, Oh Young Bang, Yun Joong Kim, Ming-Che Kuo, Hsiang-Hsuan Lin-Wang, Ruey-Meei Wu, Shau-Ping Lin, Seok Keun Cho, Phil Hyu Lee, Seong Wook Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrent biomarkers for Parkinson’s disease (PD) diagnosis, including imaging tracers, α-synuclein seeding assays, proteomic markers, and metabolomic signatures, are often limited by invasiveness, cost, scalability, or insufficient reproducibility. Circulating microRNAs (miRNAs) are attractive minimally invasive biomarkers because they are mechanistically linked to disease-associated pathways and remain stable in biofluids. However, the clinical translation of miRNA biomarkers has been hindered by multistep workflows and analytical variability arising from adapter ligation, reverse transcription, amplification drift, and cross-platform inconsistency in qRT-PCR, sequencing, and hybridization-based assays. To address these limitations, we developed XENO-Q, a rapid three-step platform that enables bias-minimized miRNA quantification through target-selective synthesis, in which only sensor-confirmed miRNAs are converted into amplifiable chimera products. XENO-Q enables linear quantification across several orders of magnitude with femtomolar sensitivity and high reproducibility while maintaining compatibility with qRT-PCR, ddPCR, and nanopore sequencing workflows. As a demonstration of clinical applicability, XENO-Q identified a two-marker circulating miRNA signature that accurately distinguished PD from other neurodegenerative conditions. Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder and remains challenging to diagnose in its early stages. More than ten million individuals are affected worldwide, yet clinical diagnosis still depends on motor symptoms such as bradykinesia, rigidity, and resting tremor, which appear only after 30–60% of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra have degenerated [1−4]. Although several dopaminergic positron emission tomography (PET) or single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) tracers have been developed to mark various aspects of nigrostriatal dopaminergic pathology, these imaging methods are costly, rely on specialized facilities, and are not suitable for large-scale or longitudinal screening. Recent efforts have focused on molecular diagnostics that capture disease-relevant biological processes more directly rather than downstream functional deficits. Several molecular biomarker classes have been investigated for PD diagnosis, each with distinct limitations. CSF α-synuclein seeding amplification assays (SAA) demonstrate high sensitivity and specificity for α-synucleinopathy but require invasive lumbar puncture, lengthy protocols of 5–7 days, and specialized infrastructure [5, 6]. Neurofilament light chain (NfL) correlates with disease progression yet lacks specificity, being similarly elevated in multiple system atrophy, progressive supranuclear palsy, and Alzheimer’s disease [7]. Plasma proteomics and metabolomics have identified candidate signatures with promising predictive power, but remain susceptible to pre-analytical variability and have shown limited cross-cohort reproducibility [8–10]. In contrast, circulating miRNAs offer stability in blood through encapsulation in extracellular vesicles, compatibility with minimally invasive liquid biopsy, and the capacity for multiplexed combinatorial profiling from a single blood draw — advantages that position them as particularly suited to scalable clinical screening [11–13].

Over the past decade, numerous studies have investigated whole blood, plasma, serum, CSF, and EV-derived miRNAs in PD; however, the concordance of reported markers across cohorts remains surprisingly low, and meta-analyses confirm that many fail independent replication [14−16]. This lack of consistency is largely attributed to analytical variability in current detection technologies, underscoring the need for a robust and bias-minimized quantification approach to distinguish true biomarkers from technical noise. Standard platforms, including quantitative RT-PCR and small RNA sequencing, introduce significant biases through adapter ligation efficiency and exponential amplification drift, often masking true biological signals especially for low-abundance circulating miRNAs [17−19]. This underscores the urgent need for a bias-minimized quantification approach.

To address this long-standing analytical gap, we developed XENO-Q, a rapid and bias-minimized miRNA quantification platform built on a synthetic DNA sensor architecture. XENO-Q employs a three-step workflow—completed within one hour—that couples selective hybridization of target miRNAs to a rationally designed Xeno Nucleic Acid (XNA) probe with target-selective amplification, in which only probe-bound miRNAs undergo controlled amplification. Unlike conventional methods that indiscriminately convert all miRNA to cDNAs, XENO-Q converts only detected miRNA into a miRNA-DNA chimera strand, eliminating nonspecific amplification, exponential drift, and concentration-dependent distortion. To assess the diagnostic capabilities of the platform, we selected PD–related miRNA markers and examined their clinical performance.

Materials and methods

RNA purification and quality control

Total RNA, including the small RNA fraction, was isolated from whole blood and plasma using XENOPURE small RNA purification kit (XENOHELIX, cat. 93667873-WB and 93667873-SP, respectively). Exosomal RNA was isolated using EVARI kit (XENOHELIX, cat. EVARI-765) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified RNA from whole blood was quantified using a spectrophotometer (NanoDrop, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The quality and integrity of the isolated RNA were assessed by gel electrophoresis.

Participants

The study participants comprised patients clinically diagnosed with Parkinson’s disease and other neurodegenerative conditions, including those with cognitive impairment. Neuropsychological evaluations were conducted by neurologists at the movement and dementia clinics of Yonsei University Severance Hospital between July 2015 and December 2020. Patients with PD were diagnosed according to the Movement Disorder Society clinical diagnostic criteria for Parkinson’s disease. PD-MCI and PDD were diagnosed using the MDS level II criteria and clinical criteria for probable PDD, respectively. AD was diagnosed based on the NIA-AA criteria, and probable DLB was defined by the McKeith criteria. Healthy control (HC) individuals had no history of neurological or major systemic diseases. For differential diagnostic validation, specific sub-cohorts were established. General cognition was evaluated using the Korean version of the Mini-Mental State Examination (K-MMSE). The study was approved by the Severance Hospital IRB (No. 4-2013-0407), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Target transcript expression analysis

XENO-Q assay

Each XENO-Q sensor is a chimeric single-stranded oligonucleotide (180–200 nt) with three functional regions: a 3′ target hybridization region (~20–22 nt DNA) complementary to the target miRNA; a central scaffold encoding Illumina-compatible indices (Index-I5/I7) and a molecule-specific barcode; and two classes of chemical modification. Arabinonucleic acid (ANA) residues within the scaffold render unbound sensors highly susceptible to S1 nuclease digestion in the single-stranded state, while becoming protected upon duplex formation. XNA-based blocker sequences prevent PCR extension of the upper sensor strand, restricting amplification to the lower miRNA-derived chimera strand only. The 3′-C3-NH₂ modification blocks inadvertent sensor self-extension by Xeno-polymerase and prevents sensor incorporation into nanopore sequencing libraries. For the XENO-Q reaction, 100 ng of total RNA was mixed with a pool of XENO-sensors and incubated at 63°C for 10 minutes. Upon cognate miRNA–sensor hybridization, the bound miRNA serves as a primer for Xeno-polymerase (XENOHELIX Co., Ltd.), a proprietary engineered polymerase derived from a Thermococcus species–derived archaeal TNA polymerase scaffold with broad substrate tolerance, which extends the RNA primer across the ANA/XNA-modified template using standard dNTPs, generating a stable double-stranded RNA–DNA chimera without a separate reverse transcription step. The reaction mixture was then subjected to a two-stage cleanup: (i) S1 nuclease digestion (60°C, 10 min) under manufacturer-optimized buffer conditions (XENOHELIX Co., Ltd.) to degrade free unbound sensors, excess single-stranded RNA, and non-specifically bulged hybrids while preserving intact chimera duplexes; and (ii) passage through a XENOHELIX Clean-up Column (upper membrane filter to remove protein debris; lower membrane filter to selectively retain residual single-stranded species) by centrifugation at 2,500 × g for 1 min. The eluate containing purified double-stranded chimera duplexes was used directly as input for downstream qPCR, ddPCR, or nanopore sequencing without further processing. RNA-DNA chimera strands were subsequently used as templates for amplification and were monitored in real-time using quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR) to determine the Cq value for each target miRNA. Absolute copy numbers of target miRNA present in the samples were determined using the QX200 Droplet Digital PCR (ddPCR) system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). ddPCR was performed with gene-specific probes for the target miRNA according to the manufacturer's protocol, allowing for the precise quantification of miRNA copy numbers per volume of input RNA [20].

Performance evaluation and diagnostic implementation of candidate biomarkers

We developed a diagnostic model using Logistic Regression (Generalized Linear Model) to classify the target groups based on biomarker expression levels. To identify the optimal combination of markers with the highest predictive power, we performed an exhaustive subset analysis, evaluating all possible combinations of candidate biomarkers. To ensure the robustness of the model and mitigate the risk of overfitting, repeated 10-fold cross-validation was implemented with 40 independent iterations. The diagnostic performance was rigorously assessed using a confusion matrix, from which accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and the F1-score were derived. The overall discriminative power of the model was measured by the Area Under the Curve (AUC) of the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve. Furthermore, potential multicollinearity among variables was monitored using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). All statistical procedures were conducted in R (v4.2.2) using the caret and pROC packages. To quantify the diagnostic utility of the selected biomarkers, we derived the regression coefficients from the optimized model. The diagnostic score (Z) was established through the following linear equation based on the Cq values: Z = -1.5972 × [miR-PD1] − 0.6338 × [miR-PD2] + 61.0927, where [miR-PD1] and [miR-PD2] represent the respective Cq values of the markers. This formal model allowed us to quantify the individual contribution of each biomarker and determine their collective effectiveness as reliable indicators for predicting the probability of Parkinson’s disease.

XENO-Q based nanopore sequencing

Target microRNA (miRNA) expression levels were determined using the XENO-Q assay coupled with Nanopore sequencing, which represents an adaptation of the standard XENO-Q reaction used for real-time PCR. The initial steps, including the hybridization of target miRNA with xeno-sensors and the subsequent synthesis of RNA-DNA chimera strands by Xeno-polymerase, were performed as previously described. Instead of real-time PCR, the barcode modules embedded within the newly synthesized RNA-DNA chimera strands were specifically amplified for 1 h using the XENO-ONT miRNA sequencing kit (CAT: 93666680, XENOHELIX). The amplified barcode modules were then processed to prepare the sequencing library using the SQK-LSK109 Ligation Sequencing Kit (Oxford Nanopore Technology, Oxford, UK). The processed library was loaded onto a MinION sequencer (Oxford Nanopore Technology) and subjected to sequencing analysis for 4 h. The raw sequencing data generated by the MinION were analyzed using the proprietary XENO-ONT Readout program (XENOHELIX) to determine the absolute copy numbers of the target miRNA.

XENO-R assay

To compare miRNA detection methods, we examined the expression patterns of miRNA using RNA purified from the whole blood (n=40). To evaluate the performance of adapter-ligation method, the XENO-R Green PCR kit (cat. 9366MSK, XENOHELIX) was utilized according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 100 ng of RNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA using the XENO-R Kit. A 1:20 dilution of reverse-transcribed (RT) products was used as the template for subsequent PCR amplification. The qRT-PCR was conducted using the Bio-Rad CFX connect System (Bio-Rad).

Small RNA blot analysis

Total RNA, including the small RNA fraction, was extracted from whole blood samples using a XENOPURE small RNA purification kit (XENOHELIX, cat. 93667873-WB). Extracted total RNAs were dissolved in 50% formamide loading buffer. The samples were then separated by denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis using 15% polyacrylamide gel. Following electrophoresis, RNA within the gel was transferred to a Nylon membrane (RPN303B, 103 Amersham, Little Chalfont, England) using a semi-dry transfer (Bio-Rad) for a duration of 1 hour. DNA probes complementary to the target miRNAs were synthesized and 5’-end-labeled with 32P using T4 Polynucleotide Kinase (PNK) and [γ-32P] -ATP. The nylon membrane containing the transferred RNA was incubated with labeled probes in the hybridization solution (AM8670, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for 12 h at 37 °C in a rotating incubator to allow hybridization of the radioactive probes with the target miRNAs. The probe-hybridized membrane was washed twice for 20 min each at 37 °C using a washing solution containing 2% Saline Sodium Citrate (SSC) and 0.1% Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate (SDS). The blot signals, corresponding to the hybridized miRNA-probe complexes, were detected and quantified using a phosphor imager scanner (Typhoon phosphorimager, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA).

Small RNA sequencing analysis

Illumina sequencing

The total RNA, encompassing the small RNA fraction, was extracted from whole blood samples obtained from Healthy Control (HC), Parkinson’s Disease (PD), and Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) patients using a XENOPURE small RNA purification kit (XENOHELIX, cat. 93667873-WB). Small RNA libraries were prepared using the TruSeq™ Small RNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina; San Diego, CA, USA), including adapter ligation, reverse transcription, PCR amplification, and size selection. The quality of the constructed small RNA libraries was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NextSeq 500 platform to generate 75 bp single-end reads. To ensure high-quality data for downstream analysis, raw reads were processed using Skewer to trim sequencing adapters and filter out low-quality bases. The processed clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using miRBase as the primary alignment resource. Mapping and quantification of miRNA expression levels were conducted using QuickMIRSeq with default parameters. To account for variations in sequencing depth across libraries, miRNA expression was normalized and reported as Reads Per Million (RPM). Differential expression analysis between biological groups (HC vs. PD, HC vs. AD, and PD vs. AD) was performed using NOISeq v2.16.0 (Bioconductor) with the following parameters k=NULL norm=“n” lc = 1 r = 20 adj = 1.5 a0per = 0.9 filter = 2 (if no replicates, k=NULL norm=“n” lc = 1 pnr = 0.2 nss = 5 v = 0.02) was used. Significant differential expressions were defined based on a threshold of an adjusted P-value < 0.05 and a fold change ≥ 2.0.

Nanostring

miRNA expression profiling was performed using the nCounter Human v3 miRNA Expression Assay Kit (NanoString Technologies, Seattle, WA, USA), targeting 827 human miRNAs. For each sample, 5 µL of concentrated total RNA was used as input. The assay incorporated a comprehensive internal control system, consisting of six positive, eight negative, and six ligation controls, supplemented by five mRNA reference controls (ACTB, B2M, GAPDH, RPL19, and RPLP0). Data processing and normalization were executed via nSolver Analysis Software v4.0 (NanoString Technologies). To mitigate platform-specific variations, raw counts were first normalized to the geometric mean of internal positive controls. Subsequent codeset content normalization was performed using the geometric mean of miRNAs with expression levels exceeding the Limit of Detection (LOD). Background noise was minimized by filtering out miRNAs falling below the empirically defined LOD thresholds (Low, Medium, and High LOD). Fold-change (FC) values were derived from the normalized count data through nSolver’s ratio analysis. To visualize differential expression patterns, heatmaps were generated using the “gplots” and “RColorBrewer” packages in R. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering was conducted based on Euclidean distance employing the “complete” linkage method.

Data processing and statistical analysis

Group comparison (ordinary one-way ANOVA)

To compare the mean expression levels (represented by Cq values) of target miRNAs across the three independent groups — Healthy Control (HC), Alzheimer’s Disease (AD), and Parkinson’s Disease (PD) patients — an Ordinary One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was performed. Following a determination of a statistically significant overall ANOVA result (P < 0.05), a Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post-hoc test was applied. This test was used for pairwise multiple comparisons between all possible group combinations (AD vs. PD, HC vs. PD, and HC vs. AD). P-values and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for pairwise differences between group means were calculated and reported.

Diagnostic performance assessment (ROC curve analysis)

The diagnostic capability of Cq values of each target miRNA to distinguish between disease groups (AD or PD) and the HC group was assessed using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The Area Under the Curve (AUC) was calculated for each ROC curve to quantify the overall discriminatory power of the microRNA, The ROC curve was generated by plotting sensitivity against (1 – specificity).

Software and visualization

All statistical analyses and graphical representations, including the dot plot visualization, Ordinary One-way ANOVA, Tukey’s post-hoc test, and ROC curve generation, were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.6.0).

Results

Overview and analytical performance of the XENO-Q platform

Conventional miRNA detection workflows rely on multiple enzymatic steps—such as 5′ and 3′ adapter ligation or 3′ poly(A) tailing, reverse transcription for cDNA synthesis, and one- or two-step PCR amplification, typically requiring approximately three to four hours to generate results (Fig. 1A) [21, 22]. In contrast, the XENO-Q platform employs a synthetic DNA sensor consisting of a target hybridization region and a scaffold region. The scaffold region encodes both Illumina sequencing indexing (Index-I5/Index-I7) and molecule-specific barcoding, enabling immediate Illumina or Nanopore sequencing assignment without secondary library construction [23, 24]. Furthermore, the 3′ end carries an amine modification that prevents extension, while internal XNA-based blocker sequences in the scaffold region suppress unintended PCR amplification of the sensor. The scaffold region of the sensor additionally incorporates ANA nucleotides at defined positions, rendering the unprotected single-stranded sensor highly susceptible to S1 nuclease digestion. Upon target binding, the miRNA itself serves as a primer for Xeno-polymerase–mediated extension across the complementary region of the sensor. This process generates a stable double-stranded hybrid exclusively when the correct miRNA–sensor interaction occurs. Xeno-polymerase is a type of DNA polymerase capable of recognizing RNA and XNA templates and treating them as conventional DNA substrates for nucleotide incorporation. After synthesis, a S1 nuclease digestion step removes all single-stranded RNAs, unbound sensors, and nonspecific bulged target-sensor hybrids [25]. A membrane-based filtration step retains these digested fragments, while the newly formed double-stranded sensor–miRNA hybrids pass through the membrane filter. During the final amplification step, only the lower chimera strand is capable of PCR extension, because the upper sensor strand remains blocked by the XNA-based blockers. This architecture mostly eliminates nonspecific amplification (Fig. 1B). Figure 1C presents a schematic overview of the complete XENO-sensor-based miRNA detection workflow, beginning with the collection of 200 µL of whole blood and extraction of small RNA using the XENOPURE small RNA kit, followed by quality control via NanoDrop quantification and gel electrophoresis. The purified RNA (100 ng per reaction) is then subjected to the XENO-Q reaction, which consists of three sequential steps: XENO-sensor hybridization, target-selective polymerization by Xeno-polymerase to synthesize RNA–DNA chimeras, and cleanup via S1 nuclease digestion and membrane filtration to remove non-specific hybrids and unbound sensors. The entire XENO-Q reaction is completed within 20 min, yielding a chimera strand that serves as the PCR template for downstream quantification. Finally, the workflow branches into one of three orthogonal detection platforms — qPCR, droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), or nanopore sequencing — each normalized to an A1 exogenous spike-in control to enable reliable inter-run quality control and absolute or relative miRNA quantification for clinical biomarker profiling.

Next, we characterized its analytical performance beginning with sensitivity, specificity, and target-selective amplification (Fig. 2A–D, and Supplementary Fig. 1–3). To validate the quantitative linearity of XENO-Q against absolute copy number measurements, cross-platform evaluation was performed using a synthetic artificial miRNA spike-in (A1) across a defined concentration range (6.25–100 fM; Fig. 2A). XENO-Q with qPCR readout produced a consistent and monotonic decrease in Cq values across the A1 concentration series, confirming concentration-dependent linear signal response to increasing target abundance. ddPCR-based absolute quantification of the identical spike-in series demonstrated a proportional increase in measured copy numbers (copies/µL) across the tested range, with reliable quantification observed from 50 fM onwards. Direct cross-platform comparison of ddPCR-derived absolute copy numbers and the corresponding XENO-Q Cq values, plotted as log(copy number) vs. Cq, revealed a highly linear relationship (y = − 2.279x + 29.93, R² = 0.996), demonstrating that XENO-Q Cq values are quantitatively proportional to independently measured absolute copy numbers across the dynamic range evaluated. This linearity was preserved even in complex biological matrices, such as whole blood and plasma, across a range of RNA inputs spanning several nanograms (Fig. 2B, and Supplementary Fig. 1A-B). To evaluate the quantitative performance and sensitivity of the XENO-Q platform, we assessed the relationship between input RNA amount and detection across multiple miRNA targets and sample types. Six endogenous miRNAs (hsa-miR-92a-3p, hsa-miR-191-5p, hsa-miR-144-3p, hsa-miR-451a, hsa-miR-182-5p, and hsa-let-7d-5p) were measured using seven serial dilutions of total RNA extracted from whole blood (10–1000 ng), and Cq values increased proportionally as input RNA decreased across all targets (Fig. 2B). Linear regression analysis confirmed robust linearity for each miRNA, with R² values ranging from 0.89 to 0.96, demonstrating consistent and reproducible detection across a broad dynamic range. To further validate quantitative performance across different biological matrices and individual donors, hsa-miR-486-5p expression was measured using varying amounts of RNA purified from both whole blood and plasma obtained from two independent donors (Supplementary Fig. 1A and 1B). In both sample types, Cq values shifted consistently and proportionally with input RNA amount, confirming that XENO-Q maintains reliable quantitative sensitivity regardless of the biological matrix or interindividual variation. Collectively, these results demonstrate that XENO-Q provides accurate, input-dependent miRNA quantification across a wide range of RNA amounts and sample sources, supporting its potential for robust liquid biopsy-based miRNA profiling. To validate the analytical accuracy and matrix robustness of XENO-Q in complex biological samples, a spike-and-recovery experiment was performed using plasma-derived RNA as the background matrix (Supplementary Fig. 1C and 1D). An exogenous synthetic spike-in (cel-miR-54-3p) was added at four defined concentrations (2, 10, 100, and 1000 fM) to identical aliquots of plasma RNA, and XENO-Q detection was performed simultaneously for both the spike-in target and an endogenous reference miRNA (hsa-miR-93-5p) present at a fixed abundance within the plasma matrix. XENO-Q detection of cel-miR-54-3p yielded a monotonic, concentration-dependent decrease in Cq values across the tested range, with a highly linear relationship between spike-in concentration and Cq output (y = − 3.94x + 34.64, R² = 0.99), confirming accurate and proportional signal recovery across a 500-fold dynamic range within the plasma matrix (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Concurrently, hsa-miR-93-5p Cq values remained stable across all four spike-in conditions, demonstrating that the presence of varying exogenous target concentrations did not perturb the detection of the endogenous reference target and that competitive interference or signal suppression within the plasma matrix was not observed under these conditions (Supplementary Fig. 1D).

To assess specificity, we tested eight highly homologous members of the let-7 family, which differ by as little as one nucleotide (Fig. 2C). XENO-Q selectively produced amplification only when the cognate target was present, while nearly all mismatched sequences failed to generate products. Only two near-isogenic miRNAs—hsa-let-7b-5p and hsa-let-7c-5p—showed slight overlap in signal due to their almost identical sequences (Fig. 2D). Quantitative readout demonstrated that the number of positive droplets for target miRNAs varied across species, with several targets achieving between 12,000 and 15,000 droplets (e.g., 12,535 for hsa-miR-92a-3p and 14,746 for hsa-miR-210-3p), while others showed lower but distinct positive populations (Supplementary Fig. 2). Non-target droplets remained consistently near background levels, generally below 100 droplets in most conditions. This yields a signal-to-noise ratio ranging from approximately 48-fold (for hsa-miR-132-3p) to over 459-fold (for hsa-miR-92a-3p), indicating effective suppression of unintended amplification. Specifically, hsa-miR-92a-3p generated 12,535 positive droplets with negligible non-target detection, which is well within the expected statistical noise threshold of ddPCR. High specificity was maintained across all validated miRNAs, confirming that each miRNA–sensor pair produces a precise reaction with minimal cross-interference from closely related sequences (Supplementary Fig. 2). Furthermore, under multiplex conditions, mixtures of 10 or 50 sensors produced consistent amplification exclusively from cognate miRNA–sensor pairs, with no detectable cross-interference (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Notably, a comparison with a conventional adapter-ligation PCR method demonstrated the superior capability of XENO-Q in identifying authentic biological variations. Across 40 individual whole-blood samples, adapter-ligation workflows produced tightly clustered Ct values with minimal inter-individual differences, indicating signal compression and limited responsiveness to biological variability. In contrast, XENO-Q consistently captured subject-specific miRNA expression profiles with a broader dynamic range, demonstrating its ability to capture critical biological information often obscured by conventional workflows. For instance, XENO-Q exhibited a wider spread in hsa-miR-144-3p expression (SD of Cq = ± 1.48) compared to the adapter-ligation PCR method (SD of Cq = ± 0.87), reflecting its enhanced sensitivity to genuine biological variance (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Taken together, these results suggest that XENO-Q achieves high sensitivity, reliable specificity, and accurate representation of biological differences.

To examine whether XENO-Q reflects true biological abundance of circulating miRNAs, we compared miRNA expression profiles obtained by small RNA blotting, Illumina sequencing, and XENO-Q-based quantification using whole-blood specimens (Fig. 3). Small RNA blot analysis was first performed using RNA isolated with two different commercial extraction kits to establish baseline expression patterns (Fig. 3A). Across both extraction conditions, hsa-miR-92a-3p was consistently detected at substantially higher levels than hsa-miR-451a and other tested miRNAs, indicating a robust and method-independent abundance pattern. To assess whether sequencing-based quantification is consistent with these observations, small RNA libraries were prepared from whole blood samples collected from 26 donors and analyzed using adapter-ligation Illumina sequencing. The top 20 most abundant miRNAs were identified and ranked according to read counts (Fig. 3B). In contrast to the blot-based results, Illumina sequencing consistently identified hsa-miR-451a as the most abundant species, while hsa-miR-92a-3p was markedly underrepresented. This discrepancy is consistent with known sequence- and ligation-dependent biases associated with adapter-ligation library preparation. We next evaluated the same panel of miRNAs using XENO-Q combined with nanopore sequencing (Fig. 3C). The XENO-Q workflow involves target-selective chimera strand generation followed by product ligation and barcode-indexed nanopore sequencing, enabling completion within approximately 4 h. Quantitative analysis revealed a distribution pattern consistent with the small RNA blot results rather than Illumina sequencing, with hsa-miR-92a-3p identified as the most abundant species relative to hsa-miR-451a (Fig. 3D). This observation was further validated by qRT-PCR using XENO-Q–amplified DNA, which reproduced the same relative abundance pattern (Fig. 3E), supporting that XENO-Q preserves relative stoichiometric relationships observed by direct hybridization-based measurement.

To further investigate factors that may contribute to differences in quantification across platforms, we systematically evaluated potential sources of variability associated with miRNA sequence composition and structural features using a panel of 104 human miRNA targets (Table S1). No statistically significant correlation was observed between GC content and XENO-Q Cq values (Pearson r = -0.164, p = 0.096), and stratification into low (< 40%), mid (40–60%), and high (> 60%) GC content groups similarly revealed no significant differences (one-way ANOVA, F = 1.477, p = 0.233; Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). Likewise, analysis of predicted secondary structure using minimum free energy (MFE) did not reveal a significant association with Cq values (Pearson r = 0.019, p = 0.849), and no significant differences were observed across structural categories (one-way ANOVA, F = 0.449, p = 0.640; Supplementary Fig. 4C, D). The distribution of GC content and MFE values across the dataset confirmed that a wide range of sequence compositions and structural features was represented (Supplementary Fig. 4E, F). Across this set of 104 miRNA targets, Cq values remained within a comparable range despite variation in sequence and structural properties (Table S1).

In addition to assessing sequence- and structure-related variability, we evaluated the robustness of XENO-Q in extracellular vesicle–derived miRNA samples. Exosomes isolated from human placental mesenchymal stem cells (hP-MSCs) were independently prepared from three separate culture batches and analyzed for four representative miRNAs ([26], Supplementary Fig. 5). XENO-Q amplification products were validated by droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), which showed clear separation of positive and negative droplet populations for all targets (Supplementary Fig. 5A–D). XENO-Q quantification revealed distinct abundance profiles among the four miRNAs, with hsa-miR-92a-3p consistently showing the highest expression and hsa-miR-27a-3p the lowest. This ranking pattern was reproducible across all three independent batches (Supplementary Fig. 5E). The coefficient of variation (CV) for each miRNA ranged from approximately 10% to 20%, with hsa-miR-92a-3p and hsa-miR-210-3p exhibiting the lowest variability (~ 11% and ~ 10%, respectively), and hsa-miR-27a-3p showing the highest variability (~ 19.75%) (Supplementary Fig. 5F). Taken together, these results demonstrate that adapter-ligation–based sequencing can substantially distort apparent miRNA abundance, particularly for preferentially ligated species such as hsa-miR-451a, whereas XENO-Q produces quantification patterns consistent with hybridization-based measurements and maintains reproducible performance across diverse sample types.

Cross-platform screening and validation of Parkinson’s disease–associated circulating miRNAs using XENO-Q

To identify robust circulating miRNA candidates associated with PD and Alzheimer’s disease (AD), we performed three independent Illumina small RNA sequencing analyses using total RNA extracted from whole blood. The sequencing cohorts consisted of PD (n = 11) and AD (n = 9) in the first dataset, PD (n = 5) and AD (n = 7) in the second, and PD (n = 10) and AD (n = 9) in the third, together with a total of 17 age-matched healthy controls (HC). Across all three datasets, global miRNA expression profiles were largely similar between PD and AD, showing only modest population-level fold changes (Fig. 4A). However, analysis of individual miRNAs revealed subsets displaying apparent differential regulation. Despite comparable sample processing workflows, both the identity and direction of up-regulated and down-regulated miRNAs varied markedly among the three sequencing analyses, with several miRNAs appearing PD-enriched in one dataset but showing the opposite trend or no observable change in another. In the first sequencing run, 86 miRNAs were up-regulated in PD and 63 in AD, with 383 miRNAs shared, while 63 miRNAs were down-regulated in PD and 37 in AD, with 76 commonly altered. In the second sequencing, 80 miRNAs were up-regulated and 141 were down-regulated in PD, whereas 144 miRNAs were up-regulated and 80 were down-regulated in AD, with 243 and 140 overlapping, respectively. In the third dataset, 16 miRNAs were up-regulated in PD, and no miRNAs were down-regulated, while 111 were up-regulated and 16 down-regulated in AD, with 111 and 10 shared miRNAs, respectively (Fig. 4B). Collectively, the weak concordance in both the direction and magnitude of miRNA changes across sequencing analyses indicates substantial technical variability, strongly suggesting that adapter-ligation–based small RNA sequencing introduces significant platform-dependent bias, particularly in low-input clinical RNA samples [27−29]. Despite the substantial variability observed across the three independent Illumina sequencing analyses, a small but reproducible subset of miRNAs consistently demonstrated PD-associated expression patterns. To identify these convergent markers, we generated a fold-change heatmap representing Log2 fold-change (FC) values across three comparative conditions: PD vs. HC (PD/HC), AD vs. HC (AD/HC), and PD vs. AD (PD/AD) (Fig. 4C), which highlights a group of PD-specific miRNAs showing reproducible expression elevation in PD relative to both HC and AD, including hsa-miR-320a, hsa-miR-423-3p, hsa-miR-194-5p, hsa-miR-18a-5p, hsa-miR-106b-5p, and hsa-miR-424-3p, which display strong positive Log2 FC values in PD/HC and PD/AD comparisons. Because the Illumina small RNA sequencing results showed limited reproducibility, we next used a hybridization-based NanoString platform to refine the selection of PD-associated circulating miRNAs. Total RNA from whole blood of PD (n = 10), AD (n = 10) patients and HC (n = 10) was analyzed using a multiplex NanoString miRNA panel, which quantifies targets by direct probe hybridization without enzymatic amplification [30, 31]. The expression analysis against HC identified a broad spectrum of dysregulated miRNAs across both neurodegenerative cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 6A). In the AD/HC and PD/HC comparisons, NanoString identified 59 miRNAs that were up-regulated in AD, 355 miRNAs were up-regulated in PD, and 201 miRNAs that overlapped in both disorders. Conversely, for miRNAs showing decreased expression relative to health controls, 355 miRNAs were down-regulated in AD, 59 were down-regulated in PD, and 183 miRNAs were commonly down-regulated in both groups (Supplementary Fig. 6B). To visualize the most relevant PD-associated candidates, we generated a heatmap of Log₂FC values for miRNAs that were consistently enriched in PD relative to both HC and AD (Supplementary Fig. 6C). Several miRNAs—including hsa-miR-423-3p, hsa-miR-652-3p, hsa-let-7d-5p, hsa-miR-191-5p, and hsa-miR-106b-5p—exhibited strong yellow signals in the PD/HC and PD/AD comparisons, while showing minimal or no changes in the AD/HC group. Next, we compared the Illumina sequencing data with the NanoString data to identify reproducible PD-associated circulating miRNAs. By selecting only those miRNAs consistently up-regulated in PD relative to both HC and AD, we derived a convergent candidate panel visualized as a Log₂FC heatmap. Several miRNAs, including hsa-miR-423-3p, hsa-miR-194-5p, hsa-miR-106b-5p, hsa-miR-424-3p, hsa-miR-183-5p, hsa-miR-151a-5p, and hsa-miR-126-3p, exhibited strong PD-specific elevation, while showing minimal or inverse changes in AD/HC (Fig. 4D).

Among the miRNAs consistently highlighted as PD-associated across both Illumina sequencing and NanoString hybridization analysis, hsa-miR-423-3p and hsa-miR-106b-5p were selected for further validation. Using the XENO-Q platform, we analyzed whole-blood samples from HC (n = 112) and PD (n = 78) patients. Of these 78 patients with PD, 47 (60.3%) were drug-naïve newly diagnosed patients, and 68 (87.2%) were at Hoehn & Yahr stage 1 or 2 (i.e., early stages) (Supplementary Table S2). Although both candidates showed the highest elevation in PD, the diagnostic performance of each marker was limited, yielding unexpectedly low AUC values (AUC < 0.65 and AUC < 0.72, respectively) (Fig. 4E). To identify robust circulating miRNA candidates with high disease specificity, we performed a convergent analysis by integrating results from both Illumina small RNA sequencing and NanoString hybridization platforms. We specifically prioritized miRNAs that exhibited reproducible up-regulation in patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD) relative to both healthy controls (HC) and patients with Alzheimer’s disease (AD). As shown in the Log₂ fold-change (FC) heatmap (Fig. 4D), a distinct subset of miRNAs—hsa-miR-144-3p, hsa-miR-107, hsa-miR-126-3p, and hsa-miR-151a-5p—displayed consistently elevated Log₂FC values in the PD/HC comparison while showing minimal changes in the AD/HC group, a pattern indicative of PD-preferential upregulation. Among these, hsa-miR-144-3p and hsa-miR-107 showed particularly strong PD/HC enrichment, whereas their PD/AD separation was comparatively modest, suggesting that their discriminatory value lies primarily in distinguishing PD from healthy controls rather than from AD. This profile underscores their potential as PD-specific biomarkers that can differentiate PD from other neurodegenerative mimics like AD. Subsequently, these four high-ranking candidates were individually validated using the XENO-Q platform in a larger clinical cohort (HC, n = 112; PD, n = 78). Consistent with the screening data, all four miRNAs demonstrated significant discriminatory power between the PD and HC groups (all P < 0.0001) (Fig. 4F). Notably, hsa-miR-144-3p and hsa-miR-107 exhibited superior diagnostic performance, yielding Area Under the Curve (AUC) values of 0.9067 and 0.8703, respectively. Furthermore, hsa-miR-126-3p (AUC = 0.8127) and hsa-miR-151a-5p (AUC = 0.7572) also maintained significant predictive capabilities, confirming the reliability of our cross-platform screening strategy, although their discriminatory power was relatively moderate compared to the top-ranking candidates.

In addition to the miRNAs commonly identified by both Illumina sequencing and NanoString hybridization analyses, we further tested additional high-ranking candidates uniquely highlighted by each platform. NanoString screening identified 15 miRNAs that were differentially expressed in PD compared to HCs (Supplementary Fig. 6C). Among the 15 candidates, two miRNAs—has-let-7b-5p and hsa-let-7d-5p—exhibited statistically significant elevation in PD and yielded meaningful diagnostic performance. hsa-let-7b-5p achieved an AUC of 0.906 and hsa-let-7d-5p also showed an AUC of 0.839 (Supplementary Fig. 6D). In contrast, one of the remaining candidates, hsa-miR-191-5p, demonstrated only marginal separation between HC and PD, yielding an AUC value of 0.746. From the Illumina analysis, we additionally evaluated hsa-miR-320a-3p and hsa-miR-18a-5p using the XENO-Q platform. However, hsa-miR-320a-3p demonstrated limited discriminatory power (AUC = 0.691), whereas hsa-miR-18a-5p exhibited comparatively stronger performance (AUC = 0.842) (Supplementary Fig. 7A). We next selected a subset of miRNAs previously reported in the literature as PD-associated biomarkers and examined them using the XENO-Q platform [32–35]. Eight candidates— hsa-miR-19a-3p, hsa-miR-210-3p, hsa-miR-451a, hsa-miR-22-3p, hsa-miR-20b-5p, hsa-miR-17-3p, hsa-miR-32-5p, and hsa-miR-26b-5p—were further evaluated with the HC and PD patients. Among these, hsa-miR-20b-5p exhibited the most promising diagnostic performance, achieving an AUC of 0.905. In contrast, the remaining miRNAs demonstrated limited separation between groups, with AUC values of 0.754 for hsa-miR-19a-3p, 0.659 for hsa-miR-210-3p, 0.701 for hsa-miR-451a, 0.801 for hsa-miR-22-3p, and 0.741 for hsa-miR-17-3p, 0.710 for hsa-miR-32-5p, and 0.719 for hsa-miR-26b-5p (Supplementary Fig. 7B).

Validation of selected miRNA biomarkers in differentiating HC, PD, and AD

We next performed a head-to-head comparison of all XENO-Q–validated candidates to determine which miRNAs most robustly discriminate PD from HC and AD. Five miRNAs (hsa-let-7d-5p, hsa-miR-144-3p, hsa-miR-107, hsa-miR-20b-5p, hsa-let-7b-5p) and a negative control hsa-miR-574-3p were quantified in 200 µL of whole blood samples from HC (n = 115), PD (n = 63), and AD (n = 78) (Supplementary Table S3). For each marker, we compared expression across the three groups and generated ROC curves for PD vs. HC (blue) and AD vs. HC (green), followed by calculation of sensitivity, specificity, likelihood ratio (LR), and AUC at the optimal Youden index cut-off (Fig. 5A–L, and Supplementary Fig. 8). Among all tested markers, hsa-let-7d-5p demonstrated strong discriminatory performance between neurodegenerative diseases (PD and AD) and HC, showing highly consistent elevation in PD and AD patients. Dot-plot distributions revealed a pronounced increase in both PD and AD compared with HC, suggesting a shared neurodegenerative signature rather than PD specificity. For PD vs. HC, hsa-let-7d-5p achieved an AUC of 0.884, with sensitivity 80.95%, specificity 86.46%, and an LR of 5.978. Performance remained comparably strong in AD vs. HC (AUC 0.865; sensitivity 83.33%; specificity 77.08%; LR 3.636) (Fig. 5A, and Supplementary Fig. 8). Post-hoc pairwise testing (Tukey’s multiple comparisons) revealed that both PD and AD had significantly higher hsa-let-7d-5p levels than HC (PD vs. HC, p < 0.0001; AD vs. HC, p < 0.0001). However, there was no significant difference between PD and AD (PD vs. AD, p = 0.2010). These results suggest that hsa-let-7d-5p is elevated in neurodegenerative disease in general but does not distinguish PD from AD (Fig. 5B). By contrast, hsa-miR-144-3p displayed more PD-biased behavior, with HC and AD values clustered more closely while PD values were distinctly elevated. In PD vs. HC, hsa-miR-144-3p reached an AUC of 0.931, with sensitivity 88.89%, specificity 80.21%, and LR 4.491, indicating strong rule-in utility for PD (Fig. 5C, and Supplementary Fig. 8). Post-hoc pairwise testing showed that hsa-miR-144-3p expression was significantly elevated in PD compared with both HC and AD (PD vs. HC, p < 0.0001; PD vs. AD, p < 0.0001). Additionally, AD also exhibited higher levels than HC (AD vs. HC, p < 0.0001), but to a lesser extent than PD. Unlike hsa-let-7d-5p, which failed to separate PD from AD, hsa-miR-144-3p demonstrated a clear PD-specific elevation, clearly differentiating PD from both HC and AD groups (Fig. 5D).

Other miRNAs showed moderate or weak performance. For instance, hsa-miR-107 and hsa-miR-20b-5p discriminated PD vs. HC with AUC 0.841 and 0.797, respectively (sensitivity 77.78% and 87.30%, specificity 75.65% and 62.61%, and LR 3.194 and 2.335), while AD vs. HC with AUC 0.776 and 0.730 (sensitivity 75.64% and 75.64%; specificity 63.48% and 64.35%; LR 2.071 and 2.122) (Fig. 5E and G, and Supplementary Fig. 8). Post-hoc pairwise testing further confirmed that these miRNAs were ineffective in distinguishing PD from AD, as the comparisons between PD and AD were not statistically significant (PD vs. AD: p = 0.1546 and p = 0.1934) (Fig. 5F and H). Despite strong expectations based on the prior result (Supplementary Fig. 6D), hsa-let-7b-5p did not exhibit satisfactory performance in the validation cohort. hsa-let-7b-5p showed modest separation between groups, yielding AUC = 0.781 (sensitivity 79.37%, specificity 64.35%, LR = 2.226) for PD vs. HC, and AUC = 0.675 for AD vs. HC. In addition, one-way ANOVA with post-hoc testing revealed significance for PD vs. HC and AD vs. HC, but the effect size was notably smaller than anticipated, indicating limited robustness (Fig. 5I and J, and Supplementary Fig. 8). Similar to hsa-miR-107 and hsa-miR-20b-5p, hsa-let-7b-5p demonstrated variable performance, possibly attributable to sample quality, cohort heterogeneity, or biological state-dependency, suggesting that these markers may be highly sensitive to physiological or preprocessing variability rather than constitutively dysregulated in PD. Consequently, hsa-let-7b-5p was not selected for further modeling. The negative control hsa-miR-574-5p showed no significant differences among HC, PD, and AD, and its ROC curves hovered around the diagonal (AUC ≈ 0.5), ruling out systematic technical biases in XENO-Q measurements (Fig. 5K and L, and Supplementary Fig. 8).

Diagnostic performance of hsa-miR-144-3p and hsa-let-7d-5p across neurodegenerative cohorts

To further evaluate the clinical utility of the selected miRNA biomarkers, we validated hsa-miR-144-3p and hsa-let-7d-5p using the XENO-Q platform in an expanded clinical cohort consisting of HC (n = 65), PD (n = 38; of whom 15 were diagnosed with PDD), and AD (n = 59)/Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB; n = 5) (Supplementary Table S4, Fig. 6). Both markers were significantly elevated in PD compared with HC (one-way ANOVA, p < 0.0001), indicating robust dysregulation associated with neurodegenerative disease. For hsa-let-7d-5p, ROC analysis demonstrated a PD/PDD vs. HC AUC of 0.9141, with sensitivity 82.76%, specificity 89.39%, and an LR of 7.803, indicating good discriminatory performance. However, performance against AD/DLB was limited (AUC 0.7204; sensitivity 64.06%, specificity 68.87%, and LR 1.964). The statistical comparison showed a less pronounced significance in the difference between PD/PDD and AD/DLB (p = 0.0046) compared to other group pairings, suggesting that hsa-let-7d-5p reflects a general neurodegenerative signal rather than PD-specific pathology (Fig. 6A-D). In contrast, hsa-miR-144-3p demonstrated superior discriminative power, yielding AUC 0.9458 for PD/PDD vs. AD/DLB, with sensitivity 84.38%, specificity 88.68%, and an LR of 7.453, outperforming hsa-let-7d-5p in distinguishing PD from AD/DLB. hsa-miR-144-3p also demonstrated meaningful discrimination between PD/PDD and HC (AUC 0.8682; sensitivity 71.70%, specificity 90.77%, LR 7.767), supporting its rule-in diagnostic capability (Fig. 6E-H). To determine whether combining miRNA markers could enhance diagnostic accuracy beyond individual measurements, we constructed a logistic regression model integrating hsa-miR-144-3p and hsa-let-7d-5p and evaluated classification performance across the same clinical cohorts (HC, PD/PDD, and AD/DLB). The final regression model identified hsa-miR-144-3p and hsa-let-7d-5p as significant predictors, with the model equation defined as Z = -1.5972 × [hsa-miR-144-3p] − 0.6338 × [hsa-let-7d-5p] + 61.0927, where [hsa-miR-144-3p] and [hsa-let-7d-5p] represent their respective Cq values. A higher Z score, driven by lower Cq values (higher expression), is associated with a higher probability of Parkinson’s disease. As shown in Fig. 6I-K, the optimized two-marker model substantially improved separation between PD/PDD and HC, achieving an AUC of 0.9493 (standard error [SE] 0.0189) with sensitivity 87.93%, specificity 89.39%, and a corresponding LR of 8.291, indicating excellent diagnostic strength. For the AD/DLB vs. HC, the combined model maintained a strong performance (AUC 0.8151; sensitivity 76.56%; specificity 71.21%; LR 2.660), reflecting reliable capture of neurodegeneration-associated signals. Importantly, the regression model also improved differentiation between PD/PDD and AD/DLB compared to individual single-marker analyses, yielding an AUC of 0.8273 (SE 0.0388), sensitivity 78.13%, specificity 77.59%, and an LR of 3.486, demonstrating enhanced clinical value toward differential diagnosis. These findings suggest that although hsa-miR-144-3p is the most robust individual marker, combining it with hsa-let-7d-5p provides superior diagnostic accuracy and could serve as a strong foundation for multi-marker PD panels.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and analytically validated XENO-Q, a bias-minimized miRNA detection platform engineered to overcome long-standing technical challenges of amplification-dependent assays. The platform demonstrated high sensitivity, strict sequence specificity, and a broad dynamic range, enabling accurate measurement of biologically meaningful miRNA variation. Standard detection platforms introduce substantial bias through amplification drift, adapter ligation efficiency, and nonlinear response at low input levels [36−39], and pre-analytical variability further limits cross-cohort reproducibility [40, 41]. Among alternative miRNA detection strategies, padlock probe–based rolling circle amplification (RCA) provides ligation-dependent single-nucleotide specificity; however, its performance is highly sensitive to target secondary structure and ligation junction composition, and its multi-step workflow (typically 4–6 h) limits scalability in clinical settings [42, 43]. CRISPR-Cas12a/13a–based platforms enable isothermal detection with very high analytical sensitivity (often reported at the attomolar level under optimized conditions), but most implementations require pre-amplification to achieve clinically relevant thresholds, thereby reintroducing sequence-dependent bias; additionally, Cas enzyme activity can be inhibited by components present in complex biological matrices such as whole blood [44−46]. Droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) offers highly sensitive absolute quantification (LOD ~ 50 copies/µL) based on Poisson statistics, but reverse transcription remains an intrinsic source of sequence-dependent bias, and the requirement for specialized partitioning instrumentation limits accessibility and increases per-sample cost [47–49]. In comparison, XENO-Q achieved an analytical LOD of approximately 10 fM with high analytical specificity, without requiring adapter ligation, reverse transcription, or pre-amplification. Target-selective chimera strand synthesis was completed within approximately one hour using standard laboratory equipment, and compatibility with standard qPCR instruments eliminates the need for specialized partitioning systems, potentially reducing per-sample cost and infrastructure requirements relative to ddPCR and CRISPR-based platforms. A summary comparison of key performance parameters across platforms is provided in Table S5.

In addition to demonstrating a reduction in ligation-dependent bias relative to conventional adapter-based approaches, the present study systematically evaluated potential sources of variability associated with miRNA sequence composition and structural features across a broader panel of 104 human miRNA targets (Supplementary Fig. 4, Table S1). No statistically significant correlation was observed between GC content and XENO-Q Cq values (Pearson r = -0.164, p = 0.096), and stratified analysis across low, mid, and high GC content groups similarly revealed no significant differences (one-way ANOVA, F = 1.477, p = 0.233; Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). Although a weak positive trend was noted, the effect size was negligible, suggesting that GC content is not a dominant determinant of assay variability within the evaluated range. Similarly, analysis of predicted secondary structure using minimum free energy (MFE) as an approximation of global thermodynamic stability did not reveal a significant association with Cq values (Pearson r = 0.019, p = 0.849), and no significant differences were observed across simple, moderate, and complex structural categories (one-way ANOVA, F = 0.449, p = 0.640; Supplementary Fig. 4C, D). Although MFE does not fully capture local structural accessibility at the probe-binding site, the absence of a detectable trend across this diverse miRNA panel suggests that global secondary structure is unlikely to be a major driver of assay variability under the tested conditions. With respect to potential Xeno-polymerase sequence preference, the absence of systematic Cq variation across miRNAs with diverse sequence compositions and structural contexts (Table S1) argues against strong sequence-dependent enzymatic bias under the evaluated conditions, although direct kinetic characterization across defined sequence contexts remains an important area for future investigation. Taken together, while subtle sequence-dependent effects cannot be excluded, these observations indicate that major sources of variability commonly associated with hybridization- and ligation-based miRNA detection — such as GC content dependence and global secondary structure effects — are not dominant contributors to assay variability in XENO-Q across the evaluated target set.

By integrating Illumina sequencing, NanoString hybridization screening, and extensive XENO-Q clinical validation, we identified a reproducible circulating miRNA signature with strong diagnostic value for PD. Across three independent Illumina sequencing analyses, global miRNA expression patterns showed minimal separation between PD and AD, and substantial inconsistency in fold-change directionality was observed both within and across datasets, reinforcing the well-recognized limitation that sequencing-based quantification is highly susceptible to ligation and size-dependent bias. Although NanoString screening reduced variability by eliminating enzymatic amplification, cross-platform comparison revealed that the overlap of PD-associated miRNAs remained modest, confirming that platform-dependent variability remains a major barrier to robust biomarker discovery. Direct XENO-Q quantification in two independent clinical cohorts demonstrated that several miRNAs highlighted by sequencing or literature failed to validate in a larger cohort — for example, hsa-let-7b-5p showed weak discriminative ability despite strong preliminary performance, suggesting cohort-specific sensitivity rather than constitutive PD dysregulation, with similar results observed for hsa-miR-107 and hsa-miR-20b-5p.

Among all tested candidates, hsa-miR-144-3p and hsa-let-7d-5p emerged as the most reliable circulating markers [50−55]. hsa-let-7d-5p is broadly implicated in neuroinflammatory pathways shared between PD and AD — it targets HMGA2 and has been reported as upregulated in both disease groups [53, 56] — consistent with our NanoString analysis showing elevation in both conditions (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. 6C), confirming that hsa-let-7d-5p reflects a pan-neurodegenerative signal rather than PD-specific pathology. By contrast, hsa-miR-144-3p is downregulated in MPTP-treated dopaminergic neurons, in which it exerts neuroprotective effects through at least two validated target genes: APP, whose suppression mitigates mitochondrial dysfunction specific to dopaminergic neurotoxicity [50], and FOXO1, a transcription factor that governs apoptosis, autophagy, and oxidative stress responses across multiple disease contexts [51]. This dual-target architecture positions hsa-miR-144-3p at the intersection of dopaminergic vulnerability and broader cellular stress pathways. hsa-miR-144-3p is also downregulated in the prefrontal cortex of PD patients but upregulated in the frontal cortex of AD patients and reduced in serum of drug-naïve PD patients [57−59] — a disease-divergent directionality recapitulated in the peripheral circulation. This mechanistic complementarity — hsa-let-7d-5p capturing a high-sensitivity pan-neurodegenerative signal and hsa-miR-144-3p anchoring PD-specific discrimination within the panel — provides a biologically coherent rationale for the superior diagnostic performance of the two-marker panel over either marker alone (Fig. 6).

The specificity values observed (62.6–86.5% at the Youden index–optimized cut-off) should be interpreted in the context of biological and methodological limitations inherent to blood-based biomarkers, including molecular overlap with other neurodegenerative conditions and healthy aging. Within the broader PD biomarker landscape, no blood-based approach has consistently achieved a specificity of 90% to date: CSF α-synuclein specificity ranges from 75 to 90% in systematic analyses [60]; CSF α-synuclein SAA demonstrates an inherent sensitivity–specificity trade-off [61], and skin α-synuclein SAA, while showing a pooled sensitivity of 0.89, yields specificity as low as 0.25 against MSA. A more informative metric is the positive likelihood ratio (LR+), which quantifies the shift in post-test probability independently of disease prevalence [62]. By the Jaeschke/McGee criteria, an LR + ≥ 5 represents moderate and clinically useful diagnostic evidence. hsa-let-7d-5p achieved an LR + of 5.978, meeting this threshold, while hsa-miR-144-3p (LR + = 4.491) approached it as a single marker. The two-marker combinatorial panel further improved LR + to 8.291 for PD vs. HC and 3.486 for PD vs. AD, corresponding to clinically meaningful shifts in post-test probability in a movement disorders referral setting. These findings support the panel’s utility as an adjunctive triage tool where invasive CSF sampling or specialized imaging is unavailable, rather than as a standalone diagnostic evaluated against an isolated specificity threshold.

Several limitations of the present study warrant acknowledgment. All clinical specimens were recruited and validated within a single tertiary referral center, and the absence of an independent external validation cohort limits the generalizability of the identified miRNA panel. Although repeated 10-fold cross-validation was implemented to mitigate overfitting, performance metrics — including AUC values for the two-marker panel — should be interpreted as likely reflecting the upper bound of real-world performance, as site-specific differences in recruitment criteria, blood collection protocols, and patient demographics may introduce systematic variation not captured by internal validation alone. The cross-sectional design precludes longitudinal assessment of miRNA dynamics across disease stages, and the limited sample sizes of the PDD (n = 15) and DLB (n = 5) subgroups render those comparisons exploratory. Prospective replication in geographically and ethnically diverse multicenter cohorts with harmonized pre-analytical protocols will be necessary to establish the clinical robustness of these findings.

Collectively, these findings underscore the critical importance of analytical reliability in miRNA biomarker discovery and establish that the XENO-Q platform enables reproducible identification of clinically meaningful signatures. The combined two-marker signature (hsa-miR-144-3p + hsa-let-7d-5p) provides strong discriminatory accuracy for PD screening and differential diagnosis against AD, representing a promising foundation for minimally invasive, scalable PD diagnostics. Its bias-minimized quantification framework positions XENO-Q as a suitable candidate for prospective multicenter validation, with harmonized pre-analytical protocols to mitigate interlaboratory variability. Future work should investigate the biological mechanisms underlying PD-selective regulation of hsa-miR-144-3p, assess its predictive utility in early or prodromal PD, and validate the panel across multi-center cohorts.
