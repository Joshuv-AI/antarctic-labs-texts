Depression trajectory modifies the association between estimated glucose disposal rate and incident stroke: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Xiaohan Yu, Junjun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInsulin resistance is an established risk factor for stroke. The estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) serves as a surrogate marker for insulin sensitivity. Depression is linked to both insulin resistance and cerebrovascular disease. However, whether depression trajectories modify the association between eGDR and stroke risk remains unknown.MethodsThis prospective cohort study used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Participants aged ≥ 45 years with baseline eGDR measurements (2011), complete depression assessments (2011, 2013, 2015), no prevalent stroke (2011–2015), and follow-up data (2018 or 2020) were included. Depression trajectories were identified using latent class growth analysis based on the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale. Covariates were selected a priori with the aid of a directed acyclic graph. Multivariable logistic regression estimated odds ratios (ORs) for incident stroke, stratified by depression trajectory; the primary model adjusted for sociodemographic and lifestyle factors. Interaction was assessed using likelihood ratio tests.ResultsAmong 4,592 participants (mean age 58.3 years; 54.9% female), 430 stroke cases (9.4%) occurred. Three trajectories were identified: Low (65.1%), Moderate (28.1%), and High (6.8%). In the primary model (Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, marital status, education, residence, smoking status, and alcohol consumption), each standard deviation increase in eGDR was associated with lower stroke odds overall (OR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.60–0.73). This inverse association was strongest in the Low trajectory (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.50–0.67) and was attenuated in the Moderate (OR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.63–0.88) and High (OR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.55–0.99) trajectories. The attenuation with greater cumulative depressive burden was supported by significant interactions using binary trajectory classification (Low vs. Moderate/High; P = 0.021) and continuous mean CESD-10 score (P = 0.035); the three-class interaction test was borderline (P = 0.067).ConclusionsHigher eGDR was associated with reduced stroke risk; this protective association was progressively attenuated with greater cumulative depressive symptom burden, being strongest among individuals with persistently low depressive symptoms. These findings suggest that cumulative depressive burden may attenuate the inverse association between insulin sensitivity and stroke risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06144-6.

Introduction

Stroke remains the leading cause of death and disability-adjusted life-years lost in China, with over 2 million new cases annually [1, 2]. The burden of stroke in China is projected to increase substantially through 2050 due to population aging and the high prevalence of modifiable risk factors [34]. Identifying novel predictive markers and modifiable determinants of stroke risk is therefore a public health priority [5].

Insulin resistance, characterized by diminished cellular responsiveness to insulin signaling, has emerged as an important contributor to cerebrovascular disease [6, 7]. Beyond its central role in glucose metabolism, insulin resistance promotes atherosclerosis through mechanisms including endothelial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, and dyslipidemia [8, 9]. The estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR), derived from waist circumference, hypertension status, and glycated hemoglobin, provides a validated and clinically practical surrogate marker of insulin sensitivity [10, 11]. Recent population-based studies have demonstrated that lower eGDR levels are associated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease and stroke [12, 13]. A nationwide Swedish cohort study found that each category increase in eGDR was associated with progressively lower stroke risk among individuals with type 2 diabetes [14]. Similarly, studies using China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) data have confirmed the inverse association between eGDR and incident stroke in middle-aged and older Chinese adults [15, 16].

Depression is another well-established risk factor for stroke. Meta-analyses of prospective cohort studies have consistently demonstrated that depression increases stroke risk by approximately 34% to 45% [17, 18]. The underlying mechanisms linking depression to stroke are multifaceted, involving hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation, sympathetic nervous system activation, heightened inflammatory responses, and adverse health behaviors [19, 20]. Notably, insulin resistance and depression share common pathophysiological pathways, including chronic low-grade inflammation, HPA axis hyperactivity, and neuroplasticity impairment [21, 22]. A growing body of evidence supports a bidirectional relationship between insulin resistance and depression, with each condition potentially exacerbating the other [23, 24].

Despite these parallel lines of research, the potential interaction between insulin sensitivity and depression in determining stroke risk has not been investigated. Importantly, depression is not a static condition; rather, depressive symptoms exhibit heterogeneous longitudinal trajectories that may differentially influence cardiovascular outcomes [25, 26]. Studies using latent class growth analysis have identified distinct depression trajectories in population-based cohorts, revealing heterogeneous patterns ranging from persistently low to chronically elevated depressive symptoms [27, 28]. These trajectory-based approaches capture the dynamic nature of depressive symptoms and may reveal associations that are obscured by single-timepoint assessments [29].

Biologically, it is plausible that chronic depression may be associated with attenuation of the inverse association between insulin sensitivity and stroke risk. Persistent depression is associated with sustained HPA axis activation, elevated cortisol levels, and chronic inflammation [30, 31], which could counteract the vascular protective effects of higher insulin sensitivity. Furthermore, depression impairs adherence to healthy lifestyle behaviors and medication regimens [32], potentially diminishing the clinical benefit of favorable metabolic profiles. However, whether depression trajectories modify the association between eGDR and stroke risk remains unknown.

Therefore, we conducted a prospective cohort study using data from CHARLS to investigate the association between baseline eGDR and incident stroke, and to examine whether this association is modified by longitudinal depression trajectories. We hypothesized that: (1) higher eGDR would be associated with lower stroke risk in the overall population; and (2) the inverse association between higher eGDR and stroke risk would be attenuated or absent among individuals with persistently high depressive symptoms. Findings from this study may inform targeted stroke prevention strategies that account for both metabolic and psychological risk profiles.

Methods

Study design and population

This prospective cohort study used data from the CHARLS, a nationally representative survey of Chinese adults aged 45 years and older. CHARLS employed multistage probability sampling to recruit participants from 150 counties across 28 provinces [33]. Baseline data were collected in 2011, with follow-up surveys conducted biennially. Detailed descriptions of the study design and sampling procedures have been published previously.

For the present analysis, we included participants who met the following criteria: (1) aged 45 years or older at baseline; (2) available eGDR data in 2011; (3) complete CESD-10 depression assessments at three time points (2011, 2013, and 2015) for trajectory classification; (4) no self-reported physician-diagnosed stroke during the baseline period (2011–2015); and (5) available stroke outcome data at follow-up (2018 or 2020). Exposure: estimated glucose disposal rate

The eGDR was used as the primary exposure variable. eGDR is a validated surrogate marker of insulin sensitivity, originally derived from euglycemic-hyperinsulinemic clamp studies in the Pittsburgh Epidemiology of Diabetes Complications cohort [10]. The formula was calculated as follows: eGDR (mg/kg/min) = 21.158 − (0.09 × waist circumference) − (3.407 × hypertension) − (0.551 × HbA1c), where waist circumference is measured in centimeters, hypertension is coded as 1 (yes) or 0 (no), and HbA1c is expressed as a percentage. Higher eGDR values indicate greater insulin sensitivity, whereas lower values indicate insulin resistance. The validity of eGDR as a surrogate of insulin sensitivity has been confirmed against the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp, the reference standard for insulin sensitivity measurement, in both diabetic and non-diabetic populations [10]. Baseline eGDR was analyzed both as a continuous variable (per standard deviation [SD] increase) and as quartiles (Q1–Q4), with Q1 (lowest eGDR, highest insulin resistance) serving as the reference category.

Effect modifier: depression trajectory

Depression trajectories were identified using latent class growth analysis (LCGA) based on CESD-10 scores measured at three time points (2011, 2013, and 2015). The CESD-10 is a validated 10-item self-report scale assessing depressive symptoms over the past week, with total scores ranging from 0 to 30 and higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms [34]. For participants who answered 8 or 9 of the 10 items, scores were prorated by dividing the sum of answered items by the number of items answered and multiplying by 10. Participants who answered fewer than 8 items were considered to have missing depression data and were excluded from the analysis.

LCGA is a data-driven, person-centered method that identifies subgroups of individuals following qualitatively distinct patterns of change over time, without requiring pre-specified symptom cutoffs or external classification criteria. Trajectory classes are derived entirely from the longitudinal patterns of observed CESD-10 scores within the analytic sample. LCGA was performed using the lcmm package in R, with models specifying one to five latent classes [35]. The optimal number of classes was determined based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), entropy, and clinical interpretability. Each participant was assigned to the trajectory class for which their estimated posterior probability of membership was highest; the entropy value of 0.768 for the three-class solution indicates adequate class separation and assignment certainty. LCGA simultaneously models the level (intercept) and rate of change (slope) of depressive symptoms over time within each latent class, thereby capturing both overall symptomatic severity and longitudinal trajectory patterns.

Outcome: incident stroke

The outcome was incident stroke occurring between baseline (2011) and follow-up (2018 or 2020). Stroke was ascertained through self-reported physician diagnosis. At each survey wave, participants were asked: “Have you been diagnosed with stroke by a doctor?” Participants who reported a new stroke diagnosis at the 2018 or 2020 wave, but not during the baseline period (2011–2015), were classified as incident stroke cases. The CHARLS questionnaire does not distinguish between ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke subtypes.

CHARLS collects disease status at each biennial survey wave without precise event dates. Accordingly, person-time could not be calculated, and we used logistic regression rather than Cox proportional hazards regression to estimate associations between eGDR and incident stroke.

Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on established risk factors for stroke and potential confounders of the eGDR-stroke relationship. Demographic variables included age (continuous), sex (male/female), marital status (married/other), education (below college/college and above), and residence (rural/urban) [36–39]. Lifestyle factors included smoking status (never/former/current) and alcohol consumption (yes/no) [40, 41]. Comorbidities included hypertension, dyslipidemia, heart disease, and kidney disease, all ascertained through self-reported physician diagnosis [42]. Diabetes mellitus was not included as a covariate because eGDR is derived from HbA1c, and adjustment for diabetes would introduce collinearity and potential overadjustment bias. Body mass index (BMI) was not included in the primary analysis due to its potential role as a mediator, but was examined in sensitivity analyses. Covariate selection was guided by a prespecified directed acyclic graph (Supplementary Figure S1), which informed the minimally sufficient adjustment set for estimating the total effect of eGDR on stroke. Physical activity, although a potential confounder, was not adjusted for in the primary models owing to substantial non-response in the 2011 physical activity module, and was instead examined in a sensitivity analysis.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized according to depression trajectory using means and standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Differences across trajectory groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Missing data for covariates were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations with the mice package in R, generating five imputed datasets with 20 iterations [43]. All binary covariates were imputed using logistic regression.

Multivariable logistic regression models estimated odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between eGDR and incident stroke. Three sequential models were constructed: Model 1 adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 additionally adjusted for marital status, education, residence, smoking status, and alcohol consumption; Model 3 further adjusted for hypertension, dyslipidemia, heart disease, and kidney disease. Model 2 corresponds to the minimally sufficient adjustment set derived from the directed acyclic graph — with the exception of physical activity, which could not be reliably ascertained owing to module non-response and was therefore examined separately — and was prespecified as the primary model for estimating the total effect of eGDR on stroke; Model 3 additionally conditions on hypertension—a component of the eGDR formula—and on comorbidities that may lie on the insulin-resistance–stroke pathway; it therefore estimates a partial (direct) rather than total effect and is presented as a sensitivity analysis rather than as the basis for primary inference. Analyses were conducted in the overall sample and stratified by depression trajectory. Due to the small sample size in the High trajectory group (n = 314, 57 events), a simplified covariate adjustment strategy was applied: education was excluded (due to complete separation), and the comparison model was restricted to age, sex, marital status, residence, hypertension, and heart disease to ensure adequate events per variable. Results were pooled across imputed datasets using Rubin’s rules.

Multiplicative interaction between eGDR and depression trajectory was assessed by including cross-product terms and tested using likelihood ratio tests. Dose-response relationships were examined using restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of eGDR distribution, with the median value as the reference. To indicate the data support for each curve, the corresponding distribution of eGDR was displayed beneath each spline, and curves were plotted over the 2.5th–97.5th percentile range of the observed eGDR distribution. Subgroup analyses assessed the consistency of the eGDR-stroke association across demographic and clinical characteristics. Sensitivity analyses included: (1) using unified simplified covariates across all trajectory groups; (2) excluding participants with baseline heart disease; (3) additionally adjusting for BMI; (4) additionally adjusting for diabetes mellitus; and (5) using binary depression trajectory classification (Low vs. Moderate/High); and (6) using the mean CESD-10 score across three time points (2011, 2013, and 2015) as an alternative depression measure, examined as tertile groups, binary classification (mean score < 10 vs. ≥10), and a continuous variable in interaction analyses. Physical activity (MET-minutes per week) was additionally adjusted for among participants with available activity data as a further sensitivity analysis. E-values were calculated to assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding. As an exploratory analysis to examine whether depressive symptoms act as a mediator rather than a modifier of the eGDR–stroke association, we additionally conducted a causal mediation analysis with baseline (2011) eGDR as the exposure, incident stroke as the outcome, and post-baseline mean CESD-10 (2013 and 2015) as the candidate mediator, so that the mediator followed the exposure in time; the average causal mediation (indirect) effect, average direct effect, and proportion mediated were estimated under the Model 2 covariate set using nonparametric bootstrap with 1,000 resamples. Because causal mediation relies on assumptions that cannot be verified in observational data, this analysis was regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.3, with two-sided P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

Of 17,705 CHARLS 2011 baseline participants, 4,592 were included after excluding those with prevalent stroke (n = 743), loss to follow-up (n = 3,056), incomplete CESD-10 data (n = 6,847), missing eGDR (n = 2,373), and age below 45 years (n = 94) (Fig. 1). The mean age was 58.3 (SD 8.4) years, and 54.9% were female. During follow-up, 430 participants (9.4%) developed incident stroke. Baseline eGDR ranged from 1.13 to 17.81 mg/kg/min, with quartile cut-points of 7.23, 9.99, and 11.11 mg/kg/min. Baseline characteristics according to depression trajectory are presented in Table 1. Missing covariate data were limited (0.47%–2.33% per variable; Supplementary Table S1). Notably, stroke incidence increased substantially across trajectories (Low: 7.2%, Moderate: 12.2%, High: 18.2%; P < 0.001), whereas baseline eGDR (continuous) did not differ significantly across trajectories (P = 0.147), although the distribution across quartiles showed slight variation (P = 0.028).

Depression trajectories

Latent class growth analysis identified three depression trajectories (Fig. 2): Low (65.1%, n = 2,988; mean CESD-10 score ranging from approximately 4.7 to 5.2 across the three waves), Moderate (28.1%, n = 1,290; mean score ranging from approximately 11.5 to 12.4 across waves), and High (6.8%, n = 314; mean score ranging from approximately 18.6 to 20.7 across waves). Trajectory class membership was determined by maximum posterior probability assignment. The three classes were derived from longitudinal symptom patterns in the data without reference to pre-specified CESD-10 cutoff values. The three-class solution demonstrated adequate model fit (BIC = 86,826.80; entropy = 0.768; Table S2). Depressive symptom scores remained relatively stable across the three assessment time points within each trajectory class (2011, 2013, and 2015).

Association between eGDR and incident stroke stratified by depression trajectory

In the overall sample, each SD increase in eGDR was associated with 34% lower odds of stroke in the primary model (Model 2: OR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.60–0.73), with a significant dose-response trend across quartiles (Q4 vs. Q1: OR = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.26–0.47; P for trend < 0.001); the estimate was attenuated under the additionally adjusted Model 3 (OR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.68–0.89; Table 2). The Model-2 covariate set corresponds to the minimally sufficient adjustment set specified by the prespecified directed acyclic graph (Supplementary Figure S1).

Critically, the strength of this inverse association varied across depression trajectories. In the primary Model 2, higher eGDR was most strongly associated with reduced stroke risk in the Low trajectory (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.50–0.67 per SD; Q4 vs. Q1: OR = 0.23, 95% CI: 0.14–0.37; P for trend < 0.001), and the association was attenuated, though still significant, in the Moderate trajectory (OR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.63–0.88 per SD; Q4 vs. Q1: OR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.27–0.72; P for trend < 0.001). In the High trajectory, the inverse association was attenuated but remained significant under the primary Model 2 (OR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.55–0.99 per SD; Models 1 and 2 OR = 0.75 and 0.74, respectively), and was reduced to the null only under the additionally adjusted Model 3 (OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.67–1.52 per SD). Given the small size of this subgroup (n = 314, 57 events) and that Model 3 additionally conditions on hypertension—a component of the eGDR formula—and on other comorbidities, this attenuation is not interpreted as a true absence of association. The attenuation of the eGDR–stroke association with greater depressive burden was supported by a significant interaction using binary trajectory classification (Low vs. Moderate/High; P = 0.021; Table S3) and using continuous mean CESD-10 score (P = 0.035; Table S4); the three-class interaction test was borderline (P = 0.067).

At the descriptive level, stroke cases tended to have lower eGDR than non-stroke participants in the Low (P < 0.001) and Moderate (P < 0.001) trajectories, but not in the High trajectory (P = 0.06; Fig. 3); these unadjusted comparisons are presented for description only. Figure 4 visualizes the differential effect sizes across trajectories, clearly demonstrating the attenuation of eGDR’s protective effect with increasing depression severity. Figure 5 presents the RCS analyses together with the eGDR distribution in each trajectory: a monotonic inverse dose-response relationship is evident in the Low trajectory, where data are dense, and is attenuated in the Moderate and High trajectories, with the sparse data in the High trajectory (n = 314) yielding a wide confidence band and an unstable curve that should not be over-interpreted. Tests for non-linearity were non-significant for all trajectories (P > 0.05; Table S5), confirming that linear models adequately captured the relationships. Figure 6 further illustrates this pattern: stroke incidence decreased from 13.2% (Q1) to 3.4% (Q4) in the Low trajectory, whereas the High trajectory showed persistently elevated incidence across all quartiles (range: 14.5%–23.7%) without a clear protective gradient.

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of findings (Tables S3 and S6–S10). Results remained consistent when using unified simplified covariates (Table S6), excluding participants with baseline heart disease (Table S7), additionally adjusting for BMI (Table S8) or diabetes (Table S9), and using binary trajectory classification (Table S3). E-values for the overall (Model 2) association were 1.76 for the point estimate and 1.62 for the confidence interval bound (Table S10). Additional adjustment for physical activity among the 1,982 participants with available activity data did not materially alter the association (OR per SD 0.63–0.64 vs. 0.66 in the full sample; Table S11).

To further assess robustness, we conducted an additional sensitivity analysis using the mean CESD-10 score across three time points as an alternative depression measure (Table S4). When modeled as a continuous variable, the interaction between eGDR and mean CESD-10 score was statistically significant (P = 0.035). Interactions using tertile and binary grouping approaches did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.140 and P = 0.051, respectively). Notably, within the highest mean CESD-10 tertile (n = 1,530), eGDR remained significantly associated with lower stroke risk (OR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.62–0.83). This finding is consistent with the primary analysis: this tertile estimate closely matches that within the LCGA-defined High trajectory (OR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.55–0.99), indicating that both approaches identify an association that is attenuated relative to the Low trajectory (OR = 0.58) but not abolished in individuals with high cumulative depressive burden. The highest tertile is nonetheless a compositionally heterogeneous group, combining the LCGA-defined High trajectory individuals (n = 314, mean CESD-10 ≈ 19–21) with a substantial proportion of Moderate trajectory individuals with relatively higher scores (mean CESD-10 ≈ 12); this mixing across the symptom-burden gradient, together with the limited power of single-cutoff groupings, helps explain why the interaction reached significance for the continuous mean CESD-10 score (P = 0.035) and the binary trajectory contrast (P = 0.021) but not for mean CESD-10 tertiles (P = 0.140) or the mean CESD-10 binary cutoff (P = 0.051). Latent class growth analysis adds to this by resolving the symptom-burden gradient and isolating a small, persistently and severely symptomatic phenotype (n = 314) that fixed mean-score cutoffs blur together with less severely affected individuals. In the exploratory causal mediation analysis, the eGDR–stroke association was almost entirely a direct effect (average direct effect ≈ total effect), whereas the indirect effect operating through post-baseline depressive symptoms was negligible and non-significant (proportion mediated − 1.7%; 95% CI − 4.6% to + 1.0%; P = 0.15), indicating that depressive symptoms did not act as an important mediator of the eGDR effect (Table S12).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study of 4,592 middle-aged and older Chinese adults, we found that higher eGDR was associated with lower risk of incident stroke, consistent with prior evidence [12, 14]. More importantly, we identified for the first time that depression trajectory modified this association. Specifically, in the primary model the inverse association of higher eGDR with lower stroke risk was strongest in the Low trajectory (OR = 0.58 per SD) and was progressively attenuated in the Moderate (OR = 0.74) and High (OR = 0.74) trajectories; the complete attenuation to the null seen in the High trajectory under the additionally adjusted Model 3 (OR = 1.01) most plausibly reflects the small size of this subgroup together with adjustment for hypertension (a component of the eGDR formula) and other comorbidities, rather than a true absence of association. This graded attenuation was supported by significant interactions using binary trajectory classification (P = 0.021) and continuous mean CESD-10 score (P = 0.035). Notably, this graded modification by depression trajectory was not mirrored by clinical cardiovascular risk factors; among the conventional subgroups examined, only age showed a significant interaction with eGDR (P = 0.044), whereas sex, dyslipidemia, heart disease, residence, smoking, and alcohol consumption did not (all P for interaction > 0.05). These findings suggest that greater cumulative depressive symptom burden may attenuate the inverse association between higher insulin sensitivity and stroke risk.

The interaction between eGDR and depression trajectory reached statistical significance when trajectory was operationalized as a binary contrast (Low vs. Moderate/High; P = 0.021) and when cumulative symptom burden was modeled continuously (mean CESD-10 score; P = 0.035), whereas the three-class interaction test was borderline (P = 0.067), consistent with the limited power of the small High trajectory group (n = 314, 57 events). The consistency of this gradient across operationalizations—together with the monotonically protective eGDR–stroke relationship evident in the descriptive data (Fig. 6) and the restricted cubic spline curves (Fig. 5) in the Low and Moderate trajectories and its attenuation in the High trajectory—indicates that cumulative depressive burden shapes the strength of the eGDR–stroke association. Because the marginal distribution of baseline eGDR was similar across trajectory groups (P = 0.147; Table 1), this reflects a difference in the strength of association across strata rather than in exposure range; the underlying causal interpretation rests on the prespecified directed acyclic graph (Supplementary Figure S1) and the formal interaction analyses.

Our finding that higher eGDR is associated with reduced stroke risk is consistent with accumulating evidence from multiple cohorts. A Swedish nationwide study of 104,697 individuals with type 2 diabetes reported that compared with the lowest eGDR category (< 4 mg/kg/min), those with eGDR > 8 mg/kg/min had a 40% lower risk of stroke (hazard ratio (HR) = 0.60) [14]. Similarly, studies using CHARLS data have demonstrated significant inverse associations between eGDR and cardiovascular outcomes, with each 1-SD increase in eGDR associated with a 16–17% reduction in cardiovascular disease risk [12, 13]. The effect size observed in our overall analysis (Model 2 OR = 0.66 per SD) aligns well with these prior findings.

While several mechanisms have been proposed to link higher insulin sensitivity with lower cerebrovascular risk, the critical question raised by our findings is why this inverse association was substantially attenuated in individuals with persistently high depressive symptoms. Chronic depression is characterized by sustained activation of the HPA axis, leading to elevated cortisol levels and the development of glucocorticoid resistance [44, 45]. Prolonged HPA axis dysregulation promotes oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and chronic low-grade inflammation, thereby accelerating atherosclerosis and compromising vascular function [46, 47]. Insulin resistance and inflammation share bidirectional relationships: elevated proinflammatory cytokines impair insulin signaling pathways, while insulin resistance itself promotes endothelial dysfunction and vascular injury [6, 48]. In individuals with persistently high depressive symptoms, the overwhelming inflammatory burden and HPA axis dysregulation may diminish the favorable metabolic profile reflected by higher eGDR. In addition to these biological pathways, behavioral mechanisms may also contribute to the attenuation of eGDR’s protective effect in the High depression trajectory group. Chronic depression is strongly associated with unfavorable health behaviors that independently increase stroke risk [49, 50]. The Heart and Soul Study demonstrated that depressive symptoms prospectively predicted a range of adverse health behaviors over a 5-year follow-up period [51]. Importantly, these behavioral factors largely mediated the association between depression and subsequent cardiovascular events [52]. Even if an individual maintains relatively preserved insulin sensitivity (higher eGDR), the concurrent presence of physical inactivity, poor diet, medication non-adherence, and other adverse health behaviors associated with chronic depression may offset the potential favorable metabolic profile reflected by higher eGDR.

These findings carry important clinical and public health implications. First, our results suggest that assessing depression trajectory, rather than single-point depression measurement, may enhance cardiovascular risk stratification. Individuals with persistently elevated depressive symptoms represent a high-risk subgroup in whom the expected inverse association between favorable metabolic profiles and stroke risk is markedly attenuated, highlighting the need for integrated approaches that address both metabolic and mental health simultaneously [49]. Current guidelines recommend depression screening for patients with cardiovascular disease, yet optimal management strategies remain under investigation [53]. Second, the identification of depression trajectory as a potential effect modifier underscores the importance of early intervention for chronic depressive symptoms. Exercise has been recognized as a promising intervention that may simultaneously improve insulin sensitivity, reduce inflammation, and alleviate depressive symptoms [52, 54]. Finally, these results suggest that stroke prevention strategies should be tailored according to mental health status, as uniform approaches targeting metabolic factors alone may be insufficient for individuals experiencing chronic depression.

Our study has several strengths. First, this is, to our knowledge, the first study to examine whether depression trajectory modifies the association between insulin sensitivity and stroke risk. This approach moves beyond the traditional focus on single-point depression assessment and provides novel insights into how the longitudinal pattern of depressive symptoms may influence metabolic-cerebrovascular relationships. Second, we utilized data from a large, nationally representative cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, enhancing the generalizability of our findings. Third, the application of latent class growth analysis to identify distinct depression trajectories based on three assessments spanning 4 years (2011, 2013, and 2015) captures the dynamic and heterogeneous nature of depressive symptom patterns, which has been shown to be more predictive of health outcomes than single-point measurements [25, 26]. Fourth, the robustness of our findings was supported by multiple sensitivity analyses, including alternative covariate adjustment strategies, exclusion of participants with baseline cardiovascular disease, and different trajectory classification approaches, all of which yielded consistent results. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis using mean CESD-10 scores across three time points as an alternative to LCGA-derived trajectories yielded consistent findings: the interaction between eGDR and cumulative depressive symptom burden was statistically significant when mean CESD-10 was modeled as a continuous variable (P = 0.035), while tertile and binary grouping approaches did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.140 and P = 0.051, respectively). As detailed in the Results, this pattern reflects the lower power of coarse single-cutoff groupings, relative to the continuous score and the binary trajectory contrast, to capture a graded modifying effect of cumulative symptom burden (Table S4).

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, stroke events were ascertained through self-report of physician diagnosis, which may be subject to recall bias and could not distinguish between ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke subtypes. However, self-reported stroke has been validated in previous studies and shown to have acceptable accuracy [55]. Second, the use of logistic regression rather than Cox proportional hazards models precluded the estimation of time-to-event relationships; this was necessitated by the biennial data collection design of CHARLS, which captures disease status at each wave without precise event dates. Third, the relatively small sample size in the High depression trajectory group (n = 314, events = 57) necessitated a simplified covariate adjustment strategy in this subgroup, which differs from models applied to the Low and Moderate trajectory groups and limits strict comparability of effect estimates across trajectories. We acknowledge that modest inverse associations were observed in Models 1 and 2 within this group (OR = 0.75 and 0.74, respectively), which attenuated to null following additional adjustment for hypertension and cardiovascular comorbidities in Model 3. Whether these variables act as confounders or play a more complex role in this specific subgroup cannot be fully determined from the current data. Because hypertension is a component of the eGDR formula and the remaining comorbidities may lie on the insulin-resistance–stroke pathway, the primary (Model 2) analysis did not adjust for them (see Statistical Analysis); the Model-3 estimate in this small subgroup should therefore be interpreted with caution. A sensitivity analysis using unified simplified covariates across all trajectory groups yielded consistent results (Table S6). Fourth, although we adjusted for a comprehensive set of covariates and conducted E-value to quantify the potential impact of unmeasured confounding, the observed E-values indicate only moderate robustness, and residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as dietary patterns, genetic susceptibility, and subclinical vascular disease cannot be excluded. Fifth, eGDR was calculated at baseline only, and we could not assess how changes in insulin sensitivity over time might interact with depression trajectories. In addition, physical activity could be assessed for only 1,982 participants (43.2%), because the remainder did not complete the 2011 physical activity module; although additional adjustment for physical activity among those with available data did not materially change the association (Supplementary Table S11), residual confounding by physical activity cannot be excluded. Sixth, although the prospective design with baseline eGDR measurement supports a temporal sequence consistent with the proposed direction of effect, the possibility that insulin resistance partly mediates rather than independently modifies the depression–stroke relationship cannot be excluded. Depression may promote insulin resistance through lifestyle-related pathways such as physical inactivity and poor diet, and the extent to which effect modification persists under this alternative causal framework cannot be formally tested with the current data. Conversely, because the depression trajectory was measured contemporaneously with baseline eGDR, we cannot fully exclude that depression lies partly on the eGDR–stroke causal pathway; in that case, stratified estimates would approximate controlled direct effects and could be susceptible to mediator–outcome confounding. In an exploratory causal mediation analysis (Supplementary Table S12), the eGDR–stroke association was almost entirely direct (ADE ≈ total effect), and the indirect effect through post-baseline depressive symptoms was negligible and non-significant (proportion mediated − 1.7%; P = 0.15), consistent with depression acting as an effect modifier rather than a mediator of the eGDR effect. We therefore interpret our findings as hypothesis-generating effect modification that requires confirmation in studies designed to distinguish mediation from modification. Seventh, because eGDR is derived from HbA1c, waist circumference, and hypertension status, it reflects a composite of insulin resistance and glycemic control — two physiologically distinct constructs. While eGDR was originally derived from euglycemic-hyperinsulinemic clamp data and validated as a surrogate of insulin sensitivity,10 the inclusion of HbA1c in its formula means that its associations with stroke cannot be entirely attributed to insulin resistance in isolation, as glycemic control may contribute independently. By the same reasoning, hypertension is also a constituent component of the eGDR formula; adjustment for hypertension in the additionally adjusted Model 3 therefore conditions on part of the exposure and is liable to overadjustment. We did not separately adjust for HbA1c in our models because HbA1c is a constituent component of the eGDR formula; such adjustment would introduce severe collinearity and constitute overadjustment, distorting rather than clarifying the exposure–outcome relationship. The observed associations should therefore be interpreted as reflecting the combined influence of insulin sensitivity and glycemic status. Eighth, although the CHARLS dataset includes a survey item on antidepressant use, reported antidepressant use in our analytic sample was extremely sparse, with fewer than a handful of individuals recorded as users; this precludes its reliable inclusion as a covariate and represents a potential source of residual confounding. Moreover, the eGDR equation was derived and validated against the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp in predominantly Western (US) cohorts and has not, to our knowledge, been formally validated against clamp-derived insulin sensitivity in a Chinese population; although its components (waist circumference, blood pressure, and HbA1c) are routinely measured and eGDR has been widely applied in CHARLS-based cardiovascular research, its quantitative performance as a surrogate of insulin sensitivity in Chinese adults remains to be established, and any resulting non-differential measurement error would be expected to bias the observed associations toward the null. Finally, our findings are derived from a Chinese population, and caution should be exercised when generalizing to other ethnic groups.

Future studies should replicate these findings in larger and more diverse samples—particularly individuals with persistently high depressive symptoms, and using clinically adjudicated stroke subtypes—and should use repeated measures of both eGDR and depression to clarify whether insulin resistance mediates or modifies the depression–stroke relationship and to probe the underlying biological pathways.

Conclusion

In this prospective cohort study of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, we found that higher eGDR was associated with reduced risk of incident stroke in the overall population. However, this protective association was strongest among individuals with persistently low depressive symptoms and was progressively attenuated with greater cumulative depressive symptom burden. These findings suggest that persistently elevated depressive symptom burden may be associated with attenuation of the inverse association between higher insulin sensitivity and stroke risk, highlighting the importance of integrated approaches that address both metabolic and mental health in stroke prevention strategies.
