Assessing Malawi’s animal health surveillance system using the FAO surveillance evaluation tool: gaps, strengths, and opportunities for one health resilience
Authors: Yusuf Mtila, Stewart Chikomola, Eric Kazadi Kawaya, Thoko Kapalamula, Sabenzia Wekesa, Fredrick Kivaria, Gladson Kamwendo, Julius Chulu, Marvin Phonera, Upile Kachepa, Edwin Nkhulungo, Michael Luwe, Peter Mwale, Gisella De Oliveira Dias Da Silva, Henson Kainga, Ihab El Masry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and methodsAnimal health surveillance is essential for early disease detection, outbreak response, and the prevention of zoonoses. In June 2024, Malawi conducted its first national assessment of its animal health surveillance system using the FAO Surveillance Evaluation Tool (SET), specifically the 2024 SET Evaluation Guide, version 2 to evaluate institutional, operational, and technical capacities. A participatory assessment was undertaken by experts from the Department of Animal Health and Livestock Development (DAHLD) and FAO, involving document reviews, stakeholder interviews, and site visits in seven districts. The SET was used to score 96 indicators across various domains on a scale of 1 (no capacity) to 4 (advanced).ResultsThe results indicated low overall performance. The weakest domains were active surveillance (5%), internal communication (12.6%), and risk assessment (16.7%). Relative strengths were observed in external communication (50%) and information systems (44.3%), though these were still below optimal levels. The system suffers from significant workforce shortages, with approximately 33% of officer positions vacant, placing a heavy burden on inadequately resourced community-based assistants. Laboratory capacity was found to be centralized and limited, with regional laboratories offering only basic diagnostic services. Data management is predominantly paper-based, and formal risk-based surveillance plans and feedback mechanisms are lacking.Conclusion and recommendationsIn conclusion, while Malawi’s animal health surveillance system has an established institutional framework, it is weakly structured and inconsistently operationalized. This is characterized by limited proactive surveillance, significant workforce gaps, under-resourced laboratory diagnostics, and fragmented data and communication channels. Nonetheless, a foundation for improvement exists, including legal provisions, a dedicated Epidemiology Unit within DAHLD, and a tiered surveillance workforce. Prioritized recommendations include developing and operationalizing a national surveillance strategy, strengthening laboratory and data systems, addressing critical workforce gaps, and integrating animal health surveillance into broader One Health and emergency preparedness frameworks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05662-x.

Introduction

Animal health surveillance is a cornerstone for the early detection of the animal diseases including zoonoses, and for enabling rapid outbreak response and prevention [1, 2]. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) such as Malawi, where more than 80% of the population relies on agriculture and livestock contributes substantially to agricultural gross domestic product, resilient surveillance systems are essential for food security, market access, livelihoods and public health [3].

Globally, more than 70% of emerging infectious diseases are zoonotic, often originating at wildlife-livestock - human interfaces that are common in Malawi [4]. Experiences from Eastern and Southern Africa have shown that syndromic and event-based surveillance can strengthen detection of high impact zoonoses such as anthrax, Rift Valley fever (RVF), and Rabies [5–9]. Yet, in Malawi, surveillance remains rudimentary and largely project-driven, constrained by limited sustainable financing, weak diagnostic capacity, fragmented data systems, and under-resourced frontline veterinary services [7, 9, 10]. Endemic and transboundary animal diseases including foot-and-mouth disease (FMD), Rabies, East Coast fever (ECF), and African Swine fever (ASF) compound these challenges despite the existence of legal instruments such as the Control and Diseases of Animals Act (1969).

To enable countries to systematically evaluate and strengthen their systems, the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) developed the Surveillance Evaluation Tool (SET), a standardized Excel-based instrument. The tool assesses animal health surveillance performance across 96 indicators in 19 categories spanning seven thematic areas [11, 12]. SET builds on global frameworks including OASIS, the Laboratory Mapping Tool, and the Epidemiology Mapping Tool, and was designed to support the Global Health Security Agenda (GHSA). By providing a structured scoring approach, it allows identification of strengths, diagnosis of priority gaps, and alignment of surveillance with One Health and risk-based principles.

This paper reports on Malawi’s first national assessment mission using the 2024 SET Evaluation Guide version 2. It also interprets the findings in relation to comparable South-eastern Africa regional experiences and proposes sequenced actions to strengthen the country’s animal health surveillance system into a more agile, risk-based, and One Health aligned framework.

Materials and methods

Assessment tool and team composition

Malawi’s Surveillance Evaluation Tool (SET) mission used the standardized methodology described in the FAO Surveillance Evaluation Tool Evaluation Guide, 2024 edition, version 2 [13]. The evaluation team comprised ten experts from the Department of Animal Health and Livestock Development (DAHLD), the FAO Emergency Centre for Transboundary Animal Diseases (ECTAD) in Malawi, the FAO Subregional Office for Eastern Africa, and FAO Headquarters. The team included epidemiologists, veterinarians, laboratory specialists, livestock experts, and surveillance professionals, with representation from the Central Veterinary Laboratory (CVL), regional veterinary laboratories in Blantyre and Mzuzu, Agricultural Development Divisions (ADDs), district offices, and academic institutions. The ten-member evaluation team was responsible for reviewing evidence, conducting interviews and site visits, assigning indicator scores by consensus, documenting score justifications, and formulating recommendations. This multidisciplinary composition enabled a comprehensive, One Health-oriented assessment.

The FAO SET framework encompasses 96 indicators grouped into 19 categories and seven thematic areas, as detailed in Table 1. In addition to these area and category scores, It evaluates cross-cutting performance attributes that reflect the effectiveness, quality, and sustainability of surveillance. The FAO SET Evaluation Guide identifies the core stakeholder groups to be engaged during national assessments [14]. For this study, stakeholder-specific question sets complementary to the SET guide version 2 and further adapted to Malawi’s context are provided in Supplementary File 1, alongside the FAO SET Evaluation Guide. The 42 stakeholders interviewed provided evidence on governance, reporting pathways, laboratory services, field implementation, intersectoral collaboration, and data management, they did not independently assign scores. Ethical considerations

The mission received formal endorsement from Malawi’s national authorities, including the Department of Animal Health and Livestock Development (DAHLD) and the Public Health Institute of Malawi (PHIM). All stakeholders interviewed including officers from DAHLD, public health services, laboratories, district offices, and community-level agents were briefed on the purpose of the evaluation and provided informed consent before participation. Data collected were anonymized and used exclusively for assessment purposes. Findings were presented during a restitution meeting, where participants validated factual interpretations and provided feedback on the proposed recommendations.

SET evaluation process

The Malawi SET mission was implemented from 10 to 21 June 2024 and followed a participatory, structured methodology comprising four main phases:

Preparation and document review

The team familiarized themselves with the SET toolkit and reviewed relevant legislation, surveillance plans, standard operating procedures and previous evaluation reports. A request-for-information questionnaire was circulated to national authorities to collect baseline data.

Stakeholder interviews and site visits

Forty-two stakeholders at central, district and community levels were interviewed. Sites visited included districts in each of Malawi’s three regions: Mzuzu, Mzimba and Karonga in theNorth, Lilongwe and Nkhotakota in the Centre, and Blantyre and Chikwawa in the South. Regional laboratories in Blantyre and Mzuzu were visited separately. Interviews elicited qualitative insights into structure, functionality, resource availability and intersectoral coordination, while quantitative data on workforce numbers, diagnostic capacity and reporting practices were collected using structured questionnaires. These interviews, together with document review and direct observations, informed the evidence base used by the evaluation team during the scoring process. Scoring session

For each of the 96 SET indicators, the evaluation team assigned a score from 1 (no capacity) to 4 (advanced capacity). Scores were based on documentary evidence, interview findings, site observations and consensus discussion among the evaluators. The SET workbook then normalized each indicator score to a percentage scale, where lower values indicate absent or weak capacity and higher values indicate more advanced capacity.

Category scores were generated by aggregating the normalized scores of indicators within each category. Thematic-area scores were generated by aggregating the relevant category or indicator-level scores within the seven SET areas. Performance-attribute scores were calculated separately by the SET attribute module using pre-defined cross-cutting mappings of relevant indicators. Because attributes cut across thematic areas, an indicator may inform both a structural category score and one or more attribute scores. The same interpretive cut-offs were applied to area, category and attribute scores: <25% very weak, 25–50% weak, 51–75% moderate and > 75% strong.

Analysis and recommendations

Scores were analysed to identify systemic strengths, capacity gaps and priority actions. Recommendations were prioritized by urgency and sequencing: Priority 1 actions were considered immediate system-strengthening needs, Priority 2 actions were medium-term measures requiring additional coordination or resources, and Priority 3 actions were longer-term enabling reforms. Prioritization considered feasibility, resource implications and dependence on other actions. Findings and draft recommendations were shared with stakeholders during a restitution workshop to confirm factual interpretation and assess feasibility; the workshop did not replace the evidence-based scoring process or alter raw indicator scores unless factual corrections were agreed by the evaluation team.

Results

Surveillance framework and organization

Malawi’s animal health surveillance system operates through a tiered administrative structure comprising central, district (intermediary) and community levels. The organizational structure of the Department of Animal Health and Livestock Development (DAHLD), including the positioning of the Epidemiology Unit, is shown in Fig. 2.

At the central level, surveillance is coordinated under the Animal Health and Regulatory Services Division of DAHLD. Within this division, a dedicated Epidemiology Unit exists led by a Chief Epidemiologist and supported by a Principal Epidemiologist and a Biometrician with the mandate to plan, coordinate, analyse, and disseminate surveillance information nationally.

Figure 3 integrates the hierarchical structure and functional information flow of Malawi’s animal health surveillance system. Disease signals originate from communities, farms, wildlife interfaces, markets, abattoirs and borders, then move through field or extension-planning-area staff, district animal health offices, regional laboratories, the Central Veterinary Laboratory and national DAHLD structures.

The Epidemiology Unit is responsible for the design of national surveillance frameworks, development of technical guidelines, collation and analysis of surveillance data from districts, and preparation of official reports to national decision-makers and international bodies such as the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) and FAO. It also provides feedback to districts and collaborating partners on surveillance findings and outbreak investigations.

Parallel to the Epidemiology Unit, the Central Veterinary Laboratory (CVL) plays a technical and diagnostic role, focusing on laboratory investigations for both active and passive surveillance samples. CVL undertakes diagnostic testing using bacteriology, parasitology, and limited molecular/serological methods for priority diseases including ASF, FMD, rabies and trypanosomiasis. Importantly, CVL channels laboratory results and feedback to submitting officers and district teams, supporting case confirmation and response actions.

Laboratory capacity remains centralized, with CVL not yet accredited and lacking an electronic Laboratory Information Management System (LIMS). Thirteen laboratory technicians support eight veterinarians. Two Regional Veterinary Laboratories (Blantyre and Mzuzu) provide basic diagnostic services. Blantyre uniquely performs rabies testing, while Mzuzu focuses on parasitology and bacteriology. Each is staffed by one to two veterinarians and four to five technicians.

At the district level, District Animal Health and Livestock Development Officers (DAHLDOs) oversee surveillance implementation. District teams consist of Senior Assistant Veterinary Officers (SAVOs), Assistant Veterinary Officers (AVOs), and Animal Health Surveillance Assistants (AHSAs). Of the approximately 700 surveillance personnel nationwide, 228 AVO positions were vacant, representing one-third of the workforce, with vacancies most acute in remote districts. Operational challenges at this level include shortages of sampling kits and cold chain supplies, limited transport, and the absence of digital data entry platforms.

At the community level, surveillance is driven primarily by AHSAs and is occasionally complemented by Health Surveillance Assistants (HSAs) from the Ministry of Health. Although Community Animal Health Workers (CAHWs) are not formally integrated into the national animal health surveillance architecture, their potential role in first-line disease reporting and vaccination support mirrors practices in other low-resource settings. Reporting currently relies heavily on phone calls and WhatsApp.

Intersectoral collaboration with public health, wildlife, and environmental sectors remains ad hoc, with no formal memoranda of understanding or joint surveillance frameworks. Surveillance activities at border posts and abattoirs are largely limited to visual inspection, with minimal sampling capacity or diagnostic confirmation.

System performance and scoring

Scores across the seven thematic areas ranged from 0% to 50%. Active surveillance received a score of 5%, reflecting the limited availability of formalized, disease -specific and risk-based active surveillance plans for priority threats. The examples considered during the assessment included serological surveys, targeted sampling, sentinel surveillance, and other planned approaches; however, the SET interpretation does not imply that every disease requires the same active surveillance method. Rather, it assesses whether the surveillance approach selected for each priority disease is epidemiologically justified, documented, resourced, and implemented. Risk assessment and internal communication scored 16.7% and 12.6%, respectively, indicating that formal risk analysis and feedback loops are virtually non-existent. The highest scoring areas were external communication (50%) largely due to use of WhatsApp groups and unstructured newsletters and information systems (44.3%), reflecting the nascent roll-out of digital reporting within selected pilot districts. In general, laboratory operational aspects scored higher (41.6%) than technical (24.1%) or analytical (22.2%) aspects. Surveillance data collection scored 24.6%, underscoring the reliance on paper forms and inconsistent reporting from field officers. Workforce management scored 29.1%, driven by the existence of some job descriptions and training modules, whereas training provision scored only 16.7%, highlighting the lack of continuous professional development. No area reached the “moderate” performance threshold (> 50%), highlighting the need for comprehensive system strengthening.

Performance attributes

Performance attributes derived from SET scores highlighted systemic weaknesses. Representativeness (14%) and utility (17%) were particularly low, indicating that surveillance data neither capture all relevant populations nor support decision-making. Data quality scored 19%, reflecting inconsistencies, missing values and absence of quality control. Flexibility (22%) was limited, as the system is poorly equipped to incorporate new diseases or data sources. Rapidity (35%) and stability (30%) were slightly higher but remain suboptimal. Acceptability scored 37%, which falls within the SET “weak” category under the same cut-offs used for thematic areas and categories. This score should therefore be interpreted as weak but relatively higher willingness of stakeholders to participate in surveillance compared with several other attributes, rather than as evidence of strong acceptability. Priority recommendations for strengthening Malawi’s animal health surveillance system

Implementation of these recommendations should be accompanied by named institutional responsibilities, measurable outputs and routine monitoring indicators so that progress can be tracked through annual surveillance reviews and national One Health coordination mechanisms.

Legal reform was retained as a Priority 3 recommendation not because it is unimportant, but because the existing Control and Diseases of Animals Act provides a basic mandate under which immediate operational improvements can begin. Priority 1 actions, including a national surveillance strategy, laboratory strengthening, workforce gap reduction and digital reporting, can be initiated under the current framework while generating practical evidence to inform later legal modernization. Legal reform remains a cross-cutting requirement for long-term sustainability, enforcement and alignment with international standards.

Discussion

The SET assessment provided the first comprehensive evaluation of Malawi’s animal health surveillance system. The findings mirror experiences from other LMICs but also illuminate country-specific opportunities. On the positive side, Malawi benefits from a legally mandated surveillance framework, a tiered administrative structure with agents at central, district and community levels, and established surveillance points such as dip tanks, veterinary stations and border posts. Collaborations with development partners through initiatives like the Global Health Security Program and the Pandemic Fund present strategic windows to leverage resources and expertise.

However, the system remains weakly structured and inconsistently operationalized. The absence of a formal national surveillance strategy harmonized standard operating procedures and routine performance monitoring limits coordinated planning, early warning and rapid response. Although the Epidemiology Unit provides an institutional anchor within DAHLD, its coordination mandate requires stronger formalization through SOPs, routine reporting calendars and documented feedback mechanisms. Similarly, the relationship between epidemiological coordination and the diagnostic responsibilities of the Central Veterinary Laboratory should be clarified through standard workflows that link sample submission, laboratory confirmation, outbreak investigation and district-level feedback.

The most important weaknesses identified by SET are therefore structural rather than isolated operational gaps. Workforce shortages reduce the capacity to collect representative data and investigate outbreaks; under-resourced laboratories delay confirmation, paper-based reporting weakens timeliness and data quality, and limited risk assessment constrains prioritization. Similar challenges have been reported in Tanzania before adoption of the District Health Information System 2 (DHIS2) and in Mozambique and Zambia where field networks exist but are hampered by inadequate resources and coordination. The regional comparison in this paper does not intend to imply that these countries have all been evaluated using SET. Rather, it situates Malawi’s findings within published regional experiences showing similar bottlenecks in coordination, field resourcing, data validation, community-level surveillance and multisectoral response [15, 16]. These experiences reinforce the need for stronger digital reporting, formal feedback loops and One Health coordination in Malawi.

Data management remains a critical weakness. Paper-based forms and ad-hoc sharing through WhatsApp constrain representativeness, timeliness, traceability and data quality. Although DHIS2 rollout for animal health is planned, its impact will depend on standardized case definitions, harmonized reporting tools, data-quality checks, trained data managers and clear feedback mechanisms. External communication scored highest in the SET but it remains below the level required for a mature surveillance system; informal WhatsApp groups and occasional newsletters cannot substitute for structured bulletins, annual reports and formal feedback loops.

The evaluation underscores the need for a paradigm shift towards risk based, One Health oriented surveillance. Proactive surveillance should be prioritized for high impact zoonoses and transboundary animal diseases, but methods must be tailored to disease epidemiology, transmission pathways, diagnostic feasibility and control objectives. Serological surveys may be appropriate for selected pathogens and objectives, whereas event-based reporting, targeted sampling, syndromic surveillance, abattoir surveillance, vector surveillance or sentinel approaches may be more suitable for others. Formal integration of community-based reporting, together with structured collaboration among animal health, human health, wildlife and environmental sectors, would improve representativeness and early detection. Digital reporting and stronger laboratory networks would improve timeliness and data quality, while consent, confidentiality and feedback to data providers should remain integral to future evaluations.

The study has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. SET scores reflect the evidence available during the mission and the consensus judgement of the evaluation team; they do not measure disease incidence or outbreak burden directly. Site visits were selected to provide national and regional representation but could not cover all districts, production systems or border points. Nevertheless, triangulation across documents, interviews, field observations, laboratory visits and stakeholder validation strengthens confidence that the identified weaknesses reflect system-wide constraints rather than isolated local problems.

In summary, Malawi’s animal health surveillance system has substantial weaknesses, but it also has important building blocks for developing a modern and effective surveillance architecture. Implementing the prioritized recommendations, particularly a national surveillance strategy, laboratory and workforce investment, digital reporting, and routine communication platforms, will be essential for transitioning from a reactive, paper-based system to one that proactively safeguards animal and human health and contributes to national resilience.
