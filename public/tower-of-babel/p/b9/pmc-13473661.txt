Modulating mechanisms of marine hydrodynamics on global coastal carbon burial
Authors: Linjing Ren, Hao Yang, Haifei Yang, Ruiting Gu, Zhenchang Zhu, Ning Zhao, Ya Ping Wang, Lijun Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Coastal blue-carbon burial is vulnerable to climate change, yet marine hydrodynamic effects remain poorly quantified. Here we estimate hydrodynamic effects on carbon accumulation rate (CAR), by integrating global observations across mangroves, marshes and seagrasses with multiple drivers. Across all ecosystems, hydrodynamics explains 11.8–16.2% of CAR variability, whereas within individual ecosystems it explains 14.5–31.4%, 13.2–13.9% and 14.7–17.7% in mangroves, marshes and seagrasses, respectively. Limited tidal ranges leave low-CAR, inundation-tolerant seagrasses more common, whereas larger tidal ranges support broader intertidal habitats for high-CAR mangroves and marshes. Wave forcing shows contrasting relationships with CAR: positive in mangroves and seagrasses, potentially linked to organic matter inputs, but negative in marshes, likely reflecting lateral export. As sea-level rise accelerates and storms intensify, carbon-release risks may become heterogeneous, with marshes most vulnerable and mangroves and seagrasses more conditional and regionally uncertain. These findings provide a hydrodynamic framework for predicting future carbon dynamics.

Introduction

The escalating global climate crisis, driven by anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions, necessitates the urgent development of effective carbon mitigation strategies1,2. In this context, coastal ecosystems, including mangrove forests, tidal marshes, and seagrass meadows, are globally recognized as critical “blue carbon” sinks due to their remarkable capacity for long-term carbon sequestration3,4. The study of coastal carbon accumulation rate (CAR) is therefore fundamental to understanding and quantifying the role of blue-carbon ecosystems in climate change mitigation5,6. These ecosystems accumulate substantial amounts of organic carbon (OC) in their sediments at rates significantly higher than those observed in terrestrial forests7,8. This enhanced capacity, driven by high annual biomass inputs and slower organic matter decomposition, enables more efficient and sustained carbon storage, thereby cementing their critically important role in the global carbon budget9.

Recent advancements have greatly enhanced our understanding of CAR across diverse coastal habitats5,6,10–13. Growing evidence indicates that CAR is profoundly modulated by marine hydrodynamics, including tides, waves and sea-level rise14–16. Substantial research has focused on vertical carbon cycling processes, particularly vegetation-mediated trapping and stabilization of OC in sediments13,17–19. However, coastal wetlands also laterally export significant quantities of OC as particulate organic carbon (POC), dissolved organic carbon (DOC), and dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) via hydrodynamic forcing20,21. This lateral flux typically represents 10–40% of their net primary productivity22,23. Despite its significance, a major knowledge gap persists regarding how marine hydrodynamics regulate the lateral transport and ultimate fate of OC20–23. In particular, the process of outwelling, the lateral export of OC from coastal wetlands to adjacent waters, as initially hypothesized by Odum24, remains poorly understood in terms of its hydrodynamic controls, despite its potential importance in linking coastal and offshore carbon cycling25. This gap is particularly pivotal in high-energy coastal ecosystems, where strong hydrodynamic forcing such as tidal currents and storm surges can remobilize sedimentary OC, thereby modulating net OC accumulation15,26,27. Blue-carbon ecosystems continuously exchange organic material with adjacent waters through tides and waves over diel to seasonal timescales, resulting in net OC import or export that remains inadequately constrained28,29.

The stability of carbon-rich blue-carbon ecosystems is increasingly challenged by intensifying hydrodynamic forces under global climate change16,30. Traditionally, enhanced wave and tidal energy are viewed as drivers of carbon loss, not only through direct sediment erosion but also by triggering a cascade of biogeochemical responses31–33. Specifically, intensified hydrodynamics can facilitate the release of dissolved organic carbon via enhanced porewater exchange and advective transport34. Moreover, these forces modulate soil redox potential by regulating oxygen delivery, thereby shifting the microbial balance toward aerobic respiration and accelerating organic matter decomposition35. Under high-energy conditions, soil aggregate breakdown can reintroduce protected carbon into active microbial loops36–38. Crucially, such hydrodynamic impacts extend beyond immediate physical disruption; instead, sediment grain size often serves as the integrated sedimentary outcome of hydrodynamic processes, reflecting the cumulative energy environment that dictates long-term carbon preservation27.

Far from being a purely destructive force, this hydrodynamic sorting of sediments plays a dual role in coastal carbon dynamics, particularly when fine-grained mineral particles are abundant. In settings with sufficient sediment supply, increased inundation frequency driven by relative sea-level rise enhances the deposition of both mineral and organic sediments and the creation of accommodation space16. Hydrodynamic forces also act as a delivery mechanism, transporting fine mineral particles (e.g., clays), which can bind with organic matter in the water column to form protective organo-mineral complexes, facilitating the stabilization of organic matter against decomposition39–41. These processes are highly context-dependent: in high-energy tidal marshes dominated by autochthonous carbon production, strong currents frequently cause erosion and promote lateral carbon export, whereas low-energy seagrass meadows often function as sediment traps, facilitating deposition of allochthonous carbon and minerals under modest wave conditions42–44. Resolving these context-dependent dualities is essential for accurately predicting and managing blue-carbon sequestration under global changes.

This study investigates the under-explored relationship between hydrodynamic energy and CAR, focusing on how wave height, tidal range and sea-level rise distinctly impact coastal carbon burial by modulating both vertical accumulation and lateral redistribution. We hypothesize that the impact of these forces, spanning facilitative transport to disruptive resuspension and decomposition, varies significantly across different environmental settings and ecosystem types. To test this, a comprehensive global dataset of coastal carbon, hydrodynamic and environmental parameters was integrated and reanalyzed. This approach allows us to decipher the influence of hydrodynamics on the global distribution patterns of coastal carbon and to elucidate ecosystem-specific dynamic response mechanisms across mangroves, salt marshes, and seagrass meadows. By clarifying these multifaceted interactions, our research provides a critical perspective on the physical drivers of carbon burial, thereby establishing a foundation for more accurate blue-carbon accounting and targeted conservation strategies under future hydrodynamic disturbances intensified by rising sea levels and more frequent storms.

Results and discussion

Global patterns of coastal CAR and marine hydrodynamics

The global mean CAR of coastal wetlands is 181.5 ± 245.2 g C m−2 yr−1 (mean ± SD), with a median of 114.6 g C m−2 yr−1. This value is basically consistent with the reported estimate of 202.8 ± 252.3 g C m−2 yr−1 13, although it is approximately 10.5% lower. This difference likely reflects both our broader data sources and a difference in scope—our analysis specifically targeted low-water levels in hydrodynamically active areas, disregarding locations beyond the reach of tidal influence. When grouped by ecosystem type, mean CAR is 169.1 ± 189.8 g C m−2 yr−1 in mangroves, 217.6 ± 278.7 g C m−2 yr−1 in marshes, and 38.7 ± 78.4 g C m−2 yr−1 in seagrasses. CAR is therefore approximately 4.4- and 5.6-fold higher in mangroves and marshes than in seagrasses, respectively. Spatially, CAR values show notable geographic heterogeneity. Higher CARs are generally concentrated in low- to mid-latitude coastal regions, such as the eastern coasts of the United States, Brazil, and China, and in parts of Southeast Asia (Fig. 1a), in agreement with previous research6,13. Notably, our analysis reveals a clear inverse relationship between CAR and hydrodynamic energy: high-CAR zones predominantly occur in regions with weaker marine hydrodynamics (Fig. 1b, c), particularly where wave heights are consistently low (Fig. 1b). Exceptionally high-CAR values, even exceeding 2000 g C m−2 yr−1, have been recorded in large estuarine coasts such as the Yangtze and Mississippi river deltas13,45. These extreme values are attributed to substantial inputs of terrestrial sediments, OC and nutrients delivered by high river discharge. The Yangtze River, for example, discharges approximately 860 km3 of water annually, while the Mississippi River releases over 380 km3,46,47, thereby transporting large quantities of land-derived materials that significantly enhance carbon burial in adjacent coastal wetlands. In contrast, lower CARs are more frequently observed at higher latitudes, including the northwestern coast of North America, the western coast of Europe, the southwestern coast of Australia, and the southeastern coast of Africa (Fig. 1a). These regions are characterized by lower temperatures, which suppress net primary productivity48,49, or stronger marine dynamics, typically manifested through either larger tidal ranges or higher wave heights (Fig. 1b, c). Deviating from this general pattern, the Mediterranean and Red Seas exhibit low CARs despite their limited tidal ranges and wave heights (Fig. 1a-c). This suggests that carbon accumulation in these regions is primarily constrained by climatic conditions, which limit net primary productivity. Specifically, the Mediterranean’s hot and dry summers induce severe seasonal water stress and reduce biomass production, while the Red Sea’s hyper-arid desert climate restricts organic matter input and carbon sequestration50,51. Furthermore, a mismatch between CAR and sea-level rise is evident in certain regions such as western Australia, western Europe, and parts of South America, where low-CAR values persist despite relatively high rates of sea-level rise (Fig. 1d).

Ecosystem-specific variation in marine hydrodynamic drivers of carbon burial

The spatial distributions of CAR and its underlying drivers show clear ecosystem-specific relationships with both macro-climatic gradients and marine hydrodynamics (Fig. 2). Absolute latitude is negatively correlated with CAR in mangroves and marshes (p < 0.001), whereas this relationship is not significant in seagrasses (p = 0.419) (Fig. 2a). This pattern is broadly consistent with the well-documented decline in net primary productivity with increasing latitude, which is largely driven by decreases in mean annual temperature and solar radiation at higher latitudes52,53. Colder temperatures result in shorter growing seasons and lower biomass production, which directly constrain the supply of organic matter available for sequestration54. However, the absence of a significant latitudinal trend in seagrasses, together with the high-CAR values observed in some marshes at relatively high latitudes, suggests that local environmental settings and ecosystem-specific traits can partly offset broad climatic gradients. Compared with the broad climatic gradient, marine hydrodynamic variables vary markly among the three ecosystems. Wave height does not differ significantly among ecosystems (p > 0.05), with a mean ± SD of 0.92 ± 0.68 m. In contrast, both tidal range (p < 0.001) and sea-level rise rate (p < 0.001) show significant differences among ecosystems. Mangroves typically occupy the most macro-tidal regimes (1.41 ± 0.72 m), compared with marshes (1.28 ± 0.96 m) and seagrasses (0.96 ± 0.84 m) (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Conversely, marshes experienc the highest mean sea-level rise rates (3.64 ± 1.11 mm yr⁻¹), followed by mangroves (3.33 ± 1.25 mm yr⁻¹) and seagrasses (2.96 ± 0.93 mm yr⁻¹; Supplementary Fig. 1b).

Marine hydrodynamic variables also exhibit more distinct and process-specific relationships with CAR across ecosystems (Fig. 2b–d). Wave height stimulates CAR in mangroves (p = 0.027) and seagrasses (p = 0.003), but is negatively associated with CAR in marshes (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2b). Tidal range shows a similar ecosystem contrast, with positive relationships in mangroves (p < 0.001), negative relationships in marshes (p < 0.001) and no significant relationship in seagrasses (p = 0.478) (Fig. 2c). In contrast, accelerated SLR universally underpins enhanced CAR in all three ecosystems (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2d). These contrasting patterns suggest that hydrodynamic processes mediate carbon burial through dual (both positive and negative) mechanisms involving physical disturbance and biogeochemical processes (Supplementary Table 1). Negative effects are generally associated with erosion, sediment reworking, carbon export, and enhanced remineralization, whereas positive effects are more often linked to increased deposition, sediment stabilization, organic matter retention, and expanded accommodation space under favorable geomorphic settings35,40,44.

The negative effects of hydrodynamic forcing are particularly evident through several key mechanisms. Elevated wave activity and strong tidal currents promote sediment erosion, leading to the resuspension of both mineral sediments and buried OC55,56. This suspended material can then be transported and exported out, directly reducing the net accumulation rate57,58. Furthermore, high-energy conditions disrupt sediment aggregates, liberating previously mineral- and clay-protected organic matter and making it accessible for microbial decomposition40,59,60. Additionally, frequent inundation-exposure cycles enhance porewater exchange, facilitating the release of DOC into the water column and further reducing burial potential34. Wave turbulence and tidal flushing also enhance oxygen delivery into the typically anoxic sediments35,61. This influx of oxygen accelerates the activity of aerobic microorganisms (bacteria and fungi) that mineralize OC more efficiently than their anaerobic counterparts, leading to accelerated remineralization of organic substrates into carbon dioxide62,63. These processes collectively promote the breakdown of plant detritus, microbial residues and other organic compounds, reducing the amount of carbon available for long-term burial64,65. Thus, hydrodynamic forcing modulates sediment dynamics, redox gradients, and organic matter transport, ultimately compromising the long-term preservation of blue carbon in high-energy environments.

On the other hand, hydrodynamic processes can enhance carbon burial under specific conditions. For instance, tidal currents and wave-driven transports facilitate the lateral movement of organic matter from highly productive, high-elevation tidal flats to depositional basins and lower-elevation sink environments66. This allochthonous OC subsidy not only supplements autochthonous carbon accumulation but also significantly increases the overall CAR in these sink environments. Moreover, the deposition of fine-grained sediments under reduced flow regimes in these sinks can promote the burial and stabilization of organic particles, further contributing to long-term carbon sequestration67,68. Such mechanisms illustrate that the net effect of hydrodynamic forcing on blue-carbon ecosystems depends critically on the balance between erosional and depositional conditions.

Contrasting ecosystem-specific drivers of carbon burial

Despite the overarching importance of marine hydrodynamics at a global scale, CAR variability is jointly shaped by biotic structure, fluvial inputs, climate conditions and anthropogenic influence. The relative contribution and directional effects differed profoundly among mangroves, marshes and seagrasses, highlighting the critical role of ecosystem-specific traits (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 2). Among the hydrodynamic variables, tidal range shows a significant positive contribution to CAR in the pooled analysis (Fig. 3a), contrasting with the non-significant contributions observed for each ecosystem (Fig. 3b–d). This contrast suggests that the contribution of tidal range in the pooled analysis mainly reflects differences in the background geomorphic and hydrodynamic settings occupied by different ecosystem types, rather than an equally strong within-ecosystem control on CAR. This interpretation is supported by the distinct distributions of tidal range among mangrove, marsh, and seagrass ecosystems (Supplementary Fig. 1). In micro-tidal regimes, coastal wetlands tend to be dominated by submerged environments with low-CAR seagrasses (Fig. 2c), because limited vertical accommodation space restricts the establishment of high-CAR intertidal ecosystems such as mangroves and marshes. As tidal range increases, broader intertidal accommodation can support greater mangrove and marsh development, thereby elevating the overall CAR. In other words, tidal range may partly reflect and shape the available accommodation space and ecosystem distribution, which in turn strongly influence CAR69,70. Sea-level rise shows a similar pattern, with a positive contribution in the pooled analysis but weaker contributions within individual ecosystems (Fig. 3a–d), consistent with its distinct cross-ecosystem distribution shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Mangroves exhibit high-CAR and show a positive response to marine hydrodynamics, particularly wave height (Fig. 3b). This suggests that enhanced hydrodynamic exchange can deliver more suspended sediments and organic matter to mangrove forests. These materials can then be captured and stabilized by the robust, extensive root systems of mangroves, which biophysically buffer the system and allow them to maintain high carbon burial along high-energy coasts71–73. Meanwhile, climate conditions also contribute substantially to mangrove CAR (Fig. 3b), underscoring the importance of temperature-driven biological productivity in these tropical and subtropical ecosystems7,74.

Marshes present a more complex response, where both marine hydrodynamics and non-hydrodynamic drivers contribute to CAR variability (Fig. 3c). Marine hydrodynamics exerts a strong constraint mainly through the significant negative effect of wave height, while climate conditions, biotic structure, anthropogenic influence and fluvial inputs further modulate marsh carbon burial (Fig. 3c). This heightened sensitivity to physical disturbance is related to the lower structural complexity and less extensive root systems of marsh vegetation compared with mangroves75,76. Marshes are more vulnerable to erosion, sediment resuspension, and the associated oxygen influx, which accelerates organic matter remineralization and reduces their long-term carbon preservation potential58,63,77.

Seagrasses exhibit a distinct hydrodynamic response, with CAR jointly regulated by marine hydrodynamics and biotic structure (Fig. 3d). Among the hydrodynamic variables, wave height shows a strong positive effect on seagrass CAR (Fig. 3d), whereas biotic structure, particularly vegetation zone width, also contributes substantially to the explained variance. Unlike the primarily autogenic ecosystems of marshes and mangroves, seagrasses primarily grow in lower intertidal to subtidal areas and often function as sinks for allochthonous carbon78. We propose that wave action may facilitate the delivery of externally derived organic-rich sediment from external sources (e.g., intertidal wetlands, terrestrial runoff) into the subtidal seagrass meadows79,80. The dense above- and below-ground biomass of seagrasses then effectively traps and stabilizes this imported material, a process likely facilitated by shoot density and plant morphology41,68,81. In addition, wave-driven resuspension may increase the net primary productivity by enhancing nutrient availability in water bodies82. Thus, higher background wave exposure may be associated with enhanced carbon burial in seagrass meadows, rather than necessarily causing carbon release. Wave height here represents long-term background hydrodynamic exposure rather than event-scale storm forcing, while episodic storms may strongly influence sediment resuspension and carbon storage27. It should also be noted that seagrass CAR can be highly spatially heterogeneous, even within continuous meadows. Local variability reflects interactions among hydrodynamic intensity, external carbon supply, and meadow structural characteristics captured by zone width27,83,84. As most carbon originates externally, canopy filtering effects vary across the meadow depending on these structural traits. The relationships identified here should therefore be interpreted primarily as large-scale background patterns rather than a full resolution of within-meadow processes.

This pattern was further cross-validated using Random Forest–Shapley Additive Explanations (RF-SHAP) and hierarchical partitioning of Generalized Additive Models (GAM) analyses, which independently confirmed the importance of marine hydrodynamics across coastal wetlands and within individual ecosystems, with hydrodynamic contributions ranging from 11.8% to 31.4% (Fig. 4). Specifically, the RF-SHAP analysis revealed that wave height had the highest relative importance among hydrodynamic drivers for the overall coastal wetlands and marsh ecosystems, whereas sea-level rise and tidal range emerge as the primary contributors for mangroves and seagrasses, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 2–3). In addition, the GAM analysis also identified wave height as the dominant hydrodynamic driver across all ecosystems, particularly for mangroves and seagrasses (Supplementary Fig. 4). This multi-method validation strengthens the conclusion that marine hydrodynamics is a consistent and significant driver of carbon accumulation rates, while also highlighting the internal complexity of these physical-biological interactions. In conclusion, these findings underscore that the effects of hydrodynamic drivers on carbon burial are not universal (Supplementary Table 1). Potential risks to coastal carbon burial under changing marine hydrodynamics

Marine hydrodynamic regimes, including sea-level rise and intensifying storm disturbances under global climate change, are profoundly reshaping the carbon burial function of coastal ecosystems, threatening this function through potential large-scale habitat loss 40,86,87. However, hydrodynamic effects on carbon accumulation inferred from historical relationships should not be extrapolated linearly into the future, because some positive associations may reflect the net outcome under past conditions when many coastal systems were still able to maintain vertical accretion through sediment delivery and vegetation-mediated trapping. As sea level continues to rise, many low-lying coastal regions face the risk of inundation and erosion. These processes not only directly undermine the stability of coastal ecosystems, but also cause sediment and vegetation loss85,86. In carbon-rich ecosystems, such as marsh and mangrove ecosystems, inundation can suppress plant growth and reduce carbon sequestration capacity88. Meanwhile, rising water levels and saltwater intrusion may alter plant community structures, further weakening their role as carbon sinks89. Under these conditions, once changes in marine hydrodynamics exceed ecosystem adaptive capacity, the increasing inundation risk may ultimately drive a transition from carbon sink to carbon source90. These broad risks are likely to vary among ecosystem types because the historical relationships identified in this study indicate contrasting hydrodynamic sensitivities across mangrove, marsh, and seagrass ecosystems.

From a comparative perspective, marsh ecosystems appear to be the most vulnerable among the three systems, as stronger wave exposure is negatively associated with CAR (Fig. 5b), and their generally lower colonization capacity may make them more susceptible to erosion, inundation, and habitat loss than mangroves57,71,91,92. Consistent with this pattern, marsh ecosystems fall into the high-risk category among the three types and may therefore become major contributors to carbon emissions under potential future shifts in the coastal carbon cycle (Fig. 5e). Mangrove ecosystems, by contrast, show historically positive associations with wave-related forcing (Fig. 5a), suggesting that sediment trapping and adaptive accretion may buffer hydrodynamic disturbance under suitable depositional conditions71. However, this apparent resilience is likely to remain conditional on sufficient sediment supply and the ability of vertical accretion to keep pace with rising water levels (Fig. 5d). Seagrass ecosystems also show a historically positive relationship with wave exposure (Fig. 5c), but their future responses are likely to remain more uncertain because hydrodynamic forcing may simultaneously influence material retention, submergence stress, and habitat redistribution (Fig. 5f). Although accelerated sea-level rise may lead to the loss of seagrass meadows where coastal hardening and geomorphic constraints limit landward migration93, climate-change projections also suggest that future range shifts may involve both regional losses and gains rather than a simple unidirectional decline94. It is important to note that the net impact of sea-level rise on carbon budgets is geomorphologically dependent85. In contrast to risks of erosion and inundation, accelerated sea-level rise may enhance OC accumulation in surviving wetlands, where accommodation space increases and sediment supply is sufficient15,16. Relative sea-level rise is also highly spatially heterogeneous across coastal regions, particularly along the northwestern Atlantic coast, where some marshes may already be failing to keep pace because of subsidence and insufficient sediment supply95,96. Conversely, intensified storms and waves may accelerate coastal erosion, release previously buried carbon, and reduce wetland extent97,98. The persistence of coastal carbon pools is therefore largely determined by the balance between seaward erosion and landward migration85,96. In regions with high initial carbon burial capacity but limited sediment supply or constrained geomorphic settings, such as areas affected by dams, levees, or coastal hardening, the effects of rapid shifts in marine hydrodynamic forcing may be especially severe15. Such areas may rapidly lose their carbon storage potential under simultaneous inundation and erosion, resulting in a net transition from carbon sink to source99. Overall, the patterns identified here should be interpreted primarily as indicators of potential hydrodynamic sensitivity under future change, rather than as deterministic predictions of future global carbon accumulation. Nevertheless, enhanced marine hydrodynamics may jeopardize not only local ecosystem functions but also the stability of coastal carbon pools, highlighting the need for more targeted protection and restoration measures in vulnerable regions.

Methods

Data collection

We carried out a systematic literature search in December 2024 for articles concerning coastal carbon burial rates in Web of Science and China National Knowledge Infrastructure employing the following string:

(marsh* OR tidal wetland OR brackish OR seagrass OR mangrove OR blue carbon) AND (carbon burial OR carbon sequestration OR carbon accumulation OR carbon sink).

We did an additional search for recent studies in March 2026. In total, our search resulted in 7884 papers. We checked the abstracts of all search results for the major eligibility criterion, which is whether a study contains implications relevant to coastal CAR. After removing duplicates, we screened the abstracts and full texts of the retrieved articles to identify those relevant to coastal CAR. Subsequently, we thoroughly examined the full texts of the shortlisted articles to extract empirical data for our meta-analysis. To study the global CAR under the influence of coastal dynamic processes, only the data collected from hydrodynamically active coastal zones were retained, whereas inland sites with little or no tidal influence were excluded (see the PRISMA flow diagram in Supplementary Notes 1). Therefore, a total of 236 studies met our criteria (See the Supplementary material), comprising 1555 observations (including 447 mangroves, 916 salt marshes, and 192 seagrass meadows). The spatial distribution of CAR, wave height, tidal range and sea level across mangroves, marshes and seagrasses in Fig. 1 was mapped in QGIS using Natural Earth country boundary vector data, a freely available public geospatial dataset, as the world basemap.

To investigate the environmental drivers of CAR across coastal wetlands, we selected a comprehensive set of explanatory variables that capture key hydrodynamic, climatic, biological, anthropogenic, and fluvial processes known to influence carbon dynamics. Specifically, marine hydrodynamic variables (sea-level rise rate, wave height and tidal range) were included because hydrodynamic energy affects sediment transport, organic matter deposition, and the stability of carbon in coastal ecosystems. Climate conditions (temperature, precipitation, and solar radiation) were incorporated as they regulate primary productivity and decomposition rates, influencing both autochthonous and allochthonous carbon inputs. Biotic structure variables (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index, NDVI; Net Primary Productivity, NPP; vegetation zone width) were selected to represent vegetation condition, productivity, and spatial extent, which play critical roles in trapping and stabilizing carbon. Anthropogenic influence (global human modification index) was included to account for human-induced changes that can alter carbon inputs, sediment dynamics, and habitat condition. Finally, fluvial inputs (riverine sediment, carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus) were incorporated because rivers act as major sources of external carbon and nutrients that can be deposited and stored within coastal wetlands. These variables were chosen based on prior studies highlighting their mechanistic links to carbon accumulation, as well as the availability of global high-resolution datasets to enable spatially explicit analysis across diverse coastal ecosystems.

Global tidal range and wave height data were obtained from https://jhnienhuis.users.earthengine.app/view/changing-shores100–102. The tidal range used, based on data from this site, refers to the theoretical maximum tidal range, calculated as the sum of the tidal ranges contributed by each harmonic constituent. The wave height in this study refers to the long-term significant wave height derived from the NOAA WaveWatch III 30-year hindcast Phase II dataset and is used as a proxy for local wave conditions, thus representing background wave exposure rather than short-term or event-scale hydrodynamic forcing. Historical sea level rise data obtained from ref. 102. Bioclimatic predictors, annual NDVI and NPP, were extracted for all sampling sites in Google Earth Engine. Bioclimatic conditions were represented by Bio01, Bio12 and Bio20 from the CliMond database, corresponding to annual mean temperature, annual precipitation and annual mean radiation, respectively103. CliMond database (https://www.climond.org) provides global high-resolution historical climate surfaces for bioclimatic modeling, including a 30-years baseline climatological scenario layer at 10′ resolution. NDVI was derived from Landsat 8 Collection 2 Level 2 surface reflectance imagery (LANDSAT/LC08/C02/T1_L2) for 2013–2023 after masking cloud and cloud-shadow pixels, rescaling reflectance values, and calculating the normalized difference between the near-infrared and red bands. Annual NDVI was represented by the median composite of each year. NPP was obtained from the MODIS Version 6.1 annual productivity product (MODIS/061/MOD17A3HGF) using the NPP band. NDVI and NPP were extracted at 30 m and 500 m resolution, respectively. The vegetation zone widths of coastal wetlands (seagrasses, mangroves, and salt marshes) were derived from their corresponding global distribution datasets. All datasets were acquired from the Ocean+ Habitats database (https://habitats.oceanplus.org). For each sampling site, the corresponding vegetation layer was matched according to habitat type. The vegetation zone widths of coastal wetlands were measured as the shoreline-perpendicular span of each vegetation patch. The global human modification index was extracted for all sampling sites using Google Earth Engine. The Global Human Modification dataset provides a global cumulative measure of human modification across terrestrial lands at a 1 km2 resolution. Global human modification index ranges from 0.0 to 1.0 and is calculated by estimating both the proportion of each pixel subjected to human modification and the intensity of disturbance associated with each anthropogenic stressor. Five major anthropogenic stressors were included: human settlement, agriculture, transportation, mining and energy production, and electrical infrastructure. Riverine sediment data were calculated based on the fluvial sediment flux data from ref. 101, where the fluvial sediment flux is denoted as the Qriver indicator in the paper. Riverine carbon data were obtained from ref. 104, in which total organic carbon (TOC) was calculated as the sum of dissolved organic carbon (DOC) and particulate organic carbon (POC). Riverine nitrogen and phosphorus were represented by dissolved inorganic nitrogen (DIN) and dissolved inorganic phosphorus (DIP), respectively. Both indicators were obtained from the biogeochemical river inputs for the global ocean models database (https://zenodo.org/records/13799103). Then, each sampling site was matched to its nearest river mouth, and the corresponding distance was calculated for use in the distance-weighted estimation of sediment, carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus inputs. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.6.0; R Core Team, 2026). The CAR data were first checked for assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances. A natural logarithm transformation was applied to satisfy these assumptions where necessary105. To assess the individual effects of hydrodynamic drivers (wave height, tidal range and sea-level rise rate) and absolute latitude on CAR across ecosystems, separate Spearman correlation analyses were conducted for both the pooled dataset and individual ecosystems.

Subsequently, all environmental predictors were standardized using z-score transformation to ensure comparability across variables with different units. Multiple linear regression models were first fitted to estimate standardized effect sizes and to quantify the relative contributions of predictors to CAR. Linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were then fitted with reference included as a random intercept (1|reference) to account for hierarchical structure in the data. Two distinct sets of multiple regression models were constructed: one for all coastal wetland ecosystems pooled together to capture global-scale drivers, and another set performed for mangroves, marshes, and seagrasses independently to resolve ecosystem-specific responses. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with all variables in most models exhibiting VIF values below 3; Bio01 (mean annual temperature) was excluded from the seagrass-specific model to meet VIF criteria. Statistical significance of standardized coefficients in the multiple linear regression models was evaluated using the “car” package106. The relative contribution of each predictor to CAR was quantified via hierarchical partitioning using the “hier.part” package, and model performance of the LMMs was reported as marginal R² (R²m, variance explained by fixed effects) and conditional R² (R²c, variance explained by fixed and random effects)107.

To further validate these patterns and capture potential non-linear interactions, we employed two complementary advanced modeling approaches: Random Forest with Shapley Additive Explanations (RF-SHAP) and hierarchical partitioning of Generalized Additive Models (GAMs). Both approaches were applied to overall coastal wetlands as well as to each ecosystem separately to identify global and ecosystem-specific drivers. Random Forest models were developed using the “randomForest” package to quantify variable importance108, and model interpretability was achieved through the “fastshap” package, which provides a game-theoretic SHAP framework to estimate the individual contribution of each driver to the predicted CAR109. Relative importance was calculated as the mean absolute SHAP value for each predictor and then normalized to 100%. GAMs were fitted using the “mgcv” package110, incorporating a Gaussian process smooth term for spatial coordinates to account for spatial autocorrelation. Model adequacy was assessed by examining residual distributions and verifying that the basis dimensions (k) for smooth terms were sufficient to capture underlying non-linear patterns. Spatial independence of residuals was confirmed using Moran’s I test via the “spdep” package111. Finally, the total explained variance for the drivers was partitioned using the “rdacca.hp” package, enabling a robust comparison of the relative importance of each driver112.
