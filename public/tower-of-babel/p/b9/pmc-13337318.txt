Multisystem IgG4‐Related Disease Mimicking Malignancy With Cavitary Pulmonary Nodules, Pleural Thickening and Hepatic Mass‐Like Lesions: A Case Report
Authors: Ho Young Lee, Juyeon Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIgG4‐related disease (IgG4‐RD) is a multisystem fibroinflammatory condition that can mimic metastatic malignancy. A 49‐year‐old woman presented with a 6‐month history of progressive dyspnoea, nonproductive cough and severe weight loss. Computed tomography demonstrated diffuse bronchocentric and perilymphatic pulmonary nodules with cavitation and consolidation, right pleural thickening and multifocal peripheral‐enhancing hepatic mass‐like lesions. Given concern for disseminated malignancy, bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage was performed and was negative for infection and malignancy. Lung biopsy showed fibrosis with marked CD138‐positive plasma cell infiltration and microabscesses. As concern for malignancy persisted, a liver biopsy was performed for diagnostic clarification. Liver biopsy showed ductular reaction, patchy fibrosis and increased IgG4‐positive plasma cells, with elevated serum IgG4 (798.0 mg/dL). The patient met the 2020 revised comprehensive diagnostic criteria for possible IgG4‐RD and showed rapid improvement with glucocorticoid therapy. IgG4‐RD should be considered in patients with multisystem mass‐forming lesions to avoid misdiagnosis and delayed treatment.

Introduction

IgG4‐related disease (IgG4‐RD) is a systemic fibroinflammatory disorder that can affect nearly any organ and often presents with multiorgan involvement [1]. It is characterised by IgG4‐positive plasma cell–rich inflammation with a propensity for fibrosis, causing tumefactive lesions. Because IgG4‐RD typically responds well to glucocorticoids, early recognition may prevent progressive fibrosis and irreversible organ damage [2]. However, the disease frequently mimics malignancy, infection and other inflammatory conditions, leading to extensive evaluations and, at times, unnecessary invasive procedures [2]. Thoracic IgG4‐RD may involve the airways, lung parenchyma, pleura and mediastinum. Its heterogeneous imaging features—including pulmonary nodules or mass‐like lesions, pleural thickening and lymphadenopathy—can be indistinguishable from metastatic malignancy [3]. Accordingly, IgG4‐RD should be considered when thoracic and extrapulmonary mass‐forming lesions coexist, and diagnosis requires histopathologic confirmation with clinicoradiologic correlation [4].

Case Report

A 49‐year‐old Korean woman presented with a 6‐month history of progressive dyspnoea, nonproductive cough and significant unintentional weight loss (15 kg; baseline 45 kg), with dyspepsia, abdominal bloating and generalised weakness. She was a never‐smoker and denied tuberculosis exposure, substance use or family history of malignancy. Initial differentials included pulmonary tuberculosis, primary lung cancer and metastatic disease.

On examination, she was cachectic with tachycardia (132 beats/min) and borderline hypotension (100/60 mmHg). Respiratory rate was 20 breaths/min, temperature 36.6°C and oxygen saturation 97% on room air. Breath sounds were decreased over the right lower lung field with no abdominal tenderness or lymphadenopathy.

Chest radiography showed diffuse bilateral nodular opacities and right costophrenic angle blunting (Figure 1A). Pleural fluid was minimal; therefore, thoracentesis was not performed due to an unfavourable risk–benefit profile. Chest CT revealed bronchocentric and perilymphatic nodules with cavitation, consolidation, pleural thickening and borderline mediastinal lymphadenopathy (Figure 1B). Abdominopelvic CT demonstrated multiple peripherally enhancing hepatic lesions (up to 6 cm) (Figure 1C). Based on these multiorgan findings, differentials included pulmonary tuberculosis, primary lung cancer with hepatic metastases, disseminated malignancy of unknown origin, sarcoidosis, ANCA‐associated vasculitis and connective tissue disease–related lung disease.

(C) Contrast‐enhanced axial abdominal CT reveals lobulated, low‐attenuation mass‐like lesions with peripheral enhancement in both hepatic lobes (black arrows). (D) 18F‐FDG PET‐CT demonstrates multiple hypermetabolic lesions involving the liver, pleura, lungs, bilateral mediastinal lymph nodes and diffuse splenic uptake. (F) Follow‐up CT shows improvement of mediastinal lymphadenopathy, right pleural thickening and hepatic lesions.

Arterial blood gas analysis showed PaO2 77.4 mmHg and oxygen saturation 95.8%. Laboratory findings included leucocytosis (20.37 × 109/L), anaemia (haemoglobin 8.6 g/dL), thrombocytosis (419 × 109/L) and elevated C‐reactive protein (15.83 mg/dL). Total protein was elevated (9.8 g/dL) with hypoalbuminaemia (2.1 g/dL) and polyclonal gammopathy. Antinuclear antibody (ANA), antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (MPO‐ANCA and PR3‐ANCA) and serum angiotensin‐converting enzyme (ACE) were negative, and serum ACE was normal (36 U/L). Total IgE was normal (18.5 IU/mL) with no eosinophilia. Tumour markers were within normal limits (AFP, CA19‐9, CEA, CYFRA 21‐1, PIVKA‐II). Sputum investigations were not performed due to inability to expectorate. Pulmonary function testing was deferred initially and later limited by poor cooperation because of weakness.

Bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage was initially performed to evaluate for infection and malignancy; cultures, AFB stain, TB‐PCR and Xpert MTB/RIF assay, and cytology were negative. With infection less likely but malignancy unresolved, CT‐guided lung biopsy was performed but was non‐diagnostic. PET‐CT showed intense FDG uptake in the liver (SUVmean 12.1), pleura (SUVmean 11.8), lungs, lymph nodes and spleen. Primovist‐enhanced liver MRI demonstrated T2 hyperintensity with diffusion restriction and enlarged abdominal lymph nodes, increasing suspicion for metastatic disease. Repeat lung biopsy remained non‐diagnostic and bone marrow biopsy showed plasma cell hyperplasia without malignancy. Given persistent concern for disseminated disease, liver biopsy was performed as the largest and most metabolically active target.

Lung histopathology demonstrated plasma cell–rich inflammation with interstitial expansion and microabscesses. Immunohistochemistry showed numerous CD138‐positive plasma cells, with 25 IgG4‐positive cells per high‐power field (IgG4/IgG ratio of approximately 10%) (Figure 2A–C). Liver biopsy revealed lymphoplasmacytic infiltration, fibrosis and 30 IgG4‐positive cells per high‐power field (IgG4/IgG ratio approximately 10%) (Figure 2D–F). Microbiological stains and cultures were negative. Multidisciplinary discussion prompted evaluation for IgG4‐related disease, including serum IgG4 measurement.

Histopathologic findings of the lung (A–C) and liver (D–F). (A) Marked plasma cell infiltration with microabscess formation and widening of the interstitium by mesenchymal proliferation (H&E, ×200). (B) CD138 immunohistochemical staining demonstrates numerous plasma cells (CD138, ×100). (C) Increased IgG4‐positive plasma cells (25/high‐power field) (IgG4, ×100). (D) The liver shows patchy fibrosis replacing the normal parenchyma (H&E, ×100). (E) Dense infiltration of plasma cells and histiocytes with bile duct proliferation (H&E, ×200). (F) Frequent IgG4‐positive plasma cells (30/high‐power field) (IgG4, ×100).

With elevated serum IgG4 (798 mg/dL; total IgG 5995.7 mg/dL), multiorgan involvement and compatible histopathology, the patient met the 2020 revised comprehensive diagnostic criteria for possible IgG4‐related disease. Infectious, malignant and other autoimmune differentials were excluded.

Treatment was initiated with intravenous methylprednisolone 62.5 mg/day for 3 days, followed by 40 mg/day for 2 days and 30 mg/day for 5 days, then transitioned to oral prednisolone 40 mg/day. The dose was reduced to 30 mg/day after 4 weeks and tapered gradually according to clinical response, inflammatory markers, serum IgG4 and imaging. Tapering was cautious due to poor baseline condition. C‐reactive protein normalised, and serum IgG4 decreased to 84.1 mg/dL at 3 months. Follow‐up CT at 6 months showed marked lesion regression. Spirometry at 6 months showed forced vital capacity (FVC) 2.03 L (67% predicted), forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) 1.40 L (55% predicted) and FEV1/FVC ratio 69%. At 18 months, FEV1 improved to 1.99 L (78% predicted), with stable FVC 2.03 L (67% predicted). Diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) could not be assessed due to poor patient cooperation. From 9 months, she was maintained on 5 mg/day. Steroid‐sparing therapy was not initiated because the response to glucocorticoids was rapid and sustained, and glucocorticoids were tapered over 3 years without relapse.

Discussion

IgG4‐RD is an immune‐mediated fibroinflammatory condition with tumefactive lesions and fibrosis that can mimic malignancy or chronic infection on imaging [1, 2]. In this patient, cavitary pulmonary nodules with bronchocentric and perilymphatic distribution, pleural thickening, mediastinal lymphadenopathy, multifocal hepatic mass‐like lesions and intense FDG uptake suggested metastatic malignancy. Recognition is critical, as IgG4‐RD is glucocorticoid‐responsive and early diagnosis may prevent unnecessary procedures and delays [2].

Thoracic IgG4‐RD is diagnostically challenging due to heterogeneous CT findings overlapping with pulmonary diseases. Manifestations include nodules, interstitial thickening, pleural involvement and lymphadenopathy [3]. Differential diagnosis includes malignancy, tuberculosis, fungal infection, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, sarcoidosis and multicentric Castleman disease. Negative microbiological tests, autoimmune serologies and absence of malignancy on histology were essential for exclusion.

Organ involvement is often patterned and may help recognition of multisystem presentations [1]. The 2019 ACR/EULAR classification criteria provide a standardised framework [5], particularly when imaging mimics disseminated malignancy. Tissue confirmation remains essential, as PET‐CT findings are nonspecific. Lung and liver biopsies showed dense lymphoplasmacytic inflammation with fibrosis and increased IgG4‐positive plasma cells, although storiform fibrosis was absent and hepatic fibrosis was patchy. With markedly elevated serum IgG4 and multiorgan involvement, the patient met the 2020 revised comprehensive diagnostic criteria for possible IgG4‐RD [4]. An IgG4/IgG ratio of approximately 10% does not exclude the diagnosis when other features are supportive [4].

Glucocorticoids remain first‐line therapy and typically induce rapid improvement [2]. Steroid‐sparing agents or rituximab may be considered in relapsing or refractory cases [2]. In this patient, tapering was performed cautiously due to poor general condition, and remission was achieved without additional therapy. Serial pulmonary function testing demonstrated resolution of obstruction with normalisation of the FEV1/FVC ratio at 18 months, while a mild restrictive pattern persisted, likely reflecting residual post‐inflammatory parenchymal change. Long‐term clinical, biochemical, physiological and radiological follow‐up is warranted [2].

In conclusion, multisystem IgG4‐RD can closely mimic metastatic malignancy and should be considered when thoracic and extrapulmonary mass‐forming lesions coexist. Timely biopsy confirmation is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and treatment delay [1, 3, 4].
