Exploratory analysis of genotoxic Escherichia coli and Helicobacter pylori virulence in gastrointestinal carcinogenesis
Authors: Tayyip Karaman, Nesteren Mansur-Ozen, Arzu Tiftikçi, Suna Yapalı, Cem Aygün, Osman Ugur Sezerman, Tanıl Kocagöz, Nurdan Tozun, Sinem Oktem-Okullu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastrointestinal cancers remain among the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Beyond environmental and hereditary determinants, increasing evidence suggests that the gut microbiota may contribute to carcinogenesis through persistent infection, expression of virulence factors, and production of genotoxic metabolites. In this context, Helicobacter pylori has been firmly implicated in gastric carcinogenesis, while colibactin-producing Escherichia coli has emerged as a potential contributor to colorectal cancer development. The aim of this study was to explore the presence of colibactin-associated E. coli and virulence factor–harboring H. pylori strains in gastrointestinal cancer patients from a Turkish cohort. Tumor samples were collected from 19 patients with colorectal cancer and 4 patients with gastric cancer. In colorectal cancer samples, the presence of colibactin was assessed by screening for key genes (clbA, clbB, clbN, and clbQ). In gastric cancer samples, major H. pylori virulence determinants, including ureA, cagA, and vacA, were evaluated. Colibactin-associated genes were detected in approximately 21% of colorectal cancer patients (4/19), whereas 75% of gastric cancer cases (3/4) were positive for H. pylori virulence factors. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) of two colibactin-positive isolates identified them as Escherichia coli Nissle and Citrobacter braakii. Given the limited sample size and exploratory design, these results are descriptive and intended to provide preliminary insight rather than establish causality. In conclusion, this study provides early evidence supporting the potential presence of genotoxin-associated bacteria and virulent H. pylori strains in gastrointestinal cancer patients in Türkiye. These findings underscore the need for larger, well-powered, and multi-omics–integrated studies to further elucidate the role of host–microbe interactions in gastrointestinal carcinogenesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13568-026-02084-8.Keywords: Gastrointestinal cancer, Escherichia coli, Colibactin, Helicobacter pylori, Virulence factors

Introduction

Cancer is a disease characterized by the loss of control over the mechanisms of cell division, leading to variations in its etiology and pathogenesis depending on the individual patient. It is the second leading cause of death worldwide (Williams and Stoeber 2012; Glaviano et al. 2025). According to data from the World Health Organization, cancer is responsible for 9.6 million deaths annually, accounting for 1 in 6 deaths (World Health Organization 2025). Various factors, including lifestyle, diet, smoking, alcohol consumption, genetic predisposition, and exposure to harmful chemicals, can contribute to the development of cancer (Byrne et al. 2023; Khan et al. 2010).

Gastrointestinal cancers, including gastric, colon, and colorectal cancer, typically develop sporadically and exhibit heterogeneous features. They are among the most common cancers globally and in Türkiye, with low survival rates (Eser et al. 2023). Their pathogenesis involves genetic and epigenetic alterations driving the progression from benign polyps to malignant, invasive, and metastatic tumors (Grady and Carethers 2008). Underlying causes include stepwise mutations (spontaneous or inherited), defects in DNA repair through mutation or hypermethylation, dysregulation of tumor or cell cycle genes, and chromosomal instability from replication errors (Delgado et al. 2024; Wu and Ye 2024). Recent evidence also suggests that microorganisms within the tumor microenvironment may contribute to intestinal cancers (Aggarwal et al. 2023; Hou et al. 2022; Ogunrinola et al. 2020). It is hypothesized that bacterial products, including toxins, secondary bile acids, and reactive oxygen species, may act as procarcinogens. In colorectal cancer patients, fecal and biopsy samples have identified pathogens such as Clostridium septicum, Enterococcus faecalis, Streptococcus bovis, Bacteroides fragilis, Helicobacter pylori, Fusobacterium spp., and colibactin-producing Escherichia coli (Afzaal et al. 2022; Kim and Lee 2022; Wong and Yu 2023).

Cyclomodulin (CM) and genotoxin-producing E. coli can disrupt the host cell cycle and may contribute to carcinogenesis; however, data on their colonization in colon tumors remain limited. E. coli strains are classified into eight phylogroups (A, B1, B2, C, D, E, F, and cryptic clade I) (Reberšek 2021). Previous phylogroup analyses suggest that pathogenic E. coli strains occur across multiple phylogroups, but are frequently associated with A, B2, and D (Clermont et al. 2013). Members of phylogroup B2 are typically more virulent, and their virulence potential has been attributed to the presence of virulence genes and siderophores/iron-chelating systems, as well as colibactin-associated functions (Martin et al. 2017; Royer et al. 2023). Colibactin is a small genotoxin and secondary metabolite synthesized by a hybrid non-ribosomal peptide synthetase–polyketide synthase (NRPS–PKS) complex encoded within the pks genomic island, especially in B2 phylogroup strains (Faïs et al. 2018; Lv et al. 2025; Suresh et al. 2018). Colibactin synthesis requires NRPS, PKS, hybrid enzymes, and accessory proteins encoded in the pks island. It induces reactive oxygen species, DNA double-strand breaks, ATM-mediated damage responses, cell-cycle arrest, and genomic instability. Approximately 21% of humans harbor colibactin-producing E. coli, which can damage host DNA and promote colon cancer (Balskus 2015; Sadeghi et al. 2024; Zhang and Sun 2024).

Moreover, H. pylori is a leading global cause of gastric disorders and may drive the development of gastric cancer if left undiagnosed. Infection occurs asymptomatically in many cases; therefore, detailed evaluation of virulence factors, including ureA, ureB, dupA, hpaA, napA, cagA, and vacA, is important. These genes are key determinants of pathogenic potential, influencing survival in acidic conditions, epithelial adhesion, and activation of inflammatory pathways that can lead to chronic inflammation (Yamaoka and Graham 2021; Jones et al. 2010; Link et al. 2017).

Gastrointestinal cancers have high mortality and morbidity in Türkiye. Despite pathogen prevalence, a combined patient profile for colibactin-producing E. coli and H. pylori virulence determinants has not been established in the context of colon and gastric cancer in the Turkish population. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of colibactin-associated genes (clbA, clbB, clbN, clbQ) in colon cancer tissues and the prevalence of H. pylori virulence determinants (ureA, ureB, dupA, hpaA, napA, cagA, and vacA s1/s2) in gastric tissues.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and patient cohort

The clinical cohort was stratified based on histopathological evaluation. The gastric cohort included individuals diagnosed with active chronic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia, and moderately to well-differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma. In parallel, the colorectal cancer (CRC) cohort consisted of patients with well-to-moderately differentiated colon adenocarcinoma.

For the CRC group, paired tissue samples were collected from each patient. Tumor associated biopsies were obtained directly from macroscopically identified lesions, while matched non-lesional mucosal samples were collected from anatomically distant, visually healthy regions of the same individuals and used as internal controls. In addition, non-cancer controls were obtained from individuals undergoing routine screening colonoscopy who showed no macroscopic or histological evidence of neoplastic or pre-neoplastic lesions.

To minimize potential alterations in the native mucosal microbiota, individuals who had received systemic antibiotics, proton pump inhibitors, or probiotic supplements within 15 days before endoscopic sampling were excluded.

Overall, 31 colorectal tissue samples were collected from 19 CRC patients, 7 adjacent normal tissues, and 5 healthy controls (Table 1). The cohort included 12 female and 12 male participants, with ages ranging from 42 to 87 years (mean 61.0 ± 14.6).

Distribution of collected tissue samples

In the gastric cohort, a total of 15 tissue specimens were obtained from 12 individuals, including 4 patients with gastric cancer and 8 non-cancer controls. Tumor samples were collected from gastric cancer patients, with multiple sampling in selected cases (e.g., antrum and corpus regions) to account for intragastric heterogeneity. Healthy controls contributed single gastric mucosal biopsies, while one cancer patient additionally provided paired non-tumor samples. Altogether, this resulted in 5 tumor-derived and 10 non-tumor gastric specimens. The gastric cohort consisted of 8 female and 4 male participants, aged 28–87 years (mean 59.7 ± 15.5).

As this was a pilot study, no formal power calculation was performed. The aim was exploratory rather than hypothesis-driven, focusing on identifying preliminary microbial patterns to inform future, larger-scale investigations. Participants were selected to have broadly comparable dietary and lifestyle characteristics, and all tissue samples were immediately preserved either in RNAlater (GenemarkBio, Taiwan) for downstream nucleic acid extraction or in sterile Brucella broth for subsequent analysis of Helicobacter pylori and Escherichia coli virulence-associated factors.

Sample preparation

All tissue collection and processing steps were performed under sterile conditions to minimize contamination.

Brucella broth preparation

Brucella broth was prepared by dissolving 14 g of Brucella broth powder in 500 mL of double-distilled water (O). Sterilized steel beads (1.5 mm diameter) were added to tubes containing the broth to facilitate homogenization. Tumor tissue samples were placed into these tubes immediately after collection.

Columbia agar preparation

Columbia agar was used for the cultivation of H. pylori from gastric tumor tissues. The medium was prepared by combining 42.5 g Columbia agar with 50 mL horse blood, 10 mL β -cyclodextrin, 5 mL of a 200× antibiotic mixture, and 1 mL of a 1000× antibiotic mixture. The 200× antibiotic mixture contained 100 mg vancomycin, 50 mg cefsulodin, and 3.3 mg polymyxin B dissolved in 50 mL O. The 1000× antibiotic mixture was prepared by dissolving 100 mg trimethoprim and 160 mg amphotericin B in 20 mL DMSO.

Single colony streaking from tissue specimens

Tumor tissue in Brucella broth was homogenized using a Bead-Beater system (Biospec, USA) under sterile conditions. Homogenized samples were cultured on Eosin Methylene Blue agar and incubated at 37C overnight to support the growth of E. coli. Gastric tumor tissues were cultured on Columbia agar and incubated at 37C overnight under microaerophilic conditions using anaerobic tubes filled with a microaerophilic gas mixture (Don Whitley Scientific). After incubation, single colonies were stocked and stored at C. Negative controls (O) were included during medium preparation and plating; no blank/extraction controls were processed alongside tissue samples, which is acknowledged as a limitation.

DNA/RNA isolation

Quick-DNA/RNA Miniprep Plus kit (Zymo Research, Orange, CA) was used for DNA/RNA isolation from tissue specimens. Bacteria Genomic DNA Purification kit (GenemarkBio, Taiwan) was used for swab samples and bacterial culture isolates. Extraction steps were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nucleic acid concentrations were determined using a NanoDrop 2000/2000c Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific).

Virulence gene screening

PCR was used to evaluate colibactin-associated genes in colon tissues. The genes clbA, clbB, clbN, and clbQ were assessed in tumor, adjacent normal, and healthy control tissues. Primers are listed in Table 2. Multiplex PCR reactions consisted of 4 μ L 5× Hot Start PCR Master Mix, 1 μ L forward primer, 1 μ L reverse primer, 3 μ L DNA, and 11 μ L O. Cycling conditions included 95C for 4 min; 40 cycles of 95C for 30 s; annealing at 55C (clbA, clbQ) or 59C (clbB, clbN) for 40 s; extension at 72C for 1 min; and final extension at 72C for 5 min. Products were visualized by gel electrophoresis. O served as the negative control. An external positive control strain/plasmid was not used; the first positive sample was subsequently used as an internal positive control. Primer specificity was validated in silico.

Whole-genome sequencing

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was carried out using the Oxford Nanopore Technologies MinION platform. Libraries were prepared with the SQK-LSK104 kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Following sequencing, raw reads were subjected to quality control and preprocessing before assembly. Adapter sequences were removed, and reads of low quality or insufficient length were filtered using Fastp and Trimmomatic, applying standard quality thresholds. Overall read quality was evaluated with FastQC.

To reduce potential biases, host-derived sequences and contaminants were identified and removed using Kraken2-based taxonomic classification before downstream analysis. Because the dataset showed relatively low average read quality, the resulting assemblies were fragmented. This limitation was taken into account during downstream interpretation, particularly for genome-level analyses.

Three cancer patients were clbA positive, three were clbB positive, four were clbN positive, and two were clbQ positive (Table 3). All healthy control samples were clb negative. Colibactin PCR analysis for single colony culture

Tumor tissues that showed positivity for clb genes (CC1, CC10, and CC19) were cultured to obtain single bacterial colonies. The purpose of isolating single colonies was to specifically observe clb-positive Escherichia coli strains and to prepare pure bacterial isolates for subsequent whole-genome sequencing (WGS)–based strain identification.

A total of ten bacterial colonies obtained from these cultures were selected, and genomic DNA was extracted from each colony. PCR analyses were performed to screen for the presence of uspA, uidA, and the colibactin-associated genes clbA, clbB, clbN, and clbQ. Among the ten colonies analyzed, three colonies (K1, K2, and K7) were positive for all four clb genes (Fig. 1a).

All three colonies were positive for both markers (Fig. 1b), supporting their identification as colibactin-producing E. coli isolates derived from colon tumor tissues of patients CC1, CC10, and CC19.

Whole genome analysis

Single bacterial colonies isolated from colon cancer patients CC1 and CC19 that were PCR-positive for clb genes were selected for whole-genome sequencing (WGS) to enable species- and strain-level characterization.

For the first isolate, 200,346 raw reads were generated. The assembly yielded contigs with a maximum length of 56,282 bp and an N50 of 2133 bp. The assembled genome had an estimated size of 4,846,766 bp, with a GC content of 49% and an average read quality score of Q16. De novo assembly was performed using the MHAP algorithm with an estimated sequencing depth of 274,958,712, resulting in 65 contigs and 4 scaffolds. BLAST-based taxonomic assignment identified the isolate as Escherichia coli Nissle with 99.66% sequence similarity (Supplementary File 2).

Initial genome annotation did not recover a complete clb operon as a single contiguous unit. To further investigate this observation, predicted coding sequences were subjected to biosynthetic gene cluster analysis using antiSMASH. This analysis identified a high-confidence polyketide synthase (PKS) biosynthetic gene cluster corresponding to the colibactin (pks) genomic island (Fig. 2; Supplementary Fig. S5).

Predicted pks genomic island identified by secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene cluster analysis. The pks island encodes the non-ribosomal peptide synthetase–polyketide synthase (NRPS–PKS) machinery responsible for colibactin biosynthesis

To resolve the apparent discrepancy between PCR detection and draft genome assembly, additional BLAST-based analyses were performed at the contig level. These confirmed the presence of multiple conserved clb genes, including clbA, clbB, clbC, clbF, clbG, clbK, clbM, clbP, clbQ, clbS, clbN, and clnO, with sequence identities ranging from 96% to 100%. These loci were primarily mapped to a single large contig (contig_12), supporting the presence of a conserved pks genomic island despite fragmentation in the draft assembly (Supplementary File 3).

In addition to colibactin-associated genes, several virulence- and fitness-related determinants were identified in the E. coli Nissle genome, including curli fiber genes (csgA, csgB, csgC, csgF, csgG), fimbrial genes (fimD, fmlA), the autotransporter adhesin gene ag43, the quorum-sensing gene luxS, and the stress response regulator bolA (Table 4).

Raw quality scores (Q16–Q17) reflect initial sequencing output before downstream processing. To ensure analytical robustness, all FASTQ files were subjected to stringent quality filtering using fastp and Trimmomatic. Reads with sliding-window quality below Q20 or lengths shorter than 50 bp were removed. High-quality reads were then screened for host-derived contamination before de novo assembly. All final assemblies met the minimum coverage and quality thresholds required for downstream comparative analyses and database submission. The incomplete recovery of the clb biosynthetic locus as a single contiguous contig is consistent with known assembly limitations in repetitive, modular non-ribosomal peptide synthetase–polyketide synthase (NRPS–PKS) regions. These genomic architectures are frequently fragmented in short-read or draft assemblies due to repeat structure complexity. Accordingly, the observed discrepancy between PCR positivity and fragmented assembly was interpreted in the context of assembly resolution limits rather than biological absence. Targeted contig-level alignments and antiSMASH-based cluster reconstruction further supported the presence of the pks genomic island across multiple fragmented regions.

Discussion

Gastrointestinal cancers remain among the most prevalent malignancies in Türkiye, with gastric and colorectal cancers representing major contributors to cancer-related morbidity and mortality. Although tumorigenesis is multifactorial, increasing evidence highlights the role of microbial factors in modulating both initiation and progression. In particular, Helicobacter pylori in gastric carcinogenesis and colibactin-producing Escherichia coli in colorectal cancer have emerged as key microbial candidates implicated in these processes (Akinduti et al. 2024; Tariq et al. 2022; Yalçın 2009). In this study, we investigated the distribution of H. pylori virulence-associated genes in gastric cancer tissues and colibactin (pks)-associated determinants in colorectal cancer samples within a Turkish cohort.

The gastric cohort was limited in size due to strict exclusion criteria, particularly recent antibiotic and proton pump inhibitor use, as well as the exploratory nature of the study design. Within this group, one healthy control sample was positive for all evaluated H. pylori virulence genes, consistent with the well-established phenomenon of asymptomatic colonization in a substantial proportion of infected individuals (Liou et al. 2020; Xu et al. 2024). Conversely, one gastric cancer sample tested negative for all targeted genes. This finding may reflect several non-mutually exclusive explanations, including reduced bacterial load, sampling heterogeneity, DNA degradation, assay sensitivity limitations, or true H. pylori-negative gastric carcinogenesis.

Importantly, H. pylori-independent pathways of gastric cancer development have been previously described, including Epstein–Barr virus-associated carcinogenesis, host genetic susceptibility, and interactions with other members of the gastric and oral microbiota (Massip et al. 2019; Uemura et al. 2001; Giammanco et al. 2023). Taken together, these observations reinforce the etiologic heterogeneity of gastric cancer and highlight the need for integrated analyses combining microbial, viral, and host genomic data.

In the colorectal cancer cohort, colibactin-associated gene signatures were detected in four of 19 patients, while no positivity was observed among healthy controls. From these samples, two bacterial isolates were successfully recovered and subjected to whole-genome sequencing for higher-resolution characterization. One isolate was identified as Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN), a widely used probiotic strain that nonetheless harbors the pks genomic island responsible for colibactin biosynthesis (Nougayrède and Chagneau 2021; Falzone et al. 2024).

Although initial genome annotation did not reconstruct a complete clb operon as a single contiguous unit, complementary antiSMASH and BLAST analyses supported the presence of a conserved pks genomic island distributed across fragmented contigs. This pattern is consistent with known limitations of draft genome assemblies in resolving large, repetitive non-ribosomal peptide synthetase–polyketide synthase (NRPS–PKS) regions, rather than indicating true absence of the locus. These findings align with ongoing discussions regarding the dual biological nature of EcN, particularly its well-established probiotic properties alongside its latent genotoxic potential under specific conditions.

EcN has been extensively reported to exert beneficial effects, including enhancement of epithelial barrier integrity, modulation of host immune responses, and competitive exclusion of pathogenic enterobacteria. However, its possession of a functional pks island and capacity for colibactin production have raised important questions regarding its long-term safety, particularly in inflamed or tumor-associated microenvironments where bacterial–host interactions may be altered. Our findings do not imply a causal relationship between EcN colonization and carcinogenesis; rather, they provide baseline evidence of pks-positive strain presence within a Turkish clinical cohort, contributing to the broader characterization of its ecological distribution.

The second isolate was identified as Citrobacter braakii, an opportunistic member of the Enterobacteriaceae family. In contrast to the EcN isolate, no pks genomic island was detected. Instead, genes associated with fimbrial structures and biofilm formation were observed (Nougayrède et al. 2021; Jabeen et al. 2023). The recovery of Enterobacteriaceae species on selective media is consistent with their known adaptability to gastrointestinal niches and culture conditions (Sonnenborn 2016). While C. braakii is not classically implicated in colorectal cancer-associated microbiota signatures (Bourgieux et al. 2025; Kostic and Gevers 2019), its detection suggests that less-characterized opportunistic taxa may also contribute to the broader microbial landscape in cancer-associated environments.

Overall, the observed prevalence of B2 phylogroup-associated genotoxic E. coli appeared relatively low in this cohort. This finding suggests that, within this population, other microbial taxa such as Fusobacterium nucleatum or enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis may play more prominent roles in shaping colorectal cancer-associated dysbiosis. However, given the exploratory design and limited sample size, these interpretations should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively small cohort size, driven by strict exclusion criteria, inevitably reduced statistical power and limited robust prevalence estimation and multivariate analysis. In addition, incomplete or inconsistent clinical staging information for some retrospective samples constrained the ability to correlate microbial features with tumor progression. Despite these limitations, the present study provides preliminary but valuable baseline data on the distribution of pks-positive E. coli and H. pylori virulence profiles in a Turkish gastrointestinal cancer cohort. These findings may serve as a foundation for future multicenter, longitudinal studies with larger sample sizes and integrated multi-omics approaches.

Conclusions

These findings provide preliminary insights into the multifactorial nature of gastrointestinal cancers within the Turkish population. Although colibactin-associated signals were relatively infrequent, the detection of a pks-positive Escherichia coli Nissle isolate, together with the identification of Citrobacter braakii, underscores the need to broaden the scope of microbial profiling in colorectal cancer beyond traditionally emphasized taxa. In gastric cancer, the presence of virulent H. pylori strains in an asymptomatic individual, alongside an H. pylori-negative gastric cancer case, further supports the etiological heterogeneity of gastric carcinogenesis. Collectively, these observations reinforce the concept that gastrointestinal malignancies arise from complex and context-dependent host–microbe interactions. Larger, well-powered studies integrating microbial, clinical, and host genomic parameters are required to validate and extend these findings.
