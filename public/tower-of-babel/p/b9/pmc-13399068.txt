VISTA Deficiency Exacerbates Autoimmune Uveitis by Promoting Microglial Activation via the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB Pathway
Authors: Hui Feng, Baorui Chu, Xianyang Liu, Chao Wu, Fan Cao, Shuhao Zeng, Yakun Wang, Xinyuan Chen, Shangze Gao, Wenxian Yang, Hui Yang, Hong Wang, Shengping Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeVISTA, an immune checkpoint enriched in microglia, regulates inflammatory signaling. Given microglial activation drives autoimmune uveitis, we investigated whether VISTA protects against experimental autoimmune uveitis (EAU) by modulating retinal microglia.MethodsVISTA expression was analyzed by flow cytometry in active VKH patients and healthy controls. Functional studies in LPS/IFN-γ-stimulated BV2 microglia used genetic knockdown/overexpression and modulating antibodies (13F3, MH5A). Activation status, cytokine secretion, migration, and TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling were assessed. An EAU mouse model received intravitreal adeno-associated virus–mediated VISTA overexpression, with severity evaluated clinically and histopathologically.ResultsVISTA was downregulated in circulating immune cells of VKH patients and in retinal microglia during EAU. In vitro, inflammatory stimuli reduced microglial VISTA. Its knockdown or blockade exacerbated microglial activation, pro-inflammatory mediator secretion (TNF-α, iNOS, COX2), and migration, while overexpression or agonism suppressed activation. Critically, intravitreal VISTA overexpression alleviated EAU severity. Mechanistically, VISTA deficiency potentiated activation by enhancing TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling.ConclusionsVISTA is a crucial gatekeeper of ocular immune homeostasis. Its downregulation promotes uveitis via microglial TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway activation, making VISTA signaling restoration a promising therapeutic strategy.

Uveitis is a common ocular inflammatory disease with diverse etiologies, particularly affecting the working-age population. It is estimated that up to 10% of individuals in this demographic may experience vision impairment due to uveitis, highlighting its substantial socio-economic burden.1 Uveitis encompasses a spectrum of inflammatory conditions that can affect the uvea, retina, and other ocular structures. The pathogenesis of uveitis is complex, involving genetic predisposition, environmental triggers, and dysregulated immune responses.2 In recent years, significant progress has been made in understanding the molecular basis of uveitis pathogenesis, leading to improved diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. The identification of key immune pathways and mediators, such as Th17 cells, regulatory T cells, and cytokines including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-17, has provided valuable insights into the disease's immunopathology.3,4 Accumulating evidence suggests that activated microglia may be a pivotal factor in the pathogenesis of uveitis. For instance, using in vivo multimodal imaging, a significant increase in microglial density around the optic nerve head and retinal vasculature was recorded on day 14 following the establishment of experimental autoimmune uveitis (EAU).5 Furthermore, Berasategui et al.6 described a positive correlation between the number of hyperreflective foci, presumed to be microglia, and central macular thickness, as well as a negative correlation with visual acuity improvement in non-infectious uveitis patients with macular edema. Therefore investigating the role of activated microglia in uveitis development may be of significant importance.

As key regulators of immune homeostasis and self-tolerance, immune checkpoint molecules have been implicated in the pathogenesis of uveitis. V-domain immunoglobulin suppressor of T-cell activation (VISTA) has garnered increasing attention as a potent negative regulator of immune responses. VISTA is predominantly expressed on hematopoietic cells, including myeloid cells and T cells, where it functions to suppress T-cell activation and promote immune tolerance.7,8 Dysregulation of VISTA has been associated with various autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, suggesting its potential role in modulating ocular inflammation.9 Vsir knockout mice (Vsir⁻/⁻) exhibit a loss of T cell-mediated peripheral tolerance and develop inflammatory phenotypes in multiple organs. When bred onto an autoimmune-prone background, VISTA deficiency accelerates disease development in the experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) model of multiple sclerosis and the systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).10,11 However, the specific contribution of VISTA to the pathogenesis of autoimmune uveitis remains unclear. Elucidating the mechanisms by which VISTA regulates immune responses in the context of uveitis may provide novel insights into disease pathogenesis and pave the way for developing targeted immunotherapies.

This study aims to investigate how VISTA plays a critical role within microglia in an autoimmune uveitis model, focusing on its regulatory effects on microglial activation, cytokine production, and inflammatory pathways. By delving into the molecular and cellular mechanisms through which VISTA modulates ocular inflammation, we hope to identify novel therapeutic strategies for managing this challenging disease.

Material and Methods

Study Subjects and Clinical Evaluation

The diagnosis of Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada (VKH) disease was established based on the criteria set forth by the International Naming Committee.12 The terminology and anatomical classification of uveitis were described according to the recommendations of the International Uveitis Nomenclature Standardization Working Group.13 Specifically, active VKH patients included those in the acute uveitic phase or chronic recurrent phase, presenting with active anterior uveitis (e.g., mutton-fat keratic precipitates, anterior chamber cell count ≥1+, iris nodules), vitritis (vitreous haze score ≥1+), and/or active posterior uveitis (e.g., presence of bullous serous retinal detachment, diffuse choroiditis, or focal areas of subretinal fluid).14 All enrolled active patients had not used any systemic immunosuppressants prior to blood draw.

The human study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Tongren Hospital (approval number: TREC2024-KY106) and conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before sample collection.

Flow Cytometry

Peripheral venous blood (5 mL) was collected from all participants (healthy donors and uveitis patients) with heparin for anticoagulation. Samples were processed within four hours of collection. Whole blood staining was performed using red blood cell lysis: 100 µL of anticoagulated whole blood was added to 2 mL of 1 × RBC Lysis Buffer (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), incubated at room temperature protected from light for 10 minutes, centrifuged at 300g for 5 minutes, and the supernatant was discarded followed by one wash with PBS. Viability staining was performed using Zombie Aqua dye (diluted 1:1000; BioLegend) at room temperature protected from light for 15 minutes. Subsequently, fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies were added: anti-human CD45 (BioLegend), anti-human CD3 (BioLegend), anti-human CD14 (BioLegend), and anti-human VISTA (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), and incubated at room temperature protected from light for 20 minutes. After staining, cells were washed once with cell wash buffer and finally resuspended in 0.5 mL of cell wash buffer. All samples were acquired using a Cytek NL-CLC3000 spectral flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences, Fremont, CA, USA) and analyzed with SpectroFlo CLC software.

Animals

Female C57BL/6J mice (five to 8 weeks of age) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Animal Care Service of the Beijing Institute of Ophthalmology. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital (approval number: TRLAWEC2024-03) and the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

Reagents

Human IRBP651–670 peptide (LAQGAYRTAVDLESLASQLT) was synthesized and purified (purity >98%) by Shanghai Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Complete Freund's adjuvant and pertussis toxin were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Heat-killed Mycobacterium tuberculosis strain H37Ra was obtained from BD Biosciences (Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).

Intravitreal Injection and Induction of EAU

To establish an intraocular VISTA-overexpressing mouse model, five-week-old C57BL/6J mice were randomly divided into two groups: the VISTA overexpression treatment group and the adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector control group. Viral intervention was performed via intravitreal injection: mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% pentobarbital sodium, and corneal surface anesthesia was applied using proparacaine hydrochloride eye drops (Alcon-Couvreur, Bornem, Belgium). After disinfecting the periocular skin with iodophor, 1 µL AAV viral suspension was precisely injected into one eye using a Hamilton microsyringe (32G needle) under a dissecting microscope. Three weeks after injection, successful VISTA overexpression in the treatment group was confirmed by observing fluorescence and via Western blot analysis, after which EAU induction was performed.

To induce EAU, mice were subcutaneously immunized with 500 µg of human IRBP651–670 peptide in PBS containing 20% DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich Corp., St. Louis, MO, USA), emulsified with an equal volume of complete Freund's adjuvant containing 5 mg/mL M. tuberculosis strain H37Ra. Simultaneously, mice received an intraperitoneal injection of 1 µg pertussis toxin (Sigma-Aldrich Corp., St. Louis, MO, USA).

Clinical and Histopathological Evaluation

On day 14 post-immunization, the clinical symptoms of EAU were assessed by slit-lamp examination. The clinical severity of ocular inflammation was evaluated in a blinded manner by two independent experienced observers and scored on a scale of 0–5 (allowing half-point increments) based on five independent criteria and Caspi's criteria.15 After clinical scoring, mice were euthanized, eyeballs were enucleated, fixed in formaldehyde, and embedded in paraffin. Serial 5 µm thick sections were cut along the pupillary-optic nerve axis. After hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, histopathological severity was graded on a scale of 0–4 according to Caspi's criteria and a previously published protocol.15 Two blinded observers independently scored the samples, and inter-rater agreement was evaluated using weighted Kappa. Weighted Kappa values were 0.84 (95% CI, 0.76–0.92) for clinical scores and 0.81 (95% CI, 0.72–0.90) for histopathological scores, both indicating good agreement.

Cell Culture and Treatment

The murine BV2 microglial cell line was purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Cells were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (all from Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. The anti-VISTA antibody (clone 13F3) and isotype control antibody were purchased from BioXcell (NH, USA). Hamster anti-mouse VISTA antibody (clone MH5A) and control hamster antibody were purchased from BioLegend (San Diego, USA). Cells were seeded in six-well plates at 2 × 10⁵ cells/well and randomly divided into five groups: Control, LPS (1 µg/mL) + IFN-γ (500 ng/mL), LPS+IFN-γ+Control-Ig (10 µg/mL), LPS+IFN-γ+13F3 (10 µg/mL), and LPS+IFN-γ+MH5A (10 µg/mL). After seeding for 24 hours, cells were pretreated with 13F3 or MH5A for two hours, followed by the addition of LPS and IFN-γ. Cells were collected after 24 hours of stimulation.

Western Blotting

Total proteins were extracted by homogenization in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing 1% protease inhibitors (Beyotime). Cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions were separated using nuclear and cytoplasmic extraction reagents (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Protein concentration was measured using a BCA kit (Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated by 4% to 20% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (ACE Biotechnology) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) using the Bio-Rad TransBlot Turbo system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Membranes were blocked with Tris-buffered saline solution containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBS-T) and 5% (w/v) non-fat milk for two hours at room temperature. Subsequently, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in Primary Antibody Dilution Buffer (Beyotime) overnight at 4°C. Primary antibodies included: anti-VISTA (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-iNOS (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-COX2 (1:1000; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), anti-TNF-α (1:1000; Abcam), anti-TLR4 (1:1000; Abcam), anti-MyD88 (1:1000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-NF-κB p65 (1:1000; Abcam), anti-phospho-NF-κB p65 (1:1000; Abcam), anti-IκB-alpha (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-phospho-IκB-alpha (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), and anti-β-actin (1:10,000; Affinity Biosciences, Changzhou, China). The secondary antibody was goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) HRP (1:10,000; Affinity Biosciences).

Total RNA Extraction and Real-Time Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and RNA concentration was assessed using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix Perfect (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd, Nanjing, China). Real-time PCR quantification (RT-qPCR) was performed on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.). A β-actin was used as a housekeeping gene for normalization. Gene expression levels were normalized to the reference gene and calculated using the relative quantification method.

Immunofluorescence

Retinal paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, permeabilized with 3% Triton X-100 for 15 minutes, and blocked with goat serum for 2 hours. Sections were then incubated with specific primary antibodies at 4°C overnight: rabbit anti-iNOS (1:50; Huabio, Woburn, MA, USA), rabbit anti-COX‐2 (1:50; Huabio), rabbit anti-TNF‐α (1:50; Proteintech) antibodies, rabbit anti-VISTA (1:200; Cell Signaling Technology) and mouse anti-Iba-1 (1:200; Abcam). The following day, sections were washed with PBS and incubated with corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies at room temperature protected from light for one hour: Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H+L) and Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) (1:500; Beyotime). Finally, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, slides were mounted, and images were captured using a confocal microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The mean fluorescence intensity was measured using ImageJ 1.51t (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). For each experimental group, at least three random fields of view per retinal section were selected for quantification. A total of five mice per group (n = 5) were analyzed.

Transwell Migration Assay

The Corning Transwell (Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA) chamber system (5 µm pore size, suitable for BV2 cells) was used. BV2 cells (100,000 per chamber) from different treatment groups were seeded in the upper chamber containing medium with 2% FBS. The lower chamber contained medium with 10% FBS. After 24 hours of incubation at 37°C, LPS (1 µg/mL) and IFN-γ (500 ng/mL) were added to the lower chamber for stimulation. After another 24 hours of culture, BV2 cells were washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 minutes, and stained with crystal violet staining solution (Beyotime). Non-migrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed. Stained cells were imaged and quantified using ImageJ 1.51t (National Institutes of Health).

RNA Sequencing and Data Analysis

RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. RNA quality control, library construction, and transcriptome sequencing and analysis were performed by APExBIO Technology LLC (APExBIO Technology LLC, Houston, TX, USA). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on the criteria of Padj < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| > 1.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), with continuous data presented as mean ± SD. Prior to statistical testing, data distributions were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and for homogeneity of variances using Levene's test. For data meeting the assumptions of normality and equal variances, group comparisons were performed using unpaired Student's t-test (two groups) or one-way ANOVA (multiple groups). For data that did not meet these assumptions, non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test) were applied. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Each experiment included ≥3 independent replicates, and data visualization was conducted with GraphPad Prism 8.0 (San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Reduced VISTA Expression on T Cells and Monocytes in Autoimmune Uveitis Patients

To explore the role of VISTA in uveitis pathogenesis, we collected peripheral blood from healthy subjects and patients with active VKH disease. Ultimately, seven healthy subjects as controls and 6 patients with active VKH disease were included. To delineate the expression changes of VISTA on peripheral blood immune cells in patients with active VKH disease, flow cytometric analysis was performed on T cells and monocytes separately. Within the T-cell population, VISTA demonstrated a differential expression pattern. The proportion of VISTA-positive T cells was significantly lower in patients with active disease compared to healthy controls (P = 0.0007) (Figs. 1A–C). In contrast, VISTA was constitutively expressed on monocytes, with a positivity rate exceeding 99%. Accordingly, the expression level was quantified using the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI), which revealed a significant reduction in VISTA MFI on monocytes from active VKH patients (P = 0.001) (Figs. 1D–F).

VISTA expression is down-regulated on circulating T cells and monocytes in active VKH disease. (A–C) Representative flow plots and summary data showing the percentage of VISTA+ cells within the CD3+ T-cell gate; (D–F) Histograms and MFI quantification of VISTA on CD14+ monocytes. n = 6 patients versus 7 healthy controls.

Downregulation of VISTA in Retinal Microglia in Autoimmune Uveitis

Based on our team's single-cell RNA sequencing analysis of retinal immune cells in EAU mice at 0, 14, 21, and 28 days after immunization, we found that microglia were highly enriched at day 14, suggesting a close association between microglia and inflammatory responses and disease severity in EAU.16 Concurrently, we found that VISTA expression was significantly concentrated in microglia (Fig. 2A). Analyzing the temporal changes in VISTA expression in retinal microglia across the four time points, we observed that VISTA was significantly downregulated in the microglial population of the autoimmune uveitis mouse model at the peak of retinal inflammation (day 14), and gradually increased at days 21 and 28 after immunization as inflammation resolved (Fig. 2B).

VISTA is lost in retinal microglia at the peak of EAU inflammation. (A) T-SNE plot of scRNA-seq data showing selective VISTA enrichment in microglia at day 14 after injection. (B) Pseudotime analysis revealing down-regulation of VISTA in microglia at day 14, with recovery by days 21–28. (C, D) qPCR and Western blot of whole retina: VISTA mRNA and protein drop in EAU versu. control, paralleling iNOS/COX2 induction. (E) Retinal flat-mounts: VISTA (green) and Iba-1 (red) co-staining shows strong VISTA signal in resting microglia of controls and faint signal in activated microglia at day 14 EAU.

To validate the single-cell sequencing results, we assessed VISTA mRNA and protein levels in the retinas of EAU and control mice. First, we successfully established a stable EAU model. Compared to control mice, EAU mice exhibited conjunctival and ciliary body congestion, anterior chamber inflammatory cell infiltration, and more retinal folds. Clinical and pathological scores were consistent with typical EAU characteristics (Supplementary Figs. S1A, S1B). To clarify whether the elevated inflammatory factors in retinal tissue originated from microglia or other cell types, we performed dual immunofluorescence staining for Iba-1 (a microglial marker) together with iNOS, COX2, or TNF-α on retinal sections. Confocal imaging demonstrated that the majority of iNOS, COX2, and TNF-α positive signals were colocalized with Iba-1 cells in EAU retinas, indicating that activated microglia serve as the primary source of these pro-inflammatory mediators during active EAU (Supplementary Figs. S1C–E). We found that compared to controls, alongside increased inflammatory factors iNOS and COX2, both VISTA mRNA and protein levels were significantly decreased in EAU mice (Figs. 2C, 2D). We then performed co-staining for VISTA and Iba-1 on retinal sections from EAU and control mice. Results showed clear VISTA expression in Iba1-positive cells in control mouse retinas, whereas only faint VISTA staining was detected in activated microglia in EAU mice (Fig. 2E).

VISTA Downregulation Mediates Pro-Inflammatory Responses in Microglia In Vitro

To further investigate the role of VISTA in regulating microglial function, we established an inflammatory model in BV2 cells using LPS (1 µg/mL) and IFN-γ (500 ng/mL). These pro-inflammatory agonists are known to activate microglia into an inflammatory state. Changes in VISTA mRNA and protein expression in BV2 cells at different time points after LPS+IFN-γ stimulation were detected by RT-qPCR and Western blot. We found that as LPS+IFN-γ stimulation duration increased, the mRNA and protein expression of iNOS, COX2, and TNF-α in BV2 cells was significantly upregulated, whereas corresponding VISTA mRNA and protein expression showed an opposite downward trend (Fig. 3). Similarly, we observed the same trend in another inflammatory model stimulating BV2 cells with IL-17 (100 ng/mL) and IFN-γ (100 ng/mL) (Supplementary Material S2). These results suggest that the inflammatory response in microglia may be associated with the downregulation of VISTA.

VISTA is down-regulated in BV2 microglia upon pro-inflammatory stimulation. BV2 cells were treated with LPS (1 µg/mL) + IFN-γ (500 ng/mL) for the indicated times. (A) RT-qPCR time-course showing reciprocal regulation: iNOS, COX-2, and TNF-α rise whereas VISTA mRNA falls. (B, C) Western blots and densitometry confirming parallel loss of VISTA protein and induction of iNOS/COX-2.

VISTA Knockdown Upregulates Inflammatory Response and Promotes Microglial Migration

To further explore the regulatory role of VISTA on microglia, we used ShVsir (1, 2, and 3) lentiviruses to knock down VISTA expression in BV2 cells, and knockdown efficiency was verified by RT-qPCR and Western blot. Compared to BV2 cells transfected with Vehicle, both VISTA RNA and protein expression levels were significantly reduced in BV2 cells transfected with ShVsir (1, 2, and 3) (Figs. 4A, 4B). ShVsir1 was used in subsequent experiments because of its higher and more stable knockdown efficiency. Next, we investigated the impact of silencing VISTA in BV2 cells on microglial activation and migration. Our results showed that VISTA deficiency promoted the expression of microglial activation markers iNOS and COX2, as well as the secretion of the inflammatory factor TNF-α in the cell culture supernatant (Figs. 4C, 4D). Furthermore, silencing VISTA significantly promoted cell migration (Figs. 4E, 4F). We also constructed OE-Vsir lentivirus to overexpress VISTA in BV2 cells, and verified that both VISTA RNA and protein expression levels were significantly increased in BV2 cells transfected with OE-Vsir (Supplementary Material 3A, 3B). Our results showed that VISTA overexpression significantly reduced the expression of microglial activation markers iNOS and COX2 (Supplementary Material 3C, 3D).

Loss of VISTA signals licenses BV2 microglia to activate and migrate. (A, B) Lentiviral shRNAs (ShVsir1-3) efficiently deplete VISTA mRNA and protein versus vehicle control; ShVsir1 yielded >80% knock-down and was selected for downstream assays. (C, D) ShVsir1-transduced cells show elevated iNOS/COX-2 expression and increased TNF-α release into medium (ELISA). (E, F) Transwell migration assay: VISTA silencing raises the number of migrated cells per field. (G, H) Treatment with VISTA mAb 13F3 (20 µg/mL, 24 h) recapitulates the pro-inflammatory phenotype, enhancing iNOS and COX-2 levels relative to isotype control.

Next, we observed the effect of specific VISTA monoclonal antibodies on microglial activation. An inhibitory monoclonal antibody (mAb, 13F3) specific for VISTA can reduce VISTA's suppressive effect on T cells and monocytes.17,18 Our results showed that compared to the control IgG group, 13F3 promoted the expression of microglial activation markers iNOS and COX2 (Figs. 4G, 4H). We also explored the effect of an agonistic monoclonal antibody against VISTA (MH5A) on microglia. This type of antibody can suppress T-cell–mediated immune responses in multiple systems (including GVHD and Con A-induced hepatitis) and possesses immunosuppressive activity.19 Our results similarly showed that compared to the control IgG group, MH5A significantly reduced the expression of microglial activation markers iNOS and COX2 (Supplementary Material 3E, 3F). In summary, we conclude that VISTA deficiency accelerates microglial activation and migration.

Overexpression of VISTA Alleviates Disease Progression in EAU

To explore whether VISTA could alleviate the disease phenotype during the peak inflammatory phase of EAU, we randomly divided 5-week-old C57BL/6J mice into a VISTA overexpression treatment group and an AAV empty vector control group. The VISTA overexpression group received an intravitreal injection of AAV-OeVsir (1 µL), whereas the AAV empty vector control group received an intravitreal injection of AAV-Vehicle (1 µL). Three weeks after injection, VISTA overexpression efficiency was confirmed by observing fluorescence and Western blot (Figs. 5A, 5B). Subsequently, EAU was induced. Compared to EAU mice in the AAV vector control group, EAU mice overexpressing VISTA exhibited milder conjunctival congestion and adhesions, less fluorescein leakage on FFA angiography, and fewer retinal folds and inflammatory cell infiltration. Clinical and pathological scores were consistent with these findings (Fig. 5C, 5D). We further evaluated the clinical and histopathological scores of the contralateral (non-injected) eyes of AAV-VISTA-overexpressing mice. No significant differences in either clinical or histopathological scores were observed in the contralateral eyes of the experimental group compared with the AAV vector control group (Supplementary Fig. S3G).

(B) Western blot of retinal lysates revealed an elevation in VISTA expression in the AAV-OeVsir group compared with the AAV-Vehicle group. Retinas were collected on day 14 after immunization for Western blot analysis to determine if VISTA overexpression could modulate microglial activation and the inflammatory response. Compared to EAU mice in the AAV vector control group, EAU mice overexpressing VISTA showed significantly reduced expression of iNOS, COX2, and TNF-α (Figs. 5E, 5F). These results indicate that therapeutic intervention by overexpressing VISTA can significantly reduce microglial activation and alleviate the EAU disease phenotype.

VISTA Regulates Microglial Activation via the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB Signaling Pathway

To investigate the regulatory role of VISTA in the activation state and inflammatory response of BV2 microglial cells, we stimulated both VISTA-knockdown BV2 cells and control cells with LPS and IFN-γ, followed by transcriptomic sequencing analysis. Differential expression analysis revealed a total of 142 upregulated mRNAs and 154 downregulated mRNAs (Fig. 6A). Gene Ontology functional enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes indicated significant enrichment in pathways such as the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway (Fig. 6B). A heatmap displayed 24 upregulated and 30 downregulated genes (screening criteria: |log2FC| > 2, Padj < 0.05) (Fig. 6C). Given the more severe inflammatory response following VISTA knockdown, molecules closely associated with microglial activation and inflammation, including Ccl9, Il6, and Cd36, were significantly upregulated, along with a marked upregulation trend in Rel, a member of the NF-κB family. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that VISTA may influence microglial function by regulating the classical inflammatory pathway TLR4/MYD88/NF-κB.

VISTA tunes microglial activation by restraining the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB axis. (A, B) The volcano plot of DEGs in the vehicle+LPS+IFN-γ and ShVsir+LPS+IFN-γ cells (|log2FC| > 1, Padj < 0.05). (C) The heatmap of significant DEGs in the abovementioned groups (|log2FC| > 1, Padj < 0.05). (D) BV2 cells transduced with VISTA shRNA (ShVsir1) or control were stimulated with LPS+IFN-γ. Knock-down of VISTA elevated TLR4 and MyD88 expression and enhanced phosphorylation of IκB-α and NF-κB p65. (E) Conversely, VISTA over-expression suppressed LPS+IFN-γ–induced up-regulation of TLR4/MyD88 and reduced phospho-IκB-α and phospho-p65 levels.

Western blot analysis showed that, compared to the control group, LPS and IFN-γ treatment of VISTA-deficient BV2 cells increased the phosphorylation of IκB-α and NF-κB p65, and increased the expression of TLR4 and MYD88 in BV2 cells (Fig. 6C). Similarly, we detected the expression of proteins in the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway in OeVsir-treated BV2 cells under LPS and IFN-γ stimulation. Compared to the control group (Vehicle), the OeVsir group showed significantly reduced expression of TLR4 and MyD88 upon LPS and IFN-γ stimulation. Concurrently, the phosphorylation levels of IκB-α and NF-κB p65 were decreased, indicating suppressed activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway (Fig. 6D). The above results suggest that VISTA may regulate the activation of BV2 cells by modulating the expression of key proteins in the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway.

Discussion

This study confirms that VISTA, as a novel negative immune checkpoint molecule, plays a significant regulatory role in the occurrence and development of autoimmune uveitis. We found that VISTA is specifically highly expressed in microglia within the mouse retina; furthermore, VISTA expression was significantly downregulated in active VKH patients, the EAU mouse model, and microglia under inflammatory stimulation. This phenomenon suggests that VISTA may suppress excessive immune activation in the early stages of the disease by maintaining immune homeostasis, while its decreased expression is closely associated with the pathogenesis of uveitis.

In terms of functional validation, this study confirmed that modulating VISTA expression directly affects the activation state of microglia and the progression of EAU. Specific knockdown of VISTA enhanced microglial activation and migration capacity, whereas intraocular overexpression of VISTA effectively suppressed microglial activation and significantly alleviated the clinical and histopathological phenotypes of EAU. Further investigation confirmed that VISTA negatively regulates microglial inflammatory activation through the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway. Our findings indicate a close relationship between autoimmune uveitis and VISTA.

Our research found that VISTA is specifically highly expressed in retinal microglia and is significantly downregulated in microglia under inflammatory stimulation. Previous studies in the murine and human central nervous systems also found VISTA mRNA and protein primarily detected in microglia.20 Over 95% of murine microglia surface-express VISTA protein, and its expression levels in murine and human microglia are comparable to mature microglial markers (e.g., CX3CR1, TMEM119, P2RY12, and ITGAM).20,21 Previous studies have reported VISTA's role in microglial homeostasis maintenance and innate immune responses. For example, Yoon et al.22 showed that VISTA is crucial for the uptake of apoptotic cells by phagocytes. Similarly, in other autoimmune diseases, such as EAE (a mouse model of multiple sclerosis), microglial VISTA expression is significantly reduced at all disease stages (from onset to remission).10 Likewise, in mouse models of various neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer's disease and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, activated “disease-associated microglia” also show significant downregulation of VISTA expression.23–25 Furthermore, consistent decreases in microglial VISTA expression have been observed in models of LPS-induced systemic inflammation, cerebral ischemia (stroke), and glioma, and this decreased expression correlates with the level of inflammation.20,26,27 Therefore the downregulation of VISTA, a key negative regulator of immune homeostasis, might be a critical factor in the onset of inflammatory diseases. Similarly, our results demonstrate a close relationship between autoimmune uveitis and decreased VISTA expression. VISTA expression levels in the EAU mouse model and in LPS-stimulated microglia were markedly lower than in control mice and cells, highly consistent with the extensive evidence from CNS diseases.

As retinal tissue from uveitis patients was unavailable, we explored changes in VISTA expression on peripheral blood monocytes and T cells in patients with active autoimmune uveitis. Consistent with our finding of reduced VISTA expression in activated microglia, we observed significantly lower VISTA expression levels on peripheral blood T cells and monocytes in active uveitis patients compared to healthy controls. Previous studies on VISTA in peripheral blood of autoimmune uveitis patients also found significantly reduced VISTA expression in all peripheral blood myeloid cell subsets compared to healthy volunteers, including neutrophils, monocytes, myeloid dendritic cells (mDCs), and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs). It was also decreased in the vast majority of peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets, including CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, Treg cells, Th1 cells, Th2 cells, Th17 cells, and natural killer (NK) cells.28 This evidence demonstrates that VISTA is constitutively expressed on various immune cells and acts as a homeostatic regulatory mechanism, actively modulating the immune system at the earliest stages of an immune response; reduced VISTA expression may be closely related to the occurrence and progression of autoimmune uveitis.29 However, it must be noted that, limited by the accessibility of human retinal samples, this study did not deeply analyze the potential cell-specific functions of VISTA in retinal microglia versus peripheral immune cells (e.g., monocytes and T cells). Furthermore, the relative contribution of VISTA in the local retinal environment versus systemic peripheral immunity to disease pathogenesis remains to be elucidated.

Studies in various mouse immune disease models, such as EAE, lupus, and psoriasis, have found that VISTA plays an important role in regulating the signaling and activation of innate immune cells, including myeloid dendritic cells (DCs), macrophages, and TCRγδ T cells. Initial examination of eight- to nine-month-old Vsir⁻/⁻ mice revealed spontaneous immune cell infiltration in the lungs, liver, and pancreas, and accumulation of myeloid chemokines (Eotaxin, IP-10, MCP-1, MIG) in the serum. Splenomegaly was also observed, accompanied by increased numbers of CD11b+ myeloid cells (including myeloid DCs, macrophages, and neutrophils).10 VISTA deficiency exacerbated disease in the EAE model of multiple sclerosis and the sle1.sle3 model of lupus.10,19 Consistently, treatment with a VISTA-blocking monoclonal antibody led to more severe disease in both the EAE and BWF1 lupus models.18 VISTA blockade not only significantly enhanced CD4+ T cell activation but also, in the sle1.sle3 background, VISTA deficiency promoted the activation of myeloid cells by upregulating the expression of costimulatory molecules CD80, CD40, and MHCII, and the production of cytokines (IFN-γ and IL-10) after LPS treatment.19 In imiquimod (IMQ)-induced mouse skin inflammation studies, VISTA deficiency exacerbated IMQ-induced psoriasiform inflammation. Enhanced IMQ/TLR7 signaling in Vsir⁻/⁻ myeloid DCs led to hyperactivation of Erk1/2 and Jnk1/2 and augmented IL-23 production. IL-23, in turn, promoted IL-17A expression in TCRγδ T cells and CD4+ Th17 cells.30 These studies collectively support VISTA's role as a negative regulator of both innate and adaptive immunity during the development of autoimmune and inflammatory diseases. In the present study, we found that knockdown of VISTA in microglia or treatment with an inhibitory VISTA monoclonal antibody (13F3) increased the expression of microglial activation markers iNOS and COX2, the secretion of the inflammatory factor TNF-α in the cell culture supernatant, and significantly promoted cell migration. In contrast, overexpression of VISTA or treatment with an agonistic VISTA monoclonal antibody (MH5A) reduced the degree of microglial activation. Similarly, in the EAU animal model, intraocular overexpression of VISTA significantly reduced microglial activation and alleviated the EAU disease phenotype. These results suggest that modulating VISTA levels in vivo can impact the occurrence and development of uveitis, providing experimental evidence for VISTA as a therapeutic target for uveitis.

Previous research observed that VISTA can modulate TLR-mediated signaling in myeloid cells. In Vsir⁻/⁻ mice, Xu et al.30 observed significantly enhanced responses of myeloid cells to various TLR agonists (TLR2/3/4/7/9) and significantly enhanced activation of the MAPK/AP-1 and IKK/NF-κB signaling pathways. Further investigation revealed that VISTA downregulates TLR-mediated signaling by promoting TRAF6 protein degradation and restricting the activation of MAPKs/AP-1 and IKKα/β/NF-κB signaling pathways, whereas blocking VISTA triggered an endogenous TLR/MyD88-mediated pro-inflammatory response. As the TLR4 signaling pathway is a major component of pro-inflammatory signaling and involved in microglial activation,31,32 and combined with previous studies indicating VISTA's role in regulating TLR signaling in myeloid cells, we examined the protein expression of the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway. Compared to the control group, LPS and IFN-γ treatment of VISTA-deficient microglia increased the expression of TLR4 and MYD88, and promoted the phosphorylation of IκB-α and NF-κB p65. The above evidence indicates that VISTA deficiency may promote the expression of key proteins in the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway, exacerbating the activation of BV2 cells, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of uveitis.

The present study has several limitations. First, we locally overexpressed VISTA in the mouse eye via intravitreal AAV injection to minimize systemic interference. Although our results suggest that the protective effect is primarily attributed to local retinal immunomodulation, we also observed reduced VISTA expression in peripheral blood T cells and monocytes of active VKH patients, indicating that alterations in VISTA on peripheral immune cells may also contribute to disease pathogenesis. Therefore, the possibility of VISTA functioning in the peripheral immune system cannot be completely excluded. Second, we used an AAV empty vector as a control but did not include a sham-injected control (needle insertion without injection). Consequently, we cannot completely rule out the potential impact of the intravitreal injection procedure itself on the retinal microenvironment. Third, although our in vitro experiments showed that VISTA downregulation preceded the upregulation of inflammatory mediators, we did not directly assess this temporal relationship in the in vivo EAU model. Therefore the precise causal relationship between changes in VISTA expression and the initiation of inflammation remains to be further validated.

Conclusions

This study deepens the understanding of the mechanism of action of the immune checkpoint protein VISTA in autoimmune diseases. We confirmed that downregulation of VISTA expression (particularly in specific immune cell populations) can promote their polarization towards a pro-inflammatory phenotype and inflammatory responses, participating in the pathogenesis of autoimmune uveitis. VISTA influences the pathogenesis of autoimmune uveitis by regulating microglial function and related signaling pathways. This study provides an important perspective for further exploration of the pathogenesis of autoimmune uveitis and lays a theoretical foundation for the development of clinical treatment strategies based on VISTA-targeted modulation.
