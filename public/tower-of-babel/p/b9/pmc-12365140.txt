Incidence and predictors of respiratory distress among neonates admitted to neonatal intensive care unit, Northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Dereje Woldeyes, Ayenew Negesse, Alehegn Aderaw Alamneh, Selamawit Shita Jemberie, Abateneh Addis Anteneh, Molla Yigzaw Birhanu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRespiratory distress is one of the most common problems that neonates encounter within the first few days of life and is a regular cause of admission to both term and preterm infants. Limited evidence exists on the incidence and predictors of this serious public health problem in resource-limited settings such as Ethiopia. To assess the incidence and evaluate predictors of respiratory distress among neonates admitted to neonatal intensive care units (NICU) at West Gojjam Zone public hospitals, Northwest Ethiopia, in 2024. A hospital-based retrospective follow-up study was conducted using 394 neonates admitted to NICUs from July 1, 2019, to March 31, 2023, at Northwest Ethiopia. Neonates were selected using computer-generated random sampling. Data were entered into Epi-data 4.6 and analyzed in Stata 17.0. Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests compared categorical predictors’ failure status. A Cox proportional hazards model identified RD predictors. Significant associations were declared at a p-value < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval (CI). The overall incidence rate of RD was 5.06 (95% CI: 4.32, 5.93) per 100 neonate-days of observation. Lack of ANC visits [AHR: 1.45 (95% CI: 1.04, 2.02)], multiple pregnancies [AHR: 1.73 (95% CI: 1.07, 2.76)], being male [AHR: 1.48 (95% CI: 1.05, 2.09)], being born to a diabetic mother [AHR: 2.83 (95% CI: 1.43, 5.62)], and low birth weight [AHR: 2.32 (95% CI: 1.19, 4.52)] were identified as significant predictors of RD. One in every twenty neonates experienced respiratory distress (RD) daily. The median time to respiratory occurrence among neonates was twelve days. Absence of ANC visit, multiple pregnancies, being born to diabetic mothers, male sex, and low birth weight were the statistically significant predictors of RD. Encouraging ANC visits, better diabetes control during pregnancy, and close monitoring of multiple pregnancies and low birth weight are highly recommended.Keywords: Neonatal respiratory distress, Incidence, Predictors, NICU, West Gojjam zoneSubject terms: Health care, Risk factors, Signs and symptoms

Introduction

Neonatal respiratory distress is a condition where neonates experience difficulty breathing during the neonatal period1. It can be diagnosed by an abnormal respiratory rate (tachypnea > 60 breaths/min, bradypnea < 30 breaths/min, apnea) and signs of labored breathing (expiratory grunting, nasal flaring, chest wall retraction), with or without cyanosis1,2. Respiratory distress is one of the most common problems neonates face within the first few days of life and is the most frequent cause of admission for both term and preterm infants3,4.

A global report indicated that 47% of the 2.4 million infant deaths were neonates, with an overall newborn incidence of 7% and 1.2–7.2% in low-resource settings3,5,6. The prevalence and incidence of neonatal respiratory distress (RD) varied across the world. For example in Asia; 6.7%7 in India, determined by a prospective cohort study conducted, 34.3% and 4.6% prevalence and incidence respectively in Nepal8, 33.3% at NICU of Pakistan4, and 84.8%9 at Misan Hospital for Child and Maternity in Misan, Iraq. While in low- and middle-income countriesit was 1.2–7.2%6.Furthermore, In Africa, it was 4.83% in Sudan10, 46.5% of Qena University Hospital, Egypt11, and 47.5% in Cameron5.When we came to Ethiopia, a retrospective cohort study conducted at black Lion in Addis Ababa revealed that 8.1 per 100 neonate-days12, and another a retrospective cohort study at Hospitals(University of Gondar Teaching Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Tibebe Ghion Teaching Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, and Debre Markos Specialized Hospital) in Northwest Ethiopia,10.78 per 100 neonate-days13.

In low and middle-income countries (LMICs) including Africa, one in five neonates presenting with RD didn’t survive14. RD can progress to hypoxemia, metabolic acidosis, respiratory failure, cardiopulmonary arrest, septic shock, and death2,8. Various studies conducted in different countries have identified significant predictors of neonatal RD, including prematurity, low and very low birth weight, caesarean delivery, APGAR scores below 7 at the 1 st and 5th minutes, male gender, gestational diabetes, maternal PROM, neonatal sepsis, hypothermia, place of delivery, and history of ANC visits5,12,15–21, gestational age less than 37 weeks19, delivered via caesarean Sects22,23., being multiparous mothers24, second trimester placenta Previa25, PROM and intra-partum maternal fever5, home delivery12, maternal hypertension26, gestational DM27, and birth asphyxia19 were the identified predictors associated with RD. This study was conducted due to the limited evidence available and the priority of improving maternal and neonatal health in Ethiopian health policy, as well as to examine the effect of unobserved variables such as place of residence. This study aimed to estimate the incidence and evaluate the predictors of respiratory distress among Neonates admitted to Neonatal Intensive Care Unit at West Gojjam Zone Public Hospitals, Northwest Ethiopia.

Methods

Study setting, period and design

A hospital-based retrospective cohort study was conducted in West Gojjam Zone, Amhara region, Northwest Ethiopia. The capital, Finote Selam, is located 387 km from Addis Ababa and 176 km from Bahir Dar. West Gojjam Zone is divided into fourteen rural woredas and two administrative towns. According to the 2022/23 annual report of the Zonal health department, the estimated total population is 2,841,894 (1,406,738 males and 1,435,156 females) with 95,778 live births. The zone has 108 health centers, 404 health posts, 180 private clinics, and 8 public hospitals (1 General hospital and 7 primary hospitals). The seven public hospitals provide neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) services28. Finote Selam General Hospital and Bure Primary Hospitals were selected by lottery method and samples were taken from. The NICU at Finote Selam General Hospital has 12 neonatal beds, 18 neonatal nurses, and an average annual admission of 1035 neonates with a total of 3881 neonates admitted over three years and nine months (July 1, 2019, to March 31, 2023). The NICU at Bure Primary Hospital has 6 neonatal beds, 6 neonatal nurses, and an average annual admission of 447 neonates, with a total of 1675 neonates admitted over the same period, resulting in a combined total of 5556 neonates admitted in their first 28 days of life in both hospitals. The study was conducted from May 1 to 30, 2023.

Population

All neonates who developed respiratory distress (RD) at the time of admission of West Gojjam Zone public hospitals’ NICU of Northwest Ethiopia were included in the study. This encompassed all 4500 neonates admitted from July 1, 2019, to March 31, 2023, who met the eligibility criteria were the study population. However, 12 charts were excluded during the data extraction period due to unavailability (5 charts) and incompleteness (7 charts, missing information on sex, age, date of admission, date of RD diagnosis/discharge, and outcome status).

Sample size and sampling procedure

The sample size for both objectives was determined using STATA version 17 statistical software, based on significant predictors from a previous study conducted in Addis Ababa Ethiopia. The following statistical assumptions were considered: withdrawal probability (Pw) = 0.1, assuming a 10% incomplete chart, alpha (significance level) = 0.05, SD = 0.5, and power = 0.8 (Table 1). The largest sample size (N = 406) was selected as the final sample size. There were two sampling methods that were applied to both hospitals and neonates. The first sampling method was selecting Hospitals located in West Gojjam Zones which provide neonatal intensive care (NICU) services were randomly selected by lottery method. Finally, neonates were selected using a computer-generated simple random sampling method using their medical record number (MRN) from NICU register logbook as sampling frame and allocated proportionally.

Studying variables

Time to neonatal RD is the dependent variable. There are different themes of independent variables like socio-demographic predictors: socio-demographic predictors: maternal age, residence, birth weight in gram, sex, date of admission, age in day at admission, date of discharge/event, hospital stay in days1. Obstetrics and gynecological predictors: mode of delivery, place of delivery, GA at birth parity, PROM, preeclampsia, ANC, multiple pregnancy, placenta Previa and prolonged labor2. Neonatal medical condition predictors: birth asphyxia, sepsis, Meconium Aspiration Syndrome (MAS), hypoglycemia, hypothermia, Apgar score, breast feeding initiation2. Maternal medical condition predictors: HIV/AIDS, hypertension, DM, chorioamnionitis3.

Operational definition

Respiratory distress syndrome is defined as the presence of the following two or more signs: abnormal respiratory rate, expiratory grunting, nasal flaring and chest wall recession with or without cyanosis4 and it was diagnosed by physician.

Outcome status

The outcome of neonates admitted in NICU in the follow-up period; either neonatal RD or censored that ascertained by neonates document review.

Event

The occurrence of neonatal RD after admission to time of until discharged.

Censored

Neonates did not develop RD discharged with recovered, being died, transfer out or loss to follow-up until time of discharge.

Time to neonatal RD

The time in days from admission to occurrence of RD among neonates admitted in NICU.

Median time to RD

The time (in days) at which 50% of neonates developed the event (RD) among neonates admitted in NICU in the follow-up time.

Follow-up time

Measures time in days from admission to the occurrence of the event (RD) or censorship.

The incidence rate of neonatal RD

the occurrence of neonatal RD dividing by the number of neonates - day at risk of the follow-up period and adjusted per 100 neonate-days.

Data extraction tool and procedure

A checklist was adapted from various literature1,5,8,12,19,21 and pretested was used to extract the data from the neonates’ charts. Two trained BSC nurses working in the NICU extracted data by reviewing the complete neonates’ cards from the previous three and nine months that met the eligibility criteria. RD was confirmed by reviewing the neonates’ medical charts.

Follow-up methods

Samples were selected using simple random sampling techniques among neonates admitted to NICUs at selected hospitals. The selected neonates’ charts were closely monitored from their admission date until they developed RD or were censored (neonates who did not develop RD and were discharged due to recovery, death, transfer out, or loss to follow-up). Outcome status was determined by reviewing neonates’ medical charts from admission until discharge. Follow-up time in days was calculated by subtracting the admission date from the date of the event or censorship.

Data quality assurance

Data quality was ensured by developing appropriate data abstraction tools. The checklist was reviewed by experienced researchers. The data abstraction instrument was pretested on 20 charts (5% of the sample size), and modifications were made based on identified gaps. A one-day training session was conducted on data abstraction checklist and extraction process for both data collectors and a supervisor (one supervisor and two data collectors were selected). During data abstraction, daily meetings, close supervision, and monitoring were conducted by the supervisor, investigator, and advisors. Coding and data entry were performed using Epi Data 4.6 software.

Data processing and analysis

After coding and entering the extracted data using EPI Data version 4.6, it was exported to Stata™ version 17.0 for further management and analysis. The data was cleaned and edited before analysis. Univariable analysis was conducted based on the nature of the variables. Mean with standard deviations was used for normally distributed continuous data, while median with Inter Quartile Range (IQR) was used for skewed continuous data. Categorical variables were summarized using frequency, percentage, and figures. The outcome of each study participant was categorized as either censored or an event. Kaplan-Meier Failure curves were used to estimate the cumulative probability of failure and the median follow-up time. The log-rank test was employed to statistically compare the failure curves for categorical variables. The Cox proportional hazards model was used to identify predictors of neonatal respiratory distress (RD). Both bivariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were computed to select variables for the final model and to address the effect of confounders. Variables with p-values ≤ 0.25 in the bivariable Cox proportional hazards model were eligible for the multivariable model. Different Cox proportional hazard models were built and compared using AIC and BIC, with the model having the lowest AIC (AIC = 1532.76) being the best fit for the final model. The assumption of the Cox proportional hazards model was checked and satisfied using the Log-Log plot and Schoenfeld residual test (P-value = 0.86). Model goodness-of-fit was assessed using the Cox-Snell residual test, and the Cox proportional hazards model was fitted. Significant associations were declared at a p-value < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval (CI). The strength of association was reported using adjusted hazard ratios (AHR) with 95% CI.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of study participants

Out of the total samples, 394 (97%) charts of study participants were included in the final analysis, while 12 (3%) charts were excluded due to incomplete data (7 charts) or unavailability (5 charts). Among the 394 study participants, 206 (52.28%) were males. Of all the neonates enrolled in this study, 216 (54.82%) were from rural areas. The mean age of the cohort at the time of admission to the NICU was 3.57 days with a standard deviation (SD) of 4.54 days. The mean age of the mothers was 29 years with an SD of 5.64 years. Three hundred and ten (78.68%) mothers were aged between 20 and 34 years. The mean gestational age was 36.96 weeks (± 2 SD) and the mean weight of neonates at admission was 2636 g (± 659.63 SD) (Table 2).

The incidence rate of Rd and overall failure (hazard) function

A total of 394 neonates admitted to the NICU were followed for 3,003 neonate-days of observation, with follow-up periods ranging from 1 to 27 days from admission to discharge. The Kaplan-Meier failure curve was used to estimate the median follow-up time and cumulative probability of failure (risk of developing RD). The median time to RD was 12 neonate-days, with an interquartile range of 7 to 22 neonate-days. During the follow-up period, the probability of experiencing RD increased over time (Fig. 1).

The overall Kaplan-Meier failure curve showing time of developing RD among neonates admitted in NICU at West Gojjam zone public Hospitals, Northwest Ethiopia, 2024(n = 394).

The overall incidence rate of RD was 5.06 per 100 neonate-days (95% CI: 4.32, 5.93), with 3,003 neonate-days of observation and an incidence proportion of 152 (38.58%; 95% CI: 33.88–43.49).

Kaplan-Meier failure curve and comparison of failure curves with log-rank test

The Kaplan-Meier failure curve was used to compare the risk of experiencing respiratory distress (RD) among different groups of variables during the follow-up period. The observed differences on the plot were statistically validated using the log-rank test (see P-values on each KM graph). The log-rank test was employed to statistically assess any significant differences in the risk of developing RD among various categorical predictors.

Separate graphs of the Kaplan-Meier failure functions were constructed for categorical variables (see Figs. 2 and 3). Generally, a failure function lying above another indicates that the group defined by the upper curve (which developed RD earlier with a shorter median time) has a higher risk of developing RD than the group defined by the lower curve (which had better survival with a longer median time).

The Kaplan-Meier failure functions in Fig. 2 show that being male (10 neonate-days median time with 95% CI: 9, 13) and having low birth weight (10 neonate-days median time with 95% CI: 9, 13) significantly increased the risk of developing RD compared to being female (17 neonate-days median time with 95% CI: 12, 22) and having normal birth weight (14 neonate-days median time with 95% CI: 11, 17), respectively (see Figs. 2). Similarly, the Kaplan-Meier failure functions indicated that neonates whose mothers had no ANC visits (10 neonate-days median time with 95% CI: 8, 14) and had diabetes mellitus (DM) (7 neonate-days median time with 95% CI: 5, 11) had a significantly higher risk of developing RD compared to neonates whose mothers had ANC visits (12 neonate-days median time with 95% CI: 11, 15) and had no DM (12 neonate-days median time with 95% CI: 10, 14), respectively (Figs. 3).

Model Building and model comparison within Cox proportional hazards model

Based on the bivariable Cox regression model, fourteen candidate variables with p-values ≤ 0.25 were selected for the multivariable Cox proportional hazard model (full model). These candidate variables included history of ANC, maternal DM, neonate’s sex, birth weight, pregnancy-related complications, PROM, placenta Previa, mode of delivery, gestational age, multiple pregnancy, hypothermia, sepsis, and APGAR scores at 1 st and 5th minutes.

Six models were built and compared within the Cox proportional hazard model (full model) to identify the best-fitted final model for the multivariable Cox proportional hazard model. Model comparison was conducted using information criteria (AIC and BIC) to determine the best fit for the given data set. As a result, by removing four variables from the full model, the Cox proportional hazard model with the smallest AIC (AIC = 1532.76) and a total of ten predictors was selected for the final multivariable Cox-proportional hazards model (Table 6).

The Schoenfeld residual test was employed to objectively verify the Cox-proportional hazard assumption for individual predictors and global tests. The Cox-Snell residual plot confirmed the goodness of fit for the Cox model, ensuring its accuracy. This indicates that the model was adequate. Neonates born to mothers without ANC follow-up had a significantly 1.45 times higher risk of developing RD compared to their counterparts [adjusted hazard ratio (AHR): 1.45 (95% CI: 1.04, 2.02)]. The study also showed that neonates from multiple pregnancies had a significantly 1.73 times higher risk of developing RD compared to those from singleton pregnancies [AHR: 1.73 (95% CI: 1.07, 2.76)]. Discussions

This retrospective follow - up study was aimed at assessing the incidence and evaluate predictors of respiratory distress in 394 neonates admitted to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit of West Gojjam Zone Public Hospitals.

The overall incidence rate of RD was 5.06 (95% CI: 4.32, 5.93) per 100 neonate-days of observation12 and this finding was lower than those of retrospective cohort studies conducted in Addis Ababa (8.1 per 100 neonate-days) and Northwest Ethiopia (10.78 per 100 neonate-days)13. The discrepancy may be due to the study settings being referral sites that provide advanced maternal and newborn services. Another possible reason for the variation in the incidence rate of neonatal RD could be the higher prevalence of maternal morbidities such as DM and preeclampsia/eclampsia in the previous studies. Clinical evidence suggests that high maternal morbidity during pregnancy increases the risk of RD26,27.

The Kaplan-Meier failure curve analysis revealed significant differences in the risk of developing respiratory distress (RD) among various groups, validated using the log-rank test. The analysis showed that male neonates with low birth weight had a higher risk of RD, with median times of 10 neonate-days, compared to females and those with normal birth weight, who had median times of 17 and 14 neonate-days, respectively. Additionally, neonates whose mothers had no antenatal care (ANC) visits or had diabetes mellitus (DM) were at a higher risk, with median times of 10 and 7 neonate-days, respectively, compared to those whose mothers had ANC visits or no DM, with median times of 12 neonate-days. The study showed that out of every twenty newborns, one experienced respiratory distress on a daily basis and half of the neonates experienced respiratory distress within twelve days of birth, while the other half experienced them after twelve days.

Neonates born to mothers without antenatal care (ANC) follow-up had a significantly 1.45 times higher risk of developing RD compared to their counterparts. This finding is supported by studies conducted in Cameroon5 and Nigeria29. Women with a high risk of unwanted prenatal and neonatal outcomes, including respiratory distress, often did not have ANC contacts. The WHO recommends a minimum of four or more ANC contacts during pregnancy30. Close ANC contact during pregnancy allows for early anticipation and management of potential complications and risks to both the newborn and mother. Providing affordable, cost-effective, and lifesaving ANC services to all women, especially in rural areas, can improve prenatal outcomes and reduce predictors of neonatal respiratory distress.

The study also revealed that neonates from multiple pregnancies had a significantly 1.73 times higher risk of developing RD compared to those from singleton pregnancies. This finding aligns with results from studies conducted in Northwest Ethiopia Comprehensive Specialized Hospitals, South West Ethiopia13, South West Ethiopia19 and Misan, Iraq9. The increased risk may be due to the higher likelihood of premature delivery in multiple pregnancies, leading to organ and physiological immaturity, which in turn raises the risk of RD31. Additionally, multiple pregnancies can increase the risk of congenital disorders and intrauterine growth restriction, further contributing to the risk of RD32.

Additionally, male neonates had a significantly 1.48 times higher risk of developing RD compared to their female counterparts. This finding aligns with studies conducted in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia12, Italy22 and Cameroon5. This may be due to the fact that the sex hormone testosterone affects the maturation of the fetal lung, pulmonary biomechanics, and vascular development33. Male neonates have higher levels of circulating testosterone than females, which increases the risk of developing RD among male neonates.

The study also indicated that neonates born to mothers with DM had a significantly 2.83 times higher risk of developing RD compared to those born to non-DM mothers. This finding is supported by research conducted in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia12 and China27. Neonates born to mothers with DM often have hyperglycemia and hyperinsulinism, leading to newborn hypoglycemia and disrupting normal lung maturation and function34. Hence, this disruption increases the risk of developing RD.

The final predictor for RD was birth weight. Neonates admitted with low birth weight had a significantly 2.32 times higher risk of developing RD compared to those with normal birth weight. This finding is supported by studies conducted at Italy22 and Saudi Arabia35. Clinical evidence suggests that neonates with low birth weights are often premature and lack lung surfactants due to lung immaturity. This immaturity of body organs, including the lungs, increases the occurrence of RD.

Another possible explanation is that neonates with low birth weights stay in hospitals longer than those with normal birth weights, increasing their vulnerability to co-morbidities such as sepsis and hypoglycemia. These co-morbidities contribute to surfactant inactivation and respiratory epithelial cell injury over time, leading to a higher risk of developing RD. Therefore, this finding suggests the need for more interventions focusing on early anticipation and management of potential complications and risks to both newborn and mother to reduce the occurrence of RD32.

Limitations of the study

Since this study utilized secondary sources, important predictors such as maternal nutritional status, occupational status, family income, educational status, and marital status were not included. This may have introduced selection bias due to the exclusion of charts with incomplete data. Therefore, an appropriate study period was chosen when the study settings began full reporting and documentation on neonatal charts to obtain adequate variables and sample size.

Conclusions

One in every twenty neonates experienced respiratory distress (RD) daily. The median time to respiratory occurrence among neonates was twelve days. The median time to RD occurrence among neonates was twelve days. The statistically significant predictors of respiratory distress included absence of ANC visit, multiple pregnancies, neonates born to diabetic mothers, being male, and low birth weight.

Recommendations

For the federal ministry of health

The federal ministries of health in Ethiopia should prioritize the identified predictors. Women who do not attend ANC follow-up have a high risk of neonatal RD. Therefore, health education and the provision of affordable and essential obstetric and neonatal care should be designed for all women, especially in rural areas.

For West Gojjam zone public hospitals

These hospitals should focus on early diagnosis and intervention for mothers and neonates with identified predictors, particularly for DM and multiple pregnancies during the pregnancy period. Additionally, hospitals should encourage healthcare providers in NICUs to closely monitor neonates with low birth weight.

For health workers

Healthcare providers in NICUs should give special attention and care to neonates born from twin pregnancies, mothers without ANC follow-up, mothers with DM, and those with low birth weight. Strengthening ANC follow-up and screening for DM during pregnancy is recommended.

For future researchers

Future researchers are advised to use a prospective cohort study design to avoid the limitations of retrospective cohort design and to ensure important socio-demographic predictors are not missed.
