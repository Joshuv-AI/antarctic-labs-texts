A promoterless AAV6.2FF-based lung gene editing platform for the correction of surfactant protein B deficiency
Authors: Sylvia P. Thomas, Jakob M. Domm, Jacob P. van Vloten, Liqun Xu, Arul Vadivel, Jacob G.E. Yates, Yanlong Pei, Joelle Ingrao, Laura P. van Lieshout, Sergio R. Jackson, Jessica A. Minott, Adithya Achuthan, Yeganeh Mehrani, Thomas M. McAusland, Wei Zhang, Khalil Karimi, Andrew E. Vaughan, Jondavid de Jong, Martin H. Kang, Bernard Thebaud, Sarah K. Wootton
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Surfactant protein B (SP-B) deficiency is a rare genetic disease that causes fatal respiratory failure within the first year of life. Currently, the only corrective treatment is lung transplantation. Here, we co-transduced the murine lung with adeno-associated virus 6.2FF (AAV6.2FF) vectors encoding a SaCas9-guide RNA nuclease or donor template to mediate insertion of promoterless reporter genes or the (murine) Sftpb gene in frame with the endogenous surfactant protein C (SP-C) gene, without disrupting SP-C expression. Intranasal administration of 3 × 1011 vg donor template and 1 × 1011 vg nuclease consistently edited approximately 6% of lung epithelial cells. Frequency of gene insertion increased in a dose-dependent manner, reaching 20%–25% editing efficiency with the highest donor template and nuclease doses tested. We next evaluated whether this promoterless gene editing platform could extend survival in the conditional SP-B knockout mouse model. Administration of 1 × 1012 vg SP-B-donor template and 5 × 1011 vg nuclease significantly extended median survival (p = 0.0034) from 5 days in the untreated off doxycycline group to 16 days in the donor AAV and nuclease group, with one gene-edited mouse living 243 days off doxycycline. This AAV6.2FF-based gene editing platform has the potential to correct SP-B deficiency, as well as other disorders of alveolar type II cells.

Introduction

Alveolar type II (ATII) cells are resident in distal lung alveoli and certain subsets of these cells are capable of dividing and repopulating ATII and ATI cells upon lung injury.1,2 While ATI cells line most of the alveoli and facilitate gas exchange, ATII cells only make up 5% of the alveolar surface and are the exclusive drivers of pulmonary surfactant synthesis and secretion.3 Proper functionality of ATII cells is essential for lung surfactant homeostasis, and several genetic surfactant dysfunctions manifest from these cells, such as surfactant protein B (SP-B), ATP binding cassette subfamily A member 3 (ABCA3) deficiencies, and surfactant protein C (SP-C) disorders.4,5,6,7,8,9 These diseases often lead to respiratory distress and early death in newborns.4 Limited treatment options are available for surfactant dysfunctions; in fact, only lung transplantation permanently affects the course of disease, yet high mortality rates are associated with this procedure.10 Therefore, a less invasive method of correcting these genetic disorders is desirable.

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) gene therapy vectors, derived from nonpathogenic dependoviruses, are capable of transducing dividing and non-dividing cells and mediating sustained transgene expression for the lifetime of the transduced cell with limited toxicity.11 Over the past 20 years, there has been a dramatic increase in the application of recombinant AAVs (rAAVs) engineered from wildtype or novel serotypes to treat monogenic or complex disorders and this has led to more than 200 clinical trials, with four AAV-based treatments approved for human use in Europe and the United States – alipogene tiparvovec (Glybera), voretigene neparvovec (Luxturna), onasemnogene abeparvovec (Zolgensma), and etranacogene dezaparvovec (Hemgenix).12,13

AAV6.2FF is an engineered lung tropic capsid that efficiently transduces both the murine lung and fetal human lung slices.14 This capsid differs from AAV6 by three amino acids—F129L, Y445F, and Y731F—which enhance its transduction and immune evasion capabilities while promoting localized gene expression in the lung following intranasal administration.15 Previously we demonstrated that AAV6.2FF-mediated delivery of the human SP-B gene to the lungs of conditional SP-B knockout mice could extend survival in a dose-dependent manner, with one mouse surviving to 294 days.14 While this gene addition approach was highly successful, the effect did wane over time, indicating that further improvements can be made to extend SP-B expression from this AAV-based therapy.

The advent of CRISPR-Cas9 technology has allowed for permanent gene knock-ins through homologous recombination that last for the lifetime of the cell.16,17 The discovery of Staphylococcus aureus Cas9 (SaCas9) allowed packaging of this smaller Cas9 within AAV, something that was not possible with the more commonly used Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 variant.18 CRISPR-Cas9 editing utilizes programmable guide RNAs (gRNA) to determine gene insertion sites, but does not contain the inherent machinery to promote cell type-specific insertions. However, recent work has demonstrated the insertion of promoterless genes using CRISPR-Cas9,19,20,21 which we applied as a means to confer cell specificity to this gene editing platform.

Here, we applied promoterless gene insertion to piggyback onto the endogenous (SP-C) promoter, which is highly expressed primarily in ATII cells.22 This technology was paired with the lung tropic AAV6.2FF vector to facilitate precise gene insertion in mice in a promoterless manner without disrupting endogenous SP-C expression. We termed this platform AAV6.2FF-CRISPR, and in this study we examined its effects with two different reporter genes in a dose- and age-dependent manner, and investigated its capabilities to extend survival in conditional SP-B−/− mice following the insertion of the (murine) Sftpb gene.

Results

Donor template construction

AAV vector genomes encoding the donor template were constructed and confirmed via Sanger sequencing. Donor template vectors were engineered to contain two 1-kb homology arms complementary to the 3′-end of the SP-C genomic locus (minus the stop codon) and the 3′ UTR of SP-C. The cDNAs for either eGFP, heat stable human placental alkaline phosphatase (AP), or murine SP-B, preceded by the self-cleaving furin 2a peptide (F2A), were cloned between these two SP-C homology arms (Figure 1A). Site-specific recombination would result in a fused mRNA (e.g., SPC-F2A-eGFP) transcribed by the strong SP-C promoter that, because of the presence of F2A self-cleaving peptide, would be translated and cleaved into two separate proteins (Figure 1B). Optimization of on-target gRNA cutting by T7E1 assay

A series of gRNA sites were initially formulated using CHOPCHOP software based on estimated cutting efficiency, number of mismatches, existence of self-complementary regions, and GC content.23,24 gRNA sites were chosen to cut downstream, but as near as possible to the SP-C stop codon so as to facilitate efficient gene insertion without disrupting SP-C gene expression if gene insertion did not occur (Figures 1B and 2A). Seven total gRNAs (gRNA5, gRNA6, gRNA7, gRNA8, gRNA9, gRNA10, and gRNA12) were selected and initially assessed for off-targets by Cas-OFFinder software,25 searching within the bounds of three or fewer mismatches, using PAM of NNNRRT, and species mouse (Table S1). Selected gRNAs had on-target efficiency with no mismatches to the SP-C UTR on chromosome 14 for mice, and any off-target chromosomal locations had two or more mismatches. A T7 endonuclease I assay was then performed in murine lung epithelial 12 cells (MLE 12) cells to estimate on-target cutting of the seven sgRNAs. After transfection, DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and then reannealing, DNA products were digested at mismatches with T7 endonuclease I and analyzed via bioanalyzing (Figure S2) and gel electrophoresis (Figure 2B). On-target cutting was calculated and displayed as the percent modification per transfected cell. gRNA5, gRNA6, gRNA9, and gRNA10 were the most efficient cutters, leading to 20.6%, 20.5%, 33.8%, and 20.8% modification per transfected cell, respectively. gRNA7 and gRNA8 cut with lower efficiencies of 17.6% and 9.7%, and gRNA12 cut at a level below detection. gRNA9 and gRNA12 displayed very different cutting efficiencies, despite both having a similar PAM and being the nearest to the transcriptionally active SP-C cDNA. sgRNA5 demonstrated no gain in cutting efficiency over the other gRNAs that also reached approximately 20%, despite being the sole cutter utilizing the PAM NGGRRT instead of NNGRRT, but it was still selected because of its unique PAM sequence specificity, along with gRNA9 that displayed the highest cutting efficiency at 33.8%.Figure 2Optimization of on-target gRNA cutting by T7E1 assay(A) Schematic showing the location of the gRNA binding sites downstream of the SP-C genomic locus. Off-target cutting was assessed using a tracking of indels by decomposition (TIDE) assay,26 which determined that the selected gRNAs 5 and 9 displayed low off-target cutting efficiencies compared with on-target cutting, especially gRNA9 (Table S4). A homology directed repair-mediated AAV6.2FF-based gene editing platform targeting the SP-C locus results in the integration and expression of eGFP in lung epithelial cells

To demonstrate the in vivo efficiency of our gene editing strategy, groups of 4-week-old C57BL/6 mice (n = 4) were intranasally administered 3 × 1011 vg AAV-SPCFlankGFP alone or in combination with 1 × 1011 vg AAV-SaCas9-gRNA5 or AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 (Figure 3A). An additional group received 3 × 1011 vg AAV-SPCFlankGFP plus a mixture of 1 × 1011 vg of both AAV-SaCas9-gRNA5 and AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9. Control mice were administered PBS. At 6 weeks after AAV administration, mice were euthanized, and lungs harvested, dissociated, and processed for flow cytometric analysis. After excluding dead cells and CD45+ immune cells, cells were gated for EpCAM+ and GFP+ to quantify the number of gene-edited lung epithelial cells (Figures 3B and S3). Mice administered AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 in combination with the AAV-SPCFlankGFP donor template had greater knock-in efficiency than mice administered AAV-SaCas9-gRNA5, with 6.523% ± 0.654% SEM and 0.390% ± 0.032% SEM GFP+ lung epithelial cells respectively (p < 0.0001), and was superior to the AAV-SPCFlankGFP only and PBS-treated controls (p < 0.0001) (Figure 3C). Combining 3 × 1011 vg AAV-SPCFlankGFP of donor template with 1 × 1011 vg of both AAV-SaCas9-gRNA5 and AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 resulted in 5.453% ± 0.476% SEM GFP+ lung epithelial cells, which was not significantly better than the AAV-SPCFlankGFP + AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 group (6.523% ± 0.654% SEM GFP+ lung epithelial cells) (Figure 3C). Gross images of lungs from mice administered AAV-SPCFlankGFP + AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 imaged using a stereo fluorescence microscope revealed widespread distribution of GFP positive cells in the lung, but with a much lower fluorescence intensity compared with mice receiving AAV-expressing GFP from the ubiquitous CASI promoter (Figure S4A). GFP-positive cells from the dissociated lungs of AAV-SPCFlankGFP + AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9-treated mice seemed to be relatively large and cuboidal shaped. To demonstrate that the AAV-CRISPR platform mediates gene insertion in SP-C-expressing cells and that gene insertion does not disrupt endogenous SP-C expression, mice were transduced with AAV-CAG-GFP or AAV-SPCFlankGFP + AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 and infected with PR8 influenza to induce transient lung injury, which promotes cell division and repair.27 At 25-days after PR8 infection, mice were euthanized, and lungs harvested for immunofluorescence analysis. Tissue sections were stained with DAPI, along with anti-ProSP-C and anti-GFP antibodies (Figure 3D). AAV-CAG-GFP led to minimal GFP-positive ATII cells after lung injury with PR8, and these cells were evenly distributed. Conversely, treatment with AAV-SPCFlankGFP + AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 led to patches of multiple GFP-expressing ATII cells after PR8 lung injury and subsequent cell division (Figure 3D, right, white arrows). These GFP-expressing cells were found adjacent and at times bordering each other. In one instance, an ATII cell with GFP knocked-in seemed to be in the process of cell division (Figure 3C, bottom right). Cells double positive for GFP and proSP-C were observed confirming that this promoterless AAV- CRISPR platform does not disrupt endogenous SP-C expression in ATII cells.

To determine the percentage of GFP positive cells that were SP-C positive, lungs from mice administered 1 × 1012 vg donor template (AAV-SPCFlankGFP) plus 1 × 1011 vg nuclease (AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9) were dissociated and subjected to flow cytometry to quantify the number of edited GFP-positive cells that were SP-C positive (CD45– EpCAM+ GFP+ SP-C+), indicating that insertion occurred in the correct location and did not interfere with upstream SP-C expression. In mice that received the donor template alone, an average of 89.28% of GFP+ cells were SP-C+, whereas in the mice that were administered donor template plus nuclease, an average of 92.48% of GFP+ cells were SP-C+, demonstrating that most gene edited cells express both GFP and SP-C and that editing does not interfere with SP-C expression (Figure S6).

The promoterless AAV-CRISPR platform facilitates gene insertion at the targeted genomic location

Primers were designed such that the forward primer bound within the SP-C genomic locus upstream of the region complementary to the donor template homology arms to avoid binding to the donor template, and the reverse primer bound within the GFP or SPB coding sequence of the donor template (Table S5). Although unlikely, it is potentially possible that concatamerization of the AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 and AAV donor genomes in a head-to-tail manner may lead to cytomegalovirus (CMV)-mediated transgene expression from donor AAV (Figure S1B). To address this possibility, we isolated genomic DNA from GFP+ lung cells sorted from mice intranasally administered 5 × 1011 vg of AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 plus 1 × 1012 vg AAV-SPCFlankGFP and euthanized 21 days later. Genomic DNA was subjected to nested PCR in an attempt to detect the AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 genome either as a monomer or concatemerized with AAV-SPCFlankGFP in a head-to-tail configuration. As shown in Figure S1C, only when primers that detected the SaCas9 gene were used was a PCR product detected. No PCR product was amplified from genomic DNA using the nested primer set designed amplify head-to-tail AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9-AAV-SPCFlankGFP, even after three rounds of PCR amplification. These results suggest that concatamerization of AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 and AAV donor genomes (AAV-SPCFlankGFP) in a head-to-tail manner is a rare event; thus, it is unlikely that transgene expression is occurring via readthrough from the CMV promoter.

The efficiency of in vivo gene insertion is influenced by age and dose of AAV administered

Next, we investigated whether variables such as the age of the animal or AAV vector dose impact the efficiency of gene insertion. We investigated the impact of age by comparing intranasal administration of AAV-SPCFlankGFP (3 × 1011 vg) alone or AAV-SPCFlankGFP (3 × 1011 vg) + AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 (1 × 1011 vg) in groups of female C57BL/6 mice aged 4, 10, or 20 weeks at the time of vector administration. Lung tissue was harvested 3 weeks after AAV administration and the number of GFP+ EpCAM+ CD45– live cells quantified by flow cytometry. The number of lung epithelial cells expressing GFP in the mice that were administered AAV-SPCFlankGFP + AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 was 6.090% ± 0.0662% SEM (n = 4), 9.593% ± 1.638% SEM (n = 4), and 3.28% ± 0.440 SEM% (n = 3) in the 4-, 10- and 20-week-old mice, respectively (Figure 5A), with 10-week-old mice having significantly more GFP-positive lung epithelial cells than the 20-week-old mice (p = 0.0002) and 4-week-old mice (p = 0.0349). In mice receiving the donor template alone (AAV-SPCFlankGFP), only the 10-week-old group demonstrated significant GFP expression compared with the PBS treated control (Figure S7A). In this experiment, we also investigated whether increasing the dose of donor template would increase gene insertion efficiency in the 4-week-old mice, with this age group selected for their early development stage that better represents our disease model. Groups of C57BL/6 mice (n = 4) were administered 3 × 1011 vg or 1 × 1012 vg AAV-SPCFlankGFP donor template with or without 1 × 1011 vg AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 and the number of GFP+ EpCAM+ CD45– live cells were gated. The number of GFP+ EpCAM+ CD45- live cells in the mice administered a dose 1 × 1012 vg AAV-SPCFlankGFP donor template (14.650% ± 0.753% SEM; n = 4) was significantly higher than the mice administered 3 × 1011 vg AAV-SPCFlankGFP donor template (6.090% ± 0.662% SEM; n = 4) in combination with AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 (p < 0.0001) (Figure 5B).

Subsequently, we investigated the impact of increasing the dose of the nuclease-containing AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 vector on gene insertion efficiency. In this experiment, 4-week-old mice were delivered a fixed dose of 1 × 1012 vg AAV-SPCFlankGFP donor repair template and either 1 × 1011 vg or 5 × 1011 vg of AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 nuclease-containing vector (Figures 5C and 5D). The higher dose nuclease group performed significantly better (p < 0.0001) with the mean percentage of GFP+ EpCAM+ CD45– live cells being 24.1% ± 1.734% SEM (n = 4) compared with 7.6% ± 1.275% (n = 4) in the lower dose group. Taken together, these data suggest that gene editing efficiency is dose dependent with both the nuclease and template concentrations.

AAV-CRISPR platform functions to insert different sized genes into ATII cells

To demonstrate that this AAV-CRISPR platform can be used to knock-in different genes larger than GFP (717 bp), we cloned the 1.6-kb cDNA for the human placental AP gene into the AAV donor template genome to create AAV-SPCFlankAP (Figure 1A). Using a similar approach to the initial experiments with AAV-SPCFlankGFP (Figure 3A), groups of 4-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were intranasally administered 3 × 1011 vg AAV-SPCFlankAP alone or in combination with 1 × 1011 vg AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9, or a fixed dose of 1 × 1012 vg AAV-SPCFlankAP alone or in combination with 1 × 1011 vg AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 or 5 × 1011 vg AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9. Three weeks after AAV administration, mice were euthanized, and lung lobes were either fixed and stained for AP expression, flash frozen, or cryopreserved in optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT). Lung lobes stained for AP expression were imaged with a stereomicroscope (Figure 6). In these gross images, AP-positive foci were distributed throughout the lungs, and, as observed with GFP expression in Figure 5, the number of AP-stained cells increased when the dose of AAV-SPCFlankAP donor template vector and/or AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 nuclease vector was increased. Tissue sections from the lung and trachea were stained for AP expression to identify cell types in which the gene editing occurred. AP expression was observed primarily in cuboidal shaped epithelial cells that phenotypically resemble ATII cells in the walls of the distal lung alveoli. Of note, there were no AP-positive foci observed in club cells lining the bronchioles or in the trachea of mice administered AAV-SPCFlankAP + AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 where SPC promoter activity would be limited. Conversely, transient AP expression in mice administered 1 × 1011 vg of a conventional AAV-CASI-AP vector was observed in several different cell types within the trachea and the lungs. Therapeutic benefit of the promoterless AAV-CRISPR gene editing platform in SP-B knockout mice

We next investigated the potential clinical relevance of our gene editing platform in a well-established mouse model of respiratory surfactant deficiency. Having demonstrated that it is possible to achieve editing efficiencies averaging 24% of lung epithelial cells using a promoterless AAV-CRISPR platform designed to insert genes under the control of the SP-C promoter, we investigated whether this strategy could extend survival in a model of SP-B deficiency.14,28 These mice are genetically engineered so both copies of the (murine) Sftpb gene are knocked out and replaced with a tetracycline-inducible copy of the (murine) Sftpb gene.28 When mice are fed doxycycline, they produce SP-B and are phenotypically healthy, but when doxycycline is removed and SP-B protein levels decrease below approximately 25%, respiratory failure and death occur within 4–11 days. The AAV donor template was revised such that the murine SP-B cDNA was inserted between the SP-C homology arms in the donor template genome to generate AAV-SPCFlankSPB (Figure 1A). Four-week-old SP-B−/− mice on doxycycline were intranasally administered 1 × 1012 vg AAV-SPCFlankSPB (n = 3) alone or in combination with 5 × 1011 vg AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 (n = 6). Positive control mice were administered 1 × 1011 vg of an AAV vector expressing human SP-B under the control of the CASI promoter (AAV-CASI-hSPB-io2; n = 4). Groups of untreated mice on and off doxycycline (n = 6) were included as controls (Figure 8A). At 3 weeks after AAV administration, doxycycline was removed from the diet and mice were monitored for signs of respiratory distress. One group of untreated mice was maintained on doxycycline as a control. Discussion

Here, we have achieved permanent gene correction in the murine lung through homology-dependent insertion of a promoterless template under the control of an endogenous promoter to target therapeutic transgene expression to ATII cells. We also demonstrated the possibility of treating a lethal monogenetic lung disorder with two promising contenders in the field of gene therapy, AAV and CRISPR. We designed our promoterless AAV-CRISPR platform to facilitate HDR-mediated gene insertion immediately downstream of the endogenous SP-C gene under the control of the SP-C promoter, which is highly expressed in ATII cells29 and regulates surfactant expression.30,31 Delivering a promoterless gene safeguards against transgene expression from cell types other than ATII cells, and sites in the genome other than behind the SP-C promoter, thereby decreasing the chance of developing off-target toxicities or transgene-related immune responses. Furthermore, the absence of a promoter in the donor template AAV allows for larger transgenes, up to 2 kb in length in this configuration to be packaged and inserted. This could possibly be increased if the size of the homology arms was reduced; however, these experiments have yet to be conducted, so the impact this would have on gene insertion efficiency remains unknown. Precautions such as repair template design and gRNA cut locations were also taken to ensure integration selectively behind the SP-C promoter without interfering with the endogenous SP-C coding sequence itself.

We chose to target HDR downstream of the Sftpc gene as the SP-C promoter is among one of the strongest promoters in the lung32 and is only active in ATII cells.33 Moreover, the SP-C promoter is well characterized and has been an important tool for overexpressing a variety of transgenes in ATII cells in mice. The SP-B promoter, in contrast, is less well characterized. As with other “gene ride” approaches, such as in the liver, the strongest promoter for that particular target tissues is preferred.34 Finally, cell turnover in the alveolar region of the adult mouse lung is normally slow,35 and the capacity for self-renewal and differentiation of adult ATII cells has been confirmed by in vivo genetic lineage tracing studies.35,36,37

In designing this AAV-CRISPR platform, we chose to utilize a donor template with homology arms of 1 kb per side and determined the optimal gRNA that cuts adjacent and downstream of the SP-C stop codon. The repair template length was modeled after Zhang et al.,38 who noted that more than 600 bp of homology in each repair template arm led to increased efficiency of HDR-mediated gene insertion. Initially a triple vector strategy (i.e., two gRNA containing AAV vectors were co-administered with the donor template AAV) was attempted based on the results of a previous study in zebrafish that demonstrated gene insertion efficiency was increased more than 2-fold when two unique gRNAs were used instead of one.39 We employed this strategy by targeting gRNA9 to cut proximal, yet downstream, of the SP-C stop codon, and gRNA5 to cut further downstream (approximately 200 bp). However, in vivo cutting efficiency of gRNAs varied. gRNA9 facilitated approximately 6% insertion efficiency in lung epithelial cells, which was not improved by the addition of gRNA5. The variation in insertion rates between these two gRNAs is possibly attributed to the reported 10% difference in cutting efficiency observed in vitro and the cut site proximity to insertion site (Figure 2C). Therefore, we moved forward with a dual vector AAV-CRISPR platform using AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 as the singular nuclease vector.

In this study, we observed gene insertion rates in lung epithelial cells ranging from 6.09% ± 0.662% SEM up to 14.65% ± 0.753% SEM, depending on the dose of donor template containing AAV administered into 4-week-old mice. These gene insertion rates were further increased when the dose of nuclease containing AAV was also increased (5 × 1011 vg), demonstrating an average peak knock-in efficiency of 24.1% ± 1.734% SEM GFP+ EpCAM+ CD45– live cells. We observed a 12% insertion rate in our moderate dose when staining for the markers described in combination with an SP-C antibody, which is in alignment with the percentage observed without the antibody included (Figure S10). This dose-dependent effect on gene insertion efficiency was between our lowest and highest donor and nuclease treatment groups when the donor template encoded a larger reporter gene; however, no significant effects were observed when the SPCFlankAP donor template dose was increased. This indicates that the nuclease dose had the most significant effect on gene editing rates when expressing a larger transgene from this platform. Nevertheless, the number of AP-positive foci in the lungs at the highest donor and nuclease doses suggests a significant portion of the ATII cells present are being edited with this AAV-CRISPR platform, which is achievable because of the high transduction efficiency of AAV6.2FF in the lungs.15 Based on previous studies in mice, achieving at least approximately 25% SP-B concentration in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid allows for survival, supported by partial deficiency leading to survival in humans28,40; therefore, further work needs to be performed to determine if the dose-dependent effects on SP-B concentration are meeting threshold levels.

Other promoterless AAV platforms have been shown to mediate efficient homologous recombination without the use of nucleases,19,34,41 although increased levels of homologous recombination were achieved in combination with CRISPR-Cas9.20,21 Here, we also investigated the efficiency of homologous recombination in the absence of a nuclease, and while we did detect evidence of homologous recombination that was influenced by dose and the age of mice, insertions rates were significantly higher in the presence of the AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 nuclease vector, suggesting that double-strand breaks induced by CRISPR-Cas9 increase homologous recombination, as has been demonstrated previously.20,21,42,43,44,45

The influence of age on gene editing efficiency was evaluated in mice 4, 10, and 20 weeks of age. Ten-week-old mice had significantly higher gene editing efficiencies, demonstrating approximately a 6%–10% knock-in rate for 4- and 10-week-old mice, respectively, compared with approximately 3% in the 20-week-old mice. The higher rate of insertion in the 10-week-old mice might be attributed to the fact that their lungs are larger than 4-week-old mice and thus potentially able to accommodate larger volumes of vector leading to improved delivery efficiencies, in addition to having a more pronounced philtrum and larger nares to easily deliver this vector using our modified method of intranasal delivery.46 One possible reason why the 20-week-old mice showed the lowest efficiency of gene insertion may be because younger mice have higher numbers of dividing cells in the lung, which may be more amenable to homologous recombination than 20-week-old mice, that typically reach maturity at 12 weeks of age.47 We elected to move forward with 4 week-old-mice for the remainder of our studies because our goal was treat as close to the postnatal period after birth as possible, which in our case was 4 weeks of age, because of the method of administration we were using and the relatively large volumes of vector we needed to delivery to the lung.

Two lines of evidence highlight the specificity of this AAV-CRISPR platform for ATII cells expressing SP-C. Immunofluorescence analysis of lung tissue harvested from mice receiving the dual AAV-SPCFlankGFP + AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 combination and stained for GFP and SP-C revealed that cells expressing GFP also expressed SP-C suggesting that homologous recombination did not interfere with SP-C expression. Furthermore, AP reporter gene expression in lung tissue from mice administered the dual AAV-SPCFlankAP + AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9 vector combination was detected only in ATII cells and was clearly absent from club cells lining the bronchioles and epithelial cells lining the trachea, which are otherwise transduced when AAV6.2FF expressing AP is administered to mice.

HDR is preferentially active during the S/G2 stages of the cell cycle. While ATII cells can self-renew and differentiate, in the adult lung their capacity for cell division is limited leading to slow turnover unless subjected to lung injury.35,36,37 In our studies, we did not induce lung injury, and previous experiments with AAV6.2FF administration to the lung would suggest that we did not cause inflammation or lung damage in these mice. By 10 weeks, murine alveoli reach alveolarization’s final phase, suggesting reduced ATII cell turnover and alveolar regeneration compared with younger mice. HDR efficiency here reached 20%–25% of lung epithelial cells and possibly higher if we had evaluated only ATII cells which raises the possibility that adult ATII cells have a heightened vulnerability to HDR from CRISPR-induced dsDNA breaks.

While ATII cells are facultative progenitors in the lungs and typically undergo division at a slow rate in the homeostatic lung, cycling every 28–35 days in a mouse,48,49 they may be more inclined to divide in a developing or injured environment,50,51 such as that induced by PR8 influenza infection.27 In our study, patches of multiple GFP-expressing gene-edited ATII cells were observed in close proximity after treatment with AAV-CRISPR and lung injury with influenza. The patches of GFP-integrated ATII cells are, therefore, suggestive that this AAV-CRISPR platform can transduce and insert genes into dividing ATII cells modifying all subsequent daughter cells; however, further evidence is required to substantiate this possibility. Nevertheless, in the context of a therapeutic transgene such as SP-B, gene-edited cells could possibly acquire a selective advantage and proliferate much more rapidly over cells in a diseased state, as demonstrated by Yin et al.52 for hereditary tyrosinemia. Initial knock-in rates of the therapeutic gene, Fah was approximately 0.4%, yet by day 30, approximately 33.5% of Fah+ hepatocytes were detected. They suggested that this increase was due to the death of non-edited cells and the expansion of edited surviving hepatocytes. In the case of lung surfactant dysfunctions, SP-B deficiency and ABCA3 deficiency both have abnormal ATII architecture that is further perturbed by the required mechanical ventilation for survival.4,5,6,53 The proteinaceous mixture present in certain cases of SP-B deficiency demonstrate desquamated ATII cells, highlighting that dysfunctional cells may be prone to death and that there may be a selective advantage to corrected cells.4 In addition, it is known that, after lung injury, a population of ATII cells is capable of expanding to repopulate the lung,1 and we similarly observed the localized proliferation of ATII cells after lung damage with PR8 in this study. However, Mahiny et al.54 utilized an mRNA- or AAV6-delivered zinc finger nuclease for gene editing in the lung and did not observe this expansion and positive selection. Gene editing using zinc finger nucleases extended survival in SP-B deficient mice; however, mice eventually reached endpoint 30–35 days after doxycycline removal. The authors suggested that these findings were due to the proliferative nature of lung epithelial cells, which may have led to the dilution of gene-edited cells over time. It is, therefore, possible that the nuclease-mRNA complex that was administered to the lungs did not successfully target lung progenitor cells, despite utilizing AAV6 to deliver their donor template. Finally, the donor template was designed to insert a CAG promoter upstream of the murine SP-B gene specific to the transgenic mouse model, represents a proof-of-concept study, and does not confer cell specificity per se. Furthermore, the therapeutic translation of this promoter insertion strategy is not applicable to SP-B patient population mutations, including the most common “121ins2” SP-B mutation, which is a frameshift change that leads to a premature stop codon.55 However, the universal gene editing strategy developed in our study does apply to most of the gene coding SPB mutations, including missense, frameshift, nonsense, and deletion mutations, broadening the applicability of this platform for therapeutic treatment.56 Thus, the promoterless AAV-CRISPR platform approach we present here is potentially more clinically relevant. Indeed, similar approaches to AAV-mediated promoterless gene editing in the liver, albeit in the absence of nucleases, are currently in clinical trials (NCT04581785 and NCT05506254). Overall, the application of AAV-based CRISPR clinical trials are currently limited, with a search of ClinicalTrials.gov indicating that out of the 74 “CRISPR” trials posted, only 3 implement AAV vectors and are applying CRISPR in methods other than gene insertion (Table S7).

As a proof of concept, the potential of this AAV-CRISPR platform to extend survival in therapeutic model of SP-B was evaluated. Given the rather small size of the SP-B gene, it was possible to include a WPRE downstream of the murine SP-B gene, bringing the size of the donor template to 4.6 kb, below the packaging limit for AAV. Previous studies in the SP-B−/− mouse model indicated that respiratory failure occurred when SP-B concentration was reduced to less than 25% of normal levels28; therefore, we administered 1 × 1012 vg AAV-SPCFlankSPB in combination with 5 × 1011 vg AAV-SaCas9-gRNA9, as that dose achieved editing efficiencies in approximately 25% of lung epithelial cells. While the promoterless AAV-CRISPR platform significantly extended survival, it was not curative. This could be because a rather large dose and, therefore, a large volume of AAV was administered to mice, increasing the potential for variability among mice. Moreover, the rather large dose of AAV used may have triggered an immune response that could have contributed to immune-mediated clearance of vector-transduced cells given the non-transient nature of SaCas9 expression in this system, as seen with the increased anti-capsid antibody response observed in Figure S12. Another factor to consider is that the homologous arms were generated based on the sequence in C57BL/6 mice and not the FVB/n mice. However, when aligning the homologous arm of the donor template to the FVB/n SP-B gene, only 11 base pairs are mismatched in one of the arms. Additionally, with this model of SP-B deficiency being so acutely lethal,28 it is possible that there was not enough time for gene-edited ATII cells expressing functional SP-B to proliferate and repopulate the alveoli,1,36,57,58 should they in fact have a selective advantage relative to unedited cells; however, this is contingent on the regenerative nature of the gene-edited cells, which remains to be determined. The lone surviving gene-edited mouse is gaining weight at a fairly robust rate, which is something not previously observed with our AAV gene replacement therapy.14 In our experience, and as seen in Figures 7C and S7, mice treated with AAV-expressing human SP-B demonstrate slight gains in body weight until the gene therapy efficacy starts to wane, at which point their weight declines until lethal respiratory distress occurs, as seen with the gene addition mice in this study who have all perished by 178 days, while the gene-corrected mouse has achieved a stable condition and persists at 238 days. Efficacy may be improving if edited cells are actively dividing, as has been observed in prior in vivo gene editing experiments.19,20,21,34,41

In terms of improving the therapeutic efficacy of this promoterless AAV-CRISPR platform, it is conceivable that higher doses of donor and nuclease AAV would further extend survival, although potentially at the cost of triggering a vector directed immune response, which may be mitigated by improvements to vector design, such as the inclusion of elements that dampen the TLR9 response and enhance transduction efficiency,59 or combining this AAV-CRISPR platform with chemicals that enhance homologous recombination (HR) such as fludarabine.43 Additionally, utilizing this lung gene editing platform in utero or in neonates may further improve the therapeutic efficacy because of the tolerogenic nature of the fetal immune system and the fact that there is greater potential for editing stem or progenitor cells, which would lead to therapeutic changes being passed on to daughter cell populations and last for the lifetime of the individual. Further work into translating this platform design from a facultative progenitor, ATII cells, in the lungs to other more active lung cell types such as basal stem cells will be considered.

Our study has certain limitations, including the fact that only 1-kb homology arms were evaluated in this study, which limits the size of the therapeutic gene to approximately 2 kb. Future experiments will endeavor to define the minimum length of homology arm required to mediate efficient gene knock-in. Second, while we investigated off-target integrations in vitro using the TIDE assay, we did not conduct whole genome sequencing of lung cells from gene edited mice due to next-generation sequencing analysis requiring a different experimental design and the need for more resources to execute the study. Although this is an important next step, the fact that the donor template is lacking a promoter suggests that, even if an off-target integration event were to occur, it is unlikely to result in therapeutic transgene expression. Third, we did not investigate how many copies of AAV vectors entered into the lung cells of mice; however, other investigators have estimated more than 20 vectors per cell in the liver.60 A study by Miao et al.61 found that, when rAAV-FIX and rAAV-TH were infused at the same time, 42% of the transduced cells contained both genomes using two-color fluorescence in situ hybridization and others have shown that and co-transduciton increases in a dose-dependent manner.60 Finally, immunity to Cas9 has been found in humans62,63 and mice,64 and there is concern that prolonged Cas9 expression could provoke elimination of edited cells and/or tissue damage. Although we did not detect any evidence of pathology associated with SaCas9 expression in the multitude of lung tissue sections we examined, it is possible to mitigate this potential problem by using self-inactivating AAV-CRISPR systems, which have been shown to achieve efficient genome editing in vivo with controlled transient expression of CRISPR-Cas9.65,66

Currently, gene knock-in using this AAV-CRISPR platform is limited to cells that express SP-C; however, there are numerous disorders of ATII cells including SP-B deficiency, SP-C deficiency,56 ABCA3 deficiency,6 Hermansky-Pudlak syndrome pulmonary fibrosis,67 pulmonary alveolar microlithiasis,68,69 and NKX2-1-related disorders70 that would be amenable to correction with this platform.71 Moreover, since SP-C is such a robust promoter, ATII cells could be edited to express therapeutic transgenes that are secreted into the lung such as alpha-1 antitrypsin. It is important to note that clinical translation of this AAV CRISPR platform will require gRNAs to be validated in human cells and the donor template be revised such that the homology arms are derived from the human SP-C gene locus. Nevertheless, the AAV6.2FF capsid is expected to transduce human ATII cells based on data from human lung slices.14

In summary, we have engineered a promoterless AAV-CRISPR platform for mediating gene knock-in within ATII cells under the control of the SP-C promoter. This first-generation platform achieved editing efficiencies as high as 25% of lung epithelial cells and, when evaluated in a therapeutic model of SP-B deficiency, significantly extended survival. The editing efficiencies reported here are similar to results recently published by the National Institutes of Health Somatic Cell Genome Editing Consortium, which demonstrated CRISPR-based gene editing efficiencies of 16%–26% of cells in both the large and small airways using a dual AAV vector approach dependent on non-homologous end-joining.72 Another study by Chen et al.73 has also demonstrated impressive gene knockdown in the lungs of a transgenic murine model using AAV. Together, these studies highlight the potential for AAV-CRISPR-based gene editing approaches in the lung, with the hope to support the pursuit of lifelong curative treatments in the lungs.

Materials and methods

AAV genome construction

To generate the donor template plasmids pAAV-SPCFlankGFP, pAAV-SPCFlankAP, and pAAV-SPCFlankSPB-WPRE, the cDNAs for eGFP, human placental AP and murine SP-B (GenBank: NM_147779.2) were cloned into the MluI and KpnI site of the pAAV-SPCFlankMCS backbone, which contains left and right homology arms of approximately 1,000 bp comprising the murine SP-C intronic sequence and its 3′ UTR and downstream sequence, respectively. The SP-C stop codon was removed from the upstream or left homology arm and replaced with an F2A self-cleaving peptide. The repair template 3′ end is complementary to the SP-C 3′ UTR, yet has sites mutated to be gRNA resistant to limit nuclease binding after gene insertion has occurred. The plasmid, px601, otherwise known as pX601-AAV-CMV::NLS-SaCas9-NLS-3xHA-bGHpA; U6::BsaI-sgRNA was purchased from Addgene (Addgene plasmid # 61591). gRNAs were cloned individually into px601 following the protocol outlined by the ZhangLab in the Genome Engineering Toolbox (https://www.addgene.org/crispr/zhang/). In brief, px601 was digested with BsaI and purified using the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-Up kit. Next, pairs of oligos designed to become gRNAs were phosphorylated and annealed using T4 PNK (New England Biolabs) in a thermocycler (37°C for 30 min, 95°C for 5 min, then ramped down to 25 °C at a rate of −5 °C/min), and then ligated into digested px601. Oligos were initially designed to begin with guanine for expression from the U6 promoter, contain either 5′-CAAC or 5′-AAAC for ligation into px601, and made to be approximately 21 nt in length.

gRNA development in silico

Potential gRNA sites were determined using CHOPCHOP software.23,24 CHOPCHOP was used to search downstream and adjacent to the murine SP-C stop codon, using the parameters of a SaCas9 specific NGGRRT PAM, 20–21 nucleotides in length, and beginning with guanine for expression from the U6 promoter. gRNAs were ranked by CHOPCHOP based on estimated cutting efficiency, number of mismatches, existence of self-complementary regions, and GC content. Seven total gRNAs (gRNA5, gRNA6, gRNA7, gRNA8, gRNA9, gRNA10, and gRNA12) were selected and further assessed for off-targets by Cas-OFFinder327 software.25 Off-targets for each gRNA were ranked based on number of mismatched base pairs between the gRNA and genome, and the location of those mismatches (mismatches closer to the PAM being more likely to disrupt gRNA targeted Cas9 cutting). The top three off-targets for gRNA5 and gRNA9 were further examined for genome locations and possible feature disruptions through Mouse Genome Informatics and Genome Data Viewer (National Center for Biotechnology Information).74,75,76

Determination of gRNA on-target cutting efficiencies

gRNA on-target cutting efficiencies were determined using a modified T7 endonuclease I assay. First, the MLE 12 murine lung epithelial cell line (ATCC ATCC-CRL-2110) was transfected in a 12-well dish with px601-sg5 to px601-sg12 plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen L3000001) to induce insertions or deletions (INDELs) caused by Cas9-gRNA. At 48 h after transfection, cells were harvest and DNA was extracted using a QIAamp DNA mini kit (Qiagen 51304) and eluted in 200 μL molecular grade H2O. Next, a 1-kb region surrounding the gRNA on-target location was amplified using Q5 polymerase (New England Biolabs M0492). Primers used can be seen in Table S3. Amplified regions from the transfected MLE 12 genome were then purified by ethanol precipitation, and individually reannealed to introduce mismatches at Cas9-gRNA cut locations using the thermocycler conditions: 37°C for 30 min, 95°C for 10 min, then a decrease from 95°C to 85 °C at −1 °C/s, 85°C–25 °C at −0.1 °C/s, and held at 4°C. Hybridized PCR products then underwent digestion for 1 h at 37°C by T7 endonuclease I (New England Biolabs M0302) to cleave at mismatches. Fragments were then analyzed by gel electrophoresis, as well as on a 2100 Bioanalyzer, donated by Agilent, with DNA 1000 chips at the Advanced Analysis Center, Genomics Facility, University of Guelph, Ontario. Bioanalyzer results were normalized to apparent MLE 12 transfection efficiency with a pCAG-GFP plasmid following calculation using the formula provided by New England Biolabs: %gene modification = 100 × (1 – (1 – fraction cleaved)1/2).

Determination of gRNA off-target cutting efficiencies

gRNA off-target cutting efficiencies were determined using a TIDE DEcomposition assay (https://tide.nki.nl/). First, MLE 12 cells were transfected in a 12-well dish with px601-sg5, px601-sg6, px601-sg7, px601-sg8, px601-sg9, px601-sg10, or px601-sg12 plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen L3000001) to induce INDELs caused by Cas9-gRNA. At 48 h after transfection, cells were harvest and DNA was extracted using a QIAamp DNA mini kit (Qiagen 51304) and eluted in 200 μL molecular grade H2O. Next, a 1-kb region surrounding the gRNA off-target location was amplified using Q5 polymerase (New England Biolabs M0492). Primers used can be seen in Table S3. Amplified regions from the transfected MLE 12 genome were then gel extraction using a NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-Up kit (Takara #40609) and then sequenced at the Advanced Analysis Center, Genomics Facility, University of Guelph, Ontario, using an Applied Biosystems 3730 DNA analyzer. Sequence traces from transfected MLE 12 gDNA and control MLE 12 gDNA were analyzed using TIDE DEcomposition software (https://tide.nki.nl/) to track INDELs produced at selected off-target locations, using parameters of max INDEL size of 10 bp, and maximum decomposition window size based on trace length.26

Cell culture

Human embryonic kidney 293 cells (HEK293) (ATCC CRL-1573) and MLE 12 (ATCC CRL-2110) were maintained at 37°C and 5% carbon dioxide. HEK293 cells were grown in high glucose DMEM with 7% cosmic calf serum (HyClone, SH30087), 2 mM l-glutamine, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (pen-strep). MLE 12 cells were grown in DMEM/F12 1:1 media including HEPES (HyClone, SH30261) with the addition of 2% fetal bovine serum, Insulin-Transferrin-Selenium (Gibco, 41400045) to a final concentration of 1× (equating to insulin 0.005 mg/mL, transferrin 0.01 mg/mL, sodium selenite 30 nM), 10 nM hydrocortisone (Sigma H6909), 10 nM Beta-estradiol (Sigma E2758), 2 mM l-glutamine, and 1% pen-strep.

AAV vector production

AAV vectors were produced in mycoplasma free HEK293 cells (ATCC CRL-1573) using the AAV6.2FF capsid, purified using a GE HiTrap heparin column, and titered as described previously.77,78,79

Lung tissue dissociation, staining, and flow cytometry

Mice were euthanized and perfused through the pulmonary artery with 10 mL cold PBS using a 27G needle. The same needle size was inserted into the trachea, and 2 mL 10 U/mL dispase II (Sigma, D4693) was instilled. The trachea was then clamped, and the lungs were removed from the thoracic cavity, placed in 1 mL dispase II, and incubated at 37°C for 6 min before being transferred to Gentle Macs C tubes. After the addition of 120 U/mL DNase I, lungs were dissociated using the Gentle Macs Dissociator using the m lung 1.01 program. After dissociation, lung cell suspensions were filtered through 40-μm cell strainers, centrifuged at 500×g for 5 min. Supernatant was removed, and cell pellets resuspended in 1mL of ACK lysis buffer (150 mM NH4Cl, 10 mM KHCO3, 0.1 mM Na2EDTA in H2O, pH 7.2–7.4) and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. Next, 5 mL Hanks balanced salt solution with Ca2+ and Mg2+ was added to neutralize the ACK lysis buffer. Samples were then centrifuged at 500×g for 5 min. Supernatant was removed, pellets were resuspended in 200 μL FACS buffer (PBS-1% BSA) and transferred to a U-bottom 96-well plate. Plates were centrifuged at 500×g for 5min. Samples were then resuspended in 50 μL of FACS buffer and an anti-mouse CD16/32 antibody (BioLegend 101301) at a 1:200 dilution to block Fc domains and incubated at 4°C in the dark for 20 min. Next, 150 μL FACS buffer was added to samples, and plates were centrifuged again at 500×g for 5 min. Supernatant was removed and samples were resuspended in 50 μL total volume of FACS buffer containing primary antibodies of PE/Cy7 anti-mouse CD45.2 (BioLegend 109829) and APC/Fire 750 anti-mouse CD326 (Ep-CAM) (BioLegend 118229) at a 1:100 dilution along with 5 μL per sample of 7-AAD Viability Staining Solution (BioLegend 420403) and incubated at 4°C in the dark for 20 min. Next, 150 μL FACS buffer was added to samples, and plates were centrifuged at 500×g for 5 min. Supernatant was removed, and samples were resuspended in 200 μL FACS buffer then filtered through 100-μm filters into round bottom tubes. Additional FACS buffer was added to dilute each sample, and then samples were analyzed on a FACSCanto II using BD FACSDiva software for processing and FlowJo v10 for analysis. Compensation was performed using the same antibodies with UltraComp eBeads Compensation Beads (Invitrogen 01-2222-41) following the manufacturer’s protocol, and pACAG-GFP transfected HEK293 cells.

AP histochemistry

Mouse lungs were perfused through the pulmonary artery with 10 mL cold PBS and then inflated through the trachea with at 27G needle dispensing PBS. Lungs were then removed and separated, and two lobes were placed in 2% paraformaldehyde and incubated for 2.5 h on rotation. The remaining lobes were stored at −80°C for further analysis. After the lobes were fixed, they were washed three times with 1× PBS for 10 min each. The lungs were then heat inactivated at 65°C for 1 h to inactivate endogenous AP. They were then stained overnight in AP staining solution [100× NBT and 100X X-Phos in AP buffer (100 mM Tris-HCL, pH 8.5, 100 mM NaCl, and 50 mM MgCl2)] before repeating the wash steps detailed above. Gross images were obtained with the Infinity 2 stereomicroscope before lungs were transferred to a cryomold and OCT embedded. We obtained 10-μm tissue sections on Superfrost plus microscope slides with the Leica cryostat and equilibrated in AP Buffer (200 mM TRIS, 100 mM NaCl, and 50 mM MgCl2, pH 8.5) for 5 min before being stained with the following AP stain solution (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.5, 100 mM NaCl, 50 mM MgCl2, 0.34 mg/mL nitroblue tetrazoliumn salt, 0.17 mg/mL X-Phos). Sectioned slides were stored in the dark overnight before being washed with 1× PBS twice for 2 min each and counterstained with nuclear fast red solution for 1 min. Slides were rinsed in water and then dipped ten times in 95% ethanol. After air drying, they were submerged in xylene twice for 3 min each and then cover-slipped with mounting medium and analyzed with the Olympus Accu-Scope EXC-400 light microscope. Four non-successive images were analyzed with ImageJ to measure the AP staining intensity within a set measurement threshold.

AP activity assay

Tissues (0.7 g) were added to a screw cap tube along with 300 μL of TMNC lysis byffer (50 mM Tris-HCL Buffer, pH 7.5, 5 mM MgCl2, 100 mM NaCl, and 4% CHAPs in MilliQ) and a 1/4″ ceramic bead (MP 6540424) and homogenized on the Precellys 24 tissue homogenizer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 6000 rpm for 2× 1-min cycles. Homogenized samples were heat inactivated for 1 h at 65°C centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C to pellet cell debris. The clarified supernatant was collected and analyzed for AP enzymatic activity. Briefly, an 8-point standard curve ranging from 10 ng/mL to 400 ng/mL of human placental AP was prepared in a black 96-well plate. Samples and standards were diluted in TMNC buffer to a final volume of 100 μL. Then we applied 100 μL 2xSEAP (2M diethanolamine, 1 mM MgCl2, 10 mM l-homoarginine) + 5 μL 44.5 mM 4-methylumbelliferyl phosphate dissolved in DMSO enzymatic reaction substrate to each well and the plate was read at a 440 nm emission 20 times in 1 h using the Promega Glomax plate reader. Slopes for each enzymatic reaction were obtained and interpolated against the standard curve with samples being standardized over the measured weights of the homogenized tissues. Samples were compared with the controls and statistical significance was determined with a one-way ANOVA test.

Lung tissue DNA extraction

Following dissociation using dispase II as described above, genomic DNA extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen 51304) and amplified using Q5 polymerase (New England Biolabs M0492), both following the manufacturer’s instructions. Primers used are listed in Table S5. Following nested PCR, samples were analyzed via gel electrophoresis, gel purified and confirmed by Sanger sequencing.

Immunohistochemistry

Lungs were inflated with 1 mL 3.2% paraformaldehyde and then placed in 3.2% paraformaldehyde to fix for 2 h. After three washes in cold PBS, lungs were equilibrated overnight in 30% sucrose-PBS, followed by 1 h in 15% sucrose-50% OCT, then embedded and frozen in 100% OCT. Sections were made and placed onto microscope slides. Slides were washed in PBS, and permeabilized for 10 min in PBS-0.1% Triton X-100 (PBS-TR). Slides were washed in PBS, incubated in 0.3% H2O2-PBS for 10 min, and then blocked in 4% normal donkey serum in PBW with Tween (PBST) (0.1% v/v Tween 20 in PBS) for 2 h. Slides were then incubated with rabbit anti-Pro-SPC antibody (Abcam ab90716) and chicken anti-GFP (Abcam ab-13970) diluted 1:500 in 5% BSA PBST at a 1:500 dilution and 4°C in a humidity chamber overnight. The next day, slides were washed in PBS-TR, and then incubated with secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (Invitrogen A-11034) and Alexa Fluor 647 AffiniPure donkey anti-chicken (Jackson Laboratories 703-605-155) diluted 1:200 in 5% BSA PBST and incubated at room temperature in a humidity chamber for 1 h. Slides were then rinsed in PBST, and a drop of ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant with DAPI (ThermoFisher Scientific P36962) was added and slides were cover slipped and allowed to cure overnight in a light-sealed box. Slides were visualized on an Olympus Fluoview 500 confocal microscope.

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were approved by the University of Guelph Animal Care Committee and the Animal Care and Veterinary Service Committee at the University of Ottawa in accordance with Canadian Council on Animal Care guidelines. All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering.

Animals

Male and female C57BL/6 mice 3–20 weeks of age were purchased from Charles River Laboratories and allowed to acclimatize for one week prior to study commencement. Mice were housed in groups of four; food (Teklad Global 14% Protein Rodent Maintenance Diet) and tap water were provided ad libitum. The animals were maintained on a 12 h on and 12 h off light cycle, room temperature was 21°C, and humidity was 45%. The conditional SP-B−/− mice maintained on doxycycline diet were housed and bred at the Ottawa Hospital Research Institute. Mice were maintained on doxycycline-supplemented feed (0.625 g/kg doxycycline hyclate; Teklad) unless otherwise noted.

AAV administration

Previously, we compared the transduction efficiency of AAV when delivered to the lungs via intranasal, modified intranasal, intratracheal, and intubation and found that the modified intranasal administration worked very well, especially for larger volumes that require multiple administrations, such as the experiments conducted in this study, and that there was less variability in vector distribution within and between animals in the modified-intranasal technique.46 Therefore, AAV vectors were administered to lightly anesthetized mice using a modified method of intranasal delivery described previously.46

PR8 influenza injury

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of University of Pennsylvania and all animal experiments were done in compliance with ethical guidelines and the approved protocols. In the influenza injury model, C57BL/6 mice were intranasally administered 75 TCID50 units of influenza A/H1N1/Puerto Rico/8/34 (PR8) 24 h after AAV administration. PR8 virus dissolved in 30 μL PBS was pipetted onto the nostrils of heavily anesthetized mice (visual confirmation of agonal breathing), whereupon mice aspirated the fluid directly into their lungs. Mice were euthanized on day 25 after AAV administration and lungs were harvested.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and graphing were performed using Prism7 software. Data was compared using either a one-way ANOVA without matching or pairing, assuming Gaussian distribution and using Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, or by using unpaired, two-tailed t-tests, again assuming Gaussian distribution. For the survival model, pairwise comparisons of the survival curves of all treatment groups were performed using the Kaplan-Meier test. The p values obtained in GraphPad Prism were then compared with the significance value calculated through Bonferroni correction, with the results listed in Table S6.
