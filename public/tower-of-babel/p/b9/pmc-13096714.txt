High Prevalence of Non‐BRCA Pathogenic Variants and a Recurrent PALB2 Variant in an Argentine Hereditary Breast and Ovarian Cancer Cohort
Authors: Claudia Alejandra Martin, Catalina Bono, Juliana Mandrile, Christine Susana Mercedes Kunst, Adriana Elizabeth Borello, Rodolfo Ávila, Florencia Celeste Salazar, Virginia Miretti, Florencia Pabletich, Verónica Andreoli, Danilo Guillermo Ceschin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHereditary breast and ovarian cancer (HBOC) syndromes are strongly associated with germline pathogenic variants. While genetic testing historically focused on BRCA1/2, multigene panels have revealed clinically relevant variants in numerous non‐BRCA genes. However, data on the spectrum and prevalence of these variants remain limited in Argentina, particularly in regions with distinctive admixture patterns. This study aimed to determine the frequency and spectrum of pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants (P/LPVs) in non‐BRCA genes among patients with a personal and/or family history of breast or ovarian cancer evaluated in Córdoba, Argentina. We conducted a retrospective analysis of 283 individuals tested within the Oncogenetics Program at Hospital Privado Universitario de Córdoba between 2016 and 2022 using multigene next‐generation sequencing panels and Sanger sequencing according to contemporary clinical criteria. P/LPVs were identified in 85 individuals (30.0%, 95% CI: 24.7–35.8), with non‐BRCA genes accounting for 32 P/LPVs (11.3%, 95% CI: 7.9–15.6 of the cohort). The most prevalent non‐BRCA genes were PALB2 (4.59%, 95% CI: 2.5–7.7) and CHEK2 (2.47%, 95% CI: 1.0–5.0), followed by TP53, MUTYH, and ATM. Notably, the PALB2 c.1653T>A variant was identified in five unrelated families, representing a recurrent variant compatible with a possible founder effect in this population. Overall, these findings highlight a substantial contribution of non‐BRCA P/LPVs—particularly PALB2—in this Córdoba‐based HBOC cohort. Post hoc analyses suggested sufficient statistical sensitivity to detect the observed differences; however, results should be interpreted considering the retrospective, single‐center design. These findings support the clinical value of incorporating non‐BRCA genes into multigene testing strategies in Argentina and underscore the need for larger multi‐center studies to confirm these observations and refine genetic counseling and surveillance strategies in Latin American populations.Keywords: argentine population, founder effect, hereditary breast and ovarian cancer, non‐BRCA genes, PALB2 variants

Introduction

Hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndromes represent a significant proportion of cancer diagnoses, driven by germline pathogenic variants in predisposition genes. Historically, clinical attention has been primarily focused on BRCA1 and BRCA2 due to their high penetrance and frequent association with HBOC [1, 2, 3]. However, the advent of next‐generation sequencing (NGS) and the subsequent development and widespread adoption of multigene panels have revolutionized genetic testing. These technological advancements have reduced costs and enabled the simultaneous analysis of multiple genes associated with hereditary cancer syndromes, leading to the identification of pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants (P/LPVs) in a wider range of genes beyond BRCA1/2 [1, 3, 4, 5]. The increasing use of multigene panels has revealed that a substantial proportion of individuals with hereditary cancer syndromes carry P/LPVs in genes other than BRCA1/2. Large‐scale studies indicate that approximately 10%–15% of women with BC harbor germline P/LPVs, with a notable fraction, around 2.7%, occurring in non‐BRCA genes such as CHEK2, ATM, and PALB2 [1, 2, 3, 5]. Recent studies confirm similar P/LPV frequencies in international cohorts, underscoring the clinical relevance of non‐BRCA genes in hereditary cancer management [6, 7]. The identification of these non‐BRCA variants carries significant clinical challenges when advising cancer patients and/or families carrying P/LPV, as they are associated with a 2‐ to 5‐fold increased risk of various malignancies. However, the precise magnitude of risk and penetrance for many non‐BRCA genes can be variable and challenging to fully ascertain, partly due to limited information on the effectiveness of surveillance interventions in these groups [1, 8]. Genes associated with hereditary cancer syndromes are broadly classified based on their penetrance, or the probability of developing cancer given the presence of a P/LPV [1, 8]. High‐penetrance genes, such as BRCA1/2, TP53, and CDH1, confer substantially elevated lifetime cancer risk (typically > 5‐fold relative risk) [1, 8, 9]. Moderate‐risk genes, including ATM, CHEK2, and NF1, are associated with more moderate risk increase (typically 2‐ to 5‐fold relative risk) [1, 8, 9]. Notably, the classification of PALB2 has evolved; while historically considered moderate penetrance, accumulating evidence, particularly from large studies, demonstrates that germline PALB2 P/LPVs are associated with a BC risk comparable to that of BRCA1/2 carriers, conferring a lifetime risk ~4‐ to 8‐fold by age 80 [10]. This evidence supports classifying PALB2 alongside high‐penetrance genes for BC risk assessment [8, 10], a shift arising from its lower impact in individuals < 50 years old but greater after age > 60 years [10]. This directly impacts clinical management and surveillance strategies for carriers [8]. Carriers of P/LPVs in high‐penetrance genes like BRCA1/2 and PALB2 often benefit from intensive surveillance programs, including early and more frequent imaging, and may consider risk‐reducing surgeries [8]. Management recommendations also vary for risk associated with other cancer, like ovarian or pancreatic cancer [8]. Furthermore, identifying PALB2 variants has therapeutic implications, as carriers may respond to targeted therapies such as PARP inhibitors [11].

In Argentina, data on the prevalence and spectrum of mutations in non‐BRCA genes among patients with personal and/or family history of BC are notably limited. Previous studies of Solano et al. [12], using multigene panels, identified seven variants in CHEK2 and one in CDH1, MUTYH, NBN, RAD51D, and TP53 among 255 individuals, reporting a 4.7% frequency of P/LPVs in moderate‐penetrance genes. Similarly, Gonzalez et al. [13] reported a comparable prevalence rate in Argentine cohorts, emphasizing the importance of expanded panel testing, but a comprehensive picture of the non‐BRCA genetic landscape, particularly regionally, is still needed. The genetic landscape of hereditary cancer syndromes is strongly influenced by population ancestry and historical migration patterns. Argentina, for instance, presents a complex genetic architecture shaped by extensive European immigration, predominantly from Spain and Italy, alongside significant Native American and African contributions. This results in a highly admixed population exhibiting considerable regional genetic heterogeneity [14, 15, 16]. Studies in other Latin and Hispanic populations have demonstrated how this admixture influences the mutation spectrum of tumor suppressor genes and contributes distinct variants, including founder effects, across different ethnic backgrounds [17, 18, 19, 20]. Understanding the specific genetic landscape and potential founder effects in diverse Argentine subpopulations is crucial for accurate regional risk assessment and implementing effective precision oncology strategies. Given this context and the limited detailed regional data, the aim of this study was to determine the frequency and spectrum of P/LPV in non‐BRCA genes among patients with a personal and/or family history of breast or ovarian cancer evaluated at the Hospital Privado Universitario de Córdoba, Argentina, between 2016 and 2022.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Population

This study was designed as a retrospective analysis of patients evaluated within the Oncogenetics Program at the Hospital Privado Universitario de Córdoba, Argentina. The study included 283 patients selected based on clinical criteria for HBOC syndrome, including: (i) personal history of breast cancer diagnosed at or before 50 years of age; (ii) ovarian cancer at any age; (iii) pancreatic cancer in the context of a personal or family history suggestive of HBOC; and/or (iv) a significant family history defined as at least two relatives with breast and/or ovarian cancer, including at least one first‐degree and one second‐degree relative. Asymptomatic individuals were included when they belonged to families meeting these criteria. Patient data and samples were collected between January 2016 and December 2022.

Genetic Testing and Analysis

Peripheral blood samples were collected from all included patients and referred to the Molecular Biology Area belonging to the Hematology, Oncology and Genetics Laboratory from the Hospital Privado Universitario de Córdoba. Molecular evaluation was performed using multigene panels for hereditary cancer predisposition. The specific NGS panel utilized varied depending on factors including financial possibilities and health insurance coverage. However, the inclusion criteria for the genes analyzed in these panels were based on recommendations from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology—Genetic/Familial High‐Risk Assessment: Breast, Ovarian, and Pancreatic Cancer (version 1.2024) [21]. The core genes included in all NGS panels were ATM, BARD1, BRCA1, BRCA2, BRIP1, CDH1, CHEK2, DICER1, EPCAM, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, NF1, PALB2, PMS2, PTEN, RAD51C, RAD51D, STK11, TP53. In addition to NGS, Sanger sequencing was performed in individuals from families with previously identified pathogenic variants to confirm segregation or detect known familial mutations. Although different multigene panels were used over the study period, all analyses included a consistent core set of high‐ and moderate‐penetrance genes recommended by NCCN guidelines. Therefore, variability in panel composition is unlikely to have substantially affected the detection of the main pathogenic variants reported, particularly those in PALB2, CHEK2, ATM, and TP53.

Variant Interpretation

Identified genetic variants were classified as pathogenic (P), likely pathogenic (LP), variants of uncertain significance (VUS), likely benign, or benign based on established guidelines for variant interpretation, such as those from the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) [18].

Statistical Analysis

Data collected for analysis included the frequency of pathogenic P/LPVs identified in non‐BRCA genes, the frequency of novel variants, and their association with other detected mutations or variants of uncertain significance (VUS). Clinical information analyzed comprised the index case tumor type, average age at diagnosis, and family history of first‐ or second‐degree relative neoplasms.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. Frequencies and percentages with 95% confidence intervals (exact binomial method) were calculated for categorical variables using the binom.confint function in R. Continuous variables were described using measures of central tendency (mean) and dispersion (range).

Post hoc statistical power calculations were performed using a binomial proportion framework to evaluate whether the available sample size was sufficient to detect the observed differences in variant prevalence compared with reference population frequencies reported in the literature. For comparisons of gene prevalence between the Córdoba cohort and frequencies reported in the literature, Fisher's exact test was used when appropriate. These analyses were not used for sample size determination but rather to assess the robustness of the observed associations. Specifically, for the observed PALB2 prevalence of 4.59% compared with the expected European frequency of approximately 1.2%, our sample of 283 individuals provided an estimated statistical power of > 95% (α = 0.05, two‐tailed binomial proportion test). Similarly, for the recurrent PALB2 c.1653T>A variant, with an observed frequency of 1.77% versus expected population frequencies below 0.1%, the estimated statistical power was also > 95%.

Given the analysis of multiple genes (n = 20 core genes in the panel), the potential for multiple testing bias was considered. For individual gene frequency comparisons with literature values, statistical significance was assessed conservatively. For multiple population comparisons, results are presented with appropriate caution regarding Type I error inflation.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 with significance set at p < 0.05. Confidence intervals were calculated using exact binomial methods to ensure accuracy for small sample sizes.

Results

Overall Cohort and Main Findings

A total of 283 individuals meeting the clinical criteria for HBOC syndrome were studied between January 2016 and December 2022. Genetic testing, which included multigene panels and Sanger sequencing for familial variants, identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants (P/LPVs) in 85 individuals (30.0%, 95% CI: 24.7–35.8). Of these, 53 P/LPVs were identified in BRCA1/2 genes (18.7%, 95% CI: 14.4–23.8 of the total cohort), and 32 P/LPVs were found in other non‐BRCA tumor suppressor genes (11.3%, 95% CI: 7.9–15.6 of the total cohort).

Clinical Characteristics of the Study Cohort

The clinical characteristics of the study group are summarized in Table 1. The most frequent diagnosis among the 283 participants was BC (BC), observed in 75% of cases (n = 213). Pancreatic cancer was the primary diagnosis in 9.3% (n = 26) of cases, and 15.7% (n = 44) were asymptomatic individuals at the time of evaluation. Among the asymptomatic participants, two were carriers of PALB2 mutations, two carried CHEK2 mutations, and one individual, for whom the index case information was unavailable, was a carrier of an ATM mutation. For individuals with a personal history of neoplasm, the average age at diagnosis was 44.5 years (range: 20–66). Early diagnosis (≤ 35 years) was noted in three cases, all of whom carried TP53 mutations. The average age of cancer onset among patients with PALB2 P/LPVs was 50 years (range: 34–66).

Prevalence and Spectrum of Non‐BRCA Variants

Detailed analysis of the 32 P/LPVs identified in non‐BRCA genes revealed a spectrum of variants across multiple tumor suppressor genes, as presented in Table 2. The most prevalent P/LPVs were found in PALB2, accounting for 4.59% (95% CI: 2.47–7.73, 13/283) of the total study population, and CHEK2, present in 2.47% (95% CI: 1.00–5.03, 7/283) of the cohort. Other genes with identified P/LPVs included TP53 (1.06%, 95% CI: 0.22–3.07, 3/283), MUTYH (0.71%, 95% CI: 0.09–2.53, 2/283), and ATM, BRIP1, FANCC, MLH1, MSH2, FANCM, and PTEN, each identified in 0.35% (1/283) of the individuals studied. Regarding tumor spectrum, PALB2 pathogenic variants were predominantly identified in individuals with breast cancer, with a mean age at diagnosis of 50 years (range: 34–66), although two carriers were asymptomatic at the time of testing. CHEK2 variants were also mainly associated with breast cancer diagnoses. TP53 variants were exclusively observed in early‐onset breast cancer cases diagnosed at ≤ 35 years, consistent with Li‐Fraumeni syndrome. Isolated cases involving pancreatic cancer were observed among carriers of PALB2 and ATM variants.

Specific Findings for PALB2

Focusing on the PALB2 gene, our study yielded several significant findings. We identified five unrelated families harboring the shared P/LPV c.1653T>A (1.77%, 95% CI: 0.58–4.07 of total cohort), and two unrelated families sharing the P/LPV c.1959T>A (0.71%, 95% CI: 0.09–2.53 of total cohort). In addition, a novel PALB2 P/LPV, c.2872_2875del, was identified in one family. Additional PALB2 variants identified in single families are summarized in Table 2. The relatively high frequency of the c.1653T>A variant among unrelated individuals in our cohort suggests a potential founder effect in this Argentine population.

Discussion

This study aimed to analyze the spectrum and frequency of pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants (P/LPVs) in non‐BRCA genes within an Argentine cohort of patients with a personal or family history of breast and ovarian cancer. Our findings reveal a substantial prevalence of germline P/LPVs in this population, with 85 individuals (30.0%) carrying a variant. Notably, 32 P/LPVs were found in non‐BRCA tumor suppressor genes (11.3% of the total cohort), representing a significant proportion (approximately 38%) of all identified variants. These results underscore the increasing importance of evaluating a broader panel of genes beyond BRCA1/2 in individuals at high risk for hereditary cancer syndromes in Argentina.

The frequency of P/LPVs in non‐BRCA genes in our cohort (11.3%) is higher than previously reported in some studies from Argentina, which identified frequencies around 4.7% for moderate‐penetrance genes. For instance, Solano et al. identified seven variants in CHEK2 and one in CDH1, MUTYH, NBN, RAD51D, and TP53 among 255 individuals studied with multigene panels, reporting a 4.7% frequency of pathogenic variants in non‐BRCA genes [12]. Gonzalez et al. also reported comparable prevalence rates in other Argentine cohorts [13]. Our frequency of PALB2 variants (4.59%, 13/283) contrasts with rates reported by Cerretini et al. (3.6%, 4/112) and Gonzalez et al. (2.78%, 53/1905) [13, 25], although it aligns with the understanding that PALB2 is a significant non‐BRCA gene in Argentina.

Carriers of pathogenic variants in BRCA1 and BRCA2 have well‐established lifetime cancer risks, including breast cancer risks exceeding 60%–70%, ovarian cancer risks of approximately 40%–45% for BRCA1 and 15%–20% for BRCA2, and increased pancreatic cancer risk. In contrast, non‐BRCA genes confer variable but clinically meaningful risks. PALB2 pathogenic variants are associated with breast cancer risks comparable to BRCA2, with cumulative lifetime risks exceeding 40%, while CHEK2 and ATM variants are generally associated with moderate risk increases. These differences have important implications for surveillance, risk‐reducing strategies, and therapeutic decision‐making, as outlined in current ESMO and NCCN guidelines [8].

Population Comparisons and Statistical Considerations

The frequency of non‐BRCA P/LPVs in our Córdoba cohort (11.3%, 95% CI: 8.0–15.5) is significantly higher than previously reported in some Argentine studies (4.7%) but aligns with recent findings from other Latin American populations. When comparing our PALB2 prevalence (4.59%, 95% CI: 2.6–7.7) with European populations (~1.2%), the difference is statistically significant with robust statistical power (99.9%) (Table 4). Similarly, the frequency of the recurrent PALB2 c.1653T>A variant (1.77%, 95% CI: 0.7–4.1) represents a substantial enrichment compared to global population databases (gnomAD: 0.02%), supporting the hypothesis of a population‐specific founder effect.

These comparisons should be interpreted considering potential differences in selection criteria, panel composition, and population ancestry between studies. Post hoc power estimates suggested that the available sample size was sufficient to detect the observed differences. However, these results should be interpreted cautiously given the retrospective and single‐center design of the study. These differences in observed frequencies within Argentina may be attributed to variations in selection criteria, methodologies, and the specific genes included in panels across different studies.

Comparison with studies from other Latin American populations reveals variable frequencies of non‐BRCA P/LPVs. For instance, cohort selected based on family history in Guatemala [18] showed high rates (similar to us), while the CHANCE cohort in Brazil reported PALB2 and CHEK2 as the most frequent non‐BRCA genes (10.3% and 7.2%, respectively), followed by ATM (4.1%) [22]. In contrast, studies in Colombia found lower frequencies for ATM and PALB2 (1.5% and 1.2%, respectively) and CHEK2 (1%) [23]. Similarly, Sandoval et al. and Carvalho et al. reported lower frequencies in Brazilian cohorts, underscoring the importance of publishing regional differences [6, 23]. Examining data from non‐Latin American cohorts further illustrates this variability; a Thai population showed higher prevalence in ATM (20.5%), PALB2 (17.8%), and TP53 (13.6%) among non‐BRCA variants [26]. In a Swedish cohort, BRCA1/2 variants were found in 5.95% and 2.94%, respectively, while CHEK2 was observed in 3.46%, ATM in 7%, and PALB2 in 0.8% [24]. Additionally, in an Emirati cohort, non‐BRCA P/LPVs were identified less frequently than BRCA1/2 variants, with CHEK2 at 2.5% and BARD1 at 2%, and PALB2 and other non‐BRCA genes identified less frequently compared to our findings [27]. These disparities across populations highlight that variations in genetic backgrounds, study methodologies, selection criteria, and the specific genes included in panels contribute significantly to the observed differences in variant prevalence.

Our findings regarding PALB2 are particularly noteworthy. We identified PALB2 as the most frequently mutated non‐BRCA gene in our cohort (4.59%). We identified five unrelated families sharing the c.1653T>A variant and two unrelated families with the c.1959T>A variant, totaling 13 carriers. Among these cases, two young asymptomatic sisters aged 24 and 29 benefited from early genetic counseling, enabling adequate surveillance planning. In our series, the average age of cancer onset in PALB2 variant carriers was 50 years (range: 34–66). The c.1653T>A variant accounts for nearly 50% of reported PALB2 cases in the literature [13, 25, 28] and in our cohort, it represents 38.46% of identified PALB2 variants. The relatively high frequency of this variant among apparently unrelated individuals suggests the possibility of a founder effect in this population. First, the 1.77% frequency represents an 80‐fold enrichment compared to global population databases (gnomAD: 0.02%) [29], a magnitude of difference rarely observed without founder effects. Second, the geographic distribution of affected families within Córdoba, despite no known consanguinity, suggests common ancestry rather than independent mutational events. Third, this finding is consistent with Gonzalez et al., who identified this variant as highly recurrent across multiple Argentine regions, indicating a country‐wide rather than localized phenomenon [13]. The convergent evidence from multiple independent Argentine studies [13, 25], together with reports of this variant in Spanish populations and the well‐documented historical migration patterns from Spain to Argentina during the 19th and early 20th centuries [16, 30], provides biological and historical plausibility for the hypothesis of a potential founder effect. While formal haplotype analysis would provide definitive confirmation, the statistical, geographic, and historical evidence collectively support the hypothesis of a possible founder effect. The PALB2 c.1653T>A (p.Tyr551) has been identified in various populations, with notable prevalence in both Spain and Argentina, probably related to the immigrant patterns between these countries [13, 30].

The genetic landscape of hereditary cancer syndromes and their mutation spectrum are strongly influenced by population ancestry and historical migration patterns, mainly from Spain and Italy, alongside the presence of Native American and African roots. Studies indicate that the European ancestry component in Argentina is predominant (78.6% on average) but is unevenly distributed, with higher proportions in urban areas and greater Native American ancestry in the northwest and central‐west regions [14, 15, 16]. The presence of different Native American components, including those from the Central Andes, Patagonia, and subtropical forests, further supports the heterogeneity of genetic backgrounds within the Argentine population [14, 15]. The impact of ancestry on hereditary cancer syndromes has been well documented in Latino and Hispanic populations, where disparities in cancer predisposition genes and variations in the mutational landscape influenced by country of origin and admixture proportions have been observed [7, 17, 18, 19]. This variability in genetic background could influence the mutation spectrum of HBOC in Argentina. Furthermore, historical migration patterns could have introduced founder mutations, affecting the prevalence of certain pathogenic variants within specific Argentine subpopulations [13, 16, 30].

It is important to emphasize that the designation of PALB2 c.1653T>A as a potential founder variant is based on convergent statistical, geographic, and historical evidence rather than on formal haplotype analysis. While definitive confirmation of a founder effect would require haplotype or ancestry‐informative marker analyses, such approaches were beyond the scope of this retrospective, single‐center study. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating and consistent with, but not definitively proving, a founder effect.

Founder Effect Mechanisms in Argentine Population

Although speculative, several historical and population genetic mechanisms could explain the observed recurrence of the PALB2 c.1653T>A variant in Argentina. The recurrence of the PALB2 c.1653T>A variant in Argentina may be related to historical European immigration between 1870 and 1930, when approximately 6 million Europeans, predominantly from Spain and Italy, settled in the country [16]. Spanish immigrants, who comprised approximately 33% of total immigration, would have introduced population‐specific variants that subsequently expanded through population bottlenecks and geographic clustering in urban centers like Córdoba.

The founder effect mechanism is supported by several population genetic principles: (i) Population bottleneck: Early Spanish settlers in Córdoba represented a small subset of the total Spanish population, potentially carrying specific variants at higher frequencies; (ii) Geographic clustering: Spanish immigrants often settled in specific regions, creating communities with shared ancestry; (iii) Population expansion: Subsequent population growth would have amplified the frequency of founder variants within these communities; and (iv) Genetic drift: Random changes in allele frequencies during population establishment could have further increased variant prevalence.

The persistence of this founder effect into contemporary populations reflects the relatively recent timing of immigration (4–5 generations ago) and the tendency for genetic founder effects to remain detectable for centuries in admixed populations. This phenomenon has been documented in other Latin American populations, where European founder variants continue to influence contemporary genetic architecture despite ongoing admixture with Native American and African populations [17, 18, 19, 31].

Beyond the specific findings for PALB2, the identification of P/LPVs in other non‐BRCA genes such as CHEK2, ATM, and TP53 also carries significant clinical implications for risk management and surveillance. These genes, while potentially conferring varying levels of risk (high or moderate), necessitate tailored approaches to patient care and family counseling [1, 8, 32]. The growing body of evidence on non‐BRCA genes highlights their increasing importance in routine clinical practice [1, 8, 33]. The observed variability in variant prevalence across different cohorts, including those in Latin America, further emphasizes the need for implementing genetic testing strategies, such as multigene panels, that are tailored to the specific population being evaluated, such as the Argentine cohort studied here [12, 13]. The clinical utility of identifying PALB2 variants is well established due to their association with BC risk comparable to BRCA1 (exceeding 40% by age 70), therapeutic response to PARP inhibitors, and the need for tailored surveillance and risk‐reduction strategies similar to those for BRCA1/2 carriers [10, 11, 21, 32].

Statistical and Methodological Considerations

Founder Effect Validation and Population Genetics Considerations

While our study provides compelling evidence for a potential founder effect of PALB2 c.1653T>A in the Argentine population, we acknowledge that definitive validation requires additional population genetic analyses not performed in this study. Formal founder effect confirmation would necessitate: (i) extended haplotype homozygosity (EHH) analysis using multiple short tandem repeat (STR) markers flanking the variant; (ii) linkage disequilibrium mapping to establish shared ancestral chromosomal segments; (iii) coalescent analysis to estimate variant age and population bottleneck effects; and (iv) comparison with Spanish ancestral populations to confirm historical migration patterns.

Nevertheless, our findings provide evidence consistent with a potential founder effect: the variant frequency (1.77%, 95% CI: 0.7–4.1) represents a markedly higher frequency compared with global population databases such as gnomAD (0.02%); five unrelated families share the identical variant despite geographic dispersion within Córdoba, and the variant has been consistently reported as recurrent in multiple independent Argentine studies. This convergent evidence, combined with the well‐documented Spanish immigration history to Argentina and previous reports of this variant in Spanish populations, is consistent with the founder effect hypothesis despite the lack of formal haplotype validation.

Post hoc statistical power estimates suggest that the available sample size was adequate to detect the observed differences in variant prevalence. Nevertheless, larger multi‐center studies will be necessary to confirm these findings across broader Argentine populations.

Because the power analyses were performed retrospectively, they should be interpreted as descriptive assessments of the observed data rather than as prospective sample size determinations.

General Considerations

This study has limitations that should be acknowledged. The retrospective design and the sample size (n = 283) from a single center in Córdoba may limit generalizability to the broader Argentine population. The inclusion criteria may also contribute to selection biases. Furthermore, detailed demographic and specific ancestry data for the study participants were not uniformly available for analysis, which could provide deeper insights into the relationship between genetic background and variant spectrum. In genetic studies where classes are imbalanced—where the frequency of certain mutations is much lower than others—it is crucial to employ techniques that address this imbalance to ensure robust analysis. This imbalance could affect the accuracy of predictive models or statistical interpretations. Differences in selection criteria and analytical techniques employed across studies can significantly impact the observed representation of P/LPVs and their subsequent management strategies. Kurian et al. highlighted this impact [4].

Our findings underscore the clinical importance of incorporating non‐BRCA genes into hereditary cancer testing panels in Argentina. The high prevalence of PALB2 and other non‐BRCA variants highlights the need for tailored genetic testing strategies in Argentina and Latin America. Expanding genetic research across diverse Argentine regions is essential for equitable access to genetic testing and precision oncology. Integrating data from multiple geographic regions will enable comprehensive understanding of hereditary cancer risk, allowing for tailored surveillance and prevention strategies reflecting the country's genetic diversity.

Study Strengths and Statistical Robustness

This study has several methodological strengths, including comprehensive confidence interval reporting, transparent statistical workflows with publicly available code, and systematic comparison with international cohorts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights a substantial prevalence of pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants in non‐BRCA genes, particularly PALB2, within a cohort of hereditary cancer patients from Córdoba, Argentina. These findings underscore the clinical importance of incorporating non‐BRCA genes into genetic testing panels to support more informed risk assessment and individualized clinical management strategies. The identification of a recurrent PALB2 c.1653 T>A variant, compatible with a possible founder effect, further emphasizes the relevance of regional genetic diversity in genetic testing and counseling approaches. While our results reveal population‐specific patterns, larger multicenter studies will be required to confirm their generalizability across different regions of Argentina and to further refine population‐adapted strategies for hereditary cancer care.
