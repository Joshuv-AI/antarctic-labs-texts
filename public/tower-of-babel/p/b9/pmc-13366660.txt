Gender stereotypes about women in pharmacy leadership: evidence from a cross-sectional study in Lebanon
Authors: Perla Nassar, Reine Rached, Petra Nassar, Sanaa Awada, Inaam Nasrallah, Layal Safa, Roula Bou Assi, Georges Hatem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGender-related stereotypes and inequities in career advancement and leadership recognition may persist in the pharmaceutical workforce. Evidence examining multiple domains of gender-related perceptions within the pharmaceutical sector remains limited. This study aimed to assess gender-related perceptions across distinct stereotype domains and to examine their associations with pharmacists’ sociodemographic and professional characteristics.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 279 pharmacists. Participants rated agreement with statements reflecting gender-related perceptions. Composite scores were calculated for three domains: stereotypes of women’s capabilities, gendered perceptions in career advancement, and recognition of women leaders, with higher scores indicating more negative perceptions or lower recognition.ResultsParticipants (62.7% women) mostly rejected negative stereotypes about women’s capabilities (63.8–77.4% disagreement across items). Men reported significantly higher stereotype scores than women (p = 0.004), and graduates of public universities showed higher stereotype and career advancement scores than private university graduates (p < 0.05). Lower recognition of women leaders was observed among older participants (p = 0.019), married or divorced respondents (p = 0.026), those with higher perceived economic status (p = 0.001), pharmacists working in the pharmaceutical field (p = 0.043), and participants with ≥ 6 years of practice (p = 0.021).ConclusionsWhile explicit gender stereotypes appear to be declining within the pharmaceutical sector, implicit biases related to career advancement and leadership recognition persist and vary across demographic, educational, and professional contexts. Addressing these disparities requires targeted educational, organizational, and policy interventions to promote equitable recognition and advancement of women. Pharmacy curricula and professional development programs should incorporate training on implicit bias, gender equity, and inclusive leadership to foster equitable workplace cultures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09482-3.

Background

Despite substantial progress in women’s participation in the pharmaceutical workforce, gender-based misconceptions continue to shape professional trajectories, leadership opportunities, and workplace dynamics [1–3]. Persistent stereotypes portraying women as less committed, overly emotional, insufficiently ambitious, or less capable in technical and high-responsibility roles may influence hiring decisions, salary negotiations, promotion pathways, and evaluations of leadership effectiveness [2, 4–6]. These beliefs often operate subtly within organizational cultures [7, 8], reinforcing structural inequalities even in sectors characterized by high educational attainment and scientific expertise.

The pharmaceutical industry represents a particularly relevant context in which to examine such misconceptions. Although women constitute a large proportion of the pharmaceutical and healthcare workforce [9], they remain underrepresented in senior leadership and decision-making positions. Evidence suggests that gendered expectations surrounding work-family balance, negotiation behavior, and leadership style continue to disadvantage women, with assertive behaviors frequently evaluated less favorably in women than in men [10–12]. At the same time, positive perceptions acknowledging women’s competence and expertise coexist with traditional stereotypes, creating ambivalent and sometimes contradictory attitudes toward women’s professional roles [12, 13].

The persistence of these dynamics can be understood through established gender and leadership frameworks. Role congruity theory, developed by Eagly and Karau [14, 15], posits that leadership is culturally associated with agentic traits such as assertiveness and independence, which are traditionally ascribed to men, whereas women are more often associated with communal traits such as nurturing and cooperation. This perceived incongruity can result in biased evaluations of women leaders, who may be penalized both for conforming to and deviating from gendered expectations. In healthcare and pharmaceutical settings, this mismatch helps explain why women in leadership may be viewed as either insufficiently assertive or overly “masculine,” and why they face criticism regardless of leadership style [14, 16]. Despite efforts to promote gender equality, women continue to face barriers, including entrenched organizational biases, limited access to sponsorship and mentorship, and structural challenges related to career interruptions [17–19]. Nevertheless, recent research suggests gradual progress, with organizations implementing mentorship programs, leadership development initiatives, and diversity and inclusion strategies to support women’s advancement [20]. Companies with active gender-focused inclusion policies report improved career outcomes for women, greater representation in executive leadership, and higher organizational performance [21, 22].

The relevance of women’s leadership in the pharmaceutical sector became particularly visible during recent global crises, including the coronavirus disease of 2019 pandemic. Studies indicate that organizations led by women or with higher proportions of women in leadership positions responded more effectively to crisis-related challenges, demonstrating strengths in communication, collaboration, and empathy [23–25]. Women-led organizations were also more likely to prioritize employee mental health and introduce flexible work arrangements, fostering resilience and engagement during periods of uncertainty [24, 26]. Nevertheless, despite growing attention to gender diversity in leadership, important knowledge gaps remain. These gaps are particularly pronounced in low- and middle-income or crisis-affected settings, such as Lebanon. Research in the Lebanese pharmaceutical and healthcare sectors has primarily focused on women’s underrepresentation in leadership [27–29], with little attention to how gender inequalities intersect with other determinants and personal characteristics. Therefore, this study sought to evaluate how pharmacists perceive women’s roles and leadership within the pharmaceutical sector by exploring three key dimensions: perceptions of women’s professional capabilities, attitudes toward women’s career advancement opportunities, and recognition of women in leadership positions. In addition, the study aimed to identify whether these perceptions differed according to pharmacists’ sociodemographic, educational, and occupational characteristics.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted over 4 months, from July 2024 to October 2024, to examine misconceptions about women in executive roles in the pharmaceutical sector from a pharmacist’s perspective. The study protocol was reviewed and registered on clinicaltrials.gov before data collection and is publicly accessible (reference: NCT06599268; 15/07/2024). The study received approval from the institutional review board and the scientific committee of the Lebanese University (Reference 17/24/D). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants provided written informed consent. They were explicitly informed that their participation was voluntary and that they retained the right to withdraw at any stage of the study, with only the responses recorded up to that point being retained. No financial incentives were offered, and the results were for research purposes only.

Study sample

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study comprised individuals aged 18 and above residing in Lebanon, including graduate and undergraduate working pharmacists. Inclusion criteria did not hinge on sex, race, or nationality. Exclusions included pharmacists who declined to participate, those living abroad, those not working, and those who declined to provide informed consent.

Sample size calculation

The OpenEpi program (version 3.01) was used to calculate the required sample size to answer our objective. Since it is the first study performed in Lebanon, a 50% interest/response rate was assumed. Given the total number of pharmacists (around 15000), a minimum sample of 267 participants was required, with 95% confidence, 6% precision, and 80% power. Given a 5% loss-to-follow-up or incomplete responses, we aimed to target 279 participants.

Study tool and data collection

A structured questionnaire was used to collect data (Supplementary file). It was constructed following a thorough literature review [20, 30–33] and based on the feedback of two external experts (one from the National Institute of Health, Porto, Portugal, and the second from the Institute of Public Health of the University of Porto) to ensure content validity and clarity. The survey was subsequently piloted among 15 participants who were not included in the final analysis. Based on participant feedback and expert recommendations, three questions were revised, and two questions were removed to improve clarity, comprehensibility, and overall coherence of the questionnaire. The instrument demonstrated good reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.840). The final survey was available in English and distributed via Google Forms links and hard copies, based on participants’ preferences. The questionnaire typically took approximately 6 to 10 min to complete. It collected general characteristics of the participants regarding their age, gender, highest level of education (student, bachelor’s degree in pharmacy, master’s degree, PharmD, PhD), marital status (single, married, widowed/divorced), perceived economic status (less than average, average and more than average), accommodation, work sector, location, position and hours, salary currency (Fully in United States Dollars (USD), fully in Lebanese Pounds (LBP), mix USD and LBP) and years of practice (< 5 years, 6–10 years, > 10 years). Gender-related misconceptions about women in the pharmaceutical sector were assessed using a set of statements reflecting common stereotypes and biased beliefs regarding women’s professional commitment, leadership capacity, emotionality, ambition, technical competence, and career advancement. Participants were asked to indicate their level of agreement with each statement using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (Strongly disagree) to 5 (Strongly agree). The items included negatively framed statements (e.g., “Women are less committed to their careers,” “Women are too emotional for high-stress jobs,” “Women can’t balance work and family”), which capture traditional gender stereotypes, as well as positively framed statements (e.g., “Women are seen as valuable contributors to the pharmaceutical industry,” “Female leaders in the pharmaceutical industry are viewed as effective and competent”), which assess recognition of women’s professional value and leadership effectiveness. Higher agreement with negatively framed items indicates stronger endorsement of gender-based misconceptions, whereas higher agreement with positively framed items reflects more egalitarian attitudes toward women’s roles and competencies.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences Version 29. The participants’ general characteristics are presented as frequencies and percentages. Age was categorized into five groups: Generation Z (< 27 years), millennials (28–43 years), Generation X (44–59 years), and baby boomers (> 60 years). Pharmacists’ conceptions regarding women in the pharmaceutical field were assessed across 14 factors. For each group, the scores ranged between 0 and 10. The first group (6 items), “Stereotypes on women’s capabilities,” has Likert scale scores of 0, 0.416, 0.833, 1.249, and 1.666 (Cronbach’s alpha of 0.710 and positive inter-item correlation). The second group (5 items), “Gendered perceptions in career advancement,” has a Likert scale of 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 (Cronbach’s alpha of 0.717 and positive inter-item correlation). The third group (3 items), “Recognition of women leaders,” has Likert-scale scores of 0, 0.833, 1.666, 2.5, and 3.333 (Cronbach’s alpha of 0.852 and positive inter-item correlations). Inverse coding was assigned to the factors in this scale since they represent positive statements. Given our data’s skewness [34], differences in misconception domain scores across participant characteristics were assessed using Mann-Whitney U tests for variables with two categories and Kruskal-Wallis tests for variables with three or more categories. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

General characteristics of the participants

Table 1 presents the general characteristics of the study sample, which included 279 participants. The mean age of respondents was 34.6 ± 10.9 years. Women represented nearly two-thirds of the sample (62.7%), compared with 36.9% men, while 0.4% identified as non-binary. Regarding marital status, 53.0% of participants were single, 39.1% were married, and 7.9% were divorced or widowed. Most respondents (75.6%) reported living with their families, whereas 16.1% lived alone and 8.2% lived with friends or others. In terms of educational attainment, 39.1% held a master’s degree, 29.0% had a PharmD, and 19.4% had a bachelor’s degree in pharmacy. A majority graduated from private universities (60.6%), while 39.4% graduated from public institutions. Concerning perceived economic status, approximately two-thirds (62.4%) rated their financial situation as average. Employment was predominantly within the pharmaceutical sector (96.8%). Regarding work location, 34.4% were employed in Beirut, 31.5% in Mount Lebanon, 8.6% in the South, 7.2% in the Beqaa, 6.8% in the North, 4.3% in Akkar, and 3.6% worked equally in Baalbek-Hermel and Nabatiyeh. The most common work settings were community pharmacies (32.6%), pharmaceutical companies (25.4%), and hospitals (11.5%). Most participants were employed full-time (79.9%). Regarding job position, 35.8% identified as mid-level workers, 34.1% as entry-level workers, 17.9% as senior-level workers, and 12.2% as pharmacy or company owners. Nearly half of respondents (48.0%) reported working 25–40 h per week, while 38.4% worked more than 40 h per week and 13.6% worked fewer than 24 h per week. In terms of remuneration, 62.7% were paid fully in USD, 34.8% received a mixed salary in USD and LBP, and 2.5% were paid exclusively in LBP. Concerning professional experience, 36.6% of participants had practiced for ≤ 5 years, 29.7% for 6–10 years, and 33.7% for ≥ 10 years.

General characteristics of the respondents

Conceptions regarding women in the pharmaceutical workplace

Figure 1 presents pharmacists’ responses to statements assessing gender-related misconceptions. Most respondents disagreed or strongly disagreed with statements reflecting negative stereotypes about women’s capabilities. Specifically, 76.7% disagreed or strongly disagreed that women are not good leaders, 63.8% disagreed or strongly disagreed that women are too emotional for high-stress jobs, and 77.4% rejected the belief that women are not as technical or skilled. Similarly, 73.5% disagreed or strongly disagreed with the statement that women are not ambitious. Regarding occupational suitability, 48.8% disagreed or strongly disagreed that women are better suited for certain jobs, while 31.6% agreed or strongly agreed. Concerning leadership norms, 33.7% agreed or strongly agreed that aggressive leadership styles are more accepted from men than women, whereas 39.8% disagreed or strongly disagreed. Regarding career advancement, 76.3% of respondents disagreed or strongly disagreed that women are less committed to their careers. The perception that women prefer administrative roles was rejected by 54.1% of participants, although 24.8% agreed or strongly agreed and 21.1% selected neutral responses. Regarding work-family balance, 65.2% disagreed or strongly disagreed that women cannot balance work and family responsibilities, while 15.4% agreed or strongly agreed. Views on salary negotiation were more mixed: 54.8% disagreed or strongly disagreed that women do not negotiate salaries, 21.1% agreed or strongly agreed, and 24.0% responded neutrally. Finally, 19.4% agreed or strongly agreed that women in high positions are there due to quotas, whereas 49.5% disagreed or strongly disagreed. Regarding women’s professional recognition, 38.0% of participants reported agreement or strong agreement that women are valuable contributors to the pharmaceutical industry, while 24.4% selected neutral responses. Similarly, 39.7% agreed or strongly agreed that women in the pharmaceutical sector are recognized for their expertise and knowledge, whereas 41.3% disagreed or strongly disagreed. Female leaders were viewed as effective and competent by 43.0% of respondents, with 28.3% reporting neutral views.

Associations between conceptions regarding women and respondents’ characteristics

Association with stereotypes of women’s capabilities scores

Table 2 presents the associations between stereotypes of women’s capabilities scores and participants’ sociodemographic and professional characteristics. A statistically significant association with gender was observed (p = 0.004), with men reporting higher stereotype scores (3.94 ± 1.68) than women (3.34 ± 1.70). Although the non-binary group showed a higher mean score (6.66), this finding should be interpreted cautiously due to the very small sample size. A significant association was also observed for university type (p = 0.003). Participants who graduated from public universities reported higher stereotype scores (3.95 ± 1.72) than those from private universities (3.33 ± 1.68). No statistically significant associations were found between stereotype scores and age group, marital status, living situation, educational level, perceived economic status, work field, years of practice, work location, type of work, employment status, current position, average weekly working hours, or salary currency (p > 0.05).

Association with gendered perceptions in career advancement scores

Table 3 presents the associations between gendered perceptions in career advancement scores and participants’ sociodemographic and professional characteristics. Statistically significant associations were observed for gender (p = 0.001), type of university (p < 0.001), work location (p = 0.043), and living situation (p = 0.020). Men reported higher mean scores (3.75 ± 1.69) than women (2.92 ± 1.84), while the non-binary group reported a mean score of 3.50. Participants who graduated from public universities reported higher scores (3.72 ± 1.89) than those from private universities (2.90 ± 1.71). Regarding work location, higher mean scores were observed in Baalbek-Hermel (4.40 ± 2.55) and Akkar (4.16 ± 1.98) than in other regions. For living situation, participants living with friends or others (3.92 ± 1.80) or alone (3.73 ± 1.81) reported higher scores than those living with family (3.05 ± 1.81). No statistically significant associations were found between career advancement perception scores and age group, marital status, educational level, perceived economic status, work field, type of work, employment status, current position, average working hours per week, salary currency, or years of practice (p > 0.05).

Association with recognition of women leaders’ scores

Table 4 presents the associations between recognition of women leaders’ scores and participants’ sociodemographic and professional characteristics. Statistically significant associations were observed for age group (p = 0.019), marital status (p = 0.026), type of university (p = 0.013), perceived economic status (p = 0.001), work field (p = 0.043), years of practice (p = 0.021), and work location (p = 0.016). Lower recognition of women leaders (i.e., higher scores) was observed among millennials (5.08 ± 2.77), Generation X (5.27 ± 2.48), and baby boomers (5.27 ± 2.42) compared with Generation Z (4.06 ± 2.44). Married (5.12 ± 2.70) and divorced/widowed participants (5.75 ± 2.41) exhibited lower recognition than single participants (4.44 ± 2.60). Participants graduating from public universities reported lower recognition (5.30 ± 2.34) than those from private universities (4.49 ± 2.79). Similarly, participants who perceived their economic status as above average demonstrated lower recognition (5.66 ± 2.57) than those reporting average (4.40 ± 2.62) or below average (4.67 ± 2.44) financial status. Regarding professional characteristics, participants working in the pharmaceutical field showed lower recognition (4.87 ± 2.65) than those in non-pharmaceutical fields (3.05 ± 1.86). Lower recognition was also observed among participants with 6–10 years (5.22 ± 2.64) and > 10 years (5.07 ± 2.66) of practice, compared with those with ≤ 5 years (4.23 ± 2.57). Significant regional differences were observed, with lower recognition in Nabatiyeh (6.66 ± 1.92), Beqaa (5.45 ± 2.45), Baalbek-Hermel (5.49 ± 2.52), and the North (5.35 ± 2.76). No statistically significant associations were found between recognition scores and gender (p = 0.267), living situation (p = 0.051), highest level of education (p = 0.153), type of work (p = 0.056), employment status (p = 0.795), current position (p = 0.208), average working hours per week (p = 0.841), or salary currency (p = 0.260).

Discussion

This study explored gender-related perceptions in the pharmaceutical workplace across three interrelated domains: stereotypes of women’s capabilities, gendered perceptions of career advancement, and recognition of women leaders. While the findings indicate a general rejection of overt gender stereotypes, they also reveal consistent and patterned disparities across gender, educational background, professional context, and sociodemographic characteristics, highlighting the persistence of implicit and structural gender bias within the profession. These findings are particularly relevant to pharmacy education and professional development, as educational and training environments may play a central role in shaping future pharmacists’ perceptions of leadership, gender equity, and workplace inclusion.

Stereotypes of women’s capabilities

Stereotypes of women’s capabilities were significantly associated with gender and type of university, with men and graduates of public universities reporting higher stereotype scores. The higher endorsement of stereotypical beliefs among men aligns with extensive literature suggesting that dominant groups are more likely to normalize existing inequalities and underestimate structural barriers faced by women [35–37]. The absence of significant associations with age, years of practice, or work-related variables suggests that these stereotypes may be shaped earlier in professional socialization rather than solely through workplace exposure. The consistent effect of university type across domains suggests that educational environments play a critical role in shaping gender-related perceptions [38]. Differences between public and private university graduates may reflect variability in institutional culture, curricular content, or exposure to gender equity and leadership models during training. This finding underscores the importance of integrating gender-sensitive education within pharmacy curricula, particularly in public institutions. Incorporating structured training on implicit bias, inclusive leadership, and gender equity within pharmacy education may help achieve more equitable professional attitudes early in career development.

Gendered perceptions of career advancement

Gendered perceptions related to career advancement showed significant associations with gender, type of university, work location, and living situation, indicating that both individual and contextual factors shape beliefs about women’s career progression. Men and graduates of public universities reported more gendered perceptions, reinforcing the consistency of these determinants across domains. The association with work location, particularly higher scores in Baalbek-Hermel and Akkar, points to regional sociocultural influences that may reinforce traditional gender roles and norms. These regional disparities may reflect differences in sociocultural conservatism, workforce participation patterns, educational opportunities for women, and prevailing expectations regarding women’s professional and family roles in these areas. In addition, regional variation in access to leadership opportunities and exposure to women in senior professional positions may further contribute to these perceptions. These findings suggest that workplace cultures and regional labor market structures may differentially affect perceptions of women’s advancement opportunities. Beyond individual and regional factors, the findings also point to structural dimensions that may sustain gendered perceptions in the workplace. The absence or limited enforcement of comprehensive legal frameworks ensuring gender equity in salaries, financial benefits, and access to leadership positions may contribute to the normalization of perceived barriers faced by women. Therefore, strengthening and enforcing policies that promote equal pay, transparent promotion criteria, and equitable access to leadership roles may represent a critical step toward addressing both gendered perceptions and actual disparities within the labor market. Living situation was also significantly associated with career advancement perceptions, with participants living alone or with friends reporting more gendered views than those living with family. This may reflect differences in social support systems, exposure to traditional family roles, or varying degrees of engagement with gender norms in daily life [39–41]. Although speculative, this finding highlights the need to consider social context beyond the workplace when examining gender-related attitudes.

Recognition of women leaders

Recognition of women leaders was the domain with the most extensive and multifaceted associations, highlighting leadership as a key area where gender inequities persist. Lower recognition was significantly associated with age, marital status, type of university, perceived economic status, work field, years of practice, and work location. Age-related differences indicate that older participants exhibit lower recognition of women leaders than younger participants, suggesting a generational shift in leadership norms [42, 43]. Similarly, participants with longer professional experience reported lower recognition, reinforcing the notion that prolonged exposure to traditional hierarchical structures may entrench leadership biases rather than mitigate them [44]. Marital status emerged as a significant determinant, with married and divorced/widowed participants demonstrating lower recognition compared with single participants. This may reflect more traditional gender role expectations for family responsibilities and leadership, particularly in sociocultural contexts where leadership remains implicitly gendered. Participants with higher perceived economic status also reported lower recognition of women leaders, suggesting alignment with established power structures that historically favor male leadership [45, 46]. This finding aligns with theories of social stratification [47], which hold that individuals who benefit from existing systems may be less inclined to challenge prevailing norms. Lower recognition among participants working in the pharmaceutical field, compared with those in non-pharmaceutical fields, suggests that industry-specific organizational structures may pose particular barriers to women’s leadership visibility. Hierarchical career pathways, limited female representation in senior roles, and gendered leadership prototypes within the pharmaceutical sector may contribute to this pattern [36, 43]. Regional differences further emphasize the influence of sociocultural context, with lower recognition observed in several regions outside Beirut and Mount Lebanon. These findings further support the need for educational and professional development initiatives that actively promote diverse leadership models and challenge persistent gender-related stereotypes within pharmacy and healthcare settings.

Implications

Findings demonstrate that gender bias in pharmacy is not uniform but varies systematically across social, educational, and professional dimensions. While explicit stereotypes regarding women’s capabilities appear to be diminishing, biases related to career advancement and leadership recognition remain deeply embedded and are reinforced by generational, institutional, and contextual factors. Addressing these disparities requires multilevel interventions that extend beyond individual attitude change. Educational reforms, organizational policies promoting transparent promotion and leadership pathways, mentorship and sponsorship programs, and regionally tailored gender equity initiatives are essential to ensure that increased female representation in pharmacy translates into equitable recognition and career advancement in leadership. Importantly, pharmacy curricula and continuing professional development programs should incorporate training on implicit bias, gender-equitable leadership, and inclusive workplace practices to better prepare future professionals for equitable and diverse healthcare environments.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths that enhance the robustness and relevance of its findings. First, it provides a comprehensive assessment of gender-related perceptions in the pharmaceutical workplace by examining multiple, complementary domains, including stereotypes of women’s capabilities, gendered perceptions of career advancement, and recognition of women leaders. Second, the inclusion of a relatively large and diverse sample of pharmacists across different regions, work settings, professional positions, and levels of experience strengthens the generalizability of the findings within the national context. Third, the use of structured, internally consistent composite scores enabled systematic comparisons across sociodemographic and professional subgroups, facilitating the identification of population groups in which gendered perceptions remain more pronounced.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and observed associations should therefore be interpreted as correlational rather than directional. Data were self-reported, which may introduce social desirability bias, particularly given the sensitivity of gender-related topics; this may have led to underreporting of stereotypical views. Additionally, the overrepresentation of women, although reflective of the pharmacy workforce, may have influenced the overall distribution of perceptions and limited gender-stratified comparisons. Some subgroups, including non-binary participants and pharmacists working outside the pharmaceutical field, were relatively small and heterogeneous. In particular, the “non-pharmaceutical field” category included participants from diverse professional backgrounds, potentially introducing variability within the subgroup. Consequently, the limited sample sizes may have reduced statistical power and compromised the robustness, interpretability, and generalizability of findings for these groups. Finally, although multiple sociodemographic and professional characteristics were examined, unmeasured factors such as organizational culture, leadership representation within workplaces, and prior exposure to gender equity training were not assessed and may have influenced perceptions. Future longitudinal and mixed-methods studies are recommended to better capture temporal changes in gendered attitudes and to explore the mechanisms underlying leadership recognition and career advancement in the pharmaceutical sector.

Conclusion

This study highlights generally positive attitudes toward women’s capabilities in the pharmaceutical workplace, with most respondents rejecting overt gender stereotypes. However, perceptions of women’s leadership and professional recognition remain mixed, indicating the persistence of more subtle forms of gender bias. Significant differences in perceptions were observed across sociodemographic and professional characteristics. Men and graduates of public universities consistently reported higher stereotype and career advancement scores, while lower recognition of women leaders was more common among older participants, those with longer professional experience, higher perceived economic status, and those working in the pharmaceutical field. Regional variations further suggest that workplace context and social environments influence gendered perceptions. Overall, while explicit stereotypes appear to be diminishing, implicit biases regarding career advancement and leadership recognition persist in the pharmaceutical sector. These findings underscore the need for targeted organizational strategies and gender-sensitive policies to promote equitable recognition and advancement of women in pharmacy practice.
