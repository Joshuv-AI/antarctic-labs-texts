In‐Solution Characterization of Extracellular Vesicles: A New Approach to Evaluating Antibody Binding and Surface Interactions
Authors: Maria‐Anthi Kakavoulia, Peter Spies, Tanja J Kutzner, Tobias Tertel, Sophie Franzmeier, Samir El Andaloussi, Bernd Giebel, Herbert Kaltner, Dominik Meinel, André Görgens, Anna‐Kristin Ludwig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExtracellular vesicles (EVs) are emerging as diagnostic and therapeutic agents, yet their nanoscale size limits quantitative single‐vesicle analysis of surface epitopes and molecular interactions. Flow‐Induced Dispersion Analysis (FIDA) is a microfluidic technique enabling real‐time measurement of hydrodynamic size and interaction kinetics using fluorescent ligands without requiring surface immobilization. Here, FIDA was applied to characterize EV–antibody interactions using anti‐CD63 antibodies across three EV sources: EVs from genetically engineered HEK cells expressing CD63‐eGFP or CD63‐NeonGreen‐Fc domain (CD63‐NG‐Fc), and EVs from clonally expanded immortalized mesenchymal stromal cells (ciMSCs). FIDA‐measured EV diameter sizes ranged from 40 to 90 nm, compared to 70–200 nm as determined by Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA), likely reflecting methodological differences or NTA's sensitivity to non‐EV particles. CD63‐binding EC50 values were 1.9 × 108 particles/mL for CD63‐eGFP EVs and 8.4 × 108 to 2.4 × 109 particles/mL for ciMSC EVs, indicating higher epitope abundance in engineered vesicles. Within a broader antibody titration, maximal antibody loading to the Fc‐receptor domain on EVs was observed at 0.5–5 nM, reaching 30%–40% for IgG1 and 15%–20% for Cetuximab. These results establish FIDA as a high‐resolution, label‐efficient tool for quantifying EV‐antibody interactions and epitope accessibility, supporting its integration into EV standardization and quality control workflows.

Introduction

Small extracellular vesicles (EVs) are nanoscale (< 200 nm), cell‐derived particles that carry molecular signatures reflective of their cells of origin. Due to their cell‐specific composition, they have emerged as a promising class of biomarkers and therapeutic agents. Beyond their diagnostic utility, a subset of EVs plays active roles in intercellular communication, mediating signal transduction pathways across diverse physiological and pathological contexts (Han et al. 2022; Kordelas et al. 2014; Ludwig and Giebel 2012; Wessler and Meisner‐Kober 2025).

Traditionally, the biological activity of EVs has been attributed to the delivery of luminal cargo such as proteins, RNAs, and lipids. However, accumulating evidence suggests that a significant portion of EV function is mediated through molecular platforms on their surfaces. These surface structures can directly engage target cells or recruit soluble biomolecules, including lipoproteins, proteoglycans, cytokines, galectins, and growth factors, leading to the formation of a surface‐bound biomolecular corona (Liam‐Or et al. 2024; Musico et al. 2024; Sonallya et al. 2025; Wolf et al. 2022). This corona may modulate vesicle function, stability, biodistribution, and immune recognition, thereby adding an additional layer of complexity to EV biology.

As the therapeutic and diagnostic applications of EVs expand, understanding their surface composition and interaction dynamics becomes increasingly critical. Dissecting the role of the EV corona in molecular trafficking and signal mediation requires analytical techniques capable of resolving EVs at the single‐particle level. A comprehensive analysis of EV composition, surface epitopes, and binding interactions is essential to harness their full functional properties (Bhagwan Valjee et al. 2024; Dayarathna et al. 2024; Hong et al. 2025; Kowalczyk et al. 2023; Yan et al. 2025).

However, the nanoscale size, heterogeneity, and low refractive index of EVs present major challenges for their characterization, particularly in complex biological fluids that contain similarly sized particles such as protein aggregates and lipoproteins. In 2011, we and others introduced nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) as a tool for EV quantification (Dragovic et al. 2011; Sokolova et al. 2011). Yet, it soon became evident that traditional NTA and similar methods, such as dynamic light scattering (DLS), are insufficient for resolving EV subpopulations or monitoring molecular interactions. These conventional techniques rely on bulk measurements based on Brownian motion and light scattering, and while suitable for homogeneous samples, their sensitivity is limited in heterogeneous and impure matrices (Kim et al. 2019; Mork et al. 2017; Stetefeld et al. 2016; Vogel et al. 2021).

To improve specificity, newer approaches like fluorescent NTA and nanoflow cytometry have been developed to detect EV‐associated antigens through fluorescence labeling. Among these, imaging flow cytometry (IFCM) has shown strong utility in profiling EVs within minimally processed biological fluids. IFCM combines the throughput of flow cytometry with high‐resolution imaging, enabling surface marker‐based subpopulation analysis of particles smaller than 200 nm (Görgens et al. 2019; Tertel et al. 2020; Tertel et al. 2020). Despite these advances, IFCM does not provide quantitative kinetic data, leaving a critical gap in our understanding of EV interaction dynamics.

This gap is partly addressed by label‐free biophysical methods such as surface plasmon resonance (SPR) (Lopez Baltazar et al. 2024; Sharar et al. 2023), quartz crystal microbalance with dissipation monitoring (QCM‐D) (Kowalczyk et al. 2023; Suthar et al. 2020), and biolayer interferometry (BLI) (Calderin et al. 2025; Hug et al. 2023; Shah and Duncan 2014). These techniques enable the real‐time quantification of molecular interactions by measuring association rates (kon), dissociation rates (koff), and equilibrium binding affinities (Kd) with high sensitivity, often reaching the nanomolar or femtomolar range. In these systems, EV interactions are monitored by detecting changes in refractive index (SPR), energy dissipation (QCM‐D), or optical interference (BLI) following EV binding to immobilized ligands.

However, a fundamental limitation of these platforms is their reliance on surface immobilization of one binding partner, commonly achieved using chemical linkers or protein A/G (Kowalczyk et al. 2023). Immobilization may obscure native epitopes, restrict molecular flexibility, or alter the orientation and accessibility of vesicle surfaces. These issues are particularly challenging when studying nanoscale vesicles like EVs, whose functional interactions may be highly sensitive to surface presentation and steric effects.

In this regard, diffusion‐based microfluidic methods have recently been developed to enable rapid, in‐solution characterization of EVs. Notably, Paganini et al. (Paganini et al. 2022) introduced a fluorescence‐based microfluidic diffusion sizing (fluoMDS) technique that integrates particle separation, sizing, and immunolabeling within a single microfluidic platform. This approach combines diffusion sizing with multi‐wavelength fluorescence detection to quantify EV size, concentration, and marker abundance directly in‐ solution without immobilization or washing steps. It resolves different particle sizes according to their diffusion over time at twelve points on a microfluidic chip. Such developments underscore the growing potential of diffusion‐based approaches for EV characterization under near‐native conditions and establish an important foundation for subsequent in‐solution analytical strategies.

However, there remains a growing demand for analytical techniques that, beyond high‐resolution single‐particle characterization, can also provide quantitative information on surface epitope abundance and molecular interaction kinetics without the need for immobilization. In this context, Flow‐Induced Dispersion Analysis (FIDA) has emerged as a powerful and non‐invasive alternative, enabling solution‐based, real‐time measurements under near‐native conditions.

FIDA is based on Taylor dispersion, a technique that estimates the diffusion coefficients of molecules in laminar flow. In this method, a fluorescently labeled (or unlabeled) indicator molecule is co‐injected with the analyte, such as EVs, into a narrow capillary. As the mixture traverses the capillary under defined flow conditions, differences in diffusion coefficients between free and bound indicator forms generate distinct dispersion profiles. The resulting Gaussian signals allow calculation of the hydrodynamic radius (Rh) using the Stokes–Einstein equation (Figure 1) (Jensen and Ostergaard 2010; Pedersen et al. 2019; Poulsen et al. 2015). Shifts in Rh upon complex formation reflect molecular binding events and can be used to derive binding affinities, stoichiometry, and complex stability in real time (Hug et al. 2023; Pedersen et al. 2019; Restan et al. 2019; Seidita et al. 2024).

Crucially, FIDA enables interaction analyses presumably without prior EV purification or surface capture, thus preserving the native biological context of EV–ligand interactions. One of FIDA's most significant advantages is its compatibility with complex biological matrices, including plasma, serum, and conditioned media, without requiring extensive sample preparation (Hug et al. 2023; Pedersen et al. 2022; Seidita et al. 2024). This capability allows EV–protein interactions to be investigated under physiologically relevant conditions.

To evaluate the potential of FIDA for the EV field, we explored EVs and EV antibody interactions at the example of three distinct EV preparations: Genetically labeled EVs from HEK293 cells expressing CD63‐eGFP, EVs derived from conditioned media of clonally expanded, immortalized mesenchymal stromal cells (ciMSCs) cultured in human platelet lysate–supplemented media and genetically engineered EVs from HEK293FS cells expressing CD63–mNeonGreen fused to an Fc‐binding domain (Wiklander et al. 2024; Zickler et al. 2024).

We compared EV size distributions obtained by FIDA and NTA and examined antibody binding to Fc‐tagged EVs using human IgG1 isotype control and Cetuximab, a monoclonal anti‐EGFR antibody (Hirsch et al. 2022; Kawaguchi et al. 2007; Kawaguchi et al. 2007; Liu et al. 2016; Zhou et al. 2025). By quantifying Rh shifts, binding parameters, FIDA offers an in‐solution platform for evaluating both Fab‐antigen and Fc‐receptor interactions on EVs.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

Mesenchymal stromal cells from bone marrow aspirated from two donors were raised and underwent immortalization and single‐cell expansion to generate clonally immortalized MSC (ciMSC) lines (Labusek et al. 2023). Frozen cell stocks aliquoted from cell lines 41.5 clone 6 and 16.3 clone 5 (referred as 41.5 and 16.3 in the following text, respectively), were cultured in DMEM low glucose (Sigma‐Aldrich, Taufkirchen, Germany) supplemented with 10% human platelet lysate (home‐made and calcium‐depleted (Mouloud et al. 2024)), 5000 U heparin, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin/glutamine (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany).

EV Isolation

The ciMSC lines (16.3 and 41.5) were cultured in low‐glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% platelet lysate until 80% confluence. For EV production, the medium was then exchanged for low‐glucose DMEM without additives. After an additional culture period of 48 hrs, conditioned medium (CM) was harvested from confluent cells, centrifuged at 900 × g for 5 min to remove cells, followed by 2,000 × g centrifugation for 15 min to remove cellular debris. The CM was either processed immediately after harvesting, or stored at 4°C for a maximum of one week. For EV isolation, 150 mL of CM was concentrated to 5 mL using an Amicon 100 kDa filter (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany), filtered through a 0.2 µm filter (TPP, München, Germany), and centrifuged at 100,000 × g using an M50 rotor in an Optimax tabletop ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter, Krefeld, Germany) at 4°C for 2 hrs and 10 min. After centrifugation, the pellet was resuspended in 10 mM HEPES/0.9% NaCl, aliquoted and stored at −80°C. EVs from CM of HEK293 Free style cells were isolated as described previously (Wiklander et al. 2024). In brief EVs were enriched from the conditioned culture medium of HEK293FS:CD63‐mNG (mNG‐EVs) and HEK293FS:CD63‐Fc‐mNG (mNG‐Fc EVs) cells, respectively, using a standardized purification protocol involving Tangential Flow Filtration (TFF), 0.22 µm filtration, and 10 kDa ultrafiltration.

EV Sources

EVs were obtained from three distinct cell sources: engineered HEK293 cells (HansaBioMed, Riga, Estonia), from the two clonal immortalized mesenchymal stromal cell lines (ciMSCs; 16.3 and 41.5) and engineered HEK293 Free Style cells (Wiklander et al. 2024). Commercially available FLuoEVs (FLuoCD63‐eGFP, Lot: R‐280922; HansaBioMed, Riga, Estonia) and HBM‐HEK EVs (HBM‐HEK293‐30/2; HansaBioMed, Riga, Estonia) were used as control EVs. According to the manufacturer's protocol, the lyophilized EVs were reconstituted in 20 mM phosphate buffered saline containing 150 mM NaCl before analysis. Percentage and size of reconstituted EVs were determined by FIDA and NTA using Zetaview instrument (Particle Metrix, Germany) and analysis in the scatter and fluorescence modes. EVs derived from ciMSCs lines, 16.3 and 41.5, are referred to as 16.3 EVs or 41.5 EVs. The isolation procedure was performed as described in the EV isolation section. Fc‐domain engineered EVs were generated from HEK293FS cells expressing CD63‐mNeonGreen (mNG) or CD63‐mNeonGreen‐Fc constructs. The control CD63‐mNG fluorescent EVs, available as Vesi‐Ref CD63‐mNG (Vesiculab Ltd, UK), and Fc‐binding CD63‐mNG‐Fc EVs, available as Vesi‐Bind Fc‐mNG (Vesiculab Ltd, UK), were previously described (Wiklander et al. 2024).

EVs Characterization

EV Size and Concentration Determination by NTA

HBM FLuoEVs were diluted 1,000‐fold in particle free PBS and analyzed on a ZetaView instrument for NTA measurements in fluorescence to estimate the size and percentage of eGFP positive particles. Particle detection was performed at 11 positions, with measurements taken for 30 s in scatter 488 mode and fluorescence 500 mode to calculate the proportion of eGFP‐positive particles (Figure S7). The particles present in the obtained ciMSC EV samples were measured by scatter mode of NTA, using the Nanosight 100 (Malvern, UK) equipped with a (CCD) camera, a 488 nm laser (Malvern, UK), and NTA software version 3.0 for data acquisition and processing. Samples were diluted 50‐fold, 100‐fold, and 500‐fold in particle‐free 10 mM Hepes/0.9% NaCl supplemented with 0.2% BSA and 0.05% Tween 20 to maintain the number of particles in the field of view between approximately 70 and 100 particles. For each sample, we recorded four videos of 30 sec duration. The histograms show the average of all the represented data (Figure S1A). The size and concentration of CD63‐mNG EVs and CD63‐mNG‐Fc EVs were determined by scatter mode of NTA using a Nanosight 500 instrument (Malvern, UK) (CD63‐mNG EVs: 9.8 × 109 p/mL, 138.6 ± 8.9 nm; CD63‐mNG‐Fc EVs: 2.9 × 1010 p/mL, 128.7 ± 3.5 nm) as described before (Wiklander et al. 2024).

Transmission Electron Microscopy

For ciMSC‐EV visualization, 4 µL of each EV sample (1:10 diluted with 10 mM Hepes/0.9% NaCl) were incubated for 2–5 min on carbon‐coated 400‐mesh copper grids (Plano, Wetzlar, Germany). Residual liquid was removed using filter paper; this step was repeated between all subsequent steps. The grids were then washed in double‐distilled water, contrasted with 1% uranyl acetate (SPI Supplies, West Chester, USA) for 1 min, air‐dried, and stored in a desiccator until imaging with an EM10 transmission electron microscope (Zeiss, Germany) (Figure S1B).

Western Blotting

The protein concentration of EV samples was determined using the Bradford assay (Biorad, München, Germany). 25 µg of protein was solubilized in 5 x Laemmli sample buffer and separated via SDS‐PAGE before electroblotted to a nitrocellulose membrane (Schleicher and Schuell, Dassel, Germany). The membranes were blocked 1 h at RT (or 4°C overnight) in PBS‐0.1% Tween‐20 (PBS‐T) containing 5% (w/v) skim milk powder (Sigma‐Aldrich) to saturate sites for nonspecific protein binding. Residual blocking solution was removed by washing the membranes three times in PBS‐T for 10 min each.

For protein detection, the following primary antibodies were used (clone, dilution, incubation conditions, supplier, and catalog number): mouse anti‐human CD63 (TSG3, 1:1,000, 4°C overnight, Invitrogen, Cat. No.10628D, Thermo, Fisher, Dreieich, Germany), and mouse anti‐Syntenin (OTI2H6, 1:1,000, 4°C overnight, Invitrogen, Cat. No. MA5‐25984, Thermo, Fisher, Dreieich, Germany). After primary antibody incubation, membranes were washed three times in PBS‐T 10 min each.

For secondary antibody detection, we used Peroxidase‐AffiniPure F(ab')2 Fragment Donkey Anti‐Mouse IgG (polyclonal, 1:10,000, 1 h at RT, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, USA, Cat. No. 715‐036‐150) and visualized bound proteins with SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (Thermo Fisher, Cat No. 34095, Dreieich, Germany). Sample buffer conditions: non‐reducing for CD63 detection; reducing (denatured with β‐mercaptoethanol) for Syntenin detection (Figure S1C). Chemiluminescence was detected and analyzed with a ChemiDoc system and Image Lab Software (Biorad, Munich, Germany)

Immunoprecipitation and Microscopic Visualization of EVs

MSC‐derived EVs were stained with 5 µg/mL anti‐CD63 PE (H5C6, BioLegend, 353003) and incubated on ice in the dark for 30 min. A control sample containing PBS and the same antibody concentration was prepared in parallel. Following incubation, samples were diluted 1:10 in 10 mM HEPES/0.9% NaCl and centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C. The pellet was resuspended in 20 µL of 10 mM HEPES/0.9% NaCl. For imaging, 5 µL of each sample (EVs and control) was placed onto a microscopy slide. Samples were observed using brightfield and 488 nm fluorescence settings with 20 × objective on a Leica DMI6000B fluorescence microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), and representative images were acquired (Figure S1D).

Flow Cytometric Analysis of EVs

For flow cytometric analysis of EVs a standard curve was generated using serial dilutions of EVs stained with 200 ng/mL mouse anti‐human CD63 FITC (H5C6, BioLegend, Cat.No. 353005, Amsterdam, Netherlands) to determine the optimal EV concentration for single‐EV analysis (Figure S1E). EVs were then diluted 1:100 in 50 mM NaCl and stained with 50 ng/mL mouse anti‐human CD63 PE (clone H5C6, BioLegend, Cat.No 353003, Amsterdam, Netherlands) for 2 hrs at 4°C in the dark. Two washing steps were performed with Milli‐Q water (Sartorius) for 1 min each, followed by a final washing step using a low flow rate of 5 µL/min to prevent cross‐contamination between samples. Controls included 50 mM NaCl, NaCl with antibodies, unstained EVs, and 1% NP40 detergent to confirm that the detected signal originated from EVs rather than debris (Figure S4). The prepared samples were analyzed using an Agilent NovoCyte Quanteon flow cytometer (Agilent, Waldbronn, Germany) with four lasers (Figure S1F).

Flow Cytometric Analysis of EVs Using Amnis ImageStreamX (IFCM) and Amnis CellStream (nFCM)

Flow cytometric analyses of EVs were performed using both the Amnis ImageStreamX Mk II (IFCM) and the Amnis CellStream nano flow cytometer (nFCM). For IFCM measurements, ciMSC‐EVs and HansaBiomed HEK293 reference EVs (5 µg per sample) were stained as previously described (Tertel et al. 2020). EVs were incubated for 1 h at room temperature (RT) in the dark with APC‐conjugated anti‐human CD63 (MEM‐259, EXBIO Praha, Czech Republic), PE‐conjugated anti‐human CD9 (MEM‐61, EXBIO Praha, Czech Republic), or FITC‐conjugated anti‐human CD81 (JS‐84, Beckman Coulter, Krefeld, Germany). Mouse IgG1 isotype controls and buffer‐only controls were included. After staining, samples were adjusted to a final volume of 100 µL without further post‐staining procedures, resulting in a concentration of 5 µg EVs per 100 µL. Antibodies and control reagents are summarized in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

Samples were acquired on the ImageStreamX Mk II (Cytek Bioscience, Amsterdam, Netherlands) using 60× magnification, low flow rate, and with bead‐removal deactivated. Measurements were recorded for 5 min per well in U‐bottom 96‐well plates (Corning GmbH, Berlin, Germany). Laser configurations and detection channels are listed in Supplementary Table S3. Data were processed using IDEAS 6.2 (Cytek Bioscience, Amsterdam, Netherlands), where fluorescence intensities were plotted against side scatter. Multiplets were identified and excluded using the spot‐counting feature (Tertel et al. 2020). Calibration curves for FITC, PE, and APC MESF values are shown in Figure S2.

For the nFCM measurements, HEK293FS‐derived CD63‐mNeonGreen EVs (mNG‐EVs) and CD63‐mNG‐Fc EVs were diluted in PBS‐HAT (DBPS supplemented with 25 µM HEPES, 0.2% human albumin, and 25 µM trehalose) (Görgens et al. 2022) to a final concentration of 1 × 1010 particles/mL (p/mL). A total of 25 µL (2.5 × 108 particles) was either left unstained or incubated overnight with anti‐human CD63‐APC antibodies (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) or human IgG1 isotype control‐APC (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) at a final antibody concentration of 4 nM. Following incubation, samples were diluted 1:10,000 in PBS‐HAT and acquired on the CellStream instrument with FSC disabled, SSC laser power set to 40%, and all other lasers at 100% power. Small EVs (sEVs) were defined as SSC‐low using CD63‐mNeonGreen‐tagged EVs as biological reference material as previously described (Görgens et al. 2019), and fluorescence‐positive gates were set using unstained controls (Figure S3A). Acquisition was performed for 3 min at a flow rate of 3.66 µL/min using CellStream Software v1.2.3, and data were analyzed with FlowJo v10.5.3. Dulbecco's PBS (pH 7.4; Gibco, Thermo Fisher, Germany) served as sheath fluid.

Fluorescence calibration for nFCM analysis was carried out following established procedures (Tertel et al. 2020, Görgens et al. 2019). FITC MESF beads (Quantum FITC‐5 MESF, Bangs Laboratories) and APC MESF beads (Quantum APC MESF, Bangs Laboratories) were recorded using the same settings as EV samples (SSC laser off). Linear regression analyses were used to convert fluorescence intensities into MESF values (Figure S3B), and MESF‐scaled plots were generated using FlowJo v10.5.3. EV size and concentration were additionally assessed using Zetaview X20 (Particle Metrix, Germany) and NanoSight instruments (Figure S3C).

Flow Induced Dispersion Analyses of EVs

FIDA Instrument

FIDA experiments were conducted using a Fida 1 instrument (Fida Biosystems ApS, Søborg, Denmark) equipped with a UV detector and a LED‐induced fluorescence detector, utilizing excitation wavelengths of 480 nm and 640 nm. Standard capillaries from Fida Biosystems with an inner diameter of 75 µm, an outer diameter of 375 µm, a total length of 100 cm and an 84 cm length to the detection window were used for all experiments.

Preparation of Capillaries

Before use, the capillaries were coated with a high‐sensitivity (HS) coating reagent (Fida Biosystems) to minimize nonspecific binding of indicator (antibodies) and analyte (EVs). Samples (indicator, EVs or mixtures of both) were stored in microtiter plates or vials with plastic inserts to prevent unspecific binding to surfaces. The assay buffer (10 mM PBS, pH 7.4) was used for flushing the HS‐coated capillary at 3500 mbar for 120 sec after the previous run. The capillary was then equilibrated by injecting the assay buffer for 30 sec at 3500 mbar.

Quality Control and Data Evaluation in FIDA Measurements

Each FIDA measurement generates a fluorescence intensity–time profile (Taylorgram) that is analyzed to determine diffusion parameters and Rh values. To ensure data reliability and reproducibility, all measurements were subjected to defined quality control criteria based on parameters automatically reported by the FIDA analysis software: signal‐to‐noise ratio (S/N), fit correlation coefficient (R2), relative fluorescence units (RFU), and peak area.

The S/N ratio serves as the primary indicator of measurement quality. It is defined as the ratio between the fluorescence peak amplitude of the indicator (signal) and the baseline noise level of the buffer prior to injection (noise). For valid Rh determination and accurate diffusion fitting, only measurements with an S/N ≥ 30 were accepted for quantitative analysis. Measurements with S/N values below 20 were excluded, as they typically reflect weak signal intensity or unstable flow conditions, leading to unreliable Rh calculations. The effective S/N threshold between 20 and 30 is also influenced by the analysis algorithm, which by default considers approximately 75% of the peak area and excludes the extreme tails of the Taylorgram to minimize artifacts from tailing.

The fit R2 quantifies the agreement between the experimental Taylorgram and the theoretical dispersion model obtained by non‐linear least‐squares fitting. Only fits with R2 ≥ 0.98 were accepted, ensuring that the calculated diffusion parameters accurately represent the experimental data.

The RFU corresponds to the integrated fluorescence intensity of the dispersion peak and reflects the fluorophore concentration within the detection window. Monitoring RFU values across replicate injections or titration series allowed assessment of sample stability and detection of potential photobleaching or aggregation.

The peak area represents the total integrated fluorescence signal of the dispersion profile and was used as an internal control for injection precision and flow stability between replicates.

The FIDA analysis software allows identification of up to three diffusion species per measurement, each characterized by its individual Rh and relative contribution to the overall signal. In this study, we refer to “optimal Taylorgram fitting” as the non‐linear least‐squares fitting process fulfilling all aforementioned quality criteria (S/N, R2, RFU, and peak area consistency).

A representative screenshot of the analysis software displaying these parameters is provided in Supplementary Figure S5.

Interaction Measurements of Antibodies and EVs

The following stepwise procedure was applied for the FIDA experiments, with an overall analysis time of 12 min per sample. Before starting, assay buffer (10 mM PBS, pH 7.4) was used to flush the HS‐coated capillary at 3500 mbar for 120 sec after the previous run. The capillary was then equilibrated by injecting the assay buffer for 30 sec at 3500 mbar. EV samples (FLuoEVs, ciMSC‐EVs, Fc‐EVs, and Control EVs) were diluted in assay buffer to the respective concentration based on the particle concentration determined by NTA and/or IFCM followed by incubation with the respective antibodies: mouse anti‐human CD63 AF647 (H5C6, BD Biosciences, Cat. No. 561983, Herlev, Denmark), REA human IgG1 isotype control‐APC (REA293, Miltenyi Biotech, Cat. No. 130‐113‐434, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) and Cetuximab (ERBITUX, 500 mg/100 mL Cetuximab, Cat. # 06185153) manually labeled with ALC647. The premixed sample was injected at a pressure of 50 mbar for 10 sec, corresponding to 1% of the capillary volume. Mobilization and measurement of the labeled complex were performed at 100 mbar for 720 sec and the resulting Taylor diagram was recorded. All samples were preincubated for 2 hrs at 37°C before the FIDA measurements and were measured in triplicate on plastic inserts to prevent unspecific binding to surfaces. Samples were stored at 4°C, while measurements were carried out at 25°C. The resulting Taylorgrams were analyzed using Fidabio software Version 3.1 with two‐species fits, where the Rh of the second species was fixed to the size of the labeled binder. The analysis fraction was set to 75% for all data points.

Binding Sites Calculation

Avogadro's number (6.022 × 1023 molecules/mol) was used to convert the antibody concentration into the corresponding number of antibody molecules per mL. The number of antibody molecules that were bound to EVs was calculated based on the experimentally determined percentage of bound antibodies. This value was then divided by the EV concentration (p/mL), as measured by NTA or IFCM, to estimate the average number of antibody binding sites per extracellular vesicle.

Validation of Indicator Antibodies for FIDA

To reliably characterize antibody–EV interactions by FIDA, we first validated all fluorescent indicator antibodies with respect to size, purity, and signal quality. In FIDA, a fluorescent indicator is introduced as a narrow plug into a dynamically coated capillary, where it travels under laminar flow and undergoes Taylor‐Aris dispersion. Under these conditions, axial diffusion and the parabolic flow profile jointly determine broadening of the indicator signal, which is directly related to its diffusion coefficient and, via the Stokes‐Einstein relationship, to the Rh. Binding of the fluorescent indicator to an unlabeled analyte in this case, EVs results in a complex with a larger Rh and a corresponding shift in the dispersion profile (Figure 1).

All fluorescent antibodies used in this study (anti‐human CD63‐AF647, REA human IgG1 isotype control‐APC, and Cetuximab‐ALC647) were therefore analyzed by FIDA in serial dilutions using the 640 nm detector. For each antibody, we defined the usable concentration range as that yielding stable Rh values and a S/N ratio ≥ 30, thereby ensuring robust and reproducible measurements and excluding interference from free fluorophore.

Anti‐CD63‐AF647 was measured at concentrations from 0.83 to 6.67 nM (Figure 2A). Across this range, S/N ratios were ≥ 30 (Table 1; Table S4C), and the antibody showed a highly consistent Rh between 5.33 and 5.40 nm (Table 1A; Table S4C). The absence of concentration‐dependent Rh changes indicates no detectable dimerization, higher‐order aggregation or smaller free fluorophore molecules present in the antibody sample. These findings indicate that only one species is present in the 640 nm detector, excluding a measurable contribution from free fluorophore in the indicator antibody. The anti‐CD63‐AF647 preparation thus consisted of a single, monomeric species suitable for EV‐binding analyses.

The IgG1‐APC isotype control antibody, used for Fc‐loading studies on engineered EVs, was tested in a serial dilution from 0.1 to 10 nM. A constant Rh of 7.58 ± 0.17 nm and S/N ≥ 30 were observed between 0.5 and 10 nM (Figure 2B; Table 1B; Table S11B), and only a single Rh species was detected. These data indicate a monomeric, dye‐free preparation of comparable quality to anti‐CD63‐AF647, confirming its suitability as an indicator antibody in EV loading experiments.

Cetuximab‐ALC647, from which free dye had been removed after labeling, was measured in a concentration range from 1 to 40 nM. At the lowest concentrations (1–2 nM), S/N values were near the validity threshold of 30, but above this range they were consistently acceptable (Figure 2C; Table 1C; Table S11B). The main labeled antibody population exhibited a constant Rh of 4.74 ± 0.12 nm across all concentrations, indicating a monomeric state. A second, smaller species with Rh 0.60 ± 0.14 nm was detected and attributed to residual free dye. A control experiment using free fluorophore was performed to confirm Rh assignment (Figure S8). The proportion of free dye was 8.33% ± 5.44% (Table S11C), meaning that > 90% of the indicator signal originated from labeled antibody. This low level of free fluorophore was considered acceptable and unlikely to interfere with EV‐binding measurements.

Together, these validation experiments confirmed that all three indicator antibodies were of sufficient purity, monomeric, and provided stable Rh and high S/N, qualifying them for subsequent FIDA‐based EV‐antibody binding studies.

Quality Measurements of HBM‐HEK EVs and FIDA Instrument Setup

To validate FIDA as a technique for EV quality assessment, we first performed flow‐cytometric characterization of the vesicle preparations. Flow cytometry was conducted using non‐fluorescent reference HBM‐HEK EVs stained for tetraspanin markers and compared to freshly prepared, non‐lyophilized ciMSC‐derived EVs (Figure 3A). A substantial reduction in tetraspanin‐positive fractions was observed in the reference HBM‐HEK EVs, indicating compromised vesicle integrity. These results were consistent with later FIDA‐based observations of EV aggregation and supported the use of FIDA as a complementary tool for detecting structural deterioration in EV samples.

Following this characterization, FIDA instrument settings and quality measurements were established using HBM‐HEK‐EGFP63 EVs (FLuoEVs), a pre‐characterized, commercially available EV source. Initial FIDA analyses employed a dynamic coated capillary, a 488 nm laser, and 20 mM PBS (pH 7.4), operated at 50 and 100 mbar, to evaluate the effect of flow velocity on EV dispersion (Figure 3B,C). Lower pressure (50 mbar) resulted in a higher S/N ratio and increased RFU in the Taylorgram, improving sensitivity and resolution for detecting single EV events. Taylorgrams displayed multiple signal spikes preceding the dispersion peak, indicative of EV aggregates (arrows, Figure 3B,C). No distinct particle subpopulations were detected, suggesting the absence of a heterogeneous size distribution but highlighting aggregation likely promoted by storage conditions.

FIDA‐Based Size Analysis of Fluorescent FLuoEVs

To assess the utility of FIDA for EV size estimation, FLuoEVs were analyzed at 50 and 100 mbar. Rh values were consistent across pressures: 45.06 ± 0.75 nm at 50 mbar and 45.41 ± 0.23 nm at 100 mbar (Table S4A). The proportion of eGFP‐labeled particles in the FIDA analysis refers to the fraction of the total fluorescence signal contributed by the eGFP‐positive EV‐sized population, derived from the area under the fluorescence intensity distribution curve rather than from peak height. The proportion of eGFP‐labeled particles decreased with increased pressure 59.9% ± 1.5% at 50 mbar vs. 31.5% ± 0.2% at 100 mbar (Table S4B). To provide independent verification, we additionally characterized the FLuoEVs by NTA in the scatter and fluorescence mode showing size distributions and eGFP‐positive fractions consistent with FIDA results (Figure S7). Analysis of the FLuoEVs by NTA showed an amount of eGFP positive particles of 70.2% and a median size of 151.5 ± 88.6 nm in the fluorescence mode. The most detected particle size in the fluorescence mode of NTA was 92.3 nm and correlates with the measured Rh in FIDA. This confirms the reliability of FIDA‐based size estimation for fluorescently labeled EVs.

Estimation of the Optimal FLuoEV Concentration for FIDA Binding Measurements

To determine the optimal EV concentration for antibody‐binding assays, a serial dilution of FLuoEVs was analyzed against a fixed antibody concentration of 3 nM (Figure S4). Across the dilution series, RFU and S/N ratio increased proportionally. As shown before in the indicator titration even at the lowest antibody concentration of 0.83 nM (CD63‐AF647), S/N ratios remained above the validity threshold (>30), ensuring reliable detection (Figure 2A and Table S4C). EV concentrations between 1.13 × 108 and 9.0 × 108 p/mL showed favorable S/N ratios, and EC50 for FLuoEVs was estimated 1.5 × 108 p/mL. It describes the EV concentration by that 50% of the accessible binding sites on EVs are occupied by antibodies but it does not provide and include any assumption about affinity, avidity or multivalency of used indicator antibodies. In the Taylorgrams of the different EV concentrations (Figure S4) up to a concentration of 4.5 × 108 p/mL irregularities/shoulders were visible which indicate not optimal fitting conditions. Optimal Taylor fitting is referred to non‐linear least‐squares fitting of the experimental Taylor dispersion curve to the theoretical model, yielding the diffusion coefficient and Rh that best describe the observed dispersion profile (minimal residuals, optimal S/N). Therefore, a concentration of ∼ 4.5 × 108 p/mL could be selected as optimal FLuoEV concentration for subsequent antibody‐binding experiments based on optimal Taylorgram fitting.

Characterization of mNG Control and mNG‐Fc Binding EVs by FIDA

To validate FIDA measurements as a rapid method for estimating the loading efficiency of engineered EVs with antibodies for therapeutic applications, we employed HEK293‐derived mNG‐Fc EVs, which express a monovalent Fc‐receptor on their surface, and mNG control EVs lacking the Fc‐binding domain. The mNG‐Fc EVs provide a defined model system to assess Fc‐mediated IgG1 antibody binding, enabling controlled decoration of EVs for targeted cell delivery.

FIDA analysis of CD63‐mNG control EVs and CD63‐mNG‐Fc EVs at 50 and 100 mbar revealed two main particle populations: sEVs and larger vesicles or aggregates. At 100 mbar, CD63‐mNG control EVs showed Rh values of 71.23 ± 1.53 nm (large) and 18.35 ± 1.81 nm (small), comprising ∼70% large and ∼26% small particles (Figure 6D, Table S10A). CD63‐mNG‐Fc EVs displayed comparable Rh values for large (65.81 ± 2.95 nm) and small (22.10 ± 1.08 nm) particles, indicating a similar distribution of particle sizes (Figure 6B, Table S10B).

Characterization of CD63‐mNG‐Fc EVs and CD63‐mNG control EVs by FIDA. A) Taylorgram of Fc EVs at 50 mbar at 1200 sec. B) Taylorgram of Fc EVs at 100 mbar at 900 sec (n = 3). C) Taylorgram of CD63‐mNG control EVs at 50 mbar at 1200 sec. D) Taylorgram of CD63‐mNG‐Fc EVs at 100 mbar at 900 sec.

Reducing the pressure to 50 mbar enhanced peak separation and improved resolution of the two EV populations, reflected by increased Rh values for both species. For CD63‐mNG‐Fc EVs, Rh increased to 110 ± 11.75 nm (large) and 38.21 ± 1.24 nm (small). CD63‐mNG control EVs exhibited Rh values of 202 ± 129.86 nm (large) and 41.80 ± 1.00 nm (small), and the proportion of detected species shifted markedly to ∼13% large and 82% small particles (Figure 6A,C; Table S10A,B).

These results demonstrate that flow pressure strongly influences the resolution and apparent distribution of heterogeneous EV populations. Optimizing pressure settings is therefore essential to avoid misassigning particles between populations and to accurately characterize EV size heterogeneity.

To detect the binding of human IgG1 antibodies (IgG1‐APC and Cetuximab) to Fc‐receptor domains on mNG‐Fc EVs, a flow pressure of 50 and 100 mbar and the 640 nm laser were used. Taken together, higher antibody concentrations of 50–100 nM led to a drop‐down of Rh back to the size of unbound antibodies. Thereby, under the same experimental settings (e.g., pressure) the increasing free antibody starts to interfere with the measurements by decreasing the signal of the EV‐antibody complex. This demonstrated the sensitivity and limitation of the system to high indicator concentrations, indicating interference with the measurement of size and binding due to an excess of free antibodies.

Discussion

This study validated FIDA as a versatile, solution‐phase method for characterizing EVs and quantifying EV‐antibody interactions. FIDA reliably measured critical EV parameters, including hydrodynamic size (Rh), population homogeneity, and fluorescent labeling efficiency across diverse EV preparations.

FIDA‐derived size range (40–90 nm) were slightly lower than those obtained by NTA (100–128 nm) and TEM (∼100 nm). These differences reflect the distinct detection principles and particle populations probed by each technique. TEM directly visualizes dehydrated and fixed vesicles and therefore reports the physical diameter of individual particles, although partial collapse or shrinkage during preparation may occur (Chuo et al. 2018; Paganini et al. 2022; Théry et al. 2018; van der Pol et al. 2014; Yuana et al. 2013). In contrast, NTA in light‐scattering mode detects all scattering particles present in the sample including EVs, lipoproteins, protein aggregates, and other non‐vesicular components resulting in a broader and often larger apparent size distribution (Sokolova et al. 2011; van der Pol et al. 2014). In addition, scattering‐based NTA shows reduced sensitivity for low–refractive index biological nanoparticles below approximately 30–50 nm, limiting accurate detection of sEVs and proteins (Filipe et al. 2010; Kowkabany and Bao 2024; Sitar et al. 2015; Ter‐Ovanesyan et al. 2023; Tian et al. 2020).

FIDA performed in UV detection mode on unlabeled ciMSC‐EVs captures the diffusion behavior of all absorbing or scattering species in‐ solution, including soluble proteins, which leads to the detection of molecules with smaller apparent hydrodynamic radii (Rh) than those obtained by NTA. When both FIDA and NTA are performed in fluorescence mode as for the FLuoEVs and mNG‐Fc EVs detection is restricted to fluorescently labeled vesicles, and the resulting Rh values align more closely with the expected EV size range.

Since NTA's apparent size distribution is strongly influenced by detection mode, a clear distinction must be drawn between scattering‐NTA and fluorescence‐NTA. Scattering mode reports the total nanoparticle content of the sample, whereas fluorescence‐NTA selectively detects labeled EVs and excludes non‐vesicular particles, yielding a more specific size readout (van der Pol et al. 2014, Ter‐Ovanesyan et al. 2023, Sitar et al. 2015, Kowkabany and Bao 2024, Tian et al. 2020, Kashkanova et al. 2023). In this study, scattering‐NTA was applied to unlabeled ciMSC‐EVs to benchmark total particle concentration, whereas fluorescence‐NTA was used for mNG‐Fc EVs, resulting in size distributions that corresponded well with FIDA‐derived hydrodynamic radii. These findings underscore the importance of aligning detection mode with labeling strategy and the biological question when comparing diffusion‐based and scattering‐based methods.

Rh measurements were reproducible across a range of flow pressures (50–150 mbar). An exception was observed for CD63–mNG–Fc EVs at 100 mbar, where elevated pressure altered Taylorgram profiles, an effect reminiscent of swarm detection in high‐flow cytometry (Görgens et al. 2019; Tertel et al. 2020; Tertel et al. 2020). These findings underscore the need to optimize EV concentration and flow parameters, particularly for unlabeled, engineered, or polydisperse EV populations.

FIDA also detected dose‐dependent Rh shifts upon titration of anti‐CD63 antibodies to ciMSC EVs, with a ∼5 nm to ∼30 nm increase indicating stable complex formation and saturation. In contrast, CD63–mNG–Fc EVs showed a decrease in Rh with increasing antibody concentrations, likely reflecting consistent Fc–antibody binding and subsequent contributions from unbound antibodies to the signal. This leads to a reduction in the detectable EV–antibody complex signal and decreases measurement sensitivity due to the excess free antibody. These findings indicate that the indicator antibody concentration must be carefully titrated for each experiment. This kind of interference of high antibody concentrations is also observed by flow cytometric analyses of EVs while the antibodies must be titrated before usage to reduce the signal of free antibodies (Welsh et al. 2020; Welsh et al. 2021). These kinetic profiles enable quantitative comparison of antibody loading across EV types and differentiate EV subtypes within heterogeneous samples.

Quantifying EVs by IFCM or nanoflow cytometry with the CellStream yielded epitope density estimates approximately 100‐fold higher than those based on NTA. This discrepancy likely reflects IFCM's ability to selectively detect fluorescently labeled EVs, whereas NTA counts all scattering particles above a size threshold. These findings emphasize the importance of selecting appropriate particle quantification methods to ensure accurate binding stoichiometry (Fortunato et al. 2021; Loconte et al. 2023; Mladenovic et al. 2025; Vogel et al. 2021). Therefore, the EV quantification approaches should be carefully chosen to ensure reliable binding stoichiometry calculations.

Unlike immobilization‐based platforms (SPR, BLI, QCM‐D), FIDA performs kinetic measurements entirely in‐ solution, preserving native EV surface architecture and epitope availability. Prior comparisons of FIDA with SPR/BLI for anti‐HLA antibodies in patient serum revealed modestly lower affinities for FIDA, attributable to its physiological assay environment (Hug et al. 2023). Thus, FIDA complements immobilization‐based techniques by enabling minimally perturbative, in‐solution measurements.

Similarly, recent work by Paganini et al. demonstrated the potential of fluoMDS for the rapid, in‐solution characterization of EVs (Paganini et al. 2022). Like FIDA, this technique enables the simultaneous measurement of EV size, concentration, and biomarker abundance in untreated samples without washing steps. While fluoMDS focuses on spatial diffusion profiles for high‐throughput screening, our results confirm that FIDA offers complementary strengths, particularly in quantifying molecular interaction kinetics under native conditions.

Although both techniques exploit diffusion phenomena under laminar flow, they differ fundamentally in detection geometry. FluoMDS relies on spatial fluorescence imaging across a microfluidic channel, whereas FIDA monitors the axial dispersion of an injected sample plug to generate a time‐resolved Taylorgram (Hug et al. 2023; Jensen and Ostergaard 2010). Importantly, the FIDA capillary can be easily adjusted via surface coatings to accommodate different indicators, enabling flexible experimental setups on a single platform.

These findings underscore the growing relevance of diffusion‐based analytical technologies for efficient, minimally invasive EV characterization under native conditions. In this context, our results further demonstrate the versatility of diffusion‐based approaches by applying FIDA to EV analysis, enabling quantitative assessment of binding events and molecular interactions under solution‐phase conditions.

Our findings also support FIDA's utility in assessing EV surface engineering. Binding site estimates for CD63–mNG–Fc EVs were consistent with fluorescence correlation spectroscopy (FCS) data reported by Wiklander et al. (Wiklander et al. 2024), validating FIDA's quantitative accuracy. Rh shifts observed upon binding human IgG1‐APC and Cetuximab ALC647 confirmed efficient antibody loading, with larger fluorophore conjugates contributing to modest size increases. EVs decorated with specific antibodies can be directed toward target cells, such as tumor or immune cells, enabling tissue‐specific delivery of therapeutic agents (Wiklander et al. 2024; Zickler et al. 2024).

Beyond characterization, FIDA holds promise for studying EV‐biomolecule interactions in diagnostics, therapeutics, and vaccine platforms. Integration with microfluidic fractionation or multiplexed fluorescence assays could further enhance throughput and analytical resolution.

FIDA offers for EV analysis a label‐compatible, immobilization‐free platform for EV analysis, integrating size determination, binding parameters, and real‐time interaction profiling. As a complement to NTA, IFCM, and biosensor technologies, it enables sensitive, physiologically relevant assessment of EV‐ligand interactions, supporting the development of EV‐based diagnostics and therapeutics.

While FIDA presents several advantages, for example, minimal sample preparation and compatibility with native assay conditions, it needs careful optimization of flow parameters, antibody titration, and EV concentration remain essential. In present, FIDA requires EV concentrations of approximately 108–109 p/mL, as achieved in concentrated samples to achieve reliable binding curves. Direct application to minimally processed or crude conditioned media without prior concentration steps remains limited because of substantially lower EV concentrations relative to the total particle background. More generally, accurate binding analysis depends on well‐defined particle populations. This is particularly relevant for engineered, highly polydisperse, or unlabeled EV populations, for which precise fluorescence‐based quantification of EVs is important to enable robust kinetic analysis. Future integration of FIDA with the usage of other molecular potential interactors of EVs, such as in the case of corona formation could further expand its utility for functional EV profiling and the development of targeted delivery systems.
