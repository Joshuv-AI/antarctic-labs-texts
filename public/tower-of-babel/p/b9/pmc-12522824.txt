Treatment options for young patients with asymptomatic severe aortic stenosis: a multicenter retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yu Mao, Yang Liu, Mengen Zhai, Ping Jin, Fangyao Chen, Yuhui Yang, Gejun Zhang, Xiaoke Shang, Zhao Jian, Haibo Zhang, Lai Wei, Jian Liu, Yingqiang Guo, Xiangbin Pan, Yongjian Wu, Nicolo Piazza, Jian Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe treatment of patients with asymptomatic severe aortic stenosis (SAS) with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) retention remains controversial.ObjectiveThe study aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of these three treatment modalities and to provide evidence for their clinical application and promotion.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective study included asymptomatic patients with SAS who accepted treatment [defined as normal left ventricular function, aortic valve area ≤ 1.0 cm2, peak velocity (Vmax) ≥ 4.0 m/s or mean pressure gradient ≥ 40 mmHg]. Patients were divided into the transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) group, the surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) group, and the conservative treatment group. The primary end point was all-cause mortality during the follow-up period.ResultsDuring follow-up of 66.5 (interquartile range: 63.1–69.2) months, the incidence of the primary end point was lower in the TAVR group than in the conservative treatment group (33.3% vs. 46.4%, P = 0.023), and the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events was reduced in the SAVR group (12.8% vs. 22.3%, P = 0.047). The incidences of the primary end point (37.3% vs. 60.4%, P < 0.001) and stroke (19.0% vs. 40.7%, P < 0.001) were significantly lower in patients who converted to aortic valve replacement (AVR) compared with those who did not convert to AVR.ConclusionsEarly AVR improved both primary and secondary end points compared with patients having conservative treatments. Conversion to AVR during conservative treatment was found to be more likely to bring net clinical benefits for such patients.Trial registration: ClinicalTrials.gov Protocol Registration System NCT02917980.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03227-6.Keywords: Severe aortic stenosis, Asymptomatic, Transcatheter aortic valve replacement, Surgical aortic valve replacement, Conservative treatment

Introduction

Aortic stenosis (AS) is one of the most common valvular heart diseases in older people. Patients with AS may remain asymptomatic for years. Once symptoms occur, the 2-year mortality rate is as high as 50% among untreated patients [1]. Aortic stenosis presents a chronic progressive course: Aortic stenosis follows a chronic progression, with most untreated cases advancing to severe AS due to the absence of effective medical therapies. Once symptoms appear, the prognosis is extremely poor [2, 3]. Therefore, the current guidelines consider aortic valve replacement (AVR) as a Class I recommendation for patients with symptomatic SAS [4, 5].

However, nearly half of SAS patients do not report any symptoms at the initial visit [6, 7], and the treatment remains controversial [8]. Furthermore, a previous study showed that the mean annual all-cause mortality and mean annual sudden death rate were significantly different, which was closely related to the different cardiovascular risks of the included population, suggesting that patients with asymptomatic SAS should be evaluated and treated individually to obtain the best risk/benefit ratio [9]. Results of the AVATAR and RECOVERY trials confirmed that patients accepting early surgical AVR (SAVR) had a significantly lower incidence of end points than those accepting conservative treatment [10–12]. However, as indications for transcatheter AVR (TAVR) continue to expand [13, 14], it may become a safe alternative to SAVR for early AVR treatment in such patients.

In summary, this multicenter retrospective study explored the long-term clinical outcomes and predictors of TAVR, SAVR, and conservative treatment in patients with asymptomatic SAS. The study aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of the three treatment modalities, thereby providing clinical evidence for further applications and promotion.

Methods

Study design and population

This multicenter retrospective study included patients with asymptomatic SAS treated at nine high-volume centers between January 2017 and December 2018. Patients were evaluated by a team consisting of interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, and echocardiographers [4, 5, 15]. According to the guideline of surgical indications for SAS, all patients were screened [16]. Exercise tests were performed on all patients to assess symptoms and to exclude patients with a positive result on an exercise test [17]. A positive result on an exercise test included AS-related symptoms, decreased systolic blood pressure (≥ 20 mmHg from baseline) (1 mmHg = 0.133 kPa), or stress echocardiographic signs of myocardial ischemia. Then, all patients were divided into three groups according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria: the TAVR group, the SAVR group, and the conservative treatment group.

This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local ethical commissions. Written informed consent for the treatment and subsequent data collection was provided by patients.

Data collection and variables

Asymptomatic SAS patients who planned to be treated with TAVR or SAVR accepted the early surgery within 4 weeks after the outpatient visit. Conservative treatments included those for risk factors and/or comorbidities. Patients were converted to AVR when the following symptoms appeared: onset of AS-related symptoms, decrease in LVEF < 50%, or follow-up echocardiography that showed an annual increase of 0.3 m/s in peak velocity (Vmax) [16, 18, 19]. Baseline characteristics, periprocedural and hospitalization data for patients who completed AVR, and follow-up data were collected. The patients were followed up for 30 days and then every year for 5 years after the procedures.

Outcomes

Clinical outcomes were defined according to the Valve Academic Research Consortium 3 criteria [20]. The primary end point was all-cause mortality during the follow-up year. Secondary end points included all-cause mortality, repeated major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) [including hospitalizations for acute myocardial infarction and unplanned heart failure (HF)], stroke, and repeated AVR during the follow-up period. In addition, periprocedural and hospital-related complications were analyzed in patients who completed AVR, including life-threatening/major bleeding, major vascular complications, acute kidney injury, valve-in-valve implant, new-onset atrial fibrillation, and a new permanent pacemaker implant.

Statistical methods

The baseline characteristics of the patients were tested for normality and homogeneity of variance. Continuous variables were compared using variance analysis or the median [interquartile range (IQR)]. If the results were not normally distributed, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. Categorical variables were reported as frequency, and the differences in dichotomous variables were evaluated using the χ2 or the Fisher precision test, if applicable. Comparisons of continuous variables between the three groups were performed by variance analysis or the Kruskal–Wallis test (as appropriate). The multivariate model included statistically significant variables in the univariate analysis (P < 0.05). Cox proportional hazard regression models were used to estimate the risk ratio to compare the TAVR, SAVR, and conservative treatment groups. The results were expressed as hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Logistic regression models compared dichotomous events (including MACE, HF hospitalization, and repeated AVR). The results were expressed as odds ratio and 95% CI. In addition, the subgroup analyses were performed to compare the corresponding clinical outcomes. Cumulative incidence estimates were estimated using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed using logarithmic rank tests. All reported bilateral P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All were statistically analyzed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R 3.6.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Institute for Statistics and Mathematics, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Participants and descriptive

A total of 476 treated patients with asymptomatic SAS were divided into the TAVR group (n = 126), the SAVR group (n = 117), and the conservative treatment group (n = 233) (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1. The median age of the patients was 63.0 (IQR: 55.0–69.0) years; 54.6% were male; and the median Society of Thoracic Surgeons score was 0.85 (IQR: 0.65–1.25)%. The causes of SAS were degenerative valvular heart diseases (n = 220, 46.2%), rheumatic valvular disease (n = 64, 13.5%), and bicuspid aortic valve morphology (n = 192, 40.3%). Except for age, body mass index, and body surface area in the TAVR group, the three groups were broadly balanced in baseline characteristics (all P > 0.05). Notably, the conservative treatment group used more angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors and fewer angiotensin receptor blockers. Additionally, with the exception of a slightly lower mean pressure gradient in the conservative treatment group (P = 0.004), the treatment groups were generally well balanced in terms of preprocedural imaging assessments (Table 2, Supplementary Table 2).

Clinical outcomes

TAVR group vs. SAVR group vs. conservative treatment group. The incidences of the primary and secondary end points are shown in Table 4. The incidence of the primary end point was significantly lower in the TAVR group compared to the conservative treatment group (P = 0.023). Notably, the incidence of MACE was significantly higher in the conservative treatment group than in the SAVR group (P = 0.047). There was no significant difference in the primary endpoint (all-cause mortality) between the TAVR and SAVR groups (33.3% vs. 36.8%, P = 0.672), indicating comparable long-term survival benefits. Regarding secondary endpoints, the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) was slightly lower in the SAVR group (12.8% vs. 18.3%, P = 0.323), although this difference was not statistically significant. The TAVR group had a slightly lower stroke incidence (19.0% vs. 24.8%, P = 0.354), which may be attributed to its minimally invasive nature. The incidences of the primary (P = 0.042) and secondary end points (P = 0.018) between the three groups calculated using Kaplan–Meier analysis are shown in Fig. 2.

Subgroup analysis

Kaplan–Meier analysis results for the three subgroups in the conservative treatment group were similar to those described previously (Fig. 2). The incidence of the primary end point for patients who did not convert to AVR was significantly higher than that of patients with early AVR (TAVR: P < 0.001; SAVR: P = 0.001) and stroke (TAVR: P < 0.001; SAVR: P = 0.022) (Supplementary Table 4). Importantly, the incidences of the primary end point (37.3% vs. 60.4%, P < 0.001) and stroke (19.0% vs. 40.7%, P < 0.001) in patients who converted to AVR were significantly lower than in those who did not convert to AVR. In addition, patients who converted to AVR in the conservative treatment group had clinical outcomes similar to those with early AVR (Fig. 3).

Predictors

Cox regression analysis was used to calculate the factors that influenced clinical outcomes among the groups (Table 5). Multivariate analysis showed that previous coronary artery disease, N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), LVEF, and Vmax were the risk factors for the primary end point (all P < 0.05). The results of the subgroup analyses are shown in Supplementary Table 5. In addition, Supplementary Table 6 shows the predictors of secondary end points during the follow-up period evaluated by logistic regression analysis. Compared with the conservative treatment group, NT-proBNP was a predictor of early AVR (TAVR: HR: 1.41, 95% CI 1.20–2.08, P = 0.001; SAVR: HR: 1.90, 95% CI 1.07–2.56, P = 0.046). Previous percutaneous coronary intervention (HR: 2.51, 95% CI 1.49–3.86, P < 0.001) and statins (HR: 1.66, 95% CI 1.07–2.58, P = 0.025) were found to be risk factors in the comparison of the TAVR group with the SAVR group.

Discussion

Key results and interpretation

The main findings of this study included the following: (1) Patients who underwent AVR had a lower incidence of primary and secondary end points compared with patients who received conservative treatment. Similar findings were found in three subgroups of the conservative treatment group. (2) The follow-up results showed that the clinical outcomes of the TAVR group were similar to those of the SAVR group. (3) The clinical outcomes of patients who converted to AVR in the conservative treatment group were better than those without AVR conversion. (4) The clinical outcomes of patients who converted to AVR were similar to those of patients with early AVR. (5) Multivariate Cox regression analysis found that previous coronary artery disease, NT-proBNP, LVEF, and Vmax were risk factors for the primary end point.

The median time from study enrollment to TAVR and SAVR procedures was 11.7 days and 8.5 days, respectively, and the median time from symptom onset to AVR was 26.3 days. Early studies showed that conservative treatment appeared to be acceptable in patients with asymptomatic SAS compared to patients with symptomatic SAS [21, 22]. If the patient is asymptomatic and at low risk, then the conservative treatment strategy may be safe [6]. In terms of drug research, although cardiologists have been exploring drugs that can delay or reverse the AS process and improve the prognosis, little has been identified except vitamin K1 [23]. A registry study showed that the 5-year mortality rate of patients with asymptomatic SAS who initially had conservative treatments was as high as 26.4%, and the hospitalization rate for HF was 19.9% [24]. Similarly, a meta-analysis showed that the mean annual all-cause mortality, HF mortality, and sudden death rates for patients with asymptomatic SAS during the conservative treatment period were 4.8%, 2.0%, and 1.1%, respectively [25]. The preceding results suggested that conservative treatment could sometimes put a large number of patients at high risk. While a conservative treatment strategy may be deemed safe for patients who are asymptomatic and at low risk, it should be noted that some patients, initially considered low risk, may still face higher risks due to disease progression, thus warranting close monitoring. This emphasizes that in certain circumstances, patients, even those initially assessed as being at low risk, could still encounter adverse outcomes due to changes in their condition, hence the clinical decision-making must be personalized.

Surgery has always been central to the treatment of severe VHDs, and SAS is no exception. Previous studies have indicated that both SAVR and TAVR are the only treatments that have been shown to improve prognosis [25]. The evidence-based medical evidence of early AVR focuses mainly on SAVR [10, 11]. Although the trauma of SAVR is relatively severe, it can be used to treat other VHDs at the same time. However, SAVR has inherent limitations and cannot be applied to all patients who require operations [26]. The vigorous development of TAVR has brought hope to patients with high surgical risk or obvious surgical contraindications. At present, the indication for TAVR has been extended to low-risk patients [13, 14]. TAVR is less invasive than SAVR, and it is even associated with a lower risk of death, stroke, or readmission [13]. Evidence that TAVR improves outcomes in patients with asymptomatic SAS is still lacking; therefore, TAVR has not been recommended by current guidelines. However, the unique technical advantages of TAVR and the general condition of patients with asymptomatic SAS are better than those of patients with symptomatic SAS, so it is reasonable to speculate that TAVR may safely replace SAVR for early AVR treatment. In addition, our study is a retrospective analysis that inherently has certain limitations in sample selection. The baseline characteristics and severity of the conditions of the patients included in our study may differ from those in randomized trials. For instance, the age and comorbidities distribution of patients in the TAVR group in our study may not align with those in randomized trials, which could potentially bias the results. Additionally, differences in surgical techniques and postoperative management across various studies may also impact the final outcomes.

It is important to note that for patients with asymptomatic SAS, the timing of TAVR implementation is critical, and the optimal timing is currently controversial. Studies have found that if the operation is performed too early, the surgical risk will not be enough to offset the risk of cardiovascular death caused by SAS [27]. However, if it is too late, the prognosis might be extremely poor once symptoms appear. SAS will continue to aggravate the pressure load of the left ventricle and will then lead to serious myocardial damage that cannot be reversed [6]. Finally, a high risk of all-cause death exists even after the surgical treatment [24]. In our study, we found that the clinical outcomes of patients in the conservative treatment group who converted to AVR were better than those who did not convert to AVR, and the clinical outcomes of patients who converted to AVR were similar to those of patients with early AVR.

Additionally, Yingchoncharoen et al. [28] proposed a scoring system for evaluating adverse events in patients with asymptomatic SAS, which included left ventricular global longitudinal stress, valvular vascular impedance, Vmax, and a calcification burden. This scoring system could better predict the risk of all-cause death and MACE and help optimize treatment strategies for such patients. Current guidelines recommend early AVR if there is a significant increase of NT-proBNP [4, 5]. We obtained similar results, that is, NT-proBNP was one of the risk factors affecting the prognostic effect in the three groups. Furthermore, it is worth noting that LVEF was found to be another major factor in the multivariate Cox regression analysis. For asymptomatic SAS patients with an LVEF < 50%, current guidelines recommend early treatment with AVR to reduce all-cause mortality and the incidence of MACE [4, 5]. In fact, in most asymptomatic patients, LVEF < 50% is uncommon, whereas patients with LVEF between 50 and 59% fare almost as badly, some of whom have no lower cardiovascular risk than patients with LVEF < 50% [29]. As expected, we found that Vmax was also a predictor of the primary end point. Guidelines recommend early AVR treatment for patients with Vmax ≥ 5.0 m/s4.This recommendation is due to the fact that the disease progresses rapidly in certain patients, and it is difficult to accurately predict the timing of MACE. Vmax may play a predictive role in this respect.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the patients in our study were relatively young, had a higher incidence of bicuspid aortic valve morphology, and exhibited baseline differences between groups in terms of body mass index and body surface area. Due to the limited sample size, propensity score matching was not performed. While this did not impact the effective orifice area index, it may have influenced other baseline characteristics. Second, the retrospective nature of the study resulted in echocardiographic data that had not been evaluated by the core laboratory. Third, stress testing is a tool for risk assessment in patients with asymptomatic SAS. It has good sensitivity to predict MACE and sudden cardiac death. However, the researchers found that only some patients in this study completed the stress test, so those results were not analyzed. Fourth, due to the small number of cases of low-flow and low-gradient SAS, this study did not conduct subgroup analyses of such patients. Low-flow and low-gradient SAS may be secondary to impaired left ventricular systolic function or reduced ventricular volume due to hypertrophy and stiffness of the left ventricle, and its prognosis is significantly worse than that of ordinary asymptomatic SAS (SVI ≥ 35 mL/m2, mean pressure gradient ≥ 40 mmHg, and LVEF > 50%). Future studies should focus on asymptomatic LFLG SAS patients to evaluate the efficacy of TAVR or SAVR. Fifth, the fact that some patients did not undergo exercise testing is indeed a significant limitation of this study, which may lead to inaccurate assessment of patients’ asymptomatic status. This issue should be improved in future studies to ensure a more accurate evaluation of patients’ symptomatic status. In addition, its management is also more challenging than the latter in clinical practice. Finally, the generalizability of the primary and secondary end points was influenced to some extent by the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic during the study.

Conclusions

The results of the study suggested that early AVR improved both primary and secondary end points compared to patients who accepted conservative treatments. The results also held true in subgroups of the conservative treatment group. In particular, with the maturity and perfection of TAVR, the clinical outcomes were slightly better than those with SAVR. Meanwhile, previous coronary artery disease, NT-proBNP, LVEF, and Vmax were risk factors affecting prognosis. However, many questions remain about treatment options for patients with asymptomatic SAS. In addition to the need to adequately evaluate surgical risks, possible complications and cost–benefit ratios need to be considered. The findings need to be further confirmed in large randomized controlled trials.
