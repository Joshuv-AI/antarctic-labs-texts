Effects of female caregiver communication and behavior on sexual behavior of South African adolescent girls and young women post-HIV/STI intervention
Authors: Alyssa Debra, Enky Mhlongo, Mary-Ellen Mackesy-Amiti, Ashley Kendall, Erin Emerson, Erin Jaworski, Anelisiwe Ngcuka, Millicent Atujuna, Linda-Gail Bekker, Natasha Crooks, Geri Donenberg
Published: 2025

Introduction

Globally, South Africa (SA) has the greatest burden of individuals living with HIV (Zuma et al., 2022; Simbayi et al., 2019). South African youth ages 15–24 experience the highest rate of incident infection (38 %), with South African adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) in this age group accounting for 75 % of all new infections in country compared to males (Zuma et al., 2022; Simbayi et al., 2019; Palanee-Phillips et al., 2022; Murewanhema et al., 2022). National data from 2017 indicate that SA-AGYW acquire HIV at a rate three times their male counterparts (Simbayi et al., 2019; Govender et al., 2022). Additionally, the prevalence of sexually transmitted infections (STIs), such as chlamydia, is >30 % for SA-AGYW 24 years-old and younger (Wand et al., 2020; Naidoo et al., 2014). These infections increase the risk of HIV acquisition (Ramjee and Daniels, 2013; Kalichman et al., 2011). Untreated STIs and HIV have a significant impact on long-term fertility and morbidity, compounding the negative health consequences for SA-AGYW (Tsevat et al., 2017; Marston et al., 2017; Mathias et al., 2024). A pressing need exists to understand what factors are influencing SA-AGYW HIV/STI risk.

Youth development

Biological (i.e., hormones that amplify sexual drive) and cognitive (i.e., decision-making skills) changes during adolescence increase young people’s vulnerability to acquiring HIV/STIs. For example, during adolescence, exploration of sexuality is common and may manifest in behaviors that place youth at risk for negative health outcomes, such as early sexual debut, unprotected sex, and having multiple sexual partners, which may carry long-term health consequences (Magnusson et al., 2019; Buzwell and Rosenthal, 1996; Gerressu and Stephenson, 2008). Additionally, young people often initiate alcohol use before age 18 (Olawole-Isaac et al., 2018; Belete et al., n.d.). Alcohol intoxication during sex can impair a youth’s ability to practice safe sex, including using condoms accurately and consistently (Carels et al., 2023; Bryan et al., 2007; Carels et al., 2022).

Parents can support youth during these normal developmental changes and help guide adolescent decision-making processes, including those related to sexual behavior. Indeed, evidence shows that sexual decisions (i.e., initiating sex, condom use) made by young people can be influenced by family members, particularly parents, who play an essential role in advising and shaping youth’s understanding of sexual behaviors (Bankole et al., 2007; Blum, 2007; Bastien et al., 2011; Sidze and Defo, 2013). For example, adolescents who receive sexual health education from parents are more likely to delay sexual debut and adopt safer sexual practices. (Usonwu et al., 2021).

South African female caregivers

In South Africa, female caregivers (FCs) (e.g., mothers, aunts, cousins, grandmothers) play a critical role in helping young people, particularly SA-AGYW, navigate sexual socialization (i.e., process of learning about sexual norms, attitudes, behaviors, and values). Sexual health information provided by FCs can be important in sustaining SA-AGYW HIV/STI prevention efforts (Oppong, 2006; Ingoldsby and Smith, 2006; Bosire et al., 2021; Baptiste et al., 2006; Mpondo et al., 2018). Adolescents express more comfort discussing sexual topics with mothers compared to fathers (Usonwu et al., 2021). In South Africa, it is common for FCs beyond biological mothers (e.g., aunties) to oversee SA-AGYW’s education about sexual relationships and ensure they receive necessary guidance and support for their sexual health (Oppong, 2006; Bosire et al., 2021). Within South African families, FCs communicate more with female adolescents about pubertal changes and abstaining from sexual activities than with male adolescents (Seif et al., 2017; Mbachu et al., 2020; Kumi-Kyereme et al., 2007; Githinji, 2020; Marthinus, 2024) and thus are in a prime position to use effective communication to help SA-AGYW prevent HIV/STIs and engage in healthy sexual behavior.

Parent-adolescent communication

Parent-adolescent communication that is open, comfortable, and frequent can help mitigate sexual and reproductive health challenges by shaping adolescents’ sexual practices and attitudes. Indeed, research in sub-Saharan Africa has shown that communication between parents and adolescents that is frequent, comfortable, and open is associated with delayed sexual initiation and more frequent condom use among adolescents (Okigbo et al., 2015; Nattabi et al., 2023). Parents who report feeling comfortable discussing sexual topics with their youth addressed diverse issues, including pubertal changes, relationships with the opposite sex, abstinence from premarital sex, teenage pregnancy, and abortion (Usonwu et al., 2021; Mbachu et al., 2020; Ndugga et al., 2023). Girls’ perceptions of parental comfort and openness discussing sexual topics are related to a greater likelihood of communicating about sexual issues (Isaksen et al., 2020).

However, parent-adolescent conversations about sex, STIs, and HIV prevention (e.g., condom use) in sub-Saharan Africa are often limited, and the reasons are multifaceted. Some parents report discomfort discussing sexual topics and thus limit conversations to a few areas, such as abstinence (Mpondo et al., 2018; Mbachu et al., 2020; Kumi-Kyereme et al., 2007; Githinji, 2020). Parents who emphasized abstinence in their communication with teens or report discomfort discussing sex and contraception with their adolescents hindered effective communication about adolescents’ sexual and reproductive health (Mbachu et al., 2020; Marthinus, 2024). Additionally, while African AGYW desire conversations with parents about condom use, HIV/STI prevention, and relationships with sexual partners, they fear parental judgment and disapproval (Mpondo et al., 2018; Githinji, 2020; Gumede et al., 2017).

These studies document associations between parent-adolescent communication and sexual risk behavior among AGYW living in sub-Saharan Africa, yet little is known about South African (SA) AGYW specifically or how changes in parent-teen communication over time may be related to SA-AGYW sexual risk behavior. Further, these studies have not investigated how individual communication components (comfort, openness, or frequency) may hold more weight in their associations with SA-AGYW specific sexual risk behaviors. This study sought to address these gaps by focusing on SA-AGYW and their female caregivers and the unique associations between communication comfort, openness, and frequency and SA-AGYW’s sexual behavior.

Adolescent perceptions of parental acceptance and rejection

Two additional parenting characteristics have been linked to lower sexual risk-taking among adolescents and may also apply to SA-AGYW: (1) greater perceived acceptance or feeling valued, understood, and supported, and (2) less perceived rejection or feeling misunderstood, dismissed, or unsupported (Devlin et al., 2018). Perceptions of parental acceptance and non-rejection may also strengthen the impact of parent-adolescent communication on teens’ sexual behavior. Indeed, existing research in sub-Saharan Africa indicates that adolescents who perceive their parents as understanding and supportive and not dismissive of their choices communicate more frequently with their parents about their sexual health and are less likely to engage in sexual intercourse (Kumi-Kyereme et al., 2007; Isaksen et al., 2020; Devlin et al., 2018). Positive parent–child relationships, characterized by affection, support, listening, spending time together, and providing material support have been associated with decreased adolescent sexual risk taking (Wamoyi and Wight, 2014), while lack of parent-adolescent connectedness and parental overprotection, lack of emotional attachment, rejection, and anxious rearing are linked to increased risky sexual behaviors (i.e., more premarital sex, engagement in transactional sex), and increased vulnerability to STIs including HIV (Wamoyi and Wight, 2014; Ezekiel, n.d.). However, additional data specific to FC-SA-AGYW relationship is needed to understand its association to SA-AGYW sexual risk behaviors. By focusing on SA-AGYW and FCs, communication, and parenting behavior, this study will explore important gaps about their combined impacts on SA-AGYW sexual risk taking.

South African family-based HIV/STI prevention program for AGYW and FC

Family-based psychosocial interventions to improve parent-adolescent communication and adolescent perceptions of parental acceptance and rejection hold promise in increasing youth’s safer sex behavior. Few such programs exist within the South African context, but a recent study of IMARA (Informed Motivated Aware and Responsible Adolescents and Adults), an evidence-based mother-daughter HIV/STI prevention program derived from the United States, reported high feasibility, acceptability, appropriateness, and fidelity for SA-AGYW and their FCs. (Merrill et al., 2023) Originally developed for African American 14–18 year-old females and their female caregivers in Chicago, a 2-arm efficacy trial revealed a 43 % lower likelihood of incident STIs among young women who received IMARA compared to those who received a general health program at one year follow-up (Donenberg et al., 2020). IMARA-SA is a unique intervention designed to strengthen SA-AGYW–caregiver relationships and reduce HIV vulnerability among female youth in South Africa. The culturally adapted intervention places a strong emphasis on skill-building (i.e., healthy communication, sexual health knowledge), role-playing, and dyadic interactions between adolescent girls and young women, ages 15–19, and their caregivers over an intensive two-day format. Additionally, the intervention targets broader psychosocial drivers of HIV risk (i.e., mental health, relationship power dynamics (intimate partner violence)) to promote sustained safer sexual behavior among SA-AGYW. However, other Sub-Saharan African family-based HIV prevention interventions differ in structure, target population, and duration. While one South African intervention included sessions on intimate partner violence, the program was centered on adult women, ages 33–39 (Pronyk et al., 2006; Phetla et al., 2008). Three other interventions across Africa targeted younger children ages 9–14 (Baptiste et al., 2006; Katahoire et al., 2019; Poulsen et al., 2010; Vandenhoudt et al., 2010). Many of these interventions had sessions with formats ranging from every two weeks to multiple weeks (Baptiste et al., 2006; Pronyk et al., 2006; Katahoire et al., 2019; Poulsen et al., 2010; Vandenhoudt et al., 2010; Thurman et al., 2018). Among them, only one intervention addressed mental health, included dyadic sessions, and incorporated skill-based sessions (Thurman et al., 2018). However, it did not explicitly include content on relationship dynamics or intimate partner violence. These distinctions highlight how IMARA-SA utilizes a comprehensive approach in promoting safer sexual behaviors and reducing HIV risk among a highly vulnerable population. Prior to testing in South Africa, IMARA was rigorously and systematically adapted for the local context (hereafter referred to as IMARA-SA) (Merrill et al., 2023; Wingood & DiClemente, 2008; Fynn et al., 2021; Atujuna et al., 2021) retaining its focus on strengthening SA-AGYW-FC communication and SA-AGYW perceptions of parenting behavior. Pilot data from IMARA-SA offer an ideal opportunity to shed light on the impact of an SA-AGYW-FC focused intervention on SA-AGYW sexual behavior.

Study focus

This paper presents a secondary data analysis of a pilot study of IMARA-SA. (Merrill et al., 2023) We hypothesized that SA-AGYW who received IMARA-SA would report more frequent, open, and comfortable communication about topics related to sex and HIV prevention compared to SA-AGYW in the control group at 6-months. We also expected SA-AGYW who participated in IMARA-SA to report less sexual risk behavior (e.g., more consistent condom use, fewer sexual partners) and higher levels of parental acceptance and lower levels of parental rejection than the control group. Finally, we expected perceptions of positive parental behavior at baseline to amplify the impact of positive SA-AGYW-FC communication on AGYW sexual risk behavior at 6-months, but only for SA-AGYW in IMARA-SA.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited from the Klipfontein/Mitchells Plain (K/MP) health sub-district of the Western Cape in South Africa. SA-AGYW were enrolled if they self-identified as (a) female, (b) black or mixed-race, (c) 15–19 years-old, and (d) an English and/or isiXhosa speaker. The mean age of SA-AGYW was 17 years (SD = 1.5), 56 % lived with the participating FC, 63 % completed some or all of secondary school, and 17 % reported supporting themselves financially within the past year.

FCs were enrolled if they identified as (a) female, (b) 24-years-old or older, (c) living with or having daily contact with the SA-AGYW, (d) an English and/or isiXhosa speaker, and (e) an FC by the SA-AGYW. The mean age of FC was 36 years (SD = 9.61). As reported by SA-AGYW, 41 % were biological mothers, 27 % were aunts, and 19 % were sisters. FCs and SA-AGYW who participated in other phases of the study (i.e., theater testing) were excluded.

Procedures

Participant recruitment started in October 2019, and 6-month follow-up assessments were completed by October 2020. Research staff used various approaches to recruit SA-AGYW (e.g., posted flyers at the primary research site, Desmond Tutu Health Foundation (DTHF), in Phillipi Village which also houses a Youth Clinic that provides sexual health services. The flyers invited FC and/or SA-AGYW to call/text/email study staff if they were interested in the project. SA-AGYW and FCs were also approached via street outreach in surrounding neighborhoods. Interested SA-AGYW and FCs were screened by research staff for eligibility and invited to DTHF for further screening and enrollment. If eligible, participants were scheduled for the baseline assessment and workshop day one. SA-AGYW and FC were required to participate together. In cases where SA-AGYW preferred to participate with someone other than her mother (such as an aunt or older sister), the mother’s permission was obtained to enroll the SA-AGYW with an alternative FC. Likewise, in cases where the mother preferred an alternate FC, the SA-AGYW chose a FC with whom to participate. At the study site, participants chose their preferred language (isiXhosa or English) for consent/assent and survey items were presented in English and isiXhosa using CASI. Once eligibility confirmed and consent/assent were obtained, FCs and SA-AGYW both completed a baseline survey on computer tablets about their demographics, sexual risk behavior, FC-SA-AGYW communication, SA-AGYW perceived caregiver acceptance and rejection, and other topics.

After the baseline assessment, AGYW-FC dyads were individually randomized to IMARA-SA or a family-based health promotion control program by selecting their group assignment from a paper bag. Both the control program and IMARA-SA were delivered in groups of two to nine dyads over two days for a total of approximately 12 h. At the end of workshop day 1, all SA-AGYW and FCs were offered STI testing (Gonorrhea, Chlamydia), HIV testing and counseling, and pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) if they tested negative for HIV. Participants who tested positive for a STI were notified as soon as results were available and given free single-dose treatment. Participants who tested positive for HIV were immediately referred to the DTHF clinic and linked to care. AGYW and FC received up to 130 Rand for transportation and their participation at baseline and follow-up, and meals and snacks were provided on both workshop days. Due to COVID restrictions, follow-up surveys were collected between 6- and 10-months post-baseline (mean = 8.7 months, SD = 0.79). Follow-up STI testing, HIV testing, and PrEP initiation (if appropriate) occurred between 8 and 17 months after baseline (mean = 13.6 months, SD = 3.7). Retention was 93 % at follow-up for IMARA-SA and 80 % for the control group (p = 0.142).

IMARA and health promotion (FUEL) program descriptions

IMARA-SA is a 2-day (~10–12 h total) workshop designed to promote healthy community norms, sisterhood, improve effective communication, encourage HIV/STI prevention, and strengthen the relationship between AGYW and their FC. (Donenberg et al., 2020) Workshop activities include interactive games and role-playing and address female empowerment (e.g., learning about unhealthy and healthy relationships), HIV/STI prevention (e.g., learning to use condoms), conflict resolution (e.g., practicing healthy communication), mental health, cultural pride, and effective communication. FC were taught monitoring strategies to reduce AGYW HIV/STI-risk. SA-AGYW and FCs engaged in activities in separate groups and jointly. During joint sessions SA-AGYW and FC practiced effective communication (e.g., open, positive), strengthened their relationship, and played games to increase warm and positive interactions.

The Health Promotion control group (FUEL) was matched in time and structure to IMARA-SA (e.g., delivered over two days), but focused on media literacy (e.g., media influences on the body and beauty standards), healthy eating, violence (e.g., gang, domestic violence), alcohol/drugs, and physical activities (e.g., benefits of exercise). Like IMARA-SA, some activities were delivered jointly with AGYW and FCs, and others were conducted in separate groups. For both arms, two facilitators co-delivered workshop activities for FCs, and two facilitators co-delivered activities for SA-AGYW. Training of group facilitators, supervision, and treatment fidelity are explained in detail elsewhere (Merrill et al., 2023).

Measures

Demographics

Demographic information included SA-AGYW age, FC caregiver status, and SA-AGYW HIV status.

FC-AGYW communication

For all communication variables, mother was defined as the FC who participated in the study with the AGYW. Communication was reported by SA-AGYW using an expanded and adapted version of the Sexual Communication Scale (SCS) developed by Miller and peers. (Miller et al., 1998) The SCS, a validated tool used for assessing sexual communication between adolescents and their parents, has been widely used to explore communication dynamics among adolescents. (Bastien et al., 2011) The SCS’s reliability and consistency in measuring communication dynamics have been evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. Cronbach alpha (α) measures internal reliability (i.e., how well items consistently measure the same underlying concept), and is considered acceptable between 0.6 and 0.8. (Hajjar, 2018) Below we report Cronbach alpha’s from the expanded and adapted version of the SCS.

Number of Topics.

SA-AGYW reported whether they ever talked to their female caregivers about 7 number of topics: dating, initiating sex, birth control, condoms, HIV/AIDS/STIs, HIV testing, and use of pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP). Sample items included “Have you and your mother ever talked about dating?” and “Have you and your mother ever talked about getting tested for HIV?” SA-AGYW responded yes = 1 or no = 0 and a summary score was calculated to represent the total number of topics discussed at baseline. Higher scores indicated more topics discussed.

Frequency.

For each topic discussed, SA-AGYW rated the frequency of the discussions in the past 6-months (0=Never, 1=Once, 2 = A few times, 3 = A lot) (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.84). A sample question was “In the past 6 months, how many times have you and your mother talked about PrEP?” An average frequency score was calculated across topics that ranged from 0 to 3 at baseline and at follow-up. Higher scores indicated more frequent discussions.

Comfort.

For each topic discussed more than once in the past 6 months, SA-AGYW rated how comfortable the discussions were (1 = very uncomfortable, 2 = a little uncomfortable, 3 = somewhat comfortable, 4 = very comfortable) (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.90). A sample question was “How comfortable do you feel when you discuss birth control with your mother?” An average comfort score was calculated across topics at baseline and 6-month follow-up and ranged from 1 to 4. Higher scores indicated more comfort in discussions.

Openness.

Ten items measured how open AGYW perceived the communication to be for each topic from 1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree. Five items were reversed coded so that higher scores indicated more open communication. An average openness score across topics was derived for baseline and follow-up reports with higher scores indicating more open communication. Sample items included “I can ask my mother the questions I really want to know about topics like these (e.g., sex, condoms, HIV/STIs, PrEP),” and “My mother tries to understand how I feel about topics like these (e.g., sex, condoms, HIV/STIs, PrEP).” Two items were dropped from further analyses due to low internal consistency (α = 0.50) indicating that these items did not align well with the communication construct being measured. After dropping items, internal reliability was acceptable for openness (α = 0.65).

Maternal acceptance-rejection

SA-AGYW completed the Children’s Report of Parenting Behavior Inventory, (Schaefer, 1965) a widely-used measure of perceived maternal acceptance and rejection. (Donenberg et al., 2003; Roman et al., 2012; Salaam, 2023; Rodriguez et al., 2024) SA-AGYW rated the FC participating in the study on acceptance and rejection, each comprised of eight items and response options ranging from 1 = not at all true, 2 = somewhat true, and 3 = very true. Sample items for acceptance are “My mother enjoys doing things with me,” and “My mother almost always speaks to me with a warm and friendly voice”. Sample items for rejection are “My mother doesn’t support me”, and “My mother gets cross and angry about little things I do”. Higher scores indicated greater acceptance and less rejection. An average score for perceptions of parental behavior across the eight items was calculated for acceptance and rejection separately. Internal reliability was acceptable: acceptance (α = 0.85) and rejection (α = 0.66).

Sexual risk behavior

The AIDS-Risk Behavior Assessment (ARBA) (Donenberg et al., 2001) measures adolescent sexual activity and has been used extensively with sub-Saharan African youth (Donenberg et al., 2022) SA-AGYW reported at baseline and at follow-up in the last six months the number of sexual partners (one/two or more), consistency of condom use (always/less than always), condom use at last sex (yes/no) and drinking during sex (0/one or more times) at baseline and 6-month follow up. A summary score was calculated for the analysis whereby one point was assigned for these responses: two or more sexual partners, less than always condom use, no condom use at last sex, or alcohol use one or more times during sex. A continuous score ranging from 0 to 4 was generated with higher scores indicating greater sexual risk.

Data analyses

We assessed the normal distribution of variables by examining kurtosis and skewness. Bivariate correlations characterized the relationships between variables, while means and standard deviations were calculated as descriptive statistics. Chi-square and t-tests were employed to identify variables that predicted missing data. Missingness was confirmed to be random, as t-tests and chi-square tests indicated that no variables predicted missingness at follow-up. Rather than excluding missing participants, and to retain power to detect effects, we used multiple imputation to estimate average scores for frequency, comfort, openness, and sexual risk at follow-up. (McNeish, 2017) Multiple imputation was conducted using the Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method, which assumes that data follows a multivariate normal distribution and uses present values to predict missing values. (McNeish, 2017) With the highest fraction of missing information for variables being 30 %, 30 iterations of imputations were used (Dong and Peng, 2013; Graham et al., 2007; Royston and White, 2011). We then examined change from baseline to follow-up in communication (average frequency, comfort, openness) and sexual risk behavior within arms (IMARA-SA and control) and differences between arms using chi-square and t-tests. Linear regression was employed to evaluate predictors of communication and sexual risk behavior. We evaluated the direct effects of arm, baseline communication, and perceptions of acceptance and rejection, as well as the interactions between arm, communication, and acceptance and rejection, on follow-up communication. Next, we examined the direct effects of arm, baseline communication, perceptions of acceptance and rejection, follow-up communication, baseline sexual risk behavior, and the interaction between arm, communication, and parental behavior, on sexual risk behavior at follow-up. Beta-co-efficients, 95 % confidence intervals, and p-values were obtained. Sensitivity analysis, comparing listwise deletion and multiple imputation, (McNeish, 2017) were conducted to address potential biases caused by missing data. Effect sizes were calculated for within- and between-group differences. All analyses were performed using SAS v.9.4.

Results

Baseline data were collected from 59 SA-AGYW (29 IMARA-SA, 30 control). Study arms did not differ on any baseline characteristics (see Table 1). One participant in the control group completed the follow up survey 8 months late and was dropped from analysis. At follow-up, 7 SA-AGYW were lost to attrition (FUEL = 5, IMARA-SA = 2). Fisher’s exact test revealed no significant differences between girls who did and did not complete the follow-up assessment (p = 0.42, two-sided).

Frequency:

In both groups, the frequency of communication decreased from baseline to follow-up (IMARA-SA MD = − 0.48, SD = 0.18, p = 0.007 and control MD = −0.6, SD = 0.17, p = 0.0006). Within-group Cohen’s d indicated a very large effect size for both groups (IMARA-SA: d = −2.67, Control: d = −3.53). Girls in the control group showed a significantly greater decrease in average communication frequency from baseline to follow-up compared to those in the IMARA group (MD = −0.12, SD = 0.88, p = 0.002). The between-group effect size was small (g = −0.14).

Comfort:

There were no statistically significant changes in average comfort from baseline to follow-up in either group (IMARA-SA: MD = −0.32, SD = 0.19, p = 0.08; FUEL: MD = 0.04, SD = 0.25, p = 0.86). IMARA-SA showed a large effect size (d = −1.68), whereas the control group showed a very small effect size (d = 0.16). Girls in the IMARA-SA group reported a greater decrease in average comfort scores from baseline to follow-up compared to those in the control group (MD = −0.30, SD = 1.1, p < 0.0001). The between-group effect size was small to moderate (g = −0.27).

Openness:

No statistically significant change was observed in openness from baseline to follow-up within either group (p = 0.49 and p = 0.22, IMARA-SA and control respectively), although raw data indicated that girls in IMARA-SA reported an increase in average openness while control girls reported a decrease in openness. However, there was a statistically significant difference in the change in openness from baseline to follow-up between groups. Compared to IMARA-SA, girls in the control group reported a greater decrease in openness scores from baseline to follow-up (MD = −0.13, SD = 0.34, p < 0.001). The between-group effect size was small to moderate (g = −0.38).

Predictors of follow-up communication

Using multiply imputed data, separate linear regression models were used to assess follow-up average frequency, comfort, and openness of communication, respectively (see Table 5). Girls who indicated less parental rejection at baseline ([95 % CI: 0.14, 1.59], p = 0.02), showed more comfort in communicating at follow-up. Girls who reported more openness ([95 % CI: 0.42, 0.998], p < 0.0001) and parental acceptance at baseline ([95 % CI: 0.08, 0.423], p = 0. 004) also reported more open communication at follow-up.

Discussion

The IMARA-SA pilot study investigated a critical need to understand the impact of family characteristics on SA-AGYW sexual risk behavior. This sub-study provided insight into how communication (frequency, comfort, openness) between SA-AGYW and their FC, along with SA-AGYW perceptions of FC parental behavior, influenced SA-AGYW sexual risk behavior (e.g., more consistent condom use, fewer sexual partners) after engaging in IMARA-SA, underscoring the impact of engaging in a family-based HIV/STI prevention program to reduce sexual risk behavior. Analysis highlighted important nuances in understanding how SA-AGYW and FC communication relate to sexual behavior.

SA-AGYW and caregiver communication

More frequent conversations between adolescents and their caregivers have been associated with consistent condom use (Katahoire et al., 2019; Namisi et al., 2009). From baseline to follow-up, while decreases in average frequency scores were observed for both groups, SA-AGYW in IMARA-SA still reported speaking about sexual topics with their caregivers at a greater frequency than AGYW in the control group. Decreases across groups may reflect challenges in sustaining communication over time without additional support. Although IMARA-SA’s two-day intensive format effectively introduces communication skills, longer-duration interventions or the inclusion of follow-up sessions may help reinforce and sustain behavioral change. For example, an evaluation of a Kenyan family-based HIV/STI prevention program showed that ongoing dialogues between adolescents and parents over longer periods (i.e., weeks) resulted in significant increases in the frequency of sexual conversations at follow-up (Poulsen et al., 2010; Vandenhoudt et al., 2010). However, circumstances external to the intervention, such as competing priorities (e.g., FC may be working) within the family or conflicts, may constrict the ability of SA-AGYW to engage in frequent communication post-intervention. While SA-AGYW may have learned about healthy communication through the IMARA-SA program, post-intervention they may face challenges applying these same skills in a real-world context. These challenges may include difficulty initiating conversations or fear of judgement. Consequently, these barriers may limit participant’s ability to sustain or increase frequency of communication. Adolescents have also reported that discussions with caregivers were more frequent when they felt comfortable (Bastien et al., 2011; Mbachu et al., 2020), indicating that adolescent comfort in communication may influence the frequency of communication with caregivers.

From baseline to follow-up, comfort in communication decreased for AGYW in IMARA-SA compared to the controls. However, raw scores remained higher at follow-up among girls in IMARA-SA compared to the control. A decrease in comfort among girls may be attributed to the sensitive nature of sexual topics and/or the possibility that this was the first time they discussed these topics with caregivers. Adolescents who are uncomfortable with topics about sex or discussing sex with their caregivers avoid conversations which may increase their sexual risk behavior (Usonwu et al., 2021; Amuyunzu-Nyamongo et al., 2024; Wangamati, 2020; Motsomi et al., 2016). SA-AGYW learned about a range of sexual topics which may take time to become comfortable in discussing. Additionally, SA-AGYW may have discomfort around FC responses to sexual topics post-intervention. Building comfort in discussing sexual topics, particularly those that may have previously avoided, can be a gradual process that may initially result in decreases in comfort, followed by increases in comfort as time goes on. Analyses of responses at 12 month follow-up may provide additional insights into whether improvements in comfort occur over longer periods of time.

From baseline to follow-up, average openness scores were lower among SA-AGYW in the control program compared to IMARA-SA, suggesting the program’s potential strength in facilitating open conversations surrounding sexual topics. When communication about sex is open between parents and children, a conversation, rather than a lecture, can occur and parents can share knowledge about sex and HIV/STI prevention with their youth (Mpondo et al., 2018; Thurman et al., 2020; Phetla et al., 2008). Consequently, improvements in openness in communication may play a critical role in ensuring SA-AGYW receive sexual health information from trusted caregivers. Scaling up family-based interventions like IMARA-SA may prove beneficial for SA-AGYW in decreasing their sexual risk behavior by improving open communication with their female caregivers who can provide support. While research shows that frequent, comfortable, and open communication between parents and children is an important factor in promoting positive health behaviors (using condoms) among youth (Okigbo et al., 2015; Nattabi et al., 2023), youth perceptions of parental behavior also play an important role.

SA-AGYW perception of caregiver acceptance and rejection

Analyses of the IMARA-SA pilot study revealed that SA-AGYW perceptions of caregiver behavior is associated with FC-AGYW communication. SA-AGYW perceptions of high acceptance and low rejection were associated with increased openness and comfort, respectively, in communicating with FC about sexual topics at follow-up. Findings align with research that underscore the importance of supportive and non-judgemental communication between SA-AGYW and caregivers. This fosters healthy and effective conversations about sexual topics which can contribute to safer sexual decision-making among SA-AGYW.

SA-AGYW sexual risk behavior

While communication and perceptions of parental behavior were not significantly associated with SA-AGYW sexual risk behavior at follow-up, reports by SA-AGYW who received IMARA-SA trended toward less risk behavior than control SA-AGYW. Findings align with previous analyses conducted on the same sample. (Merrill et al., 2023) Results underscore the complexity of factors influencing SA-AGYW sexual behavioral outcomes. Findings suggest additional research with larger samples are needed to understand the role of family communication on SA-AGYW sexual risk behaviors.

Limitations

We recognize that the term “mixed race” is not commonly used in the South African context, and that the term “Coloured” is more frequently used to self-identify race. However, in this manuscript, we retained the term “mixed race” to reflect how participants self-identified in the IRB-approved demographic survey used in the IMARA-SA pilot study. We acknowledge that race is a social, and not a biological, construct and carries specific meanings in different cultural contexts. The small sample size may account for the few statistically significant group differences. While multiple imputation was used to preserve statistical power and reduce bias due to missingness, a fully powered trial will be better suited to determine statistically significant differences that exist. Multiple imputation was used to address missing data, including for the outcome variable (sexual risk behavior). Although imputing outcomes is less common and may introduce bias if model assumptions are not met, a complete case sensitivity analyses yielded comparable results. The Sexual Communication Scale and the Children’s Report of Parenting Behavior Inventory have not been validated among South African youth. However, both instruments have been used with South African youth (Donenberg et al., 2021), and widely among youth in the United States, including African American youth (Donenberg et al., 2020; Donenberg G, Emerson E, Mackesy-Amiti ME, Fletcher, 2018; Hadley et al., 2018; Wilson & Donenberg, 2004), where they have demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity. Communication and sexual risk behavior outcomes were self-reported, which can be subject to recall bias (Schroder et al., 2003). However, literature supports a high correlation between self-reported sexual behavior and incidence of HIV/STI (DiClemente, 2016).

Conclusion

IMARA-South Africa, an evidence-based HIV-prevention program for mothers and daughters that was systematically adapted for the South African context is uniquely poised to address the critical need to reduce incident HIV-infections among SA-AGYW. Results from this pilot study provided insights into how communication between SA-AGYW and their FC and SA-AGYW perceptions of FC parental behavior influenced SA-AGYW sexual risk behavior. These findings for IMARA-SA highlight the importance of continued efforts to enhance conversations around sexual health among SA-AGYW, with their caregivers, to reduce incident HIV and STI and support current efforts underway to conduct a randomized control trial.
