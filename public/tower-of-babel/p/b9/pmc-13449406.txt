Establishment of an efficient and reliable Agrobacterium-mediated genetic transformation system for the elite male-sterile poplar ‘Siyang-1’
Authors: Zilu Qin, Haiyan Sun, Jingwei Li, Haonan Chen, Jianjun Hu, Yingnan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
‘Siyang-1’ (Populus×euramericana ‘Siyang-1’) is a male-sterile elite cultivar widely cultivated in southern China. However, with the expansion of cultivation area, ‘Siyang-1’ plantations are increasingly threatened by insect pests, especially the fall webworm (Hyphantria cunea), which causes substantial economic and ecological damage. To genetically engineer an insect-resistant cultivar using Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) toxin genes, an efficient and reliable Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation system was established. This was achieved by first developing an in vitro whole-plant regeneration system using petiole explants, which showed the highest adventitious shoot induction on SIM14 medium (0.5 mg·L⁻¹ 6-BA and 0.2 mg·L⁻¹ IAA), followed by effective rooting on RM7 medium (0.5 mg·L⁻¹ IBA). The optimal kanamycin selection pressure was determined to be 50 mg·L⁻¹. Critical transformation parameters were systematically optimized, identifying bacterial concentration (OD₆₀₀=0.6), infection time (20 min), and co-cultivation period (2 days) with strain EHA105 as the most effective combination. Using this optimized protocol, transgenic ‘Siyang-1’ plants co-expressing two Bt genes, Cry1Ac and Cry3A, were successfully generated. Genomic PCR and semi-quantitative RT-PCR verified transgene integration and expression. Leaf-feeding bioassays demonstrated that these transgenic lines exhibited significantly enhanced resistance against the H. cunea, with leaf area consumption reduced to 5.28%–12.66%, compared to approximately 50% in wild-type plants. Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) quantification further confirmed the presence of Cry1Ac protein in the transgenic lines, and a positive correlation was observed between Cry1Ac protein level and the reduction in leaf consumption. The combination of insect resistance and male sterility makes these lines a valuable germplasm resource for poplar breeding, with potential advantages for biosafety due to reduced pollen-mediated gene flow. The established transformation platform not only addresses a major technical challenge to genetically improving ‘Siyang-1’, but also provides a foundational tool for future gene-editing and precision breeding applications in this commercially important cultivar.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09155-9.

Introduction

Poplar (Populus), is a globally important economic and ecological tree species, owing to its rapid growth, high yield, and extensive applications in wood production and ecological protection [1]. As the first woody plant with a fully sequenced genome, poplar also serves as a model system for genetic and functional genomics research in trees [2]. Agrobacterium-mediated transformation has become a cornerstone technique for the genetic improvement of poplar. However, its successful application remains largely confined to a limited number of model genotypes [3–6]. The lack of efficient transformation systems for many commercially or ecologically important landrace varieties or elite cultivars presents a major bottleneck, severely limiting their targeted genetic enhancement and broader biotechnological application.

‘Siyang-1’ (Populus×euramericana ‘Siyang-1’), bred by Nanjing Forestry University, is a male-sterile cultivar characterized by fast growth, superior wood quality, and high yield. The absence of pollen and catkin dispersal makes ‘Siyang-1’ highly suitable for large-scale cultivation in Jiangsu Province, China [7]. Nonetheless, an efficient and stable transformation has not been developed for this commercially valuable cultivar. In recent years, leaf-feeding pests, particularly the fall webworm (Hyphantria cunea), have emerged as a critical threat to poplar plantations, including those of ‘Siyang-1’, posing an urgent challenge to productivity [8]. The efficacy of Bt genes in conferring insect resistance has been well demonstrated in multiple major crops, such as rice [9], corn [10], cotton [11–12], and poplar [13]. Therefore, Bt gene transformation represents a promising strategy for developing insect-resistant ‘Siyang-1’ cultivars. Establishing an efficient genetic transformation system for ‘Siyang-1’ is thus critical for enabling such targeted genetic improvement. Furthermore, such a system serves as a critical prerequisite for applying modern biotechnologies like gene editing to its precision breeding.

This study aimed to establish an efficient Agrobacterium-mediated transformation system for the elite poplar cultivar ‘Siyang-1’. To this end, a robust whole-plant regeneration system was first developed through systematically evaluating various explants and hormone regimes for shoot induction and rooting. Then, the optimal kanamycin selection pressure was determined, and key transformation parameters, including bacterial concentration, infection time, co-cultivation duration, and Agrobacterium strain, were optimized. Employing this refined system, transgenic ‘Siyang-1’ plants expressing two Bt genes (Cry1Ac and Cry3A) were successfully generated. These transgenic lines exhibited significantly enhanced resistance against H. cunea. To our knowledge, this study represents the first report of genetic transformation for this specific male-sterile germplasm, laying a technical foundation for its molecular breeding and genetic improvement.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

One-year-old branches of ‘Siyang-1’ (elite cultivar no.: S-SC-PE-001-2018) were collected from Malanghu Forest Farm (Suqian City, Jiangsu Province, China). Samples were collected from public land with appropriate permissions obtained from the relevant governing authorities responsible for the management of Malanghu Forest Farm. The branches were trimmed into approximately 15-cm cuttings and rooted in soil to serve as stock plants for explant collection. Resultant plantlets were grown in the greenhouse at Nanjing Forestry University under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod, with temperatures kept at 22 °C (day) and 18 °C (night) with a light intensity of 150 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹.

Newly sprouted shoots (15–20 cm long) were harvested. Leaves and petioles were excised, and the stem tissue was rinsed under running tap water for 30 min. Surface sterilization was performed by sequential treatment with 70% (v/v) ethanol for 30 s, followed by 10% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite for 5 min, and finally rinsing three times with sterile distilled water. To establish axenic ‘Siyang-1’ plantlets, the sterilized stems were cut into ~ 3-cm segments, each containing one or two axillary buds, and cultured on MS0 medium supplemented with 0.02 mg·L⁻¹ NAA and 0.02 mg·L⁻¹ IBA. The MS0 consisted of Murashige and Skoog (MS) salts supplemented with 30 g·L⁻¹ sucrose and 3 g·L⁻¹ Phytagel, with the pH adjusted to 5.8. Axenic plantlets were subsequently propagated by sub-culturing apical buds or nodal segments onto fresh medium of the same formulation in a tissue culture room maintained at 22 ± 2 °C under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod with a light intensity of 30–45 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹.

Development of a whole regeneration system for ‘Siyang-1’

To establish an efficient regeneration system, various explants—including leaf discs, petioles, stem segments without axillary buds, and root segments—were excised from axenic ‘Siyang-1’ plantlets and cultured on shoot induction medium (SIM). The SIM consisted of MS0 supplemented with different combinations and concentrations of plant growth regulators (PGRs) to optimize regeneration efficiency (Table 1). The explants were sub-cultured onto fresh medium every two weeks. To optimize the medium for high rooting frequency, adventitious buds, apical buds, and nodal stem segments were transferred to various rooting medium (RM) formulations. The RM was based on MS0 and supplemented with different combinations of auxins (IBA and NAA) (Table 1). For both shoot induction and rooting experiments, each treatment consisted of 30 explants with three independent biological replicates. The number of explants forming adventitious buds was recorded after 30 days of culture, and rooting was assessed 20 days after inoculation. All data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test (P < 0.05).

Kanamycin sensitivity assay

To determine the optimal selective pressure for genetic transformation, petiole explants were cultured on SIM14 containing 0, 10, 30, or 50 mg·L⁻¹ of filter-sterilized kanamycin (Kan). Each treatment consisted of 30 petioles with three independent biological replicates. Adventitious shoot formation was assessed 30 days after inoculation. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed as described in Sect. 2.2. The minimum concentration that completely inhibited adventitious shoot formation was selected for all subsequent transformation experiments.

Establishment of Agrobacterium-mediated transformation for ‘Siyang-1’

The binary vector N5 [14] was used for transformation. Its T‑DNA region contains three expression cassettes arranged from left border to right border: the Kan resistance selectable marker (NptII) driven by the NOS promoter and terminator, the Cry3A gene driven by the CoYMV promoter and NOS terminator, and the Cry1Ac gene driven by the CaMV 35 S promoter and NOS terminator. The vector was separately introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strains EHA105 and GV3101 via the freeze-thaw method [15]. To enhance plant transformation efficiency, several critical factors were systematically evaluated, including bacterial concentration (OD₆₀₀), infection time, co-cultivation period, and Agrobacterium strain. For all optimization experiments, each treatment involved 30 petioles with three independent biological replicates. All data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test for multi-group comparisons and Student’s t-test for the comparison between two groups, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The composition of all media used in the transformation procedure is presented in Table 2.

Effect of bacterial concentration

A single colony of A. tumefaciens EHA105 containing the N5 plasmid was inoculated into 5 mL of LB liquid medium supplemented with 50 mg·L⁻¹ Kan and 20 mg·L⁻¹ rifampicin (Rif), and cultured at 28 °C with shaking (200 rpm) for 48 h. Subsequently, 1 mL of this starter culture was inoculated into 50 mL of fresh LB medium with the same antibiotics. The culture was grown until the OD₆₀₀ reached 0.2, 0.6, or 1.0. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in 50 mL of sterile infection medium (Table 2). Petioles were immersed in the Agrobacterium suspension for 20 min, blotted dry, and co-cultivated in the dark for 2 days. Then, the petioles were transferred to recovery medium (Table 2) for 7 days and subcultured onto selection medium (Table 2) at 15-day intervals to screen for resistant shoots. Adventitious shoot formation was recorded 40 days post-infection. For this assay, infection and co-cultivation times were held constant to isolate the effect of bacterial concentration.

Effect of infection time

Transformation efficiency was evaluated at different infection times (10, 20, and 30 min) using A. tumefaciens EHA105 (OD₆₀₀=0.6). Methods for bacterial preparation, co-cultivation, recovery, selection, and adventitious shoot assessment were followed as described in Sect. 2.4.1.

Effect of co-cultivation time

To evaluate the effect of co-cultivation duration, infected petioles were co-cultured for 1, 2, or 3 days. The Agrobacterium strain (EHA105), concentration (OD₆₀₀=0.6), and infection time (20 min) were held constant. Bacterial preparation and all subsequent steps were performed as detailed in Sect. 2.4.1.

Effect of different Agrobacterium strain

The transformation efficiencies mediated by EHA105 and GV3101 were compared, while keeping the bacterial concentration (OD₆₀₀ = 0.6), infection time (20 min), and co-cultivation period (2 days) constant. Procedures for bacterial preparation and subsequent steps followed the protocol in Sect. 2.4.1.

Molecular identification and expression analysis of transgenic ‘Siyang-1’

In this study, resistant shoots refer to shoots that survived on Kan-containing selection medium. Putative transgenic seedlings are regenerated plantlets derived from these resistant shoots after rooting. PCR-positive seedlings are those confirmed by genomic PCR to harbor the transgene(s). For molecular analysis, resistant shoots (~ 2 cm in height) were transferred to RM containing 50 mg·L⁻¹ Kan (Table 2) to generate plantlets for DNA/RNA extraction. Genomic DNA was isolated from the leaves of wild type (WT) and putative transgenic seedlings using the CTAB method [16]. PCR amplification was performed to detect the integration of the Bt genes using gene-specific primers (Cry 1Ac_F/R and Cry3A_F/R). The poplar Ubiquitin (UBQ) gene, amplified with primers UBQ_F/R, served as an internal positive control for DNA quality and reaction integrity.

Total RNA was extracted from leaves of WT and six randomly selected PCR-positive seedlings that harbored both Bt genes using the RNA Easy Fast Plant Tissue Kit (Tiangen, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. After treatment with DNase I, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into first-strand cDNA using the FastKing cDNA First Strand Synthesis Kit (Tiangen). Semi-quantitative RT-PCR was then conducted using specific primers for Cry1Ac (Cry1Ac_rtF/rtR), Cry3A (Cry3A_rtF/rtR), with the UBQ gene (UBQ_F/R) serving as an internal reference.

The sequences of all primers, reaction components, and thermocycling conditions for both PCR and semi-quantitative RT-PCR are provided in Table S1.

Assessment of insect resistance in transgenic ‘Siyang-1’ against H. cunea

Approximately two months after acclimatization and transfer to the greenhouse, the fifth to seventh fully expanded fresh leaves from the top of both WT and transgenic ‘Siyang-1’ seedlings (OE-2, OE-3, and OE-4) were collected for indoor leaf-feeding assays. Leaf discs (1.5 × 1.5 cm) were excised and placed in sterile Petri dishes lined with moistened filter paper to maintain humidity. Four leaf discs from the same transgenic line or WT plant were arranged in each dish. Twenty vigorously growing second-instar (L2) larvae of H. cunea were gently transferred onto the leaf discs in each dish using a soft brush. The dishes were then maintained in a climate-controlled chamber at 25 °C and a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod with a light intensity of 130 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹. After 48 h of feeding, the larvae were removed. The remaining leaf area of each disc was scanned, and the consumed leaf area was quantified using ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/ij/download.html). The feeding rate was calculated as (consumed area/ initial area) × 100%. All data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3). Statistical significance between the WT and each transgenic line was determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Quantification of Cry1Ac protein by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The expression level of Cry1Ac protein in the transgenic lines (OE-2, OE-3, and OE-4) was quantified by ELISA. The fourth fully expanded leaf from the top of seven-month-old seedlings was collected from each transgenic line and WT control. Total leaf protein was extracted, and Cry1Ac protein concentration was determined using a Cry1Ac-specific ELISA kit (Norminkoda Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Each transgenic line and the WT were measured in three independent biological replicates. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was performed as described in Section "Assessment of insect resistance in transgenic ‘Siyang-1’ against H. cunea".

Results

A whole plant regeneration system established via organogenesis

Adventitious bud differentiation from four types of explants (leaf discs, petioles, stem segments, and root segments) was evaluated under various hormone combinations of 6-BA, TDZ, and IAA at different concentrations. All explant types exhibited regenerative capacity via shoot organogenesis (Fig. 1A), although their responses varied depending on the hormone composition.

In the absence of IAA (SIM1-SIM9), TDZ enhanced regeneration across all explant types when 6-BA was applied at 0.1 mg·L⁻¹. However, a higher concentration of 6-BA (1 mg·L⁻¹) combined with TDZ generally led to reduced regeneration efficiency, particularly in leaf discs. Among these nine treatments, SIM5 (0.5 mg·L⁻¹ 6-BA and 0.002 mg·L⁻¹ TDZ) achieved the highest differentiation rate in leaf discs, stem segments, and root segments (Fig. 1B-E). When TDZ was replaced by IAA (SIM10-SIM18), the lowest differentiation rate was observed in SIM10 (0.1 mg·L⁻¹ 6-BA and 0.02 mg·L⁻¹ IAA), whereas SIM14 (0.5 mg·L⁻¹ 6-BA and 0.2 mg·L⁻¹ IAA) proved to be the most efficient across all explant types. Overall, SIM14 showed the highest regeneration efficiency among all 18 combinations tested, yielding 19.67 ± 2.52, 26 ± 2.65, 27 ± 1, and 28 ± 1 explants with adventitious buds from leaf discs, petioles, stem segments, and root segments, respectively (Fig. 1B-E and Table S2). Considering both regeneration efficiency and operational convenience, petioles were selected as the preferred explant for subsequent genetic transformation experiments.

The rooting responses of adventitious buds, apical buds and nodal segments were evaluated under various combinations of IBA and NAA (Fig. 1F-H). Without auxin supplementation, apical buds and nodal segments exhibited moderate rooting, with 19.33 ± 1.15 and 14.33 ± 1.53 rooted explants, respectively (Table S3). The addition of IBA, either alone or in combination with NAA, significantly promoted rooting across all explant types. Notably, the treatment containing 0.5 mg·L⁻¹ IBA without NAA (RM7) yielded the highest number of rooted explants in adventitious buds (17.33 ± 0.58), apical buds (29 ± 1), and nodal segments (27 ± 1) (Fig. 1F-H and Table S3). Therefore, RM7 was identified as the optimal rooting medium for ‘Siyang-1’.

Determination of the optimal kanamycin selection pressure

To establish an effective selection system for genetic transformation, the tolerance of petioles to Kan was evaluated. Adventitious bud regeneration was significantly inhibited by Kan in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2 and Table S4). In the absence of Kan, adventitious bud differentiation was observed in 25.67 ± 1.15 out of 30 explants. This number decreased sharply to 8.33 ± 1.53 at 10 mg·L⁻¹ Kan and further declined to 3 ± 1 at 30 mg·L⁻¹. Complete suppression of regeneration occurred at 50 mg·L⁻¹, with no explants forming adventitious buds (Table S4). Results indicated that 50 mg·L⁻¹ Kan was suitable for selecting transformed tissues in subsequent genetic transformation experiments.

Optimization of Agrobacterium-mediated transformation conditions

To establish an efficient genetic transformation system for ‘Siyang-1’, key parameters affecting transformation efficiency—including bacterial concentration, infection time, co-cultivation period, and Agrobacterium strain—were systematically evaluated using petioles and the binary vector N5. After infection and selection, petioles exhibited three distinct fates: producing resistant buds, becoming contaminated, or turning necrotic. The number of petioles in each category was recorded for every treatment (Table S5).

Effect of bacterial concentration

Under constant infection (20 min) and co-cultivation (2 days) conditions, Agrobacterium concentration significantly affected the regeneration frequency of resistant shoots (Fig. 3A). Petioles forming resistant shoots constituted the largest proportion across all OD600 levels, an OD600 of 0.6 yielded the highest number of petioles with resistant shoots (25.67 ± 0.58), which was significantly higher than those at OD600 0.2 (17 ± 2) and 1.0 (21.67 ± 1.53) (Table S5). Concurrently, the number of contaminated petioles increased with rising bacterial concentration, while the number of necrotic petioles showed an inverse trend (Fig. 3A).

Effect of infection time

Using an optimal OD600 of 0.6 and a 2-day co-cultivation, a 20-min infection resulted in the highest regeneration (25.67 ± 0.58), significantly outperforming both shorter (10 min, 13.67 ± 1.53) and longer (30 min, 15.67 ± 1.53) exposures (Fig. 3B). A shortened infection (10 min) led to a high number of necrotic explants (15 ± 1), while a prolonged duration (30 min) resulted in elevated bacterial overgrowth (6.67 ± 1.53) (Table S5).

Effect of co-cultivation time

Under optimized Agrobacterium concentration (OD600 0.6) and infection time (20 min), a 2-day co-cultivation period was most effective, supporting regeneration in 25.67 ± 0.58 petioles. Both shorter (1 day, 12.33 ± 1.53) and longer (3 days, 17 ± 1) durations significantly reduced shoot regeneration. The 1-day co-cultivation increased necrosis, and the 3-day treatment raised contamination levels (Fig. 3C)—a pattern similar to that observed under sub-optimal infection times.

Effect of Agrobacterium strain

Under optimized conditions (OD600 0.6, 20-min infection, 2-day co-cultivation), both A. tumefaciens strains EHA105 and GV3101 yielded similar numbers of petioles with resistant shoots (17.33 ± 1.53 and 18.33 ± 1.53, respectively). However, transformation with GV3101 led to a significantly higher incidence of bacterial overgrowth (9.33 ± 0.58) compared to EHA105 (4 ± 1) (Fig. 3D and Table S6).

Based on the above results, the highest regeneration frequency of resistant shoots with minimal contamination was achieved using A. tumefaciens strain EHA105 at an OD600 of 0.6, with a 20-min infection followed by a 2-day co-cultivation period. This optimized protocol was applied to generate transgenic ‘Siyang‑1’ expressing the dual Bt genes (Cry1Ac and Cry3A).

Molecular characterization and insect resistance evaluation of transgenic ‘Siyang‑1’

From a total of 165 petiole explants, 52 putative transgenic seedlings were regenerated using the optimized protocol. Genomic PCR analysis confirmed transgene integration in 22 of these seedlings, giving a PCR-positive rate of 42.31% (22/52). Based on the total number of explants, the true transformation efficiency (PCR-positive seedlings / total explants) was 13.33% (22/165). Among the 22 PCR-positive seedlings, 19 generated clear amplification products for both Bt genes (Fig. 4A), while three showed a signal for only one of the two genes. This may be attributed to partial T-DNA integration or transgene rearrangement, a phenomenon commonly observed in Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. Thus, among the 52 putative transgenic seedlings, 19 were identified to carry both Bt genes, corresponding to a frequency of 36.54%. To demonstrate transgene expression, six of the 19 seedlings were randomly selected and analyzed using semi‑quantitative RT‑PCR. Results indicated that both the Cry1Ac and Cry3A genes were successfully expressed in these transgenic plants (Fig. 4B).

Following acclimatization, four healthy and uniformly growing independent transgenic lines (OE‑2, OE‑3, OE‑4, and OE‑5), which were among the six randomly selected lines with confirmed transgene expression by semi‑quantitative RT‑PCR, were used for insect resistance bioassay against H. cunea. At 48 h post-infestation, leaf damage in all four transgenic lines was markedly reduced compared to the WT (Fig. 5A). Quantitative analysis showed that approximately 50% of the leaf area was consumed in WT plants, while in transgenic lines, consumption ranged from 5.28% to 12.66% — an extremely significant reduction (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5B and Table S7). To confirm the causal relationship between transgene expression and insect resistance, Cry1Ac protein levels were quantified in lines OE-2, OE-3, OE-4, and OE-5. ELISA analysis confirmed the presence of Cry1Ac protein in all four transgenic lines, with mean expression levels ranging from 46.93 to 53.95 ng·g⁻¹ fresh weight (Fig. 5C and Table S7). No Cry1Ac protein was detected in WT. The Cry1Ac protein levels in all transgenic lines were extremely significantly higher than those in WT (P < 0.001). Notably, a positive correlation was observed between Cry1Ac protein level and the reduction in leaf consumption among the transgenic lines.

Discussion

The regeneration capacity of explants is highly genotype-dependent and often constitutes the primary limiting factor for genetic transformation. While a variety of explants, such as callus [17], protoplasts [18], embryos [19], cotyledons [20], hypocotyls [21], and shoot apices [22], can serve as effective recipients for Agrobacterium-mediated transformation in many herbaceous crops, the range of responsive materials is more limited in woody species. In poplar, leaf discs and stem segments are among the most commonly used explants [23–25]. In this study, we found that for ‘Siyang-1’, petioles exhibited more consistent regeneration performance, producing the highest number of adventitious buds in the majority (8 out of 18) of the media formulations. Although root segments showed the highest regeneration number on the optimal medium (SIM14), petioles were ultimately selected for transformation. This decision was based on three practical considerations. First, roots grow embedded within the medium and are more laborious to harvest, whereas petioles are more accessible. Second, root segments are thinner and more fragile than petioles, making them more difficult to handle during Agrobacterium infection and blotting without tissue damage. Third, residual medium on root surfaces is difficult to remove completely even with sterile water rinses, leading to a higher risk of contamination during subsequent co-cultivation and selection.

Besides explant type, careful calibration of the PGR regime is necessary to maximize in vitro organogenesis. Cytokinins and auxins act as the primary regulators that orchestrate cell dedifferentiation and subsequent trans-differentiation, redirecting cells to alternative developmental fates [26]. The optimal balance between cytokinin and auxin varies significantly among different poplar genotypes. For instance, bud differentiation in the hybrid poplar 741 (P. alba×(P. davidiana + P. simonii)×P. tomentosa) is promoted by a medium containing 1.0 mg·L⁻¹ 6-BA, 0.05 mg·L⁻¹ IAA, and 0.01 mg·L⁻¹ TDZ [27], whereas gray poplar (P. tremula×P. alba) requires a formulation of 0.20 mg·L⁻¹ 6-BA, 0.10 mg·L⁻¹ IBA, and 0.001 mg·L⁻¹ TDZ [28]. The most efficient SIM for ‘Siyang-1’ employs a specific combination of a moderate cytokinin (0.5 mg·L⁻¹ 6-BA) and auxin (0.2 mg·L⁻¹ IAA). Thus, identifying a specific, highly responsive explant type combined with an optimal PGR formulation is critical for developing a reliable regeneration and transformation platform.

Agrobacterium-mediated transformation is the predominant method for plant genetic engineering. Its efficiency, however, is strongly influenced by a complex interplay of factors [29]. Our systematic optimization for ‘Siyang-1’ identified an OD₆₀₀ of 0.6, a 20-minute infection time, and a 2-day co-cultivation period with strain EHA105 as optimal, yielding the highest number of petioles producing resistant shoots. Any treatment that extended bacterium-explant contact—whether through higher bacterial density, prolonged infection, or extended co-cultivation—consistently led to more severe bacterial overgrowth. Excessive Agrobacterium proliferation has been widely reported in plant transformation, such as rice, wheat, tomato, and tobacco [30]. Although preventive strategies have been explored in several plant species [31–34], this issue remains difficult to eliminate completely, thereby constraining the overall transformation efficiency. To address this limitation, auxotrophic Agrobacterium strains have been developed [35]. These engineered strains can drastically reduce post-infection bacterial proliferation during plant transformation, thereby permitting extended co-cultivation and ultimately enhancing transformation frequencies [36–37]. Notably, a thymidine auxotrophic mutant of strain LBA4404 has been successfully implemented in poplar transformation [38]. In this study, from a total of 165 petiole explants, 22 PCR-positive seedlings were obtained, corresponding to a transformation efficiency of 13.33%. Further efforts employing auxotrophic Agrobacterium strains may therefore provide a viable strategy to increase the transformation efficiency of ‘Siyang-1’ by reducing contamination-related losses.

As the most abundant insecticidal proteins from Bacillus thuringiensis, Cry toxins have been the primary choice for developing insect-resistant transgenic poplar, with Cry1-class and Cry3-class genes deployed against lepidopteran and coleopteran pests, respectively [39]. This study generated transgenic ‘Siyang-1’ plants co-expressing Cry1Ac and Cry3A and demonstrated their strong insect resistance, evidenced by a drastic reduction in leaf area consumption by H. cunea larvae (5.28%–12.66% in transgenic lines vs. ~50% in wild-type plants). This high insect resistance was consistent with the functional efficacy of Cry1Ac toxin reported in P.× euramericana ‘Neva’ [40]. During the past decades, China has developed numerous transgenic poplar materials through introducing single or stacked Cry genes into various genotypes of Aigeiros and Populus Sect [41]. Several advanced lines have entered intermediate or environmental release trials, among which BtCry1Ac-transgenic P. nigra and BtCry1Ac + API-transgenic poplar 741 have obtained biosafety certificates [42]. Notably, China hosts the world’s only significant commercial plantations of Bt poplar [43]. Given the inherent advantage of male sterility in minimizing pollen-mediated transgene flow, the transgenic ‘Siyang-1’ lines developed here offer a promising addition to the existing insect-resistant poplar germplasms, with a potential biosafety benefit that warrants further evaluation. Future work should prioritize field trials to evaluate the agronomic performance, stability of insect resistance, and environmental biosafety of these lines under realistic conditions.

In addition, several limitations associated with this study should be acknowledged. First, the possibility of chimerism cannot be completely excluded, as the transgenic lines are T0 generation plants. A robust transformation protocol ideally requires evidence of stable inheritance (e.g., T1 segregation analysis); however, such analysis is not applicable to the male-sterile cultivar ‘Siyang-1’. Therefore, alternative approaches, including multi-year, multi-tissue and clonal propagation assays, would be beneficial to assess the long-term stability of transgene integration and expression in these T0 lines. Second, we acknowledge that accurate copy number and insertion site have not been determined. Future studies will include more rigorous integration analysis (e.g., Southern blot and high-efficiency thermal asymmetric interlaced PCR) to further characterize these transgenic lines. Third, insect bioassays were performed on only four transgenic lines; while the results were consistent and statistically significant, future studies with a larger number of independent lines would further strengthen the conclusions. Fourth, although the insect resistance phenotype correlated with Cry1Ac protein accumulation, the potential contribution of somaclonal variation to phenotypic differences cannot be entirely ruled out, as tissue culture can induce genetic and epigenetic changes in regenerated plants.

Conclusion

This study established an efficient and reliable Agrobacterium-mediated transformation system for the elite male-sterile poplar cultivar ‘Siyang-1’. The protocol comprises a robust whole-plant regeneration system using petiole explants, combined with key parameters: bacterial concentration (OD₆₀₀=0.6), infection time (20 min), co-cultivation period (2 days) with strain EHA105, and a kanamycin selection pressure (50 mg·L⁻¹). Using this optimized protocol, transgenic ‘Siyang-1’ lines co-expressing Cry1Ac and Cry3A were successfully generated, which demonstrated significantly enhanced resistance against H. cunea in leaf-feeding bioassays. This work directly addresses a key technical bottleneck for this commercially valuable cultivar and provides a promising and potentially biosafe insect-resistant germplasm resource. Furthermore, the established transformation system serves as a foundational tool for enabling precision breeding in ‘Siyang-1’ via gene-editing tools such as CRISPR-Cas9.
