Detection and classification of microplastics in green tea using SERS with gold nanoparticle substrates integrating chemometrics and deep learning
Authors: Rahul Joshi, Samir Adhikari, Donghan Lee, Byoung-Kwan Cho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGreen tea is consumed worldwide for its health-promoting properties, but it remains vulnerable to microplastic contamination during packaging and processing. Microplastics such as polystyrene (PS) and polyethylene terephthalate (PET) pose potential risks to human health and food safety, underscoring the need for effective detection methods. In this study, Surface-Enhanced Raman Scattering (SERS) using gold nanoparticle substrates was applied to detect and classify PS and PET contamination in four green tea powder varieties across the 400–1650 cm−1 spectral range. To classify the samples, we compared two chemometric techniques—Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) and Support Vector Machine (SVM)—with a deep learning approach namely one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D-CNN). Using optimized preprocessing, PLS-DA achieved perfect classification accuracy (100 %) for all tea varieties except Ryokucha. SVM also showed strong performance but with slightly reduced accuracies of 83.89 % for Matcha, 100 % for Jasmine, and 93.24 % for Sencha. Although the 1D-CNN model achieved higher validation accuracies—93.52 % (Matcha), 99.91 % (Jasmine), and 94.26 % (Sencha)—than SVM, its performance was still slightly lower compared to the PLS-DA models. Additionally, for unknown samples from distinct green tea varieties, the SERS–PLS-DA approach again delivered the highest validation accuracies of 100 % (Matcha), 99.80 % (Jasmine), and 96.46 % (Sencha), further demonstrating the strong potential of this technique. These findings confirm that SERS with gold nanoparticle substrates, especially when integrated with PLS-DA, provides a highly sensitive, non-destructive, and reliable platform for the rapid detection and classification of microplastic contamination in green tea.Keywords: Surface enhanced Raman scattering, Microplastics, Green tea powder, Chemometrics, One dimensional convolutional neural network, Polystyrene, Polyethylene terephthalate

Introduction

The global tea market has grown tremendously as the production has increased and human preferences have changed. China leads the world in tea production with 3.181 million tonnes, followed by India and a few other nations like Kenya, Sri Lanka, Vietnam, and Indonesia (The Global Tea Report, 2024). Tea production increased by 2 percent from 2022 and up 26 percent from the previous ten years, reaching 6.604 million metric tonnes in 2023. By 2023, the global tea consumption rose to 6.212 million tonnes, with China consuming over 3 million tonnes and continues to dominate other nations. Green tea derived from the Camellia sinensis plant family, is the most well-known tea and is ranked as the most popular and extensively consumed non-alcoholic beverage worldwide, after water (Dou, 2019). In addition to its pleasant taste, green tea is a great source of essential phytochemical components including flavonoids, catechins, alkaloids and phenolic compounds (Meyer et al., 2023). These compounds are responsible for providing green tea various health benefits, including improved cardiovascular health, weight management, skin health, stress reduction and more (Prasanth et al., 2019; Sinija and Mishra, 2008). In addition, the presence of catechin, and other components such as phenolic, and flavonoids, are further provides antioxidant properties (Hidalgo et al., 2010; Grzesik et al., 2018). However as green tea consumption rises across the world, the existence of harmful contaminants raises questions about public health and food security.

Microplastics, a term widely recognized due to the pervasive use of plastics in modern life, refer to small plastic particles that result from the breakdown of larger plastics materials. This fragmentation occurs through various environmental processes, including exposure to UV-B radiation, as well as biological and mechanical degradation (Andrady, 2011; Cooper and Corcoran, 2010; Corcoran et al., 2009). Plastic particles that range in dimension from 1 μm to less than 5 mm are often referred to as microplastics. However, Xu et al., 2020 state that they are commonly referred to as nanoplastics if their size falls between one and hundred nanometers. Among various polymeric forms, polystyrene (PS) and polyethylene terephthalate (PET) are frequently observed MPs. PS is commonly used due to its high tensile strength and chemical stability and is widely found in consumer products (Siddiqui et al., 2023). Reports indicate that PS contributes significantly to global MP pollution, accounting for approximately 7.1 % of annual plastic production (Khan et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2017). PET, on the other hand, is one of the most extensively produced plastics worldwide, with an annual production of 53.3 million tons (Zhang et al., 2019). It is broadly used in packaging materials, bottles, and containers, making its presence in food-related contamination highly probable (Lionetto and Esposito Corcione, 2021).

The presence of MPS in green tea is highly concerning due to their hazardous nature. Furthermore, the chemical composition and bioavailability of the tea bioactive components could be influenced by MPs' interaction with them. They have been associated with numerous health disorders that can lead to serious illnesses when living beings are exposed to them through ingestion or other forms of consumption. Researches has linked MP exposure to an increased risk of cardiovascular problems, including heart attacks, as well as potential neurotoxic effects. Studies have also raised concerns about the carcinogenic potential of MPs, along with their impact on reproductive health, respiratory function, and other critical bodily systems (Kozlov, 2024; Blackburn and Green, 2022; Lim, 2021). Future studies are necessary to determine how the presence of MPs affects the chemical composition of green tea, particularly how the particles interact with the tea's beneficial chemicals and the overall quality of the tea.

Due to their smaller sizes, and widespread distribution, MPs have been detected in soil, water, air, and various other environmental locations (Ziani et al., 2023). Through these pathways, MPs have been found in vegetable brassica sprouts (López et al., 2022), canned sardines and sprats (Karami et al., 2018), fish (Akhbarizadeh et al., 2020), milk (Basaran et al., 2023), and green tea (Xing et al., 2024). However, MPs are produced in green tea from a variety of sources at every stage of the production lines, with each one making a substantial contribution. Numerous studies highlighted the critical role that irrigation water, which frequently comes from river water polluted by industrial waste, plays in the contamination of tea leaves with MPs (Lee et al., 2024). Furthermore, processing steps such as rolling, and milling process are also essential factors responsible for MPs contamination through mechanical friction with processing equipment (Moita Neto and Silva, 2023). Xing et al. (2024), reported that MPs contamination was significantly higher during the rolling stage, in an amount of 2266 ± 1206 pcs/kg tea. Also, prolonged storage of green tea in plastic packaging materials can lead to leaching of MPs into the product, particularly under conditions of high humidity or temperature fluctuations (Yousefi et al., 2024). The examination of MPs through chemical based approaches such as pressurized liquid extraction and pyrolysis-gas chromatography (Dierkes et al., 2019), liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (Vuckovic et al., 2013), high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (Patnana et al., 2024) are generally accurate, offering superior detection performances. Also, methods such as thermogravimetric Analysis-Differential Scanning Calorimetry (Mansa and Zou, 2021), Flow Cytometry (C. Li et al., 2023), and atomic force microscopy (Luo et al., 2020) are also powerful for addressing the measurement of MPs. Despite their superior detection capabilities, these techniques are labor-intensive, hazardous, chemical-based, and complex to handle. To guarantee food safety, a more efficient, dependable, and non-destructive technique for detecting MPs in green tea must be created.

With the advancement of nanotechnology and instrumentation, surface enhance Raman spectroscopy (SERS) has emerged as a highly effective and promising analytical approach. In analytical chemistry (Fan et al., 2020), diagnostics (Blanco-Formoso and Alvarez-Puebla, 2020), food safety (Adhikari et al., 2024a; Joshi et al., 2023a), and environmental monitoring (Song et al., 2019). SERS has gained prominence because to its ability to identify target compounds at the single-molecule scale. Additionally, SERS has a higher detection limit and remarkable sensitivity, which makes it ideal for detecting MPs in complex food matrices like green tea. In contrast to the other chemical-based techniques stated above, SERS offers quick, non-destructive measurement with little sample preparation. SERS is a therefore considered as strong candidate for food safety and quality control in the detection of microplastics contamination because of these characteristics.

SERS offers high sensitivity for microplastic detection, but visual analysis of Raman spectra alone is insufficient for complex matrices like green tea. To overcome this limitation, implementation of chemometrics technique is essential for extracting the full information content from the SERS data. This techniques have been successfully employed in various disciplines such as food safety (Joshi et al., 2024; Alsammarraie and Lin, 2017), pharmaceutical quality measurement (Kandpal et al., 2016; Yuan et al., 2024), and even with microplastic studies (Q. Chen et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2025) with superior performances. In our earlier work, chemometrics based classification models demonstrated high classification accuracy for detecting microplastics (MPs) in SERS data from liquid herbal medicine samples (Joshi et al., 2025). Building on this, the present study extends the approach to a more complex food matrix—green tea powder—using a SERS gold nanoparticle substrate. Powdered food products are inherently heterogeneous, often generating stronger background interference than liquid samples. Specifically, green tea powder exhibits pronounced light scattering and contains a wide variety of bioactive compounds, both of which complicate spectral interpretation. These analytical challenges highlight the urgent need for rapid and reliable tools to monitor microplastic contamination in powdered food matrices.

Although our previous work using chemometric methods showed promising results, these traditional approaches often face challenges when dealing with the high dimensionality and complexity of spectroscopic data. Moreover, the need for extensive feature selection and preprocessing can limit their overall effectiveness. Deep learning, particularly one-dimensional convolutional neural networks (1D CNNs), has recently emerged as a powerful alternative for addressing these limitations. Unlike conventional methods, 1D CNNs integrate spectral preprocessing, feature extraction, and classification into a single, end-to-end architecture (Liu et al., 2017). This automated approach reduces manual intervention and improves spectral quality during model training. Furthermore, 1D CNNs offer advantages in handling complex datasets, simplifying model development, and extracting deeper insights (Rala Cordeiro et al., 2021; Alabsi et al., 2023). While 1D CNNs have achieved notable success in various food safety applications (Yu et al., 2020; Joshi et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2019), the combined potential of chemometric and deep learning methods for microplastics detection in complex food matrices, such as green tea, remains largely unexplored.

To address this gap, the present study evaluates the use of SERS gold nanoparticle substrates in combination with chemometric algorithms (PLS-DA and SVM) and a deep learning approach (1D CNN) for detecting and classifying two common microplastics—polystyrene (PS) and polyethylene terephthalate (PET)—in four types of green tea powders (Matcha, Jasmine, Sencha, and Ryokucha). The study also compares the classification performance of PLS-DA, SVM, and 1D CNN to identify the most effective approach for this challenging analytical task.

Materials and methods

Fabrication of gold nanoparticle SERS substrate

Surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) substrates are highly promising for molecular detection due to their ability to significantly enhance Raman signals. These substrates utilize electromagnetic field enhancements in nanogap regions between adjacent metal nanostructures, such as nanoparticles, nanorods, or patterned structures, referred to as hotspots. In these regions, localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) amplifies Raman signals from nearby analytes. For SERS sensor applications, quantitative evaluation requires uniform sensitivity across the entire substrate. Our fabrication method employs a simple and cost-effective approach using commercially available silicon wafers, gold deposition via an electron-beam (e-beam) evaporator, and rapid thermal annealing (RTA), avoiding expensive and time-consuming techniques like e-beam lithography. Despite variations in individual Au nanoparticle sizes, the statistical distribution of nanoparticles across the substrate after RTA is uniform, as discussed in our published work (Adhikari et al., 2024b). This uniformity ensures similar nanoparticle counts under the laser spot, resulting in Raman spectra variations below 10 %.

Various techniques can be employed to fabricate Au nanoparticles with controlled surface properties, size, and shape. In this study, Au SERS substrates were prepared by first depositing a gold film onto a Si/SiO2 surface using an electron-beam evaporator, followed by rapid thermal annealing at 850 °C for 2 min to induce nanoparticle formation. A schematic illustration of the Au nanoparticles formation process and their interparticle spacing is presented in Fig. 1(a and b), while the corresponding tilt-view and top-view SEM images are shown in Fig. 1(c and d). The nanoparticle size can be precisely tuned by adjusting the thickness of the initial gold film: a thinner film produces smaller Au nanospheres after annealing, whereas a thicker film yields larger ones. Furthermore, the nanoparticle size and density can be modified through single or double dewetting processes using films of varying thickness, as described in (Kim et al., 2023). This fabrication strategy—combining metal deposition with annealing—provides reliable control over both particle size and interparticle gaps. The average size of Au nanoparticles is 85 nm, as described in reference (Adhikari et al., 2024b). Interparticle distances were quantified from SEM images using ImageJ software. Measurements were carried out by evaluating end-to-end distances between individual nanoparticles. The average interparticle spacing was determined to be 144 ± 21 nm. We include this information and figure in Fig. 1e. This straightforward metal deposition and annealing method enables reasonable control over nanoparticle size and gap, making it an effective and reproducible approach for fabricating high-performance SERS substrates.

Preparation of microplastics spiked green tea samples

This study investigated the presence of widely distributed microplastics, specifically polystyrene (PS) and polyethylene terephthalate (PET), in four popular varieties of green tea samples, including Matcha, Sencha, Jasmine, and Ryokucha, purchased from nearby supermarkets in Daejeon, South Korea. During the MPs spiked green tea sample preparation, firstly 2 mg of MPs was dissolved in 20 ml of deionized water (DI) to prepare a stock solution of 0.1 g/L. To ensure a homogeneous solution mixing, the produced solution was placed in vials with snap caps and then shaken using a high-speed shaker (Vortex-Genie 2, Scientific Industries, Inc., model G560, Bohemia, NY, USA). In addition, each green tea powder sample 1 g of green tea powder were dissolved in 1 mL of DI water and mixed for 1 h using vortex mixture. (Final concentration is 1 g/mL). After this, certain amount of microplastic solution prepared before (10 μL) were added in some part of green tea solution (20 mL) and then mixed properly using vortex machine for 20 min. So the final concentration is 50 ng/mL. To change the hydrophilic nature of the gold nanoparticle substrate, first UV treatment has been given to the substrate for 30 min and on the following hydrophilic surface of the substrate. The sample preparation procedure is consistent with our past published work on microplastics detection on liquid herbal drinks (Joshi et al., 2025). In this study, a final concentration of 50 ng/mL was selected to align with our objective of detecting and classifying microplastics (MPs) in complex food matrices such as green tea powders without quantifying the analyte concentration. This concentration was also chosen to minimize interference from the sample matrix while ensuring sufficient signal for identification. Using this defined concentration, we aimed to assess the feasibility and robustness of our technique and determine its ability to effectively differentiate between two types of MPs, polystyrene (PS) and polyethylene terephthalate (PET).

Raman spectral data acquisition

A LabRAM HR-800 UV–visible–NIR microscope (Horiba Jobin Yvon) fitted with a multichannel air-cooled CCD detector was used to perform Raman scattering measurements. A 785 nm laser with an incident power of around 0.5 mW served as the excitation source. With an integration time of 10 s, a 50 × objective lens (N.A.: 0.75) was used for the SERS measurement. The laser beam's spot diameter on the SERS substrates was 1.28 μm. Without any signal degradation, data were gathered in the 400–1700 cm−1 spectral region. A silicon wafer was used to fix the alignment, and the spectra were set to zero to modify the baseline. Furthermore, no signal alteration or smoothing was done.

Deep learning based classification using one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D CNN)

A powerful deep learning method known as a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D CNN) was trained and optimized for the identification of PS and PET microplastics (MPs) in green tea samples using SERS Au NPs substrate. The input data used for model development were SERS spectral data, which are inherently one-dimensional, making them suitable for processing with 1D CNNs. The model consists of input layer, three convolutional layers (ConV1D_1,2, and 3) with increasing filter sizes (16, 32, and 64) and a kernel size of 3, each activated using the ELU (Exponential Linear Unit) activation function and followed by batch normalization and MaxPooling1D with a pool size of 2. Dropout layers with a rate of 0.2 were added after each pooling layer to prevent overfitting. A GlobalAveragePooling1D layer was used before move to dense layer with 128 units and ReLU activation, accompanied by L2 regularization (0.0001) and a higher dropout rate of 0.5. The final output layer uses softmax activation for multiclass classification. Spectral preprocessing and data processing using chemometrics

To effectively detect and classify two types of microplastics within four different green tea powders, we utilized two widely recognized chemometric classifiers: Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) and Support Vector Machine (SVM). This selection was deliberate, with PLS-DA serving as a linear model to specifically address the issue of collinearity often found in spectral datasets. In contrast, SVM was chosen to investigate whether the data possessed non-linear characteristics that a linear model might not capture. By comparing the performance of these two distinct approaches, our study offers a comprehensive evaluation of the most suitable chemometric methods for accurately detecting and classifying microplastics in a complex food matrix like green tea. Prior to model construction, the raw SERS spectra, which were affected by background noise, underwent a series of preprocessing steps designed to improve signal quality and prepare the data for training. These included normalization to correct for scale differences, Standard Normal Variate (SNV) transformation to reduce multiplicative scatter effects, Multiplicative Scatter Correction (MSC) to adjust the baseline, and Savitzky–Golay (SG) derivatives to enhance spectral features. For more theoretical details regarding preprocessing analysis, following article will be helpful (Rinnan et al., 2009).

Following preprocessing, the dataset was divided into training and testing sets, and both classification models were developed. PLS-DA, which extends partial least squares regression to categorical outcomes, is widely used for discriminating classes with distinct spectral signatures (Barker and Rayens, 2003). In this study, it was applied to distinguish pure green tea from samples spiked with polystyrene (PS) or polyethylene terephthalate (PET) microplastics. During PLS-DA model development, the model was initially optimized by selecting the ideal number of latent variables. In this study, 10 principal components were chosen based on cross-validation, which ensured maximum classification accuracy. Since PLS-DA is a supervised chemometric algorithm, three class labels were assigned: 0 for pure green tea, 1 for PS-spiked green tea, and 2 for PET-spiked green tea. After optimization, a threshold of 0.5 was applied to maximize class separation. Classification performance was visualized using score plots and summarized in accuracy tables. SVM, in contrast, is also a supervised classification method that excels at finding an optimal hyperplane that effectively separates different classes. This approach is particularly advantageous for high-dimensional and non-linear data, making SVM a robust tool for both classification and regression tasks (Long et al., 2016; X. Chen et al., 2023). For a deeper understanding of the mathematical underpinnings and background of SVM, readers are directed to the following articles (Cortes and Vapnik, 1995; Vapnik N., 1995). In this study, the SVM classifier was optimized through hyperparameter tuning, with the penalty parameter (C) set to 0.1, the gamma parameter set to ‘scale,’ and the radial basis function (RBF) chosen as the kernel for its strong capability to handle nonlinear relationships in the data. By comparing the performance of PLS-DA and SVM, this study provides a comprehensive evaluation of chemometric approaches for microplastic detection in green tea. Results and discussion

MPs SERS spectrum on gold nanoparticles substrate

The SERS spectra of polystyrene (PS) and polyethylene terephthalate (PET) on a gold nanoparticle substrate in the 400–1700 cm−1 spectral range shown in Fig. 4(a and b). The primary Raman peaks for PS MPs were determined to be at 620 cm−1, 1001 cm−1, 1031 cm−1, and 1602 cm−1 as a result of ring breathing mode, ring deformation mode, C-H in-plane deformation, and ring skeletal stretch. Nevertheless, a lesser intensity peak was also seen at 1450 cm−1 as a result of CH2 scissoring, which is often thought to have less of an impact than the spectral peaks mentioned above (Bridges et al., 2003; Mazilu et al., 2010). The SERS spectrum of polyethylene terephthalate (PET) (Fig. 2) exhibits several distinct Raman peaks at 626 cm−1, 998 cm−1, 1182 cm−1, 1293 cm−1, 1453 cm−1, 1580 cm−1, and 1607 cm−1. These peaks are attributed to specific vibrational modes, including CCC in-plane bending (ring) at 626 cm−1, C-O stretching in O-CH2 at 998 cm−1, a combination of ring in-plane C-H bond and C–C stretch at 1182 cm−1, C(O)-O stretching at 1293 cm−1, a combination of CH2 bending and OCH bending at 1453 cm−1, aromatic ring stretching at 1580 cm−1, and C=C stretching at 1607 cm−1. These peak assignments are consistent with previously reported data (Feng et al., 2023; Rebollar et al., 2014; Jin et al., 2022).

Augmentation of SERS data using Dirichlet distribution

The development of reliable classification models strongly depends on the size and diversity of the training dataset. In the present study, the initial SERS dataset was limited, consisting of only 180 spectral samples (60 each for pure green tea powder and two types of microplastic-spiked samples across three groups). Such a modest dataset is often insufficient for training robust chemometrics and deep learning models and may lead to underfitting due to limited variability. To overcome this limitation and significantly enhance dataset diversity, we performed spectral data augmentation using a method based on the Dirichlet distribution, following the mathematical framework proposed by Minka, 2000. This technique generates synthetic spectra by creating realistic linear combinations of the limited real data. By applying probability weights derived from the Dirichlet distribution to existing spectral components, we effectively simulate a broad range of concentration and matrix variations expected in real-world samples. Using this approach, we generated 3600 artificial spectra, dramatically increasing the dataset size and mitigating potential underfitting.

Subsequently, these augmented datasets were used to construct chemometric and deep learning classification models. Similar augmentation strategies have been successfully applied in non-destructive spectroscopic techniques such as FT-IR, & FT-NIR carbon dot-based sensors, hyperspectral imaging, Raman, and SERS for complex matrices including Arabidopsis plant powders (Joshi et al., 2022), pesticide (Bhattacharya et al., 2024), soils (Patel et al., 2020), essential oils (Joshi et al., 2023), and food products (Adhikari et al., 2024a) demonstrating improved model performance. Motivated by these successes, we applied the Dirichlet-based augmentation to address data scarcity in our study. We acknowledge, however, that linear mixing alone cannot fully capture all chemical, matrix, and interaction effects present in real samples. To better account for non-linear interactions and the underlying physical–chemical characteristics of the spectra, future work will incorporate non-linear augmentation models and validate synthetic spectra against experimentally prepared microplastic-spiked green tea powders. Furthermore, we plan to acquire high-quality, reproducible spectra using SERS gold nanogap substrates for both pure microplastics and pure green tea, ensuring that future augmented datasets are informed by precise and physically meaningful measurements. Such approaches will guarantee that augmented data capture the essential physical and chemical features of the samples while facilitating robust model development. Fig. 8 illustrates the application of the Dirichlet-based data augmentation process, where original samples in Fig. 8a shows SERS spectra generated from two replicates of polystyrene-contaminated green tea samples (PS + Matcha_1 and PS + Matcha_2), while sample without noise on Fig. 8b presents artificially generated SERS spectra produced using the Dirichlet distribution, representing noise-free augmented and preprocessed spectra derived from the original samples. This workflow effectively increases dataset size, improves model training robustness, and supports the reliable classification of MP contamination in tea products.

Chemometrics based classification model using SVM classifier

Since distinct SERS spectral peaks of polystyrene (PS) and polyethylene terephthalate (PET) were not observed in the Ryokucha green tea sample, the classification of PS and PET microplastics was conducted using Matcha, Jasmine, and Sencha green tea samples. A Support Vector Machine (SVM), a widely used chemometric algorithm, was employed for this purpose. Before training, the input features were standardized using standard scaling as a preprocessing step. Hyperparameter tuning was also performed to optimize model performance, with the penalty parameter (C) set to 0.1, the gamma parameter set to ‘scale,’ and the radial basis function (RBF) selected as the kernel. The RBF-SVM model achieved a training accuracy of 85.19 % and a testing accuracy of 83.89 % for the Matcha_PS + PET samples. For Jasmine_PS + PET samples, the model demonstrated nearly perfect performance, achieving 99.96 % and 100 % accuracy for training and testing, respectively. Similarly, the Sencha_PS + PET samples showed strong results, with training and testing accuracies of 93.09 % and 93.24 %. 1D CNN based classification model development for PS and PET microplastics classification

While the developed Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) and Support Vector Machine (SVM) model was able to successfully and efficiently classify both polystyrene (PS) and polyethylene terephthalate (PET) microplastics in three distinct green tea samples, a deep learning-based classification model was created using 1D CNN to further improve the robustness and reliability of the classification results and performed comparative analysis. A similar number of 3600 samples were also utilized for 1D CNN model development and training and testing sets were divided in 70:30 ratios. Based on this splitting ratios, 2500 samples allocated for training and the remaining 1100 for testing. Standard metrics, such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, were used to examine the 1D-CNN model's classification performance in order to provide a thorough evaluation of its classification power and generalization to new data. For detailed model architecture and parameter settings, please refer to Section 2.4. Separate 1D CNN models were trained for each green tea sample type and compared with the previously developed rbf-SVM models. For the Matcha_PS + PET dataset, the 1D CNN achieved training and testing accuracies of 93.09 % and 93.52 %, respectively, and completely outerperformed SVM model which acquired lower classification accuracies of 85.69 % and 86.50 % for both sets. In the Jasmine_PS + PET dataset, it performed almost similar to SVM, with nearly perfect accuracies of 99.96 % (training) and 99.91 % (testing). Similarly, for the Sencha_PS + PET dataset, the model yielded accuracies of 93.49 % and 94.26 %. However, when compared, 1 D CNN classification completely outerperformed SVM models and achieved higher classification accuracies for all the three types of PS and PET spiked green teas. Spectral interpretation for PS, and PET contaminated green tea for model validation

To further evaluate and validate the classification accuracy of the developed PLS-DA model in detecting polystyrene (PS) and polyethylene terephthalate (PET) microplastics within complex powdered tea matrices, four additional distinct green tea varieties—Matcha, Sencha, Jasmine, and Ryokucha—considered as unknown set of samples were selected for analysis. The sample preparation and SERS measurement of these validation samples strictly followed the procedures outlined in Sections 2.2, 2.3, ensuring methodological consistency. The acquired SERS spectra are presented in Fig. 12, with panels (a–d) corresponding to PS-contaminated samples and panels (e–h) representing PET-contaminated samples. For PS contamination, clear and distinguishable spectral peaks characteristic of PS, as previously described in Section 3.3 were observed in the Matcha, Sencha, and Jasmine samples (Fig. 12a–c). While the peak positions exhibited slight shifts and the intensities were lower compared to the reference spectra, these deviations did not hinder the identification process. In contrast, the Ryokucha sample (Fig. 12d) failed to display discernible PS signals, as the tea matrix produced strong interfering Raman bands that overlapped with the target PS peaks, masking their detection. A similar trend was noted for PET contamination. In Matcha, Sencha, and Jasmine samples (Fig. 12e–g), the distinct PET spectral signatures described in Section 3.4 were readily identifiable, albeit with minor peak shifts likely caused by interactions between PET and the sample matrix. However, PET peaks were not detectable in the Ryokucha sample (Fig. 12h), again due to matrix interference that obscured the relevant spectral features. Despite minor shifts in peak positions and variations in intensity, PS and PET contamination was clearly identifiable in three of the four tested green tea varieties—Matcha, Sencha, and Jasmine—based on direct SERS spectral analysis. In contrast, detection in Ryokucha was hindered by strong background signals from the tea matrix, which masked the characteristic spectral peaks of the target microplastics.

The findings clearly demonstrate that the SERS gold nanoparticle substrate enables the rapid detection of microplastics in three different green tea varieties. This confirms that the developed gold nanoparticle SERS substrate, when coupled with PLS-DA models and appropriate preprocessing methods, performs effectively even when applied to previously untested sample types. Compared with conventional spectroscopic techniques such as infrared (IR) (Morgado et al., 2021), Raman (Jin et al., 2022), and ultraviolet (UV) (Tsuchida et al., 2024), which are traditionally used for microplastic (MP) identification, SERS offers a distinct advantage by providing highly sensitive, trace-level detection. Previous studies have successfully applied SERS for MP analysis in food (Kousheh et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025) and water (Luo et al., 2022; Shan et al., 2023), consistently reporting excellent detection performance.

While this study focused on detecting only two microplastics—PS and PET—due to their high production volumes and widespread applications as mentioned on the introductory part, we recognize that these polymers represent only a part of the broader microplastic problem. Moreover, this study did not fully investigate the absence of detectable PS and PET signals in the Ryokucha samples. Potential reasons for this limitation include matrix effects from the green tea powder and weaker interactions between the tea components and the SERS gold nanoparticle substrate. This remains an important drawback of the current work. To address this issue, future research will incorporate a wider variety of microplastic types and perform quantitative experiments using samples with different microplastic concentrations across various powdered food matrices.

Furthermore, assessing the sensitivity gradient and detection limit constitutes a vital next phase of our research. While the current study is limited to assessing the feasibility of detecting a single concentration, subsequent experiments will enable the development of robust predictive models for accurate quantitative analysis. These models will leverage widely adopted chemometric techniques, including partial least squares regression (PLSR) and support vector regression (SVR), to rigorously determine these essential parameters.

Furthermore, advanced analytical techniques, such as high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS), will be employed to confirm the presence of microplastics and to investigate in detail how key chemical constituents of green tea, such as phenolic and flavonoid compounds, influence detection. Such studies will offer a more comprehensive understanding of microplastic behavior in complex food matrices. Finally, advanced deep learning approaches, such as residual network architectures, and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) will be incorporated to improve prediction accuracy and reliability, supported by larger-scale datasets.

Conclusions

This research presents a rapid and accurate approach for detecting microplastics—specifically polystyrene (PS) and polyethylene terephthalate (PET)—in powdered green tea using Surface-Enhanced Raman Spectroscopy (SERS) with gold nanoparticle substrates. Characteristic spectral features were observed in the 400–1700 cm−1 range for Matcha, Sencha, and Jasmine teas, while Ryokucha samples showed no detectable microplastic signals due to interference from their natural chemical components. For data analysis and classification of samples, three methods were compared: two chemometrics based namely Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA), and Support Vector Machine (SVM), and one deep learning based i.e., one-dimensional convolutional neural networks (1D-CNN). PLS-DA outperformed the other two approaches, consistently achieving 100 % accuracy with no misclassifications. The findings of this research strongly emphasize the effectiveness and reliability of combining Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) with Surface-Enhanced Raman Spectroscopy (SERS) for the rapid detection of microplastics within complex food samples. The study further demonstrated that the gold nanoparticle SERS substrate, integrated with PLS-DA chemometric modeling, holds significant promise for accurately identifying and classifying unknown green tea powder samples. This entire methodological framework offers a non-destructive, highly reliable approach that is readily extendable to monitoring microplastics in a variety of other food products, thereby providing a valuable pathway for advancing consumer product safety and environmental monitoring.
