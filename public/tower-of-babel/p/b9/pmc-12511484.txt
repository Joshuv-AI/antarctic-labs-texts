Function Over Form: A Longitudinal Study of Predictors and Mediation Pathways for Disordered Eating in Elite Athletes
Authors: Scott J. Fatt, Phillipa Hay, Emma George, Nikki Jeacocke, Kris Rogers, Deborah Mitchison
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aetiological pathway from societal pressure to meet appearance ideals to disordered eating through appearance-based concerns (e.g., drive for thinness) is well established in the general population; however, its relevance for elite athletes is unclear. This study investigated longitudinal predictors and mediation pathways for disordered eating in athletes, including sports and societal pressures as risk factors, and appearance-based concerns and athlete-specific cognitions as mediators.MethodsElite athletes predominantly from Australia and the US completed online surveys at baseline between March-September 2023 (T1, N = 178, 72.4% female, Mage = 23.9, SDage = 7.0), with follow-ups at 6-months (T2, N = 110) and 12-months (T3, N = 91). The survey assessed disordered eating, weight pressures in sport, societal pressures for appearance, athlete-specific mediators (drive for leanness for performance, athletic identity), and body image concerns. Regression analyses employing multiple imputation were used to examine longitudinal predictors and mediation pathways with disordered eating as the outcome, adjusting for autocorrelations and gender.ResultsIncreased disordered eating over 12-months was significantly predicted by higher levels of T1-weight pressures in sport (β = 0.18, p = .046) and T1-drive for leanness for performance (β = 0.21, p = .025). T1-Societal pressures, T1-athletic identity, and T1-body image concerns did not significantly predict increases in disordered eating over time. None of the indirect pathways were significant.ConclusionsIt appears that weight pressures in sport and drive for leanness for performance contribute more to risk of disordered eating in elite athletes than traditional pathways of societal pressures and body image concerns. These findings, together with findings from previous research, were synthesised into a new model of disordered eating in elite athletes, providing targets for future intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40798-025-00920-y.

Introduction

Disordered eating refers to clinically impairing and/or distressing disturbances in cognitions, behaviours, and affect associated with eating, exercise, and body composition/appearance [1]. This definition includes clinical eating disorders [2], but also a broad spectrum of disordered eating outside of these diagnostic criteria [3, 4]. Elite athletes (i.e., those competing at a national, international, professional, or NCAA D1 level [5]) are at elevated risk for disordered eating (including eating disorders), with higher prevalence versus non-athletes [6] and severe health complications such as relative energy deficiency in sport [7]. Evidence-based interventions for eating disorders are available, which target psychological mechanisms guided by theoretical models developed for the general population [8, 9]. However, elite athletes often experience unique pressures within the elite sporting environment regarding their eating, exercise, and bodies [10], which may contribute to the onset and maintenance of disordered eating beyond the factors included in existing models. Clarification of these athlete-specific factors would allow for the development of more targeted interventions.

Common between many aetiological models of disordered eating in the general population is a pathway from (1) pressures for appearance ideals, to (2) cognitive disturbances inflating the importance of these appearance ideals, to (3) body image concerns, and (4) disordered eating [11]. For example, Stice’s [12] dual-pathway model theorised that the internalisation of societal appearance-ideals leads to disordered eating through body dissatisfaction. Petrie and Greenleaf [13] included these same appearance-based mechanisms (i.e., internalisation and body dissatisfaction) in their athlete-specific model of disordered eating. They also added the role of sports pressures – hypothesising that the appearance-based mechanisms were dually influenced by pressures from within (sports pressures) and outside (societal pressures) the sporting environment. Empirical support for these appearance-based mechanisms in elite athletes, however, is tenuous. In a study of athletes of mixed competitive level, social media pressures did predict greater disordered eating over 12 months, indirectly through appearance internalisation [14]. However, higher levels of body dissatisfaction did not predict greater disordered eating over time in this study [14], nor several other longitudinal studies of elite athletes [15, 16]. Further, multiple findings suggest that elite athletes often experience equivalent or lower body image concerns versus non-athletes, even when simultaneously reporting relatively higher disordered eating symptoms, suggesting other mechanisms are at play [17–19]. Further investigation is needed regarding pathways from societal and sport pressures to disordered eating through appearance-based concerns in elite athletes.

Recent findings also indicate that athlete-specific cognitions may be aetiologically relevant for disordered eating. In qualitative studies, athletes often attributed disordered eating to a pursuit of leanness to improve performance (“drive for leanness for performance”, DLP) and views of “being an athlete” as the most important part of one’s identity (“athletic identity”) [10]. Cross-sectionally, disordered eating in athletes has been linked to greater DLP [20] and athletic identity [21], even when controlling for appearance-based concerns [22]. Longitudinally, higher DLP predicted greater disordered eating over 12 months in elite collegiate athletes [16]. Further, in a cross-sectional study of adolescent elite athletes, the relationship between sports pressures and disordered eating was mediated by DLP [20]. DLP and athletic identity may be potential mechanisms for disordered eating in elite athletes, but these pathways have not yet been investigated longitudinally.

This study aimed to (1) investigate longitudinal predictors of disordered eating in elite athletes, (2) investigate the mediation pathways by which disordered eating develops in this population. Clarification of these pathways will inform theoretical models and provide targets for athlete-specific interventions. It was hypothesised that greater sports pressures would predict increased disordered eating over time, mediated through (a) DLP, (b) athletic identity, and (c) body image concerns, and greater societal pressures would predict increased disordered eating over time, mediated through (d) body image concerns (Fig. 1).

Method

Patient and Public Involvement

The research design was informed by athletes’ voices through qualitative studies [10] and collaborations with stakeholders, including consultation with expert clinicians from peak sports organisations in Australia (e.g., Paralympics Australia, Australian Institute of Sport). These collaborations also guided the interpretation and dissemination of findings.

Participants and Procedures

Participants were from the first three waves (baseline [T1], 6-month follow-up [T2], 12-month follow-up [T3]) of the ASPIRE (Addressing Sporting Pressures on athletes’ body Image and Relationships with Eating and exercise) study – a longitudinal online survey. Methods for the baseline survey have been reported previously [22]. Current adult athletes who had competed at a national, international, professional, or NCAA D1 level were recruited through advertising via elite sporting organisations (predominantly in Australia and at a D1 college in the US), social media, and snowball sampling between March-September 2023. Although 2960 respondents consented to the baseline survey, many of these were identified as non-legitimate spam responses (Supplementary 1) leaving 178 current athletes who passed the attention check (Mage=23.9, SDage=7.0; 72.4% female). Gender was assessed via self-report, with the options of “male”, “female”, “I identify my gender as: (please specify)” with an open-ended text box, or “prefer not to say”. However, the sample of athletes who identified as non-binary gender was too small to be included as a distinct group (i.e., one participant) and so their gender was coded as “missing” for the final analyses. Participants provided their contact details for the follow-up surveys (text message and/or email), with four reminders at weekly intervals for each timepoint. Some T3 surveys were concurrent with the 2024 Paris Olympic Games and the reminders were delayed until the completion of the Games for N = 12 participants. Participants opted to receive either a $20 AUD ($15 USD) gift card or go in the draw to win a $100 AUD gift card for T1 participation, and the option to receive either a $15 AUD ($10 USD) gift card or go in the draw to win a $100 AUD gift card for each follow-up survey. Between September 2023-March 2024, 110 athletes (61.5% of baseline) participated in T2, and 91 athletes (50.8% of baseline) participated in T3 between March-September 2024. Measures

Disordered Eating

Disordered eating was assessed via the Athletic Disordered Eating (ADE) [23, 24]. Participants responded to 17-items about current symptoms on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = Never to 4 = Always) with total scores summed. The ADE has previously been validated in adult male and female athletes [23, 24], and demonstrated adequate internal consistency across timepoints in the current study (McDonald’s Ω = 0.90, 0.90, 0.90).

Societal Pressures

Societal pressure for appearance was assessed via the pressures from media and family subscales of the Sociocultural Attitudes Towards Appearance Questionnaire-4-Revised (SATAQ-4R). Adult male and adult female versions of the SATAQ-4R have been validated in the general population [25]. Participants were asked to indicate their current level of agreement with each item on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Definitely disagree to 5 = Definitely agree), with responses summed for each subscale. The SATAQ-4R demonstrated adequate internal consistency across timepoints in the current study (McDonald’s Ω=[male: 0.85, 0.90, 0.87], [female: 0.85, 0.89, 0.91]).

Sports Pressures

Body-focused pressures within sport were assessed via the Weight Pressure in Sport (WPS) 12-item male [26] and 11-item female [27] versions. Participants were asked to indicate their current level of agreement with each item on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = Never to 6 = Always), with responses averaged. The WPS scales have previously been validated in adult athletes [26, 27], and demonstrated adequate internal consistency across timepoints in the current study (McDonald’s Ω=[males: 0.86, 0.83, 0.86], [females: 0.86, 0.82, 0.83]).

Body Image Concerns

Body image concerns were assessed via the Multifaceted Instrument for Body Image Disturbance (MI-BoD [28]). Participants were asked to indicate how frequently they experienced each item over the past 28 days using a 6-point Likert scale (1 = Never to 6 = Always), with responses summed across the 20 items. The MI-BoD has previously been validated in adult male and females [28], and demonstrated adequate internal consistency across timepoints in the current study (McDonald’s Ω = 0.98, 0.98, 0.98).

Athlete-Specific Mechanisms

DLP and athletic identity were each assessed using subscales from the ATHLETE questionnaire [29]. Participants were asked to indicate their current level of agreement with each item on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly disagree to 5 = Strongly agree), with responses summed across 4 items for the DLP (ATHLETE-DLP) subscale, short form [20], and 6 items for the athletic identity subscale (ATHLETE-AI). The ATHLETE-DLP short form was validated in male and female adolescent athletes [20] and the ATHLETE-AI in female adult athletes [29], each demonstrating adequate internal consistency across timepoints in the current study (McDonald’s Ω=[ATHLETE-DLP: 0.80, 0.81, 0.81], [ATHLETE-AI: 0.81, 0.78, 0.82]).

Analysis Plan

The statistical analyses align with the Checklist for statistical Assessment of Medical Papers [30]. Of the 178 athletes who participated in the study, 174 had any data relevant for the current study, with complete data for N = 157 at baseline, N = 91 at T2, and N = 80 at T3. Multiple imputation is a method shown to reduce bias in estimates for longitudinal analyses with missing data, particularly when data is not missing-not-at-random [31]. Investigations of the descriptive statistics for the baseline variables revealed no systematic differences between athletes with complete data versus those who dropped out (see Supplementary 2). Thus, multiple imputation was used including all participants with any non-missing data. Fifty imputed datasets were generated using chained equations via predictive mean matching, including (1) the variables in the analyses and (2) an auxiliary variable measuring functional impairment due to disordered eating (Clinical Impairment Assessment [32]) at each timepoint. Multiple imputation allowed for analyses to be conducted on the full dataset (N = 178), sufficient to achieve 80% power for small to moderate effect sizes in longitudinal mediation [33]. To examine the longitudinal predictors of disordered eating (i.e., aim 1), a series of multiple regression models were conducted using the imputed datasets, with the risk factors (sports pressures, societal pressures) and mediators (DLP, athletic identity, body image concerns) included as predictors, and disordered eating at T2 and T3 included as outcomes. Each regression model controlled for autocorrelations and gender. Assumption checks for the regression models were conducted on (1) the non-imputed dataset and (2) the first imputed dataset, with all assumptions met and no issues of multicollinearity detected (all tolerance at or above 0.27). For aim 2, the mediation pathways were investigated using the imputed data-sets via regression-based causal mediation analyses [34], with T3-disordered eating as the outcome. Gender was included as a covariate, alongside T1-disordered eating and the respective T1-mediator to investigate if the risk factors (X) led to a change in disordered eating (Y) via a change in the mediator (M).

Mediation Analyses

Results from each of the mediation analyses are reported in Table 3. The total effect of T1-weight pressures in sport on T3-disordered eating was significant when including T2-athletic identity as a mediator and T1-disordered eating, T1-athletic identity, and gender as covariates. Further, the direct effect of T1-weight pressures in sport on T3-disordered eating was significant when including T2-body image concerns as a mediator and T1-disordered eating, T1-body image concerns, and gender as covariates. Contrary to our hypotheses, none of the indirect effects were significant. Table 4 presents the results from the multiple regression models for each of the X to M pathways, controlling for autocorrelations and gender. None of these relationships were significant, although the T1-weight pressures in sport to T2-DLP relationship was marginal, controlling for T1-DLP and gender.

Discussion

This study investigated (1) longitudinal predictors of disordered eating in elite athletes, and (2) the mediation pathways of sport and societal pressures. For aim 1, when controlling for autocorrelations and gender, increased disordered eating over time was significantly predicted by greater weight pressures in sport and DLP at baseline, but not societal pressures, athletic identity, or body image concerns. For aim 2, contrary to our hypotheses, none of the indirect pathways were significant indicating that none of the variables studied significantly mediated these relationships.

These findings support a divergence for elite athletes from the general population regarding aetiological mechanisms for disordered eating, highlighting the role of cognitions focused on performance rather than appearance. Although eating disorders in the general population are generally thought to be driven by appearance-based concerns [11], body image concerns did not play a direct or indirect role in predicting disordered eating over time in the current study, congruent with previous longitudinal studies of elite athletes [15, 16]. Similarly, societal pressures for appearance did not predict increased disordered eating over time. These findings provide further support that the relationship between body image concerns and disordered eating is more complex for athletes than the established relationship in the general population [35]. Qualitative findings suggest that body image duality might contribute to this complexity, with athletes reporting separate appearance ideals within versus outside the sporting context which can sometimes be incongruent [10]. Body dissatisfaction was measured as a general construct in this study, and further research is needed investigating how the multiple facets of body image might predict disordered eating over time in athletes.

DLP did, however, predict greater increases in disordered eating, providing further evidence for its role as a potential aetiological mechanism [16, 20, 22]. Theoretically, DLP may be similar to the core mechanism of ‘overvaluation of weight and shape’ in the transdiagnostic model of eating disorders [36]. Fairburn, Cooper [36] proposed that eating disorders are maintained by a dysfunction of self-evaluation, wherein one’s value is derived almost exclusively from weight, shape, and appearance. Elite athletes may experience similar overvaluation, directed, however, towards athletic performance rather than appearance per se. DLP occurs when this overvaluation is coupled with oversimplified beliefs about the role of body weight/composition on performance, leading to disordered eating. These cognitions can be reinforced within the sporting environment (e.g., coaches’ remarks about “looking fast”) [37] and/or temporary improvements in performance associated with initial changes in body composition [38]. Exacerbations in disordered eating may occur over time as athletes pursue further leanness, particularly if the initial improvements in performance begin to plateau or regress. This aetiological mechanism aligns with athletes’ qualitative reports that disordered eating is often motivated by perceived performance benefits rather than appearance-based concerns [10].

The role of DLP as a mediator for the relationship between sports pressures and disordered eating is less clear. Whilst Krentz and Warschburger [20] demonstrated a cross-sectional indirect relationship between weight-pressures in sport and disordered eating through DLP, we did not find longitudinal support for this mediating pathway. This may have been due to the marginal X to M relationship, with weight pressures in sport predicting increased disordered eating over 12-months but not increased DLP over 6 months. Notably, scores on the WPS scale were consistent over time (i.e., strongly correlated across timepoints) and a longer period of time and/or a larger change in the sporting environment may be required to produce changes in DLP over six months (e.g., athletes’ first entry into elite sport, changing coaches or sports organisations, reducing pressures via intervention). Similarly, athletic identity did not mediate the relationship between weight pressures in sport and disordered eating, nor did it predict significant changes in disordered eating directly, despite previously cross-sectional associations [21, 22]. Measuring one’s “athletic identity” as a whole may have been too broad to predict changes in disordered eating over 12 months, and future research should instead investigate specific aspects of the athletic identity (e.g., valuing “looking like an athlete” [39]).

Taken together, these findings supported some aspects of Petrie and Greenleaf’s [13] model of disordered eating but also propose several modifications for a new hypothesised model (Fig. 2). Whilst Petrie and Greenleaf’s model provided a pivotal starting point in exploring the aetiological differences between athletes and the general population, its development did not follow an inductive approach grounded in empirical findings of athletes [13]. It is unsurprising, therefore, that its fit has not been well supported when investigated in various athlete samples [40–42]. Instead, the model was derived from established models of eating disorders in the general population, with the intention to be modified as further empirical evidence emerged [43]. Accordingly, the hypothesised model in Fig. 2 is derived from empirical findings of athlete-specific risk factors for disordered eating, including the current longitudinal study and previous qualitative [10], cross-sectional [6, 20, 22, 40], and longitudinal studies [14–16, 42]. The pathway from sports pressures (weight pressures in sport) to disordered eating was supported in the current study and previously [15, 40], although not mediated through body image concerns. An additional pathway from DLP to disordered eating is warranted [16, 20]. It is also theoretically reasonable that weight pressures in sport would lead to greater DLP; however, this relationship was weak in the current study and requires further investigation. The longitudinal pathway from societal pressures to disordered eating through body image concerns remains unsupported in elite athletes; however, there is some support for this pathway in athletes of mixed competitive level [14]. Although Petrie and Greenleaf’s model was intended to apply to athletes generally, it is possible that competition level moderates these pathways, with athlete specific factors (weight pressures in sport, DLP) more relevant for athletes immersed in the elite sporting environment and appearance-based concerns (societal pressures, body image concerns) more relevant for non-elite athletes.

Practical Implications

The hypothesised model emphasises weight pressures in sport and DLP as critical targets for athlete-specific interventions. Notably, both of these factors were commonly experienced by athletes in the current study, with ratings, on average, between 2.5 and 3 (out of 1–5) across each time point. This level of endorsement was consistent with previous findings [16, 40], suggesting that these experiences are widespread across athlete populations. A whole-of-sport approach will likely be most effective in addressing these factors [44]. Individuals may be targeted through educational programs addressing eating disorder literacy, body-focused language, and messaging about the relationship between leanness and performance. Sports organisations hold important responsibility to review policies and practices aimed at reducing weight pressures and DLP, such as guidelines for safe monitoring of athletes’ weight and other biophysiological metrics (e.g [45]). Similarly, screening for DLP may promote early identification and prevention of disordered eating in athletes. International bodies should review rules and regulations which reinforce weight pressures and/or DLP (e.g., sporting attire [46] or procedures for determining weight-classes). We point to several publications which provide more comprehensive guidelines for reducing these pressures [44, 47, 48].

Evidence-based interventions may also require modification to address cognitions about performance rather than appearance. For example, The Body Project is a group-based intervention targeting the internalisation of appearance ideals, with demonstrated effectiveness for preventing eating disorders in the general population [49, 50]. In athletes, however, the results have been relatively limited [51–53]. Although the intervention was adapted for athletes across these trials, the internalisation of appearance ideals was maintained as the targeted mechanism. A focus instead on DLP may produce better outcomes. Similarly, Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for eating disorders (CBT-E) is an individual treatment which targets the overvaluation of weight, shape, and appearance [36]. Whilst CBT-E has not been evaluated specifically in elite athletes, modifications of this treatment for this population could include targeting overvaluation of body weight/composition for performance reasons, rather than appearance.

Limitations

Efforts were made to recruit a diverse sample of elite athletes, including across gender and sport-type, and para athletes. A strength of the study was the inclusion gender as a covariate in analyses, providing tentative support of these findings as relevant for both female and male athletes. However, the sample size was not sufficient in the current study to investigate whether gender moderated these relationships. However, the current sample was still biased towards female and able-bodied athletes, and was also predominantly from Australia and the US. Further investigations of these findings are needed across diverse athlete demographics and various timeframes. Similarly, the sample was limited to adult elite athletes to include those most immersed in the sporting environment [10] and these pathways require investigation in sub-elite and young developing athletes. The sample may also have been biased by participant attrition across timepoints and the large number of suspected spam responses which were excluded. Although methodological and statistical measures were undertaken to reduce this risk (outlined above), replication of these findings is needed. Additionally, DLP was measured using the 4-item short-form subscale from the ATHLETE [20] rather than the full 12-item subscale [29] to reduce athletes’ response burden. The use of the full subscale in future studies may reduce measurement error, clarifying the marginal weight pressures in sport to DLP pathway. Finally, Petrie and Greenleaf’s [13] model proposed several other factors relating to the aetiology of disordered eating in athletes, such as negative affect and various moderators (e.g., perfectionism, self-esteem). The factors included in the current study were based on findings from the baseline sample [22], focusing on mediating pathways as potential targets for intervention. Future studies should consider the role of these other factors for these pathways.

Conclusions

Increased disordered eating over 12 months was predicted by greater weight pressures in sport and DLP at baseline, but not societal pressures, athletic identity, or body image concerns, in a sample of elite athletes. None of the mediating pathways were supported. These findings suggest that existing theoretical models of disordered eating in the general population may not be directly translatable to elite athletes. We proposed a new model of disordered eating in elite athletes based on the current findings and previous literature, indicating targets for intervention which should be a priority for sporting organisations and the focus of future research.
