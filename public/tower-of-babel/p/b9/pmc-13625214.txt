Virtual reality simulation for chemotherapy drug spill management training in pharmacy education: A cohort-allocated crossover quasi-experimental study in Taiwan
Authors: Yen-Lin Chang, Yi-Chen Lin, Liang-Ching Hung, Hsiu-Mei Chen, Tsu-Yi Hsieh, Chih-Ho Yen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHazardous drugs such as antineoplastic agents pose occupational risks to healthcare workers, making appropriate chemotherapy spill management essential for workplace safety. Hands-on training opportunities in pharmacy education are often limited by safety concerns and resource constraints. Virtual reality (VR) simulation may provide immersive, guided rehearsal without hazardous-drug exposure, but comparative evidence remains limited.ObjectiveThis study examined changes in pharmacy interns’ self-perceived knowledge, understanding, and ability following VR simulation–based training and traditional instructor-led instruction and explored whether short-term changes differed between the two cohort-defined instructional orders.MethodsThis cohort-allocated crossover quasi-experimental study included 37 pharmacy interns at a tertiary medical center in Taiwan. Instructional order was assigned by training cohort rather than by individual randomization and was completely confounded with cohort and calendar period. Cohort 1 (n = 17) received instructor-led instruction followed by VR training; Cohort 2 (n = 20) received the reverse order. Four single-item domains—knowledge of spill-kit contents, understanding of spill-management procedures, ability to independently don personal protective equipment (PPE), and ability to independently manage a spill—were assessed using a 5-point response scale at baseline, after the first intervention, and after crossover. Non-parametric tests were used.ResultsAfter the first session, self-perceived scores increased significantly across all four items in both cohorts (all p < 0.001). The instructor-led-first cohort showed greater first-phase changes than the VR-first cohort in self-perceived knowledge of spill-kit contents (p = 0.006) and ability to independently don PPE (p = 0.047); differences in the other two items were not statistically significant. These findings could not be attributed to instructional modality alone. Following crossover, second-phase change scores did not differ significantly between the cohort-defined orders (p = 0.221–0.956). All participants met the prespecified satisfaction criterion for the VR training (37/37, 100.0%; rating ≥ 4/5); 27 (73.0%) identified instructor-led explanation combined with VR as the most helpful option, and 28 (75.7%) indicated that VR could not replace instructor-led explanation.ConclusionsBoth first-session instructional approaches were followed by short-term increases in pharmacy interns’ self-perceived knowledge, understanding, and ability. Because instructional order was completely confounded with cohort and calendar period, the absence of significant second-phase differences neither establishes equivalence between the orders nor rules out an instructional-order effect. VR may serve a complementary rather than competing instructional role and provide a safe approach for rehearsing hazardous-drug spill-management procedures. These conclusions are limited to short-term self-perceived outcomes and post-training learner perceptions.Keywords: Virtual reality simulation, Pharmacy education, Chemotherapy spill management, Crossover study, Quasi-experimental design

Introduction

Hazardous drugs, including antineoplastic agents, pose significant occupational risks to healthcare workers because exposure may result in acute toxicity and long-term adverse health effects [1]. Chemotherapy drug spills represent a critical safety concern in clinical practice, as improper handling may result in occupational exposure through dermal contact or inhalation, as well as environmental contamination [2]. To mitigate these risks, national and institutional guidelines emphasize appropriate spill-management practices, including the correct use of personal protective equipment (PPE) and spill kits and adherence to standardized response procedures [1]. Preparing pharmacy trainees to respond appropriately to chemotherapy spills is therefore important for both pharmacy education and workplace safety.

Despite the recognized importance of chemotherapy spill-management training, opportunities for repeated hands-on practice in pharmacy education may be limited. Hands-on training involving actual cytotoxic agents is constrained by safety and regulatory requirements, which limit their routine use in educational settings and require strict engineering controls and appropriate personal protective equipment [1].

The infrequent occurrence of real-world chemotherapy spill events, together with the need for specialized equipment, trained instructors, and protected training time, further limits opportunities for repeated experiential practice [3]. Traditional instructor-led approaches may include didactic instruction, procedural demonstrations, and supervised hands-on practice when resources permit. These approaches are valuable for introducing procedural knowledge and safety principles; however, resource and scheduling constraints may limit the frequency and repeatability of experiential practice and skill rehearsal. Such constraints may affect learners’ perceived preparedness for managing high-risk clinical situations. Opportunities for simulation-based and experiential training have been associated with greater learner confidence in clinical pharmacy activities [4].

Simulation-based education offers a potential approach to these challenges by providing safe, controlled environments for practicing complex and safety-critical tasks. Virtual reality (VR)–based simulation, in particular, can provide immersive, guided learning experiences that support procedural rehearsal, clinical decision-making, and feedback without exposure to real-world hazards. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reported that VR-based training can produce comparable or favorable outcomes in some health professions education settings, including outcomes related to procedural knowledge, skills, and learner confidence [5, 6].

Beyond its potential effects on learning outcomes, VR-based simulation may also offer potential implementation benefits, including greater instructional flexibility and repeatability. Digital simulation platforms may support repeated practice and, in some educational settings, provide greater scheduling flexibility or opportunities for asynchronous learning [7, 8]. Such flexibility may be particularly relevant in clinical training environments where protected teaching time is limited and clinical service demands compete with educational activities.

In oncology-related training, randomized controlled trials of VR-based instruction for chemotherapy administration have reported favorable outcomes across measures of knowledge, procedural performance, and learner satisfaction compared with conventional educational approaches [9, 10]. Beyond oncology-specific procedures, VR simulation has been associated with improved preparedness for chemical disaster response, another high-risk, low-frequency context in which opportunities for real-world practice are limited and errors may have serious consequences [11]. A recent randomized clinical trial also found immersive VR training to be non-inferior to face-to-face instruction for teaching objectively assessed PPE donning and doffing skills, providing indirect support for the potential use of VR in safety-critical training relevant to hazardous-drug handling [12].

Despite the growing evidence supporting VR-based education, evidence specific to the use of VR for chemotherapy spill-management training in pharmacy education remains limited. The order in which VR simulation-based training and instructor-led instruction are delivered may also be relevant to short-term learning responses. Evidence from simulation-based and health professions education suggests that the effects of instructional order may vary according to task complexity, learner experience, and educational context, with mixed findings across studies [13–15].

Despite its potential educational benefits, immersive VR simulation also presents limitations that should be considered when designing and implementing training programs. Immersive environments may impose additional extraneous cognitive load on novice learners, particularly when the interface, equipment, or instructional format is unfamiliar [16]. Practical implementation may also be limited by hardware and software costs, infrastructure and technical-support requirements, and differences in learners’ familiarity with and tolerance of immersive technologies, including cybersickness and other simulator-related discomfort [17, 18]. These considerations support evaluating VR as a complementary component of a broader instructional strategy rather than assuming that it can replace established teaching approaches.

The present study assessed learners’ self-perceived knowledge, understanding, and ability rather than objectively measured competence or observed procedural performance. Although such self-assessments provide information about how learners perceive their own knowledge and abilities, they are conceptually distinct from demonstrated competence and should not be interpreted as evidence of objective skill performance.

To address these gaps, this study aimed to examine changes in pharmacy interns’ self-perceived knowledge, understanding, and ability following VR simulation–based training and traditional instructor-led instruction. Using a cohort-allocated crossover quasi-experimental design, the study also explored whether short-term changes differed between the two cohort-defined instructional orders.

Methods

Study design

This cohort-allocated crossover quasi-experimental study involving two instructional modalities was conducted at Taichung Veterans General Hospital (TCVGH), Taiwan, between January 2020 and May 2021. Participant training and outcome data collection were conducted across two training cohorts: the first from July to September 2020 and the second from November 2020 to March 2021. The study examined short-term changes in learners’ self-perceived knowledge, understanding, and ability following virtual reality (VR) simulation–based training and traditional instructor-led instruction for chemotherapy spill management. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling. Instructional order was assigned by training cohort rather than by individual randomization. Participants in the first training cohort received traditional instructor-led instruction followed by VR simulation–based training, whereas participants in the second training cohort received VR simulation–based training followed by traditional instructor-led instruction. Consequently, instructional order was completely confounded with training cohort and calendar period because each instructional order was delivered to only one cohort during a distinct time period. For clarity, the first cohort (instructor-led first) was designated Cohort 1, and the second cohort (VR first) was designated Cohort 2.

Participants

A total of 37 pharmacy interns training at the study institution were enrolled in the study. No a priori sample-size calculation was performed; the sample size was determined by the number of pharmacy interns enrolled during the two data-collection periods. Participants were excluded if they had previously completed formal chemotherapy spill-management training or had incomplete questionnaire data, including a missing post-test assessment. All 37 enrolled participants completed the assessments at baseline, after the first intervention, and after the crossover, and all were included in the analysis. Self-reported prior related coursework, such as dispensing-practice courses, was recorded separately and was not considered equivalent to formal chemotherapy spill-management training. Cohort 1 comprised 17 participants, and Cohort 2 comprised 20 participants.

Participants were undergraduate pharmacy interns from four institutions. Participants from China Medical University, Chia Nan University of Pharmacy and Science, and Tajen University were enrolled in five-year pharmacy programs and were completing their fifth-year internships at the time of training. Participants from National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University (NYCU) were enrolled in a six-year pharmacy program and were completing their fourth-year internships at the time of training. All 37 participants were completing internships at the study institution. Information on prior related coursework was obtained by participant self-report; however, the duration of any prior hospital internships or clinical rotations was unavailable in the study records.

Intervention

Traditional instructor-led training

Participants received a 60-minute face-to-face training session conducted in the oncology pharmacy and delivered by the same senior oncology pharmacist with more than 5 years of clinical experience. The session included a didactic presentation on the principles and procedures of chemotherapy spill management, demonstration of PPE donning, step-by-step demonstration of spill-management procedures using a standardized spill kit, and supervised hands-on practice.

VR simulation–based training

Participants received a 60-minute immersive training session using a course-specific chemotherapy spill-management module delivered through the VIVEPAPER platform. According to the available internal course-design document, the module was produced for the Department of Pharmacy at Taichung Veterans General Hospital with technical support from DeepQ/HTC VIVEPAPER.

Based on the available course-design documentation, participants progressed through a predetermined, guided instructional sequence. The module included a structured course menu; 360-degree instructional videos showing the oncology pharmacy environment, PPE use, and spill-management procedures; written and visual instructional materials; and embedded knowledge checks consisting of multiple-choice questions, image-based identification items, and procedural-ordering tasks. Participants received immediate correct/incorrect feedback, could re-attempt an item after an incorrect response, and proceeded after providing a correct response. This format provided a consistent, guided approach to reviewing spill-management procedures without exposure to hazardous drugs.

The module did not include free navigation, manipulation of virtual objects, automated performance scoring, or adaptive feedback. The available records did not document the platform’s commercial or licensing status, the software version used in the study, or any prior external technical or educational validation. Accordingly, the module was treated as a course-specific instructional resource rather than as an externally validated training platform.

The VR training was delivered using an HTC VIVE Pro head-mounted display with handheld controllers. According to the manufacturer’s system-requirement documentation, the minimum requirements included an Intel Core i5-4590 or AMD FX 8350–equivalent processor, an NVIDIA GeForce GTX 970 or AMD Radeon R9 290–equivalent graphics card, at least 4 GB of RAM, DisplayPort 1.2 or later, and one USB 3.0 port or later. The recommended graphics configuration included an NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1070, NVIDIA Quadro P5000, or AMD Radeon Vega 56 or higher. These manufacturer-stated requirements do not represent the verified specifications of the computer used in the study; the actual computer configuration was not retained in the study records.

Both instructional modalities were aligned with institutional spill-management guidelines and national safety standards. The two modalities addressed the same core learning objectives and spill-management procedures, although their instructional activities and feedback mechanisms differed. All participants received the 60-minute VR session using the same core digital content and predetermined instructional sequence. Operational assistance was provided in a consistent manner by the same facilitator across both cohorts. Intervention fidelity was not formally evaluated using a predefined checklist or structured observational record. Accordingly, the term “standardized” refers to the planned consistency of content, duration, instructional sequence, and operational assistance, rather than to independently verified fidelity.

Data collection instruments

A self-administered questionnaire assessed four domains of self-perceived knowledge, understanding, and ability related to chemotherapy spill management. Each item was rated on a 5-point response scale: 1 = very poor, 2 = poor, 3 = average, 4 = good, and 5 = very good. Higher scores indicated a more favorable self-assessment of the domain addressed by the item. Each domain was assessed by a single item representing a distinct aspect of self-perceived knowledge, understanding, or ability; the four items were not intended to measure a single underlying construct or to be combined into an overall score.

The four items assessed the following domains: (Q1) self-perceived knowledge of the contents of the chemotherapy spill kit; (Q2) self-perceived understanding of the procedures for managing an accidental chemotherapy drug spill; (Q3) self-perceived ability to independently don the required personal protective equipment (PPE) in the event of a chemotherapy drug spill; and (Q4) self-perceived ability to independently manage an accidental chemotherapy drug spill. The instrument was developed as a course-specific measure of self-perceived outcomes and had not undergone comprehensive psychometric validation beyond the content-validity assessment described below.

Before the formal content-validity assessment, the research team reviewed and revised all seven items—the four self-perceived outcome items and the three post-training perception questions—for wording, relevance, and alignment with institutional spill-management procedures and training objectives. Five pharmacy education experts subsequently evaluated the content validity of the final revised seven-item set. Each expert rated the appropriateness of each item using a 4-point ordinal scale, with 1 indicating “very inappropriate” and 4 indicating “very appropriate”; higher scores represented greater item appropriateness. Ratings of 3 or 4 were treated as agreement that an item had adequate content validity.

The scale-level content validity indices calculated using the average method (S-CVI/Ave) and the universal-agreement method (S-CVI/UA) were both 1.00. These results indicated that all five experts rated every item as having adequate content validity in the final revised questionnaire. Separate S-CVI values were not calculated for the four self-perceived outcome items and the three post-training perception questions. The indices therefore reflect expert agreement on the final revised version after preliminary review and revision and do not constitute evidence of comprehensive psychometric validation.

Because the four outcome domains were assessed using separate single items representing distinct constructs, they did not form a multi-item scale measuring a common underlying construct. Therefore, internal-consistency reliability across the four items, such as Cronbach’s α, was not considered meaningful and was not calculated. No test–retest reliability or other formal reliability assessment was conducted.

After completing both instructional modalities, all 37 participants completed three course-specific post-training perception questions. These questions were evaluated by the same five experts as part of the single content-validity assessment described above. The first question assessed overall satisfaction with the VR simulation-based training using a 5-point response scale: 1 = very dissatisfied, 2 = dissatisfied, 3 = neutral, 4 = satisfied, and 5 = very satisfied. Responses of 4 or 5 were classified as indicating satisfaction. The second question asked which instructional approach participants considered most helpful: combined instructor-led explanation and VR simulation-based training, VR simulation-based training alone, or instructor-led explanation alone. The third question asked whether participants believed that VR simulation-based training could replace instructor-led, in-person explanation, with “yes” and “no” response options.

Numerical values assigned to responses to the second and third questions were used only as category codes. These responses were summarized using frequencies and percentages and were not analyzed as continuous or ordinal scores. The three questions were analyzed separately and were not combined into an overall score. These three course-specific questions were not intended to constitute a single psychometric scale. The four learning-outcome items and three post-training perception questions measured self-reported outcomes; no objectively observed performance assessment was conducted.

Procedure

After being informed of the study aims and procedures, participants received the instructional order designated for their training cohort, as described under Study design. Blinding of participants and instructors was not feasible because of the readily apparent differences between the two instructional modalities. Before receiving either instructional modality, all participants completed the four-item self-perceived outcome questionnaire as the baseline assessment (T0). Cohort 1 then received traditional instructor-led instruction, whereas Cohort 2 received VR simulation-based training. Immediately after completing the first instructional modality, participants completed the same four-item questionnaire as the first post-training assessment (T1).

During the exploratory second phase, Cohort 1 subsequently received VR simulation-based training, whereas Cohort 2 received traditional instructor-led instruction; thus, both cohorts completed the two modalities sequentially. No planned washout period was included between the two instructional phases. After completing the second instructional modality, participants completed the same four-item questionnaire as the final post-training assessment (T2). After completing both instructional modalities, participants completed the three post-training perception questions described above. Data analysis

Data were analyzed using non-parametric statistical methods because the outcomes were measured on ordinal response scales and the sample size was relatively small. Descriptive statistics were reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical baseline characteristics, including sex and self-reported prior related coursework, were compared between cohorts using Fisher’s exact test.

The Mann–Whitney U test was used for between-cohort comparisons of baseline scores, first-phase change scores, and second-phase change scores (Tables 1, 4, and 5, respectively). Within each cohort, changes from baseline (T0) to the first post-training assessment (T1) were evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired observations (Tables 2 and 3). Change scores were calculated to quantify the change occurring during each instructional phase. First-phase change was defined as T1 minus T0, and second-phase change was defined as T2 minus T1. Positive values indicated higher self-perceived scores.

The second phase was analyzed exploratorily to determine whether changes from T1 to T2 differed between the two cohort-defined instructional sequences. Second-phase change scores were compared between cohorts using the Mann–Whitney U test (Table 5). Because instructional sequence was completely confounded with training cohort and calendar period, and because no washout period was included, sequence, period, and carryover effects could not be separately or validly estimated. Accordingly, the second-phase comparisons were treated as exploratory. Responses to the three post-training perception questions were summarized separately using frequencies and percentages.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. No adjustment was made for multiple comparisons. Exact p values were reported, except that values below 0.001 were presented as p < 0.001.

Results

Participants

Figure 1 summarizes participant flow, cohort allocation, the two instructional phases, and the three assessment time points. A total of 37 pharmacy interns were included: 17 in Cohort 1 (instructor-led first) and 20 in Cohort 2 (VR first). All 37 participants completed the assessments at baseline (T0), after the first instructional modality (T1), and after the second instructional modality (T2), and all were included in the analysis. No statistically significant between-cohort differences were observed in sex distribution (Fisher’s exact test, p = 1.00) or self-reported prior related coursework (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.48).

Baseline self-perceived outcomes

At baseline, Cohort 2 reported a higher median score than Cohort 1 for Q1, which assessed self-perceived knowledge of spill-kit contents (3.0 vs. 2.0, p = 0.012; Table 1). No statistically significant between-cohort differences were observed for self-perceived understanding of spill-management procedures (Q2, p = 0.103), self-perceived ability to independently don PPE (Q3, p = 0.277), or self-perceived ability to independently manage a chemotherapy spill (Q4, p = 0.158).

First-phase changes following traditional instructor-led instruction in cohort 1

In Cohort 1, self-perceived outcome scores increased significantly from baseline (T0) to the first post-training assessment (T1) for all four items following traditional instructor-led instruction (Table 2). The median change score was + 2 for each item (Q1–Q4), and all four within-cohort changes were statistically significant (all p < 0.001).

First-phase changes following VR simulation-based training in cohort 2

In Cohort 2, self-perceived outcome scores increased significantly from baseline (T0) to the first post-training assessment (T1) for all four items following VR simulation-based training (Table 3). The median change score was + 1 for Q1–Q3 and + 1.5 for Q4, and all four within-cohort changes were statistically significant (all p < 0.001).

Between-cohort comparison of first-phase change scores

First-phase change scores were greater in Cohort 1 than in Cohort 2 for Q1 (median change, 2.0 vs. 1.0; p = 0.006) and Q3 (2.0 vs. 1.0; p = 0.047) (Table 4). No statistically significant between-cohort differences were observed for Q2 (p = 0.115) or Q4 (p = 0.242). Because the first instructional modality was completely confounded with training cohort and calendar period, the observed differences cannot be attributed to instructional modality alone.

Between-cohort comparison of second-phase change scores

Following the crossover, no statistically significant between-cohort differences in second-phase change scores (T2 − T1) were observed for any of the four items (p values ranged from 0.221 to 0.956; Table 5). Median second-phase change scores were 0.0 in both cohorts for Q1–Q3; for Q4, the median change was 1.0 in Cohort 1 and 0.0 in Cohort 2. Because instructional sequence was completely confounded with training cohort and calendar period, these findings do not establish equivalence between the two instructional sequences.

Post-training perceptions

All 37 participants met the prespecified criterion for satisfaction with the VR simulation-based training (score ≥ 4 of 5); 34 participants (91.9%) selected “very satisfied,” and three (8.1%) selected “satisfied.” When asked which instructional approach was most helpful, 27 participants (73.0%) selected combined instructor-led explanation and VR simulation-based training, seven (18.9%) selected VR simulation-based training alone, and three (8.1%) selected instructor-led explanation alone. Twenty-eight participants (75.7%) indicated that VR simulation-based training could not replace instructor-led, in-person explanation, whereas nine (24.3%) indicated that it could. These responses reflect participants’ satisfaction and perceptions of instructional helpfulness and replaceability; they do not demonstrate that the combined approach produced superior learning outcomes.

Discussion

This cohort-allocated crossover quasi-experimental study examined short-term changes in pharmacy interns’ self-perceived knowledge, understanding, and ability following VR simulation–based training and traditional instructor-led instruction. Both cohorts showed significant increases in all four self-perceived outcome scores after the first instructional modality. In the first-phase comparison, the instructor-led-first cohort showed greater changes in self-perceived knowledge of spill-kit contents and ability to independently don PPE; however, these differences cannot be attributed to instructional modality alone because modality was confounded with training cohort and calendar period. Following crossover, second-phase change scores did not differ significantly between the two cohort-defined instructional orders. This finding does not establish equivalence between the orders or exclude an instructional-order effect. Collectively, these findings suggest that VR simulation and traditional instruction may serve complementary rather than competing roles in hazardous-drug training for pharmacy learners.

Because all outcomes were self-reported, increases in scores should be interpreted as changes in perceived knowledge, understanding, and ability rather than demonstrated competence. In simulation-based education, self-assessment and observed performance do not necessarily correspond; therefore, higher self-perceived scores should not be interpreted as evidence of objective skill acquisition.

Among the four assessed domains, self-perceived ability to independently don PPE warrants particular attention because this domain showed a statistically significant between-cohort difference after the first instructional session (Table 4), and PPE donning is a discrete, safety-critical procedural skill that permits comparison with existing VR-based PPE training literature. The greater first-phase change reported by the instructor-led-first cohort may partly reflect the benefits of direct demonstration and immediate instructor feedback for novices learning a precisely sequenced procedure. This interpretation is consistent with prior studies showing that structured demonstration and guided instruction support early skill acquisition in safety-focused tasks [14, 15]. However, this interpretation remains tentative because the outcome was self-perceived rather than observed and because the first instructional modality was confounded with training cohort and calendar period. Consistent with the broader PPE-training literature, a recent randomized clinical trial demonstrated that immersive VR training was non-inferior to face-to-face instruction for teaching PPE donning and doffing skills, supporting the potential role of VR in PPE-related training [12].

Although prior studies have suggested that instructional order may influence learning outcomes, findings have varied across educational contexts [13–15, 19–21]. Differences in reported effects may reflect variations in task complexity, learner experience, instructional design, and outcome measures. For example, some studies focusing on complex technical tasks or more advanced learners have reported greater skill transfer or longer-term retention with simulation-first approaches [13, 14, 19], whereas studies of structured procedural tasks and short-term performance have reported minimal or no differences by instructional order [15, 20, 21].

In the present study, second-phase change scores did not differ significantly between the two cohort-defined instructional orders following the crossover. A nonsignificant finding, however, does not establish equivalence between the two instructional orders. Several plausible explanations, which are not mutually exclusive, should be considered. First, instructional order was completely confounded with training cohort and calendar period; therefore, any observed difference—or absence of a difference—could reflect instructional order, cohort characteristics, calendar-period influences, or a combination of these factors. Second, many participants already reported high scores after the first intervention, leaving little scope for further improvement during the second phase; correspondingly, most median second-phase change scores were zero or close to zero. Third, because both cohorts received both modalities sequentially without a washout period, the cumulative effects of exposure to both modalities and residual carryover from the first phase could not be excluded. Fourth, the small sample reduced statistical power to detect between-cohort differences. Finally, the study assessed immediate self-perceived outcomes rather than objective performance or skill transfer. Taken together, these considerations support viewing exposure to both modalities as a pragmatic curricular option for teaching high-risk, infrequently encountered safety procedures rather than as evidence favoring either instructional order. Nevertheless, the present design cannot determine whether instructional order affects outcomes.

The differing patterns of self-perceived change observed after the first instructional session may partly reflect the distinct educational affordances of the two modalities, although modality effects cannot be separated from cohort or calendar-period influences in the present study. Prior literature has suggested that simulation-based and VR-enhanced training may be particularly useful for high-risk, low-frequency clinical scenarios because they permit safe, repeated rehearsal without exposure to real-world hazards [22, 23]. In chemotherapy spill-management training, the opportunity to rehearse procedures without hazardous-drug exposure may strengthen learners’ self-perceived knowledge and ability in complex, safety-critical situations. By contrast, instructor-led demonstration provides direct observation and immediate feedback, which may be particularly helpful for novices learning equipment-oriented or precisely sequenced safety procedures. These potential mechanisms were not directly assessed in the present study. Immersive VR simulation is consistent with experiential learning principles by allowing learners to engage in decision-making, rehearse procedures, and apply knowledge in a contextualized setting [23]. Such features may support learners’ self-perceived ability to manage spills independently; however, the present study did not determine whether these perceptions translated into greater autonomy or objectively demonstrated competence.

Although the present study cannot identify an optimal instructional order, VR could be considered at different points within a structured training program, depending on curricular and logistical needs. Such curricular flexibility may be practically relevant for pharmacy education programs with limited instructor availability, access to physical training materials, or opportunities for repeated hands-on practice.

Most participants’ identification of a combined instructional approach as most helpful is broadly consistent with existing literature on blended learning in health professions education. Systematic reviews and overviews have generally reported favorable educational outcomes for blended learning models that integrate face-to-face instruction with digital or simulation-based modalities, although the interventions and outcomes evaluated across studies have varied [24, 25]. In the present study, 27 of 37 participants (73.0%) identified instructor-led explanation combined with VR simulation-based training as the most helpful option. However, selecting the combined approach as most helpful does not demonstrate that it produces superior objective learning outcomes. Nevertheless, learner acceptability is a relevant implementation consideration when introducing training for high-risk, infrequently encountered clinical scenarios, even though engagement and motivation were not directly assessed in this study.

Because the present study did not establish that either modality was superior or that the two modalities were equivalent, curriculum decisions should not be based on comparative-effectiveness claims from these data alone. Practical considerations, such as potential flexibility and scalability, implementation requirements, and local resource constraints, may nevertheless inform decisions about how VR is integrated into existing training programs.

VR-based training may also offer several potential implementation advantages. Compared with exclusively instructor-led sessions, VR-based modules may provide greater scheduling flexibility and, in some educational contexts, support asynchronous learning. However, the VR intervention in the present study was delivered during scheduled training sessions with standardized operational assistance; therefore, asynchronous delivery was not evaluated. More broadly, asynchronous learning has been widely discussed as a flexible educational strategy in contemporary health professions education, particularly for adult learners balancing clinical responsibilities [7, 8]. Empirical evidence further suggests that, when thoughtfully designed and implemented, asynchronous formats may support learner engagement and self-directed learning [26, 27].

VR-based modules may, in principle, support repeated rehearsal of procedural steps without consuming additional spill-management training materials. However, repeated use and its effects on instructor workload were not evaluated in the present study. Such repeatability may be conceptually consistent with principles of deliberate practice and mastery-oriented learning, provided that repetition is accompanied by structured feedback and opportunities to refine performance.

At the same time, implementing VR-based training involves practical barriers that require careful consideration. These barriers include the upfront and ongoing costs of hardware and software, infrastructure and technical-support needs, the faculty time and training required to integrate and facilitate VR sessions, and differences in learners’ familiarity with and comfort using immersive technologies [17]. Accordingly, decisions about integrating VR should balance its potential educational benefits against implementation feasibility and local resource constraints.

Taken together, although this study did not establish that VR produced superior short-term self-perceived outcomes compared with traditional instruction, VR may have practical value as a complementary training modality because of its potential to support flexible and repeatable learning. However, resource efficiency and long-term program sustainability were not directly evaluated and should be considered alongside the implementation barriers described above.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the principal design limitation is that instructional order was assigned at the training-cohort level rather than through individual random allocation and was therefore completely confounded with training cohort and calendar period. As a result, the effects of instructional order could not be separated from cohort or calendar-period effects, and the second-phase comparisons should be interpreted as exploratory rather than as unbiased estimates of instructional-order or modality effects. No planned washout period was included between the two phases, and learning from the first phase could persist into the second; cumulative exposure, residual carryover, and limited room for further improvement may therefore have influenced the second-phase change scores. An additional concern is the baseline imbalance in self-perceived knowledge of spill-kit contents (Q1), for which Cohort 2 reported higher initial scores than Cohort 1. Although subsequent analyses focused on within-participant change scores, the baseline imbalance was not eliminated by this approach and may have affected the magnitude of the observed changes by creating unequal room for improvement between cohorts. Second, learning outcomes were assessed using self-reported measures of knowledge, understanding, and ability rather than objective performance assessments; therefore, the observed increases in scores may not correspond to actual competence in chemotherapy spill management. Self-reported measures are also susceptible to response bias and may either overestimate or underestimate learners’ perceptions of their knowledge and abilities. Third, the study was conducted at a single tertiary medical center and included a small convenience sample, which may have introduced selection bias and limited the generalizability of the findings to other institutions, trainee populations, and training settings. Fourth, outcomes were assessed only immediately after training, precluding evaluation of whether the observed increases in self-perceived scores were sustained over time. The study also did not assess whether learning transferred to performance in standardized simulations or actual clinical settings. Fifth, the principal outcome instrument comprised four single-item domains assessing distinct constructs rather than a multi-item scale designed to measure a single underlying construct. Accordingly, internal-consistency reliability was not applicable across the four domains and was not calculated. Nevertheless, the use of single-item measures may have limited the precision and comprehensiveness with which each domain was assessed, and no test–retest or other formal reliability analysis was undertaken. Sixth, documentation relevant to the complete description and technical reproducibility of the VR intervention was incomplete. Although the available internal course-design document identified the module’s production collaborators, it did not document the platform’s commercial or licensing status, its specific software version, or any prior external technical or educational validation. Although the head-mounted display and handheld controllers were confirmed as HTC VIVE Pro hardware, and the manufacturer-stated system requirements are reported in the Methods, the actual computer configuration used in the study was not retained, which may limit the technical reproducibility of the intervention. Intervention fidelity was not formally evaluated using a predefined checklist or structured observational record; therefore, consistency of delivery beyond the documented standardization of content, duration, instructional sequence, and operational assistance could not be independently verified.

Future studies should incorporate objective performance-based assessments, delayed follow-up, larger multicenter samples, and designs that better address allocation and carryover. Individually randomized parallel-group trials or randomization of instructional order across multiple training cohorts would help clarify the educational impact of VR simulation in hazardous-drug training.

Despite these limitations, this study provides preliminary insight into short-term self-perceived outcomes and learner perceptions associated with VR simulation in chemotherapy spill-management training. VR simulation warrants further evaluation as a complementary instructional modality alongside traditional instructor-led instruction. However, the present findings do not establish objective educational effectiveness or readiness for routine implementation.

Conclusion

This study found that both virtual reality (VR) simulation–based training and traditional instructor-led instruction were associated with short-term increases in pharmacy interns’ self-perceived knowledge, understanding, and ability in chemotherapy spill management. In the first-phase comparison, the instructor-led-first cohort showed greater change scores in two of the four assessed domains; however, these differences cannot be attributed to instructional modality alone because the first modality received was confounded with training cohort and calendar period. Following crossover, second-phase change scores did not differ significantly between the two cohort-defined instructional orders. Because instructional order was completely confounded with training cohort and calendar period, this finding neither establishes equivalence between the two orders nor rules out an instructional-order effect.

These findings are consistent with the possibility that VR simulation and instructor-led teaching may serve complementary rather than competing instructional roles. Integrating VR into hazardous-drug training may offer a safe complementary approach for rehearsing procedures in high-risk, infrequently encountered clinical situations. Most participants identified instructor-led explanation combined with VR simulation as the most helpful option, supporting further evaluation of blended instruction; however, this perception does not establish that the combined approach produces superior objective learning outcomes. Further studies using objective performance-based assessments, delayed retention testing, and more rigorous comparative designs are needed to evaluate the durability and transfer of learning. The present conclusions are limited to short-term self-perceived outcomes and post-training learner perceptions.
