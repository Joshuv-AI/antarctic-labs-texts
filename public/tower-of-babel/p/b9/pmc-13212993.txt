Smart medical system integrating clinical workflows for robust skin cancer detection across heterogeneous pathologies
Authors: Ahed Abugabah, Prashant Kumar Shukla, Suchi Mishra, Abhishek Dwivedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Skin cancer is among the most prevalent and life-threatening dermatological diseases worldwide, with melanoma responsible for a substantial proportion of skin cancer–related deaths due to delayed and unreliable diagnosis. Conventional clinical screening based on visual inspection and expert interpretation is inherently subjective and often affected by inter-observer variability, lesion heterogeneity, and imaging artifacts, highlighting the need for accurate and generalizable automated diagnostic systems. This study proposes a novel hybrid deep learning architecture for skin cancer classification that integrates an attention-guided autoencoder with a transformer-inspired global context modeling module, forming a unified and robust representation learning framework. The encoder–decoder structure is designed to suppress noise and reconstruct salient lesion features, while an embedded attention mechanism emphasizes diagnostically relevant regions such as irregular boundaries and pigmentation patterns. The encoded representations are subsequently refined using transformer-style self-attention to capture long-range spatial dependencies and complex color–texture correlations, enabling superior discrimination compared to conventional CNNs and standalone transformers. In addition, a new hybrid hyperparameter optimization strategy is introduced by synergistically combining Bayesian Optimization with Grey Wolf Optimization (GWO) and Whale Optimization Algorithm (WOA) into a coordinated meta-heuristic framework. Bayesian Optimization provides probabilistic guidance for efficient global search, while GWO and WOA enhance exploration–exploitation balance and prevent premature convergence by modeling collective hunting and encircling behaviors. This hybrid optimizer dynamically tunes both architectural and training hyperparameters, including learning rate, batch size, latent dimension size, attention depth, and transformer token resolution. The proposed framework is comprehensively evaluated on three benchmark dermoscopic datasets—HAM10000, ISIC-2019, and ISIC-2020—using standardized preprocessing and data augmentation to mitigate class imbalance and illumination variability. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed approach consistently outperforms state-of-the-art CNN, transformer, and hybrid models, achieving classification accuracy exceeding 98% with improved F1-score and AUROC.

Introduction

The recent research on automated diagnosis of skin cancer has been present through the use of deep learning-based models, most of which demonstrate high accuracy in normal datasets1. Nevertheless, there are still such critical considerations as a lack of robustness to different imaging conditions in the model, the impossibility to use it in new populations and devices, and issues with class imbalance and noise on a systemic level2. More recent work has gone further to more complex architectures capable of utilizing local feature extraction with global contextual reasoning, as well as aims at achieving a higher level of interpretability and clinical relevance3. Nevertheless, even with these developments, numerous systems continue to experience the issue of real-world variability and remain unable to compete with clinicians operating in less controlled conditions, which highlights the necessity of more flexible, robust, and generalized systems that contribute to the effective screening of skin cancer and its early detection4.

Although the application of the deep learning has taken a significant jump in automated detection of skin cancer, there remain several fundamental challenges that do not allow effective application of this technique in clinical practice. The available models are usually weak and ungeneralised when applied to pictures captured by other devices, institutions and groups of patients hence poor diagnostic accuracy5. The lack of balance of the classes, visual noise and huge disparities in appearances of lesions are further increased in this problem which is characteristic of real-world dermatological data6. Besides, the existing architectures are lacking both fine-grained localities and global contextual information that are critical in the provision of correct differentiation between malignant and benign lesions. The hyperparameter optimization is also incapable of converging to the model and overfitting possibilities are also limited by the static and inefficient hyperparameter optimization7. The importance of these issues is that inaccurate or untrustworthy forecasts during diagnosis of skin cancer can directly affect the probability of early diagnosis and survival of the patient, in particular, in the case of melanoma. This project will assist in ensuring more plausible computer-aided diagnostic systems through the development of more adaptable, context-driven and resilient systems to the variation in data models. Such issues can be effectively resolved to enhance clinical trust, help the dermatologists at the high-volume screening facility, reduce the subjectivity of diagnoses, and ultimately, make a patient healthier due to the possibility to detect skin cancer earlier and more accurately8.

The given approach is developed on the ground of a hybrid attention-guided autoencoder-transformer model, which would be useful to learn to apply discriminative representations to dermoscopic images9. The autoencoder encoder performs hierarchical feature encoding and reconstruction which enable noise elimination and an amplification of the salient lesion features, and an inherent attention process identifies clinically relevant features of irregular edges and pigmentation patterns10. The smooth characteristics, in turn, go through a transformer-based self-attention module recovering long-range spatial relations and global contextual associations that traditionally have been ignored by conventional convolutional models11.

Even though the deep learning systems of diagnostic can be seen as rather well-developed at present, their inherent weaknesses, such as the generalization to nonhomogeneous data, and their dependency on the subjective clinical opinion are the main barriers to the practical use. It is these gaps which led to the craving to research more adaptive and situation-sensitive computers systems that are able to do more to represent the complexity of dermatological decision making12,13. The research is also driven by the recent advances achieved on the mechanisms of attention, the hybrid paradigms of learning that can be enforced in the discovery of finer details of lesions and world patterns of context more than the traditional methods14. Moreover, the growing large data sets of dermoscopy also offer an opportunity to come up with smart methods of optimization that can modify the model behavior to discontinuous data distributions.

This paper will set up a scalable and clinically important automated model, which will enable dermatologists working in large throughput screening centers to minimize the number of conditions of observer dependence and to increase the accuracy of the diagnosis. The study will contribute to the improvement of the computer-aided dermatology development where the gap between the experimental performance and the practical clinical implementation will be bridged, thereby improving the quality of early diagnosis and patient outcomes. Literature review

As a hybrid deep learning model, Mateen et al.15 recommend utilizing medical images in dermoscopes to identify melanoma automatically with the assistance of additional features and complementary representations of the latter to enhance the strength of the classification and the diagnostic capabilities of the model. The framework incorporates the vigorous convolutional characteristic retrieval with the optimal learning strategies to spot the complex lesion appearance, such as asymmetry, border aberration, and fluctuation of colour, successfully. The applied experiment based on benchmark dermoscopic datasets demonstrates a high-performance ability relative to the conventional single-model strategies, which has the potential to outline the potential of the framework as a consistent computer-aided diagnostic method of detecting early melanoma and help physicians in clinical decision making.

Aker et al.16 introduce a combined deep learning structure of skin cancer detection that uses a hybrid feature fusion method to increase the discriminatory ability in the analysis of dermoscopic images. The proposed strategy takes deep learned characteristics of several convolutional networks and complementary handcrafted or intermediate characteristics that allow more detailed and robust characterization of lesions. Although the reported experimental findings have since been removed from the journal, the presented experimental results show significant improvements in classification accuracy and generalization performance compared to standalone deep learning models that show the possibility of great effectiveness of hybrid feature fusion approaches in the process of automated skin cancer diagnosis.

In 202317, Patil et al. propose a novel DAA-Deep model, which combines a Differential Analyzer Approach with deep learning to enhance the detection of skin cancer based on dermoscopic images. The given structure will be able to differentiate the features of the deep hierarchy of representations by connecting them to analytical differentiation mechanisms to provide more accurate delineation of boundaries and lesion characterization. Experimental validation shows that the model has better detection accuracy and robustness than standard deep learning models, thereby illustrating that the model can be applicable to reliable and clinically relevant skin cancer screening than modern deep learning models.

Another study, by Rashad et al.18, presents an automated framework of skin cancer screening based on deep learning that improves the effectiveness of the early detection of cancer and diagnostic accuracy of the specified disease. The proposed solution capitalizes on sophisticated convolutional designs to learn discriminative features through dermoscopic images and can be applied to make the correct decision between malignant and benign lesions. The results of the experimental analysis of the model in terms of competitive performance, accuracy, and sensitivity demonstrate that the model can be used to screen large masses of skin cancer and conduct routine screening activities in a cost-effective and scalable decision-support system.

One of the proposals was a deep learning-based technique of detecting and classifying skin cancer with dermoscopic images19. Convolutional neural networks are adopted by the authors to learn the characteristics of discriminative lesions and classify malignant and benign skin disorders in multi-classes. Experimental assessment results demonstrated superior classification and strength, which demonstrate the applicability of deep learning models in being effective decision-support systems in early skin cancer diagnosis.

In the study of Ashtagi et al.20, it is possible to examine an EfficientNetB0-based transfer learning model to enhance the diagnosis of melanoma skin cancer using dermoscopic images. The proposed solution exploits the existing trained deep representations to be an effective representation of the detailed lesion features and reduce the overhead and training. Using experimental evidence, EfficientNetB0 was revealed to be more effective in accurate diagnosis, sensitivity, and generalization performance than conventional CNN models, which highlights its appropriateness to conduct efficient and clinically meaningful melanoma detection.

Alzahrani et al.21 propose a multi-attention-based fusion system of AI-assisted melanoma detection, which combines Convolutional Neural Networks and Vision Transformer architectures with a gradient-directed approach to the fusion to adjust the final results. The model takes advantage of gradient-based guidance to integrate complementary local texture information given by CNNs with information given by Vision Transformers to give a finer description of the lesions. The results of the experiment indicate that multi-attention fusion is much better than single architectures towards greater diagnostic accuracy, strength and interpretability and towards the capability of finding greater and clinically viable melanoma detection systems.

A refined dermoscopic image classification system developed by Nugroho et al.22, where the Inception-V3 architecture is trained and the pigmented lesions of the skin are correctly classified, is only one of the ways to identify pigmented skin lesions. It is founded upon transfer learning such that high level discriminative features are learnt at minimum training complexity and overfitting. The results of the experiment demonstrate that the classification accuracy and generalization are superior to the conventional CNN-based techniques, which explains why Inception-V3 can be considered a powerful backbone to automated dermoscopic skin lesion into categories.

The multiclass deep learning model suggested by Patil et al.23 is an appropriate one that can be applied to analyze the dermoscopic pictures to identify benign and malignant skin lesions. The article entails complex convolutional networks to be trained, and which learn the discriminative lesion features and address the inter-class similarity and intra-class variability. It has been identified in the experiment that the model has a high classification strength and accuracy amongst the different types of lesions besides being able to form a strong and clinically supportive tool in the diagnosis of skin cancer in its entirety.

The research of Nogales et al.24 provides a critical assessment of a hybrid artificial intelligence (AI) that can detect melanoma with the help of deep learning and classical machine learning. The article is a procedure of how the deep feature representation achieved in the convolutional networks could be applied together with the traditional classifiers in a way that the performance could be improved in the area of diagnostics. The experiment findings reveal that the hybrid models are more precise, more robust, and highly generalize in comparison to the standalone models, as they are suitable in enhancing trusted and clinically relevant melanoma detection.

According to the recommendation of Sarhan et al.25, the high-accuracy deep learning model, which is optimised with the assistance of the Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) algorithm, will be suggested to identify the skin cancer basing on the dermoscopic images. In the proposed study, the bio-inspired optimization paired with deep neural networks are used to enhance the feature selection, parameter optimization, and convergence rate. The experimental results reveal a substantial improvement in the classification accuracy, stability and generalization of the ant colony-based optimization over the non-optimized deep learning models, and this therefore reflects the appropriateness of the ant colony-based optimization as far as robust and intelligent skin cancer diagnostic systems are concerned.

In 202526, Sencelikel et al. introduce a progressive skin cancer prediction model using the deep learning application of MobileNetV2 with a dynamic learning algorithm that can best analyze medical imagery. The suggested model generates lightweight convolutional representations to guarantee a high predictive accuracy and low computational burden, and can therefore be utilized in resource-constricted clinical settings. The effectiveness and potency of the experimentation techniques in comparison with the standard methods of skin cancer detection prove the validity of the proposed MobileNetV2-based optimization approach in practice and scalability.

Esteva et al.27 demonstrate the capacity to categorize skin cancer on the level of a dermatologist by teaching deep convolutional neural networks with the huge clinical and dermoscopic images datasets. The analysis shows that the end-to-end deep learning models can be as good as the trained dermatologists at the diagnosis of malignant and non-malignant skin lesions. The breakthrough paper turned the deep neural network into an efficient and disruptive solution to the automated diagnosis of skin cancer, which would lead to the further development of AI-based dermatology.

Related works in automated skin cancer diagnosis.

The recent literature has significantly contributed to automated skin cancer diagnosis by surpassing the traditional convolutional architecture to transformer-based and hybrid representation learning models. Xin et al.28 proposed a novel and better transformer network to classify skin cancer, showing that compared to the conventional CNN pipelines, self-attention mechanisms can better learn the global lesion structures and inter-region dependencies. The current state of deep learning in skin lesion classification was further verified through a thorough review by Naqvi et al.29, who also found that transfer learning, ensemble modeling and explainability are increasingly becoming prevailing trends in the field, although the issue of class imbalance, domain shift and clinical generalizability remains.

More current application-based research has investigated more robust hybridization policies. Naeem et al.30 suggested SNC_Net that combines handcrafted and deep learning generated features based on dermoscopy images, demonstrating that feature fusion continues to be an important part of describing lesion typologies that might be poorly captured using deep networks on their own. A hybrid melanoma diagnosis framework based on combining U-Net-based segmentation, Inception-ResNet-v2 feature extractors, and a Vision Transformer with self-attention refinement was proposed by Mateen et al.31, which shows the increasing relevance of combining features that capture local boundaries with those that consider the contextual information globally. Equally, Attallah32 proposed Skin-CAD, which is an explainable deep learning model, a hybrid of dual high-level CNN features, feature selection and LIME-based interpretation thus covering both classification and clinical trust.

The latest literature is also characterized by a high tendency to stronger hybrid backbones and the expansion of the methodological synthesis. Aruk et al.33 have described a hybrid ConvNeXt-based skin lesion classification model that incorporates CNN-style local features extraction with transformer-like global modeling, and performed well on HAM10000 and ISIC-2019.

Research gap

Although the recent developments in the field of deep learning in skin cancer diagnosis have made remarkable steps forward, the current research literature indicates that there are still a number of gaps in the research. The existing strategies are based on either fixed or partially hybrid networks that focus on either local appearance or global contextual representation, but do not have a common mechanism to capture joint and adaptive constructions of noise-suppressed representations, attention-driven lesion saliency, and long-range spatial interactions. Besides, hyperparameter optimization in previous literature is typically confined to single-strategy or fixed-point tuning, which does not ensure model adaptability and strength in the face of heterogeneous data and real-world imaging. Numerous works have high accuracy on benchmark data sets, but they offer little assessment of generalization when multiple data sets, computation efficiency, and clinical scalability are evaluated. Moreover, interpretability and resistance to class imbalance and changes in illumination are understated. These gaps will necessitate the development of a new and adaptive hybrid architecture and dynamic multi-strategy optimization framework to provide consistent, generalizable, and clinically reliable automated skin cancer detection.

Materials and methods

The proposed framework introduces a hybrid deep learning framework for the application of automated classification of skin lesions as cancer or non-cancerous dermoscopically acquired images. Preprocessing and data augmentation are used to prepare the raw input images, aiding in the removal of noise, the change in illumination, and the bias in classes. The denoised images are then fed through an attention-guided autoencoder in which the encoder-decoder framework acquires compact and noise-resistant representations, whereas the integrated attention system highlights a subset of clinically crucial information, including lesion delimitations, asymmetry, and pigmentation patterns. The coded maps of features are then fed into a self-attention-based module using a transformer that is able to yield long range spatial correlations and complex texture color associations across the lesion. Lastly, the smooth global representations are sent to a classification head to forecast before the lesion classification. The combination of Bayesian Optimization, Grey Wolf Optimization, and Whale Optimization Algorithm with a hybrid training strategy is a strategy implemented in the training process to optimize the overall performance by dynamically adjusting both the architecture and learning parameters. This combined architecture allows strong learning of features, better generalization, as well as the correct classification of skin cancer in an entirely automated fashion (see Fig. 1).Fig. 1Deep learning dermoscopy image classification flow.

Dataset description

HAM10000 dataset

The HAM1000034 (Humans Against Machine with 10,000 training images) dataset is a popular benchmark in automated skin lesion classification, created to overcome the shortcomings of small-scale curated dermoscopic image datasets. The dataset is published as a part of the ISIC 2018 Skin Lesion Analysis Challenge and includes 10,015 dermatoscopic images of various populations and taken on various imaging equipment and clinical environments, which implies high variability in the appearance of the lesions. It has a representative representation of seven categories of clinically significant pigmented skin lesions, which are actinic keratoses and intraepithelial carcinoma, basal cell carcinoma, benign keratosis-like lesions, dermatofibroma, melanoma, melanocytic nevi, and vascular lesions. Over 50 percent of these cases are confirmed by histopathology, and the rest of the labels are tested by follow-up evaluation, expert opinion, or in vivo confocal microscopy, which has served as a good ground truth annotation. There are also several images of each lesion in the dataset, which can be tracked with the help of the unique lesion identifiers, and longitudinal analysis is possible. In spite of the fact that the formal test set is not publicly available, testing takes place on the online server to provide a fair comparison of the proposed methods. Due to its magnitude, variability, and good annotations, HAM10000 is a highly important resource in building and testing machine learning models to detect skin cancer (see Fig. 2).Fig. 2Image from HAM10000 dataset.

ISIC 2019 and ISIC 2020 datasets

The data provided in this research were taken through ISIC 2019 and ISIC 202035 data sets, which are part of the International Skin Imaging Collaboration (ISIC), a project of the International Society of Digital Imaging of the Skin that is devoted to spreading data on high-quality dermoscopic images to reduce mortality caused by melanoma. These data sets are common standards in automated skin cancer studies and are created to assist in the creation and testing of computer-aided diagnostic systems. The aggregate data comprises about 11,400 dermoscopic images that are divided into benign and malignant skin tumors. The training sample consists of 5,200 benign and 4,000 malignant images, and the validation sample has 550 benign and 550 malignant samples. On the same note, the testing set will have 550 benign and 550 malignant images, hence a balanced test regime. The ISIC 2019 and 2020 databases are unique because of the variety of lesion appearance, imaging conditions, and location of acquisition, which further imply the usefulness of the data in determining the model robustness, generalization ability, and clinical implications of automated melanoma detection (see Fig. 3).Fig. 3Image from ISIC 2019 and ISIC 2020 Datasets.

Data preprocessing steps

In the case of HAM10000 data, a uniform preprocessing pipeline is used to improve the quality of images and minimize the inter-sample variations. First, all dermoscopic images are downscaled to the same spatial resolution in order to guarantee conformity with the proposed model structure and to guarantee computational efficiency. Normalization of color is then carried out to minimize the differences that exist among imaging devices and different lighting conditions. Artifact remedies, including hair and air bubble suppression, are used to remove the irrelevant visual noise that can have a negative influence on feature learning. In order to balance the classes within the 7 lesion types, augmentation techniques such as rotation, flipping, scaling, and brightness correction are used. The value of pixel intensities is scaled to a fixed range, and convergence during model training is more consistent and quicker.

Both the ISIC 2019 and ISIC 2020 datasets follow the same dataset-specific but consistent preprocessing workflow in order to be robust and consistent. All the images are downsized to a standard input size, and contrast enhancement is applied to enhance the appearance of lesions. These are color constancy and illumination correction that are employed to reduce variations caused by various acquisition settings. The noise reduction filters remove the background artifacts and lesion boundaries are maintained. The balanced data augmentation techniques such as random cropping, horizontal, and vertical flipping, rotation, and intensity scaling are applied to prevent the model bias since the benign and malignant classification environment is a balance. The image normalization is done such that the pixel distributions become normal so that learning and generalization between the training, validation and test sets would be achieved successfully.

Attention-guided autoencoder design

Attention-Guided Autoencoder was used in this research to promote better features representation and noise cancellation in dermoscopic scenes. Traditional autoencoders are able to fit small latent codes, yet they tend to reproduce the patterns of all the relevant lesions as well as the unnecessary background noise, potentially lowering the reliability of the classification. The proposed design with an attention mechanism incorporated into the autoencoder framework helps prioritize regions of diagnostic importance (i.e., irregular lesion edges, pigmentation patterns, and texture changes) and inhibit background noise and other artifacts due to illumination that are likely to occur in dermoscopic images.

The attention-guided autoencoder is the main part of the feature extraction of the proposed framework, which will learn salient and diagnostically significant representations of dermoscopic images. Transformer-inspired global context modeling

The Transformer-inspired global context modeling module was incorporated to overcome the limitations of conventional convolutional architectures that primarily capture local spatial features. In dermoscopic image analysis, diagnostically important patterns such as asymmetry, irregular borders, and distributed pigmentation structures often span across distant regions of a lesion, which cannot be effectively modeled using local convolution operations alone. The transformer-based self-attention mechanism enables the model to learn long-range spatial dependencies and global feature interactions by dynamically weighting relationships among different image regions. This allows the framework to capture complex contextual correlations between color, texture, and structural patterns within the lesion. Compared with traditional CNN-based approaches, the transformer-inspired module provides enhanced global feature understanding, improved representation learning, and better discrimination between visually similar lesion types, thereby contributing to the overall robustness and accuracy of the proposed skin cancer classification system.

A transformer-inspired global context module is created to improve the latent representations produced by the attention-guided autoencoder by capturing the long-range spatial patterns and complicated lesion patterns. Hybrid hyperparameter optimization strategy

In this study, the coordinated integration of BO, GWO, and WOA is strategically adopted to address the limitations of conventional meta-heuristic approaches such as Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) and Genetic Algorithms (GA), particularly in high-dimensional and computationally intensive deep learning hyperparameter spaces. Bayesian Optimization is the main global search engine because it has a probabilistic surrogate modeling through Gaussian Processes, as the intelligent acquisition-based sampling algorithm considerably lowers the costly training evaluations and increases the stability of the convergence. To complement this, the exploration exploitation trade-off has been optimized with Grey Wolf Optimization, which has a hierarchical leadership mechanism \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$( \alpha , \beta , 9)$$\end{document}, which leads to well-structured convergence and keeps the population diversity intact without premature stagnation as seen in PSO and GA. Lastly, Whale Optimization Algorithm enhances local exploitation by using a bubble-net search strategic spiral and ensures delicate fine-tuning of sensitive hyperparameters with the learning rate, attention depth, and size of latent dimensions.

Optimized hyperparameters

The parameters of hyperparameters are an important aspect which assists in optimization of the performance of the proposed skin cancer classification framework and is being optimized with the assistance of the hybrid Bayesian-GWO-WOA strategy.

Hyperparameter selection is an important factor in the process of attaining optimal performance by deep learning models, especially for medical image classification tasks like detecting skin cancer. The Bayesian, GWO, and WOA strategy that was used in this research as a hybrid approach to hyperparameter tuning is sensitive to the capacity to balance global exploration and local exploitation when performing the tuning. Bayesian Optimization gives guidance to efficient exploration of the hyperparameter space that is probabilistic and GWO as well as WOA offer adaptive population-based search strategies capable of preventing an early convergence and local optima. The hybrid method allows more rapid convergence, greater stability, and better parameter selection compared to traditional manual tuning or single optimization methods and, finally, leads to better model generalization and predictive performance using multiple datasets.

Learning rate is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta$$\end{document} that regulates the step size used in optimizing with gradients, and affects the convergence rate and stability, whereas the batch size is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B$$\end{document}, which affects gradient approximation and regularization. The stochastic gradient descent weight update can be formulated in the following way:29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{W}}_{t+1}={\mathrm{W}}_{t}-\eta \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B}{\nabla }_{\mathrm{W}}\mathcal{L}({x}_{i},{y}_{i})$$\end{document}

Salient lesion features encoded and noise suppressed in the autoencoder are determined by the latent dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d$$\end{document} and attention depth \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${L}_{a}$$\end{document} of the network. In the case of the transformer module, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${T}_{r}$$\end{document} is the token resolution of the feature map, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h$$\end{document} is the number of attention heads that determine the variety of learned feature interactions. The multi-head self-attention can be formulated as:30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$MultiHead(Q,K,V)=Concat({head}_{1},\dots ,{head}_{h}){W}_{O},{head}_{i}=Attention(Q{W}_{Q}^{i},K{W}_{K}^{i},V{W}_{V}^{i})$$\end{document}

The framework can be dynamically optimized on the hyperparameters to obtain a favorable combination of model capacity, generalization, and computational efficiency, which guarantees strong and accurate skin cancer recognition in a wide range of dermoscopic data.

Training configuration

The hybrid framework is trained with a hybrid loss that is a sum of the reconstruction loss of the attention-guided autoencoder and the cross-entropy loss of classification. The total loss \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}}_{total}$$\end{document} is given as:31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{L}}_{total}={\lambda }_{rec}\hspace{0.17em}\parallel X-\widehat{X}{\parallel }_{2}^{2}+{\lambda }_{cls}\hspace{0.17em}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{c=1}^{C}{y}_{i,c}\mathrm{log}({\widehat{y}}_{i,c})$$\end{document}

In which it is assumed that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{X}$$\end{document} are the input and reconstructed image, respectively; the weighting coefficient used is: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\lambda }_{rec}$$\end{document} reconstruction coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\lambda }_{cls}$$\end{document} classification coefficient; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${y}_{i,c}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widehat{y}}_{i,c}$$\end{document} represent the probability of sample \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i$$\end{document} and class \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c$$\end{document}, respectively.

The model training protocol makes use of the Adam optimizer using dynamically adjusted learning rates, mini-batch gradient descent, and early termination on plateauing validation loss over p successive epochs to avoid overfitting. Generalization is enhanced by data augmentation, which consists of rotations, flips, and color jittering.

It has been implemented in Python in PyTorch and trained in a multi-GPU environment, which includes NVIDIA A100 GPUs with CUDA acceleration and can work with high-resolution dermoscopic images and process large batch sizes. Other software requirements are NumPy, OpenCV, and scikit-learn to perform preprocessing and evaluation.

Evaluation metrics

A series of standard classification measures is used to evaluate the performance of the proposed hybrid framework in a holistic manner to assess the predictive accuracy, discriminative capability, and clinical reliability. Accuracy (ACC) is the percentage of the samples that are correctly classified of all the predictions:32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Accuracy=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN}$$\end{document}and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$TP$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$TN$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FP$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FN$$\end{document} are true positives, true negatives, false positive as well as false negatives, respectively. Precision (P) and Recall (R) are used to measure the correctness and completeness of the model to detect positive classes:33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Precision=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}, Recall=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}$$\end{document}

F1-score scales the precision and recall values to offer only one value of classification performance:34\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F1-score=2\cdot \frac{Precision\cdot Recall}{Precision+Recall}$$\end{document}

In probabilistic assessment, the AUROC is computed to determine the discrimination capacity of the model by classes at different decision levels. Moreover, the misclassification patterns and performance per class are examined and assessed using a confusion matrix, which allows scrutinizing sensitivity and specificity of each type of skin lesion. These measures are a strong quantitative measure of the predictive power and clinical usefulness of the framework.

The ROC curve represents the True Positive Rate (TPR) vs. False Positive Rate (FPR) at different classification thresholds.35\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$TPR=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}, FPR=\frac{FP}{FP+TN}$$\end{document}

The ROC curve is summed up to all thresholds within the range \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau \in$$\end{document} \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\mathrm{0,1}),$$\end{document} resulting in the AUROC:36\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$AUROC={\int }_{0}^{1}TPR({FPR}^{-1}(x))\hspace{0.17em}dx$$\end{document}

In the case of a finite dataset, the probability of a random positive sample appearing higher than a random negative sample is a function of the finite dataset, which can be called the AUROC:37\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$AUROC=\frac{1}{{N}_{+}{N}_{-}}\sum_{i=1}^{{N}_{+}}\sum_{j=1}^{{N}_{-}}{\mathbb{I}}({s}_{i}^{+}>{s}_{j}^{-})$$\end{document}where: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{+}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{-}$$\end{document} denote the count of positive and negative samples, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${s}_{i}^{+}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${s}_{j}^{-}$$\end{document} are positive and negative sample predicted scores. The indicator function is denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbb{I}}(\cdot )$$\end{document}.

This equation is very common in tasks involving multi-class or binary classification to assess the discriminative capability of a model regardless of the choice of threshold.

Hyperparameter optimization analysis

The convergence level of BO, GWO, WOA, and the proposed Hybrid Bayesian-GWO-WOA strategy was relatively studied under the same settings of search space and iteration. BO demonstrated a fast convergence in the beginning with its probabilistic surrogate modeling and acquisition-based search, but at times it got stuck in local optima when performing subsequent optimization. GWO was characterized by good exploration capacity using alpha-betadelta hierarchy of leadership, but tended to move at a slower rate of fine-tuning on the edge of convergence. WOA gave the best use of the spiral updating mechanism, but convergence stability was sensitive to initial population settings. Conversely, the hybrid method achieved an efficient combination of guided global search of BO with adaptive exploration–exploitation of GWO and WOA to provide smoother convergence trajectories, quicker stabilization, and better final objective values, which guaranteed robustness and optimization effectiveness. Computational efficiency and complexity analysis

The effectiveness of the proposed Attention-Guided Autoencoder with Transformer and Hybrid Bayesian GWO WOA optimization was measured in terms of training time, inference latency, parameter complexity, memory footprint, and scalability to clinical usage. Empirical research carried out using a multi-GPU setup (e.g., NVIDIA RTX/A100 class GPU) suggests that even though the proposed hybrid architecture imposes some extra attention and transformer blocks on conventional CNNs, the optimized hyperparameter optimization will result in the stabilization of the convergence with fewer epochs, thus decreasing the training overhead. The average time per epoch of the training process was about 2.8 and 4.6 min on HAM10000 and ISIC-2019, respectively, because of the scale of the datasets, and it took approximately 30–35 epochs to reach full convergence in the case of hybrid optimization (in comparison to 45–60 epochs in the case of manual tuning). The inference time per image was also clinically acceptable (about 1824 ms), and it facilitated close to real-time screening. The overall model size of the full model is of the order of 32–38 million parameters, slightly larger than standalone EfficientNetB0 (around 5.3 M) but on par with hybrid CNN-ViT models, with an average of 5.8–7.2 GB used by the model in training based on batch size. Though there is more architectural richness, optimised latent dimensions, and token resolution help to eliminate exponential growth in computation. Scalability-wise, the architecture provides scalability to batch inference, edge-assisted preprocessing, as well as cloud deployment with little latency drop. Besides, the model scales linearly to large datasets, and the inference throughput remains constant when simulating concurrent requests, which implies that it can be utilized to scale the dermatology screening system to large-scale and be applicable to smart clinical decision-making in clinical settings.

Discussion

The proposed framework has demonstrated a high amount of quantitative performance regarding the HAM10000, ISIC-2019, and ISIC-2020 datasets, a high level of accuracy, and F1-score, and the value of the AUC, which demonstrates a high level of discriminative ability and a high level of reliability of melanoma detection with a small number of false negatives.

The qualitative analysis has determined that the model can be effective in focusing on the clinically significant features of lesions such as asymmetry, irregularity of borders, and changes in pigmentation and diminish the background noise and illumination artifact. Practically it has been determined that it has a low inference latency and can be trained to be cross-dataset stable which makes it applicable in real-time screening, tele-dermatology, and decision-support systems. The challenges of integrating attention and transformer functionality can be simplified via optimized tokenization and optimized hyperparameter optimization enabling training with tolerable time and scalable inference, and deployment in circumstances where a GPU is used in clinical practice or on a server in the cloud. However, in practice, there are issues associated with implementation, including domain variations in imaging devices, regulatory acceptance terms, communication with hospital systems, and reliance on AI-based predictions in clinicians. The privacy and security concerns of sensitive patient image data must necessitate the necessity to encrypt, secure model hosting, and, perhaps, federated learning to prevent the problem of data leakage and adversarial risks. The using publicly available datasets and having little prospective multi-centre validation are weaknesses.

Along with the quantitative changes, a number of qualitative observations of the experiments were made. The attention-guided autoencoder module was found to have the capability of blanketing background noise and highlighting the areas of lesions with diagnostic value. This action led to a better robustness in the presence of noisy or illumination-varying conditions as observed by the small performance loss in the process of perturbation testing. Transformer-based global context module refined the ability of the model to explain the intricate lesion morphology through modeling long-range features interactions at the expense of providing a more dependable classification of lesions across different categories. The hybrid optimization model that integrated Bayesian Optimization, GWO, and WOA had a more stable convergence and better parameter selection than each of the independent optimization strategies. The hybrid strategy was explored to well balance exploration and exploitation in the hyperparameter search space making it fast converging and predictive in datasets.

Even though the architecture at hand shows high diagnostic capabilities, the launching of attention, transformer blocks, and hybrid optimization policies inevitably complicate the model more than the conventional CNNs. The model has around 32–38 million parameters, more than lightweight CNN ones like EfficientNetB0 but similar to state of the art hybrid CNN-Transformer architectures. The complexity of training is also moderate, with an iterative process of hyperparameters optimization, and the computational cost of self-attention operations. These computational costs are however muted by a number of design choices. The autoencoder uses the representations of features and maps them to the optimized latent dimensions to eliminate redundancy of features that is not needed. Also, the hybrid optimization strategy minimizes training steps by increasing the rate of convergence over manual tuning. Experimental findings show that the model attains convergence after about 3035 epochs, which is much lower compared to the convergence time of manually adjusted architectures.

The use of the AI-based diagnostic systems in the healthcare setting poses significant security and privacy concerns. Dermoscopic image data includes personal patient information, and their loss can result in the violation of privacy or unlawful access. In addition, machine learning models that are trained on medical data can be susceptible to either adversarial attacks, data poisoning, or model inversion attacks, and can potentially reveal sensitive patient information or impair the quality of more essential diagnostic outputs. To curb these dangers, the application of secure data governance strategies such as encrypted data transmission, patient identifier anonymization and safe storage measures must be applied into practical clinical implementation.

Although the results are promising, a number of limitations are to be admitted. To begin with, the experimental assessment is based on the public dermoscopic datasets mostly, which do not necessarily represent the variability of the clinical situation. The performance of the models in the real-world setting might be affected by the differences in the imaging equipment, the state of acquisition, patient groups, and lesion distribution. Second, despite the high degree of cross dataset-generalization of the proposed architecture, additional testing on multi-institutional clinical datasets is needed to verify its applicability in a variety of healthcare settings.

The future work will focus on multimodal integration, shrinking the model to execute at the edge, enhancing more frameworks of explainability, and scaling the clinical trials to make it more potent, scaled, and applicable in a real clinical environment.

Conclusion

This paper introduced a powerful smart medical system that used attention-guided autoencoder, transformer-based global context modeling and hybrid Bayesian-GWO-WOA approach to hyperparameter optimization to detect skin cancer automatically. The suggested framework was intended to enhance learning lesion representation, learn long-range spatial dependencies, and optimize their parameters to obtain reliable dermatological diagnosis. Both multi-class and binary classification were assessed with high generalization in experimental assessment on three popular and general disease-focused dermoscopic data sets: HAM10000, ISIC-2019, and ISIC-2020. The proposed architecture was found to be able to read images with a classification accuracy of above 98%, F1-scores of above 98% and an AUROC of over 99%, outperforming a variety of state-of-the-art CNN, transformer and hybrid models. Ablation experiments additionally supported the hypothesis that all the components which include attention-based autoencoder, transformer module and hybrid optimization have a profound effect of performance improvement. Also, cross-dataset analysis, perturbation testing, and imbalance simulation showed that the model has a constant performance when subjected to changes in dataset composition, noise, illumination, and imbalance among classes.

These promising results notwithstanding, a number of limitations and practical considerations should be taken into consideration. Even though the model exhibits high results on dermoscopic datasets that are publicly available, the more heterogeneous clinical settings in the real world have additional factors such as differences in imaging devices, acquisition protocols, and patient demographics as well. It still needs to be further validated by large-scale multi-institutional clinical databases to ensure wider clinical applicability. The intermediate level of computational complexity, especially during training because of attention and transformer modules, can be problematic in implementation in resource-limited healthcare institutions, which is brought by the hybrid architecture. Although inference latency is still appropriate when it comes to near real-time screening, more model compression or lightweight optimization measures would be able to enhance scalability. The other critical issue is real-time clinical integration and compatibility of the workflow. Implementing the hospital information system will necessitate the integration with electronic health records (EHRs), dermatology imaging systems, and telemedicine solutions, which can create limitations in operational possibilities. Maintaining the compatibility with the current clinical working processes and ensuring the reliability in diagnosis is one of the promising future research directions. Moreover, the security and privacy issues should be taken into consideration while working with sensitive healthcare data. Some risks associated with medical imaging systems are unauthorized access, adversarial manipulation, and data leakage during model training or when deployed to the cloud. Thus, the applications in practice should include privacy-saving mechanisms, data transmission safety frameworks, and legal adherence to healthcare policies, including HIPAA or GDPR-equivalent policies to be used safely and ethically with patient data.

Future research will be spent on some extensions of this framework. These involve federated learning-based collaborative training in medical institutions, integration of explainable artificial intelligence to enhance clinical interpretability, and creation of lightweight edge compatible architecture of mobile dermatology devices. Secondly, multimodal clinical data including patient history, lesion metadata, and longitudinal imaging data may be integrated to become an additional tool of accuracy and decision support in diagnostic increase.
