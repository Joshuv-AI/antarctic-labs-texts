Specific network information gain for detecting the critical state of colorectal cancer based on gut microbiome
Authors: Xueqing Peng, Rong Gao, Jing Ren, Jianbo Lu, Xu Ma, Peiluan Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere generally exists a critical state or tipping point from a stable state to another in the development of colorectal cancer (CRC) beyond which a significant qualitative transition occurs. Gut microbiome sequencing data can be collected non-invasively from fecal samples, making it more convenient to obtain. Furthermore, intestinal microbiome sequencing data contain phylogenetic information at various levels, which can be used to reliably identify critical states, thereby providing early warning signals more accurately and effectively. Yet, pinpointing the critical states using gut microbiome data presents a formidable challenge due to the high dimension and strong noise of gut microbiome data. To address this challenge, we introduce a novel approach termed the specific network information gain (SNIG) method to detect CRC’s critical states at various taxonomic levels via gut microbiome data. The numerical simulation indicates that the SNIG method is robust under different noise levels and that it is also superior to the existing methods on detecting the critical states. Moreover, utilizing SNIG on two real CRC datasets enabled us to discern the critical states preceding deterioration and to successfully identify their associated dynamic network biomarkers at different taxonomic levels. Notably, we discovered certain ‘dark species’ and pathways intimately linked to CRC progression. In addition, we accurately detected the tipping points on an individual dataset of type I diabetes.

INTRODUCTION

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks among the top three most prevalent cancers globally, with over 1.2 million new cases and approximately 600 000 deaths reported annually [1]. The majority of sporadic CRC cases arise from the development of polypoid adenomas, which are preceded by intramucosal carcinoma that are characterized by high-grade dysplastic adenomas. If left untreated, these dysplastic adenomas can progress into malignant forms [2]. This transformation is known as the adenoma-carcinoma sequence, occurring through a multi-step mechanism involving specific mutations. The progression of complex diseases such as CRC can be conceptualized as a nonlinear dynamic process. It is generally divided into three states: normal, pre-disease/critical and disease state. The pre-disease state serves as a critical tipping point just before the onset of the disease. During this phase, the system is typically reversible, allowing a restore to the normal state through appropriate processing. However, if the system progresses through this pre-disease state, rapid deterioration and transition to the disease state will follow. Timely prediction at each stage can aid in preventing further deterioration and managing the disease progression. Thus, identifying the pre-disease state can provide an opportune moment for intervention, possibly preventing or preparing for catastrophic outcomes like disease onset or decline. However, the identification of this critical state still poses challenges on account of the similarity in clinical phenotypic and molecular expressions observed between the normal and critical states.

Traditional biomarkers, such as molecular or module biomarkers, have been employed to spot the difference between a disease sample and a normal sample. These biomarkers detect the disease state by examining differential expressions in genes/proteins between normal and disease state, instead of indicating the critical state, which often exhibits the phenotypic and gene expression similarities with the normal state. To address the difficult task of identifying critical state, a novel concept, known as dynamic network biomarkers (DNBs), has been proposed [3]. Derived directly from the critical slowing-down theory [4, 5], the DNB concept offers a statistical approach to choose related variate for the tipping point. Specifically, a small set of closely related variate (DNBs) exhibit early warning signals for the upcoming critical transition through drastic statistical indices [6, 7]. Unlike traditional biomarkers, the concentrations of DNB molecules collectively fluctuate around the tipping point rather than maintaining constant values or displaying random fluctuations [8].

Accumulating evidence suggests that gut microbiome plays crucial roles in CRC [9]. Zeller et al. employed metagenomic sequencing of fecal samples to recognize taxonomic markers that can effectively differentiate CRC patients from tumor-free controls [10]. Yachida et al. performed fecal metagenomic and metabolomic analyses that were conducted on samples obtained from a substantial cohort of participants who underwent colonoscopy. The aim was for comprehensively evaluating the taxonomic and functional characteristics of the gut microbiota as well as the metabolites present in the samples [11]. Wu et al. performed an integrated analysis on public fecal samples to identify adenoma-associated microbial markers for the early detection of CRC [12]. However, current research mainly focuses on the gut bacteria’s role in CRC development and lacks the identification of CRC’s tipping point based on gut microbiome data.

In gene expression studies, associating the DNB method with information theory to identify complex diseases’ critical states is garnering interest. Inconsistent index method is developed to detect the tipping point on the basis of the hidden Markov model theory [13]. The Jensen-Shannon divergence algorithm can be used in the key transition detection of complex diseases [14]. Degree matrix network entropy have been designed to be the detection of the tipping point of Type 2 Diabetes [15]. Due to the higher dimensional and stronger noise of gut microbiome data than that of gene expression data, there are still problems with the efficiency and robustness of existing methods for probing critical states during complex diseases, including CRC, specifically for gut microbiome data. In contrast to gene expression data, gut microbiome sequencing data can be obtained from fecal samples through a non-invasive way, thus making them easier to accessible. These collected gut microbiome data encompass phylogenetic information across diverse taxonomic levels. Such information is pivotal in identifying the early warning signals associated with CRC. Consequently, formulating an algorithm with efficiency and robustness to ascertain the tipping point of the gut microbiome concerning CRC emerges as a significant yet formidable endeavor.

To address this challenge, a method called specific network information gain (SNIG) was developed, which leverages sample-specific association networks (SSAN) and network information gain (NIG). This method successfully detects the early warning signals of critical transitions for CRC, opening new avenues for transforming fluctuating species abundance datasets into stable NIG. The proposed SNIG method offers several advantages: (i) SNIG method reliably quantifies critical network fluctuation, reducing noise through explore dynamic and high-dimensional information of omics data, thereby enhancing the robustness of the method. (ii) SNIG method enables the detection of critical states before disease occurrence and identifies DNBs. (iii) SNIG method discovers ‘dark species’ related to disease progression, which can be classified as ‘positive’ or ‘negative’ dark species based on their role in CRC. (iv) SNIG method measures network connectivity and fragility, illustrating the latent relationship among species of each group. (v) The numerical simulation results show that the SNIG method has strong robustness under diverse noise levels and also outperforms existing methods in critical state detection for large-scale datasets. The SNIG method was employed on three simulated datasets as well as two CRC datasets. This method effectively discerns the early warning signals of critical transitions or tipping points, subsequently pinpointing the associated DNBs. Intriguingly, we identified ‘dark species’, which are not showing differential abundance, while exhibiting sensitivity to the SNIG score during CRC development. A comprehensive functional analysis facilitated the recognition of the association between the ‘dark species’ and CRC. These ‘dark species’ can be bifurcated into two distinct categories based on their influence on CRC, those termed ‘positive dark species’ and those termed ‘negative dark species’. In addition, we compared the connectivity and vulnerability of microbial association networks constructed between different groups and yielded notable findings. Specifically, the microbial network connectivity in the control group was more robust than in the case group. However, the latter demonstrated greater fragility. Further statistical evaluations of microbial datasets divulged insights into species richness and internal diversity, illuminating the primary compositional disparities between the control and case groups. Additionally, an in-depth Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was undertaken to elucidate the intrinsic mechanisms of the shared DNBs across the two CRC datasets. These insights might pave the way for an enhanced comprehension of how diseases impact the microbiota functionality as well as pinpoint the specific bacterial groups that react to diseases within the intestinal ecosystem.

Theoretical and background

The theoretical basis of this paper is the DNB theory [3]. DNB is a group of molecules capable of identifying the pre-disease states before critical transitions in a complex disease. The elaborated description of DNB theory is provided in the Supplementary Material: A.

RESULTS

In the Materials and Methods section, we introduced the definition and rationale of the SNIG score. Subsequently, we employed the SNIG method on three simulated datasets and two CRC datasets. The first CRC dataset encompasses information on the genus, species, KEGG orthology gene (KO gene) and metabolite profiles, while the second is limited to the genus and species levels. Thus, we designated the first CRC dataset as the primary dataset and the second as the validation dataset. The applications of SNIG method in the first/main CRC dataset is demonstrated in the main text, and the other related results of the numerical simulated, the second dataset and validation of the critical state of the second/validated CRC dataset based on the first/main dataset are provided in the Supplementary Materials: C, E and F, respectively.

Critical state of the first/main CRC dataset

In our investigation of the relationship between the gut microbiome and CRC, first, we performed the analysis of fecal metagenomic data of 616 subjects and metabolomics data of 406 subjects [11]. These subjects were categorized into nine distinct groups based on their colonoscopic and histological findings: (i) normal, (ii) a few polyps, (iii) multiple polypoid adenomas with low-grade dysplasia, (iv) stage 0 CRC, (v) stage I CRC, (vi) stage II CRC, (vii) stage III CRC, (viii) stage IV CRC and (ix) stage HS. The more detailed description of these nine groups can be found in the original paper [11]. We defined subjects in groups (i) and (ii) as healthy controls, while those with MP and stage 0 were combined into stage 0. The case samples were then partitioned into five stages: 0, I, II, III, and IV. Figure 2A–C demonstrates the sudden increases of the SNIG score at stage I across various levels, which indicates the impending critical transitions. Specifically, Figure 2A illustrates the tipping point at the genus level, Figure 2B shows the critical state at the species level and Figure 2C highlights the sharp increase at the metabolite level. These sudden changes in the SNIG score across different levels validate the efficacy of the SNIG algorithm in the identification of tipping point at stage I, emphasizing the upcoming critical transitions. Besides, as shown in Figure 2D and E, we cannot accurately figure out the difference between the critical state and other states using the relative abundance of DNB species, but we can detect the critical states in Figure 2B based on the SNIG scores of these DNB species.

‘DNBs’ revealed by SNIG

The top 10% species with the highest SNIG score at the critical state are treated as DNBs [13]. The list of DNBs are available in the Supplementary Table S1. ‘Dark species, genus and metabolites’ revealed by SNIG

In many clinical practices and biological research, differential abundance species or mutant species are employed to detect diseases. However, our findings reveal that species, which are not differentially abundant yet are sensitive to SNIG scores, also play pivotal roles during disease progression. These species are termed as the ‘dark species’ in our study.

For unearthing ‘dark species’, we initially chose the DNB species that were not differentially abundant. Intriguingly, some species within the DNBs, while not differentially abundant at the molecular level, showcased a high SNIG score at the network level. Such species have been referred to as the ‘dark species’. To delineate the evolutionary relationship of these ‘dark species’, we constructed phylogenetic trees employing the neighbor-joining (NJ) method (Figure 4).

The NJ phylogenetic tree of the ‘dark species’ at species level. The pie chart represents the proportion of evolutionary branches of different species in different group. Bootstrap is calculated 500 times.

Furthermore, to ascertain the primary pathways in which these species participated, we carried out an association analysis (R < 0.6, P < 0.05) of some of the ‘dark species’ with certain KEGG pathways (Figure 5). Initially, we analyzed the KO genes in the KO spectrum, subsequently aggregating the relative abundance of these KO genes per sample, representing the relative abundance of the respective pathway. Our results highlighted that, while the ‘dark species’ did not show any significant differences between the control and case groups, they held significant associations with methane metabolism, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, purine metabolism, lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis, among others.

Heatmap shows the association of the ‘dark species’ with pathways.

Our literature review unveiled that certain ‘dark species’ are intricately linked with the progression of CRC. For instance, both Bacteroides massiliensis and Alistipes onderdonkii are identified as potential biomarkers differentiating CRC tissues from their adjacent normal tissues. The B. massiliensis is reportedly abundant in CRC patients [18, 19]. Species like Bacteroides vulgatus and Ruminococcus gnavus correlate with an increased CRC risk [20]. Dorea longicatena is newly correlated with CRC [20]. Multiple studies have underscored the relationship between the gut presence of Bacteroides stercoris and a heightened CRC risk [16, 21]. Given the influence of these species on CRC, the ‘dark species’ can be bifurcated into two categories: ‘negative dark species’ that promote CRC progression, and ‘positive dark species’ that inhibit its progression. The influence of other confounders

Since the data may not include only the relative abundance of each species under each sample but also the difference of some other factors, such as age, gender, BMI, smoking and alcohol consumption, we consider the impact of these factors on the results. To exclude the influence of these factors on the results that we have obtained, we examined the differences in patient metadata. The results for stage 0 (S0) and stages III/IV (SIII/SIV), obtained using two distinct methods, are documented in the primary source of the CRC dataset [11]. Here, we concentrated on analyzing the stages I/II (SI/II) and present the corresponding results, as illustrated in Figure 11. While patient gender and smoking (Brinkman Index) showed significant differences between the groups, the other variables appeared less likely to confuse the analysis.

An analysis of potential confounders that could influence the metagenomic and metabolomic classifiers was performed. We examined the impact of age, sex, BMI, smoking and alcohol exposure on the AUC. DISCUSSION

Identifying the tipping point during the development of complex biological systems, for instance, the pre-disease stage of CRC, is of paramount importance. Spotting early warning signs of a critical transition before the disease progresses can offer a timely opportunity for the prevention or preparation for catastrophic decline. However, the task of detecting early warning signals of catastrophic transitions based on gut microbiome data is challenging. In this paper, we introduce a novel computational method, SNIG, which diverges from traditional methods that rely on differential expression information. SNIG explores the dynamic changes of the synergistic effect of molecular association when complex biological systems close to a tipping point mark an upcoming key change. Specifically, the SNIG method quantifies the network or collective fluctuations of molecules, the pivotal criterion for critical states, thus providing credible early warning signals. The SNIG method’s ability to detect early warning signals and pinpoint the corresponding DNBs attests to its efficacy. Moreover, species present in the DNBs of the primary dataset and also found in the validation dataset were used to be identify the tipping point of the latter. This was in alignment with the critical state detected for the validation CRC dataset based on its entire species composition. The outcomes of our numerical simulations further underscore the method’s robustness and efficacy. These findings not only lay the groundwork for early diagnosis but also facilitate the exploration of potential molecular mechanisms tied to disease progression.

The input format of microbiota composition we used is relative abundance, and we can also convert the relative abundance data to centered-log ratio values to predict the critical state (Figure. S14). For the details, please see the Supplementary Material: H. This observation highlights the occurrence of critical transitions in the progression of CRC, which is consistent with our previous results obtained with the input relative abundances. Therefore, other formats are also applicable to our method, such as centered-log ratio.

SNIG method has a broad applicability, and it is applicable not only for CRC but also for other complex diseases, such as diabetes and tumors. SNIG method can not only provide early warning signals based on samples from different patients but also on an individual basis. Moreover, it is not only suitable for microbial data, but also for other omics data, such as genomics. And to detect the critical states for the latter, we only change the microbial network in the method into other networks, such as gene interaction networks.

In conclusion, the SNIG method represents an effective and robust approach that is capable of detecting the critical state during disease progression, identifying corresponding DNBs and revealing ‘dark species’ related to CRC development. The proposed SNIG method holds significant potential for precision diagnosis and treatment. Importantly, continuous monitoring of an individual at successive time points may allow for the detection of significant dynamical changes based on SNIG by using the healthy samples of the individual as a reference.
