MEN1 Deficiency Drives Lung Cancer Progression via Activation of MMP10‐Mediated Angiogenesis
Authors: Chengyu Wei, Xiangyu Liu, Shimin Hu, Xiaodie Shen, Yingli Liu, Xinxin Qin, Ye Zhou, Jianqi Zheng, Bingke Tian, Jingfang Hong, Yongxia Song, Di Wang, Linlin Peng, Jinxia Ding, Fang Wang, Hui Zhang, Shihao Zhang, Huan Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAngiogenesis is essential for tumor progression, MEN1 gene encodes the menin protein, which is known as a tumor suppressor in lung tissue. Our previous study have shown that vascular density is significantly increased in menin‐deficient lung cancer, suggesting that menin deficiency may promote the malignant progression of lung cancer by enhancing angiogenesis. However, whether menin play an important role in angiogenesis of lung cancer and the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. In this study, lung‐specific Kras G12D mutation induced mouse lung cancer model, clinical human lung cancer tissues, lung cancer and vascular endothelial cell lines were utilized, RNA‐seq, ATAC‐seq, ChIP, tube formation, sprouting, invasion and migration experiments were applied to investigate the role and mechanism of MEN1 in lung cancer angiogenesis. The result show menin were negatively related to angiogenesis markers and phenotype. Menin deficiency indirectly upregulates MMP10 transcription through activation of the transcription factor JunD. The expression of MMP10 further activates the EGFR receptor in vascular endothelial cells, thereby initiating the AKT/ERK signaling pathway. Inhibition MMP10 reversed menin low expression induce angiogenic phenotype and tumor progress. In conclusion, menin deficiency promotes angiogenesis through upregulates MMP10, targeting MMP10 with inhibitor represent an effective anti‐angiogenic strategy for treating MEN1‐deficient lung cancer.Keywords: angiogenesis, JunD, lung cancer, MEN1, MMP10

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer‐related death worldwide and has the highest incidence and mortality rates globally [1]. Lung cancer is a highly vascularized tumor [2]. The initiation of tumor angiogenesis is necessary for tumor progression and metastasis [2, 3]. Angiogenesis plays an important role in the development of lung cancer, and antiangiogenic therapy targeting this process has become an important treatment strategy for this disease [4, 5]. Besides some lung cancer patients with antiangiogenic contraindications [6], both preclinical and clinical studies have shown that antiangiogenic therapy can enhance the antitumor effects of traditional chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy (such as TKIs), and immunotherapy [4]. In recent years, many antiangiogenic drugs, such as bevacizumab, ramucirumab, and nintedanib, have been applied in the clinical treatment of lung cancer. Data on the combined efficacy of antiangiogenic therapy with chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy are constantly being obtained [7]. The combination of the immune checkpoint inhibitor atezolizumab and bevacizumab has been approved for nonsquamous NSCLC [8]. Compared to platinum‐based chemotherapy alone, the combination of bevacizumab and chemotherapy significantly prolonged survival of previously untreated patients with advanced NSCLC [9].

Although these intervention measures were initially effective, the efficacy of most patients was limited by the emergence of drug resistance mechanisms and off target toxicities, the survival benefits ranged from a few weeks to several months [10, 11]. Suggesting that not all patients can benefit from the combination of anti‐angiogenic therapy and other anti‐tumor approaches. Therefore, further exploring the mechanism of malignant progression of lung cancer is extremely necessary for developing more targeted treatment strategies.

The multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 gene (MEN1, referred to as Men1 in mice) encodes the menin protein, which consists of 610 amino acids and is known to function as a tumor suppressor in lung tissue. MEN1 acts as an important tumor suppressor gene in lung tissue and is widely involved in gene expression [12], protein stability [13], chromatin modification [14, 15], and cell proliferation [16, 17], differentiation [18] and migration [17]. The biallelic inactivation or somatic mutation of MEN1 is 75% predictive of the occurrence of lung cancer [19]. Our previous research demonstrated that the expression of the vascular marker CD31 significantly increased in mice with Kras G12D‐mutated lung cancer after MEN1 knockdown [18]. However, the regulation of angiogenesis by menin in lung cancer remains unclear. In this study, we hypothesized that menin regulates the malignant progression of lung cancer by regulating angiogenesis and the underlying mechanism still needs to be elucidated.

Angiogenesis involves the regulation of multiple key molecules, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), angiopoietins, hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1 (HIF‐1), and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), among which matrix metalloproteinases are involved in the degradation of the extracellular matrix [7, 20]. A series of physiological processes is involved in angiogenesis [21]. MMP10 is a member of the MMP family. Previous studies have revealed that MMP10 promotes the malignant progression of liver cancer and cervical cancer by facilitating angiogenesis [22, 23]. Moreover, MMP10 is expressed and active at a higher level in human NSCLC than in normal lung tissue, and predicts that MMP10 is a potential target for therapeutic intervention in human lung carcinomas [24]. However, whether MMP10 is involved in lung cancer angiogenesis and the underlying mechanism remains unclear.

Our previous studies revealed that the absence of Men1 significantly accelerates the malignant progression of lung adenocarcinoma induced by Kras G12D mutation [13, 18]. Moreover, in mouse models of lung cancers lacking Men1 expression, the expression of the vascular marker CD31 significantly increases [18], suggesting that the absence of menin may promote the malignant progression of lung cancer through the regulation of angiogenesis. In this study, transcriptome sequencing results revealed that MMP10 expression was significantly higher in the lung cancer tissues of mice with lung‐specific Men1 deletion combined with Kras G12D mutation (KMS) than in the lung tissues of mice with the Kras G12D mutation (KS) alone. Chromatin accessibility analysis revealed that the degree of chromatin opening near the MMP10 gene was significantly increased in the lung tissue of KMS mice. Mechanistically, loss of menin leads to transcriptional upregulation of MMP10 via activation of the transcription factor JunD. The elevated MMP10 subsequently acts on vascular endothelial cells to stimulate EGFR, triggering downstream AKT/ERK signaling. This cascade ultimately drives angiogenesis and supports the malignant progression of lung adenocarcinoma.

Material and Methods

Mice

All animal experiments were conducted under the guidance of accepted animal welfare principles and ethical regulations. The experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University. All mice were maintained under specific pathogen‐free (SPF) conditions. KS (Kras LSL‐G12D/+; Sftpc‐Cre) mice were established by the specific activation of KrasG12D in alveolar type II (AT II) cells to induce lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). KMS (Kras LSL‐G12D/+; Men1 f/f; Sftpc‐Cre) mice were generated by crossing KS mice with Men1 f/f mice, which were characterized by a four‐exon deletion spanning exons 2 to 5. All the animals were maintained on a C57BL/6 background. At 6–8 weeks of age, KS and KMS mice received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of 100 mg/kg tamoxifen (TAM) once a day for 5 consecutive days. At 3 months after injection, mouse lung tissues were harvested for subsequent analyses.

To elucidate the function of MMP10, we conducted experiments using KMS model mice. The mice were divided into two groups (n = 3 per group). Mice in the control group received daily tamoxifen injections for five consecutive days, after which they were maintained under standard conditions. Mice in the experimental group, in addition to the tamoxifen regimen, were administered the MMP10 inhibitor MMP‐9/10‐IN‐2 (MCE, China, HY‐161285) (25 mg/kg, once daily, for two consecutive weeks). At the endpoint of the animal experiments, the mice were euthanized, and lung tissues were collected and weighed. The lung tissues were serially sectioned into 3 μm slices for subsequent experiments. Information on the mice used in this study is available in Table S1.

Cell Culture

Non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) A549 cells, human embryonic kidney (HEK)‐293 T cells, and HUVECs were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM, PM150210, Procell, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Australia), 100 U of penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (C0222, Beyotime, China). NCI‐H1299 cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U of penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. All the cell lines were cultured in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37°C. The cells were routinely analyzed for mycoplasma infection using a Myco‐Lumi Luminescent Mycoplasma Detection Kit (C0298S; Beyotime, China), and all the cell lines were confirmed to be negative for mycoplasma contamination. The cells used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Study of Human Primary Lung Cancer Specimens

As previously reported [13], this investigation utilized primary human lung cancer specimens. The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Review Committees of both Anhui Medical University and Guizhou Medical University. We collected 31 LUAD specimens from patients who had undergone resection at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University and the First Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University. Information on the LUAD specimens in this study is available in Table S1.

RNA Sequencing and Bioinformatics Analysis

KS and KMS mice were injected intraperitoneally with tamoxifen (TAM) for 5 consecutive days, and their genotypes were verified by PCR prior to tissue collection. Two months later, lung tissues were harvested and sent to Chi Biotech (Shenzhen, China) for RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq). After library construction, the quality of the libraries was assessed using an Agilent 4200 TapeStation to confirm the insert size. Qualified RNA‐seq libraries were subsequently sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the edgeR software package (v3.40.2), with a significance threshold of p < 0.05 for identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Significantly dysregulated genes were selected on the basis of the cutoff criteria of p < 0.05 and |log2‐fold change (FC)| ≥ 1. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the DEGs was conducted using the clusterProfiler package in R (v4.6.2). The clusterProfiler tool was also used to evaluate the statistical enrichment of DEGs in KEGG pathways. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) for the GO and KEGG datasets was performed using clusterProfiler in R (v4.22). GSEA was carried out at Chi Biotech.

ATAC‐Seq

ATAC‐seq technology was used to detect open chromatin regions in cells. Briefly, nuclei were extracted from the samples, and approximately 5 × 104 nuclear pellets were resuspended in a Tn5 transposase reaction mixture. The transposition reaction was incubated at 37°C for 30 min. After transposition, equimolar amounts of Adapter 1 and Adapter 2 were added, and the library was amplified by PCR. Following PCR amplification, the library was purified using AMPure beads, and its quality was assessed with Qubit. Library preparation and sequencing were performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform at Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China), generating 150‐bp paired‐end reads. The raw sequencing data obtained were subjected to quality control using fastp software. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using BWA (v0.7.12), and open chromatin peaks were subsequently identified and analyzed with MACS2 software.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were conducted with the SimpleChIP Plus Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit with magnetic beads (9005, CST, USA) to examine protein–DNA interactions. Potential binding sites on the MMP10 promoter were predicted using the JASPAR database, and corresponding primers were designed (Table S3). Briefly, cells transfected with MEN1 or JunD plasmids were fixed with 1% formaldehyde and sonicated (60 W). Chromatin immunoprecipitation was then carried out to enrich DNA fragments bound to specific chromatin‐associated proteins. After the reversal of cross‐linking and DNA purification, enriched DNA was detected by RT‐qPCR to determine the relative abundance of specific genomic loci.

Luciferase Reporter Assay

The wild‐type MMP10 promoter (pGL3‐basic‐MMP10‐WT) and its site‐deleted variants (pGL3‐basic‐MMP10‐del E1, pGL3‐basic‐MMP10‐del E2) were inserted into the pGL3 firefly luciferase reporter vector, which was purchased from Hefei Dingxiu Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Hefei, China). The pRL‐TK vector expressing Renilla luciferase was used as an internal control. HEK‐293 T cells were co‐transfected with the reporter plasmids, the pRL‐TK vector, and either a JunD‐expressing plasmid or a control plasmid. After 24 h of incubation, luciferase activity was measured using a dual‐luciferase reporter assay system (Yeasen).

Histology and Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Three‐micrometer‐thick deparaffinized tissue sections were subjected to heat‐induced antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) at 95°C. After treatment with 3% hydrogen superoxide, the sections were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The sections were subsequently incubated with secondary antibodies for 30 min at room temperature. Finally, immunohistochemical analysis was performed, and the results were visualized. Representative images were captured using a panoramic scanning microscope (Motic, China). The primary antibodies used are listed in Table S2.

RNA Extraction and RT‐qPCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA) and reverse transcribed using MonScript RTIII Super Mix with dsDNase (Two‐Step) (Monad, China). To quantify the expression levels of target RNAs, RTqPCR was performed with MonAmp ChemoHS qPCR Mix (Monad, China) and the Bio‐Rad CFX96 Real‐Tim‐e detection system under the following cycling conditions: 95°C for 3 min, 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s, followed by melting curve analysis. The expression of target genes was normalized to the expression of β‐actin, and relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCq method. Each RT‐qPCR experiment was repeated three times. The primers used in this study are listed in Table S2.

Plasmids and Transient Transfection

The MEN1 overexpression plasmid (PLNCX2‐MEN1) and shRNA targeting MEN1 (shMEN1) have been previously described. The MMP10 (human) overexpression plasmid (pLV3‐CMV‐mCherry‐MMP10‐3 × HA‐Puro), JunD (human) overexpression plasmids (pCMV‐EGFP‐JunD‐3 × FLAG‐Neo and pLV3‐CMV‐JunD‐3 × HA‐EF1a‐mCherry‐Puro), EGFR (human) overexpression plasmid (pLV3‐CMV‐EGFR‐3 × ‐CopGFP‐Puro), and shRNA‐mediated silencing plasmids for JunD (human) (pLV3‐U6‐JunD ‐shRNA2‐CopGFP‐Puro) and EGFR (human) (pLV3‐U6‐EGFR‐shRNA2‐CopGFP‐Fluc‐hPGK‐Puro) were all purchased from Miaoling Biology (Wuhan, China). For transient transfection, HEK‐293 T cells, A549 cells, NCI‐H1299 cells, and HUVECs at 70%–80% confluence were transiently transfected with plasmids using polyethyleneimine (PEI, Yeasen, China) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Transfection efficiency was determined by western blotting and reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR) to evaluate protein and mRNA expression, respectively. Cells were collected at the indicated time points for sample preparation.

Western Blotting Analysis

Cells were lysed in buffer [1 M Tris–HCl (pH 7.4), 3 M NaCl, 20% Triton X‐100, 1 M NaF, 0.1 M Na3VO4, 0.1 M PMSF, and glycerol] on ice. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto membranes. The membranes were blocked with 10% nonfat dry milk (NFDM) in TBST buffer (50 mM Tris, 1.37 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 0.1% Tween 20; pH 7.4) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with the indicated primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The membranes were then washed five times with TBST buffer. The membranes were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. After the membranes were washed five times with TBST, protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) western blotting substrate (Biosharp, China). The primary antibodies used in this study are listed in Table S2.

Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

For ELISAs, conditioned medium was collected from A549 and NCI‐H1299 cells cultured in 60 mm dishes, and the concentration of MMP10 was determined using a standard ELISA kit (CSB‐E04673h; Cusabio, Wuhan, China) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. To reduce the influence of variations in cell numbers on secreted protein levels, culture supernatants were harvested from human lung adenocarcinoma cells plated with an identical cell density after 24 h of incubation. Cell numbers were subsequently determined at the time of conditioned medium collection.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

For immunofluorescence (IF) staining, cells cultured on glass‐bottom dishes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min and then were washed three times with 1 × PBS. The cells were subsequently blocked with blocking buffer (1 × PBS/5% BSA/0.3% Triton X‐100) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with the corresponding primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The next day, the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Finally, nuclei were counter stained with DAPI. Images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope. The primary antibodies used are listed in Table S2.

Preparation of Conditioned Medium (CM)

A549 and NCI‐H1299 cells (2 × 106) were seeded into 60‐mm dishes in growth medium for 24 h. Following the removal of the original culture medium, the cells were incubated with 3 mL of serum‐free medium for an additional 24 h, and the collected culture supernatant was used as conditioned medium (CM). To rule out the possible influence of CM on cell proliferation, cell growth and the protein content of CM were simultaneously assessed at the time of CM harvest using a Bradford assay kit.

Transwell Assays for Cell Invasion

Cell invasion was assessed using a Transwell system (72,532, NEST, China). The upper chambers of Transwell inserts were precoated with 50 μL of Matrigel (C0371, Beyotime, China). Then, 5 × 104 cells in 200 μL of serum‐free medium were seeded into the Matrigel‐coated inserts, and the lower chambers were filled with 500 μL of complete medium. After incubation for 24 h at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator, the cells that invaded through the Matrigel layer were fixed, stained, and counted.

Tubule Formation and Three‐Dimensional Sprouting Assays

Tubule formation and three‐dimensional sprouting assays were performed using growth factor‐reduced Matrigel basement membrane matrix. Briefly, the Matrigel was gently thawed on ice, and 50 μL of the matrix was evenly distributed into each well of a 96‐well plate and allowed to polymerize before cell seeding. A total of 1 × 104 suspended HUVECs were seeded on top of the Matrigel matrix and treated with conditioned medium (CM) pretreated with or without various agents for 24 h. After incubation at 37°C for 1 h, images were captured using a 20× objective and analyzed with ImageJ software. For endothelial cell sprouting assays, HUVECs (1 × 103) were seeded into nonadherent round‐bottom 96‐well plates and maintained overnight in culture medium supplemented with 0.24% carboxymethylcellulose. The resulting spheroids were then embedded in type I collagen solution (40125ES10, Yeasen, China), overlaid with CM, and incubated at 37°C for at least 24 h. Endothelial sprouting was analyzed in at least 3 spheroids per group, and the number of branches was quantified under a microscope.

Coimmunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) Assays

A549 cells or HUVECs (90% confluence) were transfected with the indicated constructs for 24–48 h before they were harvested. The cells were placed on ice and lysed in buffer for 1 h, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatants were transferred to new 1.5 mL tubes, and 2.5 μL of the indicated antibody was added to each sample, followed by rotation overnight at 4°C. The next day, the mixtures were incubated with 20 μL of protein A/G magnetic beads (Beyotime, China) for 1 h at room temperature. After being washed five times with TBS buffer, the beads were boiled in SDS‐PAGE sample buffer and then subjected to SDS‐PAGE. Western blotting analysis was then performed using specific primary antibodies and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibodies, and protein bands were visualized by chemiluminescence.

Quantification and Statistical Analysis

All experiments were independently performed at least in triplicate. All the statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). The specific statistical tests used are indicated in the figure legends. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Low Expression of Menin Promotes Angiogenesis in Lung Cancer

Survival analysis of lung cancer patients in the kmplot database revealed that patients with low MEN1 expression had shorter overall survival (Figure 1a). Correlations between menin (MEN1) and the angiogenesis markers CD31 (PECAM1) and CD34 were assessed using the TNMplot database. The results revealed that MEN1 expression exhibited a weak negative association with PECAM1 and CD34 gene expression (Figure 1b,c). Based on our previous data [13], immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for CD31 and CD34 in human lung cancer tissues indicated that CD31 and CD34 expression in the low‐menin expression group was significantly higher than that in the high‐menin expression group (Figure 1d–f).

Our previous studies revealed that in lung tissue, alveolar type II epithelial cell‐specific Kras G12D‐mutant mice (KS), Men1 deletion (KMS) significantly accelerated the malignant progression of lung cancer [13, 18]. IHC staining revealed that the expression of CD31 and CD34 in the lung tissues of KMS mice was significantly greater than that in the lung tissues of KS mice (Figure 1g–i). These results suggest that menin suppresses the malignant progression of lung cancer by inhibiting angiogenesis.

To investigate the regulatory effect of menin on angiogenesis in lung cancer, the human embryonic kidney cell line HEK‐293 T and the human lung cancer cell lines A549 and NCI‐H1299 were utilized. First, menin was overexpressed in A549 and NCI‐H1299 cells (Figure S1b,c). The culture supernatants were collected and used as conditioned medium (CM) to treat HUVECs (Figure S1a). Cell invasion ability was evaluated via a Transwell assay. Compared with the control group, HUVEC invasion was suppressed in the group cultured with CM from menin‐overexpressing lung cancer cells (Figure 1j,k,n,o). Conversely, more HUVECs traversed the cell‐permeable membrane after culture in CM from menin‐knockdown lung cancer cells than after culture in control CM (Figure S1d–g,j,k). These findings demonstrate that menin inhibits the migration and invasion of vascular endothelial cells in the tumor microenvironment.

To further observe the effect of menin on the tubule‐forming ability of vascular endothelial cells, HUVECs were treated with CM from menin overexpression lung cancer cells. The results showed that the tubule‐forming ability of the menin overexpression group was significantly lower than that of the control group (Figure 1j,l,n,p). Conversely, the tube formation ability of the menin knockdown group was significantly higher than that in the control group (Figure S1f,h,j,l). The results of the vessel sprouting experiment showed that the germination ability of HUVECs was significantly inhibited after culture in CM from menin overexpression lung cancer cells (Figure 1j,m,n,q), whereas the germination ability of HUVECs in the menin knockdown group was significantly enhanced (Figure S1f,i,j,m). Together, the above results suggest that menin overexpression can suppress angiogenesis, whereas low menin expression promotes angiogenesis in lung cancer cells.

Angiogenesis‐Related Gene MMP10 Is a Potential Target of Menin

To further explore how menin regulates angiogenesis in the lung cancer microenvironment, RNA sequencing was conducted using lung tissues from KS and KMS mice. RNA‐seq data revealed that the expression of multiple angiogenesis‐related genes, including MMP10, IGFALS, ANG, HMGA2, and MMP11, was upregulated in KMS mouse lung tissues [25, 26, 27, 28] (Figure 2a,b). RT–qPCR assays were performed using lung tissues from KS and KMS mice as well as MEN1‐overexpressing or ‐knockdown cell lines. The results revealed that MMP10 RNA levels differed significantly among the groups (Figures 2c and S2a–f).

The TNMplot integrated database was used to analyze MMP10 expression in tumor and normal tissues. We found that MMP10 expression was significantly upregulated in lung tumor tissues (Figure 2d). Survival analysis of lung cancer patients in the kmplot database revealed that patients with high MMP10 expression had shorter overall survival (Figure 2e). To validate these findings, 64 clinical lung adenocarcinoma specimens were analyzed by IHC. Consistent with the public database results, IHC validation confirmed that high MMP10 expression predicted poor overall survival (Figure 2f). Further chromatin accessibility analysis was conducted using lung tumor tissues from KS and KMS mice. Moreover, the degree of chromatin openness at the MMP10 distal intergenic region was significantly greater in KMS mice than in KS mice (Figure 2g). Based on these findings, we speculate that menin regulates angiogenesis in lung tumors through the regulation of MMP10.

The Inhibitory Effect of Menin on Angiogenesis Is Dependents on MMP10

To determine whether the inhibitory effect of menin on angiogenesis in lung cancer is dependent on MMP10, we first transfected an ectopic MMP10 construct into A549 or NCI‐H1299 cells and evaluated the effects on invasion and tube formation in vascular endothelial cells cultured with CM from MMP10‐overexpressing lung cancer cells (Figure S3a). As expected, CM from MMP10‐overexpressing A549 or NCI‐H1299 cells resulted in marked changes in the invasion (Figures 3a,b and S3b,c) and tube formation abilities (Figures 3a,c and S3b,d) of HUVECs. Next, we investigated the role of menin in MMP10‐dependent angiogenesis in human lung cancer cells. Culture of HUVECs with CM derived from MEN1‐overexpressing cells reduced HUVEC invasion (Figure 3d–f), tube formation (Figures 3g,h and S3e,f), and spheroid sprouting (Figure 3i–k) potential of HUVECs. In contrast, co‐overexpression of MEN1 and MMP10 in both lung cancer cell lines, followed by culture of HUVECs with the collected CM, reversed the inhibitory effects of MEN1 overexpression on HUVEC invasion, tube formation, and spheroid sprouting (Figures 3d–k and S3e,f). These findings indicate that the inhibitory effect of menin on lung cancer angiogenesis is mediated by MMP10, which is essential for the angiogenesis‐promoting function of MEN1‐deficient lung cancer cells.

Menin Inhibits the Secretion of MMP10

To further investigate the regulatory relationship between menin and MMP10, we assessed MMP10 expression via IHC in lung tissues from KS and KMS mice. The results revealed that MMP10 expression was significantly higher in the lung tissues of KMS mice than in KS mice (Figure 4a,b). Furthermore, MMP10 expression was detected in human lung cancer patients from high and low‐expression groups. The results revealed that MMP10 expression was significantly greater in the low‐expression group than in the high‐expression group (Figure 4a,b). Additionally, MMP10 expression was evaluated by immunofluorescence and ELISA after MEN1 overexpression or knockdown in lung cancer cells. The results revealed that MMP10 expression decreased following menin overexpression (Figure 4c). Conversely, menin knockdown upregulated MMP10 expression (Figure 4d). ELISA results further revealed that MMP10 secretion was significantly decreased in the CM of MEN1‐overexpressing lung cancer cells, whereas co‐overexpression of MMP10 partially reversed this effect (Figure 4e,f).

Western blotting and immunofluorescence analysis revealed that menin overexpression inhibited MMP10 expression, whereas MMP10 overexpression did not affect menin expression (Figures 4g and S4a,b), suggesting that menin acts as an upstream regulator of MMP10. To determine whether MMP10 is a direct regulatory target gene of menin, chromatin immunoprecipitation analysis was performed. The results revealed that menin indirectly bound to the MMP10 promoter (Figure 4h,i). We speculate that menin inhibits angiogenesis in lung cancer by indirectly suppressing MMP10 transcription, thereby inhibiting the malignant progression of lung cancer. Loss of menin upregulates MMP10 expression, further promoting angiogenesis and subsequently facilitating the malignant progression of lung cancer.

Menin Indirectly Inhibits the Secretion of MMP10 by Suppressing the Transcriptional Activation of JunD

To further explore the mechanism by which menin regulates MMP10, we predicted the upstream transcription factors regulating MMP10 and the downstream gene groups regulated by MEN1 using the hTFtarget, ChIP‐Atlas, and FIMO‐JASPAR databases (Figure 5a,b). Interestingly, JunD was identified as a key predicted transcription factor through which menin regulates MMP10 (Figure 5c). Unexpectedly, ATAC‐seq analysis revealed that the degree of chromatin opening of JunD in the lung tissue was not significantly different between KMS mice and KS mice (Figure S5a). Analysis of the TNMplot database revealed no significant correlation between menin (MEN1) and JunD gene expression, and the results showed that there was no significant correlation between MEN1 and JunD gene expression (Figure S5b). Consistent with these findings, neither MEN1 overexpression nor MEN1 knockdown affected JunD transcription in vitro (Figure S5c,d). However, western blot analysis revealed that although menin overexpression did not affect total JunD expression, it inhibited the expression of phosphorylated JunD (Figure 5d). Conversely, MEN1 knockdown increased JunD phosphorylation (Figure 5d). JunD overexpression did not affect menin expression (Figure 5e). Protein co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) analysis further revealed that menin and JunD directly bind to each other (Figure 5f).

To elucidate the regulatory effect of JunD on MMP10, MMP10 expression was evaluated following JunD overexpression. Western blot analysis showed that MMP10 expression was positively regulated by JunD (Figures 5e and S5f). Moreover, JunD overexpression reversed the inhibitory effect of menin overexpression on MMP10 expression (Figures 5g and S5g). Transcriptional analysis of the TNMplot database revealed that MMP10 expression was weakly positively correlated with JunD (Figure 5h). Furthermore, MMP10 expression was upregulated after JunD overexpression (Figures 5i,j and S5h).

To further verify whether MMP10 is a direct transcriptional target of JunD, we performed bioinformatics analysis and a series of experiments. The analysis identified three potential JunD binding sites (E1, E2, and E3) within the MMP10 promoter (Figure 5k,l). ChIP analysis revealed significant binding at all three sites, with the most significant observed at E1 (Figure 5m). Deletion of the E1 and E2 sites from the MMP10‐luciferase construct significantly reduced luciferase activity (Figure 5n). These findings suggest that JunD binds to the MMP10 promoter and upregulates MMP10 transcriptional activity during MEN1‐deficient lung cancer progression. Collectively, these results indicate that JunD is the key mediator of the inhibitory effect of menin on MMP10.

Menin Regulates Angiogenesis in Lung Cancer Through the AKT/ERK Signaling Pathway

In previous studies, drug resistance has frequently been observed in clinical antiangiogenic therapy targeting the VEGF/VEGFR axis, and compensatory activation of the EGFR signaling pathway has been identified as one of the core underlying mechanisms [29]. Notably, EGFR‐mutant NSCLC has become a pivotal area of research in precision oncology [30]. Previous studies have demonstrated that crotonoside suppresses EGFR signaling in NSCLC, thereby inhibiting tumor angiogenesis through the downregulation of the downstream PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK signaling pathways [7, 31, 32]. Therefore, we speculated that menin deficiency may regulate angiogenesis in lung cancer by activating the EGFR/AKT/ERK signaling pathway. To validate this hypothesis, candidate targets containing MMP10 were imported into the STRING database to construct a protein–protein interaction network. The results showed that MMP10 can indirectly regulate EGFR and its downstream signaling pathways (Figure S6a). After HUVECs were cultured with CM from MEN1‐overexpressing lung cancer cells, EGFR activation was detected. The results revealed that MEN1 overexpression significantly inhibited EGFR phosphorylation, whereas MMP10 overexpression promoted EGFR phosphorylation (Figure 6a). Conversely, MEN1 knockdown promoted EGFR phosphorylation (Figure 6b). Moreover, MMP10 overexpression partially reversed the inhibitory effect of EGFR phosphorylation induced by menin overexpression (Figure 6a). In contrast, MMP10 inhibition partially reversed the effect of menin knockdown on EGFR phosphorylation (Figure 6b).

Western blot analysis further revealed that AKT and ERK phosphorylation decreased following MEN1 overexpression (Figure 6c) but increased following MEN1 knockdown in lung cancer cells (Figure S6b). AKT and ERK phosphorylation was also detected in lung tissues from KS and KMS mice, revealing that AKT and ERK were activated in lung tissues from KMS mice (Figure 6d). Furthermore, we investigated whether menin regulates the AKT and ERK pathways through EGFR. We found that EGFR overexpression attenuated the inhibitory effect of MEN1 overexpression on the AKT and ERK pathways (Figure 6e). Consistent with these findings, EGFR inhibition diminished the effect of MEN1 knockdown on the AKT and ERK pathways (Figure 6f).

Phenotypic experiments revealed that MMP10 overexpression promoted invasion and tube formation (Figures 6g–i and S6d–f). However, EGFR knockdown reversed the promoting effect of MMP10 overexpression (Figures 6g–i and S6d–f). Furthermore, the AKT and ERK signaling pathway inhibitors AZD5365 (AKT) and U0126 (ERK) were used to assess whether menin regulates lung cancer angiogenesis through these signaling pathways (Figure S6c). The results revealed that inhibition of AKT and ERK signaling pathways reversed the promoting effect of MEN1 knockdown on invasion and tube formation (Figures 6j–o and Figure S6g–l). These results demonstrate that menin deficiency promotes angiogenesis in lung cancer through MMP10 upregulation to activate the EGFR signaling pathway, ultimately facilitating the malignant progression of lung cancer.

Inhibiting MMP10 Reverses the Regulation of Low Menin Expression Induced Angiogenesis Promotion and Tumor Progression

Considering the significant role of MMP10 in the ability of low menin expression to promote angiogenesis in lung cancer, we further investigated whether MMP10 inhibitor could reverse angiogenesis and tumor‐promoting effects in lung cancer. Interestingly, the results showed that inhibiting MMP10 reversed the invasion and tube formation of vascular endothelial cells caused by MEN1 knockdown in lung cancer cells (Figures 7a–d and S7a–d). Furthermore, we assessed the pharmacological effect of MMP10 inhibitor on spontaneous lung cancer in KMS mice. KMS mice were treated with 25 mg/kg MMP10 inhibitor for 2 weeks after five days of TAM‐induced gene knockout; then the mice were sacrificed on day 16 after MMP10 inhibitor treatment. Morphological observation indicated that the size of the lung tissue clearly decreased in the MMP10 inhibitor group (Figure 7e). Assessment of lung weight and lung coefficient revealed that KMS mice had significantly higher lung weight and lung coefficient (lung weight/body weight) than WT mice. Strikingly, MMP10 inhibitor treatment abolished these elevations(Figure 7f).

Strikingly, MMP10 inhibitor‐treated mice exhibited tumor‐free development on the surface of lung tissues. H&E staining demonstrated that MMP10 inhibitor treatment significantly decreased the number of tumor nodules in mouse lung tissues, and only peripheral hyperplasia or scarce tumor nodules were occasionally observed in the lungs (Figure 7g). EGFR/AKT/ERK pathway activity was also suppressed after treatment with the MMP10 inhibitor (Figure 7h). MMP10 inhibitor treatment clearly reduced the expression of MMP10 and moderately decreased the accumulation of Ki67 in the lung tissues of KMS mice (Figure 7i). Additionally, the MMP10 inhibitor effectively attenuated the increase in CD31 and CD34 expression induced by the loss of menin in KMS mice (Figure 7i). Subsequently, the expression of the EGFR ligand HB‐EGF [31], which was cleaved by MMP10, was detected by western blotting and IHC, and the results showed that the expression of HB‐EGF was upregulated in the lung tissue of KMS mice (Figure S7e,g). However, this upregulation was reversed by the MMP10 inhibitor (Figure S7f,g). These results demonstrate that MMP10 inhibition predominantly suppresses the growth of lung tumors with low menin expression.

Discussion

Lung cancer remains a major global medical challenge, primarily because of its rapid progression and the lack of clarity regarding its underlying mechanisms. Further in‐depth investigations into its pathogenesis are essential for the development of more effective therapeutic strategies. Antiangiogenic therapy is a key approach in lung cancer treatment; however, it has certain limitations, including restricted applicability across different tumor types. Therefore, studying the mechanisms driving malignant progression in lung cancer is critical for early screening and the development of more targeted therapeutics. Previous antiangiogenic research has focused largely on the well‐known VEGF/VEGFR signaling pathway. Over the past four decades, various drugs targeting this pathway have been developed and have demonstrated some clinical efficacy. Nevertheless, challenges such as transient therapeutic effects and low sensitivity persist. This study identifies menin as a novel antiangiogenic marker in lung cancer. Clinically, menin expression inversely correlated with the expression of the endothelial markers CD31/CD34. In mice, conditional menin deletion increased tumor vascular density. Mechanistically, loss of menin disrupted its interaction with JunD, promoted JunD phosphorylation, and increased MMP10 transcription(Figure 8).

The graphical abstract of the mechanism through which menin deficiency promotes Kras mutated lung cancer by accelerating angiogenesis through MMP10, and the investigation of potential inhibitors. Left: In lung cancer cells, MEN1 deficiency reduced the menin‐JunD interaction; subsequently, increasing JunD phosphorylation, facilitating the binding of JunD to the MMP10 promoter and increasing MMP10 secretion. Secreted MMP10 cleaves pro‐HB‐EGF, releasing soluble HB‐EGF, which activates EGFR on vascular endothelial cells and triggers the PI3K‐Akt and MEK–ERK signaling pathways, thereby promoting angiogenesis and tumor progression. Right: MMP10 inhibitor treatment suppresses MMP10 activity, leading to reduced pro‐HB‐EGF cleavage and EGFR signaling activation, which ultimately inhibits angiogenesis and delays malignant progression.

Kras mutations are major drivers of NSCLC [33]. The prevalence of Kras mutations is 20%–25% in Western populations and 10%–15% in Asian populations [34]. Oncogenic Kras mutations induce sustained activation of proliferative signaling cascades, thereby promoting cancer progression and conferring resistance to standard therapies [35]. Our previous research showed that Kras mutations promote immune escape in lung cancer through SET1A‐mediated Yap activation and FGL1 overexpression through MEK/ERK signaling [36]. PI3K serves as a second key effector that supports Kras‐driven tumor growth, largely through the activation of AKT. A recent study revealed that the interaction between Ras and PI3K is required for Ras‐driven lung tumorigenesis [37]. Notably, our study revealed that the conditional deletion of the MEN1 gene significantly increased the expression of phosphorylated AKT and ERK proteins in Kras G12D mutation‐induced lung cancer in mice. In contrast, treatment with an MMP10 inhibitor markedly reduced the levels of phosphorylated AKT and ERK.

The multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1) tumor suppressor gene is mutated in patients with MEN1 syndrome, which is characterized by the development of parathyroid, pituitary, and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (PanNETs) [38]. However, a recent study identified MEN1 as the top hit that confers differential dropout effects in vitro and in vivo [39]. JunD was the first menin‐interacting protein identified following the cloning of the MEN1 gene [40], and it is the AP‐1 transcription factor required for Ras‐induced lung cancer [41]. Recent studies have also indicated that both JunD and β‐catenin are necessary for the altered oncogenic potential triggered by MEN1 inactivation in AR‐independent prostate cancer cells [42] and that menin missense mutations directly lead to the loss of the menin–JunD interaction [43]. Consistent with previous reports, our experiments further elucidated the molecular mechanism by which menin interacts with JunD: this complex suppresses JunD phosphorylation, thereby preventing its binding to the MMP10 promoter and repressing MMP10 transcription(Figure 8).

The inhibition of angiogenesis has been demonstrated to be an effective and promising combination therapy option [44], and numerous antiangiogenic agents targeting VEGFα have been used in clinical lung cancer treatment [20]. However, the characteristics of targeted therapies frequently lead to drug resistance or insensitivity, highlighting the need to explore alternative molecular mechanisms of angiogenesis. With increasing investigations into the role of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) in cancer progression, migration, and metastasis, various MMPs have emerged as attractive therapeutic targets for anticancer drug development [45]. These enzymes play major roles in cellular differentiation, proliferation, wound healing, apoptosis, and angiogenesis [46]. Inhibitors targeting MMPs [1, 2, 3, 7, 12, 14] have attracted considerable attention as potential anticancer drug targets and as inhibitors that specifically target different MMPs involved in cancer progression [45]. Notably, our study provides the first evidence that the loss or downregulation of menin promotes malignant progression in lung cancer through proangiogenic factor MMP10‐mediated angiogenesis. This finding prompts us to consider the use of MMP inhibitors (MMPIs) to suppress angiogenesis in lung cancer. Based on analyses of a database of 2166 lung adenocarcinoma patients and 64 tissue microarray samples, approximately 40%–55% of patients exhibited high MMP10 expression, suggesting that this population may be a potential candidate group for MMP10 inhibitor‐targeted therapy. Although only a small number of mice were used to validate the MMP10 inhibitor over a short experimental period, our findings suggest that early assessment of menin and MMP10 protein expression levels could enable screening of lung cancer patients. Specifically, lung cancers characterized by low menin expression and high MMP10 expression may be more responsive to MMP10‐therapeutic strategies. Furthermore, these findings may contribute to the development of early screening strategies for lung cancer patients, thereby improving the early prediction of drug efficacy.

In summary, analyses of human lung cancer datasets, together with experiments in animal models and cultured cells, validated the role of menin in regulating angiogenesis in lung cancer. These findings provide novel insights and conceptual frameworks for understanding angiogenic mechanisms in lung cancer patients. Tumor angiogenesis is regulated by multidimensional interactions within the tumor microenvironment, including hypoxia, immune‐inflammatory responses, proangiogenic factors, oncogene activation, and metabolic reprogramming. Future research should comprehensively investigate the connections among these mechanisms to enable more holistic analyses of the processes driving malignant progression in lung cancer, ultimately supporting the development of personalized precision therapy strategies.
