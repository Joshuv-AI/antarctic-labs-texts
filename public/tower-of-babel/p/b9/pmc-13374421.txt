Childhood socioeconomic status and episodic memory in later life: the moderating roles of education and social class in the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (2002–2024)
Authors: Tung Le, James Gilleen, Amanda Lee, Asri Maharani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSocioeconomic disadvantage in childhood is linked to cognitive ageing, but the extent to which adult socioeconomic factors modify these associations remains unclear. We examined whether childhood socioeconomic status relates to later-life episodic memory and whether education and adult social class modify this relationship.MethodsObservational longitudinal secondary analysis of the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing, waves 1–11 (2002–2024). The sample comprised 6939 participants aged ≥50 years, contributing 52 742 observations. Childhood socioeconomic status was derived using latent class analysis from four indicators (main carer’s occupation, household overcrowding, availability of household facilities and number of books in the house). Total episodic memory (0–20) combined immediate and delayed 10-word recall at each wave. Linear mixed-effects models with repeated measures estimated associations between childhood socioeconomic status and episodic memory and tested interactions with education and adult occupational social class, adjusting for sociodemographic, behavioural, health and psychosocial covariates.ResultsA two-class (poor vs non-poor) childhood socioeconomic status solution fit best. Non-poor childhood socioeconomic status was associated with higher episodic memory across models (β=0.53–0.99; p<0.001). The advantage of non-poor childhood socioeconomic status was smaller among those in higher adult occupational classes (interaction β=−0.40; p<0.001), whereas the interaction with education was not significant. Findings were robust in imputed analyses.ConclusionPoor childhood socioeconomic status is associated with lower episodic memory in later life. Adult occupational social class modestly attenuates this association, whereas education shows no evidence of effect modification. These longitudinal associations suggest that future public health research should evaluate structural frameworks that consider both early-life conditions and sustained socioeconomic support across the life course to address cognitive ageing.Keywords: Sociodemographic Factors, Epidemiologic Factors, Education, statistics and numerical data

Introduction

Episodic memory, the capacity to recall personally experienced events, is a key component of cognitive function and one of the earliest domains to decline with age.1 2 Such decline threatens independence and quality of life.3 4 Impaired episodic memory can be an early clinical indicator of dementia and a predictor of Alzheimer’s disease and related neuropathologies.5 Understanding the social and biological factors that shape variability in memory trajectories is therefore essential to promoting cognitive health in ageing populations.

Socioeconomic status (SES) is a major determinant of cognitive development and ageing.6 Disadvantage in childhood is associated with lower cognitive performance and faster cognitive decline across adulthood and later life.7 8 This life-course persistence is often framed by the pathways hypothesis: early disadvantage is linked with less favourable educational and occupational trajectories, reduced cognitive reserve and greater vulnerability to age-related decline.9 Cognitive reserve, neural and cognitive resilience to pathology and ageing,10 11 is accumulated across the life course, including through lifelong education.12 Adult occupational social class and material resources may also shape reserve via cognitive stimulation, stress exposure and health behaviours.11 13

Yet evidence is mixed on the extent to which later-life circumstances can attenuate the effects of early disadvantage. Some studies suggest that upward social mobility and education may buffer associations between childhood SES and later cognition,14 whereas others emphasise cumulative disadvantage, whereby early adversity continues to constrain later outcomes.15 Most of the evidence relies on single childhood SES indicators such as parental education16 or parental status,17 which reduces the multidimensional nature of early socioeconomic environments and limits validity.

To address these gaps, this study employs latent class analysis (LCA)18 19 to classify distinct childhood SES profiles based on multiple indicators. We include caregivers’ occupation, household crowding and access to learning materials as indicators in SES classifications. This offers a more comprehensive evaluation, based on multidimensional profiles, and offers a basis for evaluating how adult education and social class may influence episodic memory trajectories. Our analysis uses a large, nationally representative cohort of adults aged 50 years and older to classify distinct childhood SES profiles using LCA. It then examines longitudinal associations with episodic memory performance and applies linear mixed-effects modelling to evaluate whether adult education and social class moderate these associations.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted an observational longitudinal study using secondary data from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA).20 21 All available waves (1–11; 2002–2024) were included (figure 1). Briefly, the analytic sample comprised community-dwelling adults aged ≥50 years with data on episodic memory and childhood socioeconomic indicators.

ELSA sample flow. ELSA, English Longitudinal Study of Ageing.

Childhood SES

Childhood SES was measured using four indicators: (1) main carer’s occupation (manual or no job vs non-manual), (2) household overcrowding (rooms-to-people ratio <1 indicating overcrowding),8 22 (3) availability of household facilities and (4) number of books in the house (<10 vs ≥10). For household facilities, five individual binary amenities from the ELSA questionnaire (bathing, cold and hot running water, indoor toilet and heating) were summed to create a composite score (0 to 5), which was categorised into low (0 to 2) versus high (3 to 5) availability.23 For learning materials, the number of books was dichotomised based on the lowest category in the original questionnaire (0–10 books vs more than 10 books), which serves as an established marker of severe cognitive and literacy deprivation in early life.24

Episodic memory

Episodic memory was assessed at every wave using immediate and delayed word-recall tasks. Participants heard a list of 10 unrelated words and recalled as many as possible immediately and after a short delay. Each subtest ranged from 0 to 10; the sum (0–20) represented total episodic memory. These measures are validated and sensitive to age-related decline.25 Episodic memory was treated as a time-varying, continuous outcome measured across all available waves (waves 1–11).

Education and social class

Education was defined by the highest qualification attained, using the most recent available record, grouped as: lower than high school (eg, Certificate of Secondary Education, National Vocational Qualification 1–2 or none), high school (A-levels or equivalent) and higher than high school (National Vocational Qualification 4–5, diploma or degree).20 26 Adult social class was derived from the National Statistics Socio-economic Classification (NS-SEC) using the participant’s current or most recent occupation, collapsed into: higher (managerial/professional), intermediate (intermediate and small employers/own-account workers) and lower/unemployed (lower supervisory/technical, semi-routine, routine and other).20

Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on ELSA and international evidence linking them to socioeconomic background and cognition.20 27 28 Age (years; time-varying) and sex (male/female) were included, along with marital status (living alone vs married/partnered), ethnicity (Caucasian vs other), smoking (current vs not current) and alcohol consumption (≥5 days/week vs <5 days/week). Health indicators comprised the number of chronic conditions (hypertension, angina, myocardial infarction, heart failure, arthritis, diabetes, stroke, chronic lung disease, asthma, osteoporosis, cancer, Parkinson’s disease), mobility limitations and need for help with activities of daily living (ADL). Social capital, reflecting social engagement/support, was derived from five items (frequency of going to the cinema, eating out, visiting museums, attending theatre, caring for someone) and treated as continuous. Depressive symptoms were measured using the 10-item Centre for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10).

Life-course conceptual framework

A directed acyclic graph (DAG) was developed a priori using DAGitty to represent hypothesised life-course relationships between childhood SES, adult socioeconomic factors, health-related characteristics and episodic memory (figure 2). The DAG served as a conceptual framework to guide model specification, covariate selection and interpretation of findings. It was not used to identify a formal causal estimation, nor were direct and indirect effects formally estimated through mediation, path analysis or structural equation modelling.

Within this framework, childhood SES was specified as the primary exposure and episodic memory as the outcome. Age, sex, ethnicity and marital status were treated as background characteristics that may influence both life-course socioeconomic trajectories and later-life cognitive function. Educational attainment and adult occupational social class were conceptualised as key socioeconomic pathways through which childhood circumstances may shape cognitive outcomes across the life course. Health behaviours (smoking and alcohol consumption), psychosocial factors (social capital and depressive symptoms) and health status (chronic conditions, mobility limitations and ADL) were considered potential downstream pathways linking childhood socioeconomic conditions to episodic memory in later life. The DAG therefore provides a theoretical representation of hypothesised relationships among study variables and should be interpreted as a conceptual model rather than evidence of causal pathways.

Statistical analyses

Analyses proceeded in two stages. First, LCA was applied to derive childhood SES profiles from the four indicators. Competing models were compared using Akaike (AIC) and Bayesian (BIC) information criteria, parsimony (avoiding classes with <5% of the sample) and classification accuracy (entropy ≥0.80).18 19 Individuals were assigned to their most probable class for subsequent analyses. Second, linear mixed-effects models with repeated longitudinal observations across all eleven waves (waves 1 to 11) were used to estimate associations between childhood SES and episodic memory and to test effect modification by education and adult social class. Four models were specified: model 1a estimated the main effects of education, adjusting for poor childhood SES, educational attainment, and a predefined set of covariates: age, age-squared (to account for non-linear ageing effects), sex, marital status, ethnicity, smoking status, alcohol use, number of chronic conditions, mobility limitations, help with ADL, social capital and depressive symptoms. Adult occupational social class was omitted from model 1a. Model 1b added the multiplicative interaction term between poor childhood SES and educational attainment to the parameters of model 1a. Model 2a evaluated the main effects of adult social class by removing educational attainment from model 1a and replacing it with adult occupational social class, holding all other covariates and poor childhood SES constant. Finally, model 2b directly added the multiplicative interaction term between poor childhood SES and adult occupational social class to the specifications of model 2a. Results are presented as regression coefficients (β) with 95% CIs and two-sided p values.

Sensitivity analysis

To address partial non-response and evaluate the robustness of our complete-case findings, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was implemented, generating 20 imputed datasets.29 Within the longitudinal analytical pool of 52 742 observations, a formal missingness pattern analysis revealed that 82.68% of observations were completely observed, while 17.32% had missing data on at least one covariate. Among health and lifestyle covariates, missingness on individual variables ranged from 0.02% for functional limitations to 10.54% for alcohol consumption (online supplemental table 1). Under the assumption that data were Missing at Random (MAR),30 four specific variables with missing values were imputed: highest educational qualification, alcohol use, chronic conditions and depressive symptoms. Neither the longitudinal outcome variable (episodic memory) nor the main exposure (childhood SES) was subject to imputation. Both were registered as regular variables to ensure that the estimated exposure-outcome coefficients relied strictly on observed parameters. The imputation model included all baseline demographic characteristics, wave fixed effects and observed longitudinal memory scores to optimise the plausibility of the MAR assumption. Mixed-effects models were re-estimated across the imputed datasets and pooled using Rubin’s rules.31 All statistical analyses were performed using Stata V.19.5.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Descriptive characteristics of the analytic sample (52 742 person-observations from 6939 participants) are shown in online supplemental table 1. The mean age was 68.6 years (SD=9.6); 55.3% were female, and all were aged ≥50 years. The mean total episodic memory score was 10.33 (SD=3.62), with 2.3% missing. Most participants were married/partnered (65.4%), and 63.1% had at least a high-school education. Adult occupational social class (three-category NS-SEC) comprised 48.2% lower/unemployed, 22.3% intermediate and 29.6% higher. The majority identified as Caucasian (97.9%). Regarding health and behaviours, 11.3% were current smokers and 20.5% reported alcohol use on ≥5 days/week. Participants reported a mean of 1.84 chronic conditions (SD=1.54; range 0–10), a mean CES-D-10 score of 1.39 (SD=1.85; range 0–8) and a mean social capital score of 3.21 (SD=1.29; range 0–5).

LCA identified two childhood SES profiles (table 1). The two-class solution (entropy=0.81) was preferred to the three-class model on AIC/BIC and classification accuracy. Classes were labelled ‘non-poor’ (63%) and ‘poor’ (37%). Compared with the non-poor class, participants in the poor class were more likely to have a manual/unemployed main carer (89.4% vs 53.0%), live in overcrowded households (95.2% vs 80.7%), have limited household facilities (62.9% vs 10.5%) and report <10 books at home (68.2% vs 0.5%) (online supplemental table 2).

Discussion

Using LCA applied to four complementary childhood indicators, this study identified a two-class solution distinguishing non-poor from poor childhood SES with high classification accuracy. This multidimensional characterisation, rather than reliance on a single proxy such as parental occupation, revealed coherent and empirically driven profiles of childhood deprivation.32 Non-poor childhood SES was consistently associated with higher episodic memory across all model specifications, even after adjustment for adult socioeconomic position, health and psychosocial factors. Adult occupational class attenuated, but did not eliminate, this association, whereas education showed no evidence of effect modification. These findings are interpreted within a cumulative disadvantage framework33 and address two specific gaps: the methodological limitations of single-indicator approaches to childhood SES and the unresolved question of whether adult socioeconomic achievement can compensate for early disadvantage.

The application of LCA to derive childhood SES profiles merits discussion as a methodological contribution in its own right. Additive composite indices, which are widely used in the literature, assume that each indicator contributes equally and independently to an underlying SES dimension.23 This assumption is unlikely to hold in practice, as material deprivation, occupational disadvantage and cognitive resource scarcity tend to cluster together rather than occur in isolation.34 LCA identifies latent subgroups based on the joint probability distribution of multiple indicators simultaneously, allowing the data to reveal whether coherent profiles exist without imposing an a priori scoring structure.35 Crucially, the conditional probabilities across the four indicators were substantively coherent: the poor class was characterised by near-universal material disadvantage, limited household facilities, a manual or unemployed main carer and, most strikingly, an almost complete absence of books in the house. This convergence of deprivation across material, occupational and cognitive-resource domains is consistent with cumulative disadvantage theory.36 It confirms that the class distinction is not an artefact of a single dominant indicator. By contrast, studies relying on parental occupation or parental education alone cannot capture this multidimensional clustering.16

Our primary finding that non-poor childhood SES is associated with better episodic memory after accounting for adult socioeconomic position supports a cumulative disadvantage framework.13 Prior work similarly links favourable childhood environments with cognitive advantages.16 23 37 38 A recent meta-analysis by Skoblow et al reported stronger associations for verbal memory,37 and international evidence links higher parental education with slower decline.16 Analyses using harmonised Health and Retirement Study (HRS) and ELSA data have also reported higher childhood SES related to better baseline cognition,38 while ELSA-based work identified limited access to books in childhood as a strong predictor of lower later-life memory.23 Collectively, these studies indicate that both material and cognitive resources in childhood have enduring relevance for later-life cognition.

However, other investigations have reported attenuated or null effects once adult SES is controlled. Evidence from the Wisconsin Longitudinal Study (5074 high school graduates from one US state) indicated that midlife socioeconomic position accounted for most of the link between childhood conditions and late-life cognition.39 This raises the issue of whether geo-historical contexts should be considered. Many of the cohorts we study now have lived through increasingly improved circumstances. University education tends to be more accessible now than it was in the latter phases of the last century.40 Relative ‘poverty’ is complex; access to books, for example, has been changed by easy access to community libraries, facilitated through easier and more available transport networks.41 We must consider all circumstances throughout the life course, and capture all relevant, contextual socioeconomic, demographic and clinical states. Our study focused on episodic memory, a domain particularly sensitive to environmental enrichment, but also to the clinical covariates. Menopause, or COVID-19 infection, for example, has been linked with episodic memory loss and brain ‘fogging’.42,44 Marden et al evaluated more nationally representative HRS data, and their findings support lower adult education and wealth as predictors of poorer cognitive function.45 Yet their application of global cognition measures may have masked the domain-specific effects of episodic memory measures.

Our longitudinal regression analysis modelling of social class supported a ‘diminishing-returns’ or ‘compression’ pattern: among individuals in higher occupational groups, meaning that the cognitive benefit of favourable childhood conditions lessened. However, we did find that early disadvantage exerted a stronger and more persistent influence. This pattern aligns with a cumulative-advantage rather than a compensatory model, in which socioeconomic resources accumulate across the life course but remain constrained by early endowments.46 Previous longitudinal analyses of ELSA concur, indicating that greater occupational complexity in midlife may slow decline, but it will not close the effects of early disadvantage.4 Several mechanisms may explain this partial compensation. Individuals from disadvantaged backgrounds may attain high-status jobs later or less consistently, limiting exposure to cognitively stimulating environments that enhance neural plasticity.11 47 Upwardly mobile individuals may also carry residual health or psychosocial disadvantages that offset occupational benefits.48 Moreover, occupational advantage primarily operates through behavioural and social pathways, improved health management, reduced chronic stress and richer social networks, rather than by reversing early neurodevelopmental constraints.11 49 Consequently, the cognitive pay-offs of professional occupations are not uniform: they tend to sustain existing reserves among the advantaged while offering only partial remediation among the disadvantaged.4 50 These findings underscore that timing and duration of socioeconomic exposures, not only their presence, determine their cumulative impact on cognitive ageing.

The absence of a significant interaction between childhood SES and educational attainment warrants careful interpretation. Rather than indicating that education is irrelevant to the childhood SES-memory pathway, the null interaction is consistent with a mediation model: education may partly transmit the effects of childhood SES on cognition rather than modify the strength of that association.6 51 Children from non-poor backgrounds were substantially more likely to attain higher qualifications, and the strong independent main effect of education on episodic confirms its predictive importance. From a sociohistorical perspective, access to grammar schools and universities in post-war Britain remained highly stratified by parental background well into the 1970s,26 52 meaning that educational attainment in this cohort was itself largely determined by childhood SES. This structural confounding may attenuate any apparent moderating effect.14 Studies using single childhood SES indicators may have attributed a moderating role to education partly through residual confounding: by deriving childhood SES profiles from multiple simultaneous indicators, the present analysis provides a more stringent test of the moderation hypothesis.

Taken together, these findings address both gaps identified at the outset of this study. The use of LCA across four concurrent indicators moves beyond the reductionism of single-proxy approaches. It demonstrates that multidimensional childhood SES profiles retain independent predictive validity for later-life episodic memory, even after accounting for adult socioeconomic position. The interaction analyses provide evidence that adult socioeconomic improvement compresses, but does not eliminate, the cognitive legacy of childhood disadvantage. This is consistent with a cumulative rather than compensatory model of life-course socioeconomic influence on cognitive ageing and underscores the primacy of early-life conditions as determinants of later cognitive health.

This study has several strengths. It used nationally representative ELSA data, encompassing eleven waves and two decades of follow-up, allowing robust longitudinal estimation of within-person change. Childhood SES was constructed from multiple complementary indicators via LCA, offering a multidimensional representation of early environments. The explicit testing of interactions between early-life and adult-life SES adds to evidence on the timing and accumulation of socioeconomic influences. Repeated cognitive assessments and the use of multiple imputation enhance reliability and generalisability.

Some limitations warrant consideration. One important consideration when interpreting our results is the potential for recall bias53 in reporting childhood conditions in mid to late adulthood. Crucially, our reliance on retrospective data may introduce systematic, differential recall bias, whereby participants with lower episodic memory performance at the time of the interview may recall their early-life circumstances differently and potentially less accurately than those with preserved cognitive function. In terms of the potential direction of this bias, if individuals with poorer memory systematically under-report their childhood SES resources, this differential misclassification could artificially inflate the observed coefficients, biasing the estimates away from the null. On the other hand, if cognitive decline primarily introduces random recall errors, this non-differential misclassification would attenuate the observed associations, leading to more conservative estimates of the life-course influence. In terms of the potential magnitude of this bias, existing literature comparing retrospective life-history reports against prospective birth cohort data suggests that while the recall of specific historical housing infrastructure may be less precise, retrospective accounts of broad, structural childhood SES demonstrate moderate-to-good long-term accuracy.54 55 To mitigate the magnitude of these recall errors, we parameterised our individual childhood indicators into broad dichotomous categories rather than continuous metrics, which has been documented to provide a more stable and reliable reflection of general early-life socioeconomic environments.56 Nevertheless, the possibility of differential recall bias cannot be entirely ruled out, and these longitudinal associations should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Second, the generalisability of our findings must be considered alongside the inevitable impact of survivor bias. Selective survival may have influenced our results at two levels. Initially, by restricting the sampling frame to community-dwelling adults aged 50 years and older, individuals who experienced the most severe childhood socioeconomic deprivation and subsequently died before reaching midlife were excluded.4 Subsequently, over the course of 11 waves and two decades of follow-up, attrition due to mortality or severe morbidity naturally led to a sample composed of individuals with greater health and cognitive resilience. Third, unmeasured factors such as childhood cognitive ability, parental mental health or genetic influences may confound the associations observed. Adjusting for adult health and behavioural covariates yields conservative direct effects, potentially underestimating total life-course influence. Fourth, the ELSA database restricts the operationalisation of adult education to the highest qualification attained. Consequently, our models lose valuable contextual information regarding any subsequent lifelong learning, vocational upskilling or mid-career job training that participants may have undertaken across their life course, which could independently contribute to cognitive reserve accumulation.57 Finally, the analysis focused on episodic memory; other cognitive domains, such as attention or processing speed, may exhibit distinct life-course patterns.

The empirical findings offer speculative directions for future public health research and life-course intervention designs. Although this observational analysis did not directly test specific policy interventions, these robust long-term associations suggest that early-life environments may be critical parameters to examine when investigating cognitive outcomes decades later. These data suggest that hypothetical target areas for future structural intervention research could focus on early-life conditions, such as optimising housing quality, increasing access to home learning materials and reducing household overcrowding to evaluate their potential to foster cognitive resilience.2 Similarly, in adulthood, prospective research is warranted to evaluate whether promoting equitable educational and occupational opportunities, supporting mental health and strengthening social capital can successfully mitigate accumulated disadvantage. The observed interaction with occupational class highlights the potential for adult socioeconomic improvement to compress, but not fully erase, early inequalities, suggesting that future evaluative research should focus on coordinated frameworks spanning the life course. Specifically, future studies should quantify direct and indirect pathways linking poor childhood SES to later cognition using causal mediation and counterfactual frameworks, and explore heterogeneity by birth cohort, gender and ethnicity. Extending longitudinal analyses to other cognitive domains and incident dementia would clarify whether similar mechanisms operate across outcomes, while validation of retrospective measures through administrative data or sibling comparisons would further enhance causal inference.

Conclusions

Childhood socioeconomic disadvantage is linked to poorer episodic memory in later life, even after accounting for adult socioeconomic position, health and psychosocial resources. Education and occupational class remain strong, independent predictors of memory, and occupational class was also found to moderate the long-term effects of early conditions. Our findings support an approach that combines early investment in childhood environments with sustained support for education and employment in adulthood as the most effective means to promote healthy cognitive ageing.
