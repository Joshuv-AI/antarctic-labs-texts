Distal tibial nail for complex nonunion of open distal tibial fracture with diabetes and prior pin-site infection: A case report
Authors: Wei Wu, Hai Yang, Junqiu Wang, Wei Wang, Lu Yin, Lei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

DTN, a type of tibial intramedullary nail retrogradely inserted through the medial malleolus, represents a novel internal fixation modality for distal tibial fractures [1]. It provides rigid mechanical support to the fracture fragments. This technique provides significant mechanical advantages and stable fixation [2]. Previous studies report a nonunion incidence as high as 6.8 % following tibial fractures, which not only increases the financial burden but also causes physical and psychological sequelae in affected individuals [3,4]. While DTN has shown promise for acute distal tibial fractures, its application in the challenging scenario of infected nonunion following high-grade open distal tibia fractures in diabetic patients remains largely unreported. This case report addresses this gap by presenting the successful application of the DTN in a challenging scenario of Gustilo Type IIIA open distal tibial nonunion complicated by diabetes mellitus and prior pin-site infection. This case report has been prepared in accordance with the Surgical CAse REport (SCARE) 2025 Guidelines [5].

Patient information

A 55-year-old female with documented type 2 diabetes mellitus sustained an open fracture of her left lower leg in a car accident and was diagnosed with an open fracture of the distal left tibia and fibula (Gustilo-Anderson Type IIIA) at a local hospital in April 2022 without any other injuries sustained. She underwent emergency debridement, suturing, and external fixation. The patient had a 2-year history of diabetes mellitus, with insulin therapy achieving good glycemic control. After confirming adequate glycemic control (admission HbA1c: 6.7 %) in February 2023, open reduction with DTN internal fixation combined with autologous iliac crest bone grafting was performed under general anesthesia to manage distal tibial fracture nonunion. No significant complications occurred intraoperatively or postoperatively. Postoperatively, prophylactic antibiotics were administered for 24 h in accordance with standard guidelines for fracture fixation surgery [6]. On postoperative day 1, radiographs showed good alignment of the distal tibial fracture. Three months postoperatively, callus formation was observed at the fracture site. Six months postoperatively, the fracture line had resolved with complete union (Fig. 3). During the latest follow-up examination, two years after the surgery, the patient demonstrated a significant recovery and regained normal running/jumping and full weight-bearing capacity (Fig. 4). Surgical technique

The procedure was performed by an experienced orthopedic trauma team specialized in complex lower extremity reconstruction. During the operation, the patient was positioned supine with the left buttock elevated. A 6-cm longitudinal incision was made on the anterolateral aspect of the left calf to expose the nonunion site. The fracture ends were debrided thoroughly, with removal of fibrous scar tissue and sclerotic bone until bleeding bone surfaces were exposed. The medullary canal was reamed.

A 2-cm longitudinal incision was made over the tip of the medial malleolus, taking care to protect the deltoid ligament. The guide pin was inserted under fluoroscopic guidance to ensure proper entry point and trajectory. Through a drill sleeve, the medullary canal was sequentially reamed, followed by insertion of the DTN. The DTN system (Double Medical, Model RTN-2) was implanted with an 8 × 140 mm main nail, two proximal 24-mm cortical locking screws, and three distal cancellous locking screws (38 mm, 40 mm, 40 mm). Fluoroscopy confirmed satisfactory intramedullary nail position and fracture reduction before locking screws and end caps were placed. Following harvest of a corticocancellous graft (7 × 3 cm surface dimensions) from the ipsilateral iliac crest, the cancellous portion was morselized yielding approximately 25 g of bone graft, which was densely packed into the distal tibia defect. After confirming fracture stability, appropriate implant positioning, and adequate compaction of the autologous iliac graft, a layered wound closure was performed.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first report of the successful use of DTN in the particularly challenging scenario of nonunion following a Gustilo IIIA open distal tibial fracture (initially treated with external fixation) complicated by pin-site infection in a patient with type 2 diabetes mellitus - multiple risk factors significantly elevating the risk of failure.

Biomechanically, DTN promotes bone healing by providing rigid axial stability through load-sharing principles, reducing micromotion at the nonunion site. This stable environment facilitates callus formation, angiogenesis, and osteogenic differentiation, critical in diabetic patients with impaired healing [2,7]. This technique not only provides rotational and shear stability comparable to the anterograde intramedullary nails, but also significantly reduces soft tissue injury while completely preserving the knee joint [8]. Moreover, DTN eliminates the need for extensive medullary reaming, thereby reducing risks of fat embolism and thromboembolism [9].

Current mainstay treatments for distal tibia nonunion include internal fixation (steel plates or intramedullary nails) and external fixation [10]. In this case, steel plates were contraindicated due to the patient's slender build with associated risks of soft tissue irritation and flap necrosis, compounded by post-traumatic scar formation [11]. External fixation was avoided given the patient's history of pin-site complications. Notably, some reports highlighted the efficacy of External fixation for distal tibial nonunion [12,13], yet such approaches are associated with significant pin-site morbidity and restricted mobility – limitations effectively circumvented by DTN in our case. Although antegrade intramedullary nailing is an option, it provides inadequate fixation for very distal tibial fractures and poses a risk of knee joint injury. Moreover, antegrade intramedullary nailing requires extensive reaming, which carries a significantly higher risk of venous thrombosis and fat embolism compared to the DTN [14,15].

Diabetes mellitus is a known risk factor for impaired fracture healing and postoperative complications [16]. In this diabetic patient with a history of pin-site infection, DTN's minimal soft tissue disruption and stable fixation provided a critical advantage in achieving union without infection recurrence.

While union rates for retrograde tibial nailing in non-diabetic cohorts range from 90 % to 95 % [4,14], our diabetic patient with prior pin-site infection achieved complete radiographic union and functional recovery (AOFAS 82) within 6 months. This outcome aligns with the efficacy reported in non-diabetic populations, suggesting that DTN may mitigate diabetes-associated healing impairment through its biomechanical stability and minimal soft tissue disruption.

The DTN application achieved excellent clinical and radiographic outcomes with solid union in this open distal tibial nonunion case. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. This study reports a single case, limiting generalizability and requiring validation in larger-scale studies. Furthermore, the follow-up period is currently limited to two years; longer-term data are necessary to assess the durability of the DTN and long-term risk of ankle arthritis, particularly in diabetic populations with elevated degeneration risks [16]. Current evidence on DTN remains limited to small case series, and multicenter trials are needed to establish standardized protocols for high-risk cohorts (e.g., diabetes, prior infection).

Conclusion

In this complex Gustilo Type IIIA distal tibia nonunion with diabetes and prior pin-site infection, the DTN achieved successful radiographic union and excellent functional recovery (AOFAS 82), providing a valuable biomechanically stable and minimally invasive alternative.
