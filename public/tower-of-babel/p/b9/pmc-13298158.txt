A Rare Coexistence of Biliary Atresia and Alagille Syndrome in a Neonate: Clinical Implications of Dual Etiology in Neonatal Cholestasis
Authors: Wan-Ning Wu, Hung-Chang Lee, Hsiang-Yu Lin, Nien-Lu Wang, Wai-Tao Chan, Shu-Chao Weng, Chuen-Bin Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Clinical Significance: Biliary atresia (BA) and Alagille syndrome (ALGS) represent distinct anatomic and genetic causes of neonatal cholestasis. Their overlapping clinical, biochemical, and early histological features present a formidable diagnostic challenge in early infancy, and their simultaneous coexistence is exceedingly rare. This report documents a unique case of dual diagnosis to highlight the associated diagnostic pitfalls and implications for surgical management. Case Presentation: We present the case of a Taiwanese male neonate who manifested prolonged jaundice and acholic stools. Preoperative imaging and intraoperative cholangiography confirmed biliary atresia, for which the patient underwent a Kasai portoenterostomy. The patient subsequently exhibited an atypical postoperative course characterized by persistent hyperbilirubinemia and intractable pruritus. This atypical trajectory prompted an extensive, multisystem evaluation and molecular genetic analysis, revealing a concurrent genetic diagnosis of Alagille syndrome. To our knowledge, this dual diagnosis is rarely reported in the literature, which creates a significant challenge in determining surgical candidacy and predicting long-term liver health outcomes. Discussions: Early differentiation is complicated by the fact that some ALGS patients can initially mimic BA. Beyond its exceptional rarity, this case holds profound clinical significance for the evaluation of neonatal cholestasis, serving as a stark reminder of the risks of “diagnostic premature closure.” In diagnostically challenging cases of neonatal cholestasis, intraoperative biliary exploration remains the gold standard for the timely diagnosis of BA. Genetic testing should be considered an adjunctive tool when clinical and histological findings are inconclusive. Conclusions: This case highlights a critical clinical caveat in neonatal cholestasis: while a confirmed diagnosis of anatomical BA typically stands alone as a solitary pathology, clinicians should remain mindful of the remote possibility of a concurrent genetic etiology like ALGS in highly atypical presentations. Persistently unexpected postoperative jaundice or the accumulation of multisystem anomalies should prompt an expansion of the differential diagnosis. Recognizing this rare coexistence is crucial for effective multidisciplinary management, informed surgical decision-making, and accurate genetic counseling.

1. Introduction

Biliary atresia (BA) is a progressive, obstructive cholangiopathy of neonates, characterized by the inflammatory obliteration of extrahepatic and/or intrahepatic bile ducts. It typically presents in the neonatal period with persistent jaundice, acholic stools, and hepatomegaly [1,2]. Early diagnosis is crucial for timely intervention, as untreated cases inevitably progress to biliary cirrhosis and liver failure. The key determinant of prognosis lies in the timing of the Kasai portoenterostomy, with the optimal surgical window being before 60 days of age [3,4].

Alagille syndrome (ALGS) is a multisystem autosomal dominant condition first described in 1969 by Daniel Alagille. The clinical diagnosis is established by the presence of at least three out of five primary clinical criteria: chronic cholestasis characterized by the paucity of intrahepatic bile ducts, congenital cardiac anomalies (typically pulmonary stenosis), skeletal defects (such as butterfly-like vertebrae), ocular changes (posterior embryotoxon), and distinctive facial features (e.g., broad forehead, deep-set and widely spaced eyes, small pointed chin, and saddle or straight nose) [5,6]. Molecularly, ALGS is confirmed by identifying pathogenic mutations in the NOTCH signaling pathway, including mutations or deletions in JAG1 or NOTCH2 [6,7].

The differential diagnosis between BA and ALGS in early infancy represents a significant clinical challenge due to their overlapping clinical, biochemical, and even histological features [8,9]. The coexistence of BA and ALGS is exceedingly rare. To our knowledge, only a few such cases have been reported globally, and the diagnostic dilemma often leads to delays in appropriate management or unnecessary surgical interventions. Crucially, the performance of a Kasai portoenterostomy (KPE) in patients with underlying ALGS remains a subject of intense clinical debate. Because ALGS is fundamentally characterized by an intrinsic, intrahepatic bile duct paucity, performing a KPE—which targets extrahepatic structures—may paradoxically accelerate hepatic parenchymal injury and hasten progression toward liver failure in these patients [10,11,12].

In this report, we present a rare case of a Taiwanese neonate diagnosed with both Type IIa biliary atresia and Alagille syndrome. By sharing this case, we aim to highlight the importance of multidisciplinary evaluation and genetic screening in neonatal cholestasis, even when an initial diagnosis of BA is confirmed.

2. Materials and Methods

This single-case observational study documented an infant diagnosed biliary atresia and Alagille syndrome. Intraoperative cholangiography and exome sequencing were used to confirm the diagnoses. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was executed utilizing a proband-first strategy. Subsequent targeted parental verification was performed, which confirmed the de novo nature of the heterozygous JAG1 mutation in the proband.

3. Case Report

3.1. Initial Presentation and Clinical Course

The patient was a male neonate, born at 38 weeks’ gestation via vaginal delivery to a primigravida mother with pregnancy-induced hypertension. His birth weight was 2198 g (small for gestational age). The maternal history was unremarkable for gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, G6PD deficiency, or infectious diseases, specifically HBV, syphilis, Rubella, and HIV. Prenatal ultrasonography had not identified any structural biliary or cardiac anomalies.

Postnatally, the infant was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit for transient tachypnea of the newborn. Neonatal jaundice was managed with phototherapy, and he was discharged on day 7. During the initial month of life, the patient was exclusively breastfed. At his one-month health supervision visit, failure to thrive was noted. Two days later, progressive icterus and clay-colored stools emerged.

Laboratory evaluation at 42 days of age revealed significant cholestasis: total bilirubin (TB) 11.1 mg/dL, direct bilirubin (DB) 9.9 mg/dL, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 275 U/L, alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 192 U/L, and γ-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT) 92 U/L. Screening for bacterial and viral infections, including cytomegalovirus, yielded negative results. Given the clinical triad of acholic stools, failure to thrive, and conjugated hyperbilirubinemia, a prompt evaluation for BA was initiated.

Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated a slender gallbladder. Hepatobiliary scintigraphy showed no tracer excretion into the intestine throughout the study period (Figure 1). Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) revealed non-visualization of the extrahepatic bile ducts and a slender gallbladder (1.4 cm in length), findings highly consistent with BA (Figure 2). Subsequent intraoperative cholangiography (Figure 3) demonstrated complete non-visualization of the intrahepatic biliary tree and the common hepatic duct, which is indicative of classic Type IIa biliary atresia. Crucially, direct surgical exploration confirmed the physical presence of an obliterated, fibrotic cord replacing the extrahepatic segment. The patient underwent a Kasai portoenterostomy at 54 days of age.

3.2. Histopathology and Postoperative Course

Histopathological examination of this common hepatic duct remnant confirmed a completely occluded lumen filled with prominent neurovascular hyperplasia and dense fibrotic tissue, which provides definitive anatomical evidence of BA. Furthermore, a concurrent liver wedge biopsy demonstrated significant portal and lobular inflammation, marked giant cell transformation, and a florid, diffuse ductular reaction intensely highlighted by Cytokeratin 19 (CK19) immunostaining—features that directly contrast with the lack of ductular response typically seen in isolated ALGS. Interlobular bile duct loss was focal and did not exceed 50% of the portal tracts (Figure 4). The characteristic bile duct paucity of ALGS was not yet histologically definitive.

Although stool color changed to light yellowish on the 3rd day after Kasai operation, the patient exhibited cholestasis again one week later. Follow-up laboratory studies revealed persistent cholestasis (TB 13.9 mg/dL, DB 7.9 mg/dL) (Table 1). Notable hepatosplenomegaly and intractable pruritus were observed. Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D was severely deficient (6.5 ng/mL), necessitating aggressive supplementation.

Because the patient exhibited an atypical postoperative course characterized by persistent, non-resolving cholestasis and a lack of expected clinical improvement, suspicion of a rare concurrent cause of neonatal cholestasis alongside BA was raised. Consequently, further multidisciplinary evaluations and extensive genetic investigations were initiated. Ophthalmologic examination revealed posterior embryotoxon (Figure 5). Orthopedic assessment confirmed bilateral proximal radioulnar synostosis (PRUS): a cartilaginous type on the right and a complete osseous fusion with posterior dislocation on the left (Figure 6). Echocardiography identified a small secundum atrial septal defect/patent foramen ovale and mild bilateral pulmonary artery stenosis (LPA: 0.285 cm, z-score −2.23, PG 24.5 mmHg; RPA: 0.276 cm, z-score −2.86, PG 31.1 mmHg). While distinctive facial features, renal ultrasonography and spinal radiography were unremarkable (specifically lacking butterfly vertebrae), the constellation of biliary, cardiac, and ocular findings strongly supported a clinical diagnosis of ALGS.

Genetic testing was initiated at 69 days of age (postoperative day 15). WES confirmed a pathogenic heterozygous mutation in the JAG1 gene on chromosome 20 (NM_000214.3:c.2122_2125del, p.Gln708fs). This variant is classified as pathogenic according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria, establishing a concomitant diagnosis of Alagille syndrome. Subsequent WES of both parents revealed an absence of the JAG1 mutation, thereby confirming that the patient harbored a de novo pathogenic heterozygous variant. Due to rapid progression to decompensated liver disease, the patient was referred to a tertiary medical center for liver transplantation evaluation. He is currently being managed with aggressive nutritional and medical support to optimize somatic growth and body weight while awaiting definitive liver transplantation.

4. Discussion

The coexistence of BA and ALGS is very rare, with only a few cases documented in the literature. In a retrospective study from Korea reviewing 41 patients with ALGS or confirmed JAG1 mutations, 37 underwent liver biopsy; notably, only one patient was reported to have concurrent BA [13]. However, detailed clinical characterization of that specific case was not provided, highlighting the scarcity of descriptive data on this dual pathology.

According to the joint clinical practice guidelines of the NASPGHAN and ESPGHAN, evaluating the patency of the extrahepatic biliary tree represents the primary, time-sensitive objective in the initial diagnostic workup of infants presenting with cholestasis. Currently, intraoperative cholangiography accompanied by histological evaluation of the biliary remnant remains the clinical gold standard for the definitive diagnosis of BA. Once BA is confirmed via the non-visualization of a patent extrahepatic biliary tree, a KPE is indicated immediately to maximize native liver survival [14]. In our case, the initial clinical features: acholic stools, non-visualization of the extrahepatic bile ducts on MRCP and non-excretion on hepatobiliary scintigraphy, strongly favored a diagnosis of BA, which was subsequently confirmed intraoperatively. While our management aligned with these guideline-directed anatomical evaluations, this case demonstrates that establishing a clear diagnosis of BA should not preclude parallel genetic testing if atypical clinical dimensions, such as post-KPE failure or extrahepatic anomalies, are noted.

The histological evolution of ALGS in early infancy further complicates its differentiation from BA. Classic histological signs of biliary obstruction in BA include moderate-to-marked bile ductular reaction, bile plugs within portal ducts or ductules, portal stromal edema, and progressive portal or perilobular fibrosis [14,15,16]. In contrast, ALGS is histologically characterized by a paucity of intrahepatic bile ducts (PIBD) or hepatic ductal aplasia, accompanied by a less pronounced inflammatory reaction surrounding the foci of the absent hepatic ducts [17]. However, as emphasized in the literature, this hallmark may be frequently absent or incomplete in neonates and infants younger than six months. Instead, a minority of patients with ALGS may initially present on early biopsy with bile duct proliferation or a neonatal hepatitis pattern, mimicking the obstructive features of BA [9], which can lead to initial diagnostic challenge. In this case, the initial liver wedge biopsy did not meet the criteria for PIBD (bile duct-to-portal tract ratio < 0.4), instead showing a prominent ductular reaction, inflammatory cell infiltration and giant cell transformation. This histological mimicry accounts for the initial diagnostic focus on BA.

In the present case, the suspicion of a rare concurrent genetic etiology of neonatal cholestasis was raised primarily due to the atypical postoperative course, illustrating that a failure of biliary drainage post-KPE should prompt clinicians to expand their differential diagnosis beyond standard surgical complications. The diagnosis of ALGS in this patient was further supported by the identification of bilateral pulmonary arterial stenosis, posterior embryotoxon of bilateral eyes, and PRUS. While butterfly vertebrae are the hallmark skeletal anomaly of ALGS, other skeletal anomalies involving the spine and hands are reported [18]. Finally, the confirmation of a pathogenic heterozygous mutation in JAG1 (c.2122_2125del, p.Gln708fs) was definitive. This specific frameshift mutation leads to a premature stop codon, resulting in haploinsufficiency of the NOTCH signaling pathway, which is central to the pathogenesis of ALGS [7].

The NOTCH signaling pathway plays an indispensable role in postnatal liver repair mechanisms and biliary tree development during ductal plate remodeling. Specifically, it controls the transdifferentiation of hepatoblasts and mature hepatocytes into functional cholangiocytes by altering the downstream expression of liver-enriched transcription factors. Pathogenic disruptions in the NOTCH pathway cause a failure of reactive ductular cell proliferation and result in an abnormal accumulation of intermediate hepatobiliary cells (IHBCs). Because these IHBCs lack the crucial biliary-specific transcription factor HNF1β, they are incapable of transitioning to a true biliary phenotype [19,20]. Consequently, ALGS is molecularly and pathologically characterized by an absence of ductular reaction, which constitutes the core mechanistic feature differentiating its histology from the florid ductular proliferation typically observed in BA.

Early differentiation is further complicated by the fact that some ALGS patients can initially phenocopy BA clinically, radiographically, and histologically. Clinically, infants with ALGS can present with progressive conjugated hyperbilirubinemia, failure to thrive, and completely acholic stools, identical to isolated BA. Notably, ALGS has variable expression of clinical features in early infancy [9]. Radiographically, profound intrahepatic cholestasis in neonates with ALGS can cause an absence of intestinal tracer excretion on hepatobiliary scintigraphy. Histologically, as previously noted, the diagnostic hallmark of interlobular bile duct paucity is a dynamic developmental process that is frequently absent in the early weeks of life. As demonstrated in previous comparative studies, ALGS patients typically exhibit a higher prevalence of cardiac anomalies and distinctive facial features, lower GGT levels and a lower incidence of gallbladder abnormalities compared to BA patients [8].

This case highlights the need for genetic testing in cases of “atypical” BA or when multisystem anomalies are present. Beyond its exceptional rarity, this case holds profound clinical and educational significance for the evaluation of neonatal cholestasis, serving as a stark reminder of the risks associated with ‘diagnostic premature closure’. In standard clinical practice, when an infant presents with a highly critical, time-sensitive condition like BA, and that suspicion is subsequently confirmed by gold-standard intraoperative cholangiography, the diagnostic workup invariably stops. Clinicians naturally attribute the entire presentation to a single, unifying diagnosis. However, as this case demonstrates, the definitive confirmation of an obstructive, anatomical lesion does not inherently preclude a concurrent, independent genetic etiology.

The impact of the KPE on patients with ALGS is a subject of critical concern. Several retrospective cohort studies have noted an association between a prior KPE and accelerated hepatic insufficiency in patients with ALGS, often resulting in a more rapid progression to liver failure and an earlier requirement for liver transplantation compared to those managed medically [10,11,12]. However, in the specific context of our patient, establishing a definitive causal relationship regarding postoperative decline remains highly challenging. The rapid postoperative rise in serum bilirubin and early progression to decompensated liver disease depict a complex clinical trajectory. It is difficult to definitively determine whether this clinical deterioration was driven primarily by the severe intrinsic nature of his underlying ALGS, the combined synergistic stress of coexisting dual pathologies, or the potentially detrimental parenchymal effects of a KPE executed in a liver already suffering from genetic ductular vulnerability.

It is worth noting that our patient is currently awaiting liver transplantation. In patients with dual BA and ALGS pathology, the pre-transplant management window is exceptionally complex. The presence of underlying ALGS not only accelerates progression to hepatic insufficiency following KPE but also introduces multi-system vulnerabilities, such as peripheral pulmonary artery stenosis and severe failure to thrive, which demand intensive multidisciplinary stabilization prior to undergoing major transplant surgery.

In diagnostically challenging cases of neonatal cholestasis, intraoperative biliary exploration remains the gold standard for the timely diagnosis of BA, ensuring that the window for a Kasai portoenterostomy is not missed. However, genetic testing for JAG1 and NOTCH2 should be considered an adjunctive tool when clinical and histological findings for ALGS are inconclusive [5,14]. Crucially, the establishment of a BA diagnosis does not preclude the need for comprehensive genetic surveillance if other multisystem manifestations suggest a concurrent genetic disorder [8,9]. Beyond the index patient, molecular investigations are instrumental in identifying family members with mild or subclinical phenotypes. Such genetic assessment is invaluable for providing accurate prognostic information and facilitating genetic counseling regarding the risk for future progeny [18].

5. Conclusions

In summary, this case highlights a critical clinical caveat: while the confirmation of BA through standard anatomical imaging and intraoperative cholangiography stands as a solitary diagnosis, clinicians must remain mindful of the remote possibility of a concurrent genetic disorder in atypical cases. Recognizing this rare but significant coexistence is paramount. This experience supports a multi-disciplinary diagnostic framework where genetic screening is considered a highly targeted investigative tool when extrahepatic multisystem anomalies or unexpectedly refractory postoperative courses are present, ultimately ensuring informed surgical decision-making, optimized long-term management, and precise genetic counseling for the family.
