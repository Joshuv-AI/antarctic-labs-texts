Acute Care Use and Mortality by Tele-Emergency Care Use, Modality, and Clinician Type
Authors: Kathleen Yinran Li, Linda Diem Tran, Liam Rose, Jacqueline M. Ferguson, Tracy H. Urech, Allison Engstrom Buggaveeti, Anita A. Vashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cohort study assesses the association of tele-emergency care use with the rate of subsequent emergency department care encounters, hospitalizations, and 30-day mortality.

Introduction

Emergency department (ED) crowding remains a persistent crisis in US health care,1,2,3 contributing to care delays, adverse patient outcomes, and clinician burnout.4,5,6 Addressing crowding requires solutions targeting all aspects of patient flow—input, throughput, and output. One emerging approach is tele-emergency care (TEC): real-time virtual encounters with emergency clinicians for patients identified through nurse advice lines as requiring urgent evaluation. Nurse advice lines are widely used to guide patients to appropriate care, but most rely on standardized triage algorithms with limited clinician input and, and do not substantially reduce subsequent care use.7 TEC extends these models by embedding real-time virtual evaluation with emergency clinicians, offering a more clinically nuanced alternative to protocol-based nurse triage.

First piloted within the US Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) health system in 2019, TEC offers a potential alternative to in-person ED referral by delivering timely access to emergency medicine expertise in the home setting. Early evaluations suggest TEC can reduce unnecessary ED use while maintaining safe, effective care.8,9 Similar models piloted in other settings have also demonstrated reductions in ED use, suggesting broader applicability.10,11

Despite these findings, important evidence gaps remain. In particular, little is known about the potential implications of specific implementation features, such as visit modality (phone vs video) and clinician type (physician vs advanced practice clinician [APC]), in tele-emergency care outcomes. These design choices have important implications for access, equity, and workforce planning.

To address these gaps, we assessed the association between TEC use, modality, and clinician type and subsequent health care use relative to standard nurse advice line recommendations. We also examined associations with 30-day mortality and concordance between clinical recommendations and patient behavior. Our study seeks to inform the refinement and broader adoption of TEC as a strategy to improve access, safety, and system efficiency in acute unscheduled care.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This cohort study was designated as a quality improvement project and received a nonresearch determination from the VA Quality Enhancement Research Initiative and therefore did not require institutional review board oversight or informed consent. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline. This was a retrospective analysis of calls to the VA nurse advice line from January 2018 through April 2024, including calls before and after TEC implementation. The VA is a large integrated health care system in the US serving approximately 9 million veterans across 18 regional networks and 130 facilities. Veterans calling their local nurse advice line are triaged using 1 of 2 standardized, commercially available algorithms (TriageXpert Clinical Content [TXCC] or ClearTriage) that guide care recommendations, including referral to the ED. With TEC in place, the same triage algorithm is followed, but nurses can offer eligible callers a virtual visit with an emergency clinician as an alternative to ED referral. Eligibility for TEC based on triage recommendation is depicted in eFigure 1 in Supplement 1. Receipt of TEC is dependent on multiple factors, including nurse discretion, the availability of a TEC clinician, and veteran willingness and ability to participate in a virtual visit. TEC was piloted at separate sites before a centrally defined model was established, resulting in regional variation in implementation date, workflows, staffing, and hours of operation (eTable 1 in Supplement 1).

Selection of Participants

The study included calls to the nurse advice line with a triage recommendation for evaluation by a clinician within 8 hours irrespective of TEC implementation status. This included 3 categories: emergent 911 (those advised to call 911 but declined), emergent (evaluation within 2 hours), and urgent (evaluation within 2 to 8 hours). We excluded calls with less urgent follow-up recommendations (eg, 12 to 24 hours, self-care) or occurring outside of typical business hours (weekdays 8 am to 6 pm) due to limited outpatient resources during nights and weekends. Calls or visits with missing baseline veteran characteristics required for risk adjustment were excluded, as were calls from facilities (n = 10) where TEC programs did not address emergent concerns. To account for an initial ramp-up period, we defined TEC implementation as the first month in which a site exceeded 30 TEC visits; visits occurring before this threshold were excluded. For modality and clinician type analyses, we excluded calls with missing modality or clinician type, respectively. To maximize the use of available data, these calls were retained in the primary analysis of overall TEC use. Application of inclusion and exclusion criteria and derivation of the primary analytic cohort are shown in eFigure 2 in Supplement 1.

Interventions

The primary intervention in this study was receipt of TEC. We evaluated both encounter-level and facility-level effects. At the encounter level, we compared outcomes between individuals who received TEC and those who did not. Among individuals who received TEC, we further examined outcomes based on modality (video vs phone) and clinician type (physician vs APC [nurse practitioner or physician assistant]). For the facility-level analysis, we compared outcomes at facilities before and after TEC implementation.

Measurements

We obtained data on veteran demographics (age, sex, race and ethnicity, marriage status, priority group, service-connected disability status,12,13 rurality, distance to nearest VA care), clinical characteristics (Elixhauser comorbidity index14,15), care use (ED visits, hospitalizations), and mortality from the Department of Veterans’ Affairs (VA) Corporate Data Warehouse (CDW), a national repository comprising data from several VA clinical and administrative systems. Race and ethnicity data were collected to characterize the sample and not analyzed further. Patients self-reported race and ethnicity from categories including American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Black or African American, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, White, or no matching concept. Ethnicity categories included Hispanic or Latino, not Hispanic or Latino, or no matching concept. Priority group reflects eligibility for VA care based on service-connected disability, income, and other factors, with higher scores indicating lower priority. Elixhauser comorbidity index ranged from 0 to 31, with higher scores indicating a greater burden of disease. Nurse triage calls were recorded by an integrated telephone management system and saved as standardized clinical notes, also available from the CDW, from which we identified call chief concerns and triage recommendations. TEC encounters, call modality, clinician type, and TEC recommendations were identified using structured data fields from the CDW. We also included non-VA encounters (paid for by the VA) from national community care data extracts provided by the Office of Integrated Veteran Care. To validate TEC exposure, we surveyed regional networks regarding TEC start dates and staffing models to ensure alignment with available data.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was ED (VA and non-VA) use within 7 days of the index nurse advice line call. Secondary outcomes included hospitalization within 7 days, 30-day mortality, and concordance between the clinical recommendation (from either the nurse triage line or TEC) and the veteran’s subsequent action.

Statistical Analysis

We used complementary analytic approaches to estimate both the encounter-level outcomes associated with TEC and the facility-level outcomes associated with TEC implementation. For encounter-level analyses, we used augmented inverse-probability weighting to estimate average treatment effects.16 Predicted outcome probabilities were estimated using the p0 means approach, which standardizes model predictions to the observed covariate distribution. Propensity weighting incorporated triage acuity, chief concern, veteran demographic and clinical characteristics, rurality, prior ED use, time of day, day of week, month, year, and facility (eTable 2 in Supplement 1).

For facility-level analyses, we used a heterogeneous difference-in-differences (DID) model to estimate the average treatment effect on the treated facilities that adopted TEC at different times, using facilities that had not yet implemented TEC as controls. DID models similarly adjusted for nurse triage recommendation, veteran characteristics, and call timing, with SEs accounting for clustering at the facility level. We assessed the parallel trends assumption using visual inspection of pretreatment trends. We also evaluated concordance between clinical recommendations and veteran behavior using Cohen’s κ, stratified by TEC modality and clinician type. Two-sided P < .05 was considered significant. All analyses were conducted using Stata version 18 (StataCorp LLC).

Results

Characteristics of Study Participants

During the study period, we identified 2 511 932 calls (by 719 028 veterans, mean [SD] age, 62.3 [16.0] years; 91 508 [12.7%] female; 627 515 [87.3%] male) to the nurse advice line who were triaged as urgent, emergent, or emergent 911, representing 49.1% of all nurse triage calls. Of these, 1.9 million calls were prior to TEC implementation, and 576 112 calls were after. During the study period, 57 facilities (across 8 regional networks) implemented TEC, whereas 73 facilities (10 regions and 2 facilities within other regions) did not. We identified 99 994 TEC visits, of which 94 674 (17.4% of eligible calls at sites with TEC) were included in the analytic sample after excluding visits with missing key variables. Modality was identifiable for 93.0% and clinician type for 98.8% of TEC visits. Unadjusted call frequencies and rates of subsequent ED use, stratified by triage acuity and TEC receipt, are shown in eTable 2 in Supplement 1. TEC visits were predominantly conducted by phone (64.4%) and by physicians (64.4%). APCs handled a smaller share of emergent 911 (13.2%) and emergent (28.3%) visits, and approximately half (47.4%) of urgent calls. Among TEC facilities, the proportion of eligible nurse advice calls resulting in a TEC visit varied from 4.0% to 33.8% (median, 14.0%; IQR, 9.4%-23.4%).

Compared with overall Veterans Health Administration users, veterans who received TEC were slightly more likely to be female (13.9% compared with 10.1%), older (age ≥65 years, 54.0% vs 53.3%), identify as Hispanic or Latino (11.4% vs 7.2%), and unmarried (51.7% vs 44.7%) and less likely to identify as Black (13.6% vs 17.7%) or live in a rural area (28.2% vs 34.3%) (Table 1). They were also more likely to have a service-connected disability (68.6% vs 66.5%) or low income (16.4% vs 14.0%) and higher baseline ED use (3 or more visits in the past year, 15.2% vs 5.8%). Similar trends were noted when comparing TEC users with overall nurse advice line callers at TEC facilities. After applying inverse probability weights, all standardized differences were less than 0.05, indicating excellent covariate balance between TEC and non-TEC groups (eTable 3 in Supplement 1).

ED Use

TEC receipt was associated with a decrease in ED use within 7 days (−16.5%; 95% CI, −18.6% to −14.4%) compared with propensity-weighted veterans who did not receive TEC (28.5% vs 45.0%), a 36.6% relative reduction. This average treatment effect (ATE) was most pronounced among emergent (ATE, −22.0%; 95% CI, −24.1 to −19.9) and emergent 911 (ATE, −19.3%; 95% CI, −21.9 to −16.6) calls, and smaller among urgent calls (ATE, −5.6%; 95% CI, −8.4 to −2.9) (Table 2 and Figure, A). The ATEs of modality and clinician type were modest: video visits were associated with a slight increase in ED use only among emergent calls (ATE, 2.5%; 95% CI, 0.1 to 4.9) vs phone, and physician-provided TEC was associated with higher ED use than APC-provided TEC (overall ATE, 4.5%; 95% CI, 2.5-6.5) (Table 2, Figure, B).

Hospitalization, Mortality, and Concordance of Care

TEC receipt was associated with lower hospitalization rates overall (overall ATE, −2.1%; 95% CI, −2.4 to −1.8), with larger differences among emergent 911 (ATE, −5.1%, 95% CI, −6.2 to −4.1) and emergent (ATE, −2.6%; 95% CI, −2.9 to −2.2) calls and no association among urgent calls (Table 2). There was no association between TEC receipt and 30-day mortality. Facility-level TEC implementation was not associated with differences in hospitalization or mortality (Table 3).

Hospitalization rates were similar across TEC modalities and clinician types overall, with slightly higher hospitalizations among urgent calls handled by physicians compared with APCs. Mortality did not differ by modality or clinician type. Mortality differences were not estimable in some subgroups due to low event rates.

Patient flow between nurse triage recommendations and actual ED use, both prior to TEC and among veterans who received TEC, is illustrated in eFigure 5 in Supplement 1. Concordance between TEC recommendation and veteran behavior was moderate (κ = 0.47) and higher than concordance for nurse triage (κ = 0.23), with similar concordance across TEC modalities and clinician types (Table 4). Veterans were more likely to follow TEC guidance for both ED recommendations (59% vs 51% for nurse triage calls) and non-ED recommendations (87% vs 76%).

Discussion

In this large-scale evaluation of TEC embedded within the VA nurse advice line, TEC receipt was associated with substantially lower ED use rates compared with nurse triage alone. This association was strongest among veterans initially triaged as emergent (ATE −22.0%) and more modest among urgent calls (ATE −5.6%), highlighting the potential value of TEC for higher-acuity cases. In contrast, facility-level associations were smaller and only significant for emergent calls (ATE on the treated, −2.6%), reflecting that these estimates capture the average effect of making TEC available across all eligible calls, rather than the effect among individuals receiving TEC.

The reduction in ED use among emergent and emergent 911 calls likely reflects the conservative design of nurse triage algorithms. Thus, some emergent calls may not ultimately have required in-person emergency care but would otherwise be referred to the ED. TEC may offer these patients a timely clinical evaluation that safely averts unnecessary ED visits. In contrast, calls triaged as urgent may include some veterans who would not have pursued ED care regardless of the recommendation, limiting the marginal implications of TEC in this group.

The reduction in ED use across modalities and clinician types underscores that expanding TEC access, rather than refining an optimal version, may yield the greatest benefit. Additionally, concordance between TEC recommendations and veterans’ actions was moderate and similar regardless of TEC modality or clinician type, and substantially higher than concordance with nurse triage recommendations. Comparisons of telehealth modalities in other contexts have reached similar conclusions.17,18 Taken together, these findings reinforce that access to real-time clinical evaluation, rather than the specific mode of delivery or clinician type, may be a key factor in improved outcomes. Small differences in ED use rates based on clinician type may reflect differences in case mix, as physicians evaluated a disproportionate share of higher acuity calls, rather than differences in quality of care.

The discrepancy between the larger encounter-level associations and the more modest facility-level effects is consistent with prior studies. One reported a 17.5% reduction in ED use among individuals receiving TEC,9 whereas another found only a 2.1% decrease at facilities after TEC implementation.8 Low TEC use among eligible callers may explain this attenuation. Additionally, nearly half of TEC visits were for lower acuity calls already unlikely to result in ED care. Targeting TEC referrals to callers triaged as emergent (as opposed to urgent) may increase population-level implications. Future research should explore barriers to TEC uptake, including nurse discretion, veteran preference, clinician availability, and operational factors.

Our findings have important clinical and policy implications. ED visit volumes continue to outpace population growth,19 and ED boarding and crowding have reached crisis levels.1,20 Although boarding of admitted patients is the primary factor in crowding,21,22 strategies, like TEC, that safely reduce unnecessary ED visits remain an important component of broader system-level solutions. While the effectiveness of TEC in nonintegrated health systems remains to be determined, its core components—nurse triage, virtual unscheduled care, and real-time access to emergency clinicians—address challenges shared by many health systems and payers, including ED crowding, inappropriate ED use, and gaps in after-hours care.

From an implementation perspective, our findings underscore that system-level implications depend on TEC effectiveness among recipients but also how consistently and strategically it is used. Health systems seeking to replicate or adapt TEC should consider prioritizing increasing uptake among eligible callers, particularly those with higher-acuity concerns. Embedding TEC referral pathways into nurse triage workflows, expanding after-hours coverage, and training nurses on when and how to offer TEC may improve population-level implications. Access to comprehensive medical records and established care coordination pathways, as in integrated systems like the VA, may further support effectiveness.

Finally, our results have implications for telehealth policy. The minimal differences in outcomes observed by modality support inclusive telehealth reimbursement policies, including coverage for audio-only visits, to expand access to acute care while minimizing disparities related to the digital divide. Together, these findings suggest that TEC represents a scalable approach to extending emergency care expertise beyond the walls of the ED while preserving in-person capacity for patients who need it most.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although we used national VA data, adjusted for a wide range of veteran and clinical characteristics, and applied propensity-weighted and DID models to mitigate bias, residual confounding may remain. Veterans who were sicker or already inclined to follow triage recommendations to seek emergent evaluation may have been less likely to be offered or to accept TEC, which could lead to overestimation of differences in ED use. However, directionally similar associations in facility-level analyses, consistent with partial uptake and heterogeneous implementation across sites, suggest that selection bias alone is unlikely to fully explain our findings. Second, TEC implementation varied across sites and was staggered over time. While we leveraged this to compare differences by modality and clinician type and control for time-related trends, sites with later TEC adoption may have differed in telehealth familiarity and use. Implementation timing was also defined using facility-level encounter thresholds, which may imperfectly reflect the true onset of TEC availability at individual sites. Third, we were only able to capture ED visits and hospitalizations that occurred within the VA or were paid for by the VA. Encounters reimbursed through Medicare and other insurers were not available, which may result in undercounting certain outcomes. Fourth, we did not include a measure of broadband access, which may influence a veteran’s ability to participate in telehealth encounters, as this was only available as an aggregate measure. However, most TEC visits were conducted by phone, a modality less reliant on broadband access.23 Lastly, other important dimensions of TEC, such as its reach, patient experience, and perceived value were outside of the scope of this analysis.

Conclusions

In this study, receipt of TEC was associated with fewer subsequent ED visits, particularly among callers initially triaged as emergent, without increases in hospitalization or mortality. At the facility level, we estimated ATE on the treated for TEC implementation and subsequent ED use were smaller and less consistent, potentially reflecting incomplete uptake among eligible callers. Minimal differences by visit modality and clinician type suggest implementation flexibility. Future research should examine TEC transferability beyond the VA and strategies to optimize uptake and systemic impact.
