Prophylactic Sacral Dressing in Prevention of Pressure Injury among ICU Patients: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Sonam Y Bhutia, Sushma K Saini, Man Singh Jat, Manish K Balai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsPressure injury (PI) is a common problem among patients admitted to intensive care units (ICUs). Sacral PI, a specific type of PI, leads to serious complications, thereby reducing the quality of life, increasing morbidity, mortality, and financial cost on patients and the healthcare system. This study aimed determine the efficacy of prophylactic sacral dressings (PSD) in reducing PI among ICU patients.MethodsPRISMA guidelines were followed. Relevant studies were electronically searched from databases, including PubMed, Scopus, and through supplementary sources such as the Wiley Online Library and Google Scholar. Three reviewers extracted the studies and performed quality assessment of the selected studies. Disagreements were resolved by consultation with the fourth reviewer.ResultsA total of seven randomized controlled trials (RCTs), involving 3,735 patients, were included. Among them, 2,144 received PSD and 1,591 received standard care. The risk of bias in the included studies was low and considered acceptable. The pooled odds ratio (95% CI) for our binary outcomes was calculated. A significant decrease in sacral PI rate (p < 0.001) and ≥ stage II PI was observed in the PSD group (p < 0.006). Visual assessment of the funnel plot revealed no presence of publication bias.ConclusionThe use of PSD led to a notable decrease in sacral PI rate, including ≥ stage II sacral PI among the ICU patients. Further studies with larger sample sizes and specific ICU populations are recommended to confirm these findings.How to cite this articleBhutia SY, Saini SK, Jat MS, Balai MK. Highlights

Patients admitted to the ICU in critical condition are at higher risk of developing pressure injuries. Use of prophylactic sacral dressing in combination with other preventive measures can bring a significant impact in reducing the pressure injury rate.

Introduction

Pressure injury (PI), also known as bedsore or decubitus ulcer, is a localized injury to the skin and underlying soft tissue, usually over a bony prominence, as a result of prolonged pressure or pressure in combination with shear. It is one of the most common problems affecting patients worldwide.1–3 The associated risks are prolonged immobility, mechanical irritation, sensory loss, ageing skin, poor nutrition and hydration status, incontinence of urine/stool, and co-morbid conditions, etc. Despite advancements in health care technology and improved access to health care services, the global prevalence of pressure ulcers remains significant. In the United States alone, approximately 2.5 million patients are affected annually.4 Literature indicates an increased burden of PI in high and middle-income countries.3,5–7 However, the prevalence remains underreported in low-income countries due to limited research studies. A recent systematic review conducted in Sub-Saharan Africa (2024) predicted an exponential rise in PI cases in low- and middle-income countries, largely due to resource limitations and inconsistent adherence to evidence-based interventions.8

PIs are particularly prevalent among critically ill patients admitted to intensive care units (ICUs).9 These patients are often immobile, mechanically ventilated, sedated, and may experience incontinence and poor nutritional status, all of which contribute to their elevated PI risk. Studies by Rubulotta F et al. (47.6%),10 Isfahani P et al. (16%)5 found a high incidence of PI among ICU patients. Additionally, the use of vasopressors, shocks and sepsis, and compromised tissue perfusion further elevate the risk.11,12 The presence of co-morbidities, such as diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and renal impairment, also increases PI susceptibility.13–15

The predominant areas for PI development include the sacrum, ankles, heels, greater trochanter, ischial tuberosities, and scapulae. Among these, the sacrum is the most commonly affected site for PI among ICU patients. This is supported by studies such as Coyer F et al. (22%) in their comparative study, Jacq G et al. (57.4%) point prevalence study, and Hu B et al. (80.4%) cross-sectional study reporting overall sacral injury rates.13,16,17 PI can lead to serious complications, like cellulitis, abscess, sepsis, and bone and joint infections. These complications can prolong hospital stays, increase patient morbidity, mortality, and psychological distress. Additionally, they impose significant financial constraints on families and place an economic strain on the health care system.18–20

To address the issue on PI prevention, several international guidelines, such as those published by the National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel (NPIAP), the European Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel (EUPAP) and the Pan Pacific Pressure Injury Alliance (PPPIA), 2019, recommended the use of prophylactic sacral dressing (PSD) in conjunction with standard preventive measures.21 These strategies include frequent repositioning and the use of pressure redistribution devices, regular skin assessments. Proper application and adherence to PSD protocol are emphasized as essential components of PI prevention strategies, especially in high-risk ICU patients.

Although several systematic reviews and meta-analyses (SRMA) have evaluated the effectiveness of PSD in preventing PI, most have included pooled data from heterogeneous designs, settings and diverse populations from ICUs, general wards, emergency departments, surgery wards, and operating theatres.22–24 This heterogeneity limits the applicability of findings specifically to ICU patients, who are at high risk of developing PI. Therefore, there is a need for a focused review to assess the effectiveness of PSD exclusively in preventing PI among ICU patients and to strengthen evidence-based clinical practice for this high-risk group.

Our objective was to determine the efficacy of prophylactic sacral dressing in the prevention of sacral pressure injury among ICU patients.

Methods

Design

A structured Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (SRMA) of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) was conducted following Cochrane guidelines. The review protocol was registered officially with PROSPERO, the international platform for registering systematic reviews (CRD420251016871).

Search Strategy

The relevant studies were identified through electronic searches of the PubMed and Scopus databases using MeSH terms and a combination of keywords. Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT) were applied to refine the search, and database-specific filters were used. Additional searches were conducted via the Wiley Online Library (publisher platform) and Google Scholar. The detailed search strategy is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Study Criteria

The eligibility of each study was appraised using specific inclusion and exclusion standards. The PICOS criteria followed were: participants: adult inpatients admitted to ICUs aged above 18 years or above irrespective of gender, caste and ethnicity; intervention: use of PSD; comparator: standard care (SC) with no sacral dressing; outcome: incidence of sacral pressure injury (sacral PI) rate and ≥ Stage II sacral PI; study design: RCTs. Peer-reviewed journals published between 1st January 2015 and 30th March 2025 were included. We excluded pediatric patients, study designs: all non-RCT designs, studies without ICU patients, and patients with current sacral injury/sacral skin condition.

Intervention Description

The included studies used two types of PSD, i.e., Mepilex and Allevyn brands. Both are multi-layered silicone foam dressings designed with similar adhesive properties, fluid absorption capacity, and moisture permeability. Evidence from multiple studies showed no significant differences between the two brands in terms of PI incidence, product performance, and safety outcomes.24–26 Given their comparable clinical effectiveness and their safety outcomes, the Mepilex and Allevyn brands were pooled as a single intervention group in our SRMA.

Retrieval of Relevant Articles

Three reviewers independently screened the titles and the abstracts of all identified research articles as per the pre-set selection criteria using Rayyan software. The full texts of each study were assessed to check for eligibility to include in our SRMA. Discrepancies in the selection process were resolved through consultation with a fourth reviewer until a final consensus was achieved.

Evaluation of the Study Quality

The Jonna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tool was used to assess the risk of potential bias in the selected RCTs in our SRMA.27 Three reviewers independently appraised the quality of the studies using the JBI tool. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a fourth reviewer until consensus was reached. The quality assessment covered 13 standard methodological domains, including aspects of randomization, allocation concealment, similarity of treatment groups, blinding of participants, personnel, and outcome assessors, uniformity of intervention, completeness of follow-up, participants’ analysis, outcome measures, and use of appropriate statistical analysis.27 Each domain was selected as Yes, No, or Unclear as their option.

Certainty of Evidence

GRADE approach was employed to evaluate the certainty of evidence for each outcome, i.e., incidence of sacral PI rate and ≥ Stage II sacral PI in our SRMA.28 Two reviewers independently assessed the certainty of evidence based on five standard GRADE domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, imprecision, indirectness, and publication bias. Discrepancies in judgment were resolved through discussion with the third reviewer. The certainty of evidence was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low.

Data Extraction

Three reviewers extracted data independently to ensure its consistency and to assess the risk of bias. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion with the fourth reviewer and consultation with the expert. The extracted data were critically assessed and included the following details: author name, publication year, region, type of interventions, total patient population, population in the PSD and the SC group, primary and secondary outcome measures, and the participants’ descriptions, i.e., age, gender, Braden score.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome measure included was sacral PI rate, and the secondary outcome measure was the number of patients identified with ≥ Stage II sacral PI. The description and classification of PI followed the standardized definitions outlined in the 2019 guidelines issued by the EPUAP, NPIAP, and PPIA.21

Quantitative Analysis

The odds ratio (OR) with 95% CI was measured for binary outcomes, i.e., for sacral PI rate and ≥ Stage II sacral PI with a fixed and random effect model. I2 statistics were used to assess the heterogeneity between the groups. The I2 index ranged 0% specifies no, 25% with low, 50% with moderate, and 75% with high heterogeneity.29 The I2 index > 50% would use the random effect model, and for I2 index < 50% fixed effect model would be applied. If heterogeneity is borderline, both the fixed and random effect models would be applied to calculate the combined OR. Publication bias was assessed using Egger's test quantitatively and qualitatively by funnel plot for both the primary and secondary outcome measures.30 p-value ≥ 0.05 would be considered as publication bias through the Eggers test.

Results

The initial database search yielded a total of 2,522 articles. Among them, 19 articles were flagged as duplicates, and a further 2,482 articles failed to qualify the selection criteria and were excluded after title and abstract screening. The remaining 21 articles were thoroughly reviewed with the full text, which further led to the exclusion of 14 articles due to different designs, different populations, standard care without sacral dressing, and different outcomes. A total, 7 articles met the criteria and were incorporated in our study following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis, 2020 (PRISMA) guidelines as presented in Figure 1.31

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram outlining the screening and inclusion of studies in the meta-analysis.31

The seven selected RCTs studies included a total of 3,735 adult patients admitted to ICUs at the start of the study, where 2144 patients were included in the interventional group using PSD, and 1591 patients received SC with no sacral dressing.31–38 Among these two RCTs enrolled a mixed patient population comprising ICU and non-ICU patients.37,38 The selected five RCT studies included a trial with two groups, i.e., the PSD and SC.32–36 Whereas two RCT studies had 1:1:1 trial allocation, i.e., Mepilex Border dressing, Allevyn dressing, and one with SC (without sacral dressing).36,37 The Mepilex and Allevyn groups were combined as one, i.e., PSD group, and compared with the SC in our SRMA. Mepilex border multi-layered silicone foam dressing was used as a PSD to prevent sacral PI in three studies.32,33,35 Allevyn multi-layered silicone was used in two studies.34,38 Whereas two studies used both Mepilex and Allevyn silicone foam dressing as a prophylactic measure in the PSD group.36,37 The Supplementary Table 2 outlines the participants’ description of the included seven studies in our SRMA, including the authors’ name, year, region, total patient population, number of patients allocated in PSD and SC groups, and the outcome measures.32–38 Supplementary Table 3 outlines the participants’ description of the selected studies, which includes age, gender, and the Braden score of both groups.32–38

The study employed the use of the JBI critical appraisal tool checklist to appraise the methodological quality across the 13 domains.27 This tool assessed the internal and statistical conclusion validity to assess the risk of bias of seven RCT studies included in our meta-analysis.32–38 Notably, a higher risk of bias was observed in three domains, i.e., participants had knowledge of their assigned treatment, treatment administrator, and the person assessing outcomes were aware of the group allocations in all the selected studies.32–38 Allocation concealment was not done in one study and was unclear in three RCT studies.34,36–38 Outcome measured was not reliable in one RCT study as it includes the findings of both ICU and non-ICU patients.37 Overall, studies showed a low to moderate risk of bias. A total of 7 RCTs that met the criteria were analyzed. Both fixed and random effect models were applied, indicating moderate heterogeneity.32–38 The Chi-squared test yielded (χ2 = 9.25, df = 6, p = 0.16), with an I2 value of 35.1%. The pooled OR under the fixed-effect model was 0.42 (95% CI: 0.29–0.62) with a statistically significant overall effect (Z = 4.35, p < 0.001). Similarly, the random effect model yielded an OR = 0.41 (95% CI: 0.26–0.62) showing a significant reduction in sacral PI rate (Z = 4.10, p < 0.001). These findings stated that the sacral PI rate was notably reduced in the group receiving PSD compared to the SC group, as depicted in Figure 2. Although dressing brands varied across studies, i.e., three studies used Mepilex, two studies used Allevyn and two studies used both brands as an intervention.32–38 All studies uniformly used multi-layered silicone foam dressings, which are considered clinically equivalent, and were therefore pooled as a single intervention group in our analysis. Based on the GRADE approach, the certainty of evidence for this outcome was rated as moderate due to risk of bias. No downgrading was applied for indirectness, as although two RCTs enrolled mixed ICU and non-ICU patient populations, one RCT reported data specific to ICU patients.38

A total of four RCTs were analyzed to evaluate the effect of PSD on the ≥ Stage II sacral PI.33,35,36,38 Both fixed and random effect models were used, indicating low heterogeneity (χ2 = 3.44, df = 3, p = 0.32) with an I2 value of 12.8%. The pooled OR under the fixed-effect model was 0.45 (95% CI: 0.25–0.80), showing a statistically significant reduction in ≥ Stage II sacral PI in the PSD group compared to the SC group (Z = 2.73, p = 0.006). Similarly, the random effect model yielded OR = 0.45 (95% CI: 0.25–0.80) (Z = 2.73, p = 0.006), confirming the significant effect as depicted in Figure 3. Discussion

Silicone foam dressing has been recommended as a prophylactic measure for individuals at risk to prevent pressure injury (PI), particularly in the intensive care unit (ICU). These guidelines have been endorsed based on evidence-based practice issued by the EPUAP, NPIAP, and PPPIA in 2019.21 The use of PSD is still a topic of contention among ICU patients. Xia J et al. and Fulbrook et al. stated that sacral foam dressing led to a notable decrease in sacral PI rates.22,23 However, with the inclusion of a heterogeneous patient population from both community and institutional settings, a smaller study group may limit the generalizability of these study findings to ICU patients. This highlights the need for a SRMA to outline the efficacy of PSD in preventing PI among ICU patients.

Our meta-analysis evaluated the efficacy of PSD in decreasing the sacral PI rate among adult patients admitted to ICUs. Therefore, findings can be of great importance in a clinical setting to prevent the incidence of sacral PI among the ICU patients. The risk of sacral PI is elevated more among the critically ill patients because of associated conditions like advanced age, decreased mobility, presence of comorbidities, moisture and incontinence, decreased nutritional intake, hemodynamic instability, etc.39–41 The findings of our SRMA stated that the use of PSD led to a marked decrease in sacral PI rate. The sacral PI rate was substantially decreased in the PSD group as compared to the standard care (SC) group (p < 0.001). The number of ≥ Stage II sacral PI was markedly reduced in the PSD group than in the SC group (p = 0.006). The positive outcome of these findings can be due to the shear force reduction, pressure re-distribution, and moisture management properties of the silicone foam dressing.

However, due to the limited studies, we included two RCTs which had both ICU and non-ICU patients, along with the five RCTs.32–38 The overall pooled analysis showed moderate heterogeneity for sacral PI rate, low heterogeneity for ≥ Stage II sacral PI, with no publication bias in our study. Based on GRADE assessment, the certainty of evidence for the sacral PI rate was rated as moderate, due to indirectness from one study involving a mixed patient population and concerns regarding risk of bias. Similarly, for ≥ Stage II sacral PI was rated as low, due to indirectness and risk of bias in one of the included studies. Our findings align with a study conducted by Sillmon K et al. 2021, which supported a decline in hospital-acquired PI cases with the application of PSD among the ICU or critically ill patients. Though there was a presence of incorporated designs, there was no risk of bias in our study. These findings provide supportive evidence for the efficacy of PSD; nevertheless, the strength of evidence varies across the outcomes, highlighting the need for additional high-quality studies.42

To augment the robustness of our findings and increase the statistical power, data from two RCTs (1:1:1 design) and five RCTs (1:1 design) were combined in our SRMA.32–38 Given the clinical similarity between the interventions, two RCTs with a 1:1:1 design, which included both Mepilex and Allevyn groups, were combined as a single intervention, i.e., PSD group, and compared with the SC group.36,37 Therefore, in our final analysis, data from both the Mepilex and Allevyn groups were pooled to provide a comprehensive estimate of their overall effectiveness in preventing PI. To support this, evidence from studies stated that both Mepilex and Allevyn multi-layered silicone foam dressings reported no significant differences in PI incidence or dressing integrity, indicating comparable clinical performance.24–26 Although these multi-layered foam dressings differ in brand name, but share common core properties like redistributing pressure, reducing shear and friction, skin protection and cushioning, and also providing a hydrated wound setting that promotes healing. Additionally, pooling data from both types of dressings reinforces the evidence for PSD. However, in our study, the inclusion of both ICU and non-ICU patients and differences in the baseline risk may limit the generalizability of findings across critical care settings. Nevertheless, the positive outcome in our study indicated that PSD is beneficial in diverse ICU settings.37,38 Future research trials targeting more ICU patients could improve the generalizability of these results.

Although our SRMA primarily examined the clinical effectiveness of PSD in reducing sacral PI. It is also essential to address the patient-centered outcomes that can influence care quality. Patient-centered outcomes such as reduced pain, enhanced comfort, preserved skin integrity, and lower risk of secondary infections are inherently linked to using PSD. These factors can significantly influence the overall care experience, satisfaction, and recovery trajectory of ICU patients. Supporting this, studies stated that use of multi-layer silicone dressings, Mepilex and Allevyn brands of PSD, showed similar efficacy, product use, and integrity in reducing sacral PI.24–26

Additionally, the use of these silicone foam dressings was found to reduce pain before and during dressing changes and lessen the occurrence of peri-wound maceration.43 Thus, this evidence supports the integration of PSD into routine ICU practice along with the other standard preventive measures to address both clinical and patient-centered priorities.

As the clinical outcome of PSD was the main focus of our SRMA, its cost-effectiveness remains a critical consideration. Even though our study did not mention the cost related to the use of these PSDs. There are studies stating that the net cost of using sacral dressing was cost-effective than treating the patient with sacral injury. Randomized controlled trials conducted by Genedy M et al.44 stated that application of PSD for the sacral area is cost-effective among the high-risk intensive care patients. Similarly, Santamaria N et al.45 stated that the average net cost of using the Mepilex Border Sacrum and Mepilex heel dressings in the intervention group, as PSD showed a reduction in cost than the control group, leading to cost savings among the critically ill patients admitted in the hospital. Therefore, further exploration can be done on these cost-related factors by applying the PSD to address its outcomes on ICU patients. These ICU inpatients remain at a heightened risk of acquiring PI than any other hospitalized patients. Emphasizes are made to prevent PI as these can lead to additional financial burden to the patient's family. The routine use of these PSD can bring a significant impact by preventing the PI as well as reducing the financial burden caused by bedsore care and management in our clinical setting. However, caution to be taken when generalizing our findings, as the certainty of evidence ranged from moderate to low.

The overall findings of our SRMA stated that there was a relationship between the use of PSD in preventing the sacral PI. Yet, more meticulous research is to be done to substantiate the associations of our findings. Three studies did not mention age and the Braden Index Score.35–37 Therefore, future research should focus on patient-related factors like age, gender, and Braden score, as well as settings, to ensure the consistency and applicability of PSD effectiveness among the critically ill patients.

Limitations

The overall pooled analysis of our study yielded moderate heterogeneity for sacral PI rate, which may limit the precision of the pooled estimates. This may be possibly due to a diverse patient population or mixed settings (ICU and non-ICU patients) and methodological variability, i.e., inclusion of a three-arm (1:1:1) with the two-arm (1:1) design.32–39 Furthermore, increased subject dropout, post-randomization exclusion, or lost to follow-up in studies pose a risk of participant attrition bias.32–38 Additionally, studies lacked reporting of characteristics like gender, age, and Braden score data, which limited our access to compare between the study groups, affecting the external validity of our findings.35–37 Due to the nature of the intervention, the participant and the person delivering the treatment, and the outcome assessor were not blinded, which may have introduced performance bias in our study. These limitations of our findings highlight the need for larger, well-designed quality RCT studies with a homogenous population to generalize the findings in the future.

Conclusion

Prophylactic sacral dressings had significantly reduced the sacral PI rate and ≥ Stage II sacral PI among the ICU patients. Nevertheless, with the methodological limitations, i.e., mixed patient population, lack of blinding, and incomplete baseline data, it recommends the need for more studies in the future to confirm our SRMA findings. In addition, future research can explore the cost-effectiveness of the different types of PSD to assess the economic effect and its sustainable clinical implications.
