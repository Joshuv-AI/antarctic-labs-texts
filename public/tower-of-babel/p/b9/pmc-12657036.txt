Retinal detachment risk in a cohort of von hippel-lindau patients at a Major U.S. tertiary care facility
Authors: Konstantinos G. Baroutis, Gustavo Sakuno, Sandra Hoyek, Nimesh A. Patel, Joan W. Miller, Demetrios G. Vavvas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To report the retinal findings, cumulative incidence of retinal capillary hemangioblastoma (RCH), retinal detachment (RD) outcomes, and risk factors in a large cohort of von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) patients within a major U.S. Tertiary Care Facility.Design:A retrospective cohort study was conducted between January 2005 and August 2024.Methods:VHL patients were identified from Mass General Brigham’s Research Patient Data Repository. Manual chart review confirmed those with ophthalmologic examinations. A nested 1:2 matched case-control study compared RD cases with controls (VHL patients with RCH but without RD). Kaplan-Meier analysis estimated cumulative RCH incidence, and conditional logistic regression identified RD risk factors. Patients without RCH were censored at the last follow-up.Results:Among 159 VHL patients, 104 had comprehensive eye examinations with a median follow-up of 7 years [IQR, 2–11]; 95% attended at least one follow-up appointment. The incidence rate of RCH was 2.3 per 100 person-years, calculated from person-time starting at birth and ending at age of first RCH diagnosis (for cases) or last follow-up visit (for non-cases). Eighteen eyes (14 patients, 13% of the cohort; 95% CI, 8%−22%) developed RD, predominantly combined rhegmatogenous/tractional (94%), with 29% bilateral involvement. Notably, no patient without an RCH developed an RD in our cohort. A case-control study found that each additional prior focal treatment significantly lowered the risk of RD (OR = 0.15, 95% CI 0.03–0.63; p = 0.01), while the presence of renal cell carcinoma and pancreatic cysts were associated with increased risk, possibly indicating more advanced systemic disease. All RD cases exhibited proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR; Grade C: 89%, Grade B: 11%) at the time of diagnosis. Overall, 12 of 16 eyes (75%) achieved reattachment, 11 of which (69%) required multiple surgeries (median, 2 [IQR, 1–3]), but visual outcomes remained poor (median final logMAR BCVA, 2.3 [IQR, 0.55–2.7], counting fingers).Conclusions:VHL patients face a high lifetime risk of RCH and associated RD. PVR frequently complicates the surgical treatment of RD and yields limited visual recovery, underscoring the need for proactive surveillance and timely therapeutic intervention. The universal presence of PVR in these RD eyes is a novel finding that warrants investigation into a possible, though currently speculative, molecular link to pVHL dysregulation.

Introduction

Von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease, an autosomal dominant multi-system syndrome (OMIM 193,300), is caused by the loss of function of the pVHL protein resulting from pathologic mutations in the VHL gene (chromosome 3p25).1–3 This loss of function prevents the ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of its primary substrate, the hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF). HIF is a key transcriptional regulator that, when constitutively stabilized, drives the overexpression of pro-angiogenic genes, creating a tumor microenvironment that leads to tumorigenesis in multiple organs, including the retina, cerebellum, kidneys, and adrenal gland.1,4–6

Retinal capillary hemangioblastoma (RCH), first described over a century ago, is a non-cancerous tumor that arises from the vascular endothelium (capillaries) and the glial (stromal) cells of the neurosensory retina or optic disc.7 It is the most common and earliest manifestation of VHL, positioning ophthalmologists as critical first responders in its diagnosis.8,9 A recent meta-analysis revealed that the incidence of RCH in VHL is high (~50 %), increases with age, is highest in the European cohort, and may not depend on specific mutations.10 Although vision is retained in most patients, legal blindness occurs in approximately 5 % of patients, and up to one in four eyes may be severely affected.11,12

Although small RCHs are often managed effectively with laser photocoagulation or cryotherapy, advanced cases with marked exudation and its complications, such as exudative retinal detachment (RD), frequently require vitreoretinal surgery. Previous studies have explored surgical outcomes in such scenarios: Raval et al. reported that visual acuity improved in only 7 out of 16 eyes (44 %) due to complications like proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR) and new RCH development13; Gaudric et al. found that vitrectomy with either retinectomy or laser achieved initial retinal reattachment in 21 of 23 eyes, yet long-term vision was compromised by PVR and neovascular glaucoma14; Krzystolik et al. noted anatomical success in all 24 vitrectomized eyes, but performing a retinotomy was linked to new RCH at that site, as well as accelerated progression of established RCH.15 Both Gaudric et al. and Krzystolik et al. noted an increase in PVR following retinotomy.14,15

While previous studies have primarily focused on managing exudative complications of large RCH, the prevalence and long-term outcomes of rhegmatogenous and combined forms of RD remain elusive. Thus, we conducted a large retrospective study within the Mass General Brigham healthcare system to report the retinal findings and outcomes of RD in a large cohort of VHL patients.

Methods

Study design and setting

This is a retrospective cohort study of VHL patients registered in the Mass General Brigham (MGB) healthcare system between January 2005 and August 2024. Patients were identified using the Mass General Brigham’s Research Patient Data Repository (RPDR) Query Tool, a Medicare-linked database with longitudinal electronic health records of over 7 million patients, covering approximately 50 % of Boston metropolitan area residents.16 The syndrome was registered following the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) code 759.6 and Q85.8 (ICD-10-CM). The study was approved by the Partners Institutional Review Board (IRB 2024P001344). Due to its retrospective nature, informed consent from the patients was waived. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human subjects and the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE)17 reporting guideline.

Participants

Patients were identified using the MGB centralized clinical data registry, and each chart was reviewed longitudinally, including all ophthalmological visits from the initial to the most recent. The inclusion criteria were patients confirmed by genetic testing or clinical guidelines,18 with at least one ophthalmological examination; no minimum follow-up time was required.

Data collection

Primary outcomes included ophthalmologic findings in patients diagnosed with VHL, with a focus on the progression of RCH. Ophthalmological images included ultra-widefield photographs and fluorescein angiography via OptosAdvance (post-2008); in cases where pre-2008 images were unavailable or in instances of media opacity/young age, standard fundus exams or indirect ophthalmoscopy were used. Key ocular outcomes included tumor burden, such as the number, location (peripheral or juxtapapillary, retinal quadrants were defined by the horizontal and vertical meridians passing through the optic disc, dividing the retina into superior temporal, inferior temporal, superior nasal, and inferior nasal regions), laterality (unilateral or bilateral), and complications (e.g., RD, phthisis). The time to RCH occurrence was defined as the date at first identification. Additional data collected were age of first RCH, sex, race, mutation types, any ocular treatment received (hereafter referred to as focal treatments, including laser photocoagulation, cryotherapy, photodynamic therapy, anti-VEGF injections), and systemic manifestations, including tumors associated with von Hippel-Lindau syndrome (e.g., pheochromocytomas, renal cell carcinoma, pancreatic cysts, spinal hemangioblastoma).

Furthermore, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), was measured on the logMAR scale.19 For BCVA outcomes, we included data from all affected eyes and used a mixed effects model with patient ID as a random effect to account for the correlation between fellow eyes in patients with bilateral disease; for RD risk and outcomes, both eyes were analyzed. For conversion to logMAR values, visual acuity of counting fingers (CF), hand motion (HM), light perception, and no light perception were assigned values of 2.1, 2.4, 2.7, and 3.0, respectively.20

Mutation types were classified as follows: 1) missense mutations (MSs), 2) protein-truncating mutations (PTMs, including nonsense, frameshift, partial deletions), 3) complete deletion of the coding region of the VHL gene as previously described.10,21,22 Mutations with unpredictable effects, such as splice mutations, were assigned as other, as they can have structural alterations on the resulting protein with unpredictable function.23

Nested case-control design

A nested case-control study was performed within the retrospective VHL cohort, where cases (RD during follow-up) were matched 1:2 with controls from the risk set at the time of case occurrence. All cases were patients who developed RD during follow-up (n = 14). This design, which defines a case by its first RD event, ensures that each patient is included in the analysis only once, thus maintaining the independence of observations. For each case, two control patients without RD (who had RCH only) were selected and matched for: (i) sex, (ii) age ± 6 years, and (iii) ophthalmic follow-up length ± 3 years (n = 28), yielding 14 three-patient strata.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR). They were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test (for cases versus controls) or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (for paired measurements), as confirmed by the non-normal distribution detected by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables are expressed as percentages. For key proportions, 95 % confidence intervals were calculated using the Clopper-Pearson exact method.

To calculate the incidence rate of RCH, we summed the total observed person-years of follow-up for the entire cohort. For each patient, person-time at risk began at birth (age 0) since patients were disease-free at birth and ended at the age of their first RCH diagnosis (cases) or their last follow-up visit (non-cases). Cumulative incidence by age was additionally estimated using Kaplan–Meier analysis, providing a lifetime risk context. The overall incidence rate was then calculated as the total number of new RCH cases divided by the total person-years of follow-up and expressed per 100 person-years. Associations between each binary predictor and RD were assessed with univariate conditional logistic regression. A multivariate model was initially attempted but was not pursued due to model instability and overfitting. A complete-case approach was used for missing data because fewer than 10 % of observations were incomplete, and the missing data appeared to be missing completely at random.24 We had 14 patients with RD, each univariate analysis had ≥10 events-per-variable following the guidelines for safe analysis interpretation.25 Models condition on the 14 matched strata and report odds ratios (OR) with 95 % confidence intervals (CI). Two-sided p < 0.05 defined statistical significance. As these univariate analyses were considered exploratory for hypothesis generation in a rare disease cohort, no formal adjustments were made for multiple comparisons. Therefore, p-values from these specific models should be interpreted with caution. The number of prior ocular focal treatment sessions was coded as an ordinal variable (0 = none, 1 = single session, 2 = two or more sessions). In each univariate conditional logistic regression, the odds ratio therefore reflects the change in RD odds per one-level increase in treatment exposure. All clinical data were fully de-identified before export and analysis by HIPAA regulations and the requirements of the Partners Institutional Review Board (protocol: 2024P001344). Three masked graders (K.G.B., G.S., D.G.V.) independently evaluated 17 eyes for the grade of PVR (proliferative vitreoretinopathy), according to the Updated Retina Society Classification26 (stages A, B, C). Fleiss κ, appropriate for three raters, was used. Consensus resolved discrepancies. All statistical analyses were conducted using Python 3.11.7 with pandas (2.2.2), NumPy (1.26.4), statsmodels (0.14), and matplotlib (3.8.4). BCVA was analyzed using generalized estimating equations (GEE) to account for within-patient correlation between fellow eyes and repeated measurements, given the non-normal distribution of BCVA values. Analyses of the nested-control study have been deposited in an openly accessible GitHub repository: (https://github.com/kbaroutis/vhl-rd-clogit).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of 161 patients identified with potential VHL syndrome, 159 (99 %) were confirmed (95 % genetically; n=151 pathogenic VHL mutations; 5 % clinically; n=8), with 2 (1 %) false positives (Suppl. Fig. 1). Among these, 104 (65 %) from 84 families met the inclusion criteria, with 98 (94 %) genetically confirmed and 6 (6 %) clinically diagnosed. Median age was 29 years [IQR 14–46], with 56 (54 %) females. Most were White (78 %) and non-Hispanic (89 %). Mutation types included missense (35 %; n = 36), protein-truncating (29 %; n = 30), complete deletions (3 %; n = 3), other variants (2 %; n = 2), none identified (5 %; n = 5), and unknown (26 %; n = 28). Their characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Systemic manifestations of VHL disease were prevalent in this cohort, with CNS hemangioblastomas present in 72 patients (69 %), spinal hemangioblastomas in 61 patients (59 %), pancreatic cysts in 47 patients (45 %), renal cysts in 34 patients (33 %), renal cell carcinoma in 29 patients (28 %), pheochromocytomas in 19 patients (18 %), pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors in 17 patients (16 %), and pancreatic adenocarcinoma in 2 patients (2 %). Of the 104 patients, 99 (95 %) attended at least one follow-up appointment, with a median ophthalmic follow-up of 7 years [IQR 2–11]. The median age at first RCH diagnosis was 24 years [IQR 16–36], with RCH as the initial VHL manifestation in 30 (29 %) patients, resulting in an incidence rate of 2.3 new cases per 100 person-years.

Longitudinal outcomes

Among the 99 patients with follow-up data, at baseline, 38 patients (38 %) had no RCH lesions, while 61 (62 %) had lesions (unilateral: 27/61, 44 %; bilateral: 34/61, 56 %), with 13 (21 %) showing juxtapapillary involvement. At final follow-up, 31 patients (31 %) had no lesions, while 68 (69 %) had lesions (unilateral: 22/68, 32 %; bilateral: 46/68, 68 %). Among these, 13 (19 %) showed juxtapapillary involvement (Suppl. Table 1). The majority of RCH in this cohort were located in the superior temporal quadrant (84 %, 57/68 patients), followed by the inferior temporal (82 %, 56/68), superior nasal (44 %, 30/68), and inferior nasal quadrants (37 %, 25/68; Suppl. Fig. 2). GEE analysis showed no significant change in BCVA between baseline and follow-up (β=0.028 logMAR; 95% CI, −0.002 to 0.057, p=0.068).

Incidence and clinical features of retinal detachment

RD occurred in 14 of 104 patients (13 %; 95 % CI, 8 %−22 %), involving 18 of 208 eyes (9 %; 95 % CI, 5 %−13 %) in the cohort. The racial demographics of this subgroup consisted of 11 White patients (79 %), 1 Black patient (7 %), and 2 with missing data (14 %), reflecting a distribution like that of our overall study cohort. No patient without RCH detached. Of the 18 RD eyes, four (22 %) ended with chronic RD at the final visit. Bilateral RD was seen in 4 patients (29 % of the 14 RD patients, and 4 % of the entire cohort). Thirteen patients had combined rhegmatogenous and tractional (RRD/TRD; 93 % of the RD patients, and 94 % of RD eyes), and 1 had total exudative RD (7 % of the RD patients and 6 % of the RD eyes, Table 2).

All RRD/TRD cases exhibited PVR at diagnosis, predominantly Grade C (89 %), with the remainder Grade B (11 %) (Fig. 1). Inter-grader agreement on PVR staging among three observers was high (96 % identical ratings), with a Fleiss κ value of 0.78 indicating “substantial agreement.” Stage C was unanimously assigned in 15 of 17 eyes, Stage B in 1 eye, while grading was split 2:1 (two graders B, one grader C) for the remaining eye (Suppl. Table 3).

Surgical management and outcomes of retinal detachment

The management approach for these complex detachments, detailed in Table 3, reflects the advanced pathology observed. Pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) was the most common primary approach, performed in 9 of the 16 surgically treated eyes (56 %). Given the severity of PVR, intraoperative maneuvers were frequently necessary, including membrane peeling in over half of the vitrectomized eyes (55 %) and relaxing retinectomy in 27 %. The use of silicone oil as a long-term tamponade in a subset of cases (27 %) further underscores the guarded prognosis and high risk of redetachment. Reattachment of the retina was achieved in 12 out of 16 surgically treated eyes (75 %), 11 (69 %) following multiple surgeries, and single-operation anatomical success was achieved in only one eye (6 %) (Fig. 2). Visual outcomes following surgical management of VHL-related RDs deteriorated substantially post-operatively in this cohort of 17 eyes from 13 patients. Among eyes with complete pre-and post-operative visual acuity data (n=9), none retained good vision (final ≤0.3 logMAR, ≥20/40), while five eyes (56 %) lost ≥0.3 logMAR, two eyes (22 %) improved ≥0.3 logMAR, and two eyes (22 %) had stable vision. Final visual acuity analysis of all 17 eyes revealed that none achieved good vision (≤0.3 logMAR), only five eyes (29 %) attained vision better than 20/200 (logMAR < 1.0), and 12 eyes (71 %) ended with poor vision worse than 20/200 (logMAR > 1.0). The median logMAR BCVA declined from 1.4 [IQR 0.55–2] to 2.3 [IQR 0.55–2.7] from initial to final visit, respectively.

Risk factor analysis for retinal detachment (Matched case-control study)

Findings from the matched-case control analysis showed that any prior focal ocular treatment significantly reduced the risk of RD (OR=0.15; 95 % CI 0.03–0.63; p=0.01). Patients who developed RD had fewer prior focal therapy sessions (median, 0 [IQR, 0–1.25]) compared to the RCH-only group (median, 2 [IQR, 1–3]; p = 0.0028). Laser photocoagulation was the predominant modality, representing 95 % of all treatments in the RCH-only cohort and 100 % in the subset of patients with RD who had received prior treatments. Additionally, cryotherapy (1 session) and photodynamic therapy (2 sessions) were utilized in the control group (Suppl. Table 2).

Notably, systemic manifestations such as renal cell carcinoma (OR 12.23, 95 % CI 1.51–99.37; p=0.019) and pancreatic cysts (OR 11.08, 95 % CI 1.39–88.49; p=0.023) significantly increased the likelihood of RD, reflecting a more advanced stage of VHL syndrome (Table 4). A high baseline RCH burden (≥3 lesions) showed a borderline association with increased RD risk (OR=3.75; p=0.058).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study is one of the largest modern U.S. cohorts of patients diagnosed with VHL syndrome, with 95 % of cases genetically confirmed and a median follow-up of 7 years [IQR, 2–11]. Key findings revealed: (1) a high prevalence of RD (~13 %), with almost a third (29 %) of these patients having bilateral involvement; (2) PVR complicated all cases of RRD, adversely affecting both management and prognosis; (3) any prior ocular treatment, predominantly focal laser photocoagulation, was associated with reduced development of RD (approximately a seven-fold reduction in odds).

Early-onset RCH is a hallmark of von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) disease, with our patient cohort showing a median age at first diagnosis of 24 years [IQR, 16–36]. Notably, pediatric-onset RCH is rare, with fewer than 5 % of cases reported before age 10 (3/104 patients) in our study, consistent with existing literature that underscores the infrequency of childhood presentations.27,28 Furthermore, based on the cumulative incidence, 1 in 2 VHL patients had developed an RCH lesion by age 31. Almost 87 % will exhibit RCH in their lifetime (Fig. 3). This indicates a high penetrance of ocular manifestations, necessitating early monitoring, as early treatment with ablative therapies prevents complications (e.g., exudation, hemorrhage).9 Moreover, our data highlight the predilection of RCH for the temporal quadrants of the retina, particularly the superior temporal quadrant (84 %), with only 18 % of the RCH in the juxtapapillary region. These numbers appear to be a consensus in the literature.11,29

In this study, RD in VHL disease represents a severe complication characterized by universal PVR and considerable vision loss (BCVA at final visit: 2.3 [IQR: 0.55–2.7]). Of the 104 VHL patients in this cohort, 13 % developed RD, and all RRD/TRD cases developed PVR. While our study cannot establish causality, this universal finding leads us to hypothesize a potential link between pVHL dysregulation and a pro-fibrotic retinal environment. It is biologically plausible that mutations disrupting pVHL function, which drive hypoxia signaling (HIF-1α accumulation) and the overexpression of known fibrogenic mediators (PDGF, TGF-β2),30–32 could contribute to PVR pathogenesis (Fig. 4). However, this proposed mechanism remains speculative and requires dedicated molecular studies for validation.

Prior studies that focused on large RCH or RCH complicated by fibrotic tissue that could not be treated with laser photocoagulation or cryotherapy suggest that pars plana vitrectomy with relaxing retinectomy may preserve or improve vision22,33 in tractional RD, contrary to our results. Limited follow-up and selected case reports may explain the discrepancies. Indeed, Krzystolik et al. reported that among 23 eyes from 23 patients who underwent vitrectomy for severe ocular disease, within a 24-month follow-up, 16 (70 %) eyes experienced postoperative RD, mirroring our results of multiple surgeries being needed in these patients.15 Peng et al. linked Arg167Trp mutations to sustained fibrosis post-retinectomy, supporting our findings that VHL mutations drive PVR via pVHL dysregulation.22 The low rate of exudative RD in our cohort (1/18 RD eyes, 6 %), compared to 17/18 (94 %) with combined rhegmatogenous and tractional RD, contrasts with prior studies and likely reflects the standard practice in this large tertiary referral center of very early photocoagulation for RCH, when lesions are small, to prevent exudative complications.12 This highlights the importance of early intervention, treating with laser or cryotherapy, to prevent the progression of RCH to RD, particularly given the possibility of lesion growth during lapses in follow-up. Finally, the emerging new systemic therapies, such as HIF-2α inhibitors like belzutifan, could serve as a new therapeutic avenue that directly addresses the challenges identified in our cohort. Our findings underscore that preventing RD is paramount, as universal and severe PVR frequently complicates its surgical management. By targeting the underlying HIF-2α pathway, belzutifan can reduce the size and vascularity of RCH lesions. This systemic approach aligns with our study’s conclusion that early, effective treatment of RCH protects against RD. For advanced cases requiring surgery, belzutifan could be used as a neoadjuvant treatment, shrinking the tumor pre-operatively to simplify the procedure, reduce bleeding, and potentially mitigate the aggressive PVR development observed in all RRD/TRD cases in our study.34–36

Limitations of this study should be noted. Firstly, while our analysis revealed a strong protective association between prior focal therapy and risk of RD, this finding may be subject to confounding by indication. It is plausible that eyes with smaller, less aggressive, or more peripherally located RCH lesions were preferentially selected for focal treatment and carried an intrinsically lower risk of progression. Conversely, eyes with more severe disease may have been poor candidates for focal therapy from the outset. Therefore, we cannot definitively disentangle the protective effect of the treatment itself from the favorable baseline characteristics of the treated lesions. Nevertheless, this finding still supports the current clinical paradigm of proactive treatment for amenable RCH to prevent complications. Secondly, the sample size of patients with VHL and RD is relatively small (n = 14), with wide confidence intervals observed in our logistic regression analysis, and should be interpreted cautiously. A future multicenter study can increase the sample size. Thirdly, the VHL patient population in this study may be unique to our hospital. It could differ from that in other hospitals, as the disease phenotype is geographically dependent.37 Finally, by including only patients with ophthalmic exams at our tertiary center, we may have introduced a selection bias. This could overrepresent patients with more severe disease, potentially inflating our reported incidence rates for RCH and RD. However, unlike broader RCH-focused studies, our cohort uniquely isolates RD as a primary endpoint, revealing universal PVR as a defining feature in VHL. Future work should integrate molecular therapeutic avenues (e.g., HIF inhibitors like belzutifan) with surgical salvage to address this unmet need.

Conclusion

In this contemporary U.S. cohort of 104 VHL patients, 13 % (95 % CI, 8 %−22 %) developed RD, which was frequently bilateral (29 %) and predominantly RRD/TRD (94 %). Clinical outcomes were poor, with only 75 % of surgically treated eyes achieving reattachment despite multiple surgeries, resulting in a final median BCVA of 2.3 logMAR [IQR, 0.55–2.7]. Notably, all RRD/TRD cases exhibited PVR. This cohort finding raises the hypothesis that disease-specific pathways, such as pVHL dysregulation, may foster a pro-fibrotic retinal environment. Given the devastating visual outcomes and surgical complexity, our findings underscore that the primary goal must be the prevention of RD through early, proactive treatment of RCH. Future research should leverage the universal incidence of PVR in this cohort to investigate its molecular drivers and identify therapeutic targets to mitigate this aggressive phenotype.
