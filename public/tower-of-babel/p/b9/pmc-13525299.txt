Background

HPD (or obligated patella dislocation) is a rare patella instability pathology. 1 Although it is more common in children, it can also be observed in the adult population. It is an extremely detrimental condition in which the patella is reduced in full extension and dislocates with every flexion movement, most often initiating at 30° of knee flexion. Anatomically, HPD is due to multiple soft-tissue pathologies, whereas there is typically a significant contracture of the iliotibial band (ITB), lateral retinaculum, vastus lateralis, and quadriceps muscle/tendon unit. An attenuation of the medial retinaculum is also typically encountered. Concomitant bony mechanisms increasing the Q-angle may coexist, and it is important to measure for an increased tibial tubercle-trochlear groove distance, patellar height, trochlear dysplasia, femoral version, and lower extremity alignment. Given the exceptional etiopathogenic behavior of this condition, the surgical technique is unique and must be well recognized. A combination of medial and lateral procedures is typically recommended.3-6,8

The patient is a 37-year-old man with left knee pain and instability. He was an amateur soccer player. He had an ipsilateral patellar tendon rupture, which was surgically repaired 7 months prior, but reported that the day after surgery, he suddenly fell and felt significant pain in his knee. After this episode, he reported progressively worsening pain and disability during the rehabilitation period. He attempted physical therapy for 3 months with no improvement and worsening of his symptoms. He was unable to ascend or descend stairs. The Kujala knee score was 16.

Indications

Examination of the patient revealed a complete lateral patella dislocation at 30° of knee flexion, which reduced in full extension. There was significant pain when attempting to maintain it reduced.

Radiographs demonstrated neutral long limb mechanical alignment. Broken hardware from a failed patellar tendon repair, patella alta (a Caton-Deschamps ratio of 1.5), and a significantly increased TTTG distance of 26 mm were revealed.

The final diagnosis was an HPD. The patient elected to undergo surgery to stabilize his patella. This involved hardware removal followed by a planned medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction (MPFLR) and tibial tubercle osteotomy (TTO) with anteromedialization and distalization. Additionally, a proximal realignment procedure, including lateral retinaculum lengthening with a vastus lateralis tendon release and quadriceps tendon lengthening, was concurrently performed. 2

Technique Description

The patient was positioned supine. Saphenous and femoral nerve peripheral blocks were performed first, followed by spinal anesthesia. Examination under anesthesia revealed a reduced patella in full extension and dislocation at 30° of knee flexion. It was impossible to maintain its reduced position during knee flexion. A high thigh tourniquet was used. Aseptic preparation with chlorhexidine and drapes was put in the proper position. The tourniquet was insufflated, and the starting time was registered. The skin was marked with a dermographic pen for an anterior approach to the knee. The incision was performed 10 cm proximally and 10 cm distally to the proximal and lower pole of the patella, respectively. Medial and lateral soft tissue flaps were developed to both the medial and lateral epicondyles.

The first step was to harvest a partial-thickness quadriceps tendon graft for the MPFLR. A 10-mm wide and 8 to 9 cm long graft was harvested, obtaining the superficial quadriceps tendon layer (rectus anterior and the middle portion of the tendon, being the lateral and the medial vastus tendons). The patellar attachment was left intact, and the tendon graft was sutured to the patellar periosteum with nonabsorbable sutures. A soft tissue tunnel was developed along the native course of the MPFL through the medial retinaculum toward the femoral MPFL anatomical insertion site.

On the medial side, the adductor magnus tendon was identified and retracted with a hemostat. The adductor tubercle was identified immediately distal to the tendon, and the MPFL anatomic attachment was marked 4 mm distal and 2 mm anterior to it. Two suture anchors were inserted at this point (3.5 mm corkscrew anchors; Arthrex, Inc). The MPFL graft was passed under the soft-tissue tunnel and left free. The MPFL graft fixation is the last step of the procedure.

The midline approach was now developed distally up to 7 cm distal to the tibial tubercle for a Fulkerson osteotomy. Both sides of the patellar tendon were identified and released. A hemostat was placed distally under the patellar tendon to identify the most proximal aspect of the tibial tubercle. The anterior tibialis muscle was released subperiosteally from the proximal tibia, and Hohmann retractors were placed, extending to the posterior tibia. According to the preoperative plan, 2 parallel 1.8-mm Kirschner wires were inserted from medial to lateral on a 30° oblique plane to obtain approximately 1 cm of anteriorization, depending on a case-by-case basis. A motorized saw is now used to perform the osteotomy, starting at the most proximal aspect, in a transverse direction, parallel to the hemostats, followed by the tibial tubercle (TT) osteotomy in a medial-to-lateral direction, underneath the Kirschner wires. For distalization, 1 cm of the anterior tibial bone was also osteotomized to receive the TT. The osteotomy was completed with an osteotome, and an anteromedialization and distalization were performed, according to the preoperative plan and TTTG measurement. The TT was provisionally fixed with Kirschner wires and fixated with two 4.5-mm bicortical screws. Knee motion is now performed to assess for patellar tracking.

If the patella is still dislocating laterally, the lateral soft tissue complex is released from distal to proximal on a step-by-step basis. A lateral retinaculum lengthening was performed first. The superficial fibers of the ITB were incised next to the patella, and the deep fibers near the femoral side. In this context, the lateral patellotibial ligament (LPTL) and lateral patellomeniscal ligament (LPML) were also lengthened. At this point, grade 2 to 3 patellar chondromalacia was observed at the distal and lateral aspect. Tracking is again tested up to ≥90° of knee flexion. If the patella is still tracking laterally, the vastus lateralis is released from the anterior rectus femoris tendon. The knee is retested, and if it continues to track laterally, a quadriceps lengthening procedure is performed. The 2 superficial layers of the quadriceps tendon were transversely incised proximally (8 cm from the superior pole), and the deep portion (vastus intermedius) was incised distally, close to the superior pole. The quadriceps is lengthened with the knee positioned between 70° and 90° of knee flexion with the patella reduced. Patellar tracking is again tested. Once successful patellar tracking is achieved through a full range of motion (ROM), the rectus anterior tendon lengthening is repaired with a 2.0 FiberWire suture (Arthrex), as well as the vastus lateralis, more proximally, to the rectus anterior and vastus intermedius tendons. Approximating nontensioning sutures on the lateral side is strongly encouraged to prevent subsequent iatrogenic medial patellar instability.

Finally, the MPFL quadriceps tendon graft was fixated on the femoral side at 30° of knee flexion, with the patella reduced. Lateral patellar translation of 1 to 2 quadrants should be maintained to prevent overconstraint. A suction drainage was placed for 24 hours.

After wound irrigation, the deep and superficial layers are closed with suture.

Postoperative rehabilitation begins with ROM exercises from day 1 with heel slides, 3 to 4 times a day. A knee immobilizer is used between exercise sessions. Knee flexion is allowed up to 90° during the first 2 weeks, and free ROM after 2 weeks. Quadriceps-activating isometric exercises are also started on postoperative day 1 with the knee immobilizer on. Patellar mobility exercises, from lateral to medial, are also encouraged. Nonweightbearing was indicated for the first 6 weeks, and a progressive rehabilitation protocol was then established, following endurance, strength, and power. Return to play (RTP) was allowed between 6 and 9 months postoperatively.

Results

After 26 months of follow-up, the patient reported no redislocation episodes and good functional outcomes (Kujala knee score 80), and a satisfaction score (Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation) was 77. He had decided not to RTP at this time.

Discussion

This is a relatively rare and complex procedure that is not without potential complications. A knee extensor lag or lack of full knee flexion is one of the most common complications after a quadriceps tendon lengthening procedure. Recurrent HPD secondary to reformation of quadriceps contracture is a potential complication if ROM exercises are not performed starting on postoperative day 1. Other complications reported are wound hematoma, dehiscence, nonunion of TTO, stress fracture of the tibia, and skin necrosis.

Similar surgical techniques have been described, although few case series are encountered. Matsushita et al reported 2 cases of HPD with good functional outcomes and stated that lateral soft tissue release is an essential part of the surgical approach of HPD in adults. 5 Meanwhile, Song et al 7 described a surgical approach using a TT proximalization, with good functional outcomes (Kujala score of 94) on a relatively large series of 13 cases. Other techniques were described, with others being more invasive, or using >1 incision. 8

Conclusion

HPD of the adult is a rare entity, in which a meticulous surgical procedure on a step-by-step basis should be performed. Anatomic reconstruction of the MPFL and management of the soft-tissue structures are key to allowing safe early ROM. Although different surgical techniques with various approaches have been described, larger series are needed.
