Plasma ceramide ratios define metabolic–inflammatory associations with cognitive impairment in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Nuan Wang, Guoqing Zhang, Chang Li, Jinghua Chen, Qingyun Li, Congxin Sun, Liang Ma, Peng Hu, Lishun Xiao, Qingwei Lai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCognitive impairment in Parkinson’s disease (PD) remains difficult to stratify using accessible fluid biomarkers, and the metabolic–inflammatory relationships associated with cognitive decline remain incompletely quantified. Here, we examined the relationships among plasma ceramides, inflammatory cytokines, neurodegeneration biomarkers, and cognitive function in PD patients with varying degrees of cognitive impairment, patients with multiple system atrophy (MSA), and healthy controls. MSA was distinguished from PD by higher Cer 18:0/Cer 24:0 and Cer 24:1/Cer 24:0 ratios, despite overlapping cytokine and neurofilament light chain (NFL) profiles. Within PD, worsening cognitive status was accompanied by graded increases in specific ceramide ratios, inflammatory cytokines, and NFL. Cer 24:1/Cer 24:0 was independently associated with cognitive performance (B = − 2.01, P = 0.003). Structural equation modeling identified two association pathways between ceramide-related measures and cognition: a larger direct association (standardized β = −0.223; 60.8% of the total effect) and a smaller inflammation-related indirect association (standardized β = −0.133; 36.2%), whereas pathways involving NFL were not significant. These findings establish a ceramide-centered quantitative framework integrating metabolic and inflammatory dimensions of cognitive impairment in PD, supporting stratified biomarker development.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Immunology, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a leading cause of neurodegenerative disability worldwide. Although traditionally defined by motor symptoms, non-motor features—particularly cognitive impairment—increasingly dominate the clinical picture and represent a major determinant of institutionalization and reduced quality of life1. Cognitive deficits progress on a continuum from mild cognitive impairment to dementia, affecting up to 80% of patients over the disease course. However, these deficits are typically detected only after irreversible neurodegeneration has occurred, limiting therapeutic opportunities2. This diagnostic lag, together with marked heterogeneity in cognitive trajectories, underscores the urgent need for accessible fluid biomarkers that reflect underlying molecular pathophysiology at early stages.

Although α-synuclein aggregation remains the pathological hallmark of PD, emerging lipidomics has repositioned sphingolipid metabolism from a peripheral metabolic event to a central pathogenic driver3–5. Ceramides, the bioactive core of sphingolipid metabolism, regulate neuronal membrane integrity, synaptic function, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial homeostasis6–9. Genetic evidence strongly supports this link: GBA1 mutations, which impair lysosomal ceramide and glucosylceramide clearance, constitute the most common genetic risk factor for PD and strongly predict accelerated cognitive decline10,11. Nevertheless, the specific plasma ceramide signatures associated with graded severity of cognitive impairment in PD remain incompletely defined.

Ceramide accumulation does not act in isolation but intersects with neuroinflammatory processes12–14. Intracellular ceramides function as damage-associated molecular patterns that activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, promoting IL-1β release and establishing a self-amplifying lipid–immune axis15. This interaction may compromise blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity and facilitate α-synuclein propagation16,17. Consistent with this mechanism, elevated circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, have been associated with cognitive deficits in PD, potentially reflecting bidirectional communication between peripheral immunity and central neurodegeneration18–20.

Despite these advances, the relationships among circulating ceramide profiles, systemic inflammation, neurodegeneration biomarkers, and cognitive impairment severity in PD remain incompletely defined. In particular, whether distinct ceramide species or ratios show differential associations with inflammatory mediators and neurodegeneration biomarkers, such as NFL, and how these metabolic and inflammatory dimensions relate quantitatively to cognitive performance remain unclear.

To address this gap, we integrated plasma ceramide profiling, inflammatory cytokine measurements, and neurodegeneration biomarkers in cohorts of PD, MSA, and healthy controls. By stratifying PD participants according to cognitive impairment severity and applying structural equation modeling, we examined direct and indirect associations among ceramide-related measures, inflammatory mediators, and cognitive performance. This framework provides a data-driven characterization of metabolic–inflammatory associations in PD-related cognitive impairment and identifies candidate fluid biomarkers for future biomarker-based stratification studies.

Results

Comparative clinical and biomarker profiles across healthy controls, Parkinson’s disease, and multiple system atrophy

Demographic and clinical characteristics of healthy controls (HC), PD, MSA-P, and MSA-C participants are summarized in Table 1. Sex distribution, body mass index (BMI), and educational attainment did not differ significantly among groups. Global cognition, assessed by the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), was comparable across PD, MSA-P, and MSA-C groups. In contrast, significant among-group differences were observed for age, disease duration, levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD), motor severity (MDS-UPDRS Part III), and Hoehn-Yahr stage (H-Y stage).

Cer 24:0 was significantly elevated only in PD (Fig. 1D). Importantly, MSA was further distinguished by significantly higher Cer 18:0/Cer 24:0 and Cer 24:1/Cer 24:0 ratios compared with both PD and HC (Fig. 1F, G).

By contrast, cytokine and neurodegenerative biomarkers showed a largely overlapping pattern between PD and MSA. Except for IL-2, circulating cytokine levels were significantly increased in both disease groups relative to HC (Fig. 1H–R). Similarly, plasma glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and NFL concentrations were significantly elevated in PD and MSA compared with controls (Fig. 1S, T). No statistically significant differences were observed between PD and MSA in cytokines, GFAP, or NFL, and these markers, along with ceramides, were also comparable between MSA-P and MSA-C (Table S1). Collectively, these findings indicate that disease-specific alterations in ceramide metabolism—particularly elevated Cer 18:0/Cer 24:0 and Cer 24:1/Cer 24:0 ratios in MSA—may provide greater discriminatory potential for distinguishing PD from MSA.

Ceramide, cytokine, and neurodegeneration biomarker levels according to cognitive impairment severity in Parkinson’s disease

Clinical characteristics stratified by cognitive status are presented in Table 3. Patients with Parkinson’s disease and normal cognition (PD-NC) and those with cognitive decline (PD-CD) showed no significant differences in BMI, educational attainment, or disease duration. In contrast, the PD-CD group had significantly higher MDS-UPDRS III scores, more advanced H-Y stage, higher LEDD, and elevated RBDSQ scores. Ceramide profiles differed markedly between the two groups (Fig. 2A–E; Table 4). Compared with the PD-NC group, the PD-CD group showed significantly higher levels of Cer 24:1, lower Cer 24:0, and higher Cer 16:0/Cer 24:0, Cer 18:0/Cer 24:0, and Cer 24:1/Cer 24:0 ratios. In parallel, multiple cytokines were significantly elevated in PD-CD relative to PD-NC (Fig. 2F–Q; Table 4). Plasma GFAP and NFL concentrations were also significantly higher in the PD-CD group (Fig. 2R, S; Table 4).

Further stratification into PD-NC, PD with mild cognitive decline (PD-MCD), and PD with severe cognitive decline (PD-SCD) revealed graded changes across clinical and biomarker measures (Tables 3 and 4). Disease duration, education attainment, and BMI were comparable across groups, whereas PD-SCD was characterized by greater clinical burden, including higher MDS-UPDRS part III scores, more advanced H-Y stage, and higher RBDSQ scores. Age and age at onset were higher in PD-SCD than in PD-NC, and the PD-SCD group also included a greater proportion of female patients.

Ceramide levels showed a clear severity-dependent pattern (Fig. 3A–F; Table 4). Compared with PD-NC, PD-SCD patients had higher Cer 16:0 and Cer 24:1 and lower Cer 24:0. The ratios Cer 16:0/Cer 24:0, Cer 18:0/Cer 24:0, and Cer 24:1/Cer 24:0 were significantly elevated in PD-SCD relative to both PD-NC and PD-MCD. Notably, these ratios distinguished PD-SCD from PD-MCD, whereas differences in individual ceramide species were less consistent. Cytokine concentrations increased in a stepwise manner across cognitive strata (Fig. 3G–R; Table 4). PD-MCD patients already exhibited significant increases in IL-1β, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, and IL-12p70 relative to PD-NC, whereas PD-SCD patients showed broader cytokine elevations. Moreover, IL-1β, IL-5, IL-6, IL-17, IFN-α, IFN-γ, and TNF-α were significantly higher in PD-SCD than in PD-MCD. GFAP and NFL concentrations were significantly higher in PD-SCD than in PD-NC (Fig. 3S, T; Table 4). Notably, NFL levels were further increased in PD-SCD compared with PD-MCD. All biomarker differences remained significant after Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. These results demonstrate graded increases in specific ceramide ratios, multiple cytokines, GFAP, and NFL concentrations with increasing severity of cognitive impairment in Parkinson’s disease.

Ceramide species and ratios exhibited multiple significant associations with clinical phenotypes. Cer 16:0 and Cer 24:1 were positively correlated with RBDSQ score. Cer 18:0, Cer 24:1, and the ratios Cer 18:0/Cer 24:0 and Cer 24:1/Cer 24:0 were positively correlated with both MDS-UPDRS III score and H-Y stage. Inflammatory markers displayed distinct associations with clinical measures. RBDSQ score was positively correlated with multiple cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-5, and TNF-α (all r > 0.24, FDR-adjusted P < 0.001), as well as with IFN-γ, IL-2, and IL-10. IL-6 was positively correlated with both H-Y stage (r = 0.135, FDR-adjusted P < 0.05) and MDS-UPDRS III score (r = 0.137, FDR-adjusted P < 0.05).

The strongest associations overall were observed with cognitive performance. MoCA score was inversely correlated with Cer 16:0 and Cer 24:1, with the strongest ceramide association found for the Cer 24:1/Cer 24:0 ratio (r = −0.297, FDR-adjusted P < 0.001). Multiple cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-5, IL-6, and TNF-α, were negatively correlated with MoCA score (r ranging from −0.352 to −0.417, all FDR-adjusted P < 0.001). MoCA score was also inversely correlated with NFL (r = −0.164, FDR-adjusted P < 0.01) and GFAP (r = −0.140, FDR-adjusted P < 0.05).

Correlation analyses further revealed robust chain-length-dependent relationships between ceramides and both inflammatory cytokines and neurodegeneration markers. Cer 16:0 and the Cer 16:0/Cer 24:0 ratio exhibited the strongest positive correlations with multiple inflammatory cytokines, particularly IL-1β (r = 0.252, FDR-adjusted P < 0.001) and TNF-α (r = 0.211, FDR-adjusted P < 0.001). Of particular note, Cer 16:0 showed a strong positive correlation with NFL (r = 0.444, FDR-adjusted P < 0.001), as did the Cer 16:0/Cer 24:0 ratio (r = 0.277, FDR-adjusted P < 0.001). In contrast, Cer 18:0 correlated primarily with IFN-γ, while Cer 24:1 was positively correlated with IL-1β, IFN-γ, and IL-5. Weaker associations were observed between ceramide species and GFAP.

Identification of independent predictors for cognitive performance

To identify variables independently associated with cognitive performance, candidate predictors—including clinical characteristics, ceramide species, inflammatory cytokines, and neurodegenerative biomarkers—were first screened using LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation. The optimal penalty parameters were determined using both the minimum mean cross-validated error (λmin) and the minimum mean cross-validated error plus one standard error (λ1se). Educational duration was forced into the model. Under λmin, the selected variables included age, sex, H–Y stage, LEDD, RBDSQ score, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-5, TNF-α, Cer 16:0/Cer 24:0, and Cer 24:1/Cer 24:0. The more parsimonious λ1se model retained the same variables except for LEDD. Variables selected by either λmin or λ1se were subsequently entered into separate multivariable linear regression models with MoCA score as the dependent variable (Fig. 5 and Fig. S1). Multicollinearity was low in all models (all variance inflation factors <2.5).

Discussion

In the present study, we systematically characterized the relationships among ceramide metabolism, systemic inflammation, neurodegeneration biomarkers, and cognitive performance in PD, with comparative analyses in MSA. By integrating plasma ceramide profiling, cytokine assays, neurodegeneration biomarkers, and structural equation modeling, we identified three convergent findings. First, specific long-chain ceramide ratios, particularly Cer 18:0/Cer 24:0 and Cer 24:1/Cer 24:0, distinguished MSA from PD, whereas NFL, GFAP, and cytokine profiles were largely overlapping, suggesting disease-associated metabolic variation not captured by these inflammatory or neurodegeneration biomarkers. Second, structural equation modeling linked ceramide-related measures to cognition through a larger direct association (standardized β = −0.223; 60.8% of the modeled total effect) and a smaller inflammation-related indirect association (standardized β = −0.133; 36.2%), whereas NFL-related paths were not significant. Third, Cer 24:1/Cer 24:0 was independently associated with poorer cognitive performance after multivariable adjustment (B = − 2.01, P = 0.003). Together, these findings support a ceramide-centered quantitative framework that integrates metabolic and inflammatory dimensions of cognitive impairment in PD and explains 37.1% of MoCA variance within the modeled framework.

Initially, regarding the diagnostic distinction between synucleinopathies, a critical challenge lies in differentiating PD from MSA, particularly in early stages where clinical phenotypes may overlap. Although circulating cytokines were broadly elevated in both PD and MSA, their largely overlapping profiles suggest shared inflammatory activation across these synucleinopathies21. In contrast, ceramide profiling revealed disease-associated metabolic divergence. MSA patients displayed a disproportionately severe perturbation in C18 and C24:1 ceramide species compared to PD. In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed between PD and MSA in plasma NFL or GFAP levels. This lack of discrimination may reflect that both NFL and GFAP primarily index neuroaxonal injury and astroglial activation, respectively, which are shared downstream features of neurodegeneration rather than disease-specific processes. This specific metabolic signature likely reflects distinct pathophysiological substrates22–24. MSA is characterized by oligodendroglial α-synuclein cytoplasmic inclusions (GCIs) and extensive demyelination, whereas PD involves neuronal Lewy bodies. Since very-long-chain ceramides (VLC-Cers) are essential components of myelin sheaths and are regulated by Ceramide Synthase 2 (CerS2) in oligodendrocytes25, the elevation of Cer 24:1/Cer 24:0 in MSA may reflect myelin-related alterations. Collectively, these findings suggest that while inflammation and canonical neurodegeneration markers reflect shared pathological processes across synucleinopathies, plasma ceramide profiles may provide additional disease-relevant information for differentiating PD and MSA.

Within PD, the PD-SCD subgroup exhibited a convergent elevation of ceramide species (Cer 16:0, Cer 24:1, and Cer 24:1/Cer 24:0 ratio), broad inflammatory cytokines, and neurodegeneration markers (NFL, GFAP) relative to PD-NC, indicating that metabolic, inflammatory, and neurodegenerative processes co-escalate at the stage of severe cognitive decline. Importantly, all three biological axes—ceramides, cytokines, and NFL/GFAP—were inversely correlated with MoCA scores, with the Cer 24:1/Cer 24:0 ratio showing the strongest lipid-cognition association (r = −0.297), and TNF-α showing the strongest immune-cognition association (r = −0.417). This tripartite convergence suggests that cognitive impairment in PD is not driven by a single pathological domain but is associated with a coordinated metabolic–inflammatory–neurodegenerative signature, reinforcing the rationale for multi-modal biomarker panels over single-analyte approaches in monitoring PD-related cognitive decline.

Correlation analyses further revealed acyl chain-length-dependent associations between ceramides and inflammatory markers. Shorter-chain Cer 16:0 and the Cer 16:0/Cer 24:0 ratio were associated with multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β (r = 0.252) and TNF-α (r = 0.211), whereas longer-chain Cer 18:0 and Cer 24:1 showed preferential associations with IFN-γ. These differential patterns suggest that ceramide species may engage distinct inflammatory pathways depending on acyl chain length, consistent with experimental evidence linking C16-ceramide to inflammasome-related signaling15,26. The notably strong correlation between Cer 16:0 and NFL further implies that shorter-chain ceramides may be particularly relevant to neuroaxonal injury. Collectively, these findings position ceramide chain-length profiling not merely as a metabolic readout, but as a structured framework linking lipid biology, immune activation, and neurodegeneration within the spectrum of cognitive impairment in PD.

Structural equation modeling provided quantitative insight into the modeled associations among ceramide-related measures, systemic inflammation, and cognitive performance in PD. The model showed good fit (χ²/df = 2.069; CFI = 0.977; RMSEA = 0.051), with BCa bootstrap inference used to address non-normality. The total association between ceramide-related measures and MoCA score comprised a larger direct association (standardized β = −0.223, P = 0.012; 60.8% of the modeled total effect) and a smaller inflammation-related indirect association (standardized β = −0.133, P = 0.009; 36.2%). This two-component association pattern is consistent with experimental evidence implicating ceramides in mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and α-synuclein aggregation27–31, and with evidence linking systemic inflammation to poorer cognitive performance32–34. Importantly, the absence of a significant NFL-related indirect path (all P > 0.46) further refines this framework, suggesting that the modeled associations between ceramide-related measures and cognition were more closely aligned with metabolic–inflammatory processes than with NFL-indexed neuroaxonal injury in this cohort. Together, the model explained 37.1% of the variance in MoCA score and, by partitioning the modeled association, supports further evaluation of ceramide metabolism and inflammatory signaling as complementary dimensions of cognitive impairment in PD35.

These biological insights hold significant translational value. The identification of the Cer 24:1/Cer 24:0 ratio as a potent independent factor (B = − 2.01, P = 0.003) in our multivariable model indicates its robust association with cognitive performance independent of age and H–Y stage, supporting its potential utility for risk stratification. If validated prospectively, integrating this ratio into clinical assessment may improve early identification of individuals at higher risk of cognitive decline. Additionally, the observed association between RBD-related symptoms (RBDSQ score) and metabolic–inflammatory markers aligns with emerging evidence linking sleep disturbances, particularly RBD-related pathology, to impaired clearance of neurotoxic metabolites and heightened inflammatory burden36,37. These findings suggest that sleep-related pathology may represent a clinically relevant pathway influencing metabolic–inflammatory dysregulation in PD. Collectively, the integration of a specific ceramide ratio with systemic inflammatory and sleep-related features highlights a metabolism-centered and clinically actionable framework for cognitive vulnerability in PD.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes definitive causal inferences regarding the temporal sequence of lipid accumulation and cognitive decline. Longitudinal studies are required to establish temporal directionality and validate the observed effect partitioning. Second, plasma biomarkers were used as a proxy for central pathology. Although blood-brain barrier permeability is increased in PD38, correlating these findings with CSF or PET imaging would strengthen their central nervous system relevance. Relatedly, dedicated biomarkers of BBB integrity—such as the CSF/plasma albumin ratio or plasma tight junction proteins (occludin or claudin-5)—were not assessed. Incorporating such measures in future studies would clarify whether BBB compromise is involved in linking peripheral ceramide dysregulation with central neuroinflammatory processes. Third, despite adjustment for major covariates, residual confounding from dietary lipid intake, metabolic comorbidities (diabetes or dyslipidemia), gut microbiota composition, and concomitant pharmacotherapies cannot be excluded, each of which may modulate plasma ceramide profiles. Fourth, cognitive status was assessed solely using the MoCA, and patients were stratified into three groups based on MoCA score thresholds. While practical for capturing graded cognitive performance within the cohort, this screening-based categorization does not constitute a formal diagnosis of mild cognitive impairment or Parkinson’s disease dementia. Moreover, the MoCA may lack sensitivity to subtle domain-specific deficits and can be disproportionately influenced by motor disability—particularly in patients with advanced disease or MSA—potentially introducing measurement bias in the cognitive outcome variable. Notwithstanding these constraints, the present findings establish a quantitatively defined, metabolically anchored framework for cognitive vulnerability in PD, in which ceramide imbalance integrates with systemic inflammation to explain a substantial proportion of cognitive variance. Future longitudinal, multimodal studies incorporating neuroimaging, BBB integrity markers, and refined cognitive phenotyping will be critical to validate the causal architecture and translational utility of this ceramide-centered model.

In summary, this study demonstrates a coordinated relationship between ceramide dysregulation, systemic inflammation, neurodegeneration markers, and cognitive impairment in PD. Structural equation modeling further supported a two-component association pattern between ceramide-related measures and cognitive performance, with a larger direct association (60.8% of the modeled total effect) and a smaller inflammation-related indirect association (36.2%) together explaining 37.1% of MoCA variance. The Cer 24:1/Cer 24:0 ratio independently correlated with cognitive performance (B = − 2.01, P = 0.003) and differentiated MSA from PD where conventional neurodegeneration markers did not, highlighting its relevance as a metabolically informed indicator requiring prospective validation. These findings collectively support a ceramide-centered, data-driven framework that integrates metabolic and inflammatory dimensions of cognitive vulnerability in PD.

Methods

Study cohort

This study enrolled 408 patients with PD and 98 patients with MSA who were hospitalized in the Department of Neurology at Xuzhou Medical University Affiliated Hospital between November 2021 and June 2024. Additionally, 220 age- and sex-matched healthy controls were recruited during the same period. The diagnosis of PD was established according to the diagnostic criteria defined by the Movement Disorder Society (MDS)39. The diagnosis of MSA was made according to established diagnostic guidelines40, and patients were subclassified as MSA-P (n = 53) or MSA-C (n = 45) based on predominant motor phenotype. The exclusion criteria for PD patients were as follows: (i) secondary or atypical parkinsonism; (ii) severe dysfunction of vital organs including the heart, lungs, brain, or kidneys; (iii) dementia attributable to etiologies other than PD, such as Lewy body dementia or vascular dementia; (iv) active infection within the preceding 3 months; (v) concurrent malignancy; (vi) current use of statins or fibrates; (vii) history of substance abuse, alcohol dependence, or psychiatric disorders including schizophrenia within the past 12 months. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Medical University Affiliated Hospital (Ethics approval number: XYFY2025-KL664-01). The conduct of this study adhered to the fundamental principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Clinical data and neurological assessment

Demographic data were collected from all participants, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), educational level, disease duration, and levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD). Motor symptoms in PD patients were quantitatively assessed using the Movement Disorder Society-sponsored Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale Part III (MDS-UPDRS III) in the “off” state (≥12 h after discontinuing antiparkinsonian medications), and disease severity was evaluated using the Hoehn-Yahr staging system. Global cognitive assessments using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) were performed on the same day within 2–3 h of blood collection while patients were in the “on” medication state. Cognitive function was assessed using MoCA with education correction (+1 point for ≤12 years). Patients were stratified into three groups based on MoCA scores: PD-normal cognition (PD-NC, MoCA ≥26), PD-mild cognitive decline (PD-MCD, MoCA 21–25), and PD-severe cognitive decline (PD-SCD, MoCA ≤20)41. This screening-based categorization was used to capture graded cognitive status within the cohort and should not be considered a formal diagnosis of mild cognitive impairment or dementia according to Movement Disorder Society criteria. Sleep disorders were screened using the Rapid Eye Movement Sleep Behavior Disorder Screening Questionnaire (RBDSQ) (total score range 0–13, with a score ≥5 suggestive of probable RBD).

Determination of plasma ceramides

Plasma ceramide levels were determined using high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS)42. Fasting venous blood samples were collected from all participants in the early morning. Blood was drawn into tubes containing potassium ethylenediaminetetraacetate (EDTA-K₂) as anticoagulant and immediately transferred to a tabletop centrifuge (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for centrifugation at 4 °C and 3000 rpm for 10 min. Plasma was separated and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Plasma sample pre-treatment was performed using liquid-liquid extraction. Briefly, 100 μL of plasma sample was accurately pipetted and mixed with 100 μL of internal standard working solution and 400 μL of ethyl acetate/isopropanol (1:4, v/v). The mixture was vortexed for 5 minutes and then centrifuged at 4 °C and 11,000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was centrifuged again under identical conditions, and 100 μL of the supernatant was used for HPLC-MS/MS analysis.

HPLC-MS/MS analysis was performed using a Waters TQD triple quadrupole mass spectrometer equipped with a positive electrospray ionization source (ESI + ). Chromatographic separation was achieved using an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C₁₈ reversed-phase column (2.1 mm × 50 mm × 1.7 μm). Mobile phase A consisted of ultrapure water containing 1% formic acid and 5 mmol/L ammonium acetate, and mobile phase B consisted of a mixture of isopropanol and acetonitrile (4:3, v/v) containing 5 mmol/L ammonium acetate. The injection volume was 15 μL with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. Quantitative analysis of target analytes was performed using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) technology. The monitored ceramide ion pairs included palmitic acid ceramide (Cer 16:0), oleic acid ceramide (Cer 18:0), ceramide C24:0 (Cer 24:0), and ceramide C24:1 (Cer 24:1), which were calibrated using corresponding reference standards. Standard curves were established for quantitative calculations. Quality control samples were included in each batch of analysis, with recovery rates required to be within the range of 80%-120%. The relative standard deviations (RSD) for intra-batch and inter-batch precision were ≤12% and ≤15%, respectively.

Detection of cytokines with a cytometric bead array (CBA)

Circulating cytokine levels were determined using a cytometric bead array (CBA) technique according to the manufacturer’s standard operating procedures (Ruisike Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Qingdao, China) as described previously by our research team43. EDTA-anticoagulated blood samples (5 mL) were centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 minutes to separate plasma, which was then stored at −80 °C until analysis. For detection, 25 μL each of buffer, Matrix B, plasma sample, and standard were accurately pipetted into respective sample tubes and calibration tubes. Subsequently, 25 μL of capture bead mixture and 25 μL of detection antibody mixture were added to each tube and incubated at room temperature in the dark at 400 rpm for 2 h. Thereafter, 25 μL of streptavidin-phycoerythrin (SA-PE) conjugate was added, and the samples were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 0.5 h. Following the addition of 500 μL of 1× wash buffer and thorough mixing, the samples were centrifuged at 400 g for 5 minutes. The centrifuged samples were then processed according to the requirements of the flow cytometer and immediately analyzed. All samples were analyzed in duplicate using a multiplex immunoassay platform (QuickPlex™ SQ120; Meso Scale Discovery, Rockville, MD, USA). The detected cytokines included IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-17, IFN-α, IFN-γ, and TNF-α. The combined coefficient of variation (CV) for the multiplex immunoassay was 8.25%, which complied with the quality standards for bioanalytical methods.

Measurement of plasma NFL and GFAP

Fasting venous blood was collected at baseline into EDTA-anticoagulated tubes. Plasma was isolated by centrifugation (2000 × g, 10 min, room temperature), aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C within 2 h of collection to minimize pre-analytical variability. Plasma neurofilament light chain (NFL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) concentrations were quantified using a fully automated chemiluminescence immunoassay platform (Elf S240, Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s validated protocol44. All samples were processed in analytical run under standardized conditions to reduce batch effects. Measurements were performed in duplicate, and laboratory personnel were blinded to clinical and demographic information. Internal quality controls were included to monitor assay performance, ensuring high reproducibility and analytical reliability.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0, AMOS 31.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and R software (version 4.4.1). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or median (P25, P75) for non-normal data, and categorical variables as counts and percentages (n [%]). Between-group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t test or one-way ANOVA for normally distributed variables, and Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact test.

To preserve biological interpretability while controlling Type I error, biomarkers were organized a priori into three families: ceramides, cytokines, and neurodegeneration markers. Between-group differences were tested using Mann–Whitney U (two-group) or Kruskal–Wallis (multi-group) tests. Where global significance was detected, post hoc pairwise Mann–Whitney U tests were performed. Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction was applied within each biomarker family across all comparisons, with FDR-adjusted P < 0.05 denoting significance.

Spearman rank correlations (two-sided) were computed among ceramides, cytokines, neurobiological markers, and clinical variables, with FDR correction applied across the full correlation matrix. To identify independent predictors of cognitive performance (MoCA), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was performed using the glmnet package in R. All candidate predictors were standardized prior to fitting. The penalty parameter λ was determined using 10-fold cross-validation, selecting both the value that minimized the mean cross-validated error (λmin) and the most parsimonious value within one standard error of the minimum (λ1se). Variables retaining non-zero coefficients were entered into multivariable linear regression, with educational duration included as a forced covariate given its established influence on cognitive reserve. Results are reported as unstandardized coefficients (B) with 95% confidence intervals. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF), with VIF < 2.5 confirming acceptable collinearity levels.

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was performed in AMOS to examine associations among ceramide-related measures, inflammatory markers, neuroaxonal injury (NFL), and cognitive performance. A latent Ceramide construct was indicated by Cer 16:0/Cer 24:0 and Cer 24:1/Cer 24:0; a latent Cytokines construct by IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. NFL and MoCA were modeled as observed endogenous variables. The structural model specified directed paths from Ceramide → Cytokines, Ceramide → NFL, Ceramide → MoCA, Cytokines → NFL, Cytokines → MoCA, and NFL → MoCA, thereby testing both direct and sequential mediation. Given the non-normal distribution of several biomarkers, bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap with 5000 resamples was applied to derive 95% confidence intervals, and significance of direct, indirect, and total effects, and the proportion of mediated effects was calculated. Model fit was evaluated using χ²/df, comparative fit index (CFI), goodness-of-fit index (GFI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA).
