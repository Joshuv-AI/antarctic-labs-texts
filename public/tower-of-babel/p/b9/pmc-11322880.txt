Multicenter validation study of a treatment selection MAP for pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors
Authors: Masafumi Ikeda, Susumu Hijioka, Tetsuhide Ito, Shigemi Matsumoto, Yoshitaka Honma, Makoto Ueno, Naohiro Okano, Taku Aoki, Junji Furuse
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSomatostatin analogs, molecular-targeted agents and cytotoxic anticancer agents are available as therapeutic agents for the systemic treatment of pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors, and we have developed a first-line treatment selection MAP to enable selection of the optimal treatment strategy for pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors. The purpose of this study was to validate the usefulness of the treatment selection MAP.MethodsPatients who had received systemic therapy for a pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor between January 2017 and December 2020 were compared according to whether they had been treated as recommended by the MAP (matched patients) or not (unmatched patients) to determine whether better outcomes were achieved by the matched patients. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival of the matched group and unmatched groups in the somatostatin analog, molecular-targeted agent and cytotoxic anticancer agents areas of the MAP.ResultsThere were 41 (55%) MAP-matched patients in all areas among the 74 patients registered at seven hospitals. The MAP-matched rates were 100, 77 and 38% in the somatostatin analog area, molecular-targeted agent area and cytotoxic anticancer agents area, respectively. All of the unmatched patients had been selected for less intensive treatment. The median progression-free survival in the matched group and unmatched group in the molecular-targeted agent area of the MAP were 46.6 and 15.4 months, respectively, and a multivariate analysis identified MAP-matched (hazard ratio 0.18 [95% confidence interval: 0.04–0.87], P = 0.032) as the only significant independent favorable predictive factor.ConclusionThe usefulness of the MAP for treatment selection was validated in the molecular-targeted agent area of the MAP.Keywords: treatment selection MAP, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor, somatostatin analog, molecular-targeted agent, cytotoxic agent

Background

Although surgical resection is the only curative treatment for pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (PanNETs), systemic therapy is the mainstay of treatment for unresectable PanNETs (1) and includes treatment with somatostatin analogs (SSAs) such as octreotide and lanreotide (2,3), molecular-targeted agents (MTAs) such as everolimus (4,5) and sunitinib (6,7) and cytotoxic anticancer agents (CTx) such as streptozocin (8–10) and temozolomide (11–13). However, since no general strategy for selecting the optimal first-line treatment had been established for unresectable PanNETs, Japanese experts on PanNET held a series of discussions and created a first-line treatment selection MAP for unresectable PanNETs based on tumor burden and tumor aggressiveness (14) (Fig. 1). Tumor burden and tumor aggressiveness are considered important factors in selecting the initial treatment for unresectable PanNETs. The MAP was constructed by using tumor volume of liver metastasis as the index of tumor burden on the vertical axis of the MAP and the Ki-67 labeling index as the index of tumor aggressiveness on the horizontal axis. The areas of the MAP in which SSAs, MTAs and CTx are recommended as a first-line systemic therapy are shown in green, yellow and red, respectively. Since the treatment selection boundaries between the SSA area and MTA area and between the MTA area and CTx area were unclear, a color gradient was used on the MAP to represent as a yellow–green area and orange area, respectively. If a patient was plotted in a gradient area, the selection of either agent for treatment was appropriate. The treatment selection MAP was very helpful in selecting first-line treatment options for PanNET patients even when physicians were not familiar with the systemic therapy of PanNET. However, since the usefulness of the MAP had not been fully validated, this study was designed to validate the usefulness of the MAP as a means of selecting first-line systemic therapy for unresectable PanNETs.

Methods

Study design

This was a multicenter, retrospective cohort study conducted at seven leading Japanese hospitals. The progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) of MAP-matched patients and MAP-unmatched patients were compared to determine whether the matched patients had a better outcome. The primary endpoint was the PFS of patients plotted in the SSA area, MTA area and CTx area of the MAP, and the secondary endpoint was the OS of patients plotted in the SSA area, MTA area and CTx area of the MAP.

This study was conducted with the approval of the Institutional Review Board of the National Cancer Center (#2021-440) and in accordance with the principles laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki and the Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects. This was a non-invasive and non-interventional study and all data used in this study were obtained from past medical records. In accordance with the ethical guidelines, both the institutions providing the data and the institutions receiving the data disclosed the implementation of this research on bulletin boards or easily accessible websites, etc., and gave the patients the opportunity to refuse the use of data.

Eligible patients

The main inclusion criteria for patient registration in this study were (i) age 20 years or older, (ii) clinically and radiologically diagnosed unresectable PanNET, (iii) histologically diagnosed NETG1, G2 or G3 (15), (iv) presence of liver metastasis, (v) first-line systemic therapy was started between January 2017 and December 2020. The exclusion criterion was a diagnosis of neuroendocrine carcinoma.

Study period and data collection

The study period was from 1 January 2017 to 31 December 2020, and the last follow-up examination was performed on 30 June 2022. We chose 1 January 2017, when the MAP was first proposed, as the starting date for data collection, and 31 December 2020, as the final date in view of the follow-up period.

The following data were reviewed in the medical records:

Patient background (at the start of treatment): Gender, age, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG-PS), history of resection for PanNET, presence of multiple endocrine neoplasia (MEN) Type I complications.

Tumor factors: Metastatic organ, tumor volume (liver metastasis), WHO classification (G1, G2, G3) (15), Ki-67 labeling index, presence of somatostatin receptor (SSTR) expression (evaluation method: somatostatin receptor scintigraphy or immunohistochemistry staining for SSTR).

Statistical analysis

In the treatment selection MAP, patients who started treatment with an SSA in the green area, patients who started treatment with an SSA or MTA in the yellow–green area, patients who started treatment with MTA in the yellow area, patients who started treatment with an MTA or CTx in the orange area and patients who started treatment with a CTx in the red area were considered to be MAP-matched, and the other patients were considered to be MAP-unmatched. Treatments with MTA or CTx in combination with SSA were considered as MTA or CTx-containing treatments, and were considered to be MAP-matched with yellow–green area/yellow area for MTA-containing treatment and orange/red area for CTx-containing treatment. Tumor volume of liver metastases was defined as the proportion of tumor of liver metastases to the entire liver.

PFS was defined as the time from initiation of treatment until disease progression, death from any cause or the last follow up. OS was defined as the time from initiation of treatment until death from any cause or the last follow-up. PFS and OS of the MAP-matched patients and MAP-unmatched patients in the SSA area (green), MTA area (yellow) and CTx area (red) were calculated by the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between two groups were tested by the log-rank test. Multivariate analysis using a COX regression model was also performed in each area to identify factors that contributed to prolonging PFS. The statistical analysis was restricted to the areas outside the gradients, i.e. to the green area, yellow area and red area, because the data of patients located in the gradient areas, i.e. yellow–green area and orange area, would be impossible to analyze due to the multiple agent options available.

Objective response rate, PFS and OS of all eligible patients

The objective response rate and disease control rate of all 74 patients were 15 and 81%, and median PFS and OS of all 74 patients were 22.9 and 47.3 months, respectively (Supplemental Fig. 1). Objective response rate, disease control rate, PFS and OS according to treatment are shown in Supplemental Fig. 1.

MAP-matched status

The MAP-matched status of all 74 PanNET patients is plotted in Fig. 1. The overall MAP-matched rate was 55% (41 of 74 subjects). There were 11 patients in the SSA area (green + yellow–green), all of whom were matched, for a matched rate of 100%. There were 43 patients in the MTA area (yellow green + yellow + orange), and 33 of them were matched, for a matched rate of 76.7%. The 10 unmatched patients in the MTA area were treated with SSA. There were 39 patients in the CTx area (orange + red), and 15 of them were matched, for a matched rate of 38.4%. The 24 unmatched patients in the CTx area were treated with SSA (7 patients), MTA (9 patients) or SSA + MTA (8 patients). Table 2 shows the MAP-matched rates at each hospital: three hospitals had a 70% or more MAP-matched rate, and the other three hospitals had MAP-matched rates of 33% or less. With the exception of Hospital B, the MAP-matched rates were higher at the hospitals where the largest numbers of patients were registered.

MAP-matched group vs. MAP-unmatched group in the SSA area (green area only)

There was only one patient in the green area, and the patient was treated with SSA and was MAP-matched. The tumor response in this patient was assessed as stable disease, and PFS was 33.7 months, and he was receiving treatment with SSA at the end of follow-up period. The recommended treatment was selected in this patient.

MAP-matched group vs. MAP-unmatched group in the MTA area (yellow area only)

There were 24 patients in the yellow area, 15 (63%) of whom were MAP-matched, and 9 (38%) of whom were MAP-unmatched. All MAP-unmatched patients were treated with SSA. Median PFS was 46.6 months in the MAP-matched group and 15.4 months in the unmatched group, and the hazard ratio (HR) was 0.50 ([95% confidence interval (CI): 0.17–1.51], P = 0.213) (Fig. 2). A multivariate analysis was performed to identify independent predictive factors associated with favorable PFS (Table 3-1), and the only significant factor identified was MAP-matched (HR 0.18 [95% CI: 0.04–0.87], P = 0.032). Median OS was 47.0 months in the MAP-matched group and not reached in the MAP-unmatched group (HR 0.69 [95% CI: 0.14–3.42], P = 0.645). Based on this result, patients falling in the wide MTA area (yellow green + yellow + orange) were also analyzed. There were 43 patients, and 33 of them were matched and 10 were unmatched. The median PFS was 24.4 months in the matched group and 15.4 months in the unmatched group, being better in the matched group (HR: 0.61 [95% CI: 0.24–1.51], P = 0.277) (Supplemental Fig. 1).

Discussion

Although PanNET is a rare disease, and the number of patients is small, there are several treatment options for systemic therapy, including SSAs, such as lanreotide (2,3) and octreotide, MTAs, such as everolimus (4,5) and sunitinib (6,7), CTx, such as streptozocin (8–10) (1), and combination treatment with SSA + MTA and SSA + CTx. In order to facilitate the proper selection of these systemic therapies, we together with a group of Japanese experts on PanNET proposed a treatment selection MAP (14). Although the treatment selection MAP has a good reputation both in Japan and overseas as being easy to use, whether the treatment selected using this MAP really has a favorable therapeutic effect has never been validated. In order to determine whether the MAP is truly useful as a means of selecting treatment for PanNET patients with no prior systemic therapy in clinical practice, it was necessary to validate it in a large number of patients and identify any critical issues or needed modifications.

The overall MAP-matched rate was 55%, meaning that only about half of the patients were matched with the treatment selection MAP. The MAP-matched rate by area was 100% in the SSA area (green + yellow-green), 76.7% in the MTA area (yellow-green + yellow + orange) and 38.4% in the CTx area (orange + red). The MAP-matched rate tended to decrease as recommended treatment becomes more intensive. Less intensive treatments, such as SSA in the MTA area and MTA or MTA + SSA in the CTx area, were selected in the map-unmatched group as a whole, the finding that may reflect the preference of oncologists for treatments with fewer adverse effects and that allow easier management. The MAP-matched rates varied with the institutions, with three hospitals having MAP-matched rates of 70% or higher and the other three having MAP-matched rates of 33% or lower. With the exception of Hospital B, the MAP-matched rates were higher in hospitals where larger numbers of patients were registered, suggesting that there was a diversity between the hospitals.

The primary endpoint in this study was whether MAP-matched patients had a better PFS compared with MAP-unmatched patients in each of the SSA, MTA and CTx areas alone. In the SSA area, only one patient was included in the analysis and matched the treatment selection MAP. A favorable treatment outcome was obtained in that patient, but adequate evaluation was impossible because of there being only a single patient. In the MTA area, the treatment outcomes in the MAP-matched group were better than those in the unmatched group, and MAP-matched was the only independent factor that contributed to better PFS in the multivariate analysis. Even when the MTA area was enlarged to the wide MTA area (yellow-green and orange), the PFS was better in the matched group. No significant difference in OS was found between MAP-matched group and MAP-unmatched group, but this finding was consistent with no statistically significant differences in OS in any of the large phase III trials of everolimus (4) and sunitinib (16) in PanNET patients. The results of our analysis indicated that MAP-matched was most important factor in the MTA area to obtain favorable treatment efficacy.

However, the results in the CTx area were completely opposite. SSA, MTA alone and MTA + SSA were selected in a high proportion of patients (77%). PFS and OS in the MAP-matched group were worse than in the unmatched group, although the difference between the two groups was not statistically significant. Streptozocin is the only CTx that has been approved in Japan, and temozolomide has not yet been approved (1,17). The efficacy of streptozocin may have been unreliable and not widely selected, or the efficacy of streptozocin itself may have been insufficient. A Japanese phase I/II study of streptozocin (10) demonstrated an insufficient response rate of only 13.3% in PanNET subjects. By contrast, favorable objective response rates and PFS have been reported for temozolomide in combination with capecitabine (11–13). Although no randomized trials comparing streptozocin and temozolomide have been reported to date, temozolomide is expected to be highly effective. If temozolomide plus capecitabine becomes available, we would like to re-evaluate how the results of using the treatment selection MAP in the CTx area would change. We hope that temozolomide will be approved in Japan as soon as possible.

This study had several limitations. First, it was a retrospective cohort study, not a prospective study, so it is impossible to draw any clear conclusions. Second, only a small number of patients, 74 patients at seven hospitals, were registered in this study. The analyzes by SSA, MTA and CTx areas may not have shown significant differences due to the small number of patients. Third, there are two treatment options available for tumors in the marginal area, including those falling in the yellow green and orange area. However, considering clinical practice, it was not possible to limit the treatment to one treatment option. As more solid evidence becomes available, this field might become narrower. Fourth, peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) has recently been reported to have excellent efficacy as a treatment for PanNET (18) and has often been used in clinical practice. PRRT is now available as an alternative treatment when systemic therapy is ineffective against PanNET (19). However, a number of randomized controlled trials of various systemic therapies (NETTER2 (SSA vs. PRRT): NCT03972488, COMPETE (MTA vs. PRRT): NCT03049189, COMPOSE (CTx vs. PRRT): NCT04919226) are being conducted, and, depending on the results, in the future it might be necessary to revise a treatment selection MAP that also takes PRRT into account.

Conclusions

In order to validate the usefulness of a treatment selection MAP for PanNET, we retrospectively investigated the MAP-matched rate, but the results showed that it was only 55%, and that unmatched patients had been selected for milder treatment. The results demonstrated the usefulness of the MAP as a treatment strategy in the MTA area, but failed to validate it in selecting CTx in the CTx area. In the future, prospective validation of the usefulness of the MAP and its revision, if necessary, should be reconsidered in view of the possible future approval of treatment with temozolomide + capecitabine and the effectiveness of PRRT based on the results of pivotal phase III trials.
