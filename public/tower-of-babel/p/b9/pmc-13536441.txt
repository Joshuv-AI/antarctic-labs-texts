Comparative effects of plasma activation and monomer infiltration on the surface characteristics and wettability of 3D-printed permanent crown resin
Authors: Pyi Phyo Win, Szu-Yu Lai, Gannaran Narangerel, Tzu-Yu Peng, Johnson Hsin-Chung Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/purposeThe bonding performance of 3D-printed resins (3DRs) used for permanent crowns can be challenging due to their high filler content. This study evaluated the surface characteristics and wettability of non-thermal handheld plasma (NTHP) and resin-matrix penetrating primer (RMPP) treatments in comparison with conventional airborne-particle abrasion (APA) and universal adhesive (UA) protocols.Materials and methodsDisc-shaped 3DR specimens were allocated into five surface-treatment groups: no treatment (NT), APA/UA (AU), APA/RMPP (AP), NTHP/UA (PU), and NTHP/RMPP (PP). Surface roughness and morphology were assessed. Wettability was determined by contact angle (CA) measurements using distilled water and diiodomethane, and surface energy (SE) was calculated. Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test (α = 0.05).ResultsAPA significantly increased surface roughness (P < 0.001), whereas NTHP produced no significantly topographical alteration compared with the control (P > 0.05). SEM analysis revealed that UA formed a granular surface layer, whereas RMPP created a smoother and continuous film that reduced roughness. All treatments significantly increased SE (P < 0.001). The UA groups achieved the highest total SE due to enhanced hydrophilicity; however, all treated groups demonstrated similarly low CAs with diiodomethane, suggesting that both bonding agents provide sufficient dispersive energy for hydrophobic resin interaction.ConclusionAPA effectively modifies surface morphology, while NTHP activates the surface without altering its topography. Although UA yields the highest total SE, both UA and RMPP provide adequate dispersive energy to ensure favorable wetting by hydrophobic resin cements. Ultimately, the bonding agents effectively masked the mechanical topography, resulting in wettability driven primarily by the adhesive’s chemical composition rather than the underlying mechanical pre-treatments.

Introduction

Additive manufacturing is widely used in dentistry to produce complex restorations with minimal waste and fewer geometric constraints than subtractive milling.1,2 Digital light processing (DLP) printing enables rapid, full-layer curing and delivers exceptional edge sharpness through precise digital pixel control.3 The clinical application of 3Dprinted resins (3DRs) for fixed dental restorations is supported by favorable marginal adaptation and mechanical performance, which are largely attributed to their high filler content compared with provisional materials.4–6 Nevertheless, incomplete monomer conversion, oxygen-inhibited surface layers, and printing-induced surface irregularities can compromise bonding potential.2

To enhance adhesion, surface treatments including airborne-particle abrasion (APA), acid etching, and chemical primers, are employed.7–11 A systematic review reported that combining adhesives containing 10-methacryloyloxydecyl dihydrogen phosphate (10-MDP) with APA significantly enhances their bonding efficacy.12 The recommended protocol utilizes 50 μm alumina particles at 2–2.5 bar for approximately 10 s, optimizing bond strength through controlled surface roughening while preserving the resin’s structural integrity.13 However, the clinical challenges associated with APA have driven the search for alternatives. The technique is highly operator-dependent, requiring precise control over pressure, distance, angle, and duration. This sensitivity creates a significant risk of human error, potentially leading to inconsistent surface conditioning or structural damage.14 Moreover, the use of 3DR for orthodontic fixed lingual retainers is being explored; however, the impact of APA on the intrinsic strength of such small-scale devices remains a concern.15–17

Non-thermal handheld plasma (NTHP) offers a promising alternative by functionalizing polymer surfaces to improve hydrophilicity and bond strength.18–20 Although high-energy vacuum plasma (VP) combined with a universal adhesive (UA) achieves bond strength comparable to APA while preserving surface integrity,21 its high equipment cost and requirement for a sealed chamber preclude practical chairside use. On the chemical front, resin-matrix penetrating primers (RMPPs) offer a targeted solution. Originally formulated for industrially polymerized computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) resin blocks that lack a superficial oxygen-inhibited layer due to their high-temperature, high-pressure curing process.22,23 These primers are theoretically well-suited for post-cured 3DRs that exhibit similarly high cross-linking densities. However, despite the well-documented fine cleansing and surface activation effects of plasma treatments, the specific efficacy of NTHP, and the interaction between RMPPs and high-filler permanent 3DRs intended for definitive crowns remains largely unexplored.

Surface topography and wettability are critical thermodynamic determinants of adhesive behavior, as they govern liquid spreading and primer infiltration at the 3DR–cement interface.9,24,25 Accordingly, evaluating these surface characteristics provides essential insight into the potential effects of different surface treatments on bonding behavior. The aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of NTHP and RMPP on the surface morphology and wettability of 3DR for permanent crowns, in comparison with conventional APA and UA protocols.

Materials and methods

Specimen preparation

Disc-shaped 3DR specimens (10 mm in diameter and 2.5 mm in thickness) were designed using SolidWorks software (SolidWorks Corp., Dassault Systèms, Waltham, MA, USA) and exported in Standard Tessellation Language (STL) format. The specimens were fabricated from a permanent 3D-printing resin (Detax Freeprint crown, Detax GmbH & Co. KG, Ettlingen, Germany) using a 3Demax printer (DMG Medical Devices, Hamburg, Germany). Printing was performed by an experienced technician according to manufacturer’s instructions. All specimens were polished using 600-grit abrasive paper and ultrasonically cleaned in distilled water (DW) for 10 min. Subsequently, the specimens were randomly allocated into five experimental groups: NT (control); AU (APA followed by UA application); AP (APA followed by RMPP application); PU (NTHP followed by UA application); and PP (NTHP followed by RMPP application). The UA used was Single Bond Universal (3M ESPE, Seefeld, Germany), composed of 10-MDP, dimethacrylate resins, 2-hydroxymethyl methacrylate (HEMA), silane, filler, ethanol, and photoinitiators. The RMPP used was HC primer (Shofu Inc., Kyoto, Japan), composed of urethane dimethacrylate (UDMA), methyl methacrylate (MMA), solvents, and initiators. Detailed descriptions of the surface treatment procedures are presented in Table 1.

Surface analysis

Surface roughness was measured using a profilometer (Surftest SJ-210, Mitutoyo, Tokyo, Japan) with a cutoff length of 0.8 mm, an evaluation length of 6.4 mm, and a scanning speed of 0.5 mm/s. Three readings were taken per specimen, and the average roughness (Ra) value was calculated. Representative specimens from all groups, including the additional AA (APA-only) and PM (NTHP-only) groups, were examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; SU3500, Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) at magnifications of ×500 and ×5000 with an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV.

Wettability test

Surface wettability was assessed using a contact angle goniometer (Phoenix Mini, Surface Electro Optics Co., Ltd., Suwon, Republic of Korea) using the sessile drop technique with DW and diiodomethane (DIM; 99+%, Acros Organics, Geel, Belgium). For each specimen, the static contact angle (CA) was measured five times to minimize variability, and the mean value was recorded. Surface energy (SE) was then calculated using the Owens-Wendt-Rabel-Kaelble method with Surfaceware software (v9, Surface Electro Optics Co., Ltd.).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Surface roughness and wettability values were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. ANOVA was utilized based on the documented robustness of the F-test against violations of normality when sample sizes are equal across experimental groups. The significance level for all tests was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Surface analysis

Surface roughness values are summarized in Table 2 and Fig. 1. The PU group did not differ significantly from the NT group (P = 0.170), whereas the PP group exhibited significantly lower surface roughness than the NT group (P = 0.005). In contrast, APA significantly increased surface roughness, with the AU group showing the highest values; the AP group also exhibited significantly greater roughness compared with the NT and NTHP-treated groups (P < 0.001). Application of RMPP significantly reduced surface roughness in both APA- and NTHP-treated specimens, as the AP and PP groups showed lower roughness values than their UA counterparts (P < 0.001).

SEM observations of surface morphology after different treatments are shown in Fig. 2. The NT group exhibited a relatively smooth surface with distinct parallel grooves, which were residual marks from polishing with abrasive paper (Fig. 2A). In contrast, APA created a highly irregular surface with deep cavities, sharp cracks, and embedded alumina particles, as observed in the AA group (Fig. 2B). Due to the presence of deep surface irregularities created by abrasion, some irregular elevations remained visible even after the application of UA and RMPP (Fig. 2C and D). The PM group showed parallel grooves similar to those of the NT group, but with a cleaner and more distinct surface, indicating reduced superficial contamination (Fig. 2E). Following the application of UA and RMPP, the NTHP-treated surfaces exhibited a uniformly smooth morphology (Fig. 2F and G). Morphological analysis also revealed a significant difference between bonding agents at higher magnification (×5000): UA resulted in a granular, dotted structure, whereas RMPP consistently formed a uniform and smooth film (Fig. 2C, D, F and G).

Wettability test

The CA values and calculated SE are presented in Table 2. Fig. 3 provides representative droplet images and a graphical comparison of the CA data. For DW, the NT group exhibited the highest CA, indicating poor wettability, whereas all surface-treated groups showed significantly lower CA values (P < 0.001). Among the treated groups, the application of UA yielded the highest hydrophilicity, with no significant difference between the AU and PU groups (P = 1.000). These were followed by the AP and PP groups, which also showed no significant difference between them (P = 0.824). For DIM, the NT group again presented the highest CA, while all surface-treated groups exhibited significantly reduced CA values (P < 0.001); with no significant differences observed among the treated groups (P > 0.05).

The SE results showed a pattern consistent with the CA results obtained using DW. The NT group had the lowest SE, while surface-treated specimens exhibited significantly higher values (P < 0.001). The highest SE was found in the AU and PU groups, between which there was no significant difference (P = 0.970). These were followed by the AP and PP groups, which showed intermediate and statistically similar values (P = 0.323).

Overall, both CA with DW and SE were significantly influenced by differences in surface chemistry related to the type of bonding agent, as demonstrated by comparisons between AU and AP, and between PU and PP (P < 0.001).

Discussion

This in vitro study evaluated how various surface treatments influence the surface morphology and wettability of 3DR. Compared with the NT group, the PM group exhibited more distinct and continuous parallel grooves under SEM, indicating that NTHP removed superficial contaminants and enhanced surface activation, thereby revealing existing grinding features.26 APA selectively removes the resin matrix and exposes inorganic filler particles, producing a cratered surface that increased microroughness and micromechanical retention.8,12 SEM also demonstrated that both UA and RMPP infiltrated and partially filled surface irregularities created by mechanical pretreatment, resulting in surface smoothing.23,27 At × 5000 magnification, distinct interfacial morphologies were observed between bonding protocols. The AP and PP groups showed occlusion of surface irregularities and a smoother, continuous surface due to infiltration and polymerization of the MMA-containing primer, forming a relatively thick resin-rich interfacial layer.23 In contrast, the AU and PU groups exhibited a thin, retracted adhesive film caused by solvent evaporation, allowing filler particles to protrude through the adhesive layer.28 Consequently, the UA-treated surfaces showed a granular morphology, whereas the RMPP-treated surfaces formed a uniform, smooth film. Consistent with SEM findings, RMPP groups exhibited lower surface roughness than corresponding UA groups, while NTHP and UA treatment did not significantly alter roughness, as no difference was detected between NT and PU groups. Although VP can generate surface roughness comparable to APA, NTHP did not significantly modify surface topography. This difference is attributed to the high-energy particle impact in VP, which creates micro-pores and uneven structures under vacuum conditions,21 whereas NTHP operates at atmospheric pressure and primarily induces surface activation and functionalization without substantial macrostructural alteration.29

The NT group exhibited a CA of 96.62 ± 6.70°, comparable to values previously reported for permanent resin crown materials used as resin posts (85.2 ± 2.0°),30 with minor variations likely attributed to differences in material composition. Notably, UA-treated groups demonstrated significantly higher hydrophilicity than RMPP-treated groups, primarily due to the inclusion of HEMA which is a hydrophilic monomer that reduces surface tension and CA. This enhances surface adaptation and promotes monomer infiltration into the resin’s micro-topography.31 The RMPP-treated groups exhibited intermediate wettability compared to the NT group, likely due to the formation of a continuous, resin-rich surface layer with elevated SE following the infiltration and polymerization of MMA- and UDMA-containing monomers.23 The calculation of SE allows for a thermodynamic assessment of the treated surface’s ability to promote spontaneous wetting and establish a durable interface with the resin cement.32 In this study, the UA-treated groups yielded the highest total SE, a result driven by their significantly lower CA with DW (~15°) compared to the RMPP-treated groups (~48–50°). In addition, surface-treated specimens exhibited significantly lower CAs with DIM compared to the NT group. While this indicates that the UA creates a higher total energy surface due to increased polarity, the dispersive component is often more critical for resin bonding.28 Since both the UA and RMPP groups demonstrated similarly low CAs with DIM, it can be concluded that while UA is superior in total SE, both treatments provide sufficient dispersive energy to ensure wetting by the hydrophobic resin cement. It is also important to interpret these wettability results in the context of surface topography. According to Wenzel’s theory, increasing the roughness of a hydrophilic surface increases its effective surface area, thereby enhancing wettability.33 Therefore, the favorable wetting behavior observed in the APA groups should be attributed to a synergistic effect: the physical increase in roughness provided by alumina abrasion facilitates the initial spreading of the adhesive, while the chemical agents provide the necessary functional groups. Overall, while the surface wettability appeared to be primarily governed by the chemical composition of the primers and adhesives, the underlying mechanical topography likely played a supportive role in surface activation.

The present study has several limitations. First, as an in vitro investigation, it cannot fully replicate the complex dynamic conditions of the oral environment, such as temperature fluctuations and enzymatic degradation. Second, the findings are specific to the single brand of 3DR, UA and RMPP; thus, the results may not be directly extrapolated to materials with different chemical compositions. Additionally, polishing the specimens removed the original ’printing skin’ and layer lines. While this differs from the clinical scenario where the internal surface is left unpolished, this step was necessary to eliminate irregular spreading caused by the printing layers, ensuring that observed changes were strictly attributed to the surface treatments. Furthermore, this study focused exclusively on surface characterization (topography and wettability) as a thermodynamic precursor to adhesion. While increased wettability is prerequisite for effective bonding, it does not account for the quality of mechanical interlocking or chemical cross-linking. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as predictive indicators of bonding potential rather than direct evidence of bond performance. Future studies are essential to corroborate these thermodynamic data with mechanical validation, specifically shear or tensile bond strength testing, both before and after artificial aging, to confirm the clinical viability of these protocols.

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, it was concluded that while APA effectively increased surface roughness to enhance micromechanical retention, NTHP successfully activated the surface without altering its topography. Both UA and RMPP significantly improved surface wettability and SE compared to the untreated control. Although UA treatment resulted in the highest total SE due to its superior hydrophilicity, both agents provided comparable and sufficient dispersive energy to promote favorable wetting by hydrophobic resin cements. Ultimately, the bonding agents exhibited a masking effect, leveling the underlying surface irregularities. Consequently, the final wettability was determined by the chemical composition of the adhesive layer, which effectively covered the topographic modifications induced by the mechanical pretreatments.
