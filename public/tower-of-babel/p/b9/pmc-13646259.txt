Integrative multi-omics reveals MHC class II-mediated neuroinflammation and systemic metabolic dysregulation as transdiagnostic drivers in major brain disorders
Authors: Xuan Xu, Sha-Sha Fan, Hao Wu, Yanan Du, Yu Bo, Junwen He, Yang He, Na Cheng, Xiang-Yu Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPsychiatric, neurodevelopmental, and neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), bipolar disorder (BIP), major depressive disorder (MDD), and schizophrenia (SCZ), exhibit complex etiologies driven by immune and metabolic dysregulation. While distinct in their clinical onset, these conditions share overlapping molecular vulnerabilities. This study pioneers an integrative multi-omics framework, combining multi-tissue TWAS, cross-disorder pleiotropy analyses, Mendelian Randomization (MR), predictive machine learning, and BV2 microglial profiling. Crucially, our analysis uncovered a robust “Dual-Axis” etiological architecture. First, a systemic metabolic axis emerged as a primary driver, particularly involving FADS2-mediated lipid dysregulation and gut-brain axis interactions. This axis shares mechanisms between bipolar disorder and schizophrenia, with Multi-tissue TWAS revealing peripheral contributions (e.g., liver, colon) to CNS pathology. Second, MHC Class II-mediated pathways, driven by HLA-DRA, HLA-DRB1, HLA-DQB1, and HLA-DQA1, emerged as a transdiagnostic neuroinflammatory nexus across AD, BIP, MDD, and SCZ, orchestrating antigen presentation to CD4+ T-helper cells. To bridge these genomic findings with cellular function, BV2 microglial profiling was performed to provide a cellular-context reference specifically for the identified immune risk component. This cellular model confirmed that the immunogenetic risk burden maps to a specific proinflammatory activation state characterized by upregulated neurotoxins (Lcn2, Nos2, Ccl2) and suppressed lipid transport/phagocytosis (Apoe, Cd68). Machine learning models leveraging these signatures achieved robust predictive performance, particularly for BIP and MDD. MR analyses uncovered causal roles of immune, lipid, and microbial pathways, with shared metabolic signatures (e.g., N-acetylarginine) across disorders. Integration with traditional medicine databases linked lipid metabolism to Artemisia argyi, suggesting novel therapeutic avenues. This integrative approach redefines the molecular framework of these disorders by highlighting systemic metabolic dysregulation and strongly implicating MHC Class II-mediated neuroinflammation as two convergent drivers, advancing precision psychiatry through targeted immunotherapies and metabolic modulators.Subject terms: Neuroscience, Psychiatric disorders

Introduction

The human brain, with its extraordinary complexity and layered regulatory systems, remains one of the most challenging and least understood frontiers in biomedical research. Disruptions to its delicate functional equilibrium can give rise to a spectrum of brain disorders, spanning from early-life neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions to late-life neurodegenerative diseases.—including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), schizophrenia (SCZ), bipolar disorder (BIP), major depressive disorder (MDD), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and autism spectrum disorder (ASD)—that collectively impose a profound burden on individuals, families, and societies worldwide [1, 2]. Historically, efforts to elucidate the pathogenesis of these disorders were largely grounded in clinical observation and neurochemical hypotheses. However, the advent of the genomic era has fundamentally reshaped the field. Beginning in the mid-2000s, large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) enabled systematic interrogation of the genome, uncovering thousands of common genetic variants associated with increased risk for these conditions [3, 4]. These findings have firmly established the highly polygenic architecture of psychiatric and neurodegenerative disorders, offering a foundational map of their genetic risk landscapes.

The challenge now lies in translating GWAS findings into mechanistic insights. While the catalog of risk loci continues to expand, the causal genes and biological pathways they implicate often remain elusive [5]. Most risk variants reside in non-coding regions, exerting their effects through complex and often tissue-specific regulatory mechanisms. Therefore, resolving the “variant-to-function” puzzle requires integrative approaches capable of connecting genetic variation to gene expression and downstream functional consequences. Transcriptome-Wide Association Studies (TWAS) represent a major step in this direction, linking GWAS signals with expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) to infer genes whose genetically regulated expression is associated with disease susceptibility [6, 7]. TWAS not only narrows the search for causal genes but also identifies the specific tissues in which their dysregulation may be most relevant, providing a more direct route from genotype to phenotype.

Nevertheless, TWAS analyses have traditionally been restricted to single disorders and single tissues, limiting their capacity to capture the systemic and overlapping nature of psychiatric pathophysiology. Accumulating evidence points to substantial genetic and molecular pleiotropy across major brain disorders, with shared pathways—such as immune signaling, neurodevelopmental regulation, and synaptic transmission—frequently implicated in multiple phenotypes [8]. In parallel, recent advances in systems biology and multi-omics integration have revealed the importance of other molecular layers—including the epigenome, metabolome, and gut microbiome—in shaping disease risk and progression [9]. Mendelian Randomization (MR), which leverages genetic variants as instrumental variables, has become a powerful tool to infer causality between these molecular traits and disease outcomes [10–12], helping disentangle correlation from causation in observational data.

Despite these methodological advancements, critical gaps persist. While overlapping genetic risk highlights shared immunological and metabolic components across disorders, the precise mechanisms underlying cross-disorder convergence versus disorder-specificity remain unresolved [13]. Moreover, epidemiological links across the lifespan raise an additional question: which molecular liabilities are truly shared across neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders and may contribute to later neurodegeneration, versus which signals are diagnosis- or stage-enriched and therefore more likely to reflect downstream consequences or age-dependent mechanisms [14–16]. For instance, immune dysregulation, particularly involving adaptive immune pathways, has been implicated in multiple disorders, yet the functional roles of specific immune mechanisms and their interplay with central nervous system (CNS) pathology require further elucidation [17, 18]. Similarly, microglial activation, a hallmark of neuroinflammation, is increasingly recognized as a key contributor, but its molecular signatures and integration with systemic immune signals are poorly understood [19]. Prior studies have often focused on innate immunity or broad genetic associations, leaving the contributions of adaptive immune pathways and their peripheral-CNS interactions underexplored [20, 21]. Moreover, the translational impact of genetic discoveries remains limited, as few findings have yielded clinically actionable targets due to persistent mechanistic ambiguity [22].

To address these challenges, our study employs an integrative multi-omics framework, combining multi-tissue TWAS with single-tissue (S-PrediXcan) and multi-tissue (SMulTiXcan) approaches, cross-disorder genetic pleiotropy analyses, functional enrichment, multi-omics MR, predictive machine learning, and microglial inflammatory-state transcriptomic profiling as complementary cellular context profiling. Unlike previous TWAS studies restricted to brain tissues, our approach spans diverse tissues to capture systemic contributions—such as peripheral metabolic dysregulation and gut–brain axis perturbations—that may amplify CNS pathology. By integrating MR across transcriptomic, metabolomic, microbiome, and brain structural data, we aim to infer causal relationships, moving beyond correlational findings.

Furthermore, recognizing that statistical associations with immune loci do not inherently confirm a pro-inflammatory mechanism, we sought to provide a functional biological reference. To this end, we integrated transcriptomic profiling of LPS-stimulated BV2 microglia. We explicitly positioned this cell model not to recapitulate the broad genetic architecture of these disorders (which likely includes diverse non-immune drivers), but as a targeted validation platform to interrogate whether the identified genetic risks—specifically those potentially pertaining to immune pathways—functionally converge on defined microglial inflammatory programs, thereby bridging the gap between statistical associations and cellular phenotypes. Importantly, we complemented our human genetic and multi-omics analyses with transcriptomic profiling of LPS-stimulated BV2 microglia as a tractable, perturbation-controlled in vitro reference for microglial inflammatory-state programs. This experiment was included to provide interpretable cellular context for immune-related signals from population-scale analyses and to generate testable hypotheses, rather than to model disorder-specific etiologies or to imply a uniform microglia-specific causal pathway across the six conditions. Accordingly, the BV2 LPS signature is used to contextualize immune-related enrichment and predictive features (e.g., inflammatory-state programs), not to “validate” GWAS/TWAS loci one-to-one or to claim that all six disorders share identical upstream genetic architectures. Methods

Acquisition and harmonization of GWAS summary statistics

GWAS summary statistics for six major brain disorders spanning neurodevelopmental, psychiatric, and neurodegenerative domains—AD, ADHD, ASD, BIP, MDD, and SCZ—were obtained from publicly available consortia datasets. These statistics were harmonized using standardized protocols (https://github.com/hakyimlab/summary-gwas-imputation) to ensure compatibility with downstream analyses. Harmonization involved aligning genomic coordinates to the GRCh37/hg37 reference build, standardizing variant identifiers and allele definitions against a comprehensive reference panel, and reformatting data into a consistent structure. Where required by the source GWAS, genomic coordinates were converted to GRCh37 (liftover) within the same harmonization framework prior to reference matching. Quality control measures verified SNP identifiers, effect/non-effect alleles, and association metrics (Z-scores, effect sizes, and P-values), retaining non-rsID variants to maximize genomic coverage. Variants failing allele/reference consistency checks were excluded to preserve strand and effect-direction correctness for downstream TWAS.

Transcriptomic association analysis

To investigate the association between genetically regulated gene expression and disease risk, we conducted a Transcriptome-Wide Association Study (TWAS) using the MetaXcan software suite, specifically S-PrediXcan and SMulTiXcan [23] (https://github.com/hakyimlab/MetaXcan). Harmonized GWAS summary statistics for each disorder, comprising SNP identifiers, effect/non-effect alleles, Z-scores (for SMulTiXcan), and effect sizes/P-values (for S-PrediXcan), were integrated with gene expression prediction models derived from the GTEx v8 project. These models utilized the Multivariate Adaptive Shrinkage in R (MASH-R) framework across 49 human tissues to estimate genetically regulated expression components, ensuring robust cross-tissue applicability. For TWAS, we ensured that GWAS variants, the GTEx v8 MASHR-based prediction model variants, and the corresponding SNP covariance matrices were represented under a consistent coordinate and allele framework (GRCh37), as required for valid S-PrediXcan/SMulTiXcan inference.

S-PrediXcan was employed for tissue-specific TWAS, integrating disorder-specific GWAS summary statistics with tissue-specific SNP covariance matrices from GTEx v8. This approach imputed genetically regulated gene expression for each gene within a tissue and assessed its association with the respective disorder, generating gene-level association statistics for each disorder-tissue pair. To enhance statistical power and capture cross-tissue effects, SMulTiXcan aggregated Z-scores from GWAS summary statistics with S-PrediXcan-derived tissue-specific associations, leveraging a multi-tissue SNP covariance matrix optimized for numerical stability via a condition number threshold of 30. This integrative analysis synthesized evidence across tissues, producing unified gene-level associations for each disorder. Rigorous quality control was implemented, including validation of input data consistency and comprehensive logging to ensure reproducibility. Analyses were configured to terminate upon critical errors, safeguarding computational integrity. The workflow was systematically applied to all six disorders, enabling the identification of both shared and disorder-specific transcriptomic signatures.

Bayesian colocalization analysis

To establish a comprehensive regulatory baseline and formally test whether the identified GWAS signals and proximal gene expression regulation share the same underlying causal variants, we performed Bayesian colocalization analysis using the coloc R package (v5.2.3), with results further corroborated via the COLSTATS web server [24]. Detailed information regarding the specific GWAS summary datasets utilized for these analyses is systematically summarized in Table 1. We systematically evaluated the nominated genes within the 19q13 locus (APOE, TOMM40, CEACAM19) and the MHC Class II region (HLA-DRA, HLA-DRB1, HLA-DQA1, HLA-DQB1). For each gene, the colocalization window was defined as ± 500 kb flanking the gene body. Colocalization was assessed against cis-eQTL summary statistics from multiple highly relevant tissues from the GTEx v8 consortium, including Brain Cortex, Frontal Cortex BA9, Hippocampus, Cerebellum, Whole Blood, and Spleen.

The variant sets for each colocalization test were explicitly defined by the comprehensive cis-eQTL boundaries evaluated by GTEx v8, capturing approximately 4000–6000 variants per gene in the 19q13 locus and 11,000–20,000 variants in the extended MHC locus. For the Bayesian models, we strictly utilized widely accepted standard prior probabilities: p1 = 1 × 10−4 (the prior probability that a SNP is associated with the GWAS trait), p2 = 1 × 10−4 (the prior probability that a SNP is associated with the eQTL trait), and p12 = 1 × 10−5 (the prior probability that a single SNP is causally associated with both traits simultaneously).

We extracted the posterior probabilities for all five mutually exclusive hypotheses (H0–H4), focusing specifically on PP_H4 (the probability of a shared causal variant) and PP_H3 (the probability of distinct causal variants). Importantly, the standard coloc algorithm relies on a strict Single Causal Variant (SCV) assumption. Because regions like 19q13 and 6p21 are characterized by extreme linkage disequilibrium and multiple independent causal signals (allelic heterogeneity), the SCV assumption is often violated, which can artificially dilute PP_H4 and inflate PP_H3 due to LD interference. Therefore, these standard colocalization results were utilized to establish a baseline, serving as the foundational rationale for deploying our multi-causal SuSiE fine-mapping and conditioning framework to successfully disentangle these complex loci.

Locus-specific conditional fine-mapping and sensitivity analyses

To rigorously disentangle genuine biological associations from linkage disequilibrium (LD) tagging artifacts in complex genomic regions, particularly the 19q13 (APOE) and 6p21 (MHC) loci, we employed a summary-statistics-based Bayesian fine-mapping framework utilizing the Sum of Single Effects (SuSiE) model [25], implemented in the susieR package. This approach models the genetic architecture as a sparse sum of independent causal effects, allowing for the calculation of Posterior Inclusion Probabilities (PIPs) and the execution of conditional analyses using summary data (susie_rss) [26]. Linkage disequilibrium correlation matrices were estimated from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 reference panel [27], restricted to individuals of European ancestry. To rigorously evaluate the robustness of our identified associations and rule out LD tagging artifacts arising from the resolution limits of the 1000 G panel, we systematically re-executed the fine-mapping pipelines utilizing the highly dense UK Biobank (UKB) European LD reference matrix (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.13034123). To ensure precise alignment across both LD architectures, variants in the GWAS summary statistics were harmonized with the respective reference panel, excluding multi-allelic variants and strictly removing ambiguous palindromic SNPs (A/T, C/G) to prevent strand-flipping errors. Furthermore, to verify the reliability of the conditioning procedure, we assessed the consistency between the LD reference and the GWAS summary statistics (checking for allele frequency concordance) and confirmed that the conditional inputs did not introduce collinearity artifacts. Crucially, to ensure the LD correlation matrix (R) remained positive semi-definite and numerically stable during summary-based fitting, a subtle ridge penalty (λ = 1 × 10−4) was applied. Across all models, we systematically extracted 95% Credible Sets (coverage = 0.95). The residual variance was not arbitrarily fixed but empirically estimated from the data, and the scaled prior variance was learned iteratively using default EM/optim algorithms.

For the investigation of the CEACAM19 locus in AD, we adopted a component-level conditioning strategy to resolve overlaps with the APOE haplotype. We defined overlapping ± 250 kb windows centered on CEACAM19 and APOE gene boundaries based on GENCODE v19 annotations (e.g., CEACAM19: chr19: 44,915,545–45,437,631; APOE: chr19: 45,159,011–45,662,650, GRCh37/hg19). Using the SuSiE model (with the maximum number of non-zero effects set to L = 10), we identified credible sets across the broad locus, and components whose credible sets spatially overlapped the APOE gene body were designated as APOE-associated signals. We then re-estimated the posterior probabilities for the CEACAM19 window after mathematically conditioning on these APOE-overlapping components to assess whether the residual CEACAM19 association persisted independently. To definitively quantify signal independence across both the 1000 G and UKB LD frameworks, we tracked the maximum |Z|-score within the predefined CEACAM19 window before and after analytically regressing out the APOE-associated credible sets. True biological independence was defined a priori as the near-zero attenuation of the CEACAM19 lead |Z|-score in the residual model, concomitant with the preservation of high-confidence, CEACAM19-exclusive independent components.

In the MHC Class II region (defined by fixed physical coordinates spanning chr6: 32,000,000–33,500,000 to capture extended haplotype blocks), we performed sensitivity analyses for schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and major depressive disorder to test whether identified associations were driven by C4 structural variation or local expression regulation. Given the extreme polymorphism of this region, the model capacity was scaled up to L = 40. The baseline fine-mapping results were compared against three specific conditioning scenarios. First, to account for C4 structural risk, we conditioned on an established set of C4 proxy SNPs (rs13194504, rs210133, rs4282404) validated to capture C4A expression and copy number variation risk. Second, to evaluate the contribution of proximal gene expression, we implemented a systematic selection pipeline wherein significant cis-eQTLs from GTEx v8 (Brain and Whole Blood tissues, http://www.gtexportal.org/home/) were harmonized with the reference panel; for each disorder, we identified the regional GWAS lead SNP and selected the top independent eQTLs located within a ± 1 Mb local window centered on the GWAS peak. This rigorous selection ensures that we are testing against the strongest proximal regulatory effects rather than arbitrary markers. Finally, as a positive control, we conditioned on the regional GWAS lead SNP to verify that the method could successfully abolish the primary signal. Signal stability under the baseline 1000 G panel was initially defined as the persistence of a high-PIP 95% credible set containing the original lead variant after conditioning. However, given the extreme genetic complexity and extended LD inherent to this locus, genuine biological independence was ultimately evaluated using the UKB-based sensitivity framework. Under this dense UKB LD architecture, an association was deemed robust to LD tagging artifacts only if absolute model stability was achieved—specifically, the number of resolved independent credible sets and their corresponding maximum Posterior Inclusion Probabilities (PIPs) had to remain mathematically invariant across all unconditioned and specifically conditioned (C4/eQTL) scenarios.

Bulk RNA sequencing and microarray data standardization

To investigate transcriptomic profiles associated with AD, ADHD, ASD, BIP, MDD, and SCZ, bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and microarray data were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). The characteristics and sample sizes of these datasets are detailed in Table 1. Specifically, the AD dataset (GSE63063) comprised 284 cases and 238 controls from the AddNeuroMed Cohort. For ADHD, we utilized whole blood RNA-seq data (GSE159104) from 76 individuals with ADHD and 78 controls. The ASD dataset (GSE37772) included lymphoblast cell lines from 233 cases and 206 controls. For BIP, leukocyte microarray data (GSE46449) were obtained from 49 patients and 39 healthy controls. The MDD dataset (GSE98793) consisted of 128 cases and 64 controls, while the Schizophrenia (SCZ) dataset (GSE12649) involved postmortem brain tissue from 34 patients and 35 controls

Raw RNA-seq and microarray data underwent standardized preprocessing to ensure quality and consistency across datasets for downstream analyses. For RNA-seq datasets, quality control was performed using FastQC (v0.11.9, https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) to assess read quality, adapter content, and sequence duplication levels. Low-quality reads and adapters were trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.39) with parameters optimized for paired-end data. Trimmed reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using STAR (v2.7.10a) with a two-pass alignment strategy to enhance mapping accuracy. Gene expression was quantified as Fragments Per Kilbase of Transcript per Million Mapped Reads (FPKM) using RSEM (v1.3.3), leveraging GENCODE v36 annotations. Because the GEO transcriptome datasets are analyzed as measured expression traits (gene-level features) rather than as variant-level coordinates, this GRCh38-based RNA-seq preprocessing does not need to share SNP coordinate space with the GWAS/TWAS harmonization; downstream comparisons are performed at the level of gene identifiers. For microarray datasets, raw probe intensity data were normalized using Robust Multi-array Average (RMA) implemented in the Bioconductor package oligo (v1.60.0). Probes were mapped to gene-level annotations using platform-specific annotation files. RNA integrity was verified using Nanodrop 2000, ensuring A260/A280 ratios of 1.8–2.0 and A260/A230 ratios > 2.0. All standardization steps were conducted in R (v4.2.0) with comprehensive logging to ensure reproducibility, and workflows were configured to terminate upon critical errors, maintaining data integrity for subsequent analyses.

Functional enrichment analysis of TWAS-derived gene sets

To elucidate convergent biological pathways and functional gene modules enriched for association signals derived from the TWAS of six psychiatric disorders, we implemented the Marker Set Enrichment Analysis (MSEA) component using the Mergeomics pipeline [28]. This approach systematically identified over-represented biological themes within genes implicated by our TWAS analyses, focusing on cross-tissue associations derived from SMulTiXcan. The MSEA utilized gene-level association statistics (P-values) from SMulTiXcan as the primary input, enabling direct enrichment calculations without requiring additional marker mapping or linkage disequilibrium data. Enrichment analyses were conducted independently for each disorder, testing against five comprehensive gene set collections: co-expression modules from human Brain Hippocampus, Brain Cortex, Whole Blood, and Brain Hypothalamus tissues, and a curated compendium of canonical pathways from KEGG, Reactome, and BioCarta databases. Each gene set collection was accompanied by descriptive annotations to facilitate interpretation of enriched modules.

To ensure analytical rigor and comparability, standardized parameters were applied across all MSEA runs. Gene sets were filtered to include only those with 10 to 500 genes, balancing specificity and statistical power. Enrichment significance was determined using a robust marker-based permutation strategy, involving 2000 random shuffles of gene labels within the TWAS association data to generate null distributions of enrichment scores. To mitigate bias from genes with extreme association signals, the top and bottom 0.2% of gene association values were winsorized prior to score calculation. To consolidate findings and identify broader biological themes, significantly enriched gene sets with substantial overlap (minimum 33% gene overlap) were merged into representative “supra-modules”. Gene modules were considered significantly enriched if their permutation-based False Discovery Rate (FDR) was less than 0.05. This rigorous pipeline was systematically applied to each disorder across all five gene set collections, ensuring robust identification of shared and disorder-specific biological pathways

Predictive modeling of psychiatric disorders using module-derived gene expression signatures

To evaluate the predictive utility of gene expression signatures derived from functionally enriched modules for six psychiatric disorders, we implemented a comprehensive machine learning framework using the H2O AutoML platform (version 3.36.1.2) in R. Prior to any feature selection or model development, datasets were partitioned into a training set (70%) and an independent test set (30%) using a stratified sampling approach to maintain class balance across splits. This held-out test set was immediately isolated and never used for any preprocessing, feature selection, or hyperparameter optimization steps. For each disorder, feature selection was conducted by pooling the enrichment results from all five utilized gene set collections (Brain Hippocampus, Brain Cortex, Whole Blood, Brain Hypothalamus, and Canonical Pathways). To prevent information leakage, the selection of top modules and construction of feature sets were performed exclusively on the training data. Specifically, enriched modules were ranked based on their significance using only training set samples, and input datasets were constructed using gene expression matrices from the top 10 unique modules with the lowest False Discovery Rate (FDR) determined solely from the training partition. This global ranking strategy ensured that the predictive models were trained on the most robustly associated biological signals, independent of their tissue of origin. Prior to model training, to ensure rigorous evaluation, datasets were partitioned into a training set (70%) and an independent test set (30%) using a stratified sampling approach to maintain class balance across splits.

To capture the complex, non-linear relationships within the transcriptomic data, we deployed a diverse portfolio of thirteen distinct machine learning architectures. This included classical estimators and advanced tree-based ensembles: Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting (GB), and CatBoost, the latter selected for its robust handling of categorical distinctions. We also employed Support Vector Machines (SVM) with radial basis function kernels to capture high-dimensional decision boundaries. Expanding into deep learning, we utilized a suite of advanced neural network architectures implemented to capture specific data topologies: Multi-Layer Perceptrons (Advanced MLP) for general feature extraction; Convolutional Neural Networks (Advanced CNN) adapted for one-dimensional gene vectors; and sequential models including Long Short-Term Memory (Advanced LSTM) networks and Recurrent Neural Networks (Advanced RNN) to capture potential pathway dependencies. Furthermore, we integrated state-of-the-art architectures including Advanced Transformers utilizing self-attention mechanisms, TabNet for attentive tabular learning, and Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINN) to incorporate biological constraints into the learning process

Instead of manually defining hyperparameter grids for individual algorithms, we implemented an automated hyperparameter optimization framework utilizing a random grid search strategy. All hyperparameter optimization was performed strictly within the cross-validation framework on the training set only, with no access to the held-out test set. The search spaces were comprehensively defined for each algorithm family—encompassing parameters such as learning rates (0.001–0.1), tree depths, regularization penalties (L1/L2), and network topology (hidden layers/neurons). To ensure a rigorous search within a reasonable computational timeframe, we defined the stopping criterion for the optimization process as a maximum of 50 base models per functional module. During this iterative process, model selection was primarily optimized based on the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUC) to maximize classification performance. Following the training of base learners, we constructed two types of ensemble meta-models: a Voting Classifier (soft voting) and a Stacking Classifier (using Logistic Regression as a meta-learner) to integrate predictions from the optimized base models and further enhance accuracy.

We implemented a dynamic cross-validation strategy to accommodate the varying sample sizes across disorders. All cross-validation procedures were conducted exclusively within the training set (70% of data), with the test set (30%) reserved solely for final evaluation. For datasets with a total sample size of 100 or more (AD, ADHD, ASD, and MDD), we applied a standard 10-fold cross-validation on the training set. However, for smaller datasets with fewer than 100 samples (BIP and SCZ), we adjusted the protocol to 5-fold cross-validation on the training set. Within each cross-validation fold, all data-dependent preprocessing steps (including any normalization, scaling, or feature transformation) were fitted only on the training portion of that fold and then applied to the validation portion, ensuring no information leakage between folds. This dynamic adjustment ensured that each validation fold contained a sufficient number of samples to provide statistically robust error estimates. The predictive performance of the top-performing models was subsequently evaluated on the held-out test set using a suite of metrics, including AUC, Logloss, Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUCPR), and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE). Furthermore, to rigorously assess classification reliability in the context of potential class imbalance, we calculated the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) and F1-Score. Finally, feature importance was aggregated from the top 5 performing models using a weighted ensemble approach (weights: 1.0, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4, 0.2), ensuring that the identified biomarkers were robust across different algorithmic architectures.

Traditional machine learning models

A suite of classical machine learning algorithms was deployed. Hyperparameter optimization for these models was conducted using a nested cross-validation approach: within each outer cross-validation fold of the training set, RandomizedSearchCV with 3-fold inner cross-validation, optimizing for the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (ROC AUC). This nested procedure ensured that hyperparameter selection never utilized information from the validation portion of the outer fold nor from the held-out test set. Model evaluation

After completing all model development, hyperparameter optimization, and model selection procedures exclusively on the training set, the final models were retrained on the entire training set using the optimal hyperparameters identified through cross-validation. Feature importance scores were derived for each model: intrinsic importance for tree-based ensembles and permutation-based importance (scoring on ROC AUC) for other models. All results were aggregated across folds and disorders to facilitate robust comparative analyses.

Mendelian randomization analyses to investigate causal pathways in psychiatric disorders

To systematically elucidate potential causal relationships between a diverse array of biological exposures and the etiology of six major psychiatric disorders, we implemented a comprehensive two-sample MR framework. This analytical approach leverages genetic variants robustly associated with specific exposures as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer their causal impact on disease outcomes, thereby mitigating biases from confounding factors and reverse causation that often plague observational studies.

For each of the six psychiatric disorders, publicly available Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) summary statistics, predominantly derived from large-scale consortia and restricted to individuals of European ancestry, were utilized as the outcome datasets (Table 1 and Table S1). Instrumental variable selection

For each exposure, genetic variants were selected as IVs based on genome-wide significance (P < 1 × 10⁻⁸ for eQTLs; P < 1 × 10⁻⁵ for others). To ensure the independence of these selected IVs and to mitigate bias due to linkage disequilibrium (LD), SNPs were clumped using a stringent LD threshold (r² < 0.001 within a 10,000 kb genomic window, based on the 1000 Genomes European reference panel). Furthermore, to address potential weak instrument bias, SNPs with an F-statistic below 10 were excluded from the analysis; the F-statistic, serves as a measure of instrument strength.

Data harmonization

Meticulous harmonization of exposure and outcome GWAS summary statistics was then performed. This critical step involved aligning effect alleles and their corresponding effect sizes to ensure that the genetic effect on the exposure and the effect on the outcome corresponded to the same allele. Palindromic SNPs (A/T or C/G) presenting ambiguous allele frequencies were either removed or their strand orientation was inferred using allele frequency information from reference panels where feasible, thereby minimizing potential strand ambiguity issues.

Causal inference analysis

The causal effects of each harmonized exposure on the six psychiatric disorders were systematically evaluated using the random-effects Inverse-Variance Weighted (IVW) method as the primary analytical approach. The IVW method provides the most statistically powerful estimate of the causal effect under the assumption that all IVs are valid. To rigorously assess the robustness of our findings and to investigate potential violations of MR assumptions, particularly the assumption of no unbalanced horizontal pleiotropy, a comprehensive suite of sensitivity analyses was conducted. MR-Egger regression was employed, as its intercept term can provide an indication of directional pleiotropy; a statistically significant intercept (P < 0.05) would suggest that the average pleiotropic effect across the instruments is non-zero. The MR-Egger slope, while often less precise than the IVW estimate, can provide a causal estimate that is adjusted for such directional pleiotropy. The Weighted Median Estimator was also utilized, offering a consistent causal estimate provided that at least 50% of the statistical weight in the analysis derives from valid IVs. Furthermore, mode-based estimation methods, including the Simple Mode and Weighted Mode, were applied. These approaches identify the most frequent (modal) causal effect estimate among the set of instruments, which can be particularly robust to outlier IVs that may have distinct causal effects or strong pleiotropic effects. Heterogeneity among the causal estimates derived from individual IVs was quantified using Cochran’s Q statistic for both the IVW and MR-Egger methods. Significant heterogeneity (P < 0.05) could signal the presence of pleiotropic effects or indicate that different instruments are capturing distinct underlying causal pathways, prompting cautious interpretation of the IVW results. Additionally, the MR-Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) test was performed to identify and potentially correct for bias introduced by horizontal pleiotropic outliers by removing such variants and re-estimating the causal effect. Leave-one-out analyses were also conducted, where the overall causal estimate was recalculated iteratively by omitting one IV at a time, to assess whether the primary finding was disproportionately driven by any single genetic variant. Consistency of effect estimates across these different MR methods and sensitivity analyses was considered to strengthen the confidence in any inferred causal relationships.

Population stratification and statistical analysis

To minimize potential biases arising from population stratification and genetic heterogeneity, both the exposure and outcome GWAS datasets utilized in this study were predominantly sourced from individuals of European ancestry, thereby ensuring demographic coherence across the analyses. All MR analyses were conducted using publicly available GWAS summary-level data, obviating the need for additional institutional review board approval or informed consent for this component of the research. Statistical analyses were primarily performed using the “TwoSampleMR” R package [33]. Appropriate corrections for multiple testing (e.g., Bonferroni correction or a False Discovery Rate threshold, FDR < 0.05) were applied to account for the large number of exposure-outcome hypotheses tested across the various molecular and imaging domains.

Culture and LPS treatment of BV2 mouse microglial cells

Microglial activation is a recurrent biological theme implicated across multiple brain disorders, yet human microglia are difficult to obtain at scale under controlled perturbations. We therefore employed the BV2 mouse microglial cell line as a standardized, reproducible in vitro system to capture transcriptional programs induced by a canonical inflammatory stimulus (LPS). This experiment was designed to generate a reference signature of microglial inflammatory activation for downstream integration with our genetic and multi-omics results, rather than to claim that all six disorders share an identical microglia-specific causal pathway [34, 35]. Specifically, we use this perturbation-controlled signature to (i) provide cellular context for interpreting immune-related multi-omics findings and (ii) generate testable mechanistic hypotheses; it is not intended as disorder-specific causal validation of GWAS/TWAS loci. The BV2 mouse microglial cell line (Catalog No.: CL-0697, Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) was cultured in DMEM (Catalog No.: PM150210) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Catalog No.: 164210-50) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S, Catalog No.: PB180120), all sourced from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator and confirmed to be in the logarithmic growth phase before experiments. BV2 cells were seeded at a density of 2.5 × 10⁵ cells/mL in 24-well plates (0.5 mL per well) and incubated for 4 h to achieve complete adherence. The experiment included a control group (treated with an equal volume of DPBS) and an LPS-activated group (treated with 500 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide, Catalog No.: L6529, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). After 24 h of treatment, cells were harvested for transcriptomic sequencing.

RNA extraction and transcriptomic sequencing of BV2 cells

Treated BV2 cells were washed twice with pre-chilled PBS (centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, supernatant discarded). Total RNA was extracted using the MolPure® Cell/Tissue Total RNA Kit (Catalog No.: 19221ES50, YEASEN Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), with samples exhibiting an A260/A280 ratio of 1.8–2.0 and an A260/A230 ratio > 2.0 considered qualified. Qualified RNA samples were used to construct cDNA libraries, followed by paired-end 150 bp sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA). After quality control, sequencing data were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) using STAR (v2.7.10a). Gene expression was quantified as FPKM using RSEM (v1.3.3). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (v1.36.0) with criteria of |log₂FoldChange| > 1 and adjusted P < 0.05. Functional enrichment analysis was comprehensively performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.4.4). To avoid selection bias, we conducted Over-Representation Analysis (ORA) on the complete set of significant DEGs (P-adjusted < 0.05) against Gene Ontology (GO) categories (Biological Process, Cellular Component, and Molecular Function) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases. Furthermore, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was employed on the full gene list ranked by Log2 Fold Change to capture coordinated pathway alterations independent of arbitrary cutoffs. The directionality of pathway regulation (activation vs. suppression) was assessed using the Z-score (calculated as the mean Log2 Fold Change of enriched genes) for ORA results and the Normalized Enrichment Score (NES) for GSEA results. Statistical significance was strictly controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction (FDR < 0.05)

Pharmacological enrichment analysis of genetic targets in neuropsychiatric disorders

To elucidate the pharmacological basis and identify potential therapeutic compounds or herbal interventions relevant to gene targets implicated by significantly associated SNPs, a multi-faceted drug and Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) enrichment analysis was conducted. This approach leveraged the integrative capabilities of the TCMNP [36] R package, which consolidates extensive drug-target, herb-ingredient-target, and regulatory interaction data from diverse, publicly accessible resources. These resources notably include the TCM Systems Pharmacology Database (TCMSP) [37] for herb-ingredient-target relationships, DrugBank for comprehensive drug information, the Encyclopedia of TCM (ETCM) [38] for broader TCM knowledge, and the TRRUST [39] database for curated transcription factor-target interactions. The core analytical principle for identifying significantly associated drugs, herbal ingredients, or entire herbs involved assessing the over-representation of both disease-specific key gene targets and those implicated in shared pathophysiological mechanisms across the disorders within predefined sets of targets known to be modulated by these pharmacological entities. Statistically, this enrichment was quantified using the hypergeometric distribution, which evaluates the probability of observing at least the given number of shared targets between the query gene set (derived from the aforementioned SNP-implicated genes) and the set of targets for a specific drug, herb, or ingredient, compared to what would be expected by chance given the total number of targets in the background universe (typically all annotated targets within the integrated TCMNP database). P-values derived from these tests were rigorously corrected for multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedure, with an FDR threshold of < 0.05 generally considered indicative of significant enrichment. This methodology allowed for the systematic identification and prioritization of existing drugs, TCM herbs, and their constituent compounds that demonstrate a statistically significant targeting preference towards the biological entities implicated in the six psychiatric disorders under investigation, thereby providing a data-driven basis for exploring novel therapeutic strategies or repurposing opportunities.

Statistical analysis

All bioinformatics and statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.0). To address the diverse nature of the multi-omics datasets integrated in this study, specific statistical thresholds were applied based on the data type and analytical context. For high-throughput analyses, including functional enrichment (MSEA), pharmacological enrichment, and differential gene expression analysis in BV2 cells, statistical significance was strictly controlled using a Benjamini-Hochberg FDR of < 0.05 to mitigate bias derived from multiple hypothesis testing. In the context of MR, thresholds for selecting genetic IVs were determined by the statistical power of the underlying exposure GWAS. For large-scale datasets such as eQTLs, a stringent genome-wide significance threshold (P < 1 × 10⁻⁸) was utilized 8. Conversely, for gut microbiome and metabolomic datasets, which typically feature smaller sample sizes, a standard suggestive threshold (P < 1 × 10⁻⁵) was adopted to strictly retain a sufficient number of valid instruments for robust causal inference 9. For Transcriptome-Wide Association Studies (TWAS), while genome-wide significance was prioritized, a suggestive significance threshold (P < 1 × 10⁻⁵) was specifically adopted for SMulTiXcan analyses of ASD and MDD 10. This adjustment was necessary to capture polygenic risk signals in these highly heterogeneous disorders, where strict Bonferroni correction often resulted in insufficient gene targets for downstream pathway exploration. For all other standard comparisons, a two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. This study exclusively utilized publicly available datasets for which ethical approval and informed consent had been previously obtained. All research procedures were conducted in strict adherence to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Standard colocalization and locus-specific conditional fine-mapping supports independence of key signals

To establish a regulatory baseline for the highly complex 19q13 (APOE) and 6p21 (MHC) loci, we first performed standard Bayesian colocalization utilizing the coloc R package. We evaluated GWAS signals against cis-eQTLs from relevant GTEx v8 brain and immune tissues. The analyses were executed using strictly specified standard priors (p1 = 1 × 10−4, p2 = 1 × 10−4, and p12 = 1 × 10−5) and variant windows defined by the comprehensive GTEx cis-eQTL boundaries. Interestingly, this standard approach yielded generally low-to-moderate posterior probabilities for a shared single causal variant (PP_H4). For instance, at the 19q13 locus, the maximum PP_H4 for CEACAM19 and TOMM40 across all evaluated tissues reached 0.084 and 0.377, respectively. Similarly, in the MHC Class II region for SCZ, BIP, and MDD, key regulatory targets such as HLA-DRA and HLA-DRB1 exhibited maximum PP_H4 values ranging from 0.040 to 0.218 (Table S5, Supplementary Fig. 2). Rather than implying a lack of biological association, these results expose a critical statistical limitation of standard colocalization algorithms: their strict reliance on a Single Causal Variant (SCV) assumption. In genomic regions characterized by extreme linkage disequilibrium and multiple independent causal signals, such as 19q13 and 6p21, the SCV assumption is severely violated, causing significant LD interference and artificial dilution of PP_H4.

To distinguish genuine biological signals from tagging artifacts arising from complex LD structures, particularly in the 19q13 (APOE) and 6p21 (MHC) loci, we performed rigorous locus-specific conditional fine-mapping using the SuSiE model. To explicitly address the severe risk of LD tagging artifacts inherent to sparse reference panels, we performed a head-to-head LD reference sensitivity analysis comparing the baseline 1000 Genomes (1000 G EUR) reference against the highly dense UK Biobank (UKB) European reference matrix. To rigorously validate the independence of CEACAM19 (19q13) from the adjacent APOE haplotype, we performed component-level conditional fine-mapping. We explicitly identified SuSiE components L5, L9, and L10 as the primary drivers of the APOE association based on their spatial overlap with the gene body. Under the baseline 1000 G reference, conditioning on these specific components yielded a distinct “signal unmasking” effect: the CEACAM19 association strength increased, with the maximum absolute Z-statistic rising from 10.15 to 10.89, while the lead variant rs17643262 retained dominance. Crucially, transitioning to the superior UKB LD architecture revealed an exceptionally stable signal with near-zero attenuation (−1.0%, max |Z| shifting trivially from 10.15 to 10.05), with rs17643262 firmly retaining its lead status. In sharp contrast, reciprocal conditioning on CEACAM19 components resulted in the collapse of the APOE signal under both references. Specifically, the maximum |Z| decreased from 9.44 to 4.58 (−51.5%) with a lead variant shift from rs10415850 to rs8113514 in 1000 G, and dropped from 8.08 to 3.26 (−59.7%) with a lead variant shift from rs28367893 to rs597668 in UKB. These divergent responses—extreme stability (or strengthening) versus attenuation and variant displacement—confirm that CEACAM19 represents a molecular driver mathematically distinct from the canonical APOE risk pathway components (L5, L9, and L10) (Supplementary Fig. 3, Table S6).

Similarly, for MHC Class II peaks in SCZ, BIP, and MDD, sensitivity analyses using summary-based Bayesian fine-mapping confirmed that signals were not driven by C4 structural variation or local eQTLs. In our initial baseline analysis using the 1000 Genomes (1000 G EUR) reference, the robust MHC Class II signal in SCZ (lead SNP rs3891175, PIP = 0.998) remained virtually unchanged after conditioning on validated C4 tag SNPs (PIP = 0.998) or top local eQTLs (PIP = 0.988). The specificity of this signal was confirmed by the positive control analysis: conditioning on the GWAS lead SNP (rs141706335) drastically reduced the maximum PIP to 0.143 and shifted the lead variant to a residual noise signal, demonstrating that our pipeline is highly sensitive to true LD-driven correlations. Residual signals in BIP (lead SNP rs365053) and MDD (lead SNP rs113328631) exhibited similar spatial consistency under conditioning, although their maximum PIPs were severely diluted to 0.055 (lead SNP rs365053) and 0.036 (lead SNP rs113328631), respectively. Upon positive control conditioning (GWAS lead), these weak signals further fragmented, with lead variants indiscriminately shifting to rs41270464 (BIP) and rs28688207 (MDD). To further investigate this severe signal dilution and the risk of LD tagging artifacts inherent to the highly polymorphic MHC region, we performed a head-to-head LD-reference sensitivity analysis utilizing the highly dense UK Biobank (UKB) European LD reference matrix. Our initial 1000 G analysis revealed significant signal fragmentation due to LD resolution limits, artificially inflating the number of independent credible sets (yielding 35 components for SCZ and 29 for BIP). However, transitioning to the superior UKB LD architecture successfully resolved these artifacts, collapsing the signals into a biologically plausible framework (precisely 10 components for SCZ, 13 for BIP, and 9 for MDD) and drastically improving model confidence. Specifically, the maximum PIP for MDD surged from 0.036 to 0.832 (lead SNP shifted to rs9469465), and for BIP it increased from 0.055 to 0.271 (lead SNP rs482759), while SCZ remained highly robust at 0.995 (lead SNP rs10807124). Crucially, within this highly accurate UKB LD framework, the identified independent signals exhibited considerable robustness against regulatory conditioning. For all three psychiatric disorders, conditioning on validated C4 tag SNPs or the top local eQTLs yielded negligible attenuation: the number of resolved credible sets (10, 13, and 9) and their precise maximum PIPs (0.995, 0.271, and 0.832, respectively) remained highly consistent with the unconditioned baseline. However, while our conditional analyses suggest a distinct regulatory signal associated with MHC Class II genes, the extreme linkage disequilibrium and dense allelic heterogeneity characteristic of the 6p21 locus preclude absolute certainty. These findings should be interpreted as highly suggestive rather than definitive, pending functional validation. The persistence of these primary GWAS signals under the most stringent LD architectures confirms the robustness of the underlying genetic associations at these loci, providing a reliable foundation for exploring their regulatory impacts (Supplementary Figs 4-5, Table S7).

Predictive modeling of psychiatric disorders using functional module gene signatures

A systematic evaluation was conducted to assess the predictive accuracy of various machine learning models in distinguishing patients with six brain disorders investigated here. For BIP, SVM models trained on Brain_Hippocampus.MEGENA_48 (AUC = 0.876) and Whole_Blood.WGCNA_12 (AUC = 0.877) modules exhibited superior discriminatory performance. CatBoost models also demonstrated robust results for these modules, highlighting the efficacy of these approaches in capturing BIP-specific signatures. In MDD, StackingClassifiers consistently outperformed other models, achieving optimal performance with gene sets from REACTOME_TGF_BETA_RECEPTOR_SIGNALING_ACTIVATES_SMADS (AUC = 0.887) and Brain_Hypothalamus.MEGENA_235 (AUC = 0.885). These findings underscore the advantage of ensemble learning in addressing the heterogeneity of MDD.

Moderate predictive power was observed for AD, ADHD, and SCZ. For AD, an SVM model utilizing Brain_Hypothalamus.MEGENA_2 achieved the highest AUC of 0.720. In ADHD, a StackingClassifier trained on Brain_Cortex.MEGENA_349 yielded an AUC of 0.649, reflecting modest discriminatory capacity. For SCZ, a CatBoost model leveraging Brain_Hypothalamus.MEGENA_5 attained an AUC of 0.714, while RandomForest models showed promise with Brain_Cortex.MEGENA_164 and Brain_Hippocampus.MEGENA_143 modules. Predicting ASD proved more challenging, with most models yielding lower AUCs. The best performance was achieved by an advanced Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) model trained on Whole_Blood.MEGENA_203, which, despite a modest AUC of 0.556, achieved a high recall of 0.9. Causal molecular and structural drivers uncovered by mendelian randomization

To ensure the validity of causal inferences derived from eQTL, gut microbiota, metabolite, and brain MRI datasets, we performed rigorous diagnostics across all reported associations (detailed in Table S12). First, instrument strength was verified to be robust across all four domains; mean F-statistics consistently exceeded the conventional threshold of 10 (Median F: eQTL = 275.5; Metabolites = 25.8; MRI = 24.7), indicating minimal risk of weak instrument bias. Second, directional pleiotropy assessed via MR-Egger intercepts was negligible. No significant intercept deviation (FDR > 0.05) was detected for any exposures related to gene expression, gut microbiota, or brain MRI, while only a minor fraction of metabolite associations (1.9%, 43/2317) exhibited potential pleiotropy. Third, regarding heterogeneity, Cochran’s Q tests revealed varying degrees of variability: significant heterogeneity (FDR < 0.05) was observed in 11.7% of gene, 12.9% of metabolite, and 32.9% of MRI associations, whereas gut microbiota analyses showed negligible heterogeneity (0.0%). To address this, random-effects IVW models were utilized as the primary estimator. Finally, outlier correction using MR-PRESSO further validated the findings. Although the Global Test identified potential horizontal pleiotropy in very few instances (e.g., 3 associations in MRI), the outlier correction procedure successfully identified and removed outlier SNPs across datasets (107 for eQTLs, 265 for gut microbiota, 8484 for metabolites, and 97 for MRI). Crucially, all primary causal inferences remained statistically significant following outlier removal and FDR correction, confirming the robustness of the identified multi-omics networks described below.

Transcriptomic profiling of LPS-stimulated BV2 microglia reveals suppression of mitochondrial, translational, and immune pathways

To delineate the molecular mechanisms of microglial activation, we utilized an LPS-stimulated BV2 cell model. Prior to differential expression analysis, we rigorously assessed data quality across biological replicates (n = 3 per group). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) demonstrated a striking separation between the Control and Model groups, with the first principal component (PC1) capturing 97.7% of the total variance, indicating that the treatment effect is the primary driver of transcriptional changes. High reproducibility between replicates was confirmed by Pearson correlation coefficients (r > 0.99) and a median gene-wise dispersion of 0.371, indicating controlled biological variability suitable for robust differential testing (Supplementary Fig. 11A-B).

To avoid selection bias, we performed differential expression analysis on the complete dataset without pre-selection. This unbiased profiling yielded a total of 7458 DEGs based on FDR criteria (P-adjusted < 0.05). We first validated the establishment of a robust pro-inflammatory phenotype by quantifying the expression of hallmark cytokines. This analysis revealed a profound and statistically significant increase in the expression of Il6, Il1b, and Tnf-α in the model group relative to the control group (P < 0.01 for all, Supplementary Fig. 12), confirming the successful induction of a classical inflammatory response. To gain a comprehensive understanding of the transcriptional programs driving this state, we then subjected these cells to RNA-seq. This unbiased transcriptomic profiling identified 1090 significantly upregulated and 819 significantly downregulated genes (|log₂FC| > 1, P-adjusted < 0.05) (Fig. 10A, Table S24). In agreement with our initial validation, the upregulated gene set was highly enriched for inflammatory and metabolic regulators, including Lcn2, Ccl7, Nos2, Il1rn, and Ccl2. Conversely, downregulated transcripts such as Serpinb1a, Bnip3, Apoe, and Rpl10 suggested a concomitant suppression of protein translation, lipid transport, and mitochondrial respiration.

These genes were associated with recurrent exposures such as phenylpyruvate, 1,2-dipalmitoyl-gpc (16:0/16:0), MAPK3, and regional brain volumes (e.g., right pars triangularis), reflecting convergent disruption in amino acid metabolism, lipid signaling, and prefrontal cortical structure. Additional shared genes such as DNMBP and PPP2R3A were linked to androgen and sulfur-containing metabolic pathways, suggesting pleiotropic metabolic vulnerability across disorders. E Representative disorder-specific gene–exposure pairs derived from MR. In AD, BCL7C, RNF40, and USP14 were uniquely associated with ZNF668 and DHEAS levels, implicating transcriptional regulation and steroid metabolism. In ADHD, IDNK and FBXL15 were linked to dimethylglycine, highlighting dysregulated one-carbon metabolism. In ASD, PDPR and REEP3 were associated with methionine sulfone and glutarylcarnitine, indicating mitochondrial and sulfur amino acid pathway involvement. For BIP, KIF5C and ATP5G2 were linked to carnitine C6, N-acetylputrescine, and docosatrienoate (22:3n3), reflecting altered fatty acid transport and mitochondrial bioenergetics. In MDD, AKR7A2 and SAMD5 were associated with phenylpyruvate and taurine, suggesting disruptions in aromatic amino acid and osmolyte balance. In SCZ, DDAH1 and TLE3 were linked to taurine, dimethylglycine, and glutarylcarnitine, pointing to mitochondrial and methylation-related dysfunction. Together, these data highlight both convergent and disorder-specific gene–exposure mechanisms within a shared inflammatory and metabolic context.

We next performed functional enrichment analysis on the complete list of DEGs, separated by effect direction. Upregulated genes were predominantly enriched in epigenetic and nuclear regulatory processes, including histone modification and chromatin remodeling (P-adjusted < 0.05), suggesting a profound reconfiguration of the chromatin landscape to support the inflammatory state (Fig. 10B, Supplementary Fig. 11E, Table S25). Conversely, downregulated genes were heavily concentrated in metabolic pathways, specifically generation of precursor metabolites and energy, cellular respiration, and aerobic respiration (P-adjusted < 1 × 10−30), indicating a suppression of mitochondrial bioenergetics.

To capture coordinated pathway-level perturbations independent of arbitrary cutoffs, we employed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) on the full ranked gene list. GSEA identified a robust activation of inflammatory and stress-response pathways, with the acute-phase response (NES = 2.71) showing the strongest positive enrichment (P-adjusted < 1 × 10−6). Strikingly, pathways related to neuronal support were significantly suppressed, typified by the downregulation of translation at presynapse (NES = −3.59) and translation at synapse (Fig. 10C). Together, these results demonstrate that LPS treatment induces a profound shutdown of energy production and synaptic support machinery, in parallel with transcriptional/epigenetic activation of inflammatory pathways (Fig. 10B, Supplementary Fig. 11F, Table S26).

MR integration identifies pleiotropic and disorder-specific gene-exposure signatures overlapping with BV2 microglial inflammatory profiles

To determine whether the broad genetic risks identified in GWAS functionally converge on microglial inflammatory pathways, we intersected the human multi-omics data with the BV2 transcriptomic signature. Cross-comparison identified 407 overlapping genes between LPS-induced DEGs and MR-predicted causal genes, involving 341 SNPs and 288 exposures (Table S27). This significant overlap confirms that the “immune-related fraction” of the polygenic risk score—specifically genes involving cytokine signaling and lipid transport—is biologically consistent with a pro-inflammatory microglial state. Among these, 33 genes were pleiotropically linked to two or more disorders. For example, SMARCA4 was associated with BIP, MDD, and SCZ, and was linked to 1-(1-enyl-palmitoyl)-2-arachidonoyl-gpc, 1,2-dipalmitoyl-gpc (16:0/16:0), and thalamic volume. LRP8, implicated in ADHD, ASD, and BIP, was linked to 5-hydroxymethyl-2-furoylcarnitine, cerotoylcarnitine (C26), and LachnospiraceaeUCG001, suggesting convergent involvement of lipid metabolism and gut-brain axis in neurodevelopment. Genes such as BCAT2, HSD17B14, and PLEKHA4 (MDD, SCZ) were repeatedly associated with phenylpyruvate, implicating aromatic amino acid metabolism as a shared vulnerability factor. Additional pleiotropic associations included GATAD2A and NDUFA13 with prefrontal cortical volumes, and KCTD13 with MAPK3, a regulator of synaptic plasticity and inflammation (Fig. 10C, D, Table S28).

Furthermore, disorder-specific genes were identified. In AD, BCL7C, RNF40, and USP14 were associated with eQTL ZNF668 and DHEAS levels. In ADHD, IDNK and FBXL15 were uniquely linked to dimethylglycine, suggesting methylation pathway dysregulation. ASD-specific genes PDPR and REEP3 were associated with methionine sulfone and glutarylcarnitine, indicating disrupted sulfur metabolism and mitochondrial stress. In BIP, KIF5C and ATP5G2 were linked to carnitine C6 and docosatrienoate (22:3n3), while in MDD, AKR7A2 and SAMD5 were associated with phenylpyruvate and taurine. In SCZ, DDAH1 and TLE3 were linked to taurine, dimethylglycine, and glutarylcarnitine, indicating methylation and redox imbalance. Collectively, these data demonstrate that a substantial proportion of LPS-responsive genes in microglia converge with genetically instrumented molecular exposures across brain disorders, revealing both shared metabolic disruptions and disorder-specific gene–exposure axes. Specifically, shared patterns were more apparent at the level of pleiotropic metabolites and brain structural traits that mapped onto overlapping microglial-responsive genes, whereas distinct patterns were reflected by disorder-enriched gene–exposure pairings that remained largely diagnosis-specific across AD, ADHD, ASD, BIP, MDD, and SCZ (Fig. 10E, Table S29).

Discussion

The elucidation of the etiological architecture of major brain disorders spanning neurodevelopmental, psychiatric, and neurodegenerative domains, encompassing conditions such as AD, ADHD, ASD, BIP, MDD, and SCZ, remains a paramount challenge in contemporary neuroscience and genetics [40, 41]. Crucially, these disorders are not independent across the lifespan: ADHD and ASD have been associated with increased later risk of schizophrenia, and major psychiatric disorders have been associated with increased later-life risk of dementia, motivating an analytic emphasis on disentangling shared (pleiotropic/transdiagnostic) versus disorder- or stage-enriched molecular mechanisms [14–16]. Importantly, while such longitudinal associations motivate cross-disorder investigation, the present work does not directly model temporal progression or clinical mediation; rather, it uses genetic and multi-omics integration to map patterns of convergence and divergence across outcomes. In this context, our study advances the field by deploying a multi-modal integrative framework that synergizes multi-tissue TWAS, cross-disorder genetic pleiotropy analyses, functional enrichment profiling, multi-omics MR, and predictive machine learning, complemented by transcriptomic profiling of LPS-stimulated BV2 microglia as a functional reference for microglial inflammatory-state programs, used to contextualize (rather than define) cross-disorder signals from the human genetic and multi-omics analyses.

This comprehensive approach unveils novel shared and disorder-specific molecular mechanisms, highlighting a “Dual-Driver” model of pathogenesis. First, we identified robust Systemic Metabolic Dysregulation. Beyond immune factors, our study underscores the critical role of lipid metabolism, particularly centered on the FADS2 locus. The pleiotropic association of FADS2 variants with bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, and schizophrenia, linked causally to altered phospholipid and fatty acid levels, suggests a shared metabolic vulnerability affecting neuronal membrane fluidity and synaptic function independent of inflammation. Second, we elucidated a Transdiagnostic Immune Nexus. The prominence of MHC Class II-mediated pathways emerged as a central mechanism connecting psychiatric risk to neurodegeneration. It was this specific immune dimension that we sought to contextually validate using the BV2 microglial model. The directional consistency between the MR-predicted loss of Apoe/Cd68 (protective factors) and their suppression in the cellular model confirms that these genetic risk variants likely act by promoting a maladaptive, pro-inflammatory microglial state.

Explicit synthesis of shared versus disorder-/stage-enriched architecture. Across analytic layers, our results support a two-level organization. First, we observe a shared transdiagnostic immune axis: MHC Class II/HLA-centered signals recur across multiple outcomes (most prominently AD, and also BIP/MDD/SCZ) in enrichment analyses and predictive modeling, consistent with adaptive immune involvement that may couple peripheral and CNS inflammatory states. Second, we identify disorder- or stage-enriched components superimposed on this shared backbone: neurodevelopmental outcomes (ADHD/ASD) show comparatively stronger enrichment for non-HLA pathways (e.g., metabolic/DNA repair- and transcriptional regulation-related programs), while transcriptomic causal prioritization from MR and gene–tissue association profiles are often outcome-specific, suggesting heterogeneous upstream regulation even when downstream immune/metabolic signatures converge. Conceptually, this framing accommodates the empirical reality that these disorders can be clinically and temporally linked across the lifespan without assuming etiological identity; instead, it highlights where biological mechanisms plausibly converge (pleiotropy) versus where they remain enriched in particular diagnoses or stages.

Previous Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) have identified numerous susceptibility loci for psychiatric disorders [8, 42]; yet these studies often highlight broad genomic regions without pinpointing causal genes or their tissue-specific effects. While earlier TWAS applications in psychiatry often concentrated on brain tissues [43], or a restricted panel of tissues [44], the novelty of the present investigation lies in its systematic, expansive multi-tissue TWAS across a diverse array of human tissues for six major disorders, employing both single-tissue (S-PrediXcan) and sophisticated multi-tissue meta-analytic (SMulTiXcan) methodologies. This broad, tissue-agnostic exploration has yielded profound insights into the systemic component of these conditions. For instance, the discovery that AD-associated genes such as CEACAM19 exhibit significant expression associations in as many as 45 distinct tissues, and SLC39A13 in 34 tissues including thyroid and breast, radically expands the traditional neuropathological view of AD. This aligns with, and substantially elaborates upon, the burgeoning conceptualization of AD as a systemic disorder wherein peripheral dysfunctions in immunity, metabolism, and vascular integrity critically contribute to central nervous system (CNS) pathology [45, 46]. Similarly, the association of ADHD-linked genes with hepatic (PTPRF) and aortic (ST3GAL3) tissues, alongside ASD-associated XRN2 with the sigmoid colon, furnishes robust, genetically-anchored evidence for the integral involvement of metabolic systems, cardiovascular health, and the gut-brain axis in the pathogenesis of neurodevelopmental disorders [47, 48]. Beyond cataloging tissue associations, the strategic use of SMulTiXcan enhances power to detect multi-tissue-consistent signals and facilitates principled comparison of shared versus outcome-enriched gene–tissue patterns across disorders.

Building on this systemic perspective, the prominence of MHC Class II-mediated antigen presentation as a prominent transdiagnostic nexus across AD, BIP, MDD, and SCZ refines prior evidence of immune dysregulation [49, 50]. While earlier studies broadly implicated MHC loci or innate immunity in SCZ and AD [51, 52], our findings emphasize antigen presentation to CD4+ T-helper cells as a specific driver, suggesting a nuanced mechanism involving T-cell responses, potential autoimmune processes, or aberrant reactions to environmental antigens [53, 54], advancing beyond the less specific inflammatory models of earlier studies. Mechanistically, MHC Class II molecules, including HLA-DRA, HLA-DRB1, HLA-DQB1, HLA-DQA1, HLA-DMB, HLA-DPA1, HLA-DOA, and HLA-DOB, present antigenic peptides to T cells, triggering the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α. These cytokines activate microglia, amplifying neuroinflammation and exacerbating neuronal damage across the blood-brain barrier [55]. The robust MHC Class II signals detected in peripheral blood modules suggest that systemic T-cell responses are not merely epiphenomenal but actively contribute to or mirror CNS pathology, potentially through T-cell trafficking or cytokine-mediated signaling [56, 57]. A plausible mechanistic route is neurovascular: during sustained systemic inflammation, the vascular blood–brain barrier (BBB) can become more permeable, accompanied by increased lymphocyte trafficking and occasional endothelial damage, thereby facilitating peripheral immune-to-CNS signal transfer. Moreover, peripheral cytokines and inflammatory mediators can act at neurovascular interfaces to bias microglial activation states even without massive leukocyte infiltration, providing a direct link between blood-derived MHC-II–associated immune activity and central neuroinflammatory phenotypes [58]. In SCZ, autoimmune pathway enrichment suggests self-reactive T cells disrupt synaptic pruning, contributing to cognitive deficits [59, 60].

The prominence of HLA-DRA and HLA-DRB1 as top predictors in our machine learning models for AD and SCZ, alongside HLA-DQB1 and HLA-DQA1 across multiple disorders, underscores their critical role in orchestrating systemic and CNS immune responses. These genes likely facilitate chronic neuroinflammation by promoting T-cell-driven cytokine production, which may impair neuronal function and synaptic integrity [61, 62]. For instance, in AD, enhanced MHC Class II activity could exacerbate amyloid-beta accumulation by polarizing microglia toward a proinflammatory state, reducing their phagocytic capacity [63]. In SCZ, this mechanism may disrupt synaptic pruning, contributing to circuit dysfunction underlying cognitive symptoms [64]. Unlike earlier research that focused on innate immunity or broad anti-inflammatory strategies [65–67], our multi-modal approach—integrating TWAS-identified gene enrichment, functional profiling, and predictive modeling—provides convergent evidence for the transdiagnostic relevance of these specific MHC Class II genes. This refined immunological signature suggests that targeting MHC Class II pathways with immunosuppressive agents, such as anti-TNF therapies or T-cell modulators, could mitigate neuroinflammation, offering a more precise therapeutic strategy than previously explored broad-spectrum anti-inflammatory approaches [68]. By addressing these pathways, novel immunotherapies could attenuate the neuroinflammatory cascade, providing a tailored approach for precision psychiatry. This focus on adaptive immunity and its systemic-CNS interplay opens new avenues for investigating how peripheral immune signals interact with CNS pathology, potentially revolutionizing therapeutic development for psychiatric disorders.

The causal insights from our MR analyses further deepen this mechanistic understanding, moving beyond the correlational associations of prior GWAS [5, 8, 69]. In AD, elevated TAP2 expression enhances MHC Class I antigen presentation by facilitating peptide loading onto MHC Class I molecules, potentially increasing CD8+ cytotoxic T-cell activity and microglial activation that impair amyloid-beta clearance [70, 71], contrasting with prior studies noting TAP2 polymorphisms without establishing causality [72]. Reduced ITGAL expression impairs leukocyte adhesion necessary for immune surveillance, while upregulated PICALM promotes clathrinid-mediated endocytosis critical for amyloid-beta clearance. In BIP, protective CAMK1D and DAGLA signaling implicates calcium-dependent kinase regulation and endocannabinoid biosynthesis in mood stabilization through GABAergic modulation [73, 74] For SCZ, MAD1L1 and GATAD2A emerged as causal risk genes, linking spindle checkpoint defects and chromatin remodeling to neurodevelopmental synaptic dysfunction. Similarly, the protective role of Fc receptor-mediated pathways suggests enhanced phagocytosis could reduce inflammatory burden, offering a novel therapeutic avenue compared to traditional amyloid-focused strategies [75, 76]. The shared fatty acid metabolism pathways across BIP, MDD, and SCZ, critical for neuronal membrane fluidity and anti-inflammatory signaling, highlight a key enzymatic node that regulates PUFA synthesis [77, 78]. Unlike prior studies reporting PUFA abnormalities as correlates [79], our causal inferences pinpoint specific molecular targets, suggesting that interventions enhancing PUFA synthesis could reduce neuroinflammation. The identification of shared metabolites like N-acetylarginine suggests disruptions in arginine metabolism, which may amplify oxidative stress via nitric oxide pathways, further linking systemic and CNS inflammatory processes [80]. Crucially, valid interpretation requires distinguishing primary metabolic drivers from secondary comorbidities. We acknowledge that psychiatric GWAS cohorts likely include patients with metabolic conditions (e.g., obesity) driven by lifestyle or medication [81]. However, our use of Mendelian Randomization mitigates this confounding. Since germline genetic variants are fixed at conception and generally independent of environmental exposures, the causal estimates derived here reflect a genetic liability to metabolic dysregulation that precedes psychiatric onset. For instance, the link between FADS2-mediated lipid variants and schizophrenia suggests a biological etiology rather than a bystander effect. Although biological pleiotropy cannot be fully excluded, the consistency of results across robust MR sensitivity analyses (e.g., MR-PRESSO) strengthens the evidence for a primary metabolic-psychiatric axis [82]. These causal insights provide a more precise mechanistic framework than earlier correlational studies, emphasizing the potential for targeted therapies. Nevertheless, alternative interpretations warrant consideration [83]. First, MHC-region signals can be disproportionately prominent due to locus characteristics such as dense gene content and extended linkage disequilibrium, which can amplify association clustering and complicate causal gene attribution even when multi-omics prioritization is applied [84]. As explicitly demonstrated by our head-to-head LD reference sensitivity analysis, relying on sparser panels like 1000 G can lead to severe signal fragmentation and the artificial inflation of credible sets due to imperfect tagging. While transitioning to the highly dense UK Biobank LD reference successfully resolved these artifacts and consolidated the signals into a biologically plausible framework, statistical conditioning alone cannot definitively rule out residual tagging artifacts in a region characterized by such extreme allelic heterogeneity. Thus, while the survival of these signals under the stringent UKB architecture strongly supports their independence from C4 or local eQTL confounding, our specific MHC causal gene attributions remain highly suggestive rather than absolute. Second, while MR reduces confounding and reverse causation, some metabolite associations may still represent downstream consequences of preclinical disease processes, systemic inflammation, or treatment-related changes; thus, longitudinal sampling and experimental perturbation are needed to distinguish primary drivers from secondary effects.

Importantly, our human genetic analyses indicate that transcriptomic causal prioritization is often outcome-specific, whereas pleiotropy is more apparent at the level of metabolites and imaging traits; the BV2 experiment therefore serves as a cellular perturbation reference that can illuminate downstream inflammatory and metabolic programs without implying a shared upstream transcriptomic architecture across all six disorders. BV2 microglial transcriptomics revealed directionally concordant inflammatory signatures validating MR-predicted mechanisms. Proinflammatory mediators showed consistent upregulation: Il1b and Tnf—both central to MR-identified AD risk pathways—activate NFκB signaling cascades that drive astrocytic and microglial neurotoxicity [85]. Chemokine Ccl2 functionally confirms MR associations with MDD/SCZ by recruiting peripheral monocytes across disrupted BBB, amplifying neuroinflammation [86]. Conversely, homeostatic genes demonstrated consistent downregulation: Apoe, whose reduction in activated microglia aligns with MR-predicted loss of lipid-mediated amyloid clearance in AD [87]; and Cd68, reflecting diminished phagocytic capacity concordant with protective Fc receptor pathway suppression in MR models. Most strikingly, Lcn2 emerged as the highest induced gene, mechanistically linking MR-identified AD/SCZ risk through iron dysregulation-triggered ferroptosis and NLRP3 inflammasome activation that amplifies IL-1β release in feed-forward inflammatory loops. The directional consistency—inflammatory upregulation coinciding with MR risk factors, homeostatic downregulation paralleling loss of MR-identified protective mechanisms—establishes functional validation bridging human genetic causality to cellular phenotypes, advancing beyond correlational studies.

These mechanistic insights translate into precision therapeutic strategies. Targeting Lcn2 with pharmacological inhibitors (e.g., iron chelators) could interrupt ferroptotic cascades [88], while restoring Apoe function through gene therapy or small molecules may enhance lipid homeostasis essential for amyloid clearance [89, 90]. Blocking CCL2-CCR2 signaling with cenicriviroc-like antagonists could prevent peripheral immune cell infiltration [86], offering a translatable anti-neuroinflammatory approach validated in multiple sclerosis models [91]. Enhancing CAMK1D-dependent calcium buffering or augmenting DAGLA-mediated endocannabinoid tone through selective DAGL activators may stabilize mood circuits in BIP [92]. Unlike traditional neurotransmitter-targeted therapies [93], these mechanism-driven interventions address upstream causal pathways identified through multi-omics convergence. The computational link to Artemisia argyi compounds like eupatilin, predicted to modulate PUFA metabolism and PPAR signaling, provides a data-driven hypothesis for natural product therapeutics [94], contrasting with empirical ethnopharmacology.

Despite its comprehensive scope, our study faces challenges that pave the way for future investigations. Specifically, a notable disparity in the number of significant genes was observed across disorders (e.g., AD and SCZ yielding substantially more signals than ADHD and MDD). This reflects inherent differences in the genetic architecture and statistical power of the underlying GWAS datasets—such as the higher SNP-based heritability of SCZ and AD versus the high phenotypic heterogeneity of MDD—rather than tissue-specific eQTL coverage bias alone. To mitigate potential weighting biases in cross-disorder inferences, our methodological framework prioritized pathway-level enrichment (FDR-corrected) and strict signal intersection over raw gene counts. This ensures that reported transdiagnostic mechanisms, such as MHC Class II signaling, represent robust biological convergence validated by multi-omics integration, rather than artifacts of differential sample sizes. Notably, MR analyses may be confounded by horizontal pleiotropy, necessitating advanced methods like multivariable MR to refine causal inferences. Furthermore, we acknowledge that the GWAS summary statistics utilized likely include patients with metabolic comorbidities. While MR mitigates reverse causation, it cannot fully distinguish whether a shared genetic variant drives both metabolic and psychiatric phenotypes independently (biological pleiotropy) or sequentially. Thus, these findings should be interpreted as shared genetic liabilities rather than strictly distinct causal chains. Additionally, TWAS reliance on eQTL panels may miss context-specific expression, which single-cell RNA sequencing could address by capturing cell-type-specific mechanisms. Furthermore, the use of European ancestry datasets limits generalizability, underscoring the need for replication in diverse populations to ensure equitable clinical translation. The use of BV2 microglia, while insightful, may not fully recapitulate human responses, highlighting the need for human microglial studies. Finally, an important methodological limitation concerns the interpretation of our multi-tissue TWAS (SMulTiXcan) results in the context of conditional fine-mapping and LD reference sensitivity. While our SuSiE analyses particularly the robust validation of the CEACAM19 signal using the high-density UKB reference—demonstrated that the primary GWAS signals strongly persist after conditioning on adjacent regulatory or structural artifacts, we did not directly re-compute the SMulTiXcan associations using these residualized summary statistics. Because SMulTiXcan fundamentally leverages the cross-tissue covariance of predicted expression levels, the residualization process could theoretically alter the local LD structure and shift these cross-tissue covariance weights. Consequently, while the persistence of the underlying GWAS signal implies locus robustness, it does not mathematically guarantee that the precise SMulTiXcan multi-tissue association profile would remain identical post-conditioning. Future analytical frameworks that natively integrate conditionally adjusted GWAS summary statistics into multi-tissue TWAS pipelines will be required to fully resolve the residualized regulatory architecture. Future research should prioritize: (1) functional validation of Lcn2, Apoe, and Ccl2 in human microglial-neuronal co-cultures; (2) clinical trials of CCR2 antagonists or DAGL modulators; (3) pharmacological dissection of Artemisia argyi bioactives targeting PUFA pathways; and (4) multi-ancestry GWAS-TWAS-MR integration to ensure equitable precision psychiatry.

Conclusion

This study redefines the etiological landscape of psychiatric, neurodevelopmental, and neurodegenerative disorders disorders by integrating multi-omics approaches with BV2 microglial transcriptomics as complementary cellular context, and identifying two convergent molecular drivers: systemic metabolic dysregulation and adaptive neuroinflammation. First, we identified a robust metabolic axis characterized by lipid dysregulation. The pleiotropic association of FADS2 variants with BIP, MDD, and SCZ, causally linked to altered phospholipid biosynthesis, underscores a shared metabolic vulnerability that operates alongside immune risks. Second, our findings highly suggest that specific MHC Class II-mediated adaptive immune pathways act as a transdiagnostic nexus across AD, BIP, MDD, and SCZ. Although the extreme linkage disequilibrium characteristic of the 6p21 locus precludes absolute certainty, the prioritization of specific MHC Class II genes, such as HLA-DRA, HLA-DRB1, HLA-DQB1, and HLA-DQA1, underscores their potential role in antigen presentation to CD4+ T-helper cells, driving neuroinflammation through cytokine signaling and T-cell trafficking. To bridge these genomic findings with cellular function, we utilized the BV2 model. While BV2 does not model disease-specific etiologies, it provides a controlled perturbation setting in which microglial inflammatory-state programs can be examined for directional consistency with the immune-specific subset of our multi-omics findings. The BV2 microglial data further validated this immune axis through directional concordance with MR predictions: proinflammatory genes (Lcn2, Il1b, Tnf, Ccl2) amplify oxidative stress and immune cascades, while suppressed Apoe and Cd68 impair lipid transport and phagocytosis, contributing to neuronal dysfunction. These findings advance beyond prior research by offering a precise dual-axis framework and identifying novel therapeutic targets, such as metabolic modulators (e.g., targeting FADS2 or Artemisia argyi pathways) and immunosuppressive agents for MHC Class II pathways and modulators of microglial Lcn2/Apoe/Ccl2 axes. By bridging systemic metabolic and CNS immune signatures, this study paves the way for precision psychiatry, with potential applications in stratified therapies to mitigate neuroinflammation and restore metabolic homeostasis.
