Implementation of a Hybrid Closed-Loop Automated Insulin Delivery System in a 29-Day Infant With Neonatal Diabetes
Authors: Alexandra L Ghaben, Christine Byer-Mendoza, Kimberly McNamara, Andrea Huber, Anna L Cymbaluk, Puja Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeonatal diabetes mellitus (NDM) often requires insulin treatment to maintain adequate glycemic control and support growth and development. Conventional insulin treatment regimens are particularly challenging in NDM due to young age, developmentally appropriate feeding variation, and heightened insulin sensitivity leading to very small insulin dosing. We present this case as—to our knowledge—the earliest implementation of a hybrid closed-loop (HCLS) automated insulin delivery system in an NDM patient. HCLS with U10 significantly improved glycemic control compared to conventional insulin delivery with minimization of hypoglycemia. The adaptive capabilities of HCLS allowed for developmentally appropriate, ad libitum feeding without carbohydrate quantification, resulting in a meaningful quality of life improvement. This case adds to evidence supporting safe and effective use of diabetes technology in very young children. It demonstrates that the benefits seen in older populations can be achieved in neonatal patients and supports expanding clinical use of HCLS systems in this setting.

Introduction

Neonatal diabetes mellitus (NDM) is a rare form (1:90 000-160 000 births) of diabetes with onset before age 6 months and nearly always is attributed to a genetic/epigenetic variation [1]. Approximately 60% of NDM cases require insulin treatment [2]. In general, insulin treatment is uniquely challenging in NDM due to balancing the indispensable nature of insulin for growth and weight gain against the risk of hypoglycemia and technical limitations of administering low doses. Additionally, the estimation of carbohydrate content in breast milk is challenging, and the requirement for precise carbohydrate estimation alters typical infant feeding behavior with the potential for neurodevelopmental outcomes.

For other forms of diabetes, hybrid closed-loop systems (HCLS) are the gold standard of treatment and importantly allow for modification of insulin dosing based on data from continuous glucose monitors (CGMs) [3, 4]. In patients with type 1 diabetes, HCLS have been shown to improve every aspect of glycemic control over conventional insulin pumps or injections and improve patient and family quality of life [5].

There are several rare reports of HCLS in NDM [6-8], and slightly more reports of insulin pump use [9, 10], although diabetes technology remains underused due to a lack of regulatory studies regarding safety/efficacy in this population, technical challenges related to delivery of very small insulin doses, and decreased access to technology due to insurance coverage. However, the adaptability of insulin delivery in HCLS is uniquely suited to improve glycemic control and caregiver quality of life in this age group.

We describe use of HCLS in a 29-day-old term infant diagnosed with NDM due to chromosome 6q24 hypomethylation. To our knowledge, this is the youngest patient to use HCLS to date.

Case Presentation

A healthy, 2630-g infant was born at 38 weeks’ gestation via induced vaginal delivery for oligohydramnios. Prenatal history was significant for intrauterine growth restriction. The infant had routine postnatal glucose monitoring indicated for small-for-gestational-age infants and was found to have hypoglycemia at approximately 10 hours of life (38 mg/dL [SI: 2.1 mmol/L]) that was refractory to treatment with oral glucose gel and required 10% intravenous (IV) dextrose infusion. The infant developed hyperglycemia at 18 hours of life (glucose >300 mg/dL [SI: >16.7 mmol/L]), and hyperglycemia persisted after discontinuation of IV dextrose. Aside from mild resolving hypothermia, the infant was well appearing and breastfeeding typically. Fig. 1 summarizes the case presentation, diagnostic assessment, and treatment timelines.

Diagnostic Assessment

Endocrinology was consulted with persistence of hyperglycemia at 44 hours of life without secondary cause and with breast milk as the only dextrose source. Examination revealed a small infant with minimal subcutaneous tissue, without obvious dysmorphisms. Initial laboratory evaluation is outlined in Table 1 and notable for undetectable insulin and C-peptide levels. Two abdominal ultrasounds only partially visualized the pancreas. The majority of NDM is attributable to genomic variation, and thus rapid whole-genome sequencing was ordered on the fourth day of life and resulted negative for any genomic variants on the 14th day of life. With negative genomic origin of NDM, we ordered a methylation-specific multiplex ligation assay on the 14th day of life as 70% of the transient subtype of NDM can be attributed to chromosome 6 methylation anomalies. During the sixth week of life, maternal hypomethylation of 6q24 region was confirmed.

Treatment

Substantial time is required for even expedited genomic/epigenomic diagnostics, and empiric treatment of NDM is required. Approximately 40% of NDM patients respond to sulfonylurea medications, and we initially trialed glyburide (1 mg/kg/day) to possibly avoid insulin treatment. After 48 hours, this was discontinued for inefficacy, leaving insulin as the only treatment option.

Insulin treatment was initiated on the seventh day of life, initially via continuous IV infusion of regular insulin. This is conventionally the treatment of choice for NDM; however, we experienced technical challenges with this delivery, as well as significant glucose variability. Our patient experienced several episodes of hyperglycemia seemingly unresponsive to increased IV delivery. Retrospectively, we identified that these hyperglycemic episodes occurred close to scheduled tubing changes and were likely related to line malfunctions secondary to very low-volume delivery. In these instances (and others) we augmented IV insulin with subcutaneous hyperglycemic correction dosing, mimicking a basal-bolus like system.

Glycemic control was monitored using a CGM placed on the infant on the seventh day of life, coincident with initiation of insulin therapy. A Dexcom G7 (DexCom Inc) was placed on the outer thigh of the infant. CGM measurements were compared to serum glucose measurements for 24 hours, with CGM readings within the manufacturer's reported operating parameters and correlated with serum values. CGM values are reported in this study unless otherwise noted.

Off-label use of HCLS was discussed with the parents, including the anticipated benefits of improved glycemic control and quality of life balanced against the lack of broad efficacy in this population. The parents elected to proceed with the HCLS trial while the infant was hospitalized.

HCLS with insulin lispro U100 was initiated on the 29th day of life, using the Dexcom G7 CGM and a Tandem Mobi Insulin Delivery System (Tandem Diabetes Care). We used TruSteel infusion sets placed on the outside of the thigh due to the relatively increased size subcutaneous layer typically present in infants and rigid cannula. The insulin delivery device was placed on the contralateral thigh as the CGM per the manufacturer's instructions. Initial settings for the HCLS are shown in Table 2.

The HCLS system settings are shown in Table 2 and were initially programmed with a basal insulin delivery rate of 0 units/hour with an insulin sensitivity factor at the highest allowable programmable value of 1 unit:600 mg/dL. Automated insulin delivery using Control-IQ using “sleep mode” was selected to omit automated correction boluses for hyperglycemia, anticipating algorithmic modulation of basal insulin would be sufficient to achieve glycemic control. We did not advise the family to quantify or administer any boluses for carbohydrate intake and continued to advise that the infant be fed ad libitum per typical developmental status.

Initiation of HCLS with U100 insulin lispro resulted in marked improvements in glycemic control, with a reduction in mean glucose from 302 mg/dL [SI: 16.8 mmol/L] to 162 mg/dL [9.0 mmol/L] in the first week of use. Time in glycemic target range (70-180 mg/dL [SI:3.9-10 mmol/L]) similarly improved from 6.6% to 56.9%. The effective insulin basal delivery rate was between 0.02 to 0.03 units/kg/hour as modulated by the Control-IQ algorithm. We also noted increased hypoglycemia frequency (5.75% on HCLS vs 0.36% with IV and injection regimen) (Table 3). After review, we felt this was most likely because the lowest programmable insulin sensitivity factor exceeded the true needs of the patient.

Outcome and Follow-up

Fig. 2 displays a 7-day average of CGM data during each treatment modality. Consistent with prior studies [1], HCLS improves average glucose, glycemic variability (as measured by SD and coefficient of variation). The patient's mean glucose of 180 mg/dL [SI: 10 mmol/L] is slightly above the American Diabetes Associate Guidelines recommendation; however, this aligned with recommendations to maximize glycemic control while minimizing hypoglycemia to avoid adverse neurodevelopmental effects in this age group. U10 insulin lispro specifically avoided nearly all hypoglycemia, and ongoing outpatient insulin dose titration yielded a glycated hemoglobin measurement of 4.8% (reference range <6.5%) at 2 months after hospital discharge.

We specifically highlight the omission of any insulin dosing for carbohydrate consumption including via insulin pump. This infant continued to fed on-demand, without diabetes-related restrictions on feeding volume/selection or carbohydrate quantification. Although this is a single case, the parents reported satisfaction with this selection and felt the patient was less medicalized overall.

Methylation testing results were received after the infant's hospital discharge and were consistent with maternal hypomethylation of chromosome 6q24. This hypomethylation is linked to a transient NDM and interestingly this infant experienced remission of transient NDM at approximately age 4 months, consistent with prior reports of NDM remission [11].

Discussion

Insulin treatment in NDM is uniquely challenging, with minute insulin doses and highly variable feeding patterns. Additionally, diabetes technologies considered standard of care in older people have limited utilization in this age group due to a lack of safety data and technical concerns. This case details several adaptations of an existing HCLS for NDM including omission of carbohydrate dosing, usage of dilute insulin, and modulation of the glycemic target in the insulin delivery algorithm.

Hypoglycemia is the most serious medical and developmental risk in NDM. We emphasize unique features of our case, including the use of dilute insulin to essentially eliminate hypoglycemia, the power of CGMs to notify caregivers of anticipated hypoglycemia, and the relative ease of treating lows in HCLS due to automated reductions in insulin delivery (15-30 mL of formula/breast milk). In our case, significant reductions in delivered insulin preceded overt β-cell recovery in our patient and allowed for safe transition off insulin therapy.

Safe, equitable adoption of HCLS in NDM requires substantial caregiver education. In this case, education by trained diabetes education staff was completed while the infant was hospitalized. Education was modified from clinic standard curricula for patients with new onset of diabetes and those interested in pump initiation. Our team chose to initiate the HCLS inpatient and ensure caregivers had ample opportunity to physically use the technology prior to home use while under trained supervision, likely contributing to successful use in this case.

Speculatively, limitation of both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia through HCLS in NDM have the potential to meaningfully improve neurodevelopmental outcomes [12, 13]. This and several other reports [5, 7-9] of HCLS use in this population with clear benefits over conventional management strongly support routine use of HCLS and other diabetes technology in this age population.
