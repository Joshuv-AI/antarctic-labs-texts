Endoscopic Suture Ligation and Sclerotherapy for Pharyngolaryngeal Venous Malformation
Authors: Jiajun Tian, Juke Ma, Ahmad A. Hariri, Zhenghua Lv, Peng Cui, Wei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivePharyngolaryngeal venous malformations (VMs) can cause airway obstruction and bleeding. This study evaluates the effectiveness of suture ligation combined with sclerotherapy for VM management.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 53 patients with pharyngolaryngeal VMs treated between January 2017 and September 2024. Preoperative evaluation included fibreoptic laryngoscopy and imaging. Suture ligation aimed to transfix and obstruct the VM, followed by intralesional bleomycin injection.ResultsThe study included 23 males and 30 females (mean age 50.19 years). Most cases (71.7%) were solitary lesions. A single procedure achieved complete VM resolution in 79.25% of patients, with only one case showing minimal (less than 50%) reduction. Reoperation achieved complete regression in an additional 16.98% (nine cases). No prophylactic or emergent tracheostomy was necessary. One patient had a pre‐existing tracheostomy. No serious postoperative complications occurred.ConclusionSuture ligation combined with sclerotherapy offers a safe, minimally invasive, and effective approach for treating pharyngolaryngeal VMs. This technique achieves high success rates with minimal complications, promoting patient satisfaction.Level of Evidence4.

Introduction

Pharyngolaryngeal venous malformations (VMs) are a rare subtype of congenital vascular anomalies, representing the most common type overall. These malformations consist of abnormal, dilated veins that exhibit continuous growth throughout a patient's life without spontaneous regression [1]. VMs affect approximately 1–2 per 10,000 individuals, with 40% occurring in the head and neck region [1]. Notably, over half can involve the airway and lead to associated respiratory difficulties [2, 3].

While present at birth, symptoms of pharyngolaryngeal VMs often manifest later due to infection, trauma, puberty, or hormonal changes [1]. Manifestations vary based on location and size, encompassing swelling, breathing difficulties, hemorrhage, hoarseness, snoring, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), functional impairment of adjacent organs, and even disfigurement [4].

The optimal treatment strategy for pharyngolaryngeal VMs hinges on several key factors: the patient's overall health status, the specific anatomical location of the VM, and the experience and expertise of the treating surgeon. Traditionally, open surgical excision was the primary treatment for VMs [5]; however, its limitations, particularly for cervicofacial VMs due to their infiltrative nature and the risk of nerve and vascular injury [1], have driven the exploration of less invasive techniques. Therefore, reports of surgical excision for pharyngolaryngeal VMs are scant [6].

Endoscopic laser surgery (CO2, Nd:YAG, KTP) offers minimal tissue disruption and high efficacy [7, 8, 9]. Sclerotherapy, another minimally invasive approach, utilizes various sclerosing agents (anhydrous alcohol, lauromacrogol, bleomycin) delivered via oral, endoscopic, or percutaneous approaches [10, 11]. Both techniques offer advantages over open surgery but may require repeated interventions and are still associated with complications such as bleeding and swelling [8, 12].

This study explores the application of a combined approach—serial suture ligation and bleomycin sclerotherapy—for treating pharyngolaryngeal VMs in a cohort of 53 patients. This approach aims to address the limitations of existing techniques and potentially improve treatment outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Patient Selection

This retrospective study reviewed the medical records of 53 patients diagnosed with pharyngolaryngeal VMs who underwent endoscopic suture ligation and bleomycin sclerotherapy at our institution between January 2017 and September 2024. The cohort comprised 23 males and 30 females, with ages ranging from 17 to 77 years (mean age, 50.19 years). We investigated clinical features, disease progression, anatomical location, physical examination findings, imaging characteristics, treatment responses, complications, and airway status. Informed consent was obtained before surgery.

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Shandong Provincial ENT Hospital Ethics Committee Board. Due to the retrospective nature and anonymized data, individual informed consent was waived.

Study Design

Diagnosis of pharyngolaryngeal VMs was based on clinical history, physical examination, and auxiliary investigations, with confirmation via intraoperative blood aspiration.

Preoperative Evaluations

Preoperative flexible fibreoptic laryngoscopy was used to assess VM size, location, and airway impact (Figure 1A). VMs appeared purplish or dark‐blue, compressible lesions, with some exhibiting peduncles that moved on inspiration/expiration.

Radiological imaging (contrast‐enhanced CT/MRI, ultrasonography) was used to further evaluate VM size, depth of infiltration, and relationships with critical structures such as the vocal folds and arytenoid cartilages. MRI was preferred for its detailed depiction of VM extent and distribution, with lesions typically showing high signal intensity on T2‐weighted images accompanied by the presence of phleboliths appearing as tubular flow voids (Figure 1B).

Surgical Procedures

All surgeries were performed under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation. Depending on the VM location, exposure was achieved using a Boyle‐Davis mouth gag or suspension laryngoscopy (Figure 1C). The VM boundaries were identified by their appearance based on their purple/blue discoloration. Gentle manipulation confirmed the soft, compressible nature of the lesion with blanching on pressure. This was further substantiated through local intralesional puncture and blood aspiration, although no biopsy of the lesions was performed.

A 3‐0 absorbable suture, grasped by a needle holder, was inserted at the VM margin. The suture was applied in a continuous vertical looping fashion, transfixing the entire lesion in a figure‐of‐eight pattern, extending from the surface to deeper layers and one end to the other. Tightening the suture compressed vascular spaces and obstructed blood flow. Due to the low‐flow nature of VMs, any minor oozing encountered upon needle passage was effectively and immediately controlled by the tightening of the sutures, which provided definitive hemostasis without the need for other measures. The number of sutures depended on tumor size. Successful ligation resulted in reduced VM volume, impeded blood flow, and a faded appearance (Figure 1D), effectively partitioning the VM into smaller zones (Video 1).

Bleomycin Sclerotherapy

After endoscopic suture ligation, the sclerotherapy was performed. Bleomycin was used as the sclerosing agent, with each dose comprising 15 mg of bleomycin mixed with 5 mL of 2% lidocaine to create a final bleomycin concentration of 3 mg/mL. A needle was carefully inserted into the partitioned zones of the tumor, and after aspiration confirmed intralesional placement, the bleomycin solution was administered. The injection dose varied according to the size of the treated area post‐ligation, with an average dose of 6–9 mg (2–3 mL) and a maximum single dose of 15 mg. A key advantage of our combined approach is that prior suture ligation allows for this lower, more targeted dose. The ligation partitions the lesion and slows blood flow, which helps contain the sclerosant, achieve high local drug concentrations, and minimize the risk of systemic side effects. All patients were monitored closely for adverse effects following treatment.

Postoperative Management and Follow‐Up

Following surgery, patients were prescribed a three‐day course of cephalosporin and provided with a cold soft diet. Fiberoptic laryngoscopy was performed 3 days post‐surgery to assess initial recovery and monitor edema in the lesion site and surrounding areas. Patients were discharged home to complete 1 week of soft diet once edema had settled.

Follow‐up laryngoscopy was performed 1 month post‐surgery. If residual tumor persisted, the procedure was repeated following a minimum four‐week interval. Treatment completion was defined as no clinical or radiological evidence of VM.

Patient Monitoring and Evaluation

Treatment outcomes were assessed by the senior authors based on clinical manifestations and a review of fibreoptic laryngoscopy as well as postoperative imaging (MRI). Patients were followed up every 1–6 months for comprehensive evaluations. The complete follow‐up period ranged from 6 to 50 months.

Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables, such as age, are presented as mean and range. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. To quantify the precision of our primary efficacy estimates, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for proportions were calculated using the Clopper–Pearson exact method. To compare outcomes between patients with single and mixed lesions, the Chi‐square (χ 2) test or Fisher's exact test was used. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical Findings

A total of 53 patients underwent treatment involving serial suture ligation and intralesional bleomycin injection. The mean age of this patient cohort was 50.19 years, ranging from 17 to 77 years. The gender distribution included 23 males and 30 females.

Notably, 43.4% of patients (n = 23) were asymptomatic, with VMs discovered incidentally during routine pharyngolaryngeal examinations. Among the symptomatic patients, the initial clinical manifestations primarily involved pharyngeal sensation abnormalities (n = 30, 56.6%), which included foreign body sensation or indescribable pharyngeal discomfort (n = 18, 34.0%). Other functional disturbances observed in a subset of patients (n = 12, 22.6%) included sore throat (n = 4), hoarseness (n = 2), OSA and dyspnea (n = 2), and dysphagia (n = 2). Additionally, two patients had a history of blood‐streaked sputum.

Primary Lesion Sites

The anatomical locations of the VMs were determined through clinical examination with fiberoptic laryngoscopy and contrast‐enhanced MRI or CT scans. A comprehensive assessment of lesion infiltration was performed, categorizing cases into single and mixed lesions. Mixed lesions infiltrated more than one anatomical subsite. Thirty‐eight cases were solitary anatomical lesions, including nine cases in the oropharynx (comprising the soft palate, tongue base, and posterior or lateral pharyngeal wall), 11 cases in the larynx (involving the false vocal cord and supraglottic area) (Figure 2), and 18 cases in the hypopharynx (encompassing the aryepiglottic fold, arytenoid area, post cricoid region, posterior pharyngeal wall, and piriform sinus) (Figure 3). Fifteen cases presented as mixed lesions (Figure 4), with six patients exhibiting deep and extensive infiltration into the cervical intermuscular space and, in some instances, the carotid sheath.

Therapeutic Efficacy

Treatment outcomes were assessed based on visual inspection during fiberoptic laryngoscopy and/or postoperative imaging examinations. Our treatment approach demonstrated favorable outcomes in managing VMs. Following the initial procedure, the lesions completely regressed (Level IV) in 42 of 53 patients (79.3%, 95% CI: 65.9%–89.2%). Among the remaining 11 patients, 10 exhibited significant reductions exceeding 50% (Level III), with only one case showing a reduction of less than 50% (Level II). Consequently, the 11 patients with residual lesions underwent subsequent reoperations, which led to the complete disappearance of lesions in 9 cases (Level IV). After all necessary procedures, a total of 51 of the 53 patients achieved complete resolution (cumulative success rate: 96.2%, 95% CI: 87.0%–99.5%). Corresponding with the physical regression of the lesions, all symptomatic patients reported complete resolution or significant improvement of their presenting symptoms.

Two cases exhibited residual lesions after reoperation; however, only one required a third endoscopic procedure, while the other was asymptomatic and opted against further intervention. All cases demonstrated favorable outcomes, with no instances of local recurrence during the follow‐up period. One patient, who had a tracheostomy performed at the referring center due to stridor, was successfully extubated following two treatments. The remaining 52 patients did not require any preventive or emergency tracheotomy.

A sub‐analysis was performed to determine if lesion distribution influenced the number of treatments required. Patients with mixed lesions involving multiple anatomical subsites were significantly more likely to require more than one operation compared to those with single, localized lesions (p < 0.01, see Table 2). This suggests that the extent of the VM is a key predictor of treatment complexity.

Postoperative Complications Analysis

Postoperative complications were categorized as minor and major types. No major postoperative complications, such as significant hemorrhage or airway compromise, occurred in our cohort. Minor complications were common but transient. These included localized edema adjacent to the treatment site, mild sore throat, and pharyngeal foreign body sensation, reported by 88.9% of patients. All minor complications were self‐limiting, resolved spontaneously with a soft diet and supportive care, typically within 7 to 10 days of the procedure, and had no lasting impact on pharyngolaryngeal function. Specifically, no patient developed dysphonia or required a feeding tube for dysphagia.

Discussion

VMs are the most prevalent congenital vascular anomalies, characterized by the enlargement and ectatic dilation of venous channels. Although VMs can manifest throughout the body, they are most frequently encountered in the head and neck region, with approximately 40% of cases localized there. Pharyngolaryngeal VMs, although less common, are marked by their persistence and progressive nature. Typically, asymptomatic when small or situated in non‐critical areas, they may be found incidentally during routine physical examinations. However, significant lesions can lead to clinical symptoms, functional impairment, or obstructive problems as they grow [5, 16]. Symptoms include globus sensation, swelling, stridor, hoarseness, hemoptysis, pain, dysphagia, snoring, and OSA [4, 17, 18, 19]. In severe cases, VMs can pose life‐threatening complications, such as severe hemorrhage and airway obstruction [20].

The management of pharyngolaryngeal VMs remains challenging due to the absence of a standardized treatment protocol. Complete cure is rarely achieved for multifocal lesions, with lesion control being the primary objective [21]. Various treatment modalities, including surgical debulking, sclerotherapy, laser therapy, and combined approaches, have been employed. Menon et al. reported cold steel resection for laryngeal VMs via a suprahyoid pharyngotomy [6]. However, this approach often results in substantial morbidity, incomplete removal, and a high rate of complications due to the propensity of VMs to infiltrate surrounding tissues and the proximity to vital anatomical structures [22]. Consequently, surgical resection of pharyngolaryngeal VMs is seldom reported. Recent years have seen a shift toward minimally invasive methods to address cosmetic concerns and functional impairments.

Several studies have reported favorable outcomes with sclerotherapy and laser therapy for pharyngolaryngeal VMs. Laser therapy, particularly suited for flat VMs [23], is a mainstay for treating mucosal lesions [24, 25]. However, its effectiveness diminishes with bulky VMs due to limited laser penetration [7]. Sclerotherapy, while commonly used, may lead to complications such as blistering, swelling, pulmonary fibrosis, deep ulceration, and nerve injury [26]. Critically, both modalities often require multiple treatment sessions to achieve optimal responses, leading to repeated general anesthetic events and hospitalizations [27, 28]. Mohan et al., for example, reported an average of four treatments with intralesional bleomycin injection (range 2–12) [29]. Manna et al. described a combination of four serial Nd:YAG laser photocoagulation sessions and four bleomycin injections over 2 years [30]. Often, lifelong management is necessary to prevent inevitable re‐expansion [31]. Factors influencing the need for additional sessions include larger lesion size, circumferential airway lesions, mixed lesions, lesion shape, and lesion base size [15, 28, 29, 32, 33, 34].

Intra‐tumoral ligation techniques, employing sutures or copper, have been proposed for treating various vascular lesions, including Kaposiform hemangioendotheliomas and VMs in various anatomical locations [35, 36, 37, 38]. This approach works through mechanical pressure from the sutures, compressing vascular spaces, obstructing afferent and efferent vessels, inducing ischemia and hypoxia in tumor tissues, and contributing to tumor elimination [35, 38]. However, a significant proportion of VMs recur when a suture is not used [39, 40]. This is likely because the complex, sponge‐like network of venous channels can recanalize or develop collateral pathways around simple ligatures, leading to recurrence. The true value of suture ligation, therefore, appears to be as part of a combined‐modality treatment. A direct comparison between suture ligation alone and sclerotherapy is not possible, as to our knowledge, there are no reports on using suture techniques to treat pharyngolaryngeal VMs in isolation.

In our study, we successfully capitalized on this synergistic potential by combining suture ligation with bleomycin injection. This approach addresses the limitations of each technique individually. Suture ligation provides immediate mechanical debulking, interrupts the primary blood supply, and partitions the VM into smaller, contained zones. This slows blood flow, which in turn minimizes the risk of bleeding and prevents systemic washout of the sclerosant. Subsequently, bleomycin injection targets separate VM zones, achieving high drug concentrations that effectively damage vessel walls without the need for ligation [41]. A key advantage of this combined approach is the dose‐sparing effect of suture ligation. By partitioning the VM and slowing blood flow, the sclerosant is contained, allowing for effective endothelial damage with a lower total dose of bleomycin. This likely contributes to the negligible rate of major complications observed in our cohort. This combined mechanism explains the significant reduction in lesion volume observed immediately following our intervention, where 98.1% (52 cases) exhibited a reduction greater than 50%, with only one case (1.9%) showing a reduction below this threshold. This included all but one patient, who presented with substantial VMs spanning from the nasopharynx to the hypopharynx, invading the bilateral parapharyngeal space.

One‐month post‐surgery, all lesions had continued to reduce in volume. Among the cohort, 42 patients exhibited complete disappearance of their lesions, while 11 patients required reoperation. Following an extensive follow‐up period of over 6 months, 79.3% of patients achieved complete lesion resolution after a single surgical procedure. Additionally, 17.0% (nine cases) exhibited complete regression of their lesions after subsequent reoperations. Two cases retained residual lesions; however, one patient underwent his third operation, and the other was asymptomatic and opted not to undergo further procedures. The results of the current study show that the cure rate with a single operation is higher for single lesions than mixed lesions; 92.1% (35/38) of single lesions achieved complete resolution after a one‐time procedure. Patients with mixed lesions involving multiple anatomical subsites were significantly more likely to require more than one operation. Therefore, we hypothesized that the base area is an important factor affecting the surgical outcome.

Pharyngolaryngeal VMs pose unique challenges due to their complex anatomical structures and physiological functions. Inappropriate treatment choices can lead to adverse reactions, including bleeding and swelling [42]. Our results are consistent with previous studies [37, 38, 41], showing that suture ligation significantly reduces the risk of intraoperative and postoperative bleeding. Compared to previous studies reporting that intraoperative bleeding is a common complication at the puncture site, and some lesions require sutures or other methods to stop bleeding, the risk of intraoperative hemorrhage is negligible in our cohort. For larger VMs, especially circumferential airway lesions or lesions involving multiple anatomic areas, it is generally recommended that a prophylactic or emergency tracheotomy be performed to provide a safe airway [42, 43]. Previous studies have advocated a staged surgical strategy to avoid airway swelling and obstruction [23, 44], suggesting that multiple sites be treated in separate operations, with each operation focusing on only one anatomic site [28, 32]. Our treatment strategy proved effective across varying VM sizes and anatomical sites, avoiding laryngeal obstruction due to postoperative swelling and with no patients requiring a tracheostomy, prolonged intubation, or intensive care admission. The combined suture ligation and bleomycin sclerotherapy approach offers a minimally invasive, low‐risk solution with clear therapeutic benefits.

Limitations

Despite the promising outcomes, this study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single‐center design with a relatively modest sample size may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, the absence of a control group treated with a single modality prevents a direct comparative analysis to definitively establish the superiority of our combined technique. Third, the variable follow‐up period is a significant limitation. While encouraging, the absence of recurrence within our 6‐ to 50‐month timeframe may not be sufficient to capture late recurrences, a well‐known characteristic of venous malformations. Finally, treatment success was primarily determined by visual assessment of lesion regression rather than standardized functional outcome measures, such as validated dysphagia or voice questionnaires. Therefore, future prospective, multicenter, controlled trials with extended, standardized follow‐up and the inclusion of functional outcomes are warranted to corroborate our findings.

Conclusion

The combination of suture ligation and bleomycin sclerotherapy represents a safe, effective, and minimally invasive approach for the management of pharyngolaryngeal VMs. This synergistic approach offers significant therapeutic benefits, including a low risk of bleeding and swelling, and achieves excellent medium‐term lesion control. While longer‐term data are needed to assess the risk of late recurrence, our study demonstrates that this combined therapy is a highly promising treatment strategy.
