Single- and dual-target CAR-T cells targeting HBsAg and GPC3 to balance safety and antitumor efficacy in HBV-positive hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Quangang Chen, Zihan Chen, Deqin Xu, Hu Wang, Ankang Hu, Zhihui Hu, Peng Gao, Huizhong Li, Yan Li, Liang Sun, Keyu Gao, Hui Hou, Jiehui Di, Keli Chai, Bao Zhu, Qing Zhang, Gang Wang, Wei Xu, Zhengxiang Han, Junnian Zheng, Yong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAntigen-escape and on-target off-tumor effects limit the application of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells in solid tumors. Glypican-3 (GPC3) and hepatitis B virus (HBV) are two emerging therapeutic targets for CAR-T cell therapy in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). In this study, we generated single-antigen CARs against HBV surface antigen (HBsAg) and GPC3. HBsAg-CAR-T specifically targeted HBV-positive HCC cells, showing superior antitumor activity compared to GPC3-CAR-T. However, concerns arose about off-tumor liver cytolysis by HBsAg-CAR-T due to the membrane localization of HBsAg in non-malignant liver cells. Moreover, HBsAg and GPC3 were heterogeneously expressed in approximately 27 percent of tested patients with HCC. To address these challenges, we developed bicistronic HBsAg-GPC3-CAR-T cells that selectively recognized double-positive HCC cells, thereby reducing off-tumor toxicity. Co-administration of HBsAg-CAR-T and GPC3-CAR-T cells exhibited superior anticancer efficacy over single CAR-T use in heterogeneous HCC cells and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) HCC xenografts. Our study highlights the potential of HBsAg/GPC3 dual-CAR approaches to enhance therapeutic outcomes and safety in HCC treatment.Keywords: dual chimeric antigen receptor T cell, heterogeneous hepatocellular carcinoma, on-target off-tumor, hepatitis B surface antigen, glypican-3

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell therapy has demonstrated exceptional efficacy in hematological malignancies, yet its application to solid tumors, which constitute over 90% of cancer cases, faces significant challenges. The lack of ideal solid tumor antigens is a major obstacle, with current targets exhibiting on-target off-tumor toxicity or enabling immune evasion through antigen escape by tumors.1

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) ranks as the third leading cause of cancer deaths worldwide.2 Early-stage HCC often goes undetected, limiting eligibility for surgical resection or liver transplantation. While immune checkpoint inhibitors such as monoclonal antibodies targeting either PD-1 or PD-L1 have emerged as promising treatments, many patients exhibit limited response to checkpoint blockade.3 Alternatively, CAR-T cells targeting HCC antigens like alpha-fetoprotein (AFP),4 tyrosine kinase receptor c-Met,5 natural-killer group 2 member D ligands (NKG2DL),6 type I transmembrane glycoprotein CD147,7 and glypican-3 (GPC3) have been developed. GPC3, in particular, is a leading CAR-T target for liver cancer, with over forty phase I clinical trials for advanced HCC.8,9 However, GPC3 expression in normal tissues such as the lung, kidney, mammary gland, and placenta raises concerns about potential on-target off-tumor toxicity.10

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) belongs to the Hepadnaviridae family. It tropically infects hepatocytes and causes DNA damage, chronic inflammation, genetic mutations, chromosomal changes, and epigenetic alterations in infected liver cells, inducing HCC.11 The burden of HBV infection closely correlates with HCC development, contributing to over 50% of all cases and up to 85% in Africa and East Asia.12,13 During infection, HBV frequently integrates its DNA into the human genome,14 enabling continuous expression of viral antigens in host cells.15 HBV surface antigen (HBsAg) is expressed in both malignant and nonmalignant hepatocytes, and exhibits plasma membrane localization in some HCC cells.16 This unique surface expression of HBsAg presents a potential target for adoptive T cell therapies of HCC,17,18 though with some risk of off-tumor effects on HBV-positive non-malignant hepatocytes.

Several dual-targeting CAR-T strategies have been developed to improve anti-tumor safety or efficacy.19 One notable approach is the bicistronic trans-signaling strategy, in which the first CAR-linked T cell activation signal is physically separated from a second CAR-linked co-stimulatory signal. This design ensures robust immune activation occurs only upon the recognition and binding of both tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), minimizing unexpected reactivity against normal tissues expressing a single antigen.20 On the other hand, co-transduction of two single-targeting CAR molecules or mixed infusion of two single-targeting CAR-T cell populations enables the efficient killing of cancer cells expressing either target antigen, thereby reinforcing the vulnerability of heterogeneous tumors to CAR-T cell treatment.

In this study, we generated HBsAg-CAR-T cells and compared their anti-tumor efficacy with GPC3-CAR-T cells. To address the possible off-tumor effect of each single CAR-T, we developed a bicistronic HBsAg-GPC3-CAR-T system to enhance the specificity of HCC cell targeting. Biopsy analysis revealed heterogeneous distribution of HBsAg and GPC3 in certain patients with HBV-positive HCC. We therefore explored the therapeutic potential of pooled HBsAg- and GPC3- single targeting CAR-T cells in vitro and in vivo.

Results

Generation of HBsAg-CAR-T and GPC3-CAR-T cells

Surface expression is a prerequisite for the target antigen to be recognized by CAR-T cells. Initially, we examined the expression and subcellular localization of HBsAg in different HCC cells, including GPC3+HBV+ cell HepG2.2.15, GPC3+HBV− cell Huh7, and GPC3−HBV- cell SK-HEP-1. Using the sodium-potassium ATPase (Na/K ATPase) as a positive control of membrane protein, we exclusively detected HBsAg in the membrane fraction of HepG2.2.15 cells (Figure 1A). To further assess whether HBsAg is exposed on the outer surface of the membrane, we performed immunofluorescence staining on fixed HepG2.2.15 cells without permeabilization. The cells exposed to HBsAg antibody exhibited a membranous punctate or dot-like staining (Figure 1B), indicating the presence of HBsAg on the outer surface of HepG2.2.15 cells. Consequently, we concluded that HBsAg is a potential target for CAR-T cells.

Given the recent indications of the antitumor activity of GPC3-CAR-T cells, we aimed to develop HBsAg-CAR-T cells and evaluate their effect in HCC, using GPC3-CAR-T as a positive control. The lentiviral expression vectors comprised a signal peptide, a myc tag, an HBsAg- or GPC3-specific scFv, a CD8α hinge region, a CD8α transmembrane region, tandem 4-1BB co-activation domains, and the intracellular domain of CD3ξ (Figure 1C). A CAR-removed scFv sequence served as the negative control. Human peripheral blood T cells were transduced with Control-CAR, HBsAg-CAR, or GPC3-CAR lentivirus, respectively. The successful CAR-T cells were validated by positive expression of CD3ζ (Figure 1D) and a myc-tag on the membrane surface of T cells (Figure 1E).

Ten days post-transduction, T cells expressing HBsAg-CAR or GPC3-CAR ranged from 77.3% to 74.8%, while the control-CAR showed slightly higher transduction efficiency (Figure 1F). Notably, over 65% of T cells were CD8-positive, irrespective of the type of CAR transduction (Figure 1G). Furthermore, we examined the markers related to central memory T cells (TCM) and found that nearly half of the cells (ranging from 42.4% to 47.6%) were central memory T cells (CD62L+CD45RO+), with no significant difference among the transduced CARs (Figure 1H).

HBsAg-CAR-T cells reveal a more robust tumor-inhibition effect than GPC3-CAR-T cells

To investigate whether the CAR-T cells could specifically recognize target cells and acquire effector cell functions, HepG2.2.15, Huh7, and SK-HEP-1 cells were co-cultured with different CAR-T cells, respectively. Compared with control-CAR-T cells, the expression of IL-2, IFN-γ, and granzyme B was significantly up-regulated in GPC3-CAR-T when co-cultured with Huh7 (HBV−GPC3+) or HepG2.2.15 (HBV+GPC3+) cells, while HBsAg-CAR-T cells could only be activated in co-culture with HepG2.2.15 cells (Figure 2A). There was no increase in cytokine production when CAR-T cells were co-cultured with SK-HEP-1 cells, which are void of GPC3 and HBsAg.

For in vitro functional analysis, GPC3-CAR-T cells could efficiently lyse Huh7 and HepG2.2.15 cells, but not the SK-HEP-1 cells, while the control-CAR-T cells exerted no cytotoxicity on these HCC cells. Notably, HBsAg-CAR-T cells specifically killed HepG2.2.15 ,and the cytotoxicity was greater than that of GPC3-CAR-T cells (Figure 2B). The killing efficiency was positively correlated with the E:T ratios (Figure S1A). On the other hand, elevated LDH release was detected in the medium with antigen-targeting CAR-T cells cocultured with Huh7 and HepG2.2.15. Maximum LDH release was observed in HepG2.2.15 medium in the presence of HBsAg-CAR-T cells (Figure S1A). These results suggested that HBsAg-CAR-T could specifically recognize and kill HBV-positive HCC cells, and demonstrated superior tumor-killing efficacy compared to the GPC3-CAR-T cells.

To assess the anti-tumor potency of CAR-T cells in vivo, we established HepG2.2.15 and SK-HEP-1 xenografts in nude mice and treated them with the Control-CAR-T, GPC3-CAR-T, and HBsAg-CAR-T cells, respectively (Figure 2C). As expected, SK-HEP-1 xenograft mice receiving CAR-T cells showed progressive tumor growth (Figures 2D and 2E). In HepG2.2.15 xenografts, both GPC3-CAR-T and HBsAg-CAR-T suppressed tumor growth, with HBsAg-CAR-T cells demonstrating a more robust inhibitory effect than GPC3-CAR-T cells. At the endpoint of the experiment, the lowest tumor burden was observed among the HBsAg-CAR-T cells-treated groups (Figure 2F), indicating the superior antitumor efficacy of the HBsAg-CAR-T cells in vivo.

In vivo expansion and persistence of transferred T cells correlate with tumor regression.21 We next examined T cells in the peripheral blood of mice bearing xenograft tumors. Fifteen days after T cell infusion, HepG2.2.15 xenograft mice treated with HBsAg-CAR-T cells revealed slightly increased amounts of CAR-T cells, including CD4+ and CD8+ cells, compared to mice treated with GPC3-CAR-T cells (Figures 2G–2I). Consistently, we observed obvious T cell infiltration in HepG2.2.15 xenograft tumors treated with GPC3-CAR-T or HBsAg-CAR-T cells, but not in SK-HEP-1 xenograft tumors (Figure 2J). There was no specific staining in HepG2.2.15 xenograft tumors treated with Control-CAR-T cells (Figure 2K).

Besides the curative effect, the safety issue remains the major challenge during adoptive T cell therapy. To better understand the off-tumor toxicities of CAR-T cells, we evaluated the changes in body weight and damage to vital organs. None of the CAR-T infusions caused detectable loss of body weight (Figure S1B), or damage to vital organs (Figures S1C and S1D), despite both GPC3- and HBsAg-CAR-T cells successfully inhibiting HepG2.2.15 xenograft tumor growth.

HBV-positive HCC patient-derived CAR-T cells recognize and kill target cells efficiently

Chronic HBV infection is known to impair T cell response, including reduced production of IFN-γ and IL-2, as well as the loss of cytotoxicity and impaired proliferative capacities.22 To further investigate the functionality of CAR-T cells derived from patients with HBV-positive HCC, we collected PBMCs from three patients and generated CAR-T cells, which were then co-cultured with HepG2.2.15 cells to evaluate their lytic effect. We found that patients-derived HBsAg-CAR-T cells produced more inflammatory cytokines such as IL-2, IFN-γ, and granzyme B (Figure 3A), exhibited a strong cytolytic activity when exposed to HepG2.2.15 cells (Figures 3B and 3C). More importantly, the killing efficiency was equivalent to that of healthy donor-derived CAR-T cells (Figure 3D). Moreover, HCC patient-derived HBsAg-CAR-T cells consistently revealed an augmented anti-tumor effect compared to GPC3-CAR-T cells from the same patient (Figures 3A–3D). We also assessed the specificity of patient-derived CAR-T cells by treating SK-HEP-1 and Huh7 cells. Similar to the CAR-T cells from healthy donors, patient-derived CAR-Ts did not respond to SK-HEP-1, although GPC3-CAR-Ts could be activated by Huh7 cells (Figure S2). Collectively, patient-derived CAR-T cells can specifically and efficiently recognize and kill targeted HCC cells, indicating their potential for clinical application.

PD-L1 antibody synergistically boosts CAR-T cell therapy

The expression of PD-L1 on tumor cells can compromise the efficacy of T cell therapies through the PD-L1/PD-1 axis.23 Our findings revealed that nearly 100% of HepG2.2.15, Huh7, and SK-HEP-1cells were PD-L1 positive (Figure 4A), and approximately 90% of CAR-T cells were PD-1 positive (Figure 4B). Subsequently, we investigated the impact of PD-L1 blockade on the antitumor efficacy conferred by HBsAg-CAR-T cells. To achieve this, we utilized Durvalumab (DUR), an FDA-approved anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibody used in the treatment of bladder, lung, and biliary tract cancer, to block PD-L1 in HCC. In our in vitro experiments with HepG2.2.15 cells, PD-L1 blockade significantly reinforced the cytotoxic activity of HBsAg-CAR-T cells (Figure 4C). Furthermore, to assess the combined effect of DUR and CAR-T in HCC in vivo, we established a HepG2.2.15 xenograft model in NCG mice and treated them with HBsAg-CAR-Ts alone or in combination with DUR (Figure 4D). Notably, mice treated with HBsAg-CAR-T cells alone exhibited potent inhibition of tumor growth, while the concomitant PD-L1 blockade further diminished the tumors (Figures 4E and 4F). Additionally, compared with CAR-T alone, the combined treatment with the PD-L1 antibody promoted the expansion of both CD4+ and CD8+ CAR-T cells (Figures 4G and 4H), and increased CAR-T cell infiltration into tumors (Figures 4I and 4J). Importantly, the combined treatment demonstrated negligible off-tumor toxicity, with no apparent changes detected in body temperature, body weight, liver function, and organ dissections (Figures S3A–S3C). These findings suggest that PD-L1 blockade could synergistically enhance the antitumor effects of HBsAg-CAR-T cells.

Development and functional characterization of bicistronic HBsAg-GPC3-CAR-T cells for HCC

To further verify the clinical application value of HBsAg-CAR-T, we assessed the expression of HBsAg and GPC3 in tumor tissues and their matched paracancerous samples from a tissue array including samples from 32 patients with HCC, and needle biopsy specimens from another 24 patients with HCC (Figure 5A). Among 56 patients who were diagnosed with HBV-positive HCC, 53 showed positive expression of HBsAg in tumor tissues. In detail, 16 out of 56 samples exhibited surface HBsAg only in tumor tissues instead of matched paracancerous tissues, while another 13 samples displayed surface expression of HBsAg only in paracancerous tissues. Besides, there were 9 samples showing surface HBsAg in both tumor and paracancerous tissues, with the remaining 18 samples showing non-membrane cytoplasmic expression (Figure 5B). This finding suggests that solely targeting HBsAg might lead to the on-target lysis of non-malignant hepatocytes and possible liver failure when treated with HBsAg-CAR-T cells. Moreover, GPC3 was tested in 47 samples and all of them showed positive staining in tumor tissues. We then revisited the expression level of GPC3 in open-access databases with mRNA24,25 and protein expression.26 Consistent with previous reports,10 GPC3 mRNA is expressed in lung, kidney and several other normal tissues. Notably, the mRNA levels of gpc3 in normal lung tissue are comparable to those observed in HCC tissues (Figures S4A and S4B). More importantly, GPC3 protein levels in kidney, lung, pancreas, and uterus are slightly, though not significantly, higher than in HCC tissues (Figure S4C), implicating possible off-tumor side effects with GPC3-CAR-T treatment.

To avoid the possible off-tumor effect conferred by each si-CAR-T singly targeting HBsAg and GPC3, we then generated a bicistronic CAR (referred to as Bicis-CAR), with the myc-tagged HBsAg scFv being linked to a CD28 transmembrane domain and CD3ζ signaling domain, which was separated by a P2A ribosomal skip element followed by flag-tagged GPC3 scFv fused with another CD28 TM domain and the 41BB signaling domain. This comprises a second-generation CAR in which the concomitant binding of both antigens enables a robust and efficient T cell activation, meanwhile allowing the once-for-all delivery by a single lentivirus (Figure 5C). Successful CAR-T cells were then validated by positive detection of Flag- and Myc-tag on the membrane surface of T cells (Figure 5D), with approximate 70 percent cells positively expressing CARs (Figure 5E). To further assess the antigen specificity, we generated GPC3 CRISPR-knockout cells in HepG2.2.15 (HepG2.2.15GPC3KO) (Figure S5), thus creating a testing cell system including SK-HEP-1 (GPC3−HBV-), HepG2 (GPC3+HBV−), HepG2.2.15GPC3KO (GPC3−HBV+) and HepG2.2.15 (GPC3+HBV+). Our data showed that Bicis-CAR-T cells produced more cytokines such as IL-2, IFN-γ, and granzyme B when cocultured with the HBsAg/GPC3 double-positive HepG2.2.15 cells, while barely responding to single-positive HCC cells (Figure 5F). Consistently, these Bicis-CAR-T cells were capable of eradicating double-positive cells only, with minimal sacrifice of cytolytic activity (Figure 5G). Taken together, the bicistronic HBsAg-GPC3-CAR-T cells provided a substantially increased selectivity to HCC cells and reduced theoretical off-tumor risk in HBV-positive HCC.

To further assess the anti-tumor efficacy of Bicis-CAR-T cells in vivo, we established HepG2.2.15 cell xenograft models in NCG mice and treated them with control-CAR-T, GPC3-CAR-T, HBsAg-CAR-T, and Bicis-CAR-T cells, respectively (Figure 6A). Despite exhibiting a slightly weaker anti-tumor potential than GPC3-CAR-T and HBsAg-CAR-T cells, Bicis-CAR-T cells demonstrated a significant tumor growth inhibition effect (Figures 6B and 6C), which is consistent with their in vitro killing efficiency. The anti-tumor activity correlated with CAR-T cell infiltration in the tumor tissues (Figure 6D). Similar to the effects observed with GPC3-CAR-T and HBsAg-CAR-T cells, treatment with Bicis-CAR-T cells resulted in negligible changes in body temperature, body weight, liver function, and organ dissection findings (Figures 6E and 6F).

Pooled-CAR-T cells demonstrate superior inhibition in HCC cells with heterogeneous expression of HBsAg and GPC3

One of the major challenges for solid tumor therapy is the heterogeneous expression of TAAs. Although GPC3-CAR-T cells have revealed promising therapeutic responses,9 some histopathology studies revealed significant intra-tumoral heterogeneity of GPC3 expression in HCC.27 We then assessed the expression of GPC3 and HBsAg in HCC tissues and their matched paracancerous samples from the tissue samples mentioned above. 47 out of 56 HCC biopsies demonstrated positive expression of both GPC3 and HBsAg. 17 HCC samples exhibited double-positive staining for HBsAg and GPC3, while another 13 HCC samples displayed spotty heterogeneous expression of them (Figures 7A and 7C). Interestingly, we noticed that GPC3 exhibited strong positiveness in some fibrosis-like regions (6 out of 9 in cancer biopsies and 4 out of 7 in paracancerous biopsies), whereby HBsAg showed reduced expression or an altered distribution pattern compared to surrounding cells (Figures 7B and 7C). These findings suggested that targeting only one antigen may be ineffective to eliminate the tumor, leading to antigen escape. This prompted us to explore the combined CAR-T cell treatments simultaneously targeting both antigens in HCC. To model the heterogeneous expression of HBsAg and GPC3 in vitro, we used a cell mixture consisting of 50% HepG2 cells (GPC3+) and 50% HepG2.2.15GPC3KO cells (HBsAg+) (referred to as Cell-mix) and treated them with single-targeting CAR-T cells or their combination. As expected, although the GPC3-CAR-T and HBsAg-CAR-T efficiently killed HepG2 cells and HepG2.2.15GPC3KO cells, respectively, the pooled CAR-T cells produced significantly more IL-2, IFN-γ and granzyme B than either of the single targeting-CAR-T cells when co-cultured with Cell-mix (Figure 7D), and illustrated the strongest cytolytic activity toward Cell-mix (Figure 7E). These findings implies that co-administration of GPC3-CAR-T and HBsAg-CAR-T cells represents an effective regimen for CAR-T therapy of hepatocarcinoma.

Validation of dual-CAR-T strategies in vivo

To further determine the efficacy of bicistronic CAR-T and pooled-CAR-T cells in vivo, we first established an artificial heterogeneous tumor xenograft model with subcutaneous injection of the Cell-mix in NCG mice, followed by treatment with different CAR-T cells, respectively (Figure 8A). Consistent with the in vitro findings, xenograft tumors progressed rapidly in the Control-CAR-T and Bicis-CAR-T group in compared with single-targeting CAR-Ts, indicating that limited T cell activation upon single-positive HCC cells could be achieved (Figures 8B and 8C). Moreover, tumor growth was significantly delayed in Pooled-CAR-T treatment group (Figures 8B and 8C), with more infiltrated T cells in the tumors (Figure 8D). We also observed that the mice undergoing different CAR-T treatments remained in good condition and there was no significant difference in body weight and temperature throughout the experiment (Figure 8E). The vital organs, such as kidney, liver, spleen and lung did not suffer detectable damage (Figure 8F).

Discussion

Despite the notable success of CAR-T therapy in specific subsets of lymphoma and leukemia, single-targeting CAR-T cell therapy faces limitations in both solid tumors and blood cancers.28 On one hand, some cancer cells may evade the treatment by losing or masking the targeted antigen, resulting in antigen escape and subsequent cancer recurrence in patients.29 On the other hand, CAR T cell therapy can cause severe or life-threatening adverse events, such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS) or on-target off-tumor effects. Dual-targeting CAR-T cells have shown great potential to address these challenges. We here generated single- and bicistronic-CAR-T cells targeting GPC3 and HBsAg. Notably, HBsAg CAR-T cells exhibited superior anti-cancer efficacy compared to GPC3 CAR-T cells. The bicistronic CAR-T cells demonstrate potent and highly specific anticancer efficacy against GPC3+HBsAg+ double-positive HCC cells, with minimal activity against single-positive cells. Furthermore, the coadministration of HBsAg-CAR-T and GPC3-CAR-T cells significantly improved anticancer efficacy compared to the single use of either CAR-T cells in heterogeneous HCC.

A major difference between solid tumors and hematological malignancies is that solid tumors rarely express TSA.30 Instead, most solid tumors express TAAs found at low levels in normal tissues, resulting in on-target off-tumor toxicity sometimes.31 This prompts us to consider exogenous antigens that can be acquired specifically by cancer cells and expressed on the membrane. HBV is a hepatotropic virus, which infects hepatocytes and is usually maintained in HCC with integrated viral genomes. As a viral surface protein, HBsAg can either form viral particles or may reach the cell surface by physiological membrane exchange.32 HBsAg-targeting CAR-T cells have shown promise for anti-virus purposes33,34 and have recently been tested for the treatment of CD39-positive HCC.18 Besides, HBsAg-specific T cell receptor-engineered T cells (TCR-T) have also been reported to notably reduce HBV levels without exacerbation of liver inflammation or other toxicity in a patient with HCC metastases, although the HBsAg-TCR-T did not reach an objective response in the subject with end-stage metastatic disease.35 We here assessed the effect of HBsAg-CAR-T cells on HBV-positive HCC progression compared to GPC3-CAR-T cells. Our results demonstrated that primary human T cells transduced with HBsAg-CAR molecules can specifically kill HBV-positive HCC cells in vitro and suppress tumor growth of HCC in xenograft mouse models. Importantly, HBsAg-CAR-T cells demonstrated more robust tumor-suppressive potential than GPC3-CAR-T cells. Our findings suggest that HBsAg could be an equivalent or even better choice than GPC3 for the development of adoptive T cell therapy in HBV-positive HCC.

Chronic HBV infection is commonly associated with immune suppression, involving monocytes/macrophages, dendritic cells, natural killer cells, and T cells.22 During persistent HBV infection, T cells often show weak or absent effector cytotoxic activity because of exhaustion, characterized by the reduced level of IFN-γ and IL-2.36,37 Furthermore, exhausted T cells tend to express more inhibitory receptors such as PD-1, and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), which perhaps further block the immune response of HBsAg-CAR-T to target cells. To test whether HBV incapacitates T cells in patients with HCC, we specifically compared the CAR-T cells from HBV-positive patients and healthy. Encouragingly, our study revealed that CAR-T cells derived from healthy donors and HCC patients had similar immune activity. To this end, we also tested whether the immuno-checkpoint inhibitor could further increase the killing efficiency of lymphocytes. Like what has been observed for GPC3-CAR-T cells, PD-L1 blockade significantly enhanced the anti-HCC effect of HBsAg-CAR-T cells, highlighting the combined regimen of ICI with CAR-T cells as a future therapeutic approach in HCC.

Intriguingly, Marta et al. reported that HBsAg exhibited differential subcellular localization in tumor and adjacent areas. In paracancerous tissue or normal livers, HBsAg demonstrated a diffuse, scattered, or spotty cytoplasmic pattern, while it was located at the plasma membrane in malignant hepatocytes.16 However, other evidence supported that partial HBsAg exhibited surface localization in non-malignant HBV-positive liver cells.32,33 The membrane localization of HBsAg in HCC versus normal liver cells determines the potential applicability of HBsAg-CAR-T cells for HCC treatment. To investigate this, we examined HBsAg expression in tumors and paracancerous regions from two cohorts with a total of 56 patients with HBV-positive HCC. Our findings revealed the cancer cell-specific surface expression of HBsAg in 16 patients (28.6%), while another 22 patients (39.3%) showed surface localization either in paracancerous cells only or in both regions. This discovery raises concerns about the risk of on-target lysis of infected hepatocytes, as massive hepatocyte death can be fatal due to liver failure. Additionally, we observed heterogeneous expression of GPC3 and HBsAg in 13 out of 47 HCC samples in which both antigens were examined, raising concerns about possible antigen escape-associated tumor relapse following single-CAR-T treatment. Therefore, we explored the possibility of deploying dual-CAR strategies to ensure the specificity of CAR-T cells to target tumor, or to enhance CAR-T efficacy and avoid potential antigen escape for HCC immunotherapy.

Dual-CAR systems often employ AND/OR-gate logics to achieve precise activation.38 Biological AND gating requires simultaneous recognition of two or more antigens for thorough CAR-T cell activation, providing a double safety lock against single TAA-associated on-target off-tumor effects. Alternatively, OR gating allows CAR-T cells expressing multiple scFvs to be activated upon the recognition of either antigen. In mouse models, bispecific CAR-T cells targeting HER2 and IL13Rα2 showed greater efficacy against glioblastoma versus single CARs.39 However, transducing multiple CARs into one T cell frequently leads to preferential expansion of cells expressing only one dominant CAR.40 Additionally, sequential CAR-T therapy can promote antigen escape for both targets, as evidenced by CD19 and CD22 escape after sequential CD19 then CD22 CAR-T treatment.41 In this study, the bicistronic CAR-T cells were designed to simultaneously target both HBsAg and GPC3 for full activation. This design offers a theoretically safer strategy, although their antitumor efficacy was slightly compromised compared to HBsAg-CAR-T cells in uniformly double-positive xenografts. In contrast, the pooled CAR-T strategy demonstrated superior antitumor efficacy. These findings highlight the importance of patient stratification: reserving bicistronic CAR-T cells for predominantly double-positive tumors while considering pooled or sequential single-targeting CAR-T strategies for heterogeneous HCC.

In summary, our results demonstrate that CAR-T cells targeting HBsAg could be a more efficient option than GPC3-CAR-T cells for the treatment of HBV-positive HCC. Considering the heterogeneous expression of HBsAg and GPC3 in tumor tissues from certain patients with HBV+ HCC, our study validates a more potent HBsAg/GPC3 single-targeting CAR-T pool approach to overcome heterogeneous antigen-associated antigen escape in refractory and relapsed HCC. Additionally, we provide bicistronic HBsAg-GPC3-CAR-T cells as an option to reduce the possible on-target off-tumor organ toxicity.

Limitations of the study

We here established an HBsAg and GPC3 dual-CAR system using AND/OR gate logic and evaluated their anti-HCC potential in heterogeneous hepatocarcinoma. The bicistronic HBsAg-GPC3-CAR-T was designed as a theoretically safer anti-HCC approach by simultaneously targeting human GPC3 and HBsAg, thereby reducing on-target off-tumor killing of non-tumor HBsAg-positive hepatocytes or normal tissues expressing GPC3. However, tissues from immunodeficient mouse models lack the corresponding human antigens, making them inadequate for definitive safety evaluation. Therefore, rigorous assessment in more physiologically relevant models such as humanized liver chimeric mice or HBV-competent non-human primates, along with thorough evaluation of CAR-T cell biodistribution in non-tumor peripheral organs after infusion, will be essential to support clinical translation for HBV-positive HCC. Moreover, the PDX model used exhibits heterogeneous HBsAg and GPC3 expression, and uniformly double-positive models are lacking, limiting our ability to fully define the maximal potential of the bicistronic AND-gate design. These limitations partly explain the modest efficacy observed, which is consistent with AND-gate logic, and highlight the inherent safety-efficacy trade-off. This trade-off underscores the need for patient stratification and further optimization to restore potency in more physiologically relevant hosts.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

Human liver cancer cell lines (Huh7, HepG2.2.15, and SK-HEP-1) and HEK-293T cells were purchased from the Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All cell lines tested negative for mycoplasma contamination by PCR. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) and supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (10082147, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All cells were grown in a humidified atmosphere at 37°C and 5% CO2.

Animals

All mice were purchased from GemPharmatech (Jiangsu, China), and housed in the specific-pathogen-free animal facilities of the Laboratory Animal Center of Xuzhou Medical University under conditions of 12-h light/12-h dark cycle, at a temperature of 22 ± 2°C and humidity of 50 ± 10%. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Xuzhou Medical University (Approval No. 202007A053) and were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Animal experiments were designed and reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 (https://arriveguidelines.org). All experiments in this model used male mice. The potential influence of sex on antitumor efficacy was not evaluated.

Human participants

This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Xuzhou Medical University (XYFY2021-KL271-02) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Human blood samples were collected from two distinct participant groups. HBV positive HCC patients: peripheral blood and tumor tissues were obtained from three previously untreated patients (three male, aged 66, 50 and 52 years) diagnosed with HBV positive HCC at the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (Xuzhou, China). Tumor stages were Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) B (n = 2) and C (n = 1). Additionally, one patient (male, 59 years) underwent hepatectomy, and fresh tumor tissue was used to establish a patient derived xenograft (PDX) model. All patients provided written informed consent prior to inclusion. Anonymous healthy donors: buffy coats from four healthy donors (two male, two female; age range 25 45 years) were obtained from the Xuzhou Blood Center. At the time of donation, all donors provided written informed consent for their samples to be used for research purposes. Samples were de identified by the blood center prior to delivery to our laboratory; therefore, no further demographic or clinical information was available to the research team. All human data were de identified before analysis. Owing to the small sample size, the influence of sex on CAR T cell function could not be statistically assessed; this limitation is addressed in the discussion section.

HCC PDX model

To generate the patient-derived HCC xenograft (customized from Shanghai Model Organisms Center), fresh tissue samples from surgery were immediately collected from a 59 years old male patient who was diagnosed with HBV-positive grade-II HCC, with no previous treatment before hepatectomy operation. Tumor tissues were cut into pieces of 0.2–0.3 cm in diameter and implanted into NCG mice subcutaneously to generate the xenograft tumor (passage 0, P0). P0 tumors were resected from mice upon reaching a size of ∼1000 mm3, cut into pieces, and implanted into the next generation of NCG mice, as previously described. The P1 xenograft models were then serially passaged to P2 and P3 generations. On day 7 after implantation with P3 tumors, 25 mice with relatively equivalent tumor size were randomly assigned into 5 groups. A total of 5×106 CAR-T cells per mouse were injected intravenously into the mice. All mice were administered IL-2 (2000 U/mouse) daily for the course of CAR-T therapy. During the treatment, the blood was obtained from the tail vein and used for determining the proliferation of transfused CAR-T cells by Flow cytometry. Tumor size was measured every three days with digital calipers. The tumor volume was calculated. At the endpoint, mice were euthanized. Excised tumor was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin.

Human participants

This study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Xuzhou Medical University (XYFY2021-KL271-02), and followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Human blood samples were collected from anonymous healthy donors or HBV-positive HCC patients from the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (Xuzhou, China). All participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion, and all human data were de-identified before analysis.

Method details

Isolation and culture of human PBMC

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from buffy coats by Ficoll-Hypaque gradient separation. PBMCs were cultured in X-VIVO™ 15 medium (02-053Q, Lonza, Switzerland), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin.

Construction of CAR-encoding lentiviral vector

Anti-HBsAg scFv and anti-human GPC3 scFv were fused with the CD8α leader, the hinge and transmembrane domains of human CD8α and cytoplasmic regions of 4-1BB and CD3ζ to construct a second-generation CAR, which was then cloned into the lentiviral vector pRRL. The same construct with absent scFv was used as the negative control. The three constructs were referred to as HBsAg-CAR, GPC3-CAR, and Control-CAR, respectively. The HBsAg (HBsAg scFv and CD3ζ) and the GPC3 (GPC3 scFv and 41BB cytoplasmic region) chimeric costimulatory receptor were fused using P2A to generate Bicis-CAR. The lentiviral particles were produced by the transfection of 293T packaging cells with the lentiviral vector pRRL encoding HBsAg, GPC3, control, or Dual CAR together with delta 8.9 packaging plasmid and envelope plasmid VSVG. After 48 h, the culture supernatants containing lentiviral particles were harvested and concentrated through ultracentrifugation at 25,000 rpm for 2 h at 4°C. Concentrated lentivirus was resuspended with X-VIVO™ 15 serum-free media.

Transduction of recombinant lentiviral particles

PBMCs were activated with CD3/CD28 Dynabeads (11131D, Thermo Fisher, USA) at a ratio of 2:1 and then cultured in X-VIVO™ 15 medium containing IL-2 (GMP-101-02, PrimeGene, China. 500 U/mL) in 48-well plates. After 48 h, activated PBMCs were then infected with lentiviral particles at the multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 50 in the presence of RetroNectin (T110A, Takara Bio Inc). Beads were removed at 7 days after stimulation. The CAR-T cells were expanded and maintained with a cell density of 5×105 cells/ml.

Flow cytometry

For the analysis of lentivirus transduction efficiency, 5×105 HBsAg-CAR-T, GPC3-CAR-T, or Control-CAR-T cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The fixed cells were suspended in the staining buffer (phosphate-buffered saline containing 1% BSA) and stained with an anti-myc tag antibody (#9402, Cell Signaling Technology, USA). To examine cell subset and memory phenotype, CAR-T cells were stained with fluorescent-labeled antibodies against CD3 (340542, BD Biosciences, USA), CD4 (300550, BioLegend, USA), CD8 (344722, BioLegend, USA), CD45RO (304228, BioLegend, USA), and CD62-L (980702, BioLegend, USA). After that, cells were detected using a fluorescence-activated cell sorter (BD FACS Canto II) and data were analyzed by FlowJo software (Tree Star, San Carlos, CA).

Isolation of cell surface membrane and plasma protein

Cell surface and plasma membrane protein was isolated using Plasma membrane protein isolation and cell fractionation kit (Invent Biotechnologies, SM-005) according to the specification. In brief, 5 ×106 cells were collected and washed with cold PBS. After removing PBS, cells were resuspended with 200 μL buffer A and incubated for 5 min, and transfered to the filter cartridge for centrifuge at 16000 g for 30 s. Then cells were resuspended and centrifuged at 700 g for 1 min to remove intact nuclei, and further centrifuged at 16000 g to pellet the membrane fractions, and collecting supernatant as cytosol fractions. Pellet was washed with 200 μL buffer B, and centrifuged at 7800 g, followed by carefully transferring the supernatant to a fresh microcentrifuge tube, adding 1.6 mL cold PBS, and centrifuging at 16000 g for 20 min. Discard the supernatant and save the plasma membrane proteins in pellet.

Western blot

Cells were lysed using NP-40 lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl,0.5% NP-40), and protein concentrations were quantified using a colorimetric detection assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The protein lysates were separated by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 30 min at room temperature. Then, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies against myc tag, CD3ζ, or β-actin (60008, Proteintech, China) overnight at 4°C, followed by a secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature (ZSGB-BIO, China). The membrane was then detected using the enhanced chemiluminescence system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA).

RNA isolation and qPCR analysis

CAR-T cells were co-cultured with target cells for 24 h. Total RNA was isolated with Trizol reagent (T9108, TaKaRa, Japan) and then was reverse transcribed to cDNA using a PrimeScript reverse transcription (RT) reagent kit (R201, Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qPCR was performed with 2×ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master (Low ROX Premixed) on Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). Human β-actin was measured as an endogenous control. The primers are as follows: β-actin (forward, 5′-AGAAAATCTGGCACCACACC-3’; reverse, 5′-CTCCTTAATGTCACGCACGA-3′), IL-2 (forward, 5′-TCCTGTCTTGCATTGCACTAAG-3’; reverse, 5′-CATCCTGGTGAGTTTGGGATTC-3′), IFN-γ (forward, 5′-TCGGTAACTGACTTGAATGTCCA-3’; reverse, 5′-TCGCTTCCCTGTTTTAGCTGC-3′), Granzyme B (forward, 5′- CCCTGGGAAAACACTCACACA-3’; reverse, 5′-GCACAACTCAATGGTACTGTCG-3′).

Cytotoxicity assay

The ability of T cells to kill tumor target cells was measured by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay. Briefly, CAR-T cells were co-cultured with target cells at different ratios (from 1:1 to 5:1) for 24 h in a 96-well plate. Then, LDH release was measured by the LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance values of wells containing effector cells alone and target cells alone were detected and subtracted as the background from the values of the co-cultures. Wells containing target cells alone were mixed with a lysis reagent for 30 min at 37°C, and the resulting luminescence was set as 100% lysis. Cytotoxicity was calculated by using the following formula: %Cytotoxicity = OD490(Experimental LDH release)/OD490(Maximum LDH release).

Cytotoxic activity of CAR-T cells in vitro was determined using the xCELLigence real-time cell analysis (RTCA) instrument (ACEA biosciences, USA) as described previously.42 Briefly, background impedance was measured by adding 50 μL of culture medium to the E-plate 16 PET plate (ACEA Biosciences). Dissociated adherent target cells were seeded at a density of 104 cells/well into the plate and then transferred to the incubator. Cell attachment and growth were continuously monitored by recording impedance data. At the time of initiating treatments, data recording was paused and CAR-T cells were added into the target cells at an indicated effector: target (E: T) ratio in duplicate. The target cell-only control was included. After transferring the E-plate back into the xCELLigence system, data acquisition was resumed to monitor the CAR-T cytotoxic activity based on the viability of attached target cells, as reflected by cell index values.

H&E and immunohistochemistry

Tumor-bearing mice were treated with CAR-T cells as mentioned above. Xenograft tumors or tissue samples were harvested and stained according to standard procedures. Briefly, tissue samples were fixed with formalin and paraffin-embedded, then sectioned at 4 μm thickness, and applied to H&E staining. For immunohistochemistry, slides were blocked with 1% BSA and stained with anti-CD3 antibody overnight at 4°C. After that, slides were washed and incubated with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody. Images of both H&E staining and immunohistochemistry samples were obtained using a Nikon Multispectral high throughput immunohistochemical scanner.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence, the anti-HBsAg (NB100-62652, Novus), anti-GPC3 (#ab282189, GPC3), anti-Flag (#F1804, Sigma) and anti-myc-PE (#3739s, CST) were used as primary antibodies, and cells were labeled with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibody (VA023, VICMED) or Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (VA1022, VICMED). DAPI (D9542, Sigma) was used for the staining of the nucleus. Fluorescent signals were detected using a confocal microscope.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 5.0 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for all of the statistical analyses. Statistical significance was determined using two-way ANOVA analysis for tumor growth curve, and unpaired 2-tailed Student’s t test for other comparisons, and a p < 0.05 was considered significant.
