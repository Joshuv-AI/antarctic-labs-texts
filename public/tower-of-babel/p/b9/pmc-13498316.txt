Opportunities and Challenges in Translating Genomics into Population Health Impact: Lessons from Familial Hypercholesterolemia
Authors: Jeffery Osei, Sai Sripad Kodukula, Baffour Otchere, Om Raval, Webster Donaldy, Abhilash Suresh, Shoshana H. Bardach, Jennifer Farnell, Ann Dodge, Diane MacDougall, Katherine Wilemon, Alexander C. Razavi, Yan V. Sun, Muin J. Khoury, Laurence S. Sperling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFamilial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is a common, autosomal dominant genetic disorder characterized by lifelong elevations in low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and a markedly increased risk of premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. Despite a well-defined genetic architecture, highly effective therapies, and strong evidence demonstrating the benefits of early identification and treatment, FH has historically remained underdiagnosed and undertreated worldwide. This persistent gap highlights broader challenges in translating genomic discovery into measurable population health impact.SummaryIn this review, we examine how initiatives led by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the Family Heart Foundation, and other stakeholders have advanced awareness, case identification, surveillance, evidence-based care, and implementation of genomic medicine in cardiovascular disease prevention. Using the framework of the Essential Public Health Services – assessment, policy development, and assurance/implementation – we evaluate progress achieved, identify implementation barriers, and highlight opportunities and recommendations to strengthen the population-level impact of FH detection, treatment, and prevention efforts.Key MessagesThe FH experience provides valuable lessons for other genomic conditions, demonstrating that achieving public health benefit requires more than scientific discovery. Greater investment in implementation science, a continued focus on health equity, and sustained systems-level engagement will be essential to translate genomic knowledge into durable and scalable improvements in population health.

Introduction

Familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is an autosomal dominant disorder of lipoprotein metabolism characterized by markedly elevated levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) from birth and increased risk of premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) if left untreated [1, 2]. FH most commonly results from pathogenic variants in the LDL receptor (LDLR) gene, accounting for approximately 85–90% of cases, followed by mutations in apolipoprotein B (APOB; 5–10%) and proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9; <5%) [1–5]. Less frequent genetic variants, including mutations in the LDL receptor adaptor protein 1 (LDLRAP1), signal transducing adaptor family member 1 (STAP1), and apolipoprotein E (APOE), have also been implicated in the pathogenesis of FH [6]. These mutations impair LDLR-mediated clearance of circulating cholesterol, leading to pathological LDL-C accumulation. FH exhibits a gene-dosage effect: individuals with heterozygous FH inherit one defective allele and typically develop atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease in adulthood, whereas those with homozygous FH inherit 2 defective alleles, resulting in markedly higher LDL-C levels – often exceeding 400 mg/dL – and cardiovascular manifestations beginning in childhood. Heterozygous FH affects approximately 1 in 250 individuals worldwide, making it one of the most common inherited genetic disorders, while homozygous FH is rare, occurring in approximately 1 in 300,000 individuals globally [7–10]. Prevalence of FH is substantially higher in certain populations, including French Canadians, Ashkenazi Jews, South African Afrikaners, and Lebanese individuals, due to founder effects [11].

FH has a recognizable clinical phenotype and there are highly effective therapies. There is robust evidence demonstrating that early identification and treatment substantially reduce premature morbidity and mortality. Yet FH remains markedly underdiagnosed and undertreated worldwide, underscoring a persistent gap between scientific discovery and real-world implementation [12]. Over the past decade, coordinated efforts from public health agencies, healthcare systems, advocacy organizations, scientific societies, and patient communities have contributed to the growing recognition of FH as an important public health genomic condition. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the Family Heart Foundation, and other stakeholders have served as important catalysts for initiatives focused on awareness, case identification, surveillance, evidence-based care, and implementation of genomic medicine into cardiovascular disease prevention. In this review, we examine these efforts through the lens of Essential Public Health Services – assessment, policy development, and assurance/implementation – while critically evaluating persistent implementation gaps, barriers to population-level impact, and future directions for advancing the integration of FH genomic discoveries into public health practice.

Translating FH Genetics into Public Health Practice

The public health approach for integrating genomic discoveries into clinical and population health practice is grounded in the principles of precision public health – delivering the right intervention to the right population at the right time [13]. Khoury and colleagues described a four-phase translational continuum that progresses from gene discovery to candidate health applications, the development of evidence-based guidelines, implementation in health practice, and evaluation of population health impact (shown in Fig. 1) [14]. Advancing this continuum requires addressing challenges related to health equity, including disparities in genomic discovery, clinical evaluation, and real-world implementation [15]. Historically, FH has been viewed as not common enough to be a public health priority yet not uncommon enough to be classified as a rare genetic disorder. In recognition of its population-level burden, the World Health Organization declared FH a global public health issue in 1998 and issued recommendations to address its impact [16]. Yet, despite early calls to action, FH has continued to hold a limited position within public health efforts in the USA [17]. Although it is estimated that more than 1.3 million Americans are affected, FH is still widely misconstrued as a rare condition by clinicians, public health professionals, and policymakers [17, 18]. In the USA, a rare disease affects fewer than 200,000 individuals; in contrast, heterozygous FH affects approximately 1 in 250 people, extending across generations of impacted families [19]. FH is also one of the most actionable conditions in genomic medicine: evidence-based recommendations support early and aggressive cholesterol reduction [20, 21]. Early identification and intervention substantially lower risk, and family-based cascade testing can identify affected individuals across generations [18, 22–24]. Despite these advances, progress across the translational continuum has been uneven. A translational gap persists partly because genomic-based interventions – even those with strong evidence – are often perceived as difficult to scale across public health systems or are viewed as lacking demonstrated clinical utility at a population level [25]. Thus, the FH experience provides a useful case study for examining both the opportunities and challenges of translating genomic discoveries into population health impact. To better understand the progress achieved and the barriers that remain, the Essential Public Health Services framework provides a structured approach for evaluating the activities and initiatives undertaken by various stakeholders to advance the implementation of FH genomic discoveries and improve population health outcomes [26, 27].

Assessment

The emergence of Public Health Genomics at the CDC helped establish a framework for integrating genomic and precision health applications into population health practice [28]. Central to this effort was the designation of FH as a tier 1 genomic condition, reflecting strong evidence base, clinical actionability, and substantial potential to reduce premature cardiovascular morbidity and mortality through early identification and treatment [29, 30]. Collaborative initiatives involving the CDC, the Family Heart Foundation, the National Lipid Association, and other stakeholders have supported FH assessment and surveillance efforts. For example, the advocacy for the introduction of specific International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision diagnostic codes for FH (ICD-10-CM E78.01 and Z83.42) in 2016, followed by expanded subtype-specific codes in 2025 (E78.010 for homozygous FH, E78.011 for heterozygous FH, and E78.019 for unspecified FH), has improved the ability to monitor national diagnostic patterns, estimate disease burden, and quantify gaps in case identification across healthcare systems. Analyses of national electronic health record (EHR) databases using these codes suggest that only approximately 30–40% of the expected FH population in the USA has been identified, highlighting a substantial burden of undiagnosed or uncoded FH and providing a measurable estimate of the implementation gap in FH detection [31, 32]. Approaches aimed at improving case ascertainment including cascade testing, machine learning algorithms, and EHR-based screening tools such as FIND FH®, the Familial Hypercholesterolemia Case Ascertainment Tool, and Mayo SEARCH have evolved over the past decade [33–36]. These EHR-based screening tools have demonstrated better performance compared with traditional screening methods in select healthcare settings. FIND FH®, for example, has been deployed across 6 US healthcare systems [37, 38]. In parallel, national registries such as the CASCADE FH Registry have strengthened infrastructure for longitudinal surveillance, real-world evidence generation, identification of gaps in care, and evaluation of emerging therapies [39].

Policy Development

Over the past decade, multiple initiatives have supported policy development by strengthening education, communication, multisector collaboration, and public awareness surrounding FH. Programs such as the FIND FH Collaborative Learning Network have supported clinician education, development of EHR-based clinical support tools, and dissemination of educational resources aimed at improving implementation of FH identification strategies across healthcare systems [37]. The Family Heart Global Summit® and similar collaborative forums have also helped convene clinicians, researchers, advocates, policymakers, and public health stakeholders to advance scientific exchange and implementation strategies for FH and elevated lipoprotein(a) [40]. Community-centered programs including care navigation initiatives have sought to improve access to specialized care, support family communication, and reduce barriers related to insurance coverage and treatment access. In pediatric populations, initiatives such as the Leveraging Evidence and Data (LEAD) Pediatrics Program have contributed to broader policy discussions surrounding pediatric screening, cascade testing, and integration of genomics into preventive cardiovascular care [41, 42].

Assurance and Implementation

Achieving meaningful public health impact in FH requires systems capable of translating evidence into sustained clinical care for individuals, families, and communities. In this context, initiatives focused on patient navigation, clinician education, registry-based research, and community engagement have sought to improve linkage to care, treatment access, and long-term disease management. Programs such as Cholesterol Connect, which provides free at-home cholesterol testing kits as well as an opportunity to connect participants with trained navigators who offer guidance and support informed discussions with healthcare professionals, have helped address practical barriers related to cholesterol testing, specialist referral, insurance approval, and patient education. Such initiatives also help reduce broader social, financial, and structural barriers that often impede timely diagnosis, treatment initiation, and long-term management of FH.

Persistent Barriers and Implementation Gaps

Although initiatives over the past decade have improved awareness and advanced infrastructure for FH identification and care, these efforts have not yet fully translated into broad and sustained population-level impact. A barrier has been knowledge gaps and inconsistent recognition of FH among clinicians, particularly in primary care settings [43]. Limited familiarity with FH genetics, diagnostic criteria, indications for genetic testing, and evidence-based screening and management strategies has contributed to missed diagnoses and delayed treatment initiation [44]. Additional challenges include perceptions that genetic testing may not alter clinical management or that limited therapeutic options are available following identification of increased genetic risk [44]. Collectively, these factors continue to impede the effective implementation of FH detection and treatment strategies in routine clinical practice.

Implementation of initiatives by various organizations and stakeholders has remained fragmented and uneven. Many healthcare systems continue to face challenges related to workforce limitations, inadequate infrastructure, privacy concerns, fragmented care coordination, limited access to lipid specialists and genetic counseling, and insufficient reimbursement mechanisms. Importantly, identifying individuals at high risk through EHR systems does not necessarily ensure successful follow-up, treatment initiation, or sustained engagement in care if long-term support systems are lacking.

Another major challenge is the absence of a nationally accepted definition of FH. In the USA, multiple diagnostic criteria are used in clinical practice, reflecting the complexity of FH diagnosis [45]. Heterogeneity of genetic background and phenotypic expression further complicates efforts to establish standardized diagnostic approaches and implement population-wide screening strategies [46, 47]. Importantly, many individuals who meet clinical criteria for FH do not have identifiable pathogenic variants in the classical FH genes, while some carriers of pathogenic variants identified through genomic screening may not exhibit markedly elevated LDL-C levels. These observations highlight the limitations of relying on either clinical or genetic information alone and have contributed to ongoing uncertainty regarding the role of genomics as a gold standard for FH identification. As a result, broader implementation of genomic screening has been met with hesitancy, underscoring the need for integrated approaches that combine genetic data with lipid phenotyping and clinical assessment.

Furthermore, the current state of public health disarray coupled with the absence of coordinated leadership and the sunsetting of several public health genomics and cardiovascular disease prevention programs has further contributed to persistent implementation challenges. Uncertainty surrounding sustained institutional support and long-term program continuity has limited the development and scaling of coordinated national FH screening efforts in the USA. As a result, unlike several European countries that have successfully integrated child-parent screening strategies into routine childhood healthcare encounters, the USA has not widely implemented population-based FH screening programs despite FH being recognized as a tier 1 genomic condition.

Future Directions and Opportunities for Population Health Implementation

Despite the challenges described above, several opportunities exist to strengthen the integration of FH into population health practice. Broader adoption of EHR-based screening tools, clinical decision support systems, and machine learning algorithms may improve identification of individuals living with FH, particularly in primary care settings where most affected individuals first enter the healthcare system. Achieving this goal will require sustained support for healthcare systems and partner organizations to expand implementation of these tools, including investments in infrastructure, integration of automated EHR-based alerts, in addition to clinician education and training. Clear pathways linking case identification to patient notification, genetic counseling, specialist referral, treatment initiation, and long-term follow-up will be needed. Clinicians must be equipped not only to recognize FH but also to communicate findings effectively and guide patients through subsequent steps in care. Existing initiatives provide useful models. For example, the Family Heart Foundation has developed a HIPAA-compliant outreach process that notifies participating clinicians of patients with probable FH identified within their practices [37]. Expanding similar implementation strategies may help bridge the gap between case identification and sustained clinical management.

Greater consideration should be given to population-based screening strategies. As a tier 1 genomic condition, FH may be well suited for integration into broader public health screening frameworks alongside other highly actionable genomic conditions and routine preventive services. One promising approach is child-parent screening, which combines cholesterol measurement and genetic testing in children aged 1–2 years during routine childhood healthcare encounters, such as immunization visits [48]. When a child screens positive, testing is extended to parents, enabling the identification of affected individuals across 2 generations while facilitating early intervention and cascade testing [48]. Embedding screening within existing healthcare interactions minimizes the need for additional clinic visits and leverages a period when parents may be particularly receptive to preventive health interventions. Similar approaches have been implemented in several European countries, including FH Kids Austria, a pilot program that integrates FH screening into school enrollment visits for children aged 5–7 years [49, 50]. Given the potential of these strategies to improve early detection and treatment at a population level, further evaluation of their feasibility, effectiveness, and cost-effectiveness within the US healthcare system is warranted.

Additionally, alignment with childhood and adolescent lipid screening recommendations across professional societies and public health organizations may help reduce uncertainty surrounding FH screening and improve implementation in clinical practice. For example, several organizations, including the American Academy of Pediatrics and the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, recommend routine lipid screening during childhood and adolescence [51, 52]. The US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF), an independent panel of primary healthcare professionals that evaluates the effectiveness of preventive services, has for several years concluded that the current evidence is insufficient to recommend for or against routine lipid screening in asymptomatic children and adolescents [53]. These differing recommendations contribute to the uncertainty among clinicians and healthcare systems regarding best practices [54]. More coordinated and consistent guidance across organizations could strengthen clinician confidence, support broader adoption of screening programs, and facilitate earlier identification of individuals and families affected by FH.

Finally, additional research is needed to evaluate the clinical utility, cost-effectiveness, equity implications, and optimal implementation pathways of emerging FH screening strategies across diverse healthcare settings. Future studies could assess alternative screening models, including a genome-first approach and newborn screening using dried blood spot testing [55, 56]. Such approaches have the potential to complement existing clinical and family-based screening strategies by identifying affected individuals earlier in the disease course and expanding access to preventive care. Importantly, evaluation of these strategies could follow the four-phase translational continuum described in Figure 1 to ensure that promising genomic applications are rigorously evaluated, effectively implemented, and ultimately translated into measurable population health benefits. Collectively, these efforts will be critical to advancing the integration of genomics into population health while ensuring that emerging genomic approaches complement, rather than replace, established clinical detection and management strategies.

Conclusion

FH represents one of the most mature and actionable use cases for public health genomics, with an established genetic basis, validated diagnostic strategies, and an expanding range of evidence-supported lipid-lowering therapies. However, the lessons learned from FH demonstrate that translating genomic discoveries into population health impact requires more than scientific evidence and effective treatments. Sustainable progress depends on coordinated public health leadership, robust surveillance systems, clinician engagement, equitable access to care, and implementation strategies that link case identification to effective long-term management. As illustrated in Figure 3 (central illustration), achieving population-level benefit from genomic medicine requires an integrated implementation infrastructure spanning systematic risk identification, precision care delivery, family-based cascade testing, and continuous real-world evaluation. Continued investment in implementation science, health equity, and population-based approaches will be essential to ensure that the promise of genomic discovery translates into meaningful improvements in health outcomes for individuals, families, and communities.
