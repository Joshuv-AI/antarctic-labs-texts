Arsenic and Cadmium in Cigar Fillers: A Comparative Study within Cigar Types and with Cigarettes
Authors: Mona Shrestha, R. Steven Pappas, Nathalie Gonzalez-Jimenez, Naudia Gray, Clifford H. Watson, Liza Valentín-Blasini, Munkhzul Otgonsuren, Kenneth M. Taylor, Matthew Hassink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cigar use has increased since the early 1990s; however, there is limited research on the quantity of carcinogenic metals such as arsenic and cadmium in cigars in general and especially within each of the numerous cigar types. The levels of cadmium and arsenic present in the tobacco filler of 67 little cigars, 21 cigarillos, and 19 large cigars were quantitatively analyzed using “triple-quad” inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Median arsenic concentrations in little cigars, cigarillos (medium-sized cigars), and large cigars were 0.219, 0.224, and 0.193 μg/g, respectively. For cadmium, the median concentration in little cigars was 1.21 μg/g while both cigarillos and large cigars had median cadmium concentrations of 1.32 μg/g. Median arsenic and cadmium concentrations on a per gram basis differed significantly across cigar types (Kruskal–Wallis, both p < 0.05). Pairwise Wilcoxon tests showed no difference in median arsenic concentrations between little cigars and cigarillos, and no difference in median cadmium concentrations between cigarillos and large cigars. Metal concentrations in cigar types also varied by manufacturer and marketed flavor. High arsenic concentrations did not necessarily correspond with high cadmium concentrations for specific manufacturers and marketed flavors. Cigarettes had higher median arsenic concentration than each cigar type, and median arsenic concentration differed significantly for all cigarette-cigar comparisons (all p < 0.05). For cadmium, median concentrations in cigarettes and little cigars were similar (p = 0.308), whereas cigarettes had lower median cadmium concentration than cigarillos and large cigars (both p < 0.05). Although in some cases the carcinogenic metal concentrations are similar between cigars and cigarettes in this study, these two tobacco product categories have many different physical characteristics and are used differently, which likely affects mainstream smoke exposure and the related health impacts of the metal content.

Introduction

A cigar is defined as a roll of tobacco wrapped in leaf tobacco or any substance that comprises tobacco that is not a cigarette. , Cigars are commercially available in a variety of sizes, weights, and designs (e.g., filtered vs unfiltered). The U.S. Department of the Treasury differentiates cigars into small cigars (weight ≤3 pounds per 1000 cigars) and large cigars (weight >3 pounds per 1000 cigars) for taxation purposes, while the U.S. Federal Trade Commission divides cigars into three weight categories, namely little cigars (≤1.36 g), medium cigars (1.36–4.54 g) and large cigars (>4.54 g) based on the weights. Moreover, in the monograph by Hoffman and Hoffman, cigars are classified into little cigars, small cigars/cigarillos, regular cigars and premium cigars. Little cigars have a weight range of 0.9–1.3 g and are 70–100 mm (mm) in length, similar to cigarettes. The physical parameters of cigarillos include a length range of 70–120 mm and weight range of 1.3–2.5 g. Regular cigars have a length of 110–150 mm, a diameter ≤17 mm, and a weight of 5–17 g. Premium cigars have diameters and lengths ranging from 12 to 23 mm and 12.7–21.4 cm, respectively. Although various cigar groupings have been reported, there is no consensus cigar categorization system at this time.

Cigars are under the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) jurisdiction although the courts have ruled that premium cigars are no longer subject to the Deeming Rule. , Cigar sales have grown since early 1990s. Some potential reasons for this growth are consumer misconceptions that cigars are less hazardous than cigarettes, availability of flavored cigars that make them more appealing to new users (particularly young consumers), and lower pricing of some cigars in comparison to cigarettes. , Increased cigar use and the similarity of some cigar types to cigarettes highlight why thorough characterization of cigars is important in the evolving regulatory and public health environment.

Cigars have been less thoroughly investigated than cigarettes. Even within the few studies examining harmful and potentially harmful constituents from cigars, the investigation of toxic metals present in cigars has been notably limited. Arsenic and cadmium occur in cured tobacco and the mainstream smoke of tobacco products. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classifies these metals as “IARC Group 1 carcinogens”. In addition to the metals’ carcinogenicity, cadmium can cause renal dysfunction, bone diseases, decreased lung function, destruction of lung epithelial cells, and emphysema, while arsenic can cause respiratory irritation, nausea, high blood pressure, and increased risk of diabetes. − Metals accumulate in tobacco plants primarily by absorption via the soil, and therefore any additives introduced to the soil such as sludge, fertilizer, and irrigation with polluted water can also influence the quantity and types of metals in the soil. In addition, soil pH affects cadmium concentrations, while field history and fertilizer conditions have the potential to affect arsenic quantity. , Although metal quantity is dependent on local growing conditions and varies with geographical location, metals accumulate at relatively high concentrations in tobacco leaves. , Metal quantities in cigarettes and cigarette smoke have been examined; − however, such information is not readily available for all types of cigars. The quantities of metals in filler are one of the factors that could affect the quantities of metals in mainstream smoke. ,,, Therefore, this study characterizes and compares the concentrations of arsenic and cadmium in the tobacco of the three cigar types, with further analysis based on cigar manufacturer and flavor. Cigar metal concentrations are also compared with those of cigarettes from a previous Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)/FDA collaborative study.

Methods

Analytical Method

Cigars used in this study were purchased through Lab Depot (Dawsonville, GA, USA) from retail outlets in the greater Atlanta, Georgia metropolitan area during 2016 and 2017 and were organized into different types using the marketed description. Sixty-seven little cigars, 21 cigarillos, and 19 large cigars were quantified for cadmium and arsenic concentrations using “triple-quad” inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) as described in detail below.

Tobacco filler samples from cigars were dried for a minimum of 1 h at 90 °C. Dried tobacco was rendered more homogeneous by grinding for 20 s in a Smart Grind coffee grinder (Black & Decker). Weighed samples (0.100 to 0.150 g dried tobacco) were prepared for analysis by microwave digestion in 75 mL (mL) TFM (tetrafluoroethylene, modified) vessels. Digestion was accomplished with ultrapure nitric and hydrofluoric acids (GFS, Powell, OH, USA, 9.0, and 0.5 mL, respectively) and 0.5 mL ultrapure 35% hydrogen peroxide (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Lous, MO, USA). It is noted that hydrofluoric acids may pose safety risks for laboratory personnel due to the strongly corrosive nature and acute toxicity. It is recommended to use appropriate handling procedures and personal protective equipment. , Procedural blanks were prepared by addition of all reagents to a vessel without tobacco before heating in a MARS6 microwave system (CEM, Matthews, NC, USA). The microwave heating profile was programmed to start at ambient temperature and increase to 200 °C in 20 min, followed by a 10 min digestion at 200 °C. Digested samples were diluted with ultrapure water in acid-cleaned 100 mL class A polymethylpentene volumetric flasks. Procedural blanks were transferred to volumetric flasks and diluted in the same manner as tobacco samples. Calibration standard solutions were prepared by dilution of high purity standards (HPS, North Charleston, SC, USA) in 9% v/v nitric acid and 0.5% hydrofluoric acid to match the diluted acid digest concentrations. For sample results that were greater than the highest calibration standard, the sample was diluted, and an appropriate dilution factor was applied. Internal standard solutions were prepared by dilution of HPS rhodium, tellurium, iridium, and thulium standards. The internal standards (20.0 μg/L rhodium and tellurium, 2.0 μg/L thulium and iridium) were prepared in 1% v/v ultrahigh purity nitric acid and 2% v/v semiconductor VLSI grade 2-propanol (Honeywell/Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Quality Control (QC) was maintained with daily digestions of Smokeless Tobacco Reference Product 1S3 (Center for Tobacco Reference Products, CTRP, Lexington, KY, USA) and Certified Reference Material (CRM) INCT-PVTL-6 (Institute of Nuclear Chemistry and Technology, IChTJ, Warsaw, Poland). QC results were monitored using SAS software (Cary, NC, USA). Analyses were performed using an Agilent 8800 “triple-quad” ICP-MS in oxygen mass shift mode with a Peltier-cooled spray chamber and platinum-tipped nickel sampler and skimmer cones. Cadmium was analyzed on mass in oxygen mode as 111Cd+. Arsenic was analyzed as 75As16O+ (mass 91) after reaction with oxygen. The internal standard and the sample solutions were introduced simultaneously via the peristaltic pump at 0.45 rps with 0.44 mm internal diameter PVC pump tubing from each channel teed together. For arsenic and cadmium analyses, 7 packs of cigars were analyzed. One representative sample from each of the 7 packs was sampled, prepared, and analyzed per day in 7 analytical runs. Spike recoveries for cadmium in tobacco ranged from 100% at the low spike concentration (6.30 μg/L) to 101% at the high spike concentration (18.8 μg/L). Spike recoveries for arsenic in tobacco ranged from 104% at the low spike concentration (0.630 μg/L) to 106% at the high spike concentration (1.88 μg/L). Method calibration standard solutions in previous publications had concentration ranges of 0.0500–2.50 mg/L (arsenic) and 0.500–25.00 mg/L (cadmium). Method accuracy determined by analysis of standard and certified reference materials has also previously been reported and ranged from 93% to 108%. Similarly, the procedural limit of detection (LOD) was previously determined using an established method employing Taylor plot analysis. The LODs for arsenic and cadmium were determined to be 0.024 and 0.016 μg/g, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

Products were included if their labeled subcategory/type was cigarillos, large cigars and little cigars. For each product, 7 replicate measurements per metal were analyzed. Products with a reported manufacturer of Swisher and Swisher International Inc. are all reported under Swisher International Inc. for data analyses. Data normality was evaluated graphically and using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Given the observed non-normal distributions of metal concentrations in the data, all analyses were performed using nonparametric methods. Because nonparametric methods were used, results were presented as median with interquartile ranges (IQR). Differences in metal concentrations among cigarillos, large cigars and little cigars were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, with the null hypothesis (H0) that analyte concentration measurements in cigarillos, large cigars, and little cigars were drawn from the same underlying distribution; or that the medians of all groups are the same H0:F(cigarillo)=F(largecigars)=F(littlecigars) where F = cumulative distribution/median

The alternative hypothesis was that at least one product type had an underlying distribution of analyte concentrations that differed from the others. A p-value< 0.05 was considered statistically significant for these overall comparisons. When Kruskal–Wallis test across the three cigar types was significant, pairwise-comparisons among the cigar types were conducted using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Additional pairwise comparisons between each cigar type and cigarettes were also conducted. To control for multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction of 0.017=(0.053) was applied. Therefore, p-value < 0.017 indicated statistical differences for pairwise comparisons.

Outliers were defined a priori as observations with median absolute deviation |MAD-z| > 3.5. Sensitivity analyses were then conducted after excluding these values to assess whether main findings were affected. After excluding (n = 31 observations) within- and between-product outliers (defined as |MAD-z| > 3.5), overall medians and interquartile ranges for both arsenic and cadmium remained similar across cigar types (Tables S1 and S2). Statistical significance patterns were largely unchanged, indicating that extreme values primarily affected the dispersion of the data rather than its central tendency. Moreover, the complete cigar data set in this study represents the commercial market at a particular time and therefore, all the concentrations measured in this study were considered valid observations. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results and Discussion

Arsenic and Cadmium Concentrations within Cigar Types

We analyzed arsenic and cadmium concentrations for 67 little cigars, 21 cigarillos and 19 large cigars in this study. The median concentration of arsenic was highest in cigarillos (0.224 μg/g) followed by little cigars (0.219 μg/g) and then large cigars (0.194 μg/g). The arsenic concentration ranges in these cigar types were as follows: 0.082–0.377 μg/g for cigarillos, 0.101–0.394 μg/g for little cigars, and 0.095–0.405 μg/g for large cigars. Large cigars and cigarillos had identical median concentrations for cadmium at 1.32 μg/g and higher median concentrations than little cigars (1.21 μg/g). The cadmium concentration ranges in these cigar types were as follows: 0.846–5.56 μg/g for large cigars, 0.617–2.04 μg/g for cigarillos, and 0.637–2.24 μg/g for little cigars. For little cigars, both cadmium and arsenic concentration ranges observed for this study are wider, specifically the maximum concentrations, than ranges reported in another investigation by Pappas et al. Based on nonparametric analyses, there were statistically significant differences among the three cigar types (p < 0.0001) for both arsenic and cadmium concentrations as determined by Kruskal–Wallis test. Although the arsenic medians and IQRs (0.167–0.257) were similar for all cigar types, the overall distributions differed significantly. Similarly, for cadmium, the median and IQRs were comparable among cigarillos (IQRs 1.14–1.46), large cigars (IQRs: 1.09–1.59), and little cigars (IQRs: 0.99–1.44). However, the statistically significant differences within the cigar types for cadmium may reflect distributional differences such as skewness or outliers, rather than differences in central tendency. Pairwise Wilcoxon test showed the following results: (1) For arsenic, little cigars and cigarillos (p = 0.363) were statistically similar but little cigars and large cigars (p < 0.0001) along with cigarillos and large cigars (p < 0.0001) differed significantly; (2) For cadmium, cigarillos and large cigars (p = 0.308) were statistically similar but little cigars were statistically different from both large cigars (p = 0.0002) and cigarillos (p = 0.004). A study of filtered cigar fillers by Caruso et al., which classified small and large cigars based on pack labeling, concluded that there were no statistically significant differences for cadmium and arsenic concentrations between cigar types. However, the small and large cigars in the Caruso study have very similar product characteristics (e.g., all filtered cigars with 22 of 23 cigars weighing between 1.12 and 1.4 g). Therefore, our analyses highlighted that median arsenic and cadmium concentrations had statistically significant differences among all three cigar types while while median arsenic concentration of little cigars compared to cigarillos as well as median cadmium concentration of cigarillos compared to large cigars were not significantly different.

To further evaluate the observed similarities and differences in the measured metal concentrations, cigar ingredient information submitted by tobacco manufacturers to FDA, pursuant to §904­(a)(1) of Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, was also analyzed. These data can provide additional information regarding the tobacco blends present in different cigar types which can influence the observed metal concentrations as well as the similarities and differences among the three cigar types. Based on the FDA 904­(a) data, little cigars were observed to have tobacco blends consisting of air- and flue-cured tobacco as well as tobacco stems, while cigarillos contained the same tobacco types as little cigars with the addition of sun-cured tobacco. Large cigar tobacco blends included the tobacco types listed for little cigars and cigarillos as well as additional tobacco types including expanded (leaf and stem) tobacco, reconstituted tobacco, and several others. The reported FDA 904­(a) tobacco blend data showed that each cigar type is generally composed of different tobacco blends, which could lead to differences in metal concentration.

Median arsenic and cadmium concentrations based on the cigar manufacturers were also evaluated and are illustrated in Figures and . For little cigars, the median arsenic concentrations ranged from 0.110 μg/g (John Middleton) to 0.357 μg/g (Grand River Enterprises) while the median cadmium concentrations ranged from 0.86 μg/g (Sandia Tobacco manufacturers) to 2.13 μg/g (Royal Agio Cigars). For cigarillos, in contrast, median arsenic concentrations were more consistent across a majority of manufacturers (∼0.20 μg/g), other than Garcia y Vega and White Owl, which had slightly higher median concentrations (∼0.30 μg/g). Median cadmium concentrations for cigarillos had similar variation to little cigars, ranging from 0.62 μg/g (National Tobacco Company) to 1.98 μg/g (Thompson & Co). Among large cigars, median arsenic concentrations ranged from 0.110 μg/g (Davidoff, Dominican Republic) to 0.257 μg/g (Macanudo, Swedish Match) while median cadmium concentrations varied widely from 0.97 μg/g (Swedish Match, Swisher International) to 4.91 μg/g (Acid Cigars). To further analyze the patterns observed in this study, for both metals in a particular cigar type, concentrations measured as lower than the median concentration for that type were identified as low while those greater than the median concentration for that type were identified as high. High cadmium concentration did not necessarily correspond to high arsenic concentration, and vice versa, for the cigar manufacturers. For instance, while cigars from manufacturers such as Royal Agio Cigars and Altadis (little cigars), Dominican Republic (cigarillos), Acid Cigars and Thompson & Co. (large cigars) have low arsenic and high cadmium concentrations, Cherokee Tobacco Company, Rouseco (little cigars), White Owl and National Tobacco Company (cigarillos), Macanudo and Swedish match (large cigars) contain high arsenic and low cadmium concentrations. Although high concentrations of each metal do not consistently correspond with one another, there are some manufacturers where this occurs; Grand River Enterprises, A&T Tobacco Imports (little cigars), Garcia y Vega, Thompson & Co. (cigarillos), and Scandinavian Tobacco Group and Altadis (large cigars) have high concentrations of both arsenic and cadmium. Conversely, manufacturers such as John Middleton, Good Times USA (little cigars) and Swisher International (cigarillos and large cigars) have low concentrations of arsenic and cadmium. These variations are likely a result of distinct manufacturing factors such as different tobacco blends, growing locations and agricultural conditions. Additionally, Swisher International, the only manufacturer with cigars of every type in this study, had low median concentrations for both metals for each cigar type except for slightly high median arsenic concentration for little cigars. This result could suggest that Swisher International uses a similar tobacco blend across various cigar types. Therefore, these results demonstrate that concentrations of metals varied based on cigar manufactures for each cigar type and that median arsenic and cadmium concentrations do not consistently correspond with each other.

The concentrations of arsenic and cadmium in cigar types with marketed flavors and certain cigar descriptors (such as full flavor, original, regular, classic, light, mild, natural, red, blue, palma, and straight up) were also analyzed since characterizing flavors play a role in the appeal of cigars. Out of the 67 little cigars in this study, 9 marketed cigar names did not have an identified flavor or descriptor. Similarly, a specific flavor or descriptor was not listed for 10 out of 21 large cigars and 5 out of 19 cigarillos. These products without any specific flavor or flavor descriptor were not included in the analyses. For little cigars (Tables S3 and S4), median arsenic concentrations ranged from 0.168 (red) to 0.265 μg/g (grape) while median cadmium concentrations varied from 0.82 (natural) to 2.13 μg/g (coffee). Additionally, for cigarillos (Tables S3 and S4), median arsenic concentrations were generally consistent across flavors at ∼0.2 μg/g, while slightly elevated levels were observed in flavors/descriptors such as black sweets, green sweets, and pineapple flavors (∼0.3 μg/g), which are similar to the trends observed for manufacturers due to specific flavors or descriptors only being used by certain manufacturers. Median cadmium concentrations, however, varied more widely, from 0.62 μg/g (straight up) to 1.54 μg/g (berry fusion). Among large cigars (Tables S3 and S4), a sweet flavored product had the highest median arsenic concentration, and concentrations ranged from 0.137 (cao) to 0.288 (sweet honey) μg/g, unlike arsenic a sweet flavored product had the lowest cadmium concentration, which ranged from 0.94 μg/g (sweet) to 2.11 μg/g (cao). When examining distribution patterns across flavors, certain cigars in this study, with flavors such as regular (little cigars), sweets cognac dipped, tropical, cherry (cigarillos) sweet (large cigars) had low concentrations of both metals, while other cigars with flavors and descriptors such as full flavor, mild (little cigars), blue, wine, pineapple (cigarillos), original, spiced rum, and sweet honey (large cigars) contained high concentrations of arsenic and cadmium. Some cigars with flavors and descriptors such as natural, peach, original, caramel, light (little cigars), green sweets, black sweets, straight up (cigarillos) and strawberry and grape (large cigars) had relatively low concentrations of cadmium but high arsenic concentrations. Similarly, some cigars with flavors or descriptorsblueberry, cherry, coffee, menthol (little cigars), berry fusion, palma (cigarillos) and cao (large cigars)had low concentrations of arsenic and high concentrations of cadmium. Notably, in this study, grape was the only flavor tested in all cigar types, and a high median concentration of arsenic but a low concentration of cadmium was seen in all three cigar types. Given that fruity flavors are popular among cigar users; concentrations of both metals in fruit-flavored little cigars were compared with the metal concentrations in tobacco-flavored little cigars in this study using nonparametric procedures (see Table S5). Due to the limited number of cigarillos and large cigars with tobacco flavor/descriptor, statistical analyses were not conducted for those cigar types. For this analysis, little cigars with the descriptors original, blue, classic, regular, natural, full flavor, mild, light, and red were considered tobacco flavors. The results showed that both arsenic and cadmium concentrations differed significantly between fruit- and tobacco-flavored little cigars (p < 0.0001). Specifically, median arsenic concentrations were slightly higher in fruit-flavored little cigars (0.242 μg/g; IQR:0.210–0.265) compared with tobacco-flavored ones (0.227 μg/g; IQR:0.193–0.257). Conversely, cadmium concentrations were similar across the two flavor groups, with fruit flavors showing a median concentration of 1.17 μg/g (IQR: 1.03–1.46) and tobacco flavors at 1.21 μg/g (IQR: 1.01–1.42). Despite similar median and IQRs, unequal sample sizes and greater variability in tobacco-flavored little cigars likely drove the significant nonparametric test results. In conclusion, while metal concentrations varied widely across flavors or descriptors with no consistent trends, both arsenic and cadmium median concentrations for fruit-flavored little cigars were statistically different compared to tobacco-flavored little cigars in this study.

Comparison of Arsenic and Cadmium Concentrations (Per Gram) in Cigars vs Cigarettes

Arsenic and cadmium concentrations from 50 U.S. cigarettes measured in 2013 by Fresquez et al. were compared to the metal concentrations from the cigars in this study. It should be noted that the products in this study and those in the study by Fresquez et al. represent different market years and therefore the tobacco growing conditions and other environmental factors impacting the metal concentrations may differ between the products in the two studies. However, the cigarettes in the Fresquez study have been well-characterized and represent a relatively recent market survey that covered a majority of the U.S. market. As shown in Figure , the median concentration of arsenic on a per gram basis is higher in cigarettes compared to all cigar types while the median cadmium concentration for cigarettes is identical to that of little cigars but lower than that of cigarillos and large cigars analyzed in this study. Nonparametric analyses were also conducted to compare cigarettes with each type of cigar. For these pairwise analyses, cigarettes were used as the reference since each cigar type is being compared to the cigarette. The results showed that for arsenic, cigarettes statistically different from each cigar type (p < 0.0001). For cadmium, cigarettes were statistically different compared to cigarillos and large cigars (p < 0.0001) and were statistically similar to little cigars with p-value equal to 0.6. Therefore, on a per gram basis, median arsenic and cadmium concentrations of cigarettes differed significantly than those of the cigars in this study, except for median cadmium concentration of little cigars.

Calculated Metal Quantity Per Unit and Smoke Transfer Rates in Cigar Types and Cigarettes

Since cigars and cigarettes are used on a per stick basis, the quantities of cadmium and arsenic were also estimated on a per unit basis for each product type. The weights of the cigarillos and large cigars were not measured in this study, although the weights of most little cigars (59 out of 67) were measured. Therefore, we utilized previously published weights for each cigar type (Table S6) to calculate the expected range of metal quantity per unit for the little cigars, cigarillos, large cigars, examined in this study, and cigarettes. , Although these are calculated metal quantities instead of measured quantities, this approach provides information to more fully characterize the two product categories since there are notable size differences, in particular large cigars vs cigarettes. Little cigars and cigarettes generally have similar dimensions and tobacco weights. The calculated ranges of metal quantity per unit of little cigars and cigarettes were quite similar in this study. Cigarillos and large cigars generally have more tobacco than little cigars and cigarettes. The calculated metal quantities per unit for cigarillos and large cigar types were higher than those of the little cigars and cigarettes (see Table S6) for this study. Based on previously reported cigar weights, arsenic and cadmium quantities could be as high as 3.28 and 22.4 μg/unit, respectively, for large cigars investigated in this study. These arsenic and cadmium quantities are approximately 30 and 45 times larger, respectively, compared to those calculated for little cigars and cigarettes. Therefore, upon calculating metal quantity per unit using previously published weights, little cigars, in this study, and cigarettes were observed to have comparable arsenic and cadmium quantities per unit while the metal quantities per unit in cigarillos and large cigars were considerably higher, which is expected due to differences in tobacco product size.

Metals may transfer from filler to smoke. In the absence of smoke yield data or transfer rates for each cigar type, we calculated the quantity of cadmium in smoke for cigars in this study using the metal quantities per unit, calculated above, and the transfer rate range (0.5–2.3%) reported by Franzke et al. In comparison, cigarettes have higher reported transfer rates (1.3–7.3%) than cigars. Using the reported transfer rates, we calculated the range of cadmium in smoke for the three types of cigars and cigarettes (Table S6) in this study. Notably, for this study, cigarillos which have a higher calculated cadmium quantity per unit than cigarettes (1.7 vs 0.5 μg/unit) had a calculated smoke yield range of 8.5–75 ng/cigar, which was similar to the calculated range for cigarettes (6.7–85 ng/cigarette). This result corresponds with the higher cadmium per unit of use quantities in cigarillos, discussed in this study, and the lower transfer rates reported by Franzke et al., which result in smoke yields similar to cigarettes. These calculations should be interpreted within the context of significant product variability and data limitations. Cigars represent a diverse tobacco product category with varying physical parameters and smoking patterns, including dual use with cigarettes. Smoking topography differs not only between cigars and cigarettes but also varies within cigar types themselves. − Furthermore, daily cigar-only smokers across all types show toxicant exposure levels comparable to exclusive cigarette smokers, while little cigar smokers may experience even higher exposures. Given these product design and use variations that complicate metal yield analysis, along with limited data on cigar-specific metal transfer efficiencies, targeted research on metal smoke yields and differences between cigar types would provide additional informative data to more fully characterize their health impacts.

Conclusion

Metals are toxic constituents in tobacco, and their analysis is an important part of understanding a tobacco product’s potential health impacts. However, there are limited studies of cigar metal content. Therefore, we investigated cadmium and arsenic concentrations in filler of little cigars, cigarillos and large cigars, which differ in size, shape, tobacco blends and manufacturing processes, and compared the metal concentrations across these cigar types and with cigarettes. In this study, the median arsenic and cadmium concentrations were observed to be statistically different among cigar types. Only median arsenic concentrations of little cigars and cigarillos, and median cadmium concentrations of cigarillos and large cigars were statistically similar. Compared to cigarettes, only cadmium concentrations of little cigars were statistically similar. Analysis of the commercial cigar sample population, in this study, showed that metal concentrations vary depending on the manufacturer. High arsenic concentration did not always correspond to higher cadmium for the product(s) from the same manufacturer. Similarly, cigars with different marketed flavors in this study showed varying metal concentrations. An overall trend for metals based on flavors was not observed, although fruit-flavored little cigars were observed to be statistically different compared to tobacco-flavored little cigars. These variations in metal concentrations may be due to use of different tobacco blends as well as tobacco growing locations and agricultural conditions. − In addition, since cigars are combusted products, potential metal smoke yields, based on the filler metal data in this study, were evaluated using transfer rate data for cadmium in cigars from a previous study. While cigarillos have higher cadmium filler quantities, the calculated smoke yields were similar to those of cigarettes for this study. Since the cadmium smoke yields estimated in this study for cigars is solely based on published transfer rates without distinction between cigar types, it only provides preliminary insight into cadmium exposure from cigars. In addition to more experimental data for metal smoke yields, various factors such as product use behaviors, physical characteristics must be considered for a complete understanding of metal exposure of cigars and their public health impacts. Overall, this current study provides additional information for cigars on a topic that has not been heavily studied, allowing more informed evaluation of the public health impacts of cigars.
