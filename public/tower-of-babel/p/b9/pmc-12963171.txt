Accuracy of twelve intraocular lens calculation formulas in highly myopic eyes
Authors: Guang-Yue Li, Zhong-Yan Li, Zhao-Heng Yang, Peng Zhao, Meng Li, Jie Xu, Xiu-Hua Wan, Jing-Shang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate the accuracy, stability, and influencing factors of 12 intraocular lens (IOL) calculation formulas in highly myopic cataract patients.MethodsRetrospective Case Series. Clinical data were collected from highly myopic cataract patients who underwent cataract extraction surgery at Beijing Tongren Eye Center, Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University, between January 2024 and January 2025. Patients were stratified into subgroups based on axial length, keratometry, anterior chamber depth and axial length to corneal radius ratio (AL/r). The prediction error (PE), absolute error (AE), root-mean-square absolute prediction error (RMSAE) and the percentage of eyes with an absolute error within ± 0.25 D, ± 0.50 D, ± 0.75 D, and ± 1.00 D were recorded and compared for the following 12 IOL formulas: Barrett Universal II, Cooke K6, Castrop, Kane, EVO 2.0, Pearl-DGS, Hoffer QST, SRK-T, Haigis, Holladay 1, Holladay 2, and Hoffer Q. Statistical analysis was performed using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), Friedman test, Kruskal–Wallis test, Dunn's test, and Cochran Q test.ResultsA total of 255 patients (255 eyes) were included in this study, comprising 113 males and 142 females, with a mean age of 57.8 ± 9.9 years. The difference in absolute errors among the 12 IOL formulas was statistically significant (χ2 = 327.59, P < 0.01). Among them, the Hoffer QST formula yielded the lowest absolute error (0.33 D), followed by Pearl-DGS (0.35 D) and Barrett UII 0.35 D), while the Hoffer Q formula showed the highest absolute error (0.75 D). Notably, only the Hoffer QST and Pearl-DGS formulas demonstrated no significant correlation between their absolute errors and axial length, corneal curvature, or anterior chamber depth (all P > 0.05).ConclusionFor highly myopic cataract patients, modern IOL formulas demonstrated superior accuracy compared to traditional formulas. The accuracy was predominantly influenced by axial length, with keratometry and anterior chamber depth playing minor roles. The Hoffer QST and Pearl-DGS formulas, in particular, showed consistent and stable performance.Keywords: Accuracy, Intraocular lens, Formula, High myopia, Cataract surgery

Introduction

In recent years, alongside advancements in surgical techniques, enhanced precision of ocular biometry, a broader array of intraocular lenses (IOLs), and continuous updates to calculation formulas, cataract surgery had gradually evolved from a vision-restoring intervention to a refractive procedure[1, 2]. This transformation had made accurate prediction of postoperative refraction particularly crucial. Especially for patients with high myopia, cataract surgery served the dual purpose of treating cataracts and correcting refractive errors. Therefore, it was even more crucial to carefully select the appropriate IOL formula to minimize prediction errors and enhance the patient's visual quality [3–5].

To date, traditional IOL formulas widely used in clinical practice were vergence formulas [6]. They predicted the effective lens position (ELP) using key ocular parameters including AL, anterior chamber depth (ACD), and keratometry (K). For decades, extensive studies had demonstrated that these formulas yield satisfactory refractive outcomes in eyes with normal axial length [7, 8]. According to the European Registry of Quality Outcomes in Cataract and Refractive Surgery, the percentage of patients with a prediction error (PE) within.

± 0.5 D after cataract surgery was 73.7% [9]. However, high myopia involved complex anatomical variations such as excessive axial elongation, thinner corneas, deeper anterior chambers, posterior staphyloma, and zonular laxity, all of which could lead to increased errors in ocular biometry. [10, 11]. Previous studies had shown that measurement inaccuracies in ACD, AL, and K contributed to calculation errors in 42%, 36%, and 22% of cases, respectively [12]. Consequently, the predictive accuracy of traditional formulas declined significantly when applied to highly myopic eyes [13, 14]. In response, a new generation of IOL formulas had emerged. These incorporate advanced optical principles and computational techniques, such as thick-lens models (e.g., EVO 2.0), ray-tracing formulas (e.g., Barrett Universal II), and AI-enhanced algorithms (e.g., Hoffer QST, Kane, and Pearl-DGS) [15–19]. These modern formulas offered enhanced capabilities for calculating IOL power in cataract patients with high myopia.

Nevertheless, robust evidence regarding the predictive accuracy of newer-generation IOL formulas in highly myopic eyes remained limited. To address this gap, this study evaluated the refractive prediction errors of seven modern formulas (Barrett Universal II, Cooke K6, Kane, EVO 2.0, Pearl-DGS, Hoffer QST, Castrop) and five traditional formulas (SRK-T, Haigis, Holladay 1, Holladay 2, Hoffer Q). The aim was to assess the performance of modern IOL formulas in cataract surgery for highly myopic patients (especially for patients with extremely high myopia), with the goal of improving postoperative visual outcomes.

Methods

Participants

This retrospective Study enrolled 255 patients (255 eyes) who underwent cataract surgery with IQ (SN60WF) intraocular lens implantation at Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University, between January 2024 and January 2025. This study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University (TRECKY2020-069). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion.

All patients underwent a comprehensive preoperative examination, including uncorrected distance visual acuity (UDVA), corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA), intraocular pressure measurement, subjective refraction, and slit-lamp biomicroscope. Biometric parameters were measured using the IOL Master 700 (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG), including AL, K, ACD, central corneal thickness (CCT), lens thickness (LT), and white-to-white distance (WTW). Subjective refraction was performed 4 to 12 weeks postoperatively for all patients.

The inclusion criteria required patients to have completed preoperative biometry with an AL ≥ 26 mm and attained a postoperative CDVA ≥ 20/40. Exclusion criteria comprised eyes with corneal astigmatism exceeding 3.0 D, a history of ocular trauma, vision-limiting pathologies, or any severe intraoperative or postoperative complications.

Groupings

In accordance with established methodology [20, 21], eyes were stratified based on AL, K, ACD, and axial length to corneal radius ratio (AL/r). AL subgroups were categorized as long (26 mm ≤ AL ≤ 28 mm), super-long (28 mm < AL ≤ 30 mm), and extreme-long (AL > 30 mm). To ensure a balanced distribution, ACD, K, and AL/r were stratified using the 25th and 75th percentiles. ACD subgroups consisted of shallow (ACD ≤ 3.26 mm), medium (3.26 mm < ACD ≤ 3.66 mm), and deep (ACD > 3.66 mm). K subgroups included flat (Km ≤ 42.98 D), moderate (42.98 D < Km ≤ 45.15 D), and steep (Km > 45.15 D). AL/r was classified into three subgroups: ≤ 4.68, 4.68 < AL/r < 5.35, and ≥ 5.35. These groupings enabled a comprehensive assessment of formula performance under varying biometric conditions.

IOL power calculation

Preoperative ocular biometry was conducted using the IOL Master 700. The Barrett Universal II formula was employed for preoperative IOL power determination. IOL power calculations were performed using the following formulas: Barrett Universal II, Cooke K6, Castrop, Kane, EVO 2.0, Pearl-DGS, Hoffer QST, SRK-T, Haigis, Holladay 1, Holladay 2, and Hoffer Q. Among these, the Barrett Universal II, SRK-T, Haigis, Holladay 1, Holladay 2, and Hoffer Q formulas were computed directly through the built-in software of the IOL Master 700. The remaining six formulas were calculated via their respective online platforms: Kane (https://www.iolformula.com), Cooke K6 (https://www.cookeformula.com), EVO 2.0 (https://www.evoiolcalculator.com), Pearl-DGS (https://www.iolsolver.com), Castrop (https://www.iolcon.org) and Hoffer QST (https://www.hofferqst.com). Optimized lens constants were Barrett U II (LF: 1.85), Cooke K6 (A: 118.96), Castrop (C: 0.359 / H: 0.079 / R: -0.184); Kane (A: 118.7); EVO 2.0 (A: 118.7); Pearl-DGS (A: 118.7); Hoffer QST (pACD: 5.64); SRK-T (A: 118.97); Haigis (a₀: -0.025 / a₁: 0.31 / a₂: 0.173); Holladay 1 (SF: 1.8); Holladay 2 (pACD: 5.6); Hoffer Q (pACD: 5.57) respectively, which retrieved from the IOLcon website (https://iolcon.org/).

Outcome measures

In accordance with established methodology [22, 23], the prediction error (PE), absolute prediction error (AE) and root-mean-square absolute prediction error (RMSAE) were used as the primary metrics to assess the accuracy of IOL formulas. The PE was defined as the difference between the postoperative spherical equivalent (SE) and the SE predicted by the IOL formula (with negative values indicating a myopic shift and positive values a hyperopic shift). The percentages of eyes with a PE within ± 0.25 D, ± 0.50 D, ± 0.75 D, and ± 1.00 D were calculated.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0). The normality of all datasets was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed data were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the overall differences in prediction errors among different types of IOL formulas. The Friedman test was employed to compare the overall differences in absolute errors across the 12 IOL formulas, while the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for intergroup comparisons of absolute errors among the 12 formulas. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons between different IOL formulas were conducted using Dunn's test, with Bonferroni correction applied to the results (α = 0.05). Categorical data are expressed as frequency. The Cochran Q test was used to compare the percentages of eyes with absolute errors within ± 0.25 D, ± 0.50 D, ± 0.75 D, and ± 1.00 D across different IOL formulas, with Bonferroni correction applied to the results (α = 0.05). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (Fig. 1).

Results

Baseline demographic and biometric data

The study enrolled 255 cataract patients (255 eyes) with a mean age of 57.8 ± 9.9 years. The distribution between right and left eyes was comparable (51.3% vs. 48.7%, respectively). Key biometric characteristics included a median axial length of 29.05 mm (IQR: 27.58–31.04 mm), an anterior chamber depth of 3.47 ± 0.30 mm, and a mean keratometry value of 44.12 ± 1.49 D. Overall predictive accuracy

A statistically significant difference was observed in the PEs among the 12 IOL formulas (F = 363.98, P < 0.01). The Barrett UII 6, Pearl-DGS, Kane, and Castrop formulas exhibited a slight myopic shift (PE range from -0.16 to -0.08 D), whereas the SRK-T, Haigis, Hoffer Q, Holladay 1, Holladay 2, EVO 2.0, and Hoffer QST formulas produced a hyperopic shift (PE range from + 0.01 to + 0.73 D).

A statistically significant difference was found in the AEs among the 12 IOL formulas (χ2 = 327.59, P < 0.01). The AEs of the 12 IOL formulas, ranked from lowest to highest, were as follows: Hoffer QST (0.33 D), Barrett UII(0.35 D), Pearl-DGS (0.35 D), K6 (0.36 D), EVO (0.36 D), Kane (0.36 D), Castrop (0.36 D), Haigis (0.37 D), SRK-T (0.45 D), Holladay 2 (0.61 D), Holladay 1 (0.70 D), and Hoffer Q (0.75 D).

The proportions of eyes with AEs within specified ranges differed significantly among the 12 IOL formulas (P < 0.01). The percentages of eyes with AE within ± 0.50 D, listed in descending order, were as follows: Hoffer QST (69.80%), K6 (69.02%), Barrett UII (68.63%), EVO 2.0 (67.06%), Pearl-DGS (65.49%), Kane (64.71%), Castrop (63.92%), Haigis (62.75%), SRK-T (52.94%), Holladay 2 (39.61%), Holladay 1 (37.65%), and Hoffer Q (34.51%). Postoperative refractive outcomes across K subgroups

Discussion

Based on one of the largest cohorts of highly myopic eyes (AL ≥ 26 mm) to date, this study comprehensively evaluated nearly all available online new-generation IOL formulas, with focused on the impact of AL, K, ACD and AL/r on the accuracy of postoperative refractive prediction in highly myopic cataract patients. Overall, modern formulas, particularly Hoffer QST, Barrett Universal II, and Pearl-DGS, demonstrated significantly superior predictive accuracy compared to traditional formulas such as Hoffer Q, Holladay 1/2, and SRK-T. These reflected advances in IOL power calculation, driven by innovations such as machine learning-based parameter optimization, multivariate regression integrating biometric parameters, and anatomical segmentation of axial length. This collective progress addressed fundamental limitations inherent in traditional models based on simplified ocular assumptions. These findings provided important clinical guidance for the selection of IOL power calculation formulas in patients with high myopia.

For general patients, most currently available IOL formulas demonstrated satisfactory predictive accuracy. In a comparative study by Ma et al. evaluating 13 modern formulas and 5 classical formulas, the results demonstrated that the MAE of these 18 formulas ranged from 0.30 D to 0.38 D, which were significantly lower than the MAE in highly myopic patients [24]. In our study, Hoffer QST yielded the lowest MAE (0.39 D), while Hoffer Q resulted in the highest MAE (0.81 D). The reduced accuracy of IOL formulas in highly myopic patients may be attributed to their more complex ocular anatomy, including axial elongation, corneal thinning, deeper anterior chambers, posterior staphyloma, zonular laxity, and weakened capsular support [5, 10, 25]. Previous studies had confirmed that newer-generation formulas such as Barrett Universal II, EVO 2.0, and Kane achieve better accuracy in elongated eyes than traditional formulas. Research by Ronald B et al. indicated that Barrett Universal II performed best across all axial lengths, including long eyes [7, 26, 27]. A study by Darcy et al. involving 10,930 eyes showed that the Kane formula yielded the lowest MAE in patients with AL ≥ 26 mm [28]. Lin et al. also reported that EVO 2.0 was more suitable for highly myopic populations [29]. Recent studies further suggested that Hoffer QST and Pearl-DGS offer predictive precision comparable to the aforementioned new formulas, which was consistent with the findings of this study [25, 30]. However, research on the performance of formulas such as K6 and Castrop in long eyes remained relatively limited. Therefore, this study included the majority of available online new-generation IOL calculation formulas into a comparative framework to comprehensively evaluate their performance.

In this study, most modern IOL formulas demonstrated excellent accuracy, which could be primarily attributed to the application of innovative technologies such as machine learning-based parameter optimization, regression integrating multiple biometric parameters, and anatomical segmentation of the axial length. These advancements effectively overcome the limitations of traditional models based on simplified schematic eye assumptions [31, 32]. Although a statistically significant difference in absolute errors was observed among the 12 IOL formulas (P < 0.05), no significant difference was found among all modern IOL formulas specifically (P > 0.05). Furthermore, the proportion of eyes with an absolute error ≤ 1.00 D exceeded 90% for each modern formula, indicating a similarly high level of accuracy among them. In contrast, the overall accuracy of traditional IOL formulas was inferior to that of modern formulas and consistently exhibited a tendency toward hyperopic shift. This finding aligned with the inherent design limitations of traditional formulas, which were based on thin-lens theory. Furthermore, the RMSAE values for traditional formulas exceeded 1.0 D in eyes with long AL, highlighting the inherent risk of significant refractive surprises when managing outlier cases in this population. In this study, when compared against the widely used Barrett UII formula, only the Haigis formula among the five traditional formulas showed a comparable absolute error. The absolute errors of the other four traditional formulas were significantly greater than that of Barrett UII (all P < 0.01).

Subgroup analysis results further emphasized the importance of personalized selection of intraocular lens (IOL) formulas based on ocular biometric parameters in improving the refractive accuracy of cataract extraction surgery [33, 34]. In the AL subgroups, the AEs of Barrett UII, SRK-T, Hoffer Q, Haigis, Holladay 1, and Holladay 2 demonstrated statistically significant differences across the different AL categories (P < 0.05). Subsequent analysis of the AL/r subgroups demonstrated that, besides the aforementioned formulas, the absolute errors (AEs) of the K6, EVO 2.0, and Kane formulas also exhibited statistically significant intergroup differences. Both the Hoffer QST and Pearl-DGS formulas consistently demonstrated high predictive accuracy and stability. These findings suggested that axial length was a critical factor influencing the accuracy of IOL formulas. Therefore, careful consideration of the patient's axial length measurement was essential during the clinical process of personalized IOL formula selection [21, 35, 36]. In the keratometry and ACD subgroup analyses, no statistically significant differences in AEs were observed across the different subgroups for any of the IOL formulas (P > 0.05). This indicated that keratometry and ACD had a minimal overall impact on the accuracy of IOL formulas [29, 37].

This study had some limitations. First, due to its retrospective design, although an adequate sample size was included for analysis, certain subgroup analyses may remain underpowered. Second, as the study focused on a single-center Chinese population, subsequent validation through multi-center, multi-ethnic studies were warranted.

Overall, modern IOL formulas generally provided superior accuracy compared to traditional formulas in highly myopic cataract patients. Among traditional formulas, the Haigis formula represented a relatively recommended option. While modern formulas collectively demonstrated excellent performance, Hoffer QST and Pearl-DGS were particularly recommended. These findings offered valuable guidance for cataract surgeons in selecting the optimal formula for highly myopic eyes and were expected to contribute to improved postoperative visual satisfaction.
