Identifying physicians’ views on compassionate care in daily clinical practice: a multicentre Q-methodology study in Belgium
Authors: Fleur Helewaut, Dominique Benoit, Peter Pype, Jan Reniers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesCompassionate care transcends the mere clinical treatment of illness to encompass the psychological and social dimensions of patient well-being. It is often unclear how physicians from different specialities perceive this complex process. This study aimed at identifying their different views on compassionate care in daily clinical practice.DesignA Q-methodology study was conducted. This mixed-method methodology combines quantitative and qualitative analysis to identify participants’ subjective viewpoints. Participants ranked 53 statements on the dimensions of compassionate care according to their point of view, followed by an interview. Principal component analysis, followed by varimax and additional manual rotation, was used to identify groups that shared similar views.SettingTwo hospitals, one facility for people with disabilities and three private practices in Belgium.Participants29 physicians from different specialties with clinical practice were purposively sampled.ResultsThree views on compassionate care in daily practice were identified. The task-oriented view attached the most importance to making the best use of clinical knowledge and expertise and acting in the best interest of the patient, accompanied by clear and appropriate communication of medical information. The system-oriented view emphasised an authentic and non-judgemental attitude, as well as a relationship of trust, self-reflection, clinician’s well-being and a compassionate working environment. Lastly, in the relationship-oriented view, the focus was on building a relationship of respect and trust with the patient by active listening and getting to know the patient, and engaging him in the care process. The physicians’ viewpoints varied by specialty and setting.ConclusionsThe findings of our study and the approach taken contribute to further clarifying the differences in physicians’ views on compassionate care in daily clinical practice. Physicians loading on the task-oriented view worked mostly in highly technical or acute care environments. In the system-oriented view, they were active in paediatrics and primary, palliative and mental healthcare. And in the relationship-oriented view, physicians often worked with oncology patients or patients with chronic diseases. These findings may have an impact on a better understanding of compassionate care in different clinical settings, facilitate the design of compassion training and ultimately improve patient care and satisfaction.Keywords: Empathy, Physicians, Patient-Centered Care

Introduction

Compassionate care remains central to modern healthcare, transcending the mere clinical treatment of illness to encompass the psychological and social dimensions of patient well-being.1 Over time, various vocational associations, healthcare systems and organisations have recognised the importance of integrating compassionate care into their codes, missions and practices.2,4 Compassion is described as the process of being aware of another person suffering, responding to this suffering, coupled with the intention to relieve the suffering through action.5,7 Compassion in healthcare is often characterised as a complex, multifaceted process involving several key elements, including awareness8 and recognition of suffering,5 relational understanding,7 feeling personally engaged,5 the intention to do good5 and taking action.5 7 The recognition of a patient’s suffering stems from an awareness and understanding of its various causes.9 A virtuous response to suffering then arises through relational understanding, being personally touched by the suffering of others, self-awareness and action.10 11 The intention to act in the patient’s best interest is grounded in the virtues of medicine12 and virtue ethics.7 Examples of compassionate actions include providing a supportive presence,9 13 communicating with sensitivity,13 going above and beyond14 and performing small acts of kindness.14 It is important to emphasise that compassionate care does not replace clinical competence; both should be integrated into everyday clinical practice.15,17

The receivers of care, patients and their families, expect competency and compassion from clinicians.17,19 Furthermore, evidence shows the importance of compassion in healthcare for humanising care, improving patient satisfaction and health outcomes.120,22 Additionally, quality of care and patient safety improve with compassionate care, while malpractice suits and healthcare costs drop.1 23 24 Furthermore, as burnout remains a threat to the healthcare workforce, compassion and self-compassion are crucial for maintaining the well-being and professional fulfilment of clinicians.120,22 25 26 Lastly, compassion plays a vital role in social justice efforts and therefore contributes to achieving the quintuple aim of healthcare.27 28

However, despite the acknowledged importance of compassion, patients mention that this care is missing.19 29 In a US survey with 800 patients, 85% of the patients considered compassion very important to successful medical treatment.29 However, only 53% of the patients said the healthcare system generally provides compassionate care and only 54% of the patients said that most clinicians provide such care, illustrating the discrepancy between patients’ expectations and experiences. Additionally, several reports on the lack of compassion in healthcare and its detrimental effects on the quality of care were published.30 31 These reports also emphasised the organisational factors that impact compassionate care and the importance of institutional support for clinicians. While compassion in healthcare has traditionally centred on the patient–clinician relationship, there is growing recognition of the need for a compassionate work environment and compassionate leadership, thus expanding the scope of compassionate care and encouraging a shift from individual to collective responsibility.32,37

To address the lack of compassionate care, healthcare providers and institutions are expected to invest in training and education.31 Systematic reviews of compassion training in healthcare and medical education found very few evidence-based interventions.38 39 Sinclair et al identified that “compassion training may engender compassionate healthcare practice if it becomes a key component of the infrastructure and vision of healthcare organizations, improves leadership at all levels, and betters the performance of individual healthcare providers.”40 They also emphasised the importance of engaging learners prior to the training to, among others, mitigate misconceptions and understand individual training needs.

However, the literature on how physicians from different specialities perceive compassionate care and the role of the different compassion elements in their daily practice is scarce.10 41 Often, understandings of compassion are determined within a specific context such as palliative10 or primary care,41 which mostly reflect experiences in chronic care with more long-term patient–clinician relationships. However, research shows that factors such as clinical environment and processes, patient and physician characteristics, and type of clinical problem influence compassionate care.42 Physicians working in acute care environments such as emergency medicine, with other types of clinical problems, high patient turnover and no pre-existing patient–clinician relationship, may therefore have a different experience and understanding of compassion compared with chronic care contexts.43 To inform the design of targeted compassion training and improve clinical practice across specialties, it is important to understand how compassion is perceived by different medical specialties. Therefore, the aim of our study is to gain a better understanding of how compassionate care is perceived by physicians from different specialties in their daily practice. More specifically, we aim at understanding how they engage with the different elements that constitute compassionate care.

Methods

Materials and methods

Q-methodology, a mixed-methods approach, aims at studying subjective viewpoints in a group of participants regarding a particular topic.44 Therefore, this method is suitable to study prevailing views among physicians on the different elements of compassionate care in daily clinical practice. The checklist for reporting Q-methodology was used45 (see online supplemental material 1). The participants’ subjective viewpoints are exposed through the medium of a Q-sort. A Q-sort occurs when the participant places a predetermined set of statements (the Q-set) regarding the research topic in relative ranking to one another on a predetermined sorting grid.44 Q-sorting invites individuals to model their subjectivity by considering and responding to multiple issues regarding the research topic in relation to each other.46 Each completed Q-sort represents a holistic and individualised construction of the participants’ views on the research topic that is wholly subjective, valid and that cannot be deemed quantitatively superior or inferior to those of another participant.44 Subsequently, the quantitative data of the Q-sorts are analysed with a by-person factor analysis to identify groups of participants with similar statement rankings.44 After factor analysis, each factor is represented by a composite Q-sort or factor array, which is the unique pattern of statement placement corresponding to the overall view of all participants defining that factor.44 47 Finally, the factors are interpreted with the qualitative data, and a narrative description of the different viewpoints on the research topic is created.44

Step 1: developing the Q-set and sorting grid

Developing the set of statements starts with what is already written and said on the research topic. From this shared knowledge and meaning, a group of statements (the concourse) can be extracted. The set of statements used in a Q-methodology study is then sampled from this concourse and shaped by the research question. For this study, the items were verbal statements regarding elements of compassionate care in daily clinical practice. The Q-set must cover the topic as thoroughly as possible and be balanced, meaning the Q-set does not appear value-laden or biased towards a particular viewpoint.44 For this study, statements came from the academic literature on definitions and models for compassion in healthcare and studies on compassionate care in different care contexts. Studies with views from clinicians or patients and theoretical models were included. An electronic search of the literature in the PubMed, Embase and CINAHL databases was undertaken in September 2021, using the keywords [compassion] OR [compassionate care] OR [compassion model], [ [healthcare] OR [healthcare professional] OR [patient] AND [compassion]] and 27 studies were withheld. This process initially yielded 109 statements. Selected statements related to six themes: ‘recognising suffering’, ‘building an understanding relationship’, ‘benevolence’, ‘action’, ‘self-care/self-awareness’ and ‘working environment’. The first four themes related to the definition of compassion and the last two to the growing attention to self-care and working environment in compassionate care. First, the research team reviewed the statements for redundancy and overlap, and this reduced the set to 64 items. Subsequently, the Q-sorting was piloted with two physicians (non-participants) who gave their feedback on comprehensibility and feasibility of the method and on clarity, redundancy and lack of items. This resulted in a final set of 53 items, which were translated into Dutch (see table 1).

Step 2: selecting the participant sample

We recruited 29 participants through purposeful sampling and snowballing. This allowed for diversity in specialisation, years of experience, working context, age and gender. Participants were physicians with clinical practice and were recruited from one university hospital, one non-university hospital, a facility for people with disabilities and private practices in the Flanders region of Belgium. We contacted them by email with information about the study and the informed consent. Although Q-methodology generally aims at 40–60 participants as ‘rule of thumb’, it is agreed that good studies and analyses can be carried out with fewer participants.44 47 48

Step 3: data collection

The study was conducted from April 2022 to May 2023. The first author facilitated all Q-sorting and conducted all interviews. The Q-sorting and accompanying interviews lasted between 40 and 70 min and were conducted in Dutch. Prior to sorting, participants were asked to provide information on age, gender, specialisation, working experience, involvement in medical education and previous activities or readings related to compassionate care. Subsequently, the following working definition of compassionate care was given: being aware of suffering, trying to understand the other’s perspective, feeling personally addressed, wanting to do good and acting accordingly. Then instructions were given on how to sort the items (online supplemental material 2). Since participants often shared important thoughts or experiences during the sorting, we audio recorded the conversation from the beginning of the sorting until the end of the additional interview. All Q-sorts and interviews were conducted during a live one-on-one encounter. After the sorting, the researcher and participant looked at the Q-sort and discussed why a certain item got a certain ranking relative to another item and what the item meant to the participant. This conversation aimed at gaining a fuller and more detailed understanding of the participant’s Q-sort. Participants were encouraged to share experiences and to add items they felt were missing. At the end, pictures of the final sort were taken for further analysis. Additionally, all interviews were transcribed verbatim. All data were pseudoanonymised, and the names of participants were replaced with an identifier.

Step 4: data analysis and interpretation

All 29 Q-sorts were uploaded into the PQ Method V.2.35 software, which supports Q-methodological analysis.49 A by-person factor analysis was conducted to identify groups of participants who expressed similar views on their Q-sorts. Using principal component analysis, we first extracted factors with similar sorts based on total sort correlations. Factors were considered significant when defined by an eigenvalue of greater than one (Kaiser-Guttman criterion) and at least two significantly loading sorts. Additionally, we applied Humphrey’s rule, which states that factors should be retained if the cross-product of the two highest factor loadings exceeds twice the SE.44 Subsequently, varimax and additional manual rotation were used to find the best factor solution, meaning identifying the best solution for describing the different viewpoints. There is no gold standard to identify the best factor solution in Q-studies; therefore, this requires adopting different vantage points to identify the best solution, while trying to explain as much of the study variance as possible with distinctive factors and including as many of the Q-sorts as possible.44 Additionally, a low correlation between factors and a low amount of consensus statements is preferable.44 48

Q-sorts contributing uniquely and significantly at p<0.01 to a factor are used to create a composite Q-sort or factor array that uniquely represents the overall view of all participants defining that factor.44 47 To give meaning to the extracted factors, the collected quantitative and qualitative data had to be synthesised. In a first step, the statements with the highest and lowest scores were considered, as were significantly distinguishing (p<0.01) statements for that factor. Additionally, the rankings of statements on the factor arrays were compared and contrasted systematically to assess similarities and differences across views.44 47 Subsequently, the whole factor array of each factor was considered holistically along with the qualitative data of the contributing participants to create a deeper understanding of the viewpoint. In the last step, a narrative was written for each factor array to provide a consolidated and rich description of each viewpoint.44 47 Finally, quotes illustrating a particular viewpoint were selected. The initial factor analysis and different iterations of factor solutions were conducted by the lead author. The analysis and all iterations were discussed with the coauthors to maintain the transparency of the analysis and interpretation process and to keep the interpretation close to the original data.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in this study.

All participants were physicians with clinical practice and had a minimum of 5 years of working experience in their specialisation. The mean age was 50 years (ranged from 35 to 64 years). The mean years of clinical experience was 19 (ranged from 5 to 35 years). 15 women and 14 men participated. 25 physicians worked in a hospital, of which 10 worked in a university hospital and 15 worked in a non-university hospital. Three physicians had their own practice without hospital affiliation. One physician worked in a facility for people with disability. A wide range of different specialities was represented in this sample (see table 2).

Similarities across factors

Correlations between the three factors ranged from 0.19 to 0.44, indicating similarities across viewpoints. This was illustrated by seven consensus statements that do not distinguish between any pair of factors (see online supplemental material 3). All viewpoints attached similar importance to, for example, seeing the patient as a whole person, the fact that compassion is not something that should be deserved by the patient, and to accepting support or help from colleagues.

I think that ‘seeing the patient as a whole person’ is one of the most obvious statements in those items. If you are going to judge the patient beforehand ‘yes, he doesn’t know that, he can’t do that’, you can no longer be open to his care needs. I think you automatically have to start seeing the patient as a fully-fledged person. Participant 3

Sometimes you start thinking: that patient really didn’t deserve that. Or, that patient is responsible why the pathology is there. But that’s separate from the fact that the patient - all patients deserve that compassionate care. It’s not a merit, but a duty. Participant 17

Differences across factors

Next to similarities, we observed important differences in how the three factors perceived compassion in their daily clinical practice. The distinctive features of the three factors are described below, and the composite Q-sort for each factor can be found in online supplemental material 4.

Factor 1: task-oriented view

Factor 1 had an eigenvalue of 5.53 and explained 19% of the study variance. Eight physicians loaded significantly on this factor, two women and six men with an average age of 53 years and an average experience of 23 years in their field. There were two emergency physicians, two intensive care physicians, one dermatologist, one gastroenterologist, one radiotherapist and one cardiologist (see table 5).

This viewpoint attached the most importance to statements relating to the themes of ‘action’ and ‘benevolence’ in compassionate care. The emphasis was on making the best use of clinical knowledge and expertise and to act in the best interest of the patient.

If you want to give compassionate care, the purely medical-content quality is also super important. In other words, you also have to make sure that you are not only a good listener and that you are very understanding and that you are compassionate and that you guard the patient’s humanity, but you are first and foremost a doctor, and it does mean that you have to maximise your knowledge and your abilities and those of your department as much as possible. Participant 15

This use of knowledge and expertise was accompanied by the skilful execution of a clinical examination or technical procedure, and the clear and appropriate communication of medical information.

Communicating medical information clearly and appropriately is indeed what I find important. You have to know why your patient is coming, they have to know what you have done. You have to tell them that you are coming back with results and then you also have to communicate in a way that when they are leaving the emergency department they know: ‘I had this, they did that. And that’s my treatment’. Even to the extent of: ‘if it doesn’t improve, I have to go there’. Participant 26

Additionally, this viewpoint also valued the intention to do good, making time for the patient, treating him with respect and maintaining his dignity. Keeping full attention on the patient, taking responsibility in the care process and going the extra mile for the patient were also deemed important in compassionate care and scored higher than in the other factors. In contrast, building a relationship of trust, active listening, paying attention to the patient’s broader story and experience, and involving the environment of the patient scored significantly lower than the other factors.

We don’t have so much time for that relationship. It is usually acute, a heart attack for example and then yes, you introduce yourself and we are going to fix that, but it’s not that you have more time for that. We always try to show that we are calm and that we are going to deal with it, and more than that we can’t actually do. And as soon as it’s done, before the catheters and so on come out, then we explain what happened. They may not have heard half of it, but they already have a certain idea of what has happened and then we see if there are still questions. Then they can call their family. Participant 16

Even though contributing to a compassionate working environment scored lower than in the other factors, actively taking care of one’s own well-being and the welfare of colleagues was given attention. However, self-reflection on thoughts, emotions and behaviours and daring to be vulnerable were regarded as less significant elements in providing compassionate care.

Factor 2: system-oriented view

Factor 2 had an eigenvalue of 4.09 and explained 14% of the study variance. Six physicians loaded significantly on this factor, five women and one man with an average age of 54 years and an average experience of 23 years in their field. There were two paediatricians, one primary care physician, one palliative care physician, one endocrinologist and one psychiatrist (see table 5).

This viewpoint emphasised items relating to the themes of ‘building an understanding relationship’, ‘self-care/self-awareness’ and ‘working environment’. In compassionate care, authenticity and adopting a non-judgmental attitude towards the patient were strongly valued. Building a relationship of trust and trying to understand the patient’s experience were also deemed very important.

Building a relationship of trust with patients, I think that is of course essential. Everything depends on it. And that is something that is not measurable. Why that becomes a relationship of trust, that is about non-measurable variables. But you need a relationship of trust at the moment you discuss very difficult things. Participant 21

This viewpoint was very conscious that compassionate care extends beyond the patient. This was reflected by the importance attached to actively contributing to a compassionate working environment and the strong emphasis on self-reflection and being aware of the influence of one’s own thoughts and emotions. Additionally, taking care of one’s own well-being, daring to be vulnerable, distinguishing one’s own experience from the patient’s and a healthy emotion regulation also scored significantly higher than in the other factors, as well as accepting that despite one’s good intentions and efforts the outcome for the patient remains uncertain.

You always have to separate what is yours and what is someone else’s. And it may affect you emotionally, but they are your emotions and your emotions you cannot attribute to the patient. That happens very often. You should not get angry with the patient because it makes you angry. That anger is yours. I think you have to engage with it and then you will also be able to identify which patient or situations make you angry. Then you can learn not to get angry anymore. Participant 18

Engaging the context of the patient and other clinicians in the care process was highly valued, while the intention to do good, acting in the best interest of the patient and making time for the patient weighed significantly less compared with the other factors. In contrast with factor 1, the skilful execution of a clinical examination or technical procedure was regarded as less important in providing compassionate care. Additionally, this view attached less importance to professional duty, virtues and medical ethics than both other factors.

Factor 3: relationship-oriented view

Factor 3 had an eigenvalue of 5.07 and explained 17% of the study variance. Seven physicians loaded significantly on this factor, four women and three men, with an average age of 46 years and an average experience of 14 years in their field. There was one physician from a surgical discipline, one primary care physician, one anaesthesiologist/intensive care physician and four hospital physicians from non-surgical disciplines (neurology, gastroenterology, rheumatology and pain medicine) (see table 5).

This viewpoint attached most importance to statements relating to the themes of ‘building an understanding relationship’ and ‘benevolence’. The emphasis was on having respect for the patient and actively involving him in the care plan. This viewpoint invested in building a relationship of trust and giving recognition to the patient’s suffering. This was done in part by making sufficient time, active listening and exploring the broader patient story. The intention of doing good for the patient and acting in the patient’s best interest was also highly valued.

I have always taken the view that you have two ears and one mouth. So that’s what I always do, listen and take time. And starting from the principle that we don’t first ask the question: what’s the matter with you, but the question: what matters to you? And in that context, the ideas, the concerns, and the patient’s expectations are very important to me. That is about making the connection with the patient. Not only for the patient, but also for his family. That is also very important to me. And of course, giving correct information to the patient and sharing that information well and coordinating it well with the patient’s relatives. Participant 8

In contrast with the other factors, this viewpoint deemed it significantly more important to attune to where the patient is in the care process, to be a supportive presence, to be present as both a person and a professional and to have informal conversations with patients.

Supportive presence. Yes. of course. They come, often for support. You can’t always do much, but you can give support. So I think that’s important in our field as well. Participant 7

Additionally, placing the needs of the patients before one’s own needs was more important than in other factors, while the acceptance that the outcome of one’s efforts for the patient is uncertain was deemed more unimportant.

As in factor 2, the skilful execution of a clinical examination or technical procedure was regarded as less important in providing compassionate care. Since the focus was heavily on patient care, compassionate relationships with colleagues, while important, were considered secondary to the patient’s care.

Actively taking care of the well-being of colleagues is obviously very important, but if I consider that relatively to each other, compassionate care is mainly an interaction between me and the patient and others professionally involved if needed… So yes, for me compassionate care is mostly about caring for the patient. And yes, it could also be caring for colleagues. But to me that is indeed less important than the interaction with the patient. Participant 17

In comparison to the other factors, care for one’s own well-being, being understanding of one’s own shortcomings and attention to the influence of one’s own cognitive and emotional processes weighed significantly less in this view on compassionate care.

Discussion

Our study aimed to explore how physicians from different specialties perceive compassionate care in daily clinical practice. Our analysis yielded three different factors or views on compassionate care and showed that physicians with different views emphasised other elements of the compassion process. Factor 1, the task-oriented view, emphasised acting in the patient’s best interest, while making the best use of one’s knowledge and expertise. This view also focused strongly on performance of clinical and technical actions and communicating medical information. Factor 2, the system-oriented view, focused on an authentic and non-judgmental attitude, as well as building a relationship of trust, self-reflection, clinician well-being and a compassionate working environment. Lastly, factor 3, the relationship-oriented view, prioritised respect, doing good and actively engaging the patient in the care process as well as building a relationship of trust with the patient by active listening and exploring the broader patient’s story.

The different views in our study share similarities with previous research on clinicians’ understandings of compassion. Most physicians loading on factor 1, the task-oriented view, worked in highly technical or acute care environments. The dual focus of this view on both technical competency and patient-centred care is also highlighted in the review on surgical success by Owusu et al.23 This review emphasises the necessity of integrating patient-centred care in a rapidly evolving technological environment to obtain good surgical outcomes. Evidence also suggests that compassionate care reduces preoperative anxiety and postoperative pain.50 51 The importance attached to patient–clinician communication by factor 1 is also present in the study by Sonis and White on patient experience in the emergency department.52 In this high-paced environment, good communication is considered a way of showing compassionate care. Factor 1 also resonates with the findings of Siddiqui and Hartog on perceptions of intensivists on what is missing in their compassionate care.53 The intensivists rated themselves highly competent in conducting end-of-life discussions with family and showing respect. In contrast, they scored themselves lower on their competence in expressing sensitivity and caring, and striving to understand the emotional needs of patients and/or their families.

The importance attributed by factor 2, the system-oriented view, to a non-judgmental attitude, self-reflection and self-awareness in compassionate care is well documented.54,57 Self-reflection helps the clinician to navigate the intersubjective encounter with the patient, where emotions often arise on both sides and to be aware of the potential impact of the clinician’s emotions on the encounter.57 Besides the importance for patient care, self-reflection is also important for fostering resilience and self-care.55,57 The system view by factor 2 acknowledges the interconnectedness of compassion for the patient and his caregivers as well as for the clinician within a compassionate working environment and resonates in the study by Maddox and Barreto.35 In this study, conducted in an adolescent mental health ward, clinician’s views on compassionate care included elements on individual, team and organisational level. In this setting, ‘working as a collective around the person’, with compassionate engagement of the patient, his environment and all necessary clinicians was deemed important. Additionally, elements such as compassion of other team members, teamwork, team thinking and space for thinking were regarded as facilitating compassionate care. The influence of team care, team culture and organisational support on individual and team well-being, and on the provision of compassionate care was also highlighted in the review of Fox et al on relational dimensions of collaboration in healthcare contexts.33 In our study, the physicians loading significantly on this system-oriented view mainly worked in contexts where interprofessional teams worked intensively side by side to provide care, and where the involvement of the patient’s environment was important, such as paediatrics and primary, palliative and mental healthcare.

Factor 3, the relationship-oriented view in our study, shows many similarities with The Healthcare Provider Compassion Model conceptualised by Sinclair et al.10 Both this model and factor 3 place great importance on the relational space between the patient and clinician. This relational space in the model is characterised by engaging the patient in a sensitive manner, relational communication, coming to an in-depth understanding of the patient, and discerning and addressing the needs of the patient. The model of Sinclair et al was conceptualised within palliative care settings and physicians loading strongly on this style in our study frequently worked with oncology patients or patients with chronic diseases. Other findings in research show that a good patient–clinician relationship is associated with a better quality of life in oncology patients,58 helps lower resistance to therapy in patients with diabetes59 and enhances positive coping in patients with chronic renal disease.60

However, our study does not explain how a certain view emerges in a physician. A way of looking at the development of different views on compassionate care is as a pattern language, as described by Roze des Ordons et al.61 This means that to address the situation at hand, clinicians combine relational, dispositional, situational and activity facets of compassion to provide an expression of compassion. Thus, different views on compassionate care can also arise through adaptation to a specific situation. This interplay of personal, relational and situational factors and its impact on expressions of compassion is well described elsewhere.4262,64 Additionally, physicians also apply different strategies to remain compassionate in their clinical encounters. Strategies can focus on the self by cultivating distress tolerance and self-care, or focus on the patient by communication skills and recalling shared humanity.65

Furthermore, different views on compassionate care can lead to a lack of shared understanding, which in turn can become a challenge to provide this care to patients.62 On the other hand, providing compassionate care can also be divided among team members, who can modulate their behaviour to address perceived gaps in compassion shown by others around them.62 Differences in understanding of compassionate care may also be at odds with patients’ expectations. Asked about compassionate care, patients expect certain physician behaviours such as listening and paying attention,66 67 following up and running tests,67 continuity and holistic care,67 communicating clearly66 and being involved during the medical process.66 However, the behaviours and actions that are considered compassionate differ among patients and are, for example, influenced by patients’ sociodemographic characteristics.67 Although patients and clinicians both emphasise the importance of this care, patients do not always perceive the physician–patient encounter as compassionate.66 One of the reasons for this mismatch is that the good intentions of clinicians are not always expressed by compassionate behaviours that are observable for patients.66 67 One factor contributing to this gap between intentions and behaviours is physicians assuming the patient’s compassion needs instead of verifying them with the patient.66 While physicians often consider compassionate action as ‘doing more’ (medical) actions, patients rather value ‘being with’ the patient and fostering connection. Other contributing factors are physician’s limiting beliefs on compassion (eg, difficult, time-consuming), difficulties to establish a connection with certain patients, high workload and time pressure.66 In addition to compassion, patients also expect competency from physicians, but depending on their diagnosis, they attach a different importance to both qualities.16 Moreover, expectations on compassion and competence are dynamic. This was illustrated by patients being presented with a scenario of being evaluated to undergo brain surgery, preferring physician competence over compassion, but when a complication from brain surgery was added in the scenario, there was a significant increase in the preference for physician compassion.16 However, in our study, it is unclear if physicians also adapt their behaviour according to patients’ expectations.

To conclude, both patients’ expectations and clinicians’ views from different clinical settings are important for the design of compassion training and improvement of clinical practice.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, the use of Q-methodology fits with the aim to gain insight into the different views on compassionate care in daily clinical practice. The quantitative data and analysis allowed to directly compare subjective viewpoints from different participants, while the qualitative data allowed for the interpretation and description of the viewpoints with more depth. Second, our participants came from different medical specialities, which allowed us to gain insight into the complexity of compassionate care from a broader perspective. However, a first limitation of our study is that Q-methodology does not identify the prevalence of certain views among physicians. Second, the study was conducted within one Western European country and healthcare system. Other viewpoints may exist in other regions and healthcare systems.

Implications for practice and training

Our findings illustrate the need for enhancing awareness of different views on compassionate care in daily clinical practice. When discussing care goals, reflecting on and clarifying the views of different team members can add to a shared understanding and quality of care. Awareness and understanding of these views can also impact the design of compassion training and lead to adaptations for different training needs.

Directions for future research

To advance insight on how and why compassion styles emerge in different specialties, further research on the physician and specialty-related, patient-related, contextual and organisational factors that contribute to views on compassionate care is necessary. This may contribute to a better understanding of whether differences are more a result of personal preferences or of an adaptational process to situational factors. Lastly, further research on patient expectations regarding compassionate care in different care settings and the predominant compassionate care views in those settings may clarify differences in perception between patients and physicians. This can inform (future) clinicians about possible adaptations of their view and behaviour to patients’ expectations. In addition, patients’ views and experiences are a valuable source for designing compassion training programmes.

Conclusions

We believe the findings of our study contributed to further clarifying the differences in physicians’ views on compassionate care in their daily clinical practice. There is a clear pattern in the way physicians from different specialties engage with the different elements that constitute compassionate care. Physicians loading on the task-oriented view worked mostly in highly technical or acute care environments. In the system-oriented style, they were active in paediatrics and primary, palliative and mental healthcare. And in the relationship-oriented style, physicians often worked with oncology patients or patients with chronic diseases. To address the lack of compassion in healthcare, the views of clinicians from different settings must be taken into account when designing compassion training and practice policy with the ultimate goal to improve patient care and satisfaction.
