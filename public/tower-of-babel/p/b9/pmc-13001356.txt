Factors associated with delayed hospital presentation following stroke in Senegal: a mixed methods study
Authors: Mundih Noelar Njohjam, Mark Olivier Ngoule, Falonne Tiffany Niakam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTimely presentation to specialized stroke centers after a stroke is crucial for stroke outcomes and long-term prognosis. In developing countries like Senegal, several barriers may hinder timely presentation after stroke but have not been extensively studied. Analyzing the factors contributing to delayed presentation will allow for the development of context-informed interventions to reduce the time to treatment and improve outcomes for stroke patients.MethodsWe conducted a hospital-based mixed-methods study in the neurology departments in Dakar, Senegal, from January 2023 to January 2025. A cross-sectional study was conducted to gather quantitative data, while semi-structured qualitative interviews were conducted to collect qualitative data. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with delayed presentation. Qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis following a grounded theory approach.ResultsA total of 265 participants were included in the study with a mean age of 57.0642 ± 16.8786 [IQR 15–94]. Ischemic stroke was the most common type of stroke. Most patients (72.8%) presented outside the 4h30 minute time window. Factors associated with late presentation were stroke onset between 18:00–00:01 am (p = 0.001, OR 5.6, CI 2.1–15) and 12:00 and 17:59 pm (p = 0.012, OR 3.1, CI 1.3–7), arriving from a health center (p = 0.009, OR 3, CI 1.3–6.8), and arriving from another stroke-treating center (p = 0.027, OR 2.9, CI 1.1–7.6). Barriers to early presentation that emerged from the semi-structured interviews included limited awareness of stroke symptoms, traditional healers, lack of transportation means, severity of stroke, socioeconomic challenges, safety concerns, cultural beliefs, and healthcare system-related barriers.ConclusionThere are significant delays in presentation to stroke centers after a stroke in Senegal. These delays are influenced by the complex interplay of individual, sociocultural, and healthcare system-related factors. Addressing these multifaceted challenges requires a comprehensive approach involving patients, healthcare providers, policymakers, and communities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12245-026-01187-0.

Introduction

The burden of stroke is projected to rise continuously on a global scale, with a substantial proportion of the burden being shouldered by low- and –middle income (LMICs) countries particularly those in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [1–5]. The availability of acute-phase therapeutic options for stroke, such as thrombolysis, effective blood pressure-lowering medications, and endovascular therapy, has significantly contributed to the reduction of the burden of stroke in countries where such therapeutic options are being used [6]. The efficacy of such acute phase treatment options is highly time-dependent, as the efficacy of thrombolytic therapy and endovascular treatment decreases significantly with time [7–9]. Similarly, early initiation of blood pressure-lowering medications and surgical interventions for hemorrhagic stroke is critical to improve both short term and long-term outcomes. Delays in hospital presentation following the onset of stroke symptoms are associated with worse patient outcomes in both the acute and chronic phases for all stroke types, with greater stroke-associated disability and higher mortality rates [10–12].

In Senegal, the burden of stroke is substantial, with a rising prevalence among young adults [13–16]. Stroke-associated morbidity and mortality rates are disproportionately high, with significant disparities in access to stroke care [17]. Modifiable risk factors, such as hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and smoking, are increasingly common [18]. Effective therapeutic options for the management of all forms of stroke is currently available in Senegal. Although expensive and not consistently available, patients who present early within the recommended time window of ≤ 4h30 minutes and meet all other eligibility criteria could potentially benefit from thrombolysis. Likewise, patients with hemorrhagic stroke who present early and in whom a surgical intervention is indicated, would be more likely to benefit. However, delayed presentation to the hospital following the onset of stroke has been reported as a common barrier in the management of stroke in the acute phase [19, 20]. The high and consistently rising prevalence of stroke underscores the growing urgency of addressing delays in hospital presentation. It is absolutely critical to determine the factors associated with such delays in order to improve stroke care and mitigate the burden of stroke in Senegal. Data on the factors that contribute to delayed presentation will inform the development of targeted interventions to improve stroke care delivery. To the best of our knowledge, data on factors associated with delayed presentation to a stroke-treating facility in Senegal are very limited. This study aimed to determine the factors associated with delayed hospital presentation following stroke in Senegal.

Methodology

Study design

We conducted a hospital-based mixed-methods study in the neurology departments of the Fann University Hospital and the National University Hospital Center of Pikine, Senegal, from January 2023 to January 2025. A cross-sectional study was conducted to gather quantitative data, while semi-structured qualitative interviews were conducted to collect qualitative data.

Study population and settings

The study population included patients diagnosed with acute ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke who were either managed in the outpatient clinic or in the inpatient unit of the study sites during the study period. The study was conducted in two neurology departments in Dakar, the capital city of Senegal. Senegal is a developing country with a great proportion of Senegalese living below the poverty line [21]. It is a majorly Muslim country deeply rooted in cultural and religious practices. Over 40% of the population resides in rural areas, and most of the local population is employed in the informal sector [21]. Stroke services are provided by three main hospitals with neurology departments in the capital city of Dakar: the Principal hospital and the National University Hospital Centers of Pikine and Dakar. The public healthcare system in Senegal is strained by limited resources, inadequate infrastructure, and a shortage of trained healthcare professionals, particularly in rural areas [22]. Out-of-pocket payments are the main method used by the local population to pay for healthcare. Most referral hospitals with specialized services are located in Dakar [22]. To get specialized treatment, patients in rural areas have to travel to Dakar. Literacy rates in Senegal are among the lowest in sub-Saharan Africa, especially among women and in rural areas [23, 24]. Most of the local population speaks only the Wolof. These factors negatively impact health literacy. There are several ambulance services offering emergency transportation available 24 h a day, 7 days a week [25, 26].

Inclusion criteria

All patients with a confirmed diagnosis of ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, who were managed in any of the participating hospitals during the study period. We also included caregivers of stroke patients in the qualitative phase of the study.

Exclusion criteria

We excluded patients with severe communication difficulties and those who did not consent to participate.

Recruitment

Eligible patients were identified and enrolled prospectively upon presenting to the participating hospitals. Eligible participants were informed of the study objectives and procedures and subsequently invited to participate.

Data collection

Patients and their caregivers were interviewed using a structured, pre-tested questionnaire administered by the investigators. This questionnaire was adapted from the Registry of Stroke Questionnaire (RES-Q), a standard data-collection tool for stroke that has been developed, standardized, and widely used. A copy of the RES-Q for ischemic, transient ischemic attack, cerebral venous thrombosis, and hemorrhagic stroke has been attached as supplementary files. Data were collected on demographic and socioeconomic factors, including age, sex, education level, occupation, and income, as well as stroke-related clinical characteristics. Data on time metrics included time of symptom onset, time from onset to presentation, and mode of transportation.

Data collection for the qualitative phase was conducted through semi-structured interviews in French, Wolof, and other local languages, with the help of local translators. Interviews were conducted at the study sites. Before each interview, the study objectives and the interview structure were explained to each participant in the presence of their caregiver. The interviews were conducted after informed consent was obtained from the participant and their caregivers. Each interview was audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. MNN conducted all interviews using a semi-structured interview guide to explore factors linked to delayed hospital presentation after stroke. Each interview lasted between 60 and 90 min.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was pre-hospital delay, which was defined as the time from stroke onset to arrival at any of the study, >4h30 after stroke onset. The secondary outcome was factors associated with delayed hospital presentation.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant and stroke characteristics. Bivariate analyses were performed to identify factors associated with delayed presentation. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of delayed presentation. The model was built using a forward elimination with a p-value threshold of 0.05 for entry and removal. Odd’s ratios and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for each predictor variable. Model fit was assessed using the Deviance test. For the Bivariate data analysis, stroke was classified as hemorrhagic or ischemic. We used SPSS version 26 for the statistical analysis. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis following a grounded theory approach. Data collection continued until data saturation was reached.

We recruited 279 participants who met the inclusion criteria. After cleaning the data, 14 participants with incomplete data, especially regarding the time of onset, were excluded from the analysis. A total of 265 participants were included in the study with a mean age of 57.0642 ± 16.8786 [IQR 15–94]. Females accounted for 53.2% vs. 46.8% of males. Factors associated with delayed presentation

Qualitative results

The following barriers to early presentation emerged from the qualitative semi-structured interviews.

Limited awareness of stroke symptoms

Many participants presented late because they were unable to recognize stroke symptoms, which resulted from a limited awareness of these symptoms and the urgency of seeking medical attention.

My mother suddenly started having difficulties walking and I thought it was because she was experiencing pain from her arthritis. I gave her painkillers throughout the day, but there was no improvement. The next day she suddenly started speaking like she was drunk. That’s why I decided to bring her to the hospital (Female, Caregiver)

I woke up in the morning and suddenly discover that I couldn’t move the right side of my body. I initially thought it was fatigue from too much work the previous day. My daughter had to massage it, but there was no improvement. I waited for some days to see if it would go away on its own, but nothing changed. If I had known that it was stroke, I would have come to the hospital as soon as I discovered it. (Male, stroke patient)

When the headache started, we gave our brother painkillers for some days to calm the headache. We took him to a health center, where he received some drips, but the headache persisted. We didn’t know that the severe headache was a sign that he was having a stroke. (Male, Caregiver)

My symptoms started with severe dizziness and I couldn’t stand on my feet. I thought it was a shortage of blood. I decided to chew some herbal medicine first. (Male, stroke patient)

I was talking with my husband and he suddenly started saying things that were not making sense to me. I repeatedly told him that what he was saying didn’t make sense, but he kept going. I thought he was just trying to make me angry, so I left him in the living room and went to bed. When I woke up the next morning, he was still saying things that did not make sense. That’s when I knew that something was not right and I brought him to the hospital. (Female, caregiver)

Visiting traditional healers

Visit to traditional healers before seeking medical care also led to delays in hospital presentation.

I first took my wife to my traditional healer because I thought it was a spiritual attack from a bad spirit. (Male, Caregiver)

My father was discussing with my mum in the living room when out of a sudden, he fell from his seat and could not speak or move the left side of his body. We rushed him to a local traditional healer, thinking he would help. His condition did not improve after several hours. (Female, Caregiver)

Transportation

Patients who were living far away from stroke centers at the time of stroke onset, especially in rural areas, complained of facing challenges with getting a means of transportation to the hospital. This resulted in late presentation.

The symptoms started in the middle of the night, and there was no means to transport my husband to the hospital. I had to wait until the morning to get a taxi. (Male, Caregiver)

It’s not easy to see a car in my village. You have to get a bike to another area where the cars are. If you are lucky, you will find a car when you get there. So it was difficult to bring our father to the hospital on time. (Female, Caregiver)

Socioeconomic barriers

Patients who had financial difficulties at the time of stroke onset delayed presenting to the hospital.

I knew I was having a stroke, but I could not come to the hospital at that time because I didn’t have money. I only came when I had some money.” (Female, stroke patient)

Additionally, dependence on family and caregivers for finances and care was also a common barrier to early presentation, especially among women and elderly patients.

I had to wait for my husband to come back home before coming to the hospital because I didn’t have money. (Female, stroke patient)

My husband is the one who will pay the hospital bill. I tried to reach my husband, but his number was not going through. I had no option but to wait because If I had come without him, I would not have been able to pay for tests or medications.” (Female, stroke patient)

My sister is a widow; her husband died a few years ago and she has been struggling financially. She couldn’t come yesterday because she had no money. She had to call for me to help bring her to the hospital and pay for the bills.” (Female, caregiver)

“When the symptoms started, my children were all at work and there was no body in the house to take me to the hospital.” (Female, stroke patient)

Living alone was also a reported barrier to early presentation. Some patients who were living alone at the time of stroke onset were later discovered by neighbors and taken to the hospital.

I noticed that my neigbour’s door was still closed at 10 am which is unusual as he often leaves for work very early. When I didn’t hear from him after knocking for several minutes, I decided to break open the door, and I found him lying on the floor, unable to talk or move one side of his body. I don’t know for how long he had been lying on the floor or what happened to him. He lives alone. (Male, neighbor of a stroke patient)

Healthcare system challenges

Late referrals from other healthcare facilities contributed to delays. Most patients presented first to a local health center, from where they were referred to the stroke centers. Additionally, delays with ambulance services also contributed to late presentations.

We first consulted in a health center where we spent an entire day before they finally decided to refer us to Fann. She only received some perfusions at the health center. (Male, caregiver)

The ambulance took a long time to arrive, and by the time they arrived, several hours had already passed since my wife’s symptoms started. (Female, caregiver)

Staff shortages and limited resources in some healthcare facilities hindered the timely evaluation and treatment of stroke patients.

There was a very long queue in the first hospital we visited. We waited for almost 2 hours before they consulted my brother only to refer us later without giving him any treatment. (Male, caregiver)

The inability of primary healthcare workers to recognize stroke symptoms also resulted in late presentation at a stroke treatment center.

We took my brother to a health center and the person we met told us that the symptoms were caused by too much exposure to cold at the sea since my brother is a fisherman. He gave us some medications and asked us to go home. (Female, caregiver)

I visited the local hospital in my community when the severe dizziness started and I was told it was because my blood pressure was too high. They gave me medications for high blood pressure and asked me to go home. (Male, stroke patient)

Cultural beliefs

Local beliefs about the causes and treatment of illness influenced healthcare-seeking behaviors.

The way the symptoms started made us to believe that it was a spiritual attack because we had previously heard in the community that such situations are usually supernatural. So we consulted a traditional healer before coming here. (Female, caregiver)

We initially sought advice from a local marabout, who prescribed traditional remedies, but when my husband’s condition worsened, we realized we needed to go to the hospital. (Female, caregiver)

Safety concerns

Some of the patients who developed stroke at night delayed seeking medical care due to safety concerns.

We could not come to the hospital because the symptoms started late in the night and where we live is very unsafe.” one caregiver mentioned. (Male, caregiver)

Severity of stroke

Patients who developed mild stroke symptoms were less likely to seek immediate medical attention, especially when symptoms regressed partially or completely.

On the first day, my left arm suddenly became weak and my phone fell but the weakness resolved without any treatment so I didn’t go to the hospital. A few days later, the same thing happened that’s why I came to the hospital. (Male, stroke patient)

Discussion

We found that a complex interplay of demographic, socioeconomic, and healthcare system-related factors occurring at different levels influenced timely presentation of stroke patients in the study sites. These findings highlight the urgent need for a context-informed multistrategic approach, including improving stroke awareness, collaboration with traditional healers, the development of robust referral systems, and capacity building for healthcare workers.

Our results are consistent with findings from previous studies in other countries [19, 20, 27–30]. Lack of awareness about stroke symptoms among healthcare workers, stroke patients, and caregivers resulted in late presentations to stroke treatment centers. The lack of recognition of certain stroke symptoms, like severe vertigo and headache, led to delayed presentations or referrals. Stroke awareness activities in this setting must be intensive and extensive to ensure that even the most remote populations are reached. Also, education of the local population and healthcare workers on uncommon symptoms of stroke, such as headache, gait problems, and severe vertigo, would be crucial. Most campaigns to improve recognition of stroke symptoms use the FAST or BE FAST acronym, which does not capture uncommon symptoms like headaches, confusion, loss of consciousness, and seizures [31–34]. Development and validation of other culturally appropriate acronyms that capture uncommon symptoms could reduce delays in seeking medical attention, increasing opportunities for timely intervention and potentially better outcomes. Moreover, given the low literacy level in Senegal and the strong attachment of the local populations to religious and cultural practices, stroke awareness campaigns and educational interventions must be culturally and linguistically sensitive to ensure public understanding and acceptance of stroke awareness messages. Involvement rather than exclusion of religious and traditional leaders (who are often highly esteemed by the local inhabitants) in stroke awareness-raising initiatives may increase uptake of both stroke education and stroke services.

Socioeconomic factors also influenced the time of hospital presentation after stroke. People experiencing financial challenges presented late to the hospital. Similarly, women who were dependent on their husbands or were widows delayed seeking care at the onset of stroke due to the lack of personal finances. Despite major advances toward gender equality, women in Senegal continue to encounter disparities in employment, education, and economic opportunities [35]. Gendered social norms often dictate the roles women can play and limit their access to income-generating activities especially in the rural areas [35–37]. It is not uncommon to see homes where women are not allowed to decide on financial matters and have to be under the guardianship of the husband. Men are often the lone source of income for the family and are in charge of household-related expenditures including healthcare [37]. Consequently, the health-seeking behaviors of many women are influenced by their husbands. Additionally, some men may not want their wives to see male doctors without them, so they may ask their wives to wait until they are present to seek care. Such behavior can result in significant delays in presentation and poorer outcomes. In such settings, stroke interventions must be gender-sensitive to reduce disparities and include strategies that address gendered social norms. These strategies should be designed for and with women, but also with community members and, traditional and religious leaders.

Health insurance coverage and targeted financial assistance programs can significantly reduce the cost associated with healthcare and ensure that even those with the lowest socioeconomic status can present early in stroke-treating centers without fear of the financial burden. An insurance program (Plan SESAME) for elderly people aged 60 years and above currently exists in Senegal, though the coverage is limited; MRI and some essential medications are not covered [38]. Another insurance scheme for retirees, Institution de Provoyance Retraites du Senegal (IPRES) is also operational and has a program targeting low-income people [39]. However, more than half of the local populations do not have any health insurance. Interventions and policies aimed at reducing financial barriers to stroke care could include community-based insurance schemes to assist low-income families, provision of government subsidies for health insurance premiums, offering of discounts on out-of-pocket payments for the poor, pooling of local community resources to finance healthcare, implementation of income-tested subsidies, and the elimination of surprise billings. Also, the implementation of telehealth and remote monitoring could reduce the need for in-person visits, thus lowering the costs of stroke care.

Patients who initially presented to a health center were three times more likely to present late, as were those who arrived from another stroke-treating center. Improving coordination and communication between primary healthcare centers and specialized stroke centers is crucial for streamlining the referral process and ensuring that patients receive timely and appropriate referrals. While strategies to reroute patients from primary healthcare facilities to stroke centers may address this issue, it would equally be important to strengthen the infrastructure and capacity of the stroke centers to accommodate the increased patient turnover [40]. Moreover, primary healthcare providers should be regularly trained and empowered on how to recognize and respond to stroke cases at the primary care level level [41]. Continuing medical education programs will enhance competency in managing stroke patients at the primary healthcare level. Additionally, having clear stroke referral pathways and local stroke guidelines will help ensure quick referrals and treatments at every level, which can lead to better results and survival rates [42, 43]. Where timely referral may not be feasible due to factors such as transportation, telemedicine programs could facilitate communication between primary healthcare providers and a stroke expert [44]. In the absence of standard telemedicine platforms due to resource constraints, telephone calls have been shown to be equally effective [45]. Effective and efficient triage protocols would reduce waiting times and ensure expedited transfers to specialized stroke centers.

Distance to healthcare facilities and limited access to transportation were also barriers to early presentation, particularly in rural areas Despite the availability of ambulance services in Dakar, only 9.4% of the participants used them in our study. This may be due to a lack of awareness of the existence of these services or the limited number of ambulance vehicles available to meet the high demand. Increasing the number and geographical coverage of the ambulance services will ensure that even rural stroke patients would be transported early to stroke centers. While increasing the number of ambulance services can facilitate access to healthcare facilities, it would be important to raise awareness among the local populations on the existence and utility of these services, as availability does not always mean uptake. Also, equipping these ambulances with advanced diagnostic tools and a multidisciplinary team of first responders for stroke will allow for immediate stroke response and treatment in the field [46]. These measures will reduce the time from stroke onset to treatment and potentially improve outcomes. Community-based transportation programs, such as volunteer-driven cars and demand-responsive service that provides free door-to-door transportation, can facilitate transportation to stroke centers where ambulance services are not available [47]. Establishing partnerships with local ride-hailing service providers could allow users to request emergency rides at any time and access convenient and reliable transport.

Recruiting and training individuals from the community as lay volunteers or community stroke workers is another feasible strategy to reduce delays in hospital presentation after stroke. These volunteers can educate the community about stroke warnings, teach people how to recognize the signs of stroke, inform them about transport options in case of stroke, and assist stroke victims in locating the nearest stroke center. These can significantly improve early presentation to stroke centers.

Limitations

The cross-sectional and qualitative methods used in our study do not allow us to effectively assess causality. Also, the study was hospital-based and did not include community-dwelling patients who might not have presented to the study sites. Moreover, our study population was recruited from specialized stroke centers in an urban area, and so our findings may not be generalizable to other populations, such as those in rural communities.

Conclusion

We found a high prevalence of late presentation after stroke in our study. Early presentation to a stroke center could potentially improve stroke outcomes in Senegal, but patients face many barriers to early presentation. The good news is that these barriers are actionable and can be addressed to improve timely presentation. Future studies to evaluate some of the proposed strategies could provide evidence on the most feasible and effective solutions to the multilevel barriers to early presentation after stroke in Senegal.
