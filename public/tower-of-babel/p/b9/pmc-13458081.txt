Structural basis of the sweat odorant HMHA recognition by the human odorant receptor OR52E8
Authors: Chulwon Choi, Hyeonsung Byeon, Juho Lee, Hong Geun Lee, Chaok Seok, Hee-Jung Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract3-Hydroxy-3-methyl hexanoic acid (HMHA), a key component of human axillary sweat, specifically activates the human odorant receptor OR52E8. However, the molecular basis of this recognition remains elusive. A recent structural study of an odorant-bound consensus OR52 (OR52cs), a representative of the OR52 family, suggested the molecular mechanism for carboxylic acid odorant recognition. Because native OR52E8 exhibits low surface expression, hindering structural analysis, we engineered the odorant-binding pocket of OR52cs to mimic that of OR52E8. The resulting engineered receptor, OR52csE8pocket, recapitulates OR52E8 odorant specificity, including chiral selectivity for the (R)-HMHA enantiomer. We determined the cryo-EM structure of the HMHA–OR52csE8pocket–Gs complex, revealing the HMHA binding pocket and enabling structure-guided interpretation of receptor specificity. The C3 hydroxyl branch of HMHA, essential for OR52E8 activation, forms a polar interaction with N5×42, a residue unique to OR52E8 within the OR52 family. The C3 methyl group, important for enantiomer-specific HMHA recognition, interacts with residues in TM3 and TM6. We further identified natural OR52E8 variants with altered responses to HMHA, which may underlie inter-individual differences in HMHA sensitivity and perception. Together, our findings suggest the molecular determinants of OR52E8 specificity and provide molecular insights into the genetic variability in human body odor perception.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, G protein-coupled receptors

Introduction

Olfaction, the sense of smell, allows us to detect and interpret volatile chemical signals from the environment1, and plays a crucial role in various physiological and social functions, including gustation, safety assessment, and memory formation1–4. Odorant receptors (ORs), members of the class A G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) family, directly recognize odorants and initiate the olfactory process1. The human genome possesses 388 functional ORs, which are broadly categorized into two classes, class I ORs (OR51, 52, and 56 families) and class II ORs (OR1 − 14 families), based on their evolutionary origins and chemical properties of their odorants5. The large repertoire of human ORs and combinatorial nature of OR−odorant pairing enable us to perceive and discriminate an immense variety of olfactory signals5,6. The interesting feature of ORs is their high genetic variation, leading to individual differences in detection threshold, perceived intensity, and even the pleasantness of the same olfactory stimulus7–10.

Several human ORs have been reported to detect human body odorants11,12. Odorants in human sweat, derived mainly from axillary secretions, include organosulfur compounds, carboxylic acids, and steroids13. In human axillary sweat, saturated carboxylic acids containing a 3-hydroxyl group and a methyl branch (C3 or C4) with varying chain lengths (C6 to C10) have been previously identified by GC-MS analysis13–16. Of these, 3-hydroxy-3-methyl hexanoic acid (HMHA) stands out as the most abundant carboxylic acid and a human-specific sweat odorant14,15. HMHA, as a representative human sweat odorant, has been the focus of studies on gender differences, role in face perception, and individual variability in cognitive response, underscoring its significance as a major body odorant17–19.

From a molecular perspective, HMHA has been reported to activate human OR52E8, a member of the class I OR52 family that is characterized by recognition of carboxylic acid odorants20,21. However, the structural architecture of the odorant-binding pocket of OR52E8 remains unclear, leaving the molecular details of HMHA binding and the molecular basis for individual differences in HMHA perception unresolved18. Recent structural studies of propionate-bound human OR51E2 and octanoate-bound consensus OR52 (OR52cs) have provided valuable insights into how class I ORs recognize linear carboxylic acid odorants22,23. In contrast, no structural characterizations have been reported for branched carboxylate odorants like HMHA, leaving a significant gap in our understanding of these molecules’ interactions with ORs and the effect of their branch chirality on OR activation.

The structure determination of native ORs has been hindered by major challenges in their surface expression and purification. To address this issue, the “consensus strategy” has been applied to several OR families24, where the increased expression and stability of consensus ORs enabled structure determination of them in odorant-bound active state23,25. Although the structures of consensus ORs have provided valuable insights into odorant recognition in each family, they do not fully reflect the odorant-binding properties of specific native ORs. While recent advances in deep learning-based protein structure prediction26,27 and protein-ligand docking28,29 have opened new possibilities for understanding OR structures, accurate prediction of OR−odorant complexes remains difficult due to the potential for diverse conformations and binding poses of odorants within the pocket.

To overcome this limitation, we present a strategy that integrates pocket engineering with structural analysis using consensus ORs as structural templates. Applying this approach, we investigated HMHA recognition by human native OR52E8. Our engineered OR52cs, designed to mimic the odorant pocket of OR52E8 (OR52csE8pocket), successfully achieved the odorant specificity of OR52E8. The stable surface expression and structural rigidity of OR52csE8pocket enabled determination of the cryo-EM structure of HMHA-bound OR52csE8pocket in its Gs-coupled active state, which revealed detailed interactions between HMHA and OR52csE8pocket. Our structural study, together with docking simulations, mutagenesis study, and polymorphism analysis of OR52E8, suggests the molecular basis for HMHA recognition by OR52E8 and offers insights into individual differences in human body odor perception.

Results

Selective activation of human OR52E8 by HMHA

HMHA is secreted as a mixture of (S)- and (R)-HMHA at its C3 chiral center (Fig. 1a)16. To confirm the reported activation of OR52E8 by HMHA20, we performed a cell-based CRE luciferase reporter assay30, a widely used method to detect Gs- or Golf-mediated signaling of ORs, using racemic HMHA. The results showed that HMHA specifically activated OR52E8, but not other tested OR52 family subtypes or OR52cs (Fig. 1a).

Given that all tested ORs, except for OR52E6, possess highly conserved R6×59 and H45×52 (superscript numbers refer to the GPCRdb numbering scheme31), which are known to form electrostatic interactions with the carboxyl group of odorants (Supplementary Fig. 1)23, we hypothesized that other molecular features of HMHA, specifically the hydroxyl and methyl groups at the C3 position, contribute to its specific interaction with OR52E8. To test this hypothesis, we performed CRE signaling assays using chemical analogs of HMHA lacking one or both of these chemical groups at the C3 position. Neither unbranched hexanoic acid (HXA) nor hexanoic acid with only a single branch at the C3 position, such as 3-methyl hexanoic acid (HXAC3-CH3) or 3-hydroxyl hexanoic acid (HXAC3-OH), activated OR52E8 (Fig. 1b). These findings suggest that both the hydroxyl and methyl groups at the C3 position are necessary for activation of OR52E8.

The observed importance of C3 branching prompted us to investigate whether HMHA chirality affects the OR52E8 response, which has not been examined previously. Notably, our functional assays revealed that OR52E8 exhibits a strong preference for (R)-HMHA over (S)-HMHA (Fig. 1c), even though (S)-HMHA is known to be the more physiologically abundant enantiomer in human axillary sweat16. This result suggests that the two C3-branches, each with a distinct chemical property, hydroxyl or methyl, must be precisely positioned within the odorant-binding pocket of OR52E8 to stabilize its active conformation and transduce downstream signaling.

Engineering the odorant-binding pocket of OR52cs to recapitulate OR52E8 specificity

To gain molecular insights into the interaction between HMHA and human OR52E8, we aimed to determine the structure of HMHA-bound OR52E8. However, the purification of OR52E8 for structural study was unsuccessful mainly because of poor surface expression of OR52E8, a common obstacle in structural study of mammalian ORs (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 2a). Our strategy to overcome this limitation was to adopt OR52cs, which shares 73% sequence identity with OR52E8 but exhibits robust surface expression (Fig. 2a), and to engineer its odorant-binding pocket to mimic the odorant preference of OR52E8. To identify the residues constituting the odorant-binding pocket of OR52E8 suitable for HMHA, we first predicted the structure of HMHA-bound OR52E8 by running AlphaFold232. When we predicted the structure of OR52E8 with multiple sequence alignment (MSA), the AlphaFold2-generated model (OR52E8MSA) showed high structural similarity to the apo structure of OR52cs (PDB:8J46), which is characterized by a large opening between the extracellular segments of transmembrane helices (TMs) 5 and 6 (Supplementary Fig. 2b)23. Considering that several key residues of the odorant-binding pocket in OR52cs, such as R6×59 and F6×55, undergo rearrangement upon odorant binding (Supplementary Figs. 1a, 2b)23, it seems reasonable to use the active-like structural model of OR52E8 (OR52E8Active) for successful docking simulations of HMHA. To generate a structural model of OR52E8Active, we employed the AlphaFold2 pipeline without MSA32, using the active structure of octanoate (OCA)-bound OR52cs (PDB:8HTI) as a template (Supplementary Fig. 2c)23. The resulting model of OR52E8Active was relaxed using Rosetta FastRelax33, which had high structural similarity to the template structure of OR52cs, with an RMSD of 1 Å for 288 Cα atoms (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Next, (R)-HMHA was docked into the orthosteric pocket of OR52E8Active using GALigandDock, which minimally perturbs the receptor structure during flexible ligand docking (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 2c)34. As expected, the docked (R)-HMHA models were positioned within the odorant-binding pocket formed by TMs 3-6 and ECL2, where each formed an electrostatic interaction with the conserved R6×59, supporting the reliability of our predicted structures (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). However, the high conformational flexibility of the tail of (R)-HMHA, which is composed of flexible sp³-carbon atoms, resulted in substantial variation in orientation and branching direction among individual (R)-HMHA models, posing challenges for accurate conformation prediction based solely on docking simulations (Fig. 2b). Nevertheless, our predicted models identified ten pocket residues that directly interact with HMHA (Fig. 2b).

Comparison of these residues with those in the OR52cs revealed that five of the ten pocket residues differ at the corresponding positions (Fig. 2c). To mimic the odorant-binding pocket of OR52E8, we introduced five mutations at the differing positions (G1113×37A, F1614×60L, V2045×42N, A2055×43I, and I2085×46L) to OR52cs and generated OR52csE8pocket (75% sequence identity with OR52E8, Supplementary Fig. 3). This chimeric receptor exhibited a higher level of membrane surface expression than OR52E8 and a level comparable to that of the OR52cs, as detected by surface enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and confocal fluorescence imaging (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 2a). Notably, the odorant preference of OR52csE8pocket was successfully switched from that of OR52cs to OR52E8, which was demonstrated by abolished response to the linear carboxylate HXA and gain of response to the branched carboxylate HMHA in our cAMP-Glo assays (Fig. 2d, e). Furthermore, OR52csE8pocket also achieved the enantiomer preference of OR52E8, exhibiting a strong response toward (R)-HMHA and a weak response toward (S)-HMHA, implying the odorant specificity of OR52csE8pocket was successfully reprogrammed to mimic the pocket of OR52E8 (Fig. 2f). Thus, we conducted structural study using OR52csE8pocket to understand the interaction between HMHA and OR52E8.

Structure of the HMHA–OR52csE8pocket–miniGs complex

We generated a miniGαs-fused OR52csE8pocket construct to enhance G protein coupling, following a strategy previously used for structure determination of human OR51E222. OR52csE8pocket-miniGαs was successfully purified and assembled into a complex with Gβ1γ2 and complex-stabilizing Nb3535 in the presence of racemic HMHA (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). The purified complex was used for cryo-EM analysis, yielding a map with a global resolution of 2.84 Å and a receptor-focused resolution of 3.29 Å (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 4c-g, and Table 1). The overall architecture of OR52csE8pocket exhibited a high degree of structural similarity to the previously reported cryo-EM structure of OCA-bound OR52cs.

Despite the moderate resolution of the map, we could resolve the side chains of residues forming the odorant pocket and an oval-shaped density corresponding to HMHA (Fig. 3a). We modeled (R)-HMHA into the density, given its preferential activation of OR52csE8pocket and OR52E8 over (S)-HMHA, and guided by chemically plausible interactions with surrounding pocket residues, steric constraints, and geometric restraints (Supplementary Fig. 4g). Although the density of (R)-HMHA could not be fully resolved, likely due to the limited resolution and odorant flexibility, the density at a threshold of σ = 0.35 encompasses nearly all of the modeled branches (Supplementary Fig. 4g). Notably, similar limitations in resolving small odorant substituents are commonly observed in recently reported native and consensus OR structures25,36,37. In this structure, (R)-HMHA bound to OR52csE8pocket occupied a position similar to that of odorants in the cryo-EM structures of OR52cs and OR51E2, supporting a conserved binding mode of carboxylate odorants among these receptors (Fig. 3b).

Structure-guided analysis of HMHA recognition by OR52E8

Because OR52csE8pocket was engineered to recapitulate the odorant specificity of OR52E8, the structural features observed in this complex provide a basis for understanding odorant recognition in OR52E8. Based on this structure, we identified key interactions between (R)-HMHA and surrounding pocket residues, and validated their functional importance by mutagenesis of OR52E8.

Two positively charged residues, R2656×59 and H18345×52 in OR52csE8pocket, form electrostatic interactions with the carboxyl group of (R)-HMHA (Fig. 3c). The corresponding residues in OR52E8 (R2646×59 and H18345×42) were functionally validated, as alanine substitutions (R2646×59A and H18345×42A) completely abolished HMHA-induced OR52E8 signaling (Fig. 3d and supplementary Fig. 5). In our structure, the side chain of N2045×42 forms a polar interaction with the 3-hydroxyl branch of (R)-HMHA (Fig. 3c). This interaction is critical in OR52E8, as the N2045×42A mutation eliminated the response to (R)-HMHA treatment (Fig. 3d). This result is in line with the loss of activity toward HXAC3-CH3, emphasizing the importance of the polar interaction between N2045×42 and the 3-hydroxyl group of HMHA (Fig. 1b). In the engineered OR52csE8pocket, this position (5x42) corresponds to one of the engineered sites, and the side chain of N2045×42 is shifted by approximately 1 Å toward (R)-HMHA compared to V2045×42 in OR52cs (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 6a). Notably, sequence analysis shows that residues at the 5×42 position in other human OR52 subtypes are mostly occupied by small hydrophobic residues, such as valine, supporting the signaling assay data that HMHA specifically activated OR52E8 (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Although N2045×42 plays an important role in HMHA recognition, introducing V2045×42N into OR52cs alone was insufficient to confer HMHA responsiveness, suggesting that other odorant pocket residues contribute to OR52E8-like odorant preference (supplementary Fig. 6b). In addition to carboxyl group and 3-hydroxyl group, (R)-HMHA has hydrophobic moieties, including a 3-methyl branch and the hydrocarbon tail (C4, C5, and C6). H1073×33 and F2616×55 in OR52csE8pocket form hydrophobic contacts with the 3-methyl branch (Fig. 3c). The corresponding residues in OR52E8 (H1073×33 and F2606×55) were functionally validated, as alanine mutations (H1073×33A and F2606×55A) resulted in complete loss of signaling upon (R)-HMHA treatment (Fig. 3d). These results, consistent with the loss of signaling in response to HXAC3-OH (Fig. 1b), support the significance of the 3-methyl branch of HMHA for OR52E8 activation. The hydrocarbon tail of (R)-HMHA was stabilized by van der Waals interactions with surrounding residues T1103×36, L1614×60, I2055×43, and L2085×46 in OR52csE8pocket. Among these, L1614×60, I2055×43, and L2085×46 were introduced into OR52cs to generate the OR52csE8pocket (Fig. 2c). L1614×60, which replaces the bulky Phe in OR52cs, likely creates space to accommodate (R)-HMHA, as Phe would otherwise cause a steric clash with the ligand (Fig. 3c and supplementary Fig. 6c). L1614×60 and L2085×46 are important for (R)-HMHA binding to OR52E8, as alanine substitutions at these positions abolished signaling activity (Fig. 3d). In contrast, I2055×43 did not contribute significantly to OR52E8 activity, since its alanine mutation had no effect on signaling (Fig. 3d). Consistently, I2055×43 formed only limited interaction with (R)-HMHA in the structure (Fig. 3c).

Having validated the odorant-binding mode through structural analysis and mutagenesis, we revisited the docking models of (R)-HMHA in OR52E8 generated in the initial stage of this study. Among multiple binding poses, we selected the docking model with the lowest RMSD relative to the cryo-EM structure, as this pose best preserves the odorant orientation and key interactions identified experimentally (Supplementary Fig. 2d, pose 5). Comparison between the cryo-EM structure and the selected docking model showed that the overall binding modes are highly consistent, with minor differences primarily in the orientation of the methyl group of the hydrocarbon tail (Supplementary Fig. 2e). This variation likely reflects a less tightly packed region of the binding pocket, allowing limited rotational flexibility of the methyl group of the hydrocarbon tail.

Taken together, the cryo-EM structure of the engineered OR52csE8pocket demonstrates how odorant preference can be switched from the linear carboxylate (HXA) to HMHA, and provides mechanistic insights into the specific recognition of (R)-HMHA by OR52E8.

Enantiomer-specific recognition of HMHA by OR52E8

To understand the molecular basis of enantiomer-specific recognition, we generated docking models of (S)-HMHA-bound OR52E8. Among multiple binding poses, we selected a representative model for comparative analysis that preserves the ionic and polar interactions of the carboxyl and 3-hydroxyl groups observed in the (R)-HMHA-bound structure (Supplementary Fig. 7a-c). Although (S)-HMHA elicited only limited responses in our signaling assays (Fig. 1c), the docking model revealed that most pocket residues interacting with (R)-HMHA are shared with (S)-HMHA (Supplementary Fig. 7d). The major difference lies in the orientation of the C3 methyl branch. In the docking model, the C3 methyl branch of (S)-HMHA points toward TM5 and forms van der Waals contacts with I2055×43 (Supplementary Fig. 7d). In contrast, the C3 methyl branch of (R)-HMHA faces toward TM3/6 and interacts with H1073×33 and F2606×55 (Fig. 3c). Consistently, mutagenesis showed that alanine mutation of residues essential for carboxylate recognition (H18345×52 and R2646×59) reduced signaling by both enantiomers, whereas I2055×43A selectively impaired (S)-HMHA and F2606×55A selectively impaired (R)-HMHA responses (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 7e). These results indicated that the appropriate positioning of C3 methyl group is important for enantiomer-specific HMHA recognition by OR52E8.

Because (S)-HMHA activated OR52E8 and OR52csE8pocket less efficiently than (R)-HMHA, we hypothesized that (S)-HMHA does not remain stably engaged in the odorant pocket and therefore cannot sufficiently stabilize the active conformation. To test this, we performed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of (S)- or (R)-HMHA-bound OR52csE8pocket−miniGαs complexes embedded in a lipid bilayer (Supplementary Table 1). In three replicates of 1 µs MD simulations, (R)-HMHA remained stably positioned with an average RMSD of 2.0 Å (Supplementary Fig. 8a), whereas (S)-HMHA showed higher conformational variability with an average RMSD of 3.6 Å, suggesting the odorant pocket of OR52csE8pocket is more appropriate for engaging the (R)-enantiomer (Supplementary Fig. 8b). Moreover, the instability of (S)-HMHA was accompanied by increased fluctuation of key recognition residues, including R6×59 and F6×55, and greater flexibility of the upper extracellular segment of TM6 compared with the (R)-HMHA-bound receptor (Supplementary Fig. 8c, d). Since inward movement of the extracellular segment of TM6 represents a characteristic conformational change associated with OR52cs activation (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c)23, these findings suggest that (S)-HMHA is less effective in stabilizing the active conformation of OR52csE8pocket. Consistent with this, MD simulations performed in the absence of miniGαs further showed that (S)-HMHA is less capable of maintaining the active conformation, as indicated by increased fluctuations in distances between the intracellular region of TM3–TM6 and the extracellular region of TM5–TM6 (Supplementary Fig. 8e-g).

Taken together, our structural and MD analyses, along with mutagenesis, suggest that the reduced signaling efficiency of (S)-HMHA is likely due to its limited ability to engage F2606×55, which leads to incomplete stabilization of the active conformation of TM6 and weaker odorant-receptor complex formation.

OR52E8 preference for HMHA among various 3-hydroxy acids in human sweat

Along with HMHA, several saturated 3-hydroxy carboxylic acids with varying chain lengths (C6 to C10) and methyl branch positions (none, C3-CH3, or C4-CH3) have previously been detected in human sweat, including 3-hydroxy-4-methyl hexanoic acid (HXAC3-OH/C4-CH3), several methyl-branched 3-hydroxy heptanoic acids (HPAC3-OH/C3-CH3 and HPAC3-OH/C4-CH3) and octanoic acid (OCAC3-OH, OCAC3-OH/C3-CH3, and OCAC3-OH/C4-CH3), 3-hydroxy-4-methyl nonanoic acid (KNAC3-OH/C4-CH3), and 3-hydroxy decanoic acid (DKAC3-OH)14. Although their abundances are extremely low compared to HMHA (0.1 − 6% of HMHA levels), the relative proportion of each species varies among individuals, potentially contributing to characteristic differences in body odor14. Based on our HMHA–OR52E8 structural model and signaling assays indicating that the OR52E8-specific N5×42 interacts with the 3-hydroxyl group of HMHA, we hypothesized that the odorant pocket of OR52E8 might also accommodate other 3-hydroxy acids found in human sweat. To test this, we selected several representative 3-hydroxy acids and analyzed their ability to activate OR52E8.

First, we assessed the effect of methyl branch position of odorant on OR52E8 activation. In our signaling assay, HXAC3-OH/C4-CH3, which carries a 4-methyl branch instead of the 3-methyl branch in HMHA, failed to activate OR52E8, indicating that a methyl branch at the C3 position is important for receptor activation (Fig. 4a). Given that C3-CH3 and the hydrocarbon tail of (R)-HMHA form an extensive hydrophobic interaction network with H1073×33 and F2606×55, C4-CH3 of HXAC3-OH/C4-CH3 stereoisomers cannot establish a comparable interaction network with the receptor, reiterating the importance of properly positioned methyl branch.

Next, we tested 3-hydroxy carboxylic acids with longer hydrocarbon chains than HMHA. Both HPAC3-OH/C3-CH3 and OCAC3-OH/C3-CH3, which contain a C3-OH and a C3-CH3, activated OR52E8. Compared with HMHA, HPAC3-OH/C3-CH3 elicited a markedly stronger response, whereas OCAC3-OH/C3-CH3 showed a reduced response (Fig. 4a). This suggests that the optimal hydrocarbon tail length of 3-hydroxy-3-methyl carboxylic acid for receptor binding is six or seven carbons. Docking simulations indicated that C8-length tail of (3R)-OCAC3-OH/C3-CH3 adopts bent conformation due to spatial constraints imposed by T1103×36, A1113×37, and L2085×46 at the base of the odorant pocket, suggesting an optimal chain length is likely shorter than C8 (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 9). Other sweat components, OCAC3-OH and DKAC3-OH, both lacking C3-CH3 showed no response, confirming the combined requirement for a 3-hydroxy-3-methyl branch and an optimal tail length (Fig. 4a).

Natural OR52E8 variants with differential sensitivity to HMHA

A previous study revealed substantial inter-individual variability in sensitivity to HMHA, along with a high prevalence of HMHA-specific anosmia, with 8% and 19% among French and Malagasy participants, respectively18. We hypothesized that these differences in perception may arise from polymorphisms in OR52E8. Given the high genetic diversity of ORs, numerous natural variants of human OR52E8 have been reported10. Among the 314 amino acid positions in OR52E8, non-synonymous substitutions have been identified at 272 positions, including 37 severe mutations, such as start-lost, in-frame insertion/deletion, frame-shift, and stop-gained, which may abolish receptor expression or function (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Fig. 10). Missense mutations have also been reported at 263 positions, although their functional effects remain largely unknown (Fig. 5a, b).

Natural variants of OR52E8 exhibit differential responses to HMHA.

First, we focused on natural OR52E8 variants carrying missense mutations at residues predicted to form the odorant-binding pocket. Our model identified pocket variants such as H1073×33Y, A1113×37T, L1614×60M, H18345×52R/P/L, N2045×42S/T/I, L2085×46S, F2606×55L, and R2646×59H/C. Functional assays showed that R2646×59H and R2646×59C, mutations in a key residue for carboxylate recognition with allele frequencies of about 0.001% and 0.007%, respectively, completely abolished HMHA responses (Fig. 5b, c). Likewise, F2606×55, located at a critical site for van der Waals contact with the odorant’s tail or branch, when mutated to Leu (F2606×55L), showed no activity (Fig. 5c and supplementary Fig. 5). Similarly, N2045×42, which interacts with the 3-hydroxy group of HMHA, lost activity when mutated to serine or threonine (N2045×42S/T), suggesting that the amide side chain of Asn is essential as a hydrogen-bond acceptor for tight interaction with the 3-hydroxy group of HMHA. (Fig. 5c). The potential for diverse alterations of pocket residues suggested that other OR52 subtypes could acquire OR52E8-like odorant specificity through a mutation at position 5×42 to Asn. However, our polymorphism exploration revealed that such a variant was reported only for OR52E6, which does not possess carboxyl head-interacting R6×59 (Supplementary Table 2), further supporting the pivotal role of OR52E8 in recognizing HMHA.

Because odorant-binding pocket mutations are rare, with allele frequencies ranging from 0.00006% to 0.007%, we next screened additional variants with allele frequencies above 0.01% (more than one in 10,000 alleles) for HMHA responsiveness (Fig. 5b). Excluding variants with missense mutations in residues with outward-facing side chains, such as N902×67D, H17345×42P, L2887×48F, and V2957×55D, we identified the I291×35F variant, the most frequent missense mutation in OR52E8 with an allele frequency of about 5.7% (about one in 18 alleles). This mutant showed no response to HMHA in our signaling assay, suggesting that individuals homozygous for the I291×35F variant may be unable to detect HMHA via OR52E8 (Fig. 5d). In our model, I291×35 participates in hydrophobic packing with N-terminal residues, I19Nt, L22Nt, and V25Nt at a distance of 3.5–3.9 Å, implying mutation to the bulkier phenylalanine likely disrupts structural integrity (Fig. 5e). H1023×28, the second most frequent variant position (about one in 145 alleles), may also contribute to structural integrity via polar interactions with the main chains of A782×56 and C18145×50 (Fig. 5f). Replacement of H1023×28 with tyrosine reduced, but did not abolish OR52E8 function, as tyrosine may partially maintain similar polar contacts (Fig. 5d). For F2526×47, which is part of conserved FY6×48xP motif in the human OR52 family, its mutation to Val rendered OR52E8 non-functional (Fig. 5d, g). Two additional variants in ICL2, R133ICL2Q and T135ICL2A, each with an allele frequency of approximately 0.1%, were also tested (Fig. 5b). Our OR52E8−miniGαs model predicted that T135ICL2 forms a polar contact with the carbonyl oxygen of R38Gαs, whereas R133ICL2 does not interact with Gαs (Fig. 5h). Consistent with these predictions, R133ICL2Q retained wild-type activity, while T135ICL2A showed markedly reduced HMHA responses (Fig. 5d).

Altogether, we demonstrate that polymorphisms in OR52E8, particularly natural variants that form the odorant-binding pocket, contribute to structural integrity or are involved in G protein coupling, can modulate the ability of OR52E8 to recognize HMHA.

Discussion

Human body odor is composed of diverse substances secreted from the axillary gland. Among the odoriferous chemicals, HMHA is the most abundant compound, characterized by its pungent and rancid odor. In this study, we employed an integrated strategy encompassing pocket engineering, structural analysis, and computational approach to understand the molecular details of HMHA recognition by human OR52E8. By engineering the odorant-binding pocket of OR52cs, we successfully reprogrammed it to acquire the odorant specificity of OR52E8 while retaining high surface expression. This approach provides an alternative strategy to elucidate how specific native ORs recognize their cognate odorants at the molecular level. Our cryo-EM structure of HMHA-bound OR52csE8pocket suggests that the odorant pocket of OR52E8 is specifically tailored for HMHA. In particular, the interaction between the conserved R6×59 and the carboxyl group of HMHA is consistent with the general mechanism of carboxylate recognition in class I ORs. Beyond this conserved feature, our structure reveals OR52E8-specific interactions, with N2045×42 recognizing the 3-hydroxyl group and several hydrophobic residues engaging the 3-methyl group and hydrocarbon tail. Mutagenesis studies revealed that most residues in the predicted pocket are essential for function, supporting the reliability of our model and the specificity of OR52E8 for HMHA.

Despite the ORs’ reputation for promiscuous recognition of various odorants, our results reveal that OR52E8 discriminates with notable specificity based on odorant branching and chirality. HMHA has two branches at the C3 position: a methyl and a hydroxyl group. Our signaling assays demonstrated that both branches with the appropriate stereochemistry are necessary for OR52E8 activation. The importance of odorant chirality in OR activation has been demonstrated previously. For example, in OR1A1, a class II OR, residues in TM3–TM5 contribute to distinct binding orientations of carvone enantiomers38. In contrast, in OR52E8, chiral selectivity of HMHA arises from interactions of the C3-methyl branch with residues in TM3 (H1073×33) and TM6 (F2606×55). These findings suggest that ORs employ receptor-specific mechanisms to achieve chiral discrimination.

Human sweat is known to contain not only HMHA but also other 3-hydroxy carboxylic acids. While a previous study reported that 3-hydroxy heptanoic acid with a C4-CH3 branch has a similar odor to HMHA39, our signaling assays showed no activation of OR52E8 by this compound, suggesting a preference for a C3-CH3 configuration. Further experiments will be required to determine whether OR52E8 or other ORs are involved in the perception of this compound. With respect to methyl branch position, OR52E8 exhibits high selectivity for the C3-methyl group but also shows weak responses to structurally similar 3-hydroxy hexanoic acids with C2-CH3 or C5-CH3 branches (Supplementary Fig. 11). Although these compounds have not been identified in human sweat, this result suggests that OR52E8, while highly selective toward HMHA, may possess some flexibility in odorant binding. These data provide insights into the ligand specificity of OR52E8, which could inform the design of small-molecule antagonists targeting this receptor.

ORs are a highly polymorphic gene family, and natural variants of ORs influence odor recognition8,9. While the effects of OR variants have been studied, the relationship between structural roles and functional outcomes of variants remains poorly understood. In this study, we analyzed the functional roles of natural OR52E8 variants and linked them to structural insights. Our results demonstrate that variants affecting odorant binding, G protein coupling, or structural stability exhibit varying responses to HMHA. This study highlights how structural information can aid in understanding the functional impacts of natural variants of ORs, and suggests that individuals differ not only in the chemical composition of their body sweat odor (ligand-level variation) but also in their perception of sweat odorants (receptor-level variation), similar to the reported case of asparagus-induced urinary odor40. Further studies on other body odor receptors will be necessary to validate this idea. However, the impact of OR polymorphisms on odor recognition is complex and depends on individual genotypes, such as homozygous or heterozygous status and the cis/trans arrangement of multiple variants10. For example, a previous study showed that individuals with a heterozygous loss-of-function variant of OR7D4 exhibited reduced perception intensity for steroidal odorants9, suggesting that variations in each OR allele can cumulatively affect the perception of its odorant. Thus, cognitive experiments involving individuals with identified genotypes for OR52E8 are needed to validate the functional impact of OR52E8 variants and their direct association with HMHA recognition.

Overall, our approach can be extended to other native ORs by leveraging the recently published structures of OR51E222 and several consensus ORs23,25 as templates for pocket engineering. This strategy provides a versatile framework for elucidating the molecular mechanisms of odorant recognition and for understanding how OR polymorphisms underlie individual differences in odor perception.

Methods

Cell lines

All signaling assays were performed using the Hana3A cell line, kindly provided by Dr. Matsunami41. Hana3A cells were cultured in DMEM high-glucose medium (Cytiva) supplemented with fetal bovine serum (GW Vitek) and antibiotics (GenDEPOT) in cell culture incubator with 5% CO2, 37 °C conditions. Cells were maintained at a confluency range of 30–90%, and their condition was checked using an optical microscope. Cells with 5-10 passages were used for the signaling assays.

DNA constructs

DNA sequences encoding human OR52 family subtypes, OR52cs, and OR52csE8pocket are codon optimized to Homo sapiens and synthesized in the form of fragments (Integrated DNA Technologies). Each DNA fragment was cloned into the pcDNA 3.1 vector using Hifi DNA assembly system (New England Biolabs). At the N-terminus of ORs, Lucy signal sequence (MRPQILLLLALLTLGLA), FLAG tag, thermostabilized apocytochrome b562 (bRIL), and rhodopsin N-tail (MNGTEGPNFYVPFSNATGVV) were introduced to enhance the surface expression42. The OR52E8 mutations were generated by site-directed mutagenesis with Q5 High-Fidelity DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs), using primer pairs described in Supplementary Table 3. GL4.29-luc2P-CRE-Hygro and pRL Renilla Luciferase vector were purchased (Promega). Receptor-transporting protein 1 short (RTP1s) was cloned into pcDNA 3.1 vector.

Chemical compounds

Carboxylic acid compounds, including 3-hydroxy hexanoic acid (SY Innovation), 3-methyl hexanoic acid (SY Innovation), 3-hydroxy-2-methyl hexanoic acid (SY Innovation), 3-hydroxy-3-methyl hexanoic acid (HMHA, SY Innovation), 3-hydroxy-4-methyl hexanoic acid (SY Innovation), 3-hydroxy-5-methyl hexanoic acid (SY Innovation), 3-hydroxy-3-methyl heptanoic acid (SY Innovation), 3-hydroxy octanoic acid (SY Innovation), and 3-hydroxy decanoic acid (SY Innovation), were purchased as racemic mixtures. (S)-HMHA and (R)-HMHA were also purchased (SY Innovation). Each chemical species was dissolved or diluted in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich) to final concentration of 200 mM before signaling assay.

3-hydroxy-3-methyl octanoic acid was synthesized in laboratory. A flame-dried round-bottom flask charged with zinc powder (1.6 equiv) was evacuated, backfilled with nitrogen three times, and connected to a nitrogen balloon (1.0 atm). Tetrahydrofuran (0.6 M), heptan-2-one (1.0 equiv), and benzyl bromoacetate (2.0 equiv) were added sequentially via syringe, and the reaction mixture was stirred at 40 °C for 12 h. The reaction was quenched with saturated aqueous NH4Cl solution, and the aqueous layer was extracted with ethyl acetate two times. The combined organic layers were dried over MgSO4, filtered, and concentrated in vacuo. The crude product was purified by flash column chromatography to obtain benzyl 3-hydroxy-3-methyloctanoate. A flame-dried round-bottom flask charged with Pd/C (10%, 50 mg/mmol) was evacuated, backfilled with nitrogen three times, and connected to a hydrogen balloon (1.0 atm). Ethyl acetate (0.2 M) and benzyl 3-hydroxy-3-methyloctanoate (1.0 equiv) were added via syringe, and the reaction mixture was hydrogenated at room temperature for 2 hours. The reaction mixture was filtered over a pad of Celite, and the filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure to give 3-hydroxy-3-methyl octanoic acid. The NMR spectra of synthesized compound were consistent with those of previous report, 1H NMR (500 MHz, CDCl3): δ 2.62–2.46 (m, 2H), 1.58–1.51 (m, 2H), 1.39–1.23 (m, 6H), 1.28 (s, 3H), 0.89 (t, J = 6.9 Hz, 3H)43. 3-hydroxy-3-methyl octanoic acid was diluted in DMSO to final concentration of 200 mM before signaling assay.

CRE luciferase assay

4 × 104 Hana3A cells were mixed with 0.002% poly-D-lysine (GibcoTM) and seeded on 96-well white microplate (SPL). 24 h later after seeding, plasmid DNA encoding OR, CRE-luciferase, Renilla luciferase, and RTP1s were mixed at a 2:5:1:2 ratio, and treated to cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) with serum free medium. 24 h later, protein expression was initiated by recovery with medium supplemented with 6.6% FBS for 24 h. After 24 h of recovery, cells were treated odorants and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. For detection, cells were washed with PBS and lysed using passive lysis buffer (Promega). Luminescence was measured using a Tristar 2 LB 942 multimode reader (Berthold). Substrates for each luciferase were D-luciferin (Nanolight) and coelenterazine h (Nanolight) for CRE and Renilla, respectively. The result was analyzed by the luminescence ratio of CRE over Renilla and analyzed with GraphPad Prism 10.

cAMP-Glo assay

4 × 104 Hana3A cells were mixed with 0.002% poly-D-lysine (GibcoTM) and seeded on 96-well white microplate (SPL). 24 h after seeding, plasmid DNA encoding pGloSensor-22F (Promega), RTP1s, and OR52cs or OR52csE8pocket were mixed at a 2:1:1 ratio, and treated to cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) with serum free medium. 24 h later, protein expression was initiated by recovery with medium supplemented with 6.6% FBS for 24 h. 2 h before luminescence detection, culture medium was replaced to 0.375 mg mL−1 Luciferin (Nanolight)-containing CO2-independent medium (Invitrogen) for equilibrium. Luminescence was detected twice, first for basal activity and then for odorant-induced activity. Odorants were diluted in CO2-independent medium and treated to cell with final DMSO concentration less than 0.8%. As neither OR52cs nor OR52csE8pocket showed significant basal activity, the initial read before odorant treatment was used for normalizing cell confluency or transfection condition. Responses were calculated as the luminescence ratio of odorant-induced to basal activity and analyzed with GraphPad Prism 10.

Confocal microscopy

6 × 105 Hana3A cells were mixed with 0.002% poly-D-lysine (GibcoTM) and seeded on confocal plate dish (SPL). 24 h after seeding, plasmid DNA encoding N-terminal FLAG-tagged OR (OR52cs, OR52csE8pocket, and OR52E8) was treated to cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) with serum free medium. 24 h later, protein expression was initiated by recovery with medium supplemented with 6.6% FBS for 24 h. Then, OR-expressing cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and blocked with 5% BSA. The N-terminal FLAG-tag of OR was captured by rabbit anti-FLAG 1st antibody, followed by wash and goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody conjugated with Alexa555 dye. The excess antibody was washed with 0.2% tween-20 and nucleus was stained with Hoechst. The confocal imaging was conducted with Confocal Laser Scanning microscope (LSM 700) at a magnification of 400x.

Surface ELISA

4 × 104 Hana3A cells were mixed with 0.002% poly-D-lysine (GibcoTM) and seeded on 96-well clear microplate (SPL). 24 h after seeding, 100 ng of empty plasmid or plasmid DNA encoding N-terminal FLAG-tagged OR was transfected to cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) with serum free medium. 24 h later, protein expression was initiated by recovery with medium supplemented with 6.6% FBS for 24 h. Then, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and blocked with PBS supplemented with 5% BSA for 2 h. The N-terminal FLAG-tag of OR was captured by rabbit anti-FLAG 1st antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, catalogue number 14793) at a 1:1000 dilution, followed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody (Enzo, catalogue number ADI-SAB-300) at a 1:1000 dilution. After 2 h of RT incubation, the TMB solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added. 20 min later, 1 M HCL was added for reaction quenching. For quantification of HRP reaction, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a FlexStation3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices). For normalization by relative cell counts per well, 0.2% (w/v) Janus green solution (TCL) was adopted, and the absorbance at 595 nm was measured for quantification. The surface expression level of the receptor was quantified by A450/A595.

Purification of Nb35

Nanobody-35 (Nb35) with a 6xHis-tag at its C-terminus was cloned into the pET-21d expression vector and expressed in Escherichia coli (E. coli) Rosetta (DE3) cells. The protein was purified by Ni-NTA affinity chromatography, followed by size exclusion chromatography (SEC) using a Superdex 200 10/300 column (Cytiva)44. The peak fractions were collected, concentrated, and stored at −80 °C until use.

Purification of human Gβ1γ2

Full-length wild-type human N-terminal 6x-His, human rhinovirus 3C site (HRV 3C)-fused Gβ1 and Gγ2 were cloned into the pFastBac Dual expression vector. Human Gβ1γ2 was overexpressed in Spodoptera frugiperda (Sf9, Expression Systems, 94-00lF) cells using the Bac-to-Bac baculovirus expression system (Invitrogen). Cells were harvested 55-60 hr after viral infection with centrifugation, and lysed with a buffer containing 20 mM Tris-Cl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 175 µg mL−1 benzamidine, and 10 µM leupeptin using a dounce homogenizer. After centrifugation, membrane fraction containing human Gβ1γ2 was solubilized with a buffer containing 20 mM Tris-Cl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM PMSF, 175 µg mL−1 benzamidine, and 10 µM leupeptin, 0.5% (w/v) lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG, Anatrace) and 0.05% (w/v) cholesterol hemisuccinate (CHS) in 4 °C for 2 h. The solubilized fraction was loaded onto Ni-NTA affinity column and gently washed with a buffer containing 20 mM Tris-Cl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole (pH 8.0), 0.05% (w/v) LMNG and 0.005% (w/v) for 10 column volume (CV). Human Gβ1γ2 was eluted with a buffer containing 20 mM Tris-Cl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 250 mM imidazole (pH 8.0), 0.05% LMNG (w/v) and 0.005% (w/v) CHS, followed by further purification using Hitrap Q anion-exchange column (Cytiva) with same buffer without imidazole. The peak fractions were concentrated and stored at -80°C before use.

Purification of human OR52csE8pocket-miniGαs

OR52csE8pocket was generated from pcDNA-OR52cs, which was used in our previous study23 by introducing five mutations via site-directed mutagenesis. Then, the miniGαs was fused at the C-terminus of OR52csE8pocket with 29-aa flexible linker (AS-GSGGS5-GS) and cloned into the pFastBac HT B expression vector. The FLAG tag was introduced at the N-terminus of for OR52csE8pocket-miniGαs purification. OR52csE8pocket-miniGαs was overexpressed in Spodoptera frugiperda (Sf9) cells using the Bac-to-Bac baculovirus expression system. Cells were harvested 60 hr after viral infection with centrifugation, and lysed with a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM PMSF, 175 µg mL−1 benzamidine, and 10 µM leupeptin using a dounce homogenizer. After centrifugation, membrane fraction containing human OR52csE8pocket-miniGαs was solubilized with a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM PMSF, 175 µg mL−1 benzamidine, and 10 µM leupeptin, 0.5% (w/v) LMNG and 0.05% (w/v) CHS in 4 °C for 2 h. The solubilized fraction was loaded onto anti-FLAG (M1) affinity column and gently washed with a buffer containing 20 mM HEEPS (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 4 mM CaCl2, 0.05% (w/v) LMNG and 0.005% (w/v) for 20 CV. OR52csE8pocket-miniGαs was eluted with a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 6 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), 0.1 mg ml-1 FLAG peptide, 0.05% (w/v) LMNG and 0.005% (w/v) CHS. The OR52csE8pocket-miniGαs was further purified with size exclusion chromatography (SEC) using a Superdex 200 10/300 column (Cytiva). The peak fractions were concentrated, quantified, and immediately used for complex formation with Gβ1γ2.

Complex formation of human OR52csE8pocket-miniGs–Nb35 complex

The purified OR52csE8pocket-miniGαs, Gβ1γ2, and Nb35 were mixed at a 1:1.5:2 ratio in the presence of 1 mM racemic HMHA and incubated in 4 °C, O/N. The reaction mixture was loaded into anti-FLAG (M1) affinity chromatography to remove excess Gβ1γ2 and Nb35, which was pre-equilibrated with 20 mM HEEPS (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 1 mM racemic HMHA, 0.025% (w/v) LMNG and 0.0025% (w/v). The resin was washed with same buffer for 20 CV, and eluted with a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), 0.1 mg ml−1 FLAG peptide, 1 mM racemic HMHA, 0.025% (w/v) LMNG and 0.0025% (w/v) CHS. The complex formation was confirmed by SDS-PAGE. The eluent was concentrated up to 6 mg ml-1 and used for cryo-EM grid preparation.

Grid preparation and cryo-EM data collection

4 µl of the purified OR52csE8pocket−miniGs –Nb35 complex was applied to the glow-discharged 300 mesh holey carbon grid (Quantifoil R1.2/1.3) (SPI) pre-coated. Each grid was blotted for 3 s with a blot force of 5 at 4–5 °C, 90–100% humidity, and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The grids were initially screened with FEI Glacios (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a Falcon 4 detector (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at the Center for Macromolecular and Cell Imaging at Seoul National University (SNU CMCI, Korea), which allowed us clear 2D classes. The large data collection was conducted using a Titan Krios G4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at the Institute of Membrane Proteins (IMP, Korea), equipped with a K3 BioQuantum at a magnification of 105,000× with a calibrated pixel size of 0.8248 Å. 18,533 movies were collected at a total dose of 60.03 e − A−2 and 50 frames per micrograph with a defocus ranging from −0.8 to −2.0 μm.

Cryo-EM data processing and 3D reconstruction

The overall workflow of cryo-EM data processing was performed with CryoSPARC v4.5.145. The movies were motioned-corrected and CTF estimated with patch motion correction and patch CTF estimation, respectively. The micrographs with bad CTF value or large contaminants were excluded with micrograph curation. Prior to data processing of full dataset, we randomly separated small subset of micrograph to make well-trained Topaz model46. Multiple rounds of Topaz training and extract resulted in particle sets with clear feature of GPCR-Gs complex in 2D classification. As the selected particles included heterogeneous or trash particles, such as those lacking visible TM regions within the micelle, multiple rounds of heterogeneous refinement were performed to eliminate them. The selected particles underwent local motion correction and global CTF refinement to more precisely correct for beam-induced motion, followed by a single round of heterogeneous refinement. To further enhance the density for OR52csE8pocket region, multiple rounds of local refinement and TMD-focused 3D classification were performed with or without G protein subtraction for each dataset. As a result, TMD-focused maps with local resolutions of ~3.29 Å were obtained. Subsequently, blob recovery and non-uniform (NU) refinement47 were performed to obtain a globally refined map. These maps were used for model building of the HMHA–OR52csE8pocket (TMD-focused map) and the Gs–Nb35 (NU-refined map), respectively. The detailed workflow of cryo-EM data processing is presented in Supplementary Fig. 3.

Model building and refinement

The initial model of OR52csE8pocket was obtained with AlphaFold2.ipynb32, and one of the reasonable model was selected for model building. Model building was conducted by iterative cycles of real-space refinement with PHENIX48 and manual building with Coot49. The geometry restraints of individual protein residues were evaluated and manually corrected based on validation results from MolProbity50. Restraint information for (R)-HMHA was generated using the eLBOW module in PHENIX51, based on the molecule’s SMILES string. Due to limited resolution that precluded full atomic modeling of (R)-HMHA, its fitting into the density was guided by minimizing steric clashes and optimizing interactions with nearby residues. In the final model of the our structure, N-terminal 11 residues (1-11), 6 ECL2 residues (191-196), 4 ICL3 residues (232-235), 5 ECL2 residues (267–271), and C-terminal 3 residues (311-313) of OR52csE8pocket were not modeled due to insufficient map quality. The detailed model information is presented in Table 1.

Receptor structure prediction

AlphaFold2 was used for the structure prediction of OR52E832. When using the original AlphaFold2 pipeline, the Cα RMSD of the output structure was 1.85 Å compared to the active OR52cs structure (PDB:8HTI)23. To more accurately model the active state structure, was modified to use the active state structure of OR52cs as the sole structural template, without supplying MSA as input. The AlphaFold2 structure was refined by Rosetta FastRelax33. In the case of OR52E8−miniGαs, AlphaFold-Multimer was adopted for complex structure prediction52. The resulting models were displayed with PyMol software.

Receptor-ligand complex structure prediction

To dock carboxylic acid ligands into the orthosteric pocket of OR52E8, GALigandDock34 was utilized, enabling structural flexibility for both the receptor and the ligand. The input ligand coordinates for docking were generated by aligning to the complex of OCA–OR52cs. The residue H18345×52 was protonated to facilitate a charged interaction between H18345×52 and the carboxylic group of the ligands. As a result, charged interactions involving H45×52, R6×59, and the carboxylic group of the ligand were obtained. For (S/R)-HMHA, docking poses were chosen based on their comparability with (R)-HMHA in the cryo-EM structure of OR52csE8pocket. For other carboxylic acids, docking pose selections were guided primarily by the presence of polar interactions between carboxyl group and H45×52/R6×59, and 3-hydroxyl branch and N5×42.

All atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

The systems for MD simulations were prepared by CHARMM-GUI Ligand Reader & Modeler and Membrane Builder53–55. Using the output model of docking simulations as an input, Ligand Reader generated a topology and parameter file for HMHA. Membrane Builder generated the system of HMHA–OR52csE8pocket embedded in a lipid bilayer. The position and orientation of OR52csE8pocket were determined using PPM 2.056. The lipid bilayer composed of 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) and 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (POPE) (1:1). For solvation, TIP3P water and 0.15 M KCl were used, which result in the rectangular system with a box size of 102 x 102 x 147 Å3 or 88 x 88 x 111 Å3 for with and without miniGαs system, respectively. The protonation states of the carboxyl group of HMHA and the side chain of His18345×52 residue were assigned to deprotonated (COO-) and doubly protonated state (HSP), respectively.

The simulation system was prepared for the OpenMM simulation package57. Prior to MD simulations, each system was energy-minimized for up to 5000 steps until the maximum force converged below 100 kJ·mol−1·nm−1. Initial velocities were generated according to a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution at 303.15 K. The system was equilibrated under the NVT ensemble using a Langevin thermostat (friction coefficient = 1 ps−1). During this equilibration stage, strong positional restraints were applied to the protein backbone (4000 kJ·mol-1·nm−2) and side chains (2000 kJ·mol−1·nm−2), while positional and dihedral restraints were applied to lipid molecules to stabilize the membrane environment. Equilibration was carried out as 6 consecutive runs of 125 ps each, with identical restraint settings applied throughout. Then, MD simulations were carried out under the NPT ensemble at 303.15 K and 1 bar for 1 µs with all restraints removed. Pressure was controlled using a Monte Carlo membrane barostat with semi-isotropic coupling, and long-range electrostatics were treated using the particle mesh Ewald method. All bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained, allowing a 2 fs integration time step. The detailed MD simulation parameters are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Multiple sequence alignment

The amino acid sequences of the human OR52 family members were obtained from the HORDE database and pseudogenes were manually excluded for sequence analysis58. The sequences of 26 OR52 family subtypes were aligned using MAFFT with L-INS-i strategy59. Simple phylogeny was used for phylogenetic tree generation using the neighbour-joining method60.

Natural variant database

The natural variant information of human OR52E8 was collected from the gnomAD v4.1.0 database website on the date of August 202461. The ensemble ID for human OR52E8 was ENST00000537935.2. The variant species and allele frequency information were summarized and visualized using the Snakeplotter and GraphPad Prism 10.

Data availability

The data supporting the findings of this study are available in the supporting information of this article. An uncropped gel image for Supplementary Fig. 4b is provided in the Supplementary Fig. 12, and source data for all signaling assays are available in the Supplementary Data. The coordinates of (R)-HMHA−OR52csE8pocket-miniGs−Nb35 were deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) under accession number 9WG4. The cryo-EM densities were deposited in the Electron Microscopy Data Bank (EMDB) under accession numbers of EMD-65947 (composite map), EMD-65945 (consensus map), and EMD-65946 (focused map). The engineered receptor OR52csE8pocket plasmid has been deposited in Addgene and is available under accession number 256623. All other data are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.
