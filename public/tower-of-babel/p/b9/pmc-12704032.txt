Microglia Display Altered Spatial Morphology and Proteome After Stroke
Authors: Brooke J Wanrooy, Jenny L Wilson, Althea R Suthya, Joshua H Bourne, Joel R Steele, Hossein Valipour Kahrood, Ralf B Schittenhelm, Giulia Ballerin, Cameron Skinner, Shu Wen Wen, Connie H Y Wong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMicroglia are abundantly distributed throughout the central nervous system (CNS) to play critical roles in neural development and homeostasis, and act as immune sentinels to constantly monitor their surrounding neural environment. Given their high reactivity to brain insults, we hypothesised that the cerebral microenvironment altered by ischaemic stroke would significantly impact microglial morphology and function in a spatially dependent manner. To investigate this, we examined regional gene expression changes associated with microglial activation and neuroinflammation, microglial morphology using 3D image reconstruction and unbiased proteomics at 24 h after transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (tMCAO). We found the microenvironment within the ischaemic infarct core has a distinct proinflammatory profile versus that of the sham‐operated controls. Moreover, stroke induces region‐specific changes to microglia morphology with those closer to the infarct displaying a more ameboid shape and less complex dendritic processes. Additionally, we identified 108 differentially expressed proteins in microglia that were isolated from the ipsilateral ischaemic hemisphere compared to those isolated from the contralateral hemisphere. These differentially expressed proteins are predicted to influence signalling pathways that mediate TNFα superfamily cytokine production, chemokine activities and leukocyte chemotaxis and migration. These findings support microglia as critical regulators of the inflammatory signalling after stroke.Keywords: microglia, morphology, neuroinflammation, proteome, stroke

Introduction

Microglia are tissue‐resident myeloid cells in the central nervous system (CNS) that account for ∼10%–12% of all brain cells in humans and mice [1]. Although their density varies across different brain regions, microglia are typically more abundant in grey matter compared to white matter [2]. Under homeostatic conditions, microglia act as CNS sentinels and perform wide‐ranging functions, including monitoring the health of neurons via synaptic pruning, synaptogenesis, and inducing cell death or survival [3]. Microglia also directly support neurons through the release of a suite of trophic factors that regulate brain homeostasis, including brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), insulin‐like growth factor (IGF1), prostaglandin E2 and interleukin‐10 (IL‐10) [4, 5, 6]. In addition, microglia comprise the dominant immune force in the brain that function similarly to peripheral macrophages and monocytes by clearing cellular debris through phagocytosis, and are an essential component to the brains first line of defence against infection, trauma and dysfunction [7]. In fact, microglia can produce, and respond to, a range of inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1β, IL‐6 and tumour necrosis factor‐α (TNFα) [8]. In the setting of stroke, microglial secretion of TNFα exacerbates the damage of endothelial cells in the salvageable brain regions surrounding the ischaemic infarct core [9, 10], leading to disruption of the blood–brain barrier and promoting neuroinflammatory injury. On the contrary, recent studies have suggested that microglia undergo morphological and phenotypical changes after stroke, following phagocytosis of brain‐infiltrating neutrophils to facilitate brain repair [11]. Evidently, the functional and morphological modifications of microglia following stroke are key determinants of brain recovery and repair.

SUMMARY

Microglia are highly reactive to their microenvironment; thus, we hypothesised ischaemic stroke would significantly impact microglial morphology and function in a spatially dependent manner. We observed region‐specific changes to microglial morphology and identified 108 differentially expressed proteins in microglia adjacent to the ischaemic infarct core compared to those located distally. These differentially expressed proteins are predicted to influence signalling pathways that mediate TNFα superfamily cytokine production, chemokine activities and leukocyte chemotaxis and migration, thus supporting microglia as critical regulators of the inflammatory signalling after stroke.

Microglia depletion has been evaluated in different neurodegenerative diseases as a strategy to reduce neuroinflammation and brain injury [12, 13, 14]. However, the shared properties of microglia and peripheral immune cell types such as monocytes and tissue‐resident macrophages mean that any depletion strategies would have the undesired effect of removing peripheral immune subsets, leading to inconclusive and controversial results [15, 16]. Microglia display a plethora of morphologies that have fascinated researchers for decades. Recent research has shown that the morphology and inflammatory functions of microglia are highly dynamic, varying with disease stage, context (such as the presence of pathogens or toxic protein debris) and anatomical location. Under homeostasis, microglia appear ramified with sensitive dendrite‐like processes that constantly extend and retract, which allow them to survey their environment. This form of ‘microglial process motility’ is crucial for the clearance of cellular debris, interaction with neurons and other glial components, and to remodel the extracellular matrix [17, 18]. Moreover, these processes abundantly express nano‐sized filopodia on the distal terminals [19], further enhancing the sensitivity to aberrant signals. In light of this, microglia are tasked to provide immunosurveillance through coordinated and timed motility patterns to sense pathogenic stimuli in the event of an insult or infection [20, 21, 22]. Indeed, altered microglial morphology has been described in clinical and preclinical brain samples of numerous neurological conditions, tempting many to equate morphological change to function. Previous studies that characterised microglial morphology as an inference of function rely mostly on very simple visualisation methods, often in 2D, and thus the overall phenotype is only partially characterised [23, 24]. This insufficiency is largely due to limitations in microscopic imaging technology, analysis software capability and cost. Moreover, although the development of high‐throughput omics technology has provided molecular insights into microglial transcriptome following stroke [25, 26], mRNA profiling alone arguably offers an incomplete picture, and examination of changes in proteins is essential to understand biological processes that are not transcriptionally regulated. In particular, the correlation between transcripts and proteins is moderate at best in mammals [27, 28]. To this end, the exact role of microglia in neuroinflammation, injury, and the subsequent recovery and repair of the brain after stroke is not yet fully understood.

We proposed that the characterisation of post‐stroke microglial proteome, coupled with improved imaging and visualisation techniques, will enable the identification of even subtle changes in microglia after stroke. In this study, we aimed to examine the region‐specific molecular, morphological and proteomic changes of microglia after stroke. Using the preclinical cerebral ischaemia‐reperfusion injury model, we showed differential microglial gene expression and cell morphology in the contralateral (CL), peri‐infarct and infarct areas of the brain after stroke. In addition, we assessed the proteome of microglia at different regions distal to the ischaemic infarct core to assess the impact of stroke on microglial functions.

Materials and Methods

Mice

Adult C57Bl/6J and Cx3cr1gfp/+ (on C57Bl/6J background) male and female mice were obtained from the Monash Animal Research Platform and were housed at Monash Translational Health Precinct Animal Facility (MHTP‐AF, Clayton, VIC, Australia) in specific pathogen‐free (SPF) conditions with access to food and water ad libitum. Mice were housed in temperature‐controlled rooms (22°C) under a standard 12 h light–dark cycle. Before the start of experiments, mice were acclimatised for a minimum of 7 days before use. All procedures were approved by the Monash Medical Centre Animal Ethics Committee (MMCB/2018/002 and MMCB/2022/22, Monash Medical Centre, Clayton, Victoria, Australia).

Middle Cerebral Artery Occlusion (MCAO) Surgery

The mouse model of ischaemic stroke was performed as previously described [29], whereby infarct is induced in the right cerebral hemisphere. Briefly, mice were anaesthetised by intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (150 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). Once anaesthetised, an incision at the top of the head was made to expose the skull, and a laser Doppler holder (PeriFlux System 5000, Stroke model kit 407, Perimed, Järfälla‐Stockholm, Sweden) was glued to the right side of the skull to monitor blood flow above the right hemisphere of the brain. The fur at the site of the neck incision was removed, and the area was sterilised with 80% ethanol. A 1–2‐cm incision was made along the midline of the throat to expose the trachea. The right common carotid artery, external carotid artery and internal carotid artery were dissected away from any connective tissue. A vessel clip was used on the right common carotid artery, and a slit was made in the external carotid artery. A silicone‐coated monofilament with a diameter of 0.21–0.23 mm (Doccol Corporation) was immediately inserted into the external carotid artery and advanced into the internal carotid artery to occlude blood flow into the MCA. A successful occlusion was determined by >70% reduction in Doppler reading. The external carotid artery was tied off, and the vessel clip was removed. In this study, we utilised the reperfused model termed transient MCAO or tMCAO, whereby the monofilament was retracted to allow for reperfusion of blood flow into the MCA following 1 h of occlusion. After successful surgery, the neck incision was sutured closed, the Doppler probe removed and the head incision closed. As a control for surgical stress, sham operations were performed, whereby similar surgical protocols were executed without the insertion of the monofilament. After surgery, all mice were allowed to recover overnight on a heat pad.

RNA Isolation and cDNA Synthesis for Fluidigm Biomark Analysis

At the experimental endpoint, the whole brain was collected from euthanised mice. To compare the gene expression of sham‐equivalent CL, CL, ipsilateral and infarct core brain regions after stroke, total RNA was first isolated. A 2–3 mm thick coronal brain slice was obtained from the middle of the brain and separated into the left and right hemispheres. The infarct core was visually dissected from the periinfarct region based on its anaemic/white area. Samples were pulse‐sonicated in 0.3 mL of TRIzol reagent (Sigma) at 30% amplitude for 10–20 s and topped up to 1 mL with TRIzol. Samples were then incubated for 5 min at room temperature (RT) to ensure complete tissue lysis. The lysate was then incubated with 0.1 mL of 1‐bromo‐3‐chloropropane and shaken vigorously for 15 s and left at RT for 15 min. Samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 28°C for 15 min. The aqueous phase was removed and transferred to a fresh tube. To precipitate RNA, 0.5 mL of 2‐propanol was added and left at RT for 15 min. Samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 28°C for 15 min. The supernatant was removed, and 1 mL of 75% ethanol was added to purify the RNA pellet. Samples were vortexed briefly and centrifuged at 7500 × g at 28°C for 5 min. Ethanol was removed, and the RNA pellet was left to dry briefly. To dissolve RNA, 30 µL of RNase‐free water was added to each sample, and RNA purity and concentration were confirmed using a Nanodrop Spectrophotometer. The purified RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the Superscript III First‐Strand cDNA Synthesis kit (Cat: 18080051, Invitrogen) per the manufacturer's protocol.

To perform high‐throughput quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) transcript analysis of regional post‐stroke brain regions, we utilised the Fluidigm Biomark system through the Monash Health Translation Precinct Medical Genomics Facility. Preamplification was used to increase the number of copies of each gene to detectable levels as detailed in Gene Expression Preamp with Fluidigm Preamp MasterMix and Taqman Assays Quick Reference PN 100‐5876 B1. Taqman assays were firstly pooled by combining 1 µL of each of the 45 20X Taqman assays and 55 µL TE buffer for a final volume of 100 µL. The final concentration of each assay was 0.2X (180 nM). Preamplification allows multiplex amplification – 3.75 µL of Sample Pre‐Mix (Life Technologies TaqMan PreAmp Master Mix and Pooled Taqman assays) was combined with 1.25 µL of each of the 42 samples (including RT controls) for a final reaction volume of 5 µL per sample; all samples were pre‐amplified for 14 cycles. After cycling, reaction products were diluted 1:5 by adding 20 µL TE buffer to the final 5 µL reaction volume for a total volume of 25 µL. Assays and samples were loaded onto the 48.48 Gene expression IFC according to Fluidigm 48.48 IFC Standard Taqman Gene expression workflow Quick Reference PN 68000089 H1. Five microlitres of each assay at a final concentration of 10× was added to each assay inlet port and 5 µL of diluted sample to each sample inlet port according to the Chip Pipetting Map. The data was analysed by comparing the gene expression cycle threshold relative to the housekeeping gene 18s. Data were analysed using GraphPad (Prism), and a colourimetric heatmap was generated using Excel (Microsoft). Specific Taqman Assays used are presented in Table S1.

Protein Isolation for Assessment of Brain Inflammatory Mediators

At the experimental endpoint, the whole brain was collected from euthanised mice, and the cerebellum was removed. The cerebrum was separated into anatomical left (CL) and right (ipsilateral) hemispheres, weighed and snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen. Frozen brain hemispheres were homogenised in M tubes (cat: #130‐093‐236, Miltenyi Biotech) containing 800 µL of RIPA lysis buffer (cat: #89900, ThermoFisher) and cOmplete, EDTA‐free protease inhibitor (cat: #04693132001, Roche) using a GentleMACS Octo Disassociator (Miltenyi Biotech, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). The sample was then centrifuged briefly for 30 s at 300 × g. The liquid lysate was collected and transferred to an Eppendorf. Samples were then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min. The supernatant was transferred to a fresh Eppendorf and frozen at −80°C until use.

To assess the impact of stroke on hemispheric inflammatory protein changes, the amount of TNFα, interleukin (IL)‐1α, IL‐1β, IL‐12p70, IL‐10, IL‐6, IL‐17α, interferon (IFN)‐β, IFN‐γ, IL‐23, IL‐27, monocyte chemoattractant protein‐1 (MCP‐1) and granulocyte monocyte colony stimulating factor (GM‐CSF) were quantified using the LegendPlex Mouse Inflammation Panel (cat: 740446, BioLegend) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Populations of A and B analyte beads were acquired (8000 per A and B population per sample) using an LSR X Fortessa (BD Biosciences). Samples were analysed using the LEGENDplex Data Analysis Software. The amount of expressed analyte was normalised to the cerebral hemispheric weight and lysis volume of each sample and represented as pg/g of tissue.

Three‐Dimensional Morphological Analysis of Microglia

At the experimental endpoints, Cx3cr1gfp/+ mice were deeply anaesthetised with ketamine (150 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) before cardiac perfusion. Following the opening of the thoracic cavity, the right atrium was cut, and an 18G needle was inserted into the left ventricle to perfuse 30 mL of ice‐cold PBS and subsequently 30 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; in PBS, pH 7.4). After perfusion, the brain was left to fix in 4% PFA for 1 h and cryoprotected in 20% sucrose overnight. Brains were frozen in isopentane at −80°C and stored at −80°C before use. Using a cryostat [object temperature, −10 to −20°C; cryostat temperature, −20°C (Leica 3050 and 3050S)], 30 or 60 µm coronal brain sections were collected onto SuperFrost+ slides and briefly dried at RT and stored at −80°C. For immunostaining, brain sections were acclimatised at RT for 15 min, rehydrated thrice with TBST (0.1% Tween‐20) at RT for 5 min, then cover‐slipped using DAKO mounting media and left to dry for 1 h at RT before imaging.

Confocal 3D images of CL, peri‐infarct and core regions of the brain were acquired using an Olympus FVMPE‐RS Multiphoton microscope (Olympus, Shinjuku, Japan). Images were obtained via Galvano one‐way scanning of 60 µm thick coronal sections at 0.6 µm Z intervals for maximum depth using x20 water‐immersion objective (NA: 1.05) at a resolution of 512 × 512 pixels with 2‐times frame averaging. Fluorescence was obtained via scanning at 910 nm. One to two fields of view (FOV) were obtained from each region. Each region was determined using the fluorescence‐enabled eyepiece, where an absence of GFP intensity was defined as the infarct core. Tissue sections of the infarct were obtained within 2.5 mm posterior to the beginning of the olfactory bulb to ensure consistency in the regions analysed. Morphological analysis was performed on these images using Imaris 7.6.2 software (Bitplane, Zurich, Switzerland). Reconstruction of microglial dendrites was performed using the Filament Tracer tool, while reconstruction of microglial soma was performed using the Surfaces tool. Batch settings for each of the three regions were determined and used automatically by the software to avoid user bias. Cells were manually removed if they extended significantly beyond the XY plane or were incorrectly rendered. Morphometric cell body parameters analysed included: sphericity, volume and mean fluorescence intensity (MFI). Dendrite or filament parameters analysed included: filament dendrite length (sum), filament dendrite volume (sum), filament full branch depth, filament full branch level and filament no. terminal dendrite points. Microglial cell counts were determined from those determined using the surfaces tool output, while morphological measures were determined from the filament tracer tool output.

Microglia Enrichment and Preparation for Proteomics

At the experimental endpoints, C57BL/6J mice were deeply anaesthetised with ketamine (150 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) before cardiac perfusion. Following the opening of the thoracic cavity, the right atrium was cut, and an 18G needle was inserted into the left ventricle to perfuse 30 mL of ice‐cold PBS. After perfusion, the brain was dissected with the meninges and cerebellum removed. The brain was weighed, separated into contralateral and ipsilateral hemispheres, and placed into ice‐cold DPBS before digestion. To isolate microglia from post‐stroke brain, the ‘isolation and cultivation of microglia from adult mouse or rat brain’ protocol by Miltenyi Biotec was performed in conjunction with the Adult Brain Disassociation Kit (#130‐107‐677). Here, each brain hemisphere was digested in a C tube containing 1950 µL of enzyme mix one and 30 µL enzyme mix 2 using program 37C_ABDK_01 of a gentleMACS Octo Dissociator with Heaters. Each sample was centrifuged briefly to collect the sample and resuspended in ice‐cold DPBS (calcium, magnesium, glucose and pyruvate supplemented). The sample was applied through a premoistened 70 µM MACS SmartStrainer and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was aspirated completely, and the pellet was resuspended in 3100 µL of ice‐cold DPBS and 900 µL of Debris Removal Solution. Each sample was overlayed dropwise with 4 mL of ice‐cold DPBS. Samples were centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4°C with full acceleration and brake. The top two phases were aspirated and discarded. Samples were resuspended with 15 mL of ice‐cold DPBS and gently inverted thrice. Samples were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min at 4°C with supernatant aspirated. Samples were resuspended in 1 mL of ice‐cold 1× Red Blood Cell Removal Solution (1:10 ddH2O) and left to incubate for 10 min on ice. Samples were diluted with 10 mL of fresh‐prepared ice‐cold PB buffer (0.5% FCS in 1× PBS) and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was aspirated, and samples were carefully resuspended in 90 µL of ice‐cold PB buffer and 10 µL of CD11b (Microglia) human and mouse MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec) for 15 min in the dark on ice. Samples were washed with 1 mL of ice‐cold PB buffer and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at 4°C. Supernatant was removed, and cells were resuspended in 500 µL of PB buffer. MS columns suspended within a magnetic MACS separator were precalibrated with 500 µL of PB buffer before samples were then immediately added to the column. Unlabelled cells were allowed to pass through the column, while the column was washed three times with 500 µL of PB buffer. As the column reservoir neared empty, 1 mL of PB buffer was added, the MS columns were placed onto 15 mL Falcons and labelled cells were plunged into the collection tube. Each sample underwent another round of enrichment over a new MS column per above to maximise efficiency. Samples were then centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at 4°C. Supernatant was removed, and each sample was gently resuspended in 200 µL of ice‐cold nonsupplemented DPBS.

For cells undergoing proteomic analysis, 160 µL of the total cell fraction for each sample was transferred to a sterile Eppendorf. To maximise removal of contaminating FCS proteins, samples were centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant was aspirated, cells resuspended in 1 mL DPBS, centrifuged, supernatant aspirated and frozen at −80°C. To confirm isolation of microglia, 20 µL of the original cell fractions were plated into a U‐bottom plate and topped up to 200 µL with PB buffer. Cells were pelleted at 300 × g for 5 min at 4°C and stained with 100 µL of Rat anti‐mouse CD16/CD32 Fc receptor block (1:1000 in PBS buffer, #553142, Becton Dickinson) for 15 min on ice. Cells were pelleted, supernatant removed and resuspended in 100 µL of APC‐Cy7‐conjugated fixable viability dye (1:1000 in PBS, #565388, Becton Dickinson) for 20 min on ice in the dark. Samples were washed with 100 µL of PBS and pelleted, supernatant removed and incubated with 50 µL of Ab solution containing PE‐conjugated anti‐mouse CD45 (1:200, 30‐F11, #103109, Biolegend) and FITC‐conjugated anti‐mouse CD11b (1:200, M1/70, #553310, BD Pharmingen) for 20 min on ice, in the dark. Samples were washed, pelleted and fixed in 100 µL of Fix/Perm (#554714, BD Cytofix/Cytoperm Fixation/Permeabilization Kit) solution for 20 min on ice in the dark. Samples were washed with 200 µL of Fix/Perm wash solution (1:10 in dH2O), centrifuged, pelleted and resuspended in 200 µL of Fix/Perm wash solution. Microglia were confirmed via flow cytometry using an LSR X Fortessa as CD45midCD11b+ (Figure S2). Of all samples submitted for proteomic analysis, the average proportion of live microglia was confirmed as 84.62% ± 1.47%.

Proteomics Sample Preparation

Eluted cells were solubilised in 5% SDS 100 mM Tris‐HCL with heating at 95°C for 10 min to denature enzymes. Sample DNA was sheared using ultra‐probe sonication with three rounds of 30‐s intervals with resting on wet ice (Qsonica, Q125). Insoluble debris was precipitated, and the supernatant was taken for processing using the S‐trap protocol as per the manufacturer's instructions [30] with protein level normalisation. Eluted peptides were acidified to 1% trifluoroacetic acid and purified using Stage‐tips packed with SDB‐RPS (Empore) [31], iRT peptides (Biognosys) were spiked into all samples before analysis. All samples were nano‐dropped (A205 Scope, NanoDrop One, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to adjust injection volumes for a 250 ng peptide mass on column.

Liquid Chromatography Mass Spectrometry and Data Analysis

Peptides were separated utilising reverse phase chromatography on a Vanquish Neo (Thermo Scientific) with direct injection and pressure loading onto a 25 cm C18 integrated emitter at 50°C (IonOpticks, Aurora, TS 25 cm) with increasing concentrations of Buffer B (80% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid) with 53 min of separation. Mass spectrometric analysis was performed using an Orbitrap Astral (Thermo Scientific, Breman), the instrument operated in a data‐independent manner with master scans of 240,000 resolution from 350 to 1000 m/z using a 500% Ion current target, then narrow window isolation for fragmentation across the same mass range using 2 Da windows and a 1 Da overlap. The raw data files were analysed with Spectronaut (V18.6.231227.55695, Sagan) using Direct DIA+ and the MaxLFQ method of quantification [32] and no precursor imputation. The analysis utilised the Uniprot SwissProt database for Mouse (Accessed June 2023, 17,150 entries) and the Universal contaminants database (Accessed June 2022, 381 entries) with tryptic digestion settings and standard processing settings.

Flow Cytometry

At the experimental endpoint, C57BL/6J mice were deeply anaesthetised for transcardiac perfusion with ice‐cold PBS. After perfusion, the brain was dissected with the meninges and cerebellum removed. The brain was weighed, separated into contralateral and ipsilateral hemispheres, and placed into ice‐cold DPBS before digestion. Brains were processed using an Adult Brain Dissociation Kit (#130‐107‐677, Miltenyi Biotech), modified from manufacturers instruction, using a ‘C tube’ (#130‐093‐237, Miltenyi Biotech) with the 37C_ABDK_01 protocol of a GentleMACS Octo Disassociator with heaters. Upon dissociation, cells were isolated by centrifugation using a 30%/80% Percoll gradient (Bio‐Strategy) at 1850 × g for 20 min without brake at RT. Cells were collected from Percoll and washed twice with ice‐cold PBS‐EDTA (2 mM), before TNFα production was assessed. Isolated cells were cultured for 3 h in 2 mL complete Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (10% FCS, penicillin–streptomycin 100 U/mL and l‐glutamine 0.5 mg/mL, ThermoFisher) at 37°C and 5% CO2 in a sterile, humidified atmosphere. Culture was performed in the presence of GolgiPlug (containing Brefeldin A, #555029 BD Biosciences) to retain cytokines in cells, and plates were agitated every 30 min to reduce adhesion. Cells were washed, blocked and stained with antibodies as described above or in [33]. Cell acquisition was performed using an Aurora Spectral Flow Cytometer (Cytek Biosciences), and the entire sample was acquired. Flow cytometry panel design was assisted by the Cytek Cloud software, and unmixed samples were processed using FlowJo v10.1.

Data Handling and Statistical Analysis

Littermates from the same cage were randomly assigned to different experimental groups. Experimenters were not blind to group or outcome assignment. Sample sizes were determined from our previous and extensive experience of both stroke models. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism Software (La Jolla, CA, USA). The normality of some data was first assessed using a Shapiro–Wilk normality test and analysed using either an unpaired Student's t test, Mann–Whitney U test, one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn's multiple comparisons test as required. Outliers were excluded using Grubb's test α ≤ 0.05 (noniterative). For gene and proteomics analysis, where outliers were removed for a single hemisphere, the corresponding value for the other hemisphere was also removed. All values are presented as mean ± SEM. A value of p ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For proteomics data, statistical analysis was performed using the Bioconductor package DEP in R [34]. The MS2 data underwent processing and filtration to remove contaminants and retain high‐confidence proteins. Additionally, proteins with a significant proportion of missing values were excluded, refining the dataset from an initial set of 10,787 proteins to a final list of 9785 proteins for subsequent analysis. Outliers were identified and excluded, reducing the total number of biological replicates from eight to six. Protein intensity data were log2‐transformed, and samples were grouped according to their respective experimental conditions. Missing values were imputed using the ‘Missing Not At Random’ (MNAR) method, which involves generating random draws from a left‐shifted Gaussian distribution with parameters set at 1.8 standard deviations apart and a width of 0.3. Data normalisation was carried out using the Variance Stabilising Normalisation (VSN) method. Differential protein expression analysis was conducted with the batch included in the design formula using the limma package [35]. Significantly regulated proteins were identified using an adjusted p value threshold of 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg correction) and a log fold change cutoff of 1.

Results

Ischaemic Core Upregulates Genes Associated With Microglial Activation and Inflammatory Proteins

Microglia are abundantly distributed throughout the CNS, playing a critical role as immune sentinels and constantly monitoring their surrounding neural environment. Given their high reactivity to brain insults, we hypothesised that the cerebral microenvironment altered by ischaemic stroke would significantly impact microglial function in a spatially dependent manner. To investigate this, we assessed the effect of stroke‐induced ischaemia on neuroinflammation, using it as a surrogate marker for microglial activation and function. We conducted our analysis 24 h after transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (tMCAO) by examining regional changes in the expression of genes associated with microglial activation and neuroinflammation. Specifically, we compared gene expressions in the contralateral hemisphere, peri‐infarct area and core regions of the post‐stroke ipsilateral brain to those in the non‐injured hemisphere of sham‐operated control mice, providing a detailed, region‐specific insight into the impact of ischaemic stroke on the cerebral microenvironment (Figure 1a). Here, we uncovered a plethora of both classical and non‐classical genes common to microglial activation, the majority of which were upregulated in the core of ischaemic infarct, as shown in the heatmap displayed in Figure 1b. Indeed, there was significant upregulation of genes in the ischaemic infarct core associated with myeloid recruitment (Ccl2 and Ccl5; Figure 1c), inflammation (Tnfα and Il‐1β; Figure 1d), microglial activation (Cd11b, Cd16, Cd64, Cd68, MhcII and Tlr4; Figure 1e) and the resolution of inflammation (Arg1, Ym1, Cd36, Cd163, Tgf‐β and Fgf2; Figure 1f). Collectively, these data indicate that at 24 h following stroke, the microenvironment within the ischaemic infarct core differs significantly from that of the sham‐operated controls, and more importantly, this region of the brain displays dynamic and diverse inflammatory gene changes.

To elucidate the processes associated with these gene changes, we sought to examine the hemispheric protein expression of a range of these inflammatory mediators and cytokines in the brain after stroke. Of the investigated cytokines, we found MCP‐1 (Figure 1g), IL‐1α (Figure 1h) and IL‐6 (Figure 1i) were significantly increased in the ipsilateral brain hemisphere compared to the non‐infarcted contralateral hemisphere of post‐stroke mice. On the contrary, the protein levels of IL‐10 was significantly reduced in the ipsilateral hemisphere compared to contralateral at 24 h after tMCAO (Figure 1j). Intriguingly, there were comparable levels of TNFα, IL‐1β, IFNβ, IFNγ, IL‐12, IL‐17A and GM‐CSF between the brain hemispheres after stroke (Figure S1). Notably, the correlation between transcript and protein levels is generally modest in mammalian cells [27, 36, 37]. Protein abundance is controlled at the transcriptional, translational and post‐translational levels, influencing protein synthesis rates, half‐life and transport, thus transcript levels by themselves are not sufficient to predict protein levels [38]. In our study, we found increased MCP‐1, IL‐1α and IL‐6 protein levels, along with decreased IL‐10 in the ischaemic hemisphere, create an inflammatory microenvironment that likely drives significant changes in microglia.

Microglial Cell Bodies Are Enlarged and Their Processes Are Shorter Closer to Stroke Infarct Core

We next investigated whether the post‐stroke microenvironment in the ischaemic infarct core affects microglial morphology compared to regions adjacent to (peri‐infarct) and distant from (CL) the injury. Hyperactivated microglia often show an enlarged and less spherical or elongated cell body in response to inflammatory stimuli [6]. Using immunofluorescence of Cx3cr1gfp+ reporter mice, where microglia express green fluorescent protein, we assessed microglial morphology in the CL, peri‐infarct and core regions of the brain 24 h after tMCAO (Figure 2a, b). We observed no significant changes in the number of microglia (CX3CR1+ cells) in the peri‐infarct or core regions of the brain compared to the contralateral side of the brain at 24 h after stroke (Figure 2c), suggesting that our model of cerebral ischaemia‐reperfusion injury does not induce significant microglial cell death or proliferation at 24 h. However, as microglial cell bodies are reported to undergo significant swelling and elongation before cell death, we also examined their soma volume and sphericity. Indeed, we observed that the soma volume of microglia significantly increased in the core when compared to the contralateral region in post‐stroke mice (Figure 2d). Similarly, microglia were more elongated in the peri‐infarct and core regions compared to the contralateral region after tMCAO, thus further demonstrating the region‐specific changes in microglial hyperactivation following ischaemic injury (Figure 2e).

As microglia survey the brain microenvironment by forming various somatic contacts with neurons and other glia via their dendritic processes [39], we next examined the effect of stroke on microglial dendrite length, volume and number of terminal contact points. Three‐dimensional reconstructions of a representative microglia from these regions are shown in Figure 2f and Movies S1–S3. Similar to the changes in microglial cell bodies between regions, microglial dendrites also show region‐specific modifications. In the contralateral non‐injured hemisphere, microglia had a wide range of dendritic lengths, but these lengths were significantly shorter as microglia were closer to the infarct core (Figure 2g). This region‐dependent shortening was also accompanied by a decrease in dendritic volume (Figure 2h). To assess microglial process complexity, we examined dendritic levels, depth and branch points. We found that dendrite level (Figure 2i) and depth (Figure 2j) were significantly reduced in the peri‐infarct and core regions compared to the contralateral region at 24 h after tMCAO. Additionally, as a measure of direct somatic communication, the number of microglial dendrite points also decreased significantly in these regions (Figure 2k). Overall, our findings show that stroke leads to significant changes in microglial morphology, with cell bodies becoming enlarged, less spherical and having reduced dendritic processes near the ischaemic infarct core.

Discussion

It has been well documented that stroke elicits microglia activation, and this can be morphologically characterised by the physical swelling and elongation of their cell body (soma) and also thinning and loss of dendrite branches [41]. This hyperactivation of microglia in the brain is associated with their impairment to efficiently phagocytose necrotic neurons and other debris, thus preventing the resolution of neuroinflammation [42]. However, the underlying mechanism behind this impairment is unclear. Some evidence suggests microglia activate and polarise towards certain phenotypes in the forms of the classical (M1) pathway and the alternate activation (M2) pathway [43]. However, the polarisation of microglial function is not fully understood, and controversy regarding the simple relegation of microglial phenotype to M1 and M2 is increasing in the literature [44]. At present, it is now widely accepted that microglial morphology and function exist across a spectrum rather than being simply polarised between two terminal states. Thus, we hypothesised that microglial morphology, function and signalling pathways likely follow a continuum based on their distance from the activating stimulus. To this end, we investigated how localised brain injury from ischaemic stroke affects microglial morphology and function depending on their distance from the injury. In our model of preclinical ischaemic stroke, we found that microglia numbers within the proximity to the ischaemic infarct core are similar to that of brain regions distal to the injury. Using 3D imaging to examine in‐depth cellular morphology and proteomics to assess differential microglial protein expression, we uncovered that microglia within the infarct display significantly larger, elongated cell bodies with truncated dendritic processes, and exhibit elevated expression of inflammatory proteins that belong to the TNFα signalling and leukocyte chemotaxis pathways.

Although microglia have specialised functions on a molecular level, much of their function is influenced by physical morphology. Indeed, depending on their activation state and location in the brain, microglia can present with distinct morphology [45]. In the current literature, microglia are suggested to be surveillant or inactivated if they are ramified with small cell bodies and branching processes. When stimulated with an inflammatory response, however microglia become less ramified, transition into an amoeboid in shape, and lose their dendritic branches [45, 46]. However, this traditional understanding of microglia morphology is highly restricted by dichotomic categorisation. Our findings demonstrate that microglia can display a range of morphological phenotypes. Restricted to examining three regions of the brain at various distances from the ischaemic infarct core, we showed that microglia within the infarct displayed enlarged and stretched nucleation, and this was accompanied by significantly shortened dendritic processes. These morphological changes are consistent with previously reported studies. Intriguingly, we uncovered that microglia within brain regions adjacent to the infarct core, which we denoted as the peri‐infarct that would generally be considered healthy, also exhibited dramatically different morphology compared to the contralateral hemisphere. These findings suggest that microglial morphology is not limited to ‘activated’ versus ‘inactivated’, and that in a complex milieu such as the post‐stroke injured brain, physical properties of the microglia will modify depending on their dynamic microenvironment. Perhaps assessing microglial interactions with other cell types, such as neurons, astrocytes and brain‐infiltrating leukocytes, may provide new understandings into how their cell morphologies are adapted throughout the different stages of ischaemic injury development in space and time. Evidently, solely assessing microglial morphology to deduce their activation or function is inadequate, and it needs to be coupled with other molecular insights.

We performed transcript analyses of the post‐stroke brain to reveal a highly inflammatory microenvironment within the ischaemic core. Despite the high number of significantly upregulated inflammatory genes, our targeted protein quantification only validated the elevated expression of MCP‐1 (i.e., Ccl2 gene). Although not examined at the transcript level in this study, the stroke‐induced IL‐1α production is likely a host response to tMCAO that preceded Il‐1β gene upregulation. This would be consistent with a previous report that demonstrated IL‐1α expression was upregulated, predominantly in microglia localised to focal neuronal and vascular injury, before IL‐1β following acute stroke [47]. To gain an in‐depth comprehension of how the cerebral milieu induced by ischaemic stroke influences microglial function, we performed proteomics on microglia isolated from the ischaemic hemisphere and compared it to the contralateral hemisphere after stroke. The unbiased approach of proteomics offers several advantages over transcriptomics, including the ability to study kinase activities and post‐translational modifications, which significantly influence protein function and cannot be appreciated by transcriptomic analysis [48]. In an effort to obtain the highest quality of samples for proteomics analysis, we optimised the cell isolation method without the need to culture to minimise protein contamination. Notably, compared to microglia obtained directly ex vivo, in vitro culture of microglia before proteomic analysis induced rapid protein mass increases along with elevation in glycolysis and disease‐associated signatures, and reduction of homeostatic markers (bioRxiv; https://doi.org/10.1101/2022.07.07.498804v2). Evidently, comparison of results from different proteomics analyses or datasets will need to consider how the cells were obtained.

To our knowledge, the post‐stroke microglial proteome in a regional manner has never been previously elucidated. The majority of microglia proteomic analysis has focused on protein changes induced by LPS, ageing or mouse models of Alzheimer's disease (AD, APPNL‐F and 5xFAD) [49, 50]. In our study, we identified 108 proteins differentially expressed by microglia in the ischaemic hemisphere compared to the contralateral brain hemisphere at 24 h after tMCAO. These differentially expressed proteins are predicted to influence signalling pathways that mediate TNFα superfamily cytokine production, chemokine activities and leukocyte migration and chemotaxis. Remarkably, microglia in the ischaemic hemisphere express a set of proteins enriched in a number of key inflammatory responses, indicating microglia are key producers and responders of numerous inflammatory mediators and that they actively participate in the neuroinflammatory process after stroke. Indeed, our proteome data suggest stroke‐induced microglial changes at 24 h do not correspond to observed morphological changes, but rather the majority of the altered proteins found in microglia in the ischaemic/ipsilateral hemisphere belong to immune/inflammatory pathways. We speculate that the stroke‐induced proteins that promote morphological changes in microglia are expressed before the 24 h timepoint that we performed proteomics. This notion is supported in our recent publication, which reported post‐stroke microglia morphology changes occur as early as 3 h following disease onset [33]. Although the study generated microglial proteomic data on each hemisphere of the brain following cerebral ischaemia‐reperfusion injury, a major limitation is the lack of temporal profiling, which would reveal dynamic microglial protein expression across various stages of disease onset.

Microglia are specialised cells that play critical roles in CNS development, homeostasis and immune defence. The findings of this study support the notion that microglia morphology and functions are heavily influenced by their microenvironment. In a distance‐to‐injury dependent manner, microglial cell bodies enlarge and their processes retract following cerebral ischaemia‐reperfusion injury. Our findings demonstrate that microglia are key producers for numerous proteins that mediate various inflammatory signalling pathways, exemplifying the importance of tightly regulating microglial responses after stroke to minimise inflammatory damage to the brain. Dysregulated microglia are often observed in other neurodegenerative diseases, such as AD, thus understanding how microglia become dysregulated and how to prevent microglia from becoming chronically inflamed is of great importance for novel therapeutic development.
