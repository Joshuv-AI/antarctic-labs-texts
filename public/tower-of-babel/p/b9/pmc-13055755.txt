Management of Liver Cirrhosis in Percutaneous and Surgical Cardiac Interventions
Authors: Cristiano Spadaccio, Antonio Nenna, Krishna Mehta, Angelo Pisani, Asishana Osho, Fernando Juarez‐Casso, Domenico Alvaro, Imran Arif, Antonio Panza, Russell S. Vester, Louis B. Louis, Thoralf M. Sundt, Juan Crestanello, David Rose
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The systemic impairment that accompanies liver cirrhosis is known to carry a significant burden on death and postoperative complications in cardiovascular interventions. This review analyzes the impact of liver cirrhosis on short‐ and long‐term outcomes after cardiac surgery and percutaneous procedures, providing an evidence‐based guidance in the decision‐making process and perioperative management of these patients. Several parameters should be considered by the heart teams when assessing eligibility, indications for intervention, and perioperative management of these patients. Life expectancy, long‐term procedural outcomes, Child–Pugh or the Model for End‐Stage Liver Disease score, presence of gastroesophageal varices and hepatic venous pressure gradient were extrapolated as the main determinants in decision making in patients with cirrhosis. Assessment and treatment of gastroesophageal varices is mandatory. Hepatic venous pressure gradient values >20 mm Hg preclude patients from a surgical approach. Considering the poor prognosis of advanced liver cirrhosis, futile treatments should be avoided. A multidimensional heart team approach involving surgical, cardiologic, anesthesiologic, hepatological, and medical considerations is recommended.

Liver cirrhosis (LC) is the eighth most common cause of death in the United States and ranks 13th for death worldwide. 1 In developed countries, the main causes of LC are alcoholic liver disease and hepatitis C. Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, as a result of the Westernized lifestyle and diet, is also on the global increase. 1

LC is frequently encountered in the routine clinical practice of both cardiology and cardiac surgery units. The spectrum of disease includes different degrees of metabolic and hemodynamic disturbances and alterations of the coagulation cascade. The systemic impairment that accompanies LC is known to carry a significant burden on death and postoperative complications in cardiovascular interventions. 2 , 3 , 4 Currently, there are no societal guidelines or univocal codified approaches to help guide the management of these complex patients when undergoing cardiac surgery or percutaneous cardiovascular procedures.

We therefore reviewed the impact of cirrhosis in cardiac surgery and cardiovascular interventions following a systematic literature search (Supplemental Methods and Figure S1). Data from the literature have been analyzed to extrapolate evidence‐based practical recommendations for these patients’ management. Discussion on patients with end‐stage liver disease requiring transplant or on management of liver‐transplanted patients undergoing cardiac intervention will be the subject of a subsequent work.

Liver Cirrhosis

Scoring Systems and Risk Stratification

The most common risk assessment tools used to assess the severity of LC are the Model for End‐Stage Liver Disease (MELD) and the Child–Pugh (CP) classification. 1 The classic MELD score includes total serum bilirubin, creatinine, and international normalized ratio (INR), while the CP classification includes the presence of ascites, bilirubin, encephalopathy, albumin level, and prothrombin time or INR. Studies comparing the MELD and CP scoring systems have shown variable results in their accuracy in predicting overall death. Peng et al concluded that the CP score has a higher sensitivity but lower specificity than the MELD score in predicting death. 2 CP requires subjective assessment of ascites and encephalopathy, while MELD is considered a more objective score to risk stratify patients in the hepatology literature. MELD has been shown to be more accurate in some studies 5 , 6 ; however, in clinical practice, the CP score is more widely adopted and easier to implement than the calculations involved in the MELD scoring system. 6

In patients undergoing cardiovascular interventions, liver dysfunction has been shown to be an independent predictor of the long‐term mortality rate. 7 A modified version of the MELD score was developed for use in patients with elevated INR due to anticoagulation, which is commonly encountered in patients undergoing cardiovascular procedures. 7 , 8 This modified score has also been found to be related to outcomes in patients undergoing cardiac interventions 9 but is not widely adopted in clinical practice.

The prognostic accuracy of both the CP and MELD scores has been investigated in several observational studies and a recent meta‐analysis systematically assessed their performance in different conditions and time end points. Although the predictive power of both the scores was found to be overlapping at 3, 6, and 12 months, with a sensitivity of 75% and specificity ranging between 75% and 59% at short and long term, respectively, CP showed larger areas under receiver operating characteristic curves with respect to the surgical population. Also, CP had a similar sensitivity but better specificity with CI reaching 85% versus 73% for the MELD score. 2 However, because of the nature of the current literature, which derives from aggregated or pooled data on different conditions and type of surgery, conclusions on the prognostic value of these scoring systems in cardiovascular interventions remain indicative, and ad hoc studies are warranted.

Other available risk scoring systems include Mayo Postoperative Mortality Risk Scores (available at https://www.mayoclinic.org/medical‐professionals/transplant‐medicine/calculators/post‐operative‐mortality‐risk‐in‐patients‐with‐cirrhosis/itt‐20434721), the Chronic Liver Failure–Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score and the Veterans Outcomes Costs Associated With Liver Disease (VOCAL)–Penn score, but their use is still limited compared with CP and MELD, and no robust data are available in the cardiovascular literature to perform a meaningful comparison (Table 2). When applying these scoring systems to the specific circumstances of the different types of surgery, expert assessment of the anatomic and clinical conditions in the context of a multidisciplinary team discussion should guide the decision on the feasibility of intervention or surgery. 3 , 4

Percutaneous Coronary Interventions

The prevalence of patients with clinically significant cirrhosis undergoing percutaneous procedures was recently estimated in a large multicentric study involving >7 million patients admitted with myocardial infarction (MI), and cirrhosis was found in 0.8% of the cases. 20 Importantly, it was found that patients with a greater extent of liver disease were less likely to receive an angiogram or percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) (odds ratio [OR], 0.6 for both) and had significantly worse outcomes in terms of major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (OR, 1.2), death (OR, 1.3), and major bleeding (OR, 1.7). Whether this is related to the wider metabolic derangement found in these patients or to a selection bias relative to the perception of increased risk of cirrhosis remains unknown. These results are echoed by another recent analysis of the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) database. 21 Alqahtani and colleagues showed in a propensity matched analysis that PCI in patients with cirrhosis was associated with a higher mortality rate (19.1% in ST‐segment–elevation MI and 8.7% in non–ST‐segment–elevation MI), acute kidney injury, greater resource use and higher cost compared with PCI in patients without cirrhosis. 22 Similarly, a recent report showed that long‐term outcomes in PCI are significantly affected by cirrhosis with a composite adverse event rate of 40% including stroke, kidney injury, and severe bleeding at 1‐year follow‐up. Total bleeding events reached 46% and included access site complications in 10% of cases. Importantly, a direct relation with the severity of CP class was identified, with the mortality rate at 1 year reaching 80% in CP class C, 42% in CP class B, and 30% in CP class A. 23 Available clinical studies on adult patients with chronic liver dysfunction undergoing PCI are summarized in Table 3.

In another large analysis from the NIS registry involving 4 376 950 patients undergoing PCI, liver disease was an independent predictor (OR, 2.6 [95% CI, 2.2–3.0]) of in‐hospital gastrointestinal bleeding, which carries a 10‐fold increased risk in‐hospital mortality rate (9.7% in patients with bleeding versus 1.1% in other patients; P<0.001). 51 Postprocedural hemorrhage and the requirement for blood product transfusion were reported as the most frequent complication (6.6% and 11.3%, respectively). 24

An important consideration for the interventionalist is the effect of dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) on the management of patients with LC. A recent nationwide cohort study analyzing outcomes of 13 years of practice has shown the benefit of DAPT on reducing the recurrence of MI in patients with advanced liver disease; however, this was obtained at the expense of a significantly increased risk of gastrointestinal bleeding (hazard ratio, 1.49 [95% CI, 1.31–1.70]). 52 The subgroup analysis suggested that patients with other underlying conditions or those with a higher preprocedural risk could be more prone to bleeding events if administered DAPT. The authors suggested that judiciously offering a single antiplatelet therapy may be a more suitable option in patients with cirrhosis. In another retrospective study, the rate of gastrointestinal bleeding was 22% in patients administered DAPT versus 5% in those who received aspirin monotherapy. 25 Recent literature suggests avoiding long‐term DAPT in patients with LC, as 1‐month‐only therapy seems reasonable and adequate to prevent early thrombotic complications. 53 These conclusions were mainly driven by results of the LEADERS FREE (A Prospective Study of the Biofreedom Biolimus A9 Drug Coated Stent in Patients at High Risk for Bleeding) and ONYX ONE (A Randomized Controlled Trial With Resolute Onyx in One Month Dual Antiplatelet Therapy for High Bleeding Risk Patients) trials. 53

The highest bleeding risk among patients with cirrhosis occurs in the subgroup with severe thrombocytopenia (<50 kU/L) and elevated portal pressure. Therefore, every effort should be made to maintain adequate thrombocyte count and prevent variceal bleeding. 53 Data from the literature also highlight the importance of a thorough preprocedural workup for patients with LC to screen for esophageal varices or previous gastrointestinal bleeding episodes, to stratify and balance the risk of significant hemorrhage in PCI. 52 Importantly, while CP and MELD criteria are validated to predict death, they cannot be used for bleeding risk prediction, leaving a significant gap in knowledge.

Even though percutaneous options are in some instances considered a last resource in cases where surgery is deemed high risk, liver disease still poses a significant burden in terms of short‐term morbidity, death, 21 and cost of care 22 on percutaneous procedures. This observational data might invite the question as to whether the spectrum of suitability of patients for PCI could be more carefully considered, especially in cases in which optimized medical therapy is also a possible alternative. Similarly, low‐risk, stable patients with angina with reduced ischemia burden and underlying LC might be best served via optimizing medical therapy and considered for an interventional approach only if symptoms are not controlled. 54

Cardiac Surgery

Cirrhosis is a known predictor of death in cardiac surgery. 4 The main factors involved in the increased death in these patients include hemodynamic and metabolic changes inherent to the underlying liver disease rather than the surgery itself. 1 Both the CP and MELD score were repeatedly found to predict death in cardiac surgery. 15 , 16 , 46 , 47 Despite the higher death and morbidity observed in those patients, cirrhosis or CP score are not currently included in any available operative risk score (EuroSCORE II and the Society of Thoracic Surgeons score) for cardiac surgery. 10 , 45 The most recent online Society of Thoracic Surgeons score contains a field relative to “liver disease,” but it includes a wide range of liver‐related conditions (chronic stable hepatitis, decompensated LC) and does not contemplate the severity degree of cirrhosis.

The prevalence of liver disease is growing even in patients undergoing cardiac surgery, especially due to the rising incidence of obesity and diabetes. However, no specific guidelines have addressed systematically the perioperative management of patients with cirrhosis, and the current evidence on this topic is restricted to observational data. Previous publications have reviewed the available evidence on the outcomes of patients with cirrhosis following cardiac surgery. 55 Table 3 summarizes the currently available studies on patients with cirrhosis undergoing cardiac surgery.

The short‐term mortality rate in patients with cirrhosis following cardiac surgery is significantly higher than that of the general population, with the 30‐day mortality rate reported as high as 20% and a 1‐year postoperative mortality rate of 40%. 11 , 41 A systematic review by Jacob et al analyzed outcomes in relation to the CP score and found a short‐term mortality rate of 9.0% in CP class A, 37.7% in CP class B, and 52.0% in class C. The overall 1‐year mortality rate reached 42% when considering the entire cirrhotic population, but when subdivided by CP, 27.2%, 66.2%, and 78.9% were attributable to CP classes A, B and C, respectively. 14 In another systematic review by Modi et al, which included 1 prospective and 8 retrospective studies, the overall mortality rate was 17.1% (CP classes A, B, and C were 5.2%, 35.4%, and 70%, respectively). 56 However, mild or compensated cirrhosis (defined by CP score <8) was shown not to be associated with a significant increase in postoperative morbidity and death. 13 These findings suggest that perioperative outcomes are strongly associated with the preoperative severity of liver dysfunction, suggesting that LC should be considered a continuous variable (proportional to disease severity) in scoring systems rather than a binary variable.

With regard to the long‐term mortality rate, a large Taiwanese nationwide study by Chou et al reported that patients with cirrhosis had significantly lower rates of 1‐, 5‐, and 10‐year survival when compared with matched controls without cirrhosis. 50 Similarly, recent results from a single‐center Canadian historical cohort showed a relative risk of 2.0 (95% CI, 1.3–2.9) at 10 years after surgery. 57 A 2015 meta‐analysis of surgery performed with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) found a 20.6% mortality rate at 1 year for patients in CP class A, 43.6% for patient in CP class B, and 56.5% in patients in CP class C. 7 On these grounds, the most recent guidance document from the British Society of Gastroenterology did not recommend CPB in patients with MELD score >13 or CP score >8. 4 , 15 , 43

The most frequent causes of death in patients with LC undergoing cardiac surgery are sepsis (≈25%), hepatorenal syndrome (≈20%), respiratory failure, ischemic gastrointestinal complications, and mediastinitis. 32 , 33 , 34 , 36 The pathogenic mechanism underlying sepsis is thought to result from decreased activity of the reticuloendothelial system, decreased complement concentration, and impairment of cell‐mediated response. 17 Furthermore, it is well known that malnutrition and hypoalbuminemia, which often coincide with liver disease, can negatively affect immune system function. 48 , 58 Araujo et al showed increased rate of infectious complications in patients with cirrhosis compared with patients without cirrhosis after coronary artery bypass grafting (OR, 2.93). 59 Length of intensive care unit stay is also expectedly increased in these subsets of patients and ranges between 5 and 18 days. 34 , 41

Hepatorenal syndrome in patients with cirrhosis undergoing cardiac surgery has not been adequately investigated by the current literature; however, it represents a daunting complication. Based on the outcomes of noncardiac surgery, the incidence of hepatorenal syndrome leads to an 8‐fold increase in postoperative death and infection (such as spontaneous bacterial peritonitis), while sepsis leads to a 4‐fold increase in death. 60 Hyponatremia frequently is associated with hepatorenal syndrome and is considered a negative prognostic marker. Use of vasopressors and albumin supplementation is the general line of treatment until the restoration of adequate perfusion in the splanchnic circulation and the neutralization of the systemic inflammatory response. 60

The incidence of postoperative complications ranges between 15% and 60% in reported series. Patients with CP classes B and C are mainly affected, and complication rates have reached up to 100%. 11 , 32 , 35 , 56 In a large propensity matched analysis of the NIS database involving 2231 patients with cirrhosis undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting, bleeding was the most common complication in patients with LC. 61 Throughout the literature, the increased consumption of blood products has been repeatedly reported with a linear association to the severity of cirrhosis. 17 In particular, portal hypertension and esophageal varices can contribute to significant gastrointestinal ischemia with subsequent hemorrhage following the systemic heparinization needed for surgery. 48 Significant portal hypertension and preexisting esophageal varices are crucial factors in determining the risk category in surgery.

A meta‐analysis published in 2022 by Kirov et al 62 summarized 33 studies with almost 50 000 patients, reporting short‐term and long‐term outcomes based on CP or MELD score. Patients with “high” CP or MELD score (based on the cutoffs proposed by each study) had a significantly higher risk of perioperative death (OR, 3.72 [95% CI, 2.75–5.03]; P<0.001), sepsis (OR, 3.88 [95% CI, 2.07–7.26]; P<0.001) and transfusional support (OR, 1.95 [95% CI, 0.43–2.64]; P<0.001). Also, a worse grade of liver disease was associated with neurological complications (OR, 1.49 [95% CI, 1.30–1.71]; P<0.001), postoperative acute kidney injury (OR, 3.84 [95% CI, 2.12–6.98]; P<0.001), and prolonged ventilation (OR, 2.45 [95% CI, 1.94–3.09]; P<0.001). Patients in the “high score” group had an increased long‐term mortality rate (risk ratio, 1.29 [95% CI, 1.14–1.46]; P<0.001). Considering the publications that reported MELD score in this meta‐analysis, 62 the median value of “high risk” was 12.6 (interquartile range, 12–15), while the mean value was 13.2±3.2.

However, there are no consistent data stratifying the risk of death or complications on the basis of the type of cardiac surgery procedure performed. As mentioned above, the main determinant of complications is thought to rely on the physiological alterations related to the extracorporeal circulation, which leads to a state of relative malperfusion and inflammatory reaction resulting in reticuloendothelial system dysfunction and in a significant imbalance of the cardiovascular homeostasis. Some observational evidence suggests a reduced mortality rate in procedures performed off‐pump. 34 , 36 Also, open‐heart valve surgery seems to carry a higher mortality rate when compared with the corresponding transcatheter approaches. 63

Transcatheter Procedures

Cirrhosis also represents a significant risk factor for transcatheter valve procedures. A recent analysis of the US NIS database including more than 160 000 patients identified advanced liver disease as independent predictor of death (OR, 3.0 [95% CI, 2.6–3.5]). 64 Therefore, additional considerations should be made for the treatment of aortic valve stenosis in patients with cirrhosis when balancing the option of surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) or transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). Interestingly, a recent retrospective analysis including patients with both end‐stage renal and liver disease that underwent TAVR showed 30‐day mortality rates similar to the general population (3.4% versus 2.1%), even though this represents a focus on a preselected population. However, a trend toward a higher 6‐month (6.9% versus 3.2%,) and 1‐year (15.4% versus 7.0%) mortality rate was identified. Furthermore, there was a concerning readmission rate >50% at both 6 months (53.2% versus 28.6%) and 1 year (65.4% versus 41.0%) following the procedure. 30 On the other hand, some reports have concluded that TAVR is a reasonable treatment option for patients with cirrhosis with aortic valve disease, as patients with severe aortic stenosis and concomitant LC undergoing TAVR demonstrated comparable outcomes to their counterparts without cirrhosis (10%–12% 1‐year mortality rate in both groups). 26 , 28 , 29 , 31 , 68 Finally, a recent meta‐analysis by Takagi et al, found that liver disease does not significantly impact early death in patients with cirrhosis undergoing TAVR, but does negatively impact the mid‐ to long‐term mortality rate. 73

Thakkar et al analyzed data from the US NIS and found that patients undergoing SAVR had a higher rate of blood transfusions and longer hospital stays compared with those undergoing TAVR, although in‐hospital death was similar in both groups. 66 In another analysis from the NIS database, Dhoble et al found that TAVR was associated with shorter hospital stays and less bleeding compared with SAVR. 68 Reports by Greason et al found that TAVR is associated with higher rates of paravalvular regurgitation but no difference in in‐hospital death or total hospital costs compared with SAVR. 65 On the other hand, Alqahtani et al and Khan et al found that SAVR was associated with an increase in in‐hospital death compared with TAVR. 67 , 71 Khan et al showed that TAVR was also associated with a lower incidence of postprocedural acute kidney injury requiring dialysis, bleeding requiring blood transfusion, and MI. 71 In other retrospective reports, TAVR showed better postprocedural results and long‐term survival compared with SAVR, 70 although a higher 30‐day operative mortality rate was also reported. 69 Results from Elbadawi et al, who performed an in‐depth analysis on patients with coexistent chronic liver and kidney disease, suggested that TAVR may also be an available option in this scenario as providing lower rates of postprocedural complications compared with SAVR. 72

In summary, the risk of patients with cirrhosis undergoing TAVR seems higher than those without cirrhosis but likely lower than surgery. Also, TAVR is likely to be performed in many patients who otherwise would not have been treated for their aortic stenosis, thus having a better outcome than those treated with optimized medical management alone. Importantly, because of the bleeding risk related to hepatic dysfunction, oral anticoagulants are a relative contraindication for surgical aortic valve replacement with a mechanical prosthesis in patients with cirrhosis. Therefore, most patients with cirrhotic with adequate anatomy for TAVR should be considered or treated with TAVR. 63

The benefit related to the reduced invasiveness of TAVR on patients with cirrhosis seems also plausible in the immediate postprocedural period; however, it is supported by retrospective heterogeneous studies with relatively short‐term follow‐up. With the increasing availability of data on these patients and the foreseeable improvements in access and delivery systems, it is possible to speculate a progressively increasing adoption of TAVR for these patients in the coming years, especially in high‐volume and experienced centers.

There is an important development need for risk stratification tools to help with clinical decision making for this high‐risk group and define thresholds of futility.

Contraindications to TAVR in patients with LC are mainly relative to hostile access or anatomy or to considerations on the severity of LC and prognosis. In fact, TAVR is generally considered futile if prognosis quoad vitam after the procedure is expected to be inferior to 1 year or in the circumstances of low likelihood to restore an adequate quality of life or relief from heart failure symptoms. A recent expert paper proposing an approach to estimate TAVR‐specific risk and futility included LC as a major organ failure. 74 While CP classes A and B still feature favorable long‐term survival rates and do not meet futility criteria, the prohibitive risk associated with CP class C, especially in the context of other organ dysfunction, would contraindicate TAVR on the basis of the short (<1 year) life expectancy. However, a multidisciplinary approach is recommended to carefully evaluate individual cases with longer life expectancy or other markers of futility such as concomitant end‐stage renal disease or chronic lung disease, pulmonary hypertension, or frailty. 58 , 63

On the front of mitral interventions, the use of transcatheter edge‐to‐edge repair (TEER) is gaining favor as an option for patients with degenerative mitral disease and prohibitive surgical risk. In EVEREST II (Endovascular Valve Edge‐to‐Edge Repair Study), which showed the safety of TEER and improved functional status and rehospitalization rates at 1 year, 3.1% of the cohort included patients with LC. 76 In a report from the NIS database (2013–2017), patients with LC undergoing Mitra‐Clip had significantly higher rates of in‐hospital death and cardiogenic shock when compared with patients without liver disease. 77 However, in a similar analysis by the same authors comparing the outcomes of TEER with surgery, the transcatheter approach was burdened by a significantly lower mortality rate (8.2% versus 16%), blood transfusion rates (30.3% versus 61.2%), and need for prolonged ventilation (1.2% versus 17.2%). 78 Nevertheless, limited data are available on this topic, and more evidence is awaited. Contraindication to TEER would include the presence of concomitant portopulmonary hypertension, which was shown to affect up to 65% of patients with LC. 79 In fact, pulmonary hypertension is associated with poor outcomes in TEER, 80 and concomitant biventricular dysfunction could make the intervention futile. 81

Tricuspid regurgitation is considered a death predictor in patients with LC before transplantation. 82 No studies investigating the impact of transcatheter tricuspid valve repair in patients with LC are currently available. However, the indirect evidence of improvement in liver function, 83 1‐year mortality rate, 84 and nutritional status 85 in patients with different degrees of liver derangement (mainly CP classes A and B) undergoing transcatheter tricuspid valve repair, allow speculation of a potential role of this therapy in patients with cirrhosis.

Despite the constant evolution and continued advancements in transcatheter cardiovascular interventions, there is still a lack of prospective data on clinically relevant outcomes in patients with chronic liver dysfunction undergoing transcatheter valvular procedures. Most of the available evidence comes from observational retrospective studies with relatively short‐term follow‐up. Topics of further investigations regard the outcomes and safety of TAVR in specific subgroups as in patients with high CP classes or MELD scores. On the front of transcatheter mitral and tricuspid valve interventions, outcomes in patients with LC have shown to be promising. However, data are scarce, and the criteria to identify ideal candidates are still poorly defined. 63

Relevant Clinical Considerations, Management, and Decisional Algorithm

In general, the prevalence of cirrhosis in patients requiring cardiac procedures is low, but liver disease carries a significant burden in terms of increased surgical risk, encountering in many cases the reluctance of surgeons and cardiologists to intervene. 38 In fact, the low prevalence could be considered a product of a significant selection bias, as patients with LC might be not referred if in an advanced stage or decompensated.

When approaching this subset of patients, a careful preoperative evaluation is warranted. From the analysis of the available literature, we extrapolated 3 main parameters that should assist in the assessment for any intervention: CP or MELD score, the presence of gastroesophageal varices (GEVs), and the hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG). 86

Advanced CP classes (B or C) or MELD scores >12 are associated with increasing liver decompensation and death after cardiac surgery. While surgery can be undertaken with acceptable results in CP class A, class B/C or a MELD score ≥13 are associated with extremely high mortality risk and poorer outcomes. 56

Portal hypertension and the presence of GEVs in these patients represent crucial nodes in the decisional algorithm and should be always investigated to orient decision making. Measurement of the HVPG is paramount to assess bleeding risk related to clinically significant portal hypertension (HVPG >10 mm Hg) and underlying preexisting or newly formed GEVs. GEV prevalence increases with higher CP classes (42.7% in CP class A, 70.7% in CP class B, >75% in CP class C) and is associated with significant risk of bleeding, morbidity, and death. 87 The bleeding risk for small varices and large varices ranges around 5% and 15% per year, respectively, and the mortality rate after an episode of esophageal varices bleeding has been reported at ≈20%. 88 Surgery is frequently contraindicated in these patients, but in candidates for cardiac surgery in CP class A, an urgent hepatological referral should be made with the view of prophylactic endoscopic variceal ligation in preparation for surgery. As per guidelines for primary prophylaxis, patients with GEVs should also be administered nonselective β blockers (propranolol, nadolol, and carvedilol). 89 Also, in this group of patients, the use of intraoperative TEE should be contraindicated for the high risk of bleeding.

History of recent GEV bleed represents instead an absolute contraindication to surgery due to the relatively high risk of rebleeding, and GEV treatment is mandatory before considering eligibility for surgery. It is also important to consider that patients treated with endoscopic variceal ligation for GEV bleed have a rate of early rebleeding following the endoscopic procedure ranging between 9% and 19%. 90

In the absence of GEVs, patients in CP class A or with a MELD score <12 should be considered for surgery. 86 Also, in these cases, measurement of the HVPG should not be deferred, as it is crucial to identify clinically significant portal hypertension and for risk stratification. Bleeding risk progressively increases for HVPG values >12 mm Hg and is negligible for values <10 to 12 mm Hg. 86 Additionally, HVPG is a useful parameter to predict the risk of liver decompensation after surgery, which remain minimal in patients with HVPG <10 mm Hg (or an indocyanine green clearance >0.63). Patients with HVPG between 12 and 16 remain in a gray zone at increased risk of bleeding. Such risk should be balanced in the context of the heart team against the risk of leaving the heart condition untreated. On the other hand, HVPG >16 mm Hg has been associated with increased risk of death, while values >20 mm Hg identify a population at prohibitive risk. 91

High‐risk surgical procedures may be still considered in carefully selected patients in CP class B (or those with elevated MELD scores) when percutaneous options are precluded by unfavorable anatomy or clinical conditions or when surgery remains the only feasible approach, as in the case of infective endocarditis.

An important diagnosis of exclusion in the decision process regards the presence or coexistence of congestive hepatopathy associated with tricuspid regurgitation complicating the clinical scenario of a patient with cirrhosis. The reversibility of the hepatic damage and the implementation of diagnostic tools (magnetic resonance elastography or biopsy) might help in the differentiation, but large‐scale studies and standardization in the evaluation of fibrosis (Table S1) are warranted before including those elements in the preoperative workup.

Additional parameters and predictors of poor outcomes in LC should also be considered. Preoperative high levels of total plasma bilirubin, decreased cholinesterase, thrombocytopenia, EuroSCORE, and CPB times were found to be predictors of death in patients with LC. 10 , 35 , 56 However, from the currently available data, it is not possible to extrapolate consistent thresholds or cutoff values for each of the variables identified across the literature.

Perioperative Considerations and Optimization

Once a surgical indication is established, preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative measures should be put in place to optimize a patient with liver disease and help mitigate the risk of complications (Figures 2 and 3). Liver dysfunction has a detrimental impact on various organ systems, including the nervous, respiratory, cardiovascular, digestive, and urinary systems, as well as the coagulation system. Preoperative optimization should focus on addressing these abnormal physiological changes, as well as malnutrition, electrolyte disturbances, and infections. 58

More than 80% of the patients with cirrhosis are malnourished due to factors related to impaired absorption, reduced food intake, and increased catabolism. Hypoalbuminemia below 2.1 g/dL is a known predictor of death 92 ; however, targeted albumin infusion did not have a significant impact on death in both general and cardiac surgery. 93 A nutritional consultation is recommended. Low‐sodium diets and adequate supplementation of electrolytes and vitamins, especially B1 and B12, has been shown to reduce the risk of postoperative complications such as delirium. 25 In particular, patients with a significant history of alcohol abuse should be counseled for a period of abstinence before surgery to prevent the potentially lethal withdrawal syndromes.

The intraoperative management of these patients is delicate and requires careful coordination with the anesthesiologists for hemodynamic, metabolic, respiratory, and coagulative optimization. In fact, vasoactive substances such as adrenaline, atrial natriuretic factor, and agents from the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system released during CPB, may be poorly metabolized by the failing liver, resulting in vasoplegia, alteration of the fluid balance, and further detrimental effects on the coagulation cascade. Moreover, liver hypoperfusion and hypothermia associated with CPB could further exacerbate the underlying coagulopathy and gas exchange impairment typical of cirrhosis. 1 On this note, an association between CPB times and poorer postoperative outcomes has been speculated; this is indirectly suggested by reports showing a relative decrease in the short‐term mortality rate in patients operated with off‐pump techniques. 34 , 36

When assessing bleeding risk, use of thromboelastography and viscoelastic tests are strongly recommended. 94 Specific up‐to‐date guidelines from the American Gastroenterological Association and the European Association for the Study of Liver can be followed to guide best practice. 95 , 96 Guidelines do not support routine correction of prolonged INR with fresh frozen plasma, as plasma expansion is known to worsen portal hypertension and risk of bleeding. 3 , 97 However, to correct coagulopathy in cardiac surgery, 94 the first line of treatment entails the use of fresh frozen plasma if the INR is >1.5 and there are no concerns for volume overload. Further steps may include the use of cryoprecipitate (if fibrinogen is <1 g/L), prothrombin complex, fibrinogen concentrate, or desmopressin. 97 Use of platelet or thrombopoietin receptor agonists is not recommended if platelets are >50×109/L and surgical control is achievable. 96 For cases in which the platelet count is <50×109/L, and in particular <20×109/L, transfusion or thrombopoietin receptor should be considered and dose adjustment should be evaluated according to the degree of LC severity. 95 , 96 However, those recommendations derive from hepatological guidelines 97 and might not be entirely pertinent in the context of complex cardiac surgery procedures in which the surgeon’s and anesthesiologist’s clinical judgment is required. 94

The ideal management relies in the thromboelastography‐guided replacement of specific coagulation factor concentrates. 98 Similarly, the use of thromboelastography should guide treatment for thrombocytopenia and potential prophylactic platelet infusion. 4 , 96 Importantly, the use of blood products should always be balanced against the risk of transfusion‐related acute lung injury or transfusion‐associated circulation overload. 98

From the surgical perspective, considering the increased risk of bleeding and the deranged cytochrome metabolism in patients with cirrhosis, avoidance of mechanical prostheses requiring lifelong oral anticoagulant therapy is preferred. Also, optimization of iron, folic acid, and vitamin B12 before high‐risk procedures should be performed. 94 , 95 , 96

In the perioperative period, an opioid‐sparing policy should be adopted aiming at reducing the doses or prolonging the interval among doses and avoiding constipation. Multimodal analgesia is recommended, and pain relief should favor paracetamol with a maximum of 2 g/d doses while avoiding nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory medications to prevent renal insults.

Antibiotic prophylaxis is similar to patients without cirrhosis, but specific microbiological guidelines should be followed in patients with low‐protein ascites (<1.5 g/dL), advanced cirrhosis, and previous spontaneous bacterial peritonitis or gastrointestinal hemorrhage. 60 The risk of bacterial infection and associated death are low in patients with CP class A cirrhosis. However, intravenous ceftriaxone 1 g/24 h should be considered in patients with advanced cirrhosis in hospital settings with a high prevalence of quinolone‐resistant bacterial infections and in patients on previous quinolone prophylaxis. 86

Limitations and Gaps in Evidence

The findings of this analysis should be interpreted taking into account its intrinsic limitations. The retrospective nature of this review, the heterogeneity of the data, mainly deriving from observational small‐sized cohort studies, and the potential selection, reporting, and publication biases are the main methodological limitations of this study. Importantly, conclusions derived from a large‐population data set, such as the NIS database, should be considered within the limitations relative to the typical lack of robust short‐term patient follow‐up in these registries. However, data might be useful to obtain indicative epidemiological trends on clinical conditions otherwise relatively infrequent, as per LC.

While the currently available risk score systems are able to adequately predict surgical mortality or morbidity rates, the introduction in these models of parameters related to the stage of the liver disease for patients with cirrhosis is plausible and supported by the evidence here gathered. If isolated liver function variables in the context of patients who otherwise have no cirrhosis might not have a direct effect on the risk scoring, in clinically diagnosed patients with cirrhosis the CP class or MELD score (as well as future results of MELD 3.0) might provide additional value and weight in the risk prediction models, further assisting decision making. At present, decision making in these cases remains often in a gray zone with no objective assessment of the preprocedural risk and is left in routine practice to the heart team and surgical expert opinion. Additionally, no specific societies’ guidelines are currently available, and the current level of evidence remains observational, explaining the hesitance of some centers to offer interventions. In particular, the lack of appropriate risk stratification or codification of liver disease in the current risk models and metrics 10 might lead to further risk aversion on the part of practitioners subjected to public reporting based on observed rather than expected mortality rate.

The assessment of liver stiffness by means of liver elastography has been increasingly used in clinical practice in the outpatient monitoring of liver disease, but few data are available in patients undergoing cardiac surgery or percutaneous procedures as a marker of central venous congestion. Increased liver stiffness predicts adverse outcomes after left ventricular assist device implantation, 99 and values >9.5 kPa were associated with a longer postoperative hospital length of stay after nonemergent cardiac surgery. 100 However, concerns about the overestimation of fibrosis due to liver congestion, the need of validation in patients undergoing percutaneous procedures and the usefulness of sequential measurements in patients undergoing cardiac surgery are crucial aspects that need to be elucidated.

Large‐registry or prospectively designed studies are warranted to further stratify risk, identify objective cutoff values for the relevant clinical parameters, and optimize the management of patients with liver disease requiring interventional procedures or cardiac surgery. The US NIS (or a similar data set) could be easily leveraged to address most of the clinical questions related to this topic.

Conclusions

Liver disease is unjustly neglected in the cardiovascular literature yet constitutes a conundrum for heart teams, who are often called to advise on the management of these challenging patients in routine clinical practice scenarios. Patients with cirrhosis are at extremely high risk and their management involves a wide spectrum of surgical, anesthesiologic, and medical considerations. Formulation of specific societies’ guidelines and ad hoc risk stratification are warranted.
