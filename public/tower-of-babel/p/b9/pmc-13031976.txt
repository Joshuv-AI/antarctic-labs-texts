Principal component analysis of 3-dimensional facial soft-tissue morphology in three adult populations
Authors: Chung H. Kau, Peter Borbely, Alexei Zhurov, Jolaiya Oguntoba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This preliminary study aims to identify soft tissue facial morphological features that vary among ethnically diverse, facially balanced adults using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of 3D facial scans. A total of 210 3D facial scans (35 per subgroup: Chinese, Hungarian, and Hispanic; stratified by sex) were selected from a database at the University of Alabama. Facial scans were captured using 3D-laser scanning and stereophotogrammetry (3dMD). A total of 57 landmarks were manually placed on each scan. The landmark coordinates were analyzed using Generalized Procrustes Analysis and PCA (conducted in R) to identify principal components contributing most significantly to shape variation. PCA identified four principal components (PCs) accounting for 77.72% of the total variance in soft tissue morphology. PC1 (49.13%) was associated with upper facial height. PC2 (17.70%) reflected the spatial relationship between nose protrusion and eye position. PC3 (6.31%) corresponded to interocular distance and vertical eye placement. PC4 (4.14%) represented upper lip protrusion. These PCs showed that the greatest morphological variability was in the upper facial region. Observed differences were interpreted within clinical and aesthetic contexts and were consistent with findings from prior literature on facial development and attractiveness. Significant variations in upper facial soft tissue morphology exist among normal adult subjects from different ethnic backgrounds. This study demonstrates the utility of PCA in revealing clinically relevant patterns in facial morphology and highlights the importance of individualized, ethnically sensitive treatment planning in orthodontics and orthognathic surgery. Future longitudinal and AI-driven studies are recommended to refine personalized diagnostics and develop inclusive aesthetic standards.

Introduction

3D-technology has improved the study of craniofacial morphology by providing better ways to measure and analyze facial features. Modern devices are portable, allowing quick capture of facial images1. These 3D images help create databases, evaluate treatments in orthodontics and orthognathic surgery, and monitor facial growth. With advancements such as 3D imaging and techniques like principal component analysis (PCA), researchers can conduct thorough evaluations of facial structures2–7.

Previously, treatment plans focused mainly on dental structures, but there is now more emphasis on facial balance in orthodontics. Facial morphology is shaped by both genetic and environmental influences, with over 50 genes linked to normal facial development8,9. Analyzing soft tissue facial morphology is essential for assessing treatment success and aesthetics. Differences in facial morphological features make it hard to diagnose and choose the best treatment in orthodontics. With increased migration, understanding variations in facial morphology across different ethnic groups has become crucial for professionals working to correct facial irregularities.

The most popular 3D-surface imaging systems among many capturing facial images include, 3D-laser scanners, stereophotogrammetry (3dMD)10. 3D-laser scanners project a predetermined pattern of laser light onto the object of focus, utilizing principles of geometrics to generate a 3-dimensional model of the object11. Stereophotogrammetry projects light mesh on a studied surface. The fundamental principle of stereophotogrammetry involves utilizing two or more cameras set up as a stereo pair to take simultaneous pictures of the subject12.

Many methods exist to study 3-D images, including using landmarks to analyze distances and angles, and comparing facial averages to assess changes from growth or treatment14. Generalized Procrustes Analysis (GPA) is a common means for analyzing landmark coordinates. It has been applied in facial morphometrics studies by comparing multiple faces with an average constructed face. These faces undergo transformation through rotation and translation to enable direct comparison13.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a is a multivariate statistical method that simplifies large datasets by reducing the number of variables, while preserving as much of the original information as possible to facilitate easier analysis. PCA is employed to delineate shapes within a collection of images, leveraging a predefined set of landmark points14. Following the landmarking process, the principal components construct a point distribution model, which is then utilized to identify shapes in new images.15 PCA can be single-level (standard PCA) or multi-level. In a single-level PCA, clusters or multi-level images within the subject population are not recognized, whereas multi-level PCA captures relationships between images at multiple levels15,16.

Multilevel principal component analysis shows relationships between images at multiple levels, it is the most suitable method when multilevel structures naturally exist in the data set and when there is a desire to incorporate PCA into models of shape15. Multilevel principal component analysis utilizes multilevel modeling method in statistics. It is a simple method that considers covariates or clustering while modeling multivariate data, like shape or image data.

Analyzing a patient’s facial morphological characteristics is crucial in assessing their orthodontic requirements, with the aim of attaining the best possible functional and aesthetic outcomes. Clinicians depend on facial measurements as a guide, paving the way towards reaching the desired treatment outcomes17. There is currently a shift in focus towards an approach that offers additional information obtained from assessing structures in the integumental covering of hard tissues. This approach is acknowledged as even more meaningful and is referred to as the soft-tissue approach to treatment planning18.

For years, facial morphological features have been described using the Neoclassical canons introduced by the Ancient Greeks during the Renaissance17,19. The Neoclassical canons of facial norms were formulated based on European Caucasian facial morphology on 2D-imaging and was not universally applicable to other racial and ethnic groups, applying this normative data to an individual from a different ethnic background compromises or entirely erases their unique identity20.

With advancement in technology, 3D imaging of the face has provided an opportunity to assess a third dimension as it creates an impression of depth, as it captures the image of the human face in the 3 spatial planes (vertical, transverse, sagittal). Also, employing a robust technique of analysis such as PCA, a simple method that considers covariates, like shape or image data, provides an opportunity for an in-depth analysis of facial soft tissue morphological features.

Current goals in orthodontics are focused on achieving aesthetically pleasing and proportionate results. It is imperative that clinicians recognize variations among populations and adjust treatment approaches accordingly to avoid errors in care. This study has identified a research gap and as such rationale for this study, most facial norms in orthodontics are based on 2D analyses and homogenous populations, limiting their relevance for diverse ethnic groups. This can lead to inaccurate diagnoses and less effective treatment planning. There is a need for a more inclusive approach using 3D imaging and advanced statistical methods like Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to capture and quantify ethnic variations in facial soft tissue morphology. Addressing this gap will support personalized, ethnically sensitive orthodontic care.

Objective

This preliminary research study aims to utilize PCA to identify soft tissue morphological facial features that contribute to differences observed among the selected facially balanced subjects from three adult populations.

Materials and methods

Research ethical compliance

Ethical approval was obtained from University of Alabama at Birmingham Institutional Review Board as an exempt study, and written informed consent was collected from all participants. All methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and were approved as described in the Methods section.

Study design

This is a retrospective cross-sectional study. It utilizes primary quantitative and biodemographic data obtained from the subjects facial scan database of the Craniofacial unit of the Department of Orthodontics at the University of Alabama, consisting of biodemographic data and facial scans which were captured using 3D-laser scanner and stereophotogrammetry (3dMD LLC, Atlanta, Ga) systems at one time point. All facial scans within the databases were performed under standardized conditions, including neutral facial expression, consistent lighting, and subjects in natural head position.

Sample size determination

Non-probability sampling was adopted; the study used the convenience sampling method. To minimize the selection bias often associated with nonprobability sampling, we applied the quota sampling approach, to allow for sample match for the different ethnic sub-groups of subjects recruited into the study. A representative moderately sized sample of 210 facial scans were selected, with 35 facial scans for each of the six subgroups of subjects to be understudied (Hungarian male group, Hungarian female group, Hispanic male group, Hispanic female group, Chinese male group, Chinese female group).

Study groups

Facial scans were be selected in compliance with the selection criteria, the study group consist of adult subjects from Chinese, Hungarian and Hispanic ethnic background. The selection of subjects to the study population reflects even distribution by age and gender.

Landmarking

3dMD Vultus software was used for 3D landmark point placement using a non-rigid template. 57 three-dimensional landmarks were placed manually on 201 facial scans of the subjects. Each landmark was identified in space by a 3D x, y, z co-ordinate system. The coordinates were then scaled with the nasion as the 0, 0, 0 co-ordinate for the General Procrustes analysis Then facial scans were aligned to a common coordinate system using [specific alignment protocol], resampled to a uniform mesh resolution, normalized for scale and orientation for a PCA to be performed.

Reliability and reproducibility

Landmarking reliability and reproducibility of the examiner was assessed. An intraclass correlation (ICC) was used to evaluate the intra-examiner reliability and evaluate the examiner measurement of consistency.

57 facial landmarks were placed on 30 randomly selected facial scans, and later re-landmarked by the same examiner after a 2-week interval. Intra-class correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated for each landmark coordinate (x, y, z) to evaluate repeatability. Also, the reproducibility of landmarks identification at 2-week interval was assessed (Fig. 1).

Variables and analysis

The facial scan was superimposed, then GPA without scaling was performed to allow alignment of the facial scans (shapes) only by translating and rotating them, but keeping their original sizes, then quantitative data of 3D location of each landmark point in the x, y and z coordinate were obtained. Each facial shape was defined by 57 three-dimensional landmarks (Figs. 2, 3 and 4), resulting in a total of 171 statistical variables (57 × 3). For comparison with previous studies, we also used simplified facial shapes represented by only 21 landmarks, which corresponds to 63 variables. The quantitative data of 3D location of each landmark point in the x, y and z coordinate was extracted (63 in total), this means the variables for all subjects were obtained to be used in principal component analysis to assess soft tissue facial morphological variability.

The Principal Component Analysis, a multivariate statistical approach, was used to analyze high-dimensional data and capture the most important information from it. PCA was performed on the combined dataset to capture overall facial variation. This was done by transforming the original data into a lower-dimensional space while collating highly correlated variables together. Principal Component Analysis was conducted on the simplified data sets (63 variables per subject), to identify the most significant principal components using R software. PCA was used to rotate and scale the aligned shapes, generating new statistical variables known as principal components (PCs).

The first principal component (PC1) represents the direction of the largest variance within the entire sample. The second principal component (PC2) is perpendicular to the first and accounts for the second largest variance. Similarly, the third principal component (PC3) is perpendicular to the first two and explains the third-largest variance, and so on. These new variables (principal components) were linearly expressed in terms of the original variables, with individual coefficients referred to as loadings. The loading with a larger magnitude indicates a greater contribution of the variable to the principal component.

In the principal component analysis, loadings ranged from − 1 to 1, and the sum of the squares of all loadings for each principal component equals 1. It is important to state that all variables contribute to all the principal components, however each original variable contributes largely to only one principal component. Exclusions were executed when a variable enters two or three principal components with substantial and equal loadings. While all variables influence all principal components, most contributions are negligible. Therefore, we retained only the variables that have large or moderate loadings in magnitude, greater than 0.2, or the largest ones even though they are smaller than 0.2. Following these assumptions and neglecting the loadings of less than 0.1 in magnitude, we arrive at the results summarized in Table 1.

Results

Reliability and reproducibility assessment

ICC values were between 0.85 and 0.93 for all variables, which meant that reliability fell between good and excellent. Most of the X, Y, and Z coordinates of the 57 facial landmarks were reproducible to less than 1 mm which is clinically acceptable.

PCA outcome reporting

Following a PCA with scaling on the simplified data set, 63 variables were independent, each explained 1.59% (100/63) of the total variance in the sample. Some of the variables were correlated, PCA identified several new variables (principal components) that explained greater proportions of the total variance.

These components explain 85% of the total variance and, therefore, contain the most crucial information for further analysis; the remaining 56 principal components explain a tiny proportion of the total variance and are not considered for further analysis. Identified landmark coordinates contributing to the key principal component

The most significant landmark coordinates contributing to Principal Component 1 identified were found to belong to two groups: (i) negative loadings: gY, nY, enRY, and enLY and (ii) positive loadings: chRY, chLY, lsY, cphRY, and cphLY. Negative loadings indicate that the value of the principal component decreases as the coordinates increase, while positive loadings indicate that the value of the principal component increases as the coordinates increase. The first group represents landmarks around nasion, and the second group refers to landmarks around the mouth and upper lip. When the coordinates increase, the distance between the two groups also increases, and vice versa. This suggests that PC1 may be interpreted as upper face height.

Upon further analysis, we observe two groups of significant coordinates, with negative and positive loadings, that contribute to PC2. One group pertains to the Z-position of the nose landmarks (prnZ, snZ, nZ, alLZ, alRZ), while the other represents the Z-coordinates of the eye landmarks (exLZ, exRZ, enLZ, enRZ, piLZ, piRZ, psLZ, psRZ). Therefore, PC2 can be interpreted as the distance between these two groups, reflecting the nose’s protrusion relative to the eyes.

PC3 is characterized by two groups of coordinates: the X-positions of three pairs of eye landmarks (exLX, exRX, psLX, psRX, enLX, enRX), which have both positive and negative loadings, and the Y-positions of three pairs of eye landmarks (psLY, psRY, piLY, piRY, exLY, exRY), all of which have negative loadings. The first group of coordinates can be viewed as the interocular distance, while the second group indicates the vertical position of the eyes relative to the reference frame’s origin, which is defined as the centroid of the dataset located near the subnasale.

PC4 represents upper lip protrusion, since it is defined by five Z-coordinates of the upper lip: chLZ, chRZ, lsZ, cphLZ, and cphRZ.

PC5 is defined by three pairs of horizontal midface coordinates: chLX, chRX, piLX, piRX, alLX, and alRX. These coordinates have both positive and negative loadings, allowing us to interpret PC5 as a measurement of midface width. This measurement encompasses the widths of the mouth, interocular area, and nose.

PC6 can be treated as a combination of chin protrusion and lower lip height. It is defined by three significant coordinates: pgZ, liZ, and liY.

PC7 is represented by several disimilar coordinates: pgY (large positive loading), prnY, snY, alLY, alRY (small negative loadings), chLX, chRX (moderate positive and negative loadings), and gZ (moderate negative loading). This is a combination of lower face height, mouth width, and forehead protrusion.

The last three principal components, PC5, PC6, and PC7, encompass various facial features but explain considerably less variation than the first four components. Moreover, it is challenging to assign a clear meaning to them. For the sake of simplicity in our further analysis, we will disregard PC5, PC6, and PC7, focusing instead on PC1 through PC4.

Discussion

Anthropological studies have consistently shown notable variations in craniofacial structures across racial backgrounds, paralleling the differences found in facial anthropometric proportions21,22. In this present study, we observed significant variability in certain facial soft tissue morphological features in the subpopulation examined.

Identification of the principal components

Four key principal components (PC) were identified, and they cumulatively contributed 77.72% to the total variance observed. Principal Components 1 to 4 (PC1–PC4) were interpreted as representing specific morphological features of the facial soft tissue, primarily related to the upper face. This indicates that the subjects studied showed greater variation in the upper facial region compared to the lower face, suggesting that the lower face exhibited fewer differences in soft tissue morphology in the studied normal subjects. These findings align with a recent PCA study conducted on a diverse group of adult females from various racial backgrounds. That study, which analyzed common aesthetic facial features within the cohort, also found significant variation in upper facial measurements, while the only feature that did not differ significantly among the populations was lower face height23.

Principal component 1 (PC1) and face height-Fig. 5

Notably, PC1 interpreted as the upper facial height emerged as parameter which was constituted by y-co-ordinate landmarks and contributed the largest portion of the total variance (49.13%), this indicates its high degree of individual variation within the study group. This observation aligns with findings from PCA study on Neolithic Chinese populations, which reported that individuals from northern China generally exhibited a greater upper facial height compared to those from southern China24. A systematic review assessing inter-ethnic variability in facial dimension concludes that the most significant inter-ethnic variability in facial proportions are found in forehead height25. In clinical orthodontics, facial height is considered a key factor in achieving facial harmony and aesthetics26, based on this, Principal Component 1 (PC1) represents a measure that should be individually tailored to achieve facial harmony that is considered ideal for each specific individual.

Principal component 2 (PC2) and facial depth-Fig. 6

The findings of the present study highlight a relationship between two groups of landmarks along the z-coordinate. The first and second groups respectively correspond to nose protrusion and eye position. The distance between these two features, represented by PC2, accounted for the second largest contribution to the total variability (17.70%). This indicates that the distance between the tip of the nose and the location of the eyes varies significantly among the subjects. There was no study found in the literature with reports on the variability on the distance between nose protrusion and eyes position. From the present study, PC2 reflects a spatial relationship between the tip of the nose and location of the eyes, this relationship is supported by findings from a study by Richmond et al., looking at gene association in regionalized facial features in normal population, PAX3 gene has been found to be associated with Eye - Nasion distance as well as nose tip27. Also, the Circles of Prominence (COP) is a distinctive theory to describe the optimal arrangement of facial features that contribute to perceived facial beauty. The Circle of prominence (COP theory) proposes that the precise placement of the iris of the eyes contributes to the optimal positioning of other facial features. This theory further hypothesizes that the alignment between the eyes (iris) and the nasal tip establishes a foundational relationship that other facial features ideally align with28. The present study identified a relationship between two groups of landmarks associated with nose protrusion and eye position, which is in line with the Center of Prominence (COP) theory. In a study assessing perceptions of facial attractiveness in subjects that had orthognathic surgery, the eyes and nose were highly rated as key facial features that significantly contribute to perceived facial attractiveness29. The demonstrated relevance of the relationship between nose protrusion and eye position—and their variability in relation to facial attractiveness—further reinforces the need for these measurements to be individualized during diagnosis and treatment planning.

Principal component 3 (PC3) and width of the eyes-Fig. 7

This study identified landmarks within the x-and y- coordinates located around the periobital part of the face, the group landmarks on the x-coordinate represented the interocular distance and the group of landmarks on the y-coordinate represented the vertical position of the eyes relative to the subnasale. PC 3 is characterized by these 2 groups of co-ordinates and accounts for 6.31% of the total variability, this suggests that subjects varied by distance between their eyes as well as the height of the eyes from the subnasale. An example of an interocular distance measure is the intercanthal distance (enRx to enLx), a study observed a significant relationship between intercanthal distance and inter-canine width. The study found that a biometric ratio of 1:1.13 could be used to reliably estimate inter-canine width30. However, findings from another study that investigated the correlation between four extraoral facial landmarks and inter-canine width revealed that, for most individuals, the variability between the actual and calculated inter-canine widths was less than 5% for all landmarks—except intercanthal distance, which showed a variability greater than 5%. This indicates that the variability in intercanthal width largely contributed to the differences observed between the actual and calculated inter-canine widths31. In orthodontics, inter-canine width is a key determinant in predicting treatment outcomes, as it directly impacts space availability and the long-term stability of the dentition. Its observed relationship with interocular distance (Intercanthal) in the present study further underscores the significance of these findings.

Principal component 4 (PC4) and width of mouth-Fig. 8

Upper lip protrusion was identified as the fourth principal component in this study, accounting for 4.14% of the total variance observed in the population. All landmarks contributing to this feature were located along the z-coordinate. In soft tissue 2D cephalometric analysis, lip prominence is typically evaluated using various reference lines, including the S-line (Steiner), E-line (Ricketts), H-line (Holdaway), Z-line (Merrifield), Riedel plane (Riedel), Subnasale–Pogonion line (Burstone), and the true vertical line through subnasale (SnV). These reference lines have provided normative values that are widely applied across populations, often without regard to ethnic differences between the reference population and the individual being assessed. In the present study, variability in upper lip protrusion was observed among subjects and is consistent with findings from other studies, despite differences in the ethnic backgrounds of the populations studied. One of these studies assessing ethnic differences in soft tissue profile, found that Turkish subjects showed higher H angle measurements than North Americans, with increased upper lip prominence, Turkish subjects showed higher H angle values than North Americans (2 degrees for females, 3 degrees for males) and the upper lip prominence was identified to have accounted for the difference observed32.

This study contributes to the existing body of knowledge by confirming that upper facial soft-tissue structures account for the greatest variance across ethnic groups, identifying clinically relevant PCs related to upper facial height, nose–eye spatial relationship, peri-orbital width/height, and lip protrusion, also demonstrating PCA’s utility for ethnically sensitive treatment planning in orthodontics and orthognathic surgery.

Key strengths of this study lie in the application of a robust analytical method such as PCA, which enabled a comprehensive examination of a large dataset. This method of analysis effectively reduces data dimensionality by transforming variables into principal components that retain most of the original information, enhancing the clarity and interpretability of complex facial morphology data. Also, the findings of this study are relevant to Orthodontics, as they highlight the importance of recognizing ethnic differences in treatment planning. They also emphasize the need to consider variations—particularly in vertical facial dimensions and midface prominence—during 3D planning of orthognathic surgery and outcome evaluation. This study concurrently performed an automated landmarking using Diffusion Net, although not part of the current analysis but to allow for comparison with the result of a planned future research study. Also, PCA was performed on the combined dataset to capture overall facial variation, with separate subgroup analyses by ethnicity conducted to explore group-specific patterns, which is to be published later in a planned future research article.

The present study focuses on soft tissue shape which has been shown to closely reflect the underlying skeletal anatomy. Studies show that surface morphology serves as a dependable proxy for assessing skeletal shape variation and offers valuable data that can support the creation of personalized craniodental treatment plans33. 3D photogrammetry is a promising and widely adopted tool for measuring and visualizing cranial shape. However, previous studies have employed a broad range of both standardized and non-standardized measurement techniques, many of which fall short in fully capturing the complexity and detailed features of craniofacial morphology. However, principal component analysis used in the present study is robust, novel and able to summarize complex data, and as such it can be applied in the study of craniofacial anomalies.

It is important to state that, in its classical formulation, principal component analysis does not isolate or evaluate individual landmarks but instead characterizes global patterns of soft-tissue shape variation that may be broadly associated with anatomical regions. While the resulting covariation patterns may reflect relative spatial relationships among facial structures, they do not provide the level of specificity required for direct clinical inference based on discrete linear, angular, or volumetric measurements. Accordingly, attributing individual principal components to specific anatomical landmarks or localized clinical features should be approached with caution, as such interpretations may extend beyond the analytical resolution of the method. This limitation was considered when translating these findings to clinical contexts and suggests the need for complementary analytical approaches in future orthodontic and craniofacial research. While this method provides insight into overall patterns of facial shape variation, caution is warranted when attempting to link individual principal components to specific anatomical landmarks, as such interpretations may go beyond what principal component analysis can reliably resolve in a clinical context.

Another notable limitation of the study is the lack of comparable research and literature, which restricts the ability to evaluate findings against existing work. Furthermore, one limitation of this study is the relatively small sample sizes—210 subjects scans—which, while informative, are not sufficient for reliable generalization to the global population. Nonetheless, they provide valuable insights and foundation for further research.

Conclusion

This preliminary study confirms that normal subjects from three distinct ethnic groups exhibit considerable variation in certain identified soft tissue facial morphological features, primarily located in the upper region of the face. This study is believed to be the first of its kind to link insights from facial Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to clinically relevant concepts in orthodontics field where treatment decisions heavily rely on craniofacial analysis.

Future research could undertake comparative analyses within and between sub-populations from this study, with a focus on the four principal components identified. Such studies may offer valuable insights into the specificity of these principal components across variables such as age, sex, and ethnicity. Additionally, future studies could be designed as longitudinal investigations, tracking cohorts of individuals with both normal facial structures and craniofacial anomalies. The integration of artificial intelligence in these studies could enhance the identification and differentiation of facial features across ethnic groups, further advancing personalized approaches in diagnosis and treatment planning.

An enhanced understanding of facial soft tissue morphological variations across a population group will enable clinicians to provide more personalized treatment based on individual facial characteristics. However, this growing insight also exposes the limitations of traditional neoclassical beauty canons, which are insufficient for evaluating facial aesthetics in non-Caucasian populations. Consequently, there is a need to examine how variations in aesthetic subunits interact within our increasingly diverse population. This will aid in establishing new, inclusive standards of facial beauty that are relevant across all ethnicities. Such standards will support tailored aesthetic and reconstructive procedures that not only deliver visually pleasing results but also honor and maintain features representative of racial and ethnic identity.
