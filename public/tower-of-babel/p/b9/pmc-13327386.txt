Effects of Mirror Therapy on Hand Function in Children with Spastic Cerebral Palsy: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Nikita Patel, Prasad Muley, Bhavana Gadhavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Context:Mirror therapy utilizes visual feedback mechanisms to enhance motor recovery in neurological conditions. This systematic review examines its effectiveness for hand function improvement in children with spastic cerebral palsy.Methodology:Systematic searches across PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL, Physiotherapy Evidence Database, Google Scholar, and ResearchGate identified studies from 2014 to 2024 using the unified search query of mirror therapy and cerebral palsy. Eligible studies included randomized controlled trials and comparative investigations involving children aged 3–18 years with spastic cerebral palsy. Meta-analyses employed random-effects models.Results:Twelve studies (456 children) met the inclusion criteria. Meta-analysis revealed substantial improvements: hand function quality (standardized mean difference = 1.42; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.89–1.95; P < 0.001), manual dexterity (Cohen’s d = 0.71; 95% CI: 0.42–1.00; P < 0.001), functional independence (d = 0.89; 95% CI: 0.52–1.26; P < 0.001), and grip strength (d = 0.38; 95% CI: 0.12–0.64; P = 0.004).Conclusion:Evidence supports mirror therapy as an effective adjunctive rehabilitation intervention for enhancing hand function in children with spastic cerebral palsy, with moderate-to-large effect sizes across multiple functional domains.

Introduction

Cerebral palsy encompasses a heterogeneous group of permanent neurological disorders that fundamentally affect motor development and postural control mechanisms, stemming from non-progressive brain injury during critical developmental periods.[1] Spastic cerebral palsy accounts for 80%–85% of cases, with global prevalence ranging from 1.5 to 4 per 1,000 live births.[2] Upper extremity dysfunction disturbs activities of daily living, play, and academic participation.[3]

Mirror therapy, originally developed for phantom limb pain management,[4] uses visual feedback from an unaffected limb’s reflection to facilitate neural reorganization.[5678]

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizes current evidence examining mirror therapy’s effects on hand function.

Methods

This investigation followed Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines.[9] The review protocol was not prospectively registered. Comprehensive electronic searches were conducted across seven databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Google Scholar, ResearchGate, Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL, and Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro) from January 1, 2014, through December 31, 2024. The unified search strategy combined all three conceptual domains into a single query related with mirror therapy in hand function in hemiplegic cerebral palsy children

Eligibility criteria and subgroup analysis

Studies were included in this systematic review if they met the predefined criteria: children and adolescents participants aged 3–18 years with a confirmed diagnosis of spastic cerebral palsy, presenting with spasticity levels of Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS) grades 1 to 1+ and Manual Ability Classification System (MACS) levels II, III, or IV.[3] Eligible study designs encompassed randomized controlled trials (RCTs), quasi-experimental designs, or high-quality observational comparative studies that utilized mirror therapy as a primary or adjunctive intervention. Outcomes were required to provide quantitative assessments of hand function, manual dexterity, grip strength, or functional independence using validated instruments. Studies were excluded if they involved participants with severe spasticity (MAS ≥2), significant sensory impairments, fixed contractures, or moderate-to-severe cognitive impairments. Additional exclusion criteria included recent surgical procedures within 6 months, botulinum toxin injections within 3 months, current use of upper extremity orthoses, or uncontrolled epilepsy. Methodologically, the review excluded non-peer-reviewed publications, qualitative studies lacking quantitative data, and RCTs with a PEDro quality score of <6/11.[10] Subgroup analyses were conducted based on age (3–8 vs. 9–18 years), topography (hemiplegia vs. diplegia), and intervention combinations such as constraint-induced movement therapy or neurodevelopmental treatment. However, it is important to note that these subgroup analyses are inherently limited by the small number of included studies (ranging from k = 2 to k = 4 per subgroup), which reduces statistical power and increases the risk of Type II errors. Consequently, these subgroup findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive clinical evidence.

Study selection

Initial database searches using the unified query and database-specific filters identified 3247 records across all sources. After removing duplicates (n = 1391) using EndNote X9 reference management software (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA), 1856 unique records underwent title-abstract screening by two independent reviewers (N.P. and P.M.) using Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia). Full-text assessment of 48 potentially eligible articles was conducted independently by both reviewers (N.P. and P.M.), with disagreements resolved through discussion or, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (B.G.).[9] Of the 48 full-text articles assessed, 36 were excluded for the following reasons: inappropriate population (n = 8), incorrect intervention (n = 7), irrelevant outcomes (n = 5), inadequate study design (n = 10), duplicate publication (n = 2), and insufficient methodological quality (n = 4). The complete study selection process, including the flow from identification to inclusion, is illustrated in the PRISMA Flow Diagram [Figure 1].[9] Inter-rater agreement for the screening of these 1,856 records and the subsequent full-text assessment of 48 studies was substantial, with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.82 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.71–0.93). Ultimately, twelve studies published between 2014 and 2024 met all inclusion criteria for qualitative synthesis, with eleven of these studies providing sufficient quantitative data to be included in the meta-analyses. Quality Assessment of this study through RCTs was evaluated using the PEDro scale, an 11-item instrument assessing internal validity and statistical reporting.[10] Scores were interpreted as excellent (9–10), good (6–8), fair (4–5), or poor (<4), with only RCTs scoring ≥6 included in this review. Observational comparative studies were assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), which evaluates group selection, comparability, and outcome ascertainment (scores 0–9); scores ≥7 indicated high quality.[11] Systematic reviews were evaluated using the Assessment of Multiple Systematic Reviews (AMSTAR) tool, where scores ≥8 indicated high methodological rigor.[12] Two independent reviewers (N.P. and P.M.) performed these assessments, with disagreements resolved through consensus. Inter-rater agreement for quality assessment was substantial, with a Cohen’s \kappa of 0.78 (95% CI: 0.64–0.92). Detailed item-level scores for all included studies are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

The extraction criteria included

(1) Study characteristics (design, setting, year); (2) participant demographics (age, CP type, MACS/MAS levels); (3) intervention parameters (duration, frequency, intensity, and adjunctive therapies); (4) specific outcome measures; (5) quantitative results; and (6) adverse events or dropouts. One reviewer (N.P.) performed the primary data extraction, and a second reviewer (P.M.) independently verified the data from 50% of the included studies to ensure accuracy. The inter-rater reliability for the data extraction process was found to be excellent, with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.79–0.97). Discrepancies were resolved through direct discussion and verification against the source articles.

Outcome measures

Primary outcomes for this review included validated assessments of movement quality, dexterity, strength, and functional independence. The Quality of Upper Extremity Skills Test (QUEST) was utilized as a criterion-referenced assessment evaluating movement patterns across four domains consisting of 19 items; total scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating superior function.[13] The QUEST has demonstrated excellent test–retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC] = 0.95) and concurrent validity with the Peabody Developmental Motor Scales (r = 0.91). Manual dexterity was assessed via the Box and Block Test, a performance-based measure requiring participants to transfer 1-inch blocks between compartments within 60 s, which maintains excellent test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.97).[14] Grip strength served as an objective measure of hand strength obtained via calibrated handheld dynamometry (typically using a Jamar dynamometer) with standardized positioning (ICC = 0.96).[15] Functional independence was measured using the ABILHAND-Kids, a parent-reported questionnaire containing 21 bimanual activities rated on a 3-point scale; strong test-retest reliability (Pearson r = 0.91) and construct validity have been established for this tool.[16] Reliability statistics are reported as originally published by the instrument developers: ICCs are provided for the QUEST, Box and Block Test, and grip strength, while the ABILHAND-Kids reliability is reported as a Pearson correlation coefficient (r) to remain consistent with the original validation study by Arnould et al.[16] Secondary outcomes included the Melbourne Assessment of Unilateral Upper Limb Function, Jebsen-Taylor Hand Function Test, Peabody Developmental Motor Scale-2 (fine motor quotient), MAS (spasticity), and the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL).

Statistical analysis

Meta-analyses were conducted using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software version 3.0 (Biostat, Englewood, NJ, USA),[17] with effect sizes calculated as standardized mean differences (SMDs) (Hedges’ g to account for small sample sizes) and 95% CIs. Effect size interpretation followed Cohen’s conventions: small (0.20–0.49), moderate (0.50–0.79), and large (>0.80).[18] The selection of the pooling model was determined by statistical heterogeneity; a fixed-effect model was prioritized for low heterogeneity (I2 < 40%), while a random-effects model (DerSimonian-Laird) was employed only when significant heterogeneity was confirmed by Q-statistics (P < 0.10) and I2 values.[19] Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using I2 statistics accompanied by 95% CIs to account for the known instability of I2 in small-sample meta-analyses.[19] Robustness was evaluated via leave-one-out sensitivity analyses and stratification by methodological quality (PEDro 6–7 vs. 8–10). Meta-regression examined intervention duration as a continuous predictor for grip strength. Publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots; however, quantitative evaluation via Egger’s regression test[20] was strictly reserved for outcomes with >10 studies to ensure sufficient statistical power. Consequently, while funnel plots were generated for all primary outcomes, formal regression testing was only applied to grip strength and manual dexterity where the number of contributing datasets approached or met this threshold, ensuring a cautious and statistically justified approach to bias detection.

Results

Study characteristics

A total of twelve studies published between 2014 and 2024 were included in this review (n = 456),[72122232425262728293031] encompassing eight RCTs, one systematic review, one comparative cross-sectional study, and two case studies [Table 1]. The inclusion of case studies was justified to provide a comprehensive landscape of current clinical applications and to capture longitudinal individual progress not always detailed in aggregate RCT data, particularly regarding rare mixed phenotypes. The participant age range was 4–17 years, with a mean age of 8.6 years (standard deviation [SD] = .2). Nine studies focused exclusively on hemiplegic cerebral palsy and two on diplegic presentations, while one study included mixed phenotypes; this inclusion was pre-specified in the data extraction protocol to ensure a holistic evaluation of mirror therapy across diverse spastic CP presentations. Across the 12 included studies, the median sample size was 38 participants, with individual study cohorts ranging from a single case (n = 1) to 90 participants. Geographically and operationally, seven studies (58%) were conducted in outpatient rehabilitation settings, three (25%) in specialized pediatric hospitals, and two (17%) utilized combined clinic and home-based interventions. Intervention characteristics

Mirror therapy protocols demonstrated across the included studies shared core technical elements despite variability in specific play-based applications.[456] Mirror specifications typically involved standard first-surface mirrors positioned at the mid-sagittal plane, with sizes ranging from 30 cm × 40 cm to 50 cm × 60 cm, placed on a stable surface to conceal the affected limb while providing a clear reflection of the unaffected limb. Therapeutic protocols incorporated a range of age-appropriate motor activities, including object manipulation, precision tasks, finger individuation, reach-to-grasp movements, and functional task practice tailored to pediatric engagement.[7] Session parameters varied, with durations ranging from 15 to 60 min (median: 30 min) and frequencies of 3–7 days per week (median: 5 days). The total intervention period spanned 3–12 weeks, with a median duration of 6 weeks. While primarily conducted in outpatient clinics (n = 7), four studies successfully incorporated home-based practice under parental supervision. Comparison conditions included conventional physical or occupational therapy (n = 5), sham interventions using covered mirrors or transparent plastic (n = 2), bilateral training without mirrors (n = 2), and alternative approaches such as kinesiology taping, motor imagery, or waitlist controls. Mirror therapy was utilized as a standalone intervention in three studies, whereas the remainder combined it with conventional therapy (n = 5), constraint-induced movement therapy (n = 2), or neurodevelopmental treatment (n = 2).

Quality assessment results

The eight RCTs yielded PEDro scores ranging from 6 to 8 (mean = 7.25, SD = 0.71),[10] with 37.5% achieving excellent quality ratings (score = 8) and 62.5% demonstrating good quality (scores 6–7). The included systematic review demonstrated high methodological rigor, scoring 8/11 on the AMSTAR checklist, characterized by a comprehensive search strategy and duplicate selection processes.[12] The comparative cross-sectional study was rated as moderate quality, scoring 6/9 on the NOS.[11] While the two case studies[3031] were included to provide qualitative depth and descriptive clinical detail, they were excluded from the quantitative meta-analyses due to the lack of concurrent control groups and insufficient statistical power for pooling. Full methodological quality assessment data are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Primary outcome 1: Quality of upper extremity skills test

Five RCTs[2122242629] with 148 participants (75 intervention, 73 controls) provided QUEST data suitable for meta-analysis. The random-effects model (DerSimonian and Laird method) was selected a priori and is further justified by the observed heterogeneity statistics – I2 = 52% (95% CI: 0%–79%), Q = 8.32 (df = 4), P = 0.08 – which indicate moderate between-study variability according to established conventions;[19] the wide CI around I2 is expected given the small number of contributing studies (k = 5) and confirms that the point estimate should not be interpreted with undue precision, while the non-significant Q-test P value reflects the inherently low power of Cochran’s Q at small k, collectively supporting the use of the random-effects model over a fixed-effect approach. Pooled analysis yielded an SMD of 1.42 (95% CI: 0.89–1.95; Z = 5.24, P < 0.001) favoring mirror therapy, representing a large effect by Cohen’s (1988) conventions (SMD > 0.8)[18] and corresponding to approximately 18–22 points on the 100-point QUEST scale; the relatively wide CI reflects meaningful statistical uncertainty attributable to the small total sample (n = 148) and should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Sensitivity analysis confirmed robustness, with leave-one-out SMDs ranging from 1.28 to 1.56, indicating no single study disproportionately influenced the pooled estimate. Across all 12 included studies, Egger’s regression test (t = 1.84, df = 10, P = 0.096)[20] and Begg’s rank correlation test (Kendall’s τ = 0.21, P = 0.31) did not reach statistical significance, providing no strong evidence of publication bias; however, mild funnel plot asymmetry was noted, and given that both tests have low power at k = 12, the possibility of selective reporting cannot be fully excluded [Figure 2: Plotted funnel graph].[20]

Primary outcome 2: Manual dexterity (box and block test)

Six investigations (k = 6) involving 175 participants (88 intervention, 87 control) evaluated manual dexterity via the Box and Block Test,[14] representing a core primary outcome of this review. Quantitative synthesis utilizing a fixed-effect model – selected due to the low statistical heterogeneity observed (I2 = 38%; 95% CI: 0.0%–68.4%; Q = 8.06; P = 0.15) and in accordance with established categorization[19] – revealed a significant improvement in the affected hand. The pooled effect size was moderate-to-large (Cohen’s d = 0.71; 95% CI: 0.42–1.00; Z = 4.82; P < 0.001), which translates to a clinical gain of approximately 4–6 additional blocks transferred per minute over control conditions. The inclusion of the 95% CI for I2 provides a more reliable estimate of consistency given the relatively small total sample size.[19] Subsequent stratified evaluations investigating the influence of intervention combinations and cerebral palsy topography were treated as secondary subgroup analyses to further explore the drivers of this primary functional gain [Figure 3: Plotted funnel graph].

Primary outcome 3: Grip strength

Six independent investigations (k = 6) utilizing handheld dynamometry to evaluate grip strength[15] encompassed a total of 258 participants (130 intervention, 128 control). Quantitative synthesis using a random-effects model – justified by the presence of significant statistical heterogeneity (Q = 13.16; P = 0.02) – demonstrated a statistically significant but modest enhancement following mirror therapy. The pooled effect size was small-to-moderate (Cohen’s d = 0.38; 95% CI: 0.12–0.64; Z = 2.87; P = 0.004), which corresponds to a clinical improvement of approximately 1.5–2.5 kg in hand-grip force. In accordance with the classification system established by Higgins et al.(2003),[19] statistical heterogeneity was categorized as substantial (I2 = 62%; 95% CI: 28.6%–81.4%). The inclusion of the 95% CI for the I2 value is essential here to account for the potential instability of heterogeneity estimates in meta-analyses with a limited number of studies.[19] [Figure 4: Plotted funnel graph]. Detailed effect sizes for all secondary outcomes are presented in Supplementary Table 3.

Primary outcome 4: Functional independence (ABILHAND-kids)

Two high-quality RCTs (k = 2)[2125] involving 126 participants (63 intervention, 63 control) reported functional ability data via the parent-reported ABILHAND-Kids questionnaire.[16] Quantitative synthesis using a fixed-effect model – selected due to the low statistical heterogeneity observed (I2 = 18%; 95% CI: 0.0%–54.8%; Q = 1.22; P = 0.27) in accordance with Higgins et al. (2003)[19] – demonstrated significant improvements in bimanual functional capacity. The individual studies reported effect sizes of 0.82 (Kara et al.[25]: 95% CI: 0.48–1.46) and 0.97 (Al-Rashidi: 95% CI: 0.48–1.46), yielding a weighted mean effect size of $d = 0.89$ (95% CI: 0.52–1.26; $Z = 4.68$; P < 0.001$), which represents a large effect. Parents observed meaningful progress in daily activities such as dressing, eating, and hygiene, with a mean improvement from baseline reaching 16.8% (range: 14.5%–19.2%). The inclusion of the 95% CI for I2 is provided to acknowledge the instability of heterogeneity estimates with only two studies.[19] Due to this limited study count (k = 2), further subgroup and sensitivity analyses were not performed for this specific outcome [Figure 5: Plotted funnel graph].

Secondary outcomes

Analysis of secondary measures revealed diverse functional benefits, though results should be interpreted with caution due to the limited number of contributing studies for specific scales. Movement quality, as measured by the Melbourne Assessment of Unilateral Upper Limb Function (k = 3, n = 123), showed moderate improvements (Cohen’s d = 0.64; 95% CI: 0.28–1.00; P < 0.001). Heterogeneity for this outcome was categorized as moderate (I2 = 44%; 95% CI: 0.0%–78.2%) based on Higgins et al. (2003).[19] Performance speed on the Jebsen-Taylor Hand Function Test (k = 2) indicated significant time reductions for writing ($d = 0.38$; 95% CI: 0.08–0.68) and simulated feeding (d = 0.42; 95% CI: 0.12–0.72); however, these effect sizes (d) derived from only two investigations must be interpreted tentatively. Regarding developmental progress, a single RCT reported an accelerated fine motor quotient on the PDMS-2 (+12.4 points, P < 0.001). Similarly, one study noted significant gains in PedsQL physical (d = 0.68) and psychosocial (d = 0.52) domains. While these single-study findings preclude meta-analytic pooling, they are included to provide a comprehensive view of the evidence base where multiple-study data were unavailable. Finally, a random-effects pooled analysis (k = 3) of the MAS demonstrated a small, nonsignificant reduction in spasticity (d = 0.28; 95% CI: −0.04–0.60; P = 0.09), with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 52%; 95% CI: 0.0%–84.1%),[19] suggesting that while mirror therapy may influence muscle tone, current data do not support a definitive antispastic effect. Publication bias assessment

For the manual dexterity outcome (Box and Block Test, k = 6), visual inspection of the funnel plot revealed a relatively symmetrical distribution of effect sizes around the pooled estimate, indicating a low risk of publication bias. Although formal statistical testing for funnel plot asymmetry – such as Egger’s regression[20] – is traditionally optimized for meta-analyses containing at least ten studies (k = 10), it was performed here to provide a supplementary quantitative layer to the visual analysis. The results were nonsignificant (intercept = 1.38; 95% CI: −2.24–5.00; t = 0.92; P = 0.16), suggesting no statistical evidence of small-study effects. Similarly, while funnel plots for QUEST (k = 5) and grip strength (k = 6) exhibited slight visual asymmetry, formal Egger’s tests remained non-significant (P = 0.22 and P = 0.18, respectively).[20] It is explicitly acknowledged that these statistical tests possess limited power to detect bias due to the small number of included investigations; therefore, the absence of significance should be interpreted with caution and primarily as a complement to the qualitative assessment of the plot symmetry.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide comprehensive quantitative evidence supporting the effectiveness of mirror therapy for enhancing hand function in children with spastic cerebral palsy. Synthesized data from 12 investigations (n = 456) demonstrate moderate-to-large effect sizes for hand function quality (SMD = 1.42), manual dexterity (d = 0.71), and functional independence (d = 0.89), alongside a small-to-moderate impact on grip strength (d = 0.38). A rigorous assessment of publication bias across the twelve included studies – conducted via funnel plot inspection and Egger’s regression[20] – revealed a relatively symmetrical distribution, suggesting that these robust effect sizes are unlikely to be driven by small-study effects or the selective reporting of positive outcomes. Furthermore, subgroup analyses revealed that younger cohorts (5–8 years) and multimodal protocols combining mirror therapy with constraint-induced movement therapy or neurodevelopmental treatment yielded superior gains. While these subgroup findings suggest synergistic therapeutic effects, they are based on a limited number of studies (k < 10) and must be interpreted tentatively, aligning with similar pediatric neurorehabilitation meta-analyses that emphasize the preliminary nature of stratified estimates in rare clinical phenotypes.

The observed functional improvements are consistent with established principles of pediatric neuroplasticity and motor learning. Mirror therapy likely functions through several integrated neurophysiological mechanisms, most notably the activation of the mirror neuron system.[832] This visual-motor matching facilitates motor learning via action observation, reinforcing internal motor representations and strengthening neural pathways responsible for voluntary control.[33] Neuroimaging evidence suggests that mirror visual feedback enhances ipsilesional motor cortex excitability and promotes bilateral corticospinal engagement, optimizing motor planning circuits.[3435] These effects are particularly potent in the developing pediatric brain, which possesses superior synaptic density and functional reorganization capacity compared to adults.[36] Furthermore, mirror therapy may modulate maladaptive inter-hemispheric inhibition patterns characteristic of cerebral palsy, promoting a more balanced bilateral motor control and enhancing sensory-motor integration.[37] Consequently, the convergence of performance-based metrics with parent-reported functional gains suggests that mirror therapy produces meaningful clinical changes that translate into improved participation in daily life.

Evidence-based implementation parameters

The clinical implementation of mirror therapy for pediatric populations should adhere to optimized session parameters to maximize neuroplastic engagement while minimizing fatigue.[36] Evidence suggests that a session duration of 15–30 min is optimal, though younger children under 6 years of age may require shorter intervals (10–15 min) with frequent breaks to maintain attention. Extending sessions beyond 30 min has not demonstrated superior functional outcomes and may inversely affect adherence due to physical and cognitive exhaustion. Regarding frequency, near-daily practice of 5–7 days per week is recommended; however, a 5-day schedule with weekend breaks appears to yield comparable functional gains, offering a more sustainable balance for families. A minimum intervention period of 4 weeks is necessary for initial functional improvements, while extended programs of 6–12 weeks are likely to produce more durable effects, as supported by meta-regression data linking longer durations to enhanced grip strength.[15] Ultimately, activity selection must prioritize age-appropriate, play-based tasks that target the child’s specific functional goals and daily motivations to ensure high-quality motor engagement.[39] Detailed study-level protocol parameters are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Study limitations and clinical interpretation

Several methodological constraints warrant consideration when applying these findings to clinical practice. Primary limitations include the relatively small number of RCTs and modest sample sizes, which affect the precision of effect estimates. Furthermore, variability in intervention protocols, a lack of long-term follow-up data, and inherent challenges in blinding participants to mirror-based therapy introduce potential bias. The evidence is also constrained by a lack of economic evaluations and limited demographic diversity across study cohorts. While these factors suggest that the current results should be interpreted as preliminary rather than definitive, the high level of inter-study consistency and the moderate-to-large effect sizes observed across both objective and parent-reported measures provide a “probable” level of evidence for mirror therapy’s efficacy.[7] Consequently, while these findings are not yet sufficient to form a final “gold standard” guideline, they offer a strong evidence-based foundation for clinicians to integrate mirror therapy as a low-risk, high-reward adjunctive modality in pediatric neurorehabilitation, pending larger-scale validation.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide a comprehensive synthesis of the current evidence supporting mirror therapy as an efficacious intervention for enhancing hand function in children with spastic cerebral palsy. While the consistency of positive outcomes – supported by a low risk of publication bias[20] – is encouraging, the results must be viewed as a “probable” level of evidence rather than a definitive gold standard. Consequently, while mirror therapy represents a high-utility, low-risk adjunctive modality in pediatric neurorehabilitation, these findings underscore the urgent need for large-scale, multi-center RCTs to refine effect precision and establish long-term functional durability.
