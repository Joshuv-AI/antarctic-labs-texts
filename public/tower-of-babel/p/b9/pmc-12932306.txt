Extended CTA scan range for early detection of left atrial and left atrial appendage thrombus and other filling defects in acute ischemic stroke
Authors: Juan Marta-Enguita, Sara Biain, Diego Ayuso, Sergio Vasquez-Ferreccio, Cristina del Bosque, Patricia de la Riva, Noemí Diez, Felix Gonzalez, Ana de Arce, Jon Equiza, Gorka Arenaza, Virginia Gomez-Usabiaga, Maite Martinez‑Zabaleta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe left atrium (LA) and left atrial appendage (LAA) are common sites of thrombus formation leading to cardioembolic stroke. We aimed to include LA/LAA in acute-phase stroke CT angiography (CTA) to detect potential embolic sources early.MethodsThe CTA scan range was extended to include the LA/LAA. We retrospectively reviewed CTAs from patients treated at Donostia University Hospital between December 2020 and November 2021. LA/LAA images were classified as normal filling, non-well-defined filling defect, and thrombus-suggestive filling defect. Clinical, demographic, and stroke severity data were compared across groups.ResultsAmong 299 patients included, 6 (2%) had thrombus-suggestive filling defects, and 32 (11%) had non-well-defined defects. Both groups had more severe strokes (median NIHSS 16 vs. 13, p < 0.01), higher mortality (29% vs. 6%, p < 0.01), and more pre-existing heart disease (74% vs. 27%, p < 0.01) and known atrial fibrillation (AF) (63% vs. 8%, p < 0.01). AF was more frequently diagnosed during follow-up in the non-well-defined filling defect group than in the normal group (50% vs. 19%, p = 0.02). Prior AF was the only independent predictor of thrombus-suggestive filling defect, which was observed despite high rates of anticoagulation (50%).ConclusionsIncluding LA/LAA in acute CTA allows early identification of thrombus-suggestive and non-well-defined filling defects, both linked to worse outcomes and potential therapeutic consequences.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00415-026-13692-6.

Introduction

Cardioembolic stroke (CS) accounts for approximately one-third of cases of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) [1]. Moreover, in another 25% of cases, the cause of the stroke remains unknown (i.e., cryptogenic stroke), with a significant proportion of these patients exhibiting a minor cardioembolic source or occult atrial fibrillation (AF) as a potential cause [2]. In all these cases, accurate characterization of the underlying cardiac pathology enables appropriate secondary prevention and targeted diagnostic evaluation. However, the detection of cardiac abnormalities that may act as a cardioembolic source is complex and often delayed due to limited access to standardized cardiological studies (such as transthoracic echocardiography, transesophageal echocardiography, or cardiac MRI), which in most centers are performed several days after the ischemic event based on current guidelines [3], at which time a thrombus may have been already vanished. Early detection of a potential cardioembolic source in stroke cases allows for appropriate risk stratification of recurrence and correct implementation of secondary prevention measures, such as anticoagulation, to reduce the risk of recurrence.

Some of the most frequent cardioembolic sources have their pathological substrate in a process of morphological, functional, mechanical, and electrical degeneration of the left atrium (LA), known as atrial cardiomyopathy [4]. In this context, the LA and the left atrial appendage (LAA) act as important sites for a significant proportion of intracardiac thrombi [5], carrying a high embolic risk. The detection of a thrombus at this level has therapeutic implications, such as the indication for early anticoagulation or the performance of left atrial appendage closure, either as a complementary therapy or as first-line secondary prevention. The fact that 372 out of 402 (93%) stroke neurologists report that such a finding could influence their therapeutic decisions reinforces its importance [6].

In the majority of patients evaluated for acute stroke, CT angiography (CTA) is systematically performed as part of the standard protocol, in line with current guidelines [3]. This diagnostic tool is usually performed with a scanning window extending from the tracheal carina to cranial vertex, including the upper portion of the aortic arch and the supra-aortic trunks. Some studies have proposed enlarging this window to include cardiac structures within the scanning range to allow for an early, preliminary assessment of the cardiac anatomy. This extension has ranged from the inclusion of a few centimeters below the carina to capture the aortic arch and the LA/LAA, to expansion down to the diaphragm to cover all cardiac chambers. Depending on the extent of cardiac coverage, this approach has been shown to identify between 6 and 13% of potential cardioembolic sources, predominantly intracardiac thrombi [7–10]. Another explored approach has been the use of ECG-gated cardiac CT during the hyperacute evaluation of ischemic stroke, that could improve image quality and reduce artifacts, which increases the detection of potential cardioembolic sources to over 12% [11–13]. Similarly, the CACTUSES study showed that performing a delayed ECG-gated cardiac CT as part of the workup in patients with embolic stroke of undetermined source (ESUS) was able to detect intracardiac thrombi in up to 17% of this population [14].

In our study, we aimed to demonstrate the feasibility of extending the acquisition window of urgent CTA in routine clinical practice to enable early, systematic assessment of the LA and LAA in order to characterize the spectrum of filling defects detected, including thrombus-suggestive filling defects, and to explore their potential prognostic and clinical implications in the acute stroke setting as a standard complement to increase the early detection rate of cardioembolic sources of stroke.

Methods

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Gipuzkoa health area and includes a waiver of informed patient consent in accordance with current local legislation.

Study design

This is a single-center, observational study conducted between December 2020 and November 2021 at Donostia University Hospital, Gipuzkoa, Spain. All patients with suspected stroke or TIA evaluated under the Code Stroke protocol during this period at this center were included. Within this protocol, a modification was implemented in the imaging protocol by extending the scan range 4 cm below the carina to include LA and LAA in the CTA. Patients whose imaging protocol did not include CTA acquisition (mostly those with intracranial hemorrhage or stroke mimic diagnoses) were excluded from subsequent analysis and image review.

CT scans were performed in a 128-section scanner (Revolution EVO, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI) and post-processed in a GE AWServer 3.2 with Perfusion 4D and FastStroke (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI) software. The CTA study consisted of a first arterial phase from 4 cm below the carina to cranial vertex (section thickness 0.625 mm, 100 kVp, AutomA Max 480, Noise Index 10, pitch 0.984:1) that was timed to scan during the peak arterial phase and was triggered by bolus monitoring. The remaining two phases (section thickness 0.625 mm, 100 kVp, AutomA Max 320, Noise Index 20, pitch 0.984:1) were from the skull base to the vertex after a delay of 5 s respectively. A total of 50 ml of iodinated contrast material (Iopamiro 370, Bracco, Milan, Italy) was injected at a rate of 4 ml/s and followed by 30 ml of saline solution. The “dynamic bolus tracking” (SmartPrep) technique was used to optimize image acquisition timing to coincide with the contrast peak of arrival.

LA/LAA filling defects assessment

A retrospective, thorough review of the acquired images was conducted by a trained radiologist, blinded to the initial acute-phase CTA interpretation. A filling defect was defined as any area of low attenuation within the LA/LAA that did not correspond to normal cardiac structures (e.g., atrial wall, trabeculae, or pectinate muscles). A region-of-interest (ROI) tool was used to measure Hounsfield unit values within the area of reduced attenuation. The studies were classified according to the previous work by Senadeera et al. [7] into three categories: normal filling of the LA/LAA; non-well-defined filling defect, defined by blurred margins and attenuation values > 100 Hounsfield units; and filling defect highly suggestive of thrombus, characterized by well-defined borders and attenuation of less than 100 Hounsfield units (Supplemental Fig. 1). Images showing a non-well-defined filling defect were then independently reviewed by a cardiologist specialized in cardiac imaging.

Patient’s characteristics

The main clinical and demographic variables were collected retrospectively, including age, sex, cerebrovascular risk factors, and prior use of anticoagulants or antiplatelet agents. A history of pre-existing heart disease was recorded, including any type of previously diagnosed cardiomyopathy at the time of the index event (hypertensive, valvular, ischemic, dilated, or hypertrophic). Pre-existing AF was defined as AF known at the time of the event, while newly diagnosed AF was defined as AF detected during hospitalization or within the subsequent year through routine clinical practice, at the discretion of the treating neurologist, including continuous ECG monitoring during admission, periodic ECGs, and prolonged ambulatory monitoring with portable Holter devices (14–21 days). All patients underwent a standard etiologic workup according to international guidelines, including systematic cardiac imaging with transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and, when indicated, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), cardiac CT, or cardiac MRI, at the discretion of a cardiologist specialized in stroke care.

Main clinical outcomes were defined using the modified rankin scale (mRS) score at 90 days, assessed through face-to-face interviews conducted by a neurologist specialized in stroke. The primary clinical outcomes reported were 3-month all-cause mortality, 3-month functional dependence (defined as a 90-day mRS score > 2), and hemorrhagic transformation following AIS.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with normal distributions were presented as mean with standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed variables were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). Normality of distributions was assessed graphically and with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were compared between groups using the unpaired t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test depending on their distribution. Comparisons between binary categorical variables were performed using the Chi-square test or, in the case of small-expected frequencies, Fisher’s exact test.

Association of baseline characteristics with cardiac thrombus-suggestive filling defect was assessed first with univariate logistic regression models. Multivariate logistic regression models were constructed including variables with p < 0.10 in the univariate analyses as potential confounders, with age entered forcibly. Some variables were excluded from the analysis if there was a risk of collinearity or if two variables reflected redundant information. Results were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). For all analyses, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed with the STATA software (version 16, StataCorp LLC, Texas, USA).

Results

A total of 733 consecutive code stroke patients were assessed in the study. Of these, 260 (36%) were excluded because the imaging protocol did not include CTA (mainly intracranial hemorrhages and tumors). Of the 473 patients who underwent CTA, 126 (27%) were excluded due to inadequate assessment of the LA/LAA (poor image quality, motion artifacts, or incomplete coverage), and 347 (73%) underwent a complete CTA including the LA/LAA and were finally included (Fig. 1). Among patients with evaluable CTA that included the LA/LAA, 48 patients (14%) received a diagnosis other than AIS after a complete evaluation. Finally, 299 patients with reviewed CTA that included the LA/LAA and who were diagnosed with AIS were analyzed. The thrombus finding was confirmed by additional imaging in 3 cases, two with transesophageal echocardiography and one with delayed ECG-gated cardiac CT. No additional tests were performed in one case due to clinical severity and early death, and in two other cases because they were unlikely to alter therapeutic management (marked recovery after revascularization therapy and early anticoagulation).

Patients with thrombus-suggestive filling defects in the LA/LAA had more severe strokes and higher mortality. These patients more frequently had a history of heart disease and AF, and a higher percentage were anticoagulated compared to those without thrombus (Table 1). As mentioned, the presence of thrombus-suggestive filling defects in the LA/LAA was more frequent in patients with known AF (3/44 vs. 3/255). In line with this, in the multivariate model, prior AF was the only independent predictor of thrombus-suggestive filling defect presence in the LA/LAA [OR (95% CI): 11.6 (2.0–66.5)]. It is worth noting that a high percentage of patients with history of heart disease (23/32, 72%), prior AF (20/32, 63%) and anticoagulation therapy (15/32, 47%) was also found in the non-well-defined filling defect group. Moreover, strokes in this patient group exhibited a clinical profile similar to that of patients with LA/LAA thrombus, with greater severity and higher associated mortality (Table 1).

On addition, although only one case of AF was detected during hospitalization in the LA/LAA thrombus-suggestive filling defect group (1/3 patients at risk, 33%), AF was detected during the follow-up in a significantly higher proportion in the non-well-defined filling defect group (6/12 patients at risk, 50%; p = 0.02). In this regard, it should be noted that the high mortality observed in both the non-well-defined filling defect group and the thrombus-suggestive filling defect group may have influenced recurrence and AF detection rates by reducing the effective time at risk for these outcomes.

In the thrombus-suggestive filling defect group, all three patients with pre-existing AF were already receiving anticoagulation. The imaging findings led to early initiation of anticoagulation in 4/6 patients within the first 48 h (two treated with DOACs, one with early reintroduction of acenocoumarol, and one with low-molecular-weight heparin). In the remaining two patients, anticoagulation was not initiated due to clinical deterioration with early death in one case and a high risk of hemorrhagic transformation in the other.

Discussion

Our study shows that a slight modification of the imaging protocol in the hyperacute management of AIS can help identify a subgroup of patients with filling defects suggestive of thrombus in the LA and LAA, particularly in those with known AF or pre-existing cardiac disease, thereby guiding therapeutic decision-making. In addition, another filling defects that could be related to slow flow in the LA/LAA are detected which may indicate undiagnosed AF and an increased cardioembolic risk, with potential implications for AIS patient management.

In recent years, growing evidence supports the ability to enhance the detection of cardioembolic sources in AIS patients through minor adjustments to the standard imaging protocol routinely used in the acute phase. Various observational studies have explored the possibility of identifying cardiac thrombi through different modifications ranging from extending the CTA acquisition window to include cardiac structures [7–10], to performing an additional ECG-gated cardiac CT in the acute phase [11–13, 15]. These strategies have shown not only the capacity to detect cardiac thrombi but also to identify other high-risk embolic conditions with clinical relevance, such as pulmonary embolism or complicated aortic arch atheroma [8–10, 12, 13]. In our study, we evaluated thrombus detection in the main cardiac thrombus reservoir (LA/LAA) using the simplest possible modification, aiming for early pre-fibrinolysis detection of thrombi, that might dissolve spontaneously or due to the effect of fibrinolytic therapy within a few hours [16].

In our study, beyond demonstrating the potential of routine CTA to identify thrombus in the LA/LAA in AIS patients, we also focus on the subgroup showing non-well-defined filling defects, characterized by blurred margins and greater variability in attenuation and Hounsfield units. This pattern was initially considered a possible technical limitation, as full LA contrast filling is not expected with protocolized acquisition times in a proportion of patients. However, this subgroup showed a high prevalence of pre-existing heart disease and previously known AF with more severe strokes and higher mortality, closely resembling the profile of patients with definite thrombus in our cohort. In addition, a higher proportion of newly diagnosed AF after stroke was observed in this group. This suggests that these findings may reflect slow-flow in the LA/LAA or less organized thrombi, rather than technical artifacts. Thus, beyond confirming the ability of routine CTA to detect definite LA/LAA thrombus in AIS patients, the principal novelty of our study lies in the systematic characterization and clinical validation of this subgroup of patients with non-well-defined LA/LAA filling defects. Although further confirmatory studies are needed, these results suggest that extending the acquisition window in acute CTA may also help identify patients with LA/LAA slow-flow and potential cardioembolic risk, with clinical implications similar to those associated with definite thrombus detection.

To date, only one prior study in AIS patients has evaluated the implications of such findings, using a more complex approach based on an ECG-gated cardiac CT performed during the acute phase. This post hoc analysis of the Mind the Heart Study [17] compared patients with findings suggestive of slow-flow in the LA to those with definite thrombus and reported similar results. Patients with slow-flow features had a higher prevalence of AF at diagnosis and a higher rate of AF detection during the 2-year follow-up, compared to those with normal filling, and similar to the group with definite thrombus [17], replicating the findings of our study.

Early detection of cardioembolic sources is important, as it is associated with higher stroke severity [10–12] and increased mortality [11, 18], as observed in our population. In addition, identification of a thrombus in the LA/LAA could lead to a change in therapeutic approach, even in patients with atrial fibrillation. In our cohort, this finding led to the initiation of early anticoagulation after AIS in 4 out of 6 patients with a thrombus in the LA/LAA on CTA, similar to observations by other groups [8, 11]. Furthermore, the presence of a thrombus in the LA/LAA may influence other management decisions, such as consideration of LAA closure, closer monitoring during the acute phase, and anticoagulation adjustment due to potential undertreatment. However, these potential benefits remain unproven, and further studies are needed to evaluate them.

The main drawbacks of performing additional radiological tests or extending imaging protocols in the acute phase of stroke are the increased radiation exposure and the potential delay in management of this time-sensitive condition. Studies evaluating extended CTA acquisition windows have reported an additional radiation dose of approximately 1 mSv [7, 8] (for 3–6 cm below the carina) or 2 mSv [10] (down to the diaphragm). This corresponds to the equivalent of 10–20 chest X-rays (0.1 mSv each) or about 200 h of commercial flight. Although this increase in radiation should not be disregarded, the potential benefits of early detection of a cardioembolic source in AIS patients are expected to outweigh the associated risks. Moreover, this approach has not been associated with significant increases in imaging time or delays in AIS management, avoiding potentially harmful treatment delays [7, 8].

Several observational studies in recent years have explored alternative imaging strategies aimed at increasing diagnostic yield and improving image quality including cardiac assessment in the acute phase. Some of these approaches allow for left atrial measurements and detailed analysis of LAA morphology [11], but they involve additional diagnostic efforts, increased resource use, and higher radiation exposure [9]. A notable example is the study by Rinkel et al. [12], which evaluated the benefit of adding an ECG-gated cardiac CT to the acute stroke imaging protocol. This strategy identified high-risk cardioembolic sources in 11.4% of patients, compared to 4.9% with transthoracic echocardiogram, but it entailed longer acquisition times (6 min) and an estimated additional radiation dose of 2 mSv [12]. ECG-gated acquisition improves image quality and reduces motion artifacts, allowing superior visualization of cardiac chambers compared with non-ECG-gated cardiac CT. This approach has also enabled other authors to assess its ability to measure left atrial volume, quantify epicardial fat attenuation, and characterize LAA morphology, exploring their association with atrial fibrillation detection during follow-up [15]. Randomized comparative studies of these techniques would be particularly valuable to better define their respective roles in routine clinical practice.

This study has some limitations that should be considered. First, one of the main limitations of this study is that CTA was performed during the arterial phase using ungated acquisitions without delayed imaging, making the assessment susceptible to cardiac motion artifacts and reducing the specificity of the findings. In addition, among the excluded patients, a proportion was excluded due to variability in adherence to the protocol by a frequently changing emergency radiology team, resulting in inadequate inclusion of the LA/LAA. This proportion is higher than that reported in other studies and could represent a selection bias. On the other hand, the modest sample size and the retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data are important methodological shortcomings. In addition, being a single-center observational study limits the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, the study did not include a systematic confirmation strategy for thrombus or slow-flow detection and only isolated confirmation with transthoracic echocardiogram, cardiac MRI or CT, or transesophageal echocardiogram was performed at the discretion of a stroke specialized cardiologist. Finally, the impact of LA/LAA thrombus detection on acute management (altered thrombolysis decision, deferred thrombectomy, or immediate initiation of anticoagulation) was not directly evaluated, as management decisions were based on the initial CTA interpretation rather than the retrospective review; therefore, the potential effect on this outcome could not be determined.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates that extending the CTA scan range in the acute imaging protocol for AIS patients to include the LA/LAA allows early identification of filling defects highly suggestive of cardiac thrombus and slow-flow conditions associated with poor prognosis. This approach may enhance the early diagnostic workup of AIS patients and have potential therapeutic implications.
