Effects of intensive lifestyle modification incorporating calorie-carbohydrate restriction with or without time-restricted feeding on eating disorder psychopathology and diabetes-related distress in type 2 diabetes: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Saba Mohammadpour, Farzad Hadaegh, Farid Zayeri, Somayeh Hosseinpour-Niazi, Negin Badrooj, Sakineh Shab-bidar, Parvin Mirmiran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study evaluated the effects of intensive lifestyle modification (ILM) incorporating calorie-carbohydrate restriction (CCR) with or without time-restricted feeding (IFCCR) on psychological health in adults with type 2 diabetes (T2D).Research design and methodsIn a 24-week randomized controlled trial, 120 adults with T2D were allocated to CCR (n = 40), IFCCR (n = 40), or a control group (n = 40). Both intervention arms followed an energy-restricted diet (1000–1200 kcal/day), received behavioral support, and performed ≥ 150 min/week of physical activity. The IFCCR group additionally restricted eating to an 8-hour daily window. Primary and secondary outcomes were eating disorder psychopathology (EDE-Q scale) and diabetes-related distress (PAID scale), respectively.ResultsBoth CCR and IFCCR significantly reduced diabetes-related distress compared to the control (CCR: β = -1.80, p = 0.007; IFCCR: β = -1.78, p = 0.01). EDE-Q scores also improved significantly. Improvements were observed across EDE-Q subscales, particularly in weight and shape concern. After adjustment for changes in weight and HbA1c, reductions in PAID became non-significant, while improvements in several EDE-Q domains, particularly in the IFCCR group, remained significant. IFCCR showed numerically greater reductions than CCR, though differences were not statistically significant. The control group demonstrated minimal changes.ConclusionsIntensive lifestyle modification incorporating CCR or IFCCR improved eating disorder psychopathology, whereas reductions in diabetes-related distress appear largely related to metabolic changes. These findings support the integration of structured dietary and behavioral interventions into diabetes care.Trial registrationThis trial was registered in Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT), IRCT20230917059447N1 (https//irct.behdasht.gov.ir/trial/72666).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-026-02107-1.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is one of the most prevalent chronic diseases worldwide, with rapidly increasing incidence and substantial impacts on both physical and psychological health [1]. Beyond the well-established risks of cardiovascular disease, nephropathy, and neuropathy, individuals with T2D frequently experience significant psychological burdens, including diabetes-related distress, depressive symptoms, and eating-related concerns. These psychological comorbidities not only reduce quality of life but also interfere with treatment adherence and glycemic control, emphasizing the need for integrated therapeutic approaches [2].

Lifestyle modification remains a cornerstone of T2D management. Intensive lifestyle interventions (ILIs) have demonstrated beneficial effects not only on weight reduction and metabolic regulation but also on mental well-being [3, 4]. Findings from large-scale clinical trials, such as Look AHEAD, revealed that structured lifestyle programs can improve depressive symptoms and overall quality of life in individuals with T2D [4]. Moreover, regular physical activity has been consistently associated with enhanced mood, reduced diabetes distress, and improved self-efficacy, underlining the strong bidirectional link between health behaviors and psychological outcomes [5, 6]. Dietary strategies represent a critical element of lifestyle management. Among them, low-carbohydrate diets have been extensively investigated and shown to improve glycemic control, insulin sensitivity, and weight management in patients with T2D, with some evidence suggesting favorable effects on mood and treatment satisfaction as well [7]. More recently, time-restricted feeding (TRF) has gained attention as a novel dietary pattern. By aligning food intake with circadian rhythms, TRF has demonstrated metabolic benefits and potential improvements in psychological health, including mood and cognitive function, through mechanisms related to circadian alignment and neuroplasticity [8–10]. However, empirical evidence examining the effects of TRF on psychological health in T2D remains scarce. In addition to dietary modification and physical activity, behavioral education and structured self-management support are essential components of lifestyle interventions. Such approaches have been shown to enhance treatment adherence, reduce diabetes-related distress, and improve coping strategies, thereby reinforcing both metabolic and psychological benefits [11].

Psychological health in T2D is often evaluated using validated tools. The Problem Areas in Diabetes (PAID) questionnaire captures diabetes-specific emotional distress and has been linked to treatment adherence and glycemic outcomes [12]. Meanwhile, the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q) assesses disordered eating attitudes and behaviors across four domains, including restraint, eating concern, shape concern, and weight concern, factors known to significantly affect both psychological well-being and diabetes self-management [13]. These instruments provide valuable insights into the broader psychosocial impact of dietary and behavioral interventions in T2D.

Given the gaps in current evidence, we conducted a randomized controlled trial to compare the effects of an intensive lifestyle intervention incorporating a calorie-carbohydrate-restricted diet with or without time-restricted feeding on psychological health in adults with T2D.

Methods

Trial design and participants

This 24-week randomized, parallel, controlled clinical trial was conducted at Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences after obtaining ethical approval and registration in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20230917059447N1). The study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided written informed consent. Between June 2024 and April 2025, 120 adults with T2D were recruited from endocrinology clinics in Tehran. Eligible participants were men and women aged 18–60 years, with a body mass index of 27–40 kg/m², diabetes duration less than 5 years, HbA1c above 6.1%, and using at least one glucose-lowering medication. Before enrollment, participants received full information regarding study procedures, potential risks, and expected benefits, after which written informed consent was obtained. After a two-week run-in to establish baseline dietary habits, participants were randomized into three groups: CCR (n = 40), IFCCR (n = 40), and control (n = 40). This randomized controlled trial was designed, conducted, and reported in accordance with the CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) guidelines [14].

Randomization

Randomization was performed using Rand software with a stratified (gender) permuted block randomization method to ensure balanced distribution of males and females across the study groups. Within each gender stratum, participants were randomly assigned in blocks of six using a centralized, secure online system (https://www.sealedenvelope.com/simple-randomiser/v1/lists). The randomization sequence was generated and managed by an independent researcher who had no involvement in participant recruitment or intervention delivery. Allocation was concealed from all outcome assessors and the study statistician responsible for data analysis throughout the study. Due to the nature of the lifestyle interventions, participants and intervention providers were aware of group assignments; however, all psychological and metabolic assessments were conducted by personnel blinded to group allocation. Additionally, self-administered questionnaires (PAID and EDE-Q) minimized potential assessor influence on subjective outcomes.

Intervention and procedures

The intervention included two consecutive stages: an intensive lifestyle intervention (ILI) during weeks 0–12, followed by a maintenance and supportive monitoring (MSM) phase during weeks 12–24.

During the ILI phase, participants in the CCR group consumed an energy-restricted diet (1000–1200 kcal/day; 40% carbohydrate, 30% protein, 30% fat) without restrictions on meal timing. The IFCCR group followed the same diet composition but restricted eating to an 8-hour daily window (10:00–18:00) followed by 16 h of fasting. All participants were advised to limit refined carbohydrates, replace saturated fats with healthier alternatives, maintain hydration of at least 2 L per day, and use fiber supplements if needed. Physical activity recommendations included ≥ 150 min per week of moderate-intensity exercise combining aerobic and resistance activities, with gradual increases in daily steps to a target of 10,000. Behavioral support was provided through a structured diabetes self-management program based on the Information-Motivation-Behavioral Skills (IMB) model, integrating educational sessions, motivational strategies, and skill-building through in-person, telephone, and online sessions over 12 weeks [15]. Participants attended a 60–90-minute group session at the start of the ILI phase, followed by five individual biweekly consultations with the same dietitian (30–45 min each). Weekly body weight and twice-weekly self-monitored blood glucose values were reported via telephone or secure messaging platforms.

In the MSM phase, individualized dietary adjustments emphasized weight maintenance and prevention of relapse through a food-based diet rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and low-fat dairy, while limiting sweets, red meat, and refined grains. Physical activity targets were maintained. Monthly group sessions, conducted in-person, focused on sustaining lifestyle changes, enhancing self-efficacy, and supporting body image. Participants continued to submit weekly weight and twice-weekly glucose measurements. Throughout the study, the endocrinologist adjusted and recorded the dose of antidiabetic medications during visits based on participants’ blood glucose levels (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Adherence to the intervention

Adherence to the dietary protocols was promoted through an intensive behavioral support framework combined with regular self-monitoring. To enhance compliance, the study incorporated multiple reinforcing strategies. Initially, participants underwent a two-week run-in period to evaluate their motivation and capacity to meet study requirements. Throughout the 3-month intervention, adherence was supported using a high-contact approach, consisting of one group session and five individualized, in-person counseling sessions led by nutritionists. In addition, participants participated in ongoing remote monitoring by submitting weekly weight reports and blood glucose measurements three times per week. For participants in the IFCCR group, these scheduled check-ins also served as structured opportunities for dietitians to verbally verify adherence to the prescribed 8-hour eating window and to address any challenges in real time. Non-compliance was defined as missing more than two consecutive sessions or failing to follow dietary prescriptions three times per week for at least two consecutive weeks.

Measurements and outcomes

Outcomes were assessed at baseline, 12 weeks, and 24 weeks. Demographic and clinical data, medical history, smoking status, medication, and supplement use were collected via standardized questionnaires. Blood pressure was measured twice in a seated position, 10 min apart, and the mean value was recorded. Dietary intake was evaluated using three-day food records (two weekdays and one weekend day), analyzed with Nutritionist 4 software adapted for Iranian foods. Physical activity was measured using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) and expressed as MET-hours per week [16]. Anthropometric measurements, including height, weight, waist circumference, and BMI, were obtained by the same examiner. Bioelectrical impedance analysis (TANITA BC-418) was employed to evaluate body composition parameters such as fat mass and fat-free mass. Venous blood samples were collected after 8–10 h of fasting to assess HbA1c and fasting glucose, centrifuged, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Eating disorder

Eating disorder examination questionnaire (EDE-Q)

A well-known, validated self-report tool for evaluating Eating disorder psychopathology is the EDE-Q [17]. The Eating Disorder Examination interview, which is regarded as the gold standard test of Eating disorder psychopathology, has demonstrated a strong correlation with its overall score.

The purpose of the 28-item EDE-Q is to assess the participant’s psychopathology related to eating disorders throughout the last 28 days. It has four subscales: restraint, eating concern, shape concern, and weight concern, and the global score is the sum of these. Greater psychopathology associated with eating disorders is indicated by a higher global score [17–19].

Problem areas in diabetes (PAID)

Psychological distress related to diabetes is commonly assessed using the Problem Areas in Diabetes (PAID) questionnaire. This is a validated 20-item self-report instrument designed to measure emotional adjustment to diabetes. Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“no problem”) to 4 (“a serious problem”). The individual item scores are summed and multiplied by 1.25, resulting in a total score between 0 and 100, where higher scores reflect greater diabetes-related emotional distress. A score of 40 or higher suggests possible emotional burnout and indicates that the patient may require special attention. Conversely, an extremely low score (0–10) in combination with poor glycemic control may indicate denial of the illness [20].

Statistical analysis

Sample size estimation was performed a priori using the standard formula n = 2(zα + zβ)2 s2/d2. Based on a standard deviation of 0.97 derived from previous work on the discriminative validity of the EDE-Q [21], with a two-tailed α of 0.05 and 95% power (1–β = 0.95), the required sample size was calculated to be 44 participants (22 per group). After accounting for an anticipated 25% attrition, the adjusted sample size was 56 participants (28 per group). To enhance statistical power and compensate for potential variability, we enrolled 120 participants in total (40 per group). Analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle, including all randomized participants. Baseline characteristics were compared across groups using one-way ANOVA or χ² tests, as appropriate. Changes in outcomes over time were evaluated using generalized estimating equations (GEE) with an exchangeable correlation structure to account for repeated measures. Time × group interaction terms were tested to assess differential intervention effects. Effect sizes were calculated as regression coefficients (β) with standard errors (SE). The intention-to-treat (ITT) principles were used for all analyses. Missing data were imputed using Multiple Imputation, Chained Equations (MICD) procedure, and ANOVA analyses were conducted on the imputed datasets. Longitudinal outcomes were analyzed using GEE, which provides unbiased estimates in the presence of missing data under the assumption of Missing at Random (MAR). No prior imputation was performed for these analyses. Sensitivity analyses using per-protocol populations yielded similar results. Statistical significance was defined as two-tailed p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Of the 1075 individuals assessed for eligibility, 928 were excluded, mainly because they did not meet the inclusion criteria. From the 147 individuals invited to participate, 27 declined, and the remaining 120 entered a two-week run-in phase and were subsequently randomized to either CCR (n = 40), IFCCR (n = 40), or control (n = 40). Follow-up was completed by 114 participants (CCR: n = 39, IFCCR: n = 39, control: n = 37), with attrition primarily due to relocation or inability to attend visits. All randomized participants were included in the intention-to-treat analysis (Fig. 1). Participants in the CCR and IFCCR groups demonstrated high compliance, with mean adherence rates of 84.5% and 86.2%, respectively, over the 24-week intervention. Physical activity adherence was monitored via the IPAQ and weekly activity logs. Mean compliance with the recommended ≥ 150 min/week of moderate-intensity exercise was 82.3% in the CCR group and 83.7% in the IFCCR group.

Over the 24-week intervention, substantial improvements were observed in measures of eating disorder psychopathology and diabetes-related distress in both intervention groups compared with the control group (Table 2). Global EDE-Q scores declined progressively from baseline in CCR (2.48 ± 1.03 to 1.76 ± 1.11) and IFCCR (2.52 ± 0.75 to 1.52 ± 0.95), whereas no substantial change occurred in the control group (2.19 ± 1.07 to 2.48 ± 1.40). Reductions were consistent across all subscales of EDE-Q, including restraint, eating concern, weight concern, and shape concern, with the largest relative improvements observed in the IFCCR group. Similarly, mean PAID scores decreased markedly in CCR (45.2 ± 22.1 to 29.8 ± 19.6) and IFCCR (40.6 ± 21.0 to 26.8 ± 15.7), compared with only a modest decline in the control group (41.2 ± 18.5 to 36.1 ± 18.5).

Longitudinal analyses using generalized estimating equations confirmed significant time-by-group interactions for both EDE-Q and PAID outcomes (Table 3; Fig. 2). Compared with control, the CCR group was associated with a reduction in PAID (β=−1.80, SE = 0.67, p = 0.007), with a similar effect for the IFCCR group (β=−1.78, SE = 0.69, p = 0.01). Improvements were evident across all EDE-Q dimensions, with both intervention groups showing significant downward trends compared with the control. Restraint scores decreased in both CCR (β=−0.07, 0.01) and IFCCR (β=−0.14, p < 0.001), with the latter showing a steeper decline over time, while the control group remained stable. Eating concern followed a similar pattern, with moderate reductions in CCR (p = 0.005) and a larger effect in IFCCR (p < 0.001). The most pronounced changes were observed in weight concern and shape concern. Both CCR and IFCCR participants demonstrated marked improvements in weight concern (β=−0.28 vs. −0.29) and shape concern (β=−0.18 vs. −0.24), with effect sizes largely comparable across groups (both p < 0.001). By contrast, the control group exhibited little to no improvement and in some cases slight worsening across domains. Collectively, these findings demonstrate consistent psychological benefits of both interventions, with IFCCR showing the sharpest downward trajectories. Although both intervention strategies were highly effective, the IFCCR group consistently demonstrated numerically greater reductions in psychopathology and distress, though differences between CCR and IFCCR did not reach statistical significance(p > 0.05). After adjustment for weight change, the pattern of psychological outcomes differed between intervention groups. In the CCR group, the improvement remained statistically significant only for the weight concern subscale, whereas the effects on EDE-Q score, eating concern, shape concern, restraint, and PAID were attenuated and no longer significant. In contrast, in the IFCCR group, improvements in EDE-Q score, eating concern, weight concern, and shape concern remained statistically significant after adjustment for weight change, while the effects on restraint and PAID were no longer significant. In addition, after adjusting for HbA1c, only PAID lost significance in the IFCCR group, while all EDE-Q domains remained significant. In the CCR group, PAID, restraint, and eating concern became non-significant, whereas other EDE-Q subscales remained significant.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial showed that intensive lifestyle interventions were associated with improvements in psychological outcomes in adults with T2D. After adjustment for both changes in weight and HbA1c, several eating-related psychopathology outcomes remained statistically significant, particularly in the IFCCR group. These findings suggest that certain improvements in eating-related psychopathology may occur partly independent of both weight loss and glycemic improvement, especially with time-restricted feeding. In contrast, improvements in PAID were no longer statistically significant after adjustment for changes in body weight or HbA1c, indicating that reductions in diabetes-related distress were largely mediated by overall metabolic improvement associated with the lifestyle intervention.

Our findings suggest that a substantial proportion of the psychological benefits observed in the CCR group were mediated by weight loss and HbA1c improvements. In contrast, the IFCCR intervention appeared to confer additional benefits on eating-related psychopathology that were partially independent of metabolic improvement, particularly in domains related to eating concern, body shape, and weight concern. The persistence of these effects may reflect mechanisms specific to time-restricted feeding, such as improved perceived control over eating, reduced cognitive preoccupation with food, and better alignment with circadian eating patterns [8, 22, 23].

These findings align with the ARIADNE trial, which demonstrated that a low-energy total diet replacement program with behavioural support did not exacerbate eating disorder symptoms in at-risk individuals and even produced modest improvements in EDE-Q scores, affirming both safety and psychological benefit in a high-risk population of T2D. Importantly, ARIADNE also reported notable reductions in PAID scores, underscoring gains in diabetes-related quality of life paralleling our initial observation of substantial improvements in PAID scores, which appeared to be largely mediated by weight loss and glycemic improvements [24].

Complementing these results, a meta-analysis confirmed that tailored psychological interventions significantly reduce diabetes distress and HbA₁c levels, reinforcing the importance of mental health–focused care [25]. Kakoschke et al. found that carbohydrate-restricted and high‐carbohydrate diets, when paired with exercise, produced comparable long‐term improvements in PAID, mood, and quality of life, suggesting that diet composition may be less critical than holistic lifestyle modification [26]. While the evidence remains mixed, previous studies suggest that dietary composition may influence metabolic outcomes beyond mere caloric restriction. The independent effects of low-carbohydrate diets on glycemic control, insulin sensitivity, and satiety-related hormones represent plausible mechanisms through which eating-related distress may be indirectly ameliorated. This raises the possibility that not only the energy deficit, but also macronutrient composition contributes to psychological and metabolic outcomes. Nonetheless, discrepancies across studies highlight the need for further trials to disentangle the relative contributions of weight loss versus dietary composition to psychological outcomes in T2D. Supporting this, a study comparing low- versus high-fat diets reported greater improvements in health-related quality of life (physical function, vitality) despite similar weight loss, suggesting potential macronutrient-specific benefits [27].

Mechanistically, intermittent fasting may act through enhanced metabolic flexibility, circadian alignment, and neurotrophic signaling, benefiting mood, self-regulation, and stress resilience, a concept supported by emerging literature on neuroplasticity gains associated with fasting, caloric restriction, and exercise [28, 29]. Simultaneously, low-carbohydrate regimens may mimic these effects via ketogenesis, decreased glycemic variability, and appetite modulation via hormonal pathways (e.g., ghrelin, GLP-1), mechanisms intricately linked to mood and eating behavior beyond weight loss alone. However, the extent to which these mechanisms operate independently of weight loss likely varies across psychological domains [30, 31].

Exercise significantly augments the psychological benefits of dietary interventions. It improves body image, self-regulation, and emotional resilience, which are key to reducing diabetes-related distress. In overweight adults, physical activity fosters flexible eating regulation primarily by improving body image [32, 33]. It also boosts self-efficacy and self-esteem, correlates of better mental health [34], with aerobic training shown to enhance self-esteem in TD2 likely contributing to reductions in PAID scores, particularly in the context of weight reduction [35]. However, exercise must be applied carefully, as excessive or compulsive patterns can increase distress or lead to energy deficits. Therefore, regimens should be tailored, supported behaviorally, and monitored to safeguard psychological well-being [36, 37].

Structured behavioral support is a critical determinant of therapeutic success in T2D management beyond dietary changes. Meta-analytic evidence shows cognitive-behavioral therapy yields modest short-term improvements in glycemic control and psychological outcomes, though long-term effects on diabetes distress are mixed [38]. The IMB model used in this study provides a validated framework for this support. It posits that knowledge, reduced fatalistic beliefs, and social support improve self-care behaviors, leading to better glycemic outcomes [39]. Longitudinally, motivation and behavioral skills have a bidirectional relationship with diet and exercise adherence [40]. By embedding the intervention within the IMB framework, the study likely amplified engagement, reinforced self-efficacy, and sustained behavioral change, thereby contributing to the observed psychological and metabolic gains, particularly by enhancing adherence and supporting sustained weight loss.

Despite these promising outcomes, the absence of large differential effects between CCR and IFCCR suggests that overall weight reduction and structured behavioral support were likely the dominant drivers of psychological improvement, as supported by weight-adjusted analyses. Nevertheless, this does not imply that dietary composition is irrelevant. Rather, specific approaches may exert more subtle influences by enhancing perceived control over eating, aligning with circadian or lifestyle preferences, and providing greater flexibility in food choices, all of which can contribute to improved emotional well-being [41]. Importantly, individual adherence patterns, psychological traits, and lifestyle compatibility may moderate these effects, which could explain why group-level differences were less pronounced in this study. These findings are consistent with recent evidence indicating that while both energy deficit and diet composition can affect psychological outcomes, the most durable benefits are realized when interventions are embedded within robust behavioral frameworks that reinforce self-efficacy and long-term engagement [42].

An important consideration in interpreting these findings is the multifactorial nature of the intensive lifestyle modification intervention. Although the control group received comparable follow-up and contact, structured IMB-based educational and behavioral sessions were delivered only to the CCR and IFCCR groups and may have independently contributed to psychological improvements. Therefore, the observed benefits should be interpreted as the combined effects of dietary modification, physical activity, and structured behavioral support rather than dietary factors alone. Nonetheless, this integrated approach reflects real-world clinical practice and supports the effectiveness of comprehensive lifestyle interventions in improving psychological outcomes in adults with T2D.

The main strengths of our study include the rigorous randomized controlled design, with randomization performed successfully and baseline characteristics well balanced across groups, thereby ensuring strong internal validity. The parallel comparison of CCR and IFCCR arms provided a unique opportunity to evaluate two dietary strategies of growing clinical interest within the same framework. Another important strength was the integration of the IMB framework, which embedded structured behavioral support alongside dietary guidance. This approach likely enhanced adherence, reinforced self-efficacy, and amplified psychological and metabolic gains. The 24-week follow-up, though not long-term, offered meaningful insight into medium-term outcomes, while the use of validated and widely recognized instruments such as the EDE-Q and PAID ensured robust assessment of eating disorder psychopathology and diabetes-related distress. Furthermore, the application of intention-to-treat analysis minimized attrition bias and strengthened the reliability of the findings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. While the 24-week duration provided meaningful insight into medium-term outcomes, a strength compared to many short-term interventions, it does not allow firm conclusions about long-term sustainability. As cautioned by the ARIADNE trial [24], early psychological benefits may diminish without continued support, highlighting the importance of maintenance strategies in future work. The intensive behavioral counseling delivered here, although effective, may also reduce generalizability, as such resource-demanding models are difficult to replicate in routine practice; more scalable or digitally supported approaches should be tested. As with most behavioral interventions, participant blinding was not feasible and may have influenced self-reported psychological outcomes. Although we assessed diabetes-related distress and eating disorder psychopathology, additional psychological measures such as validated depression or anxiety scales were not included, which limits the comprehensiveness of conclusions regarding overall psychological well-being. In addition, the modest sample size and relatively homogeneous cohort of middle-aged adults with early T2D limit external validity and may have underpowered detection of small but relevant differences between CCR and IFCCR. Also, despite randomization and conducting sensitivity analyses adjusting for weight and HbA1c changes, residual confounding by unmeasured or uncontrolled factors cannot be completely excluded.

In conclusion, intensive lifestyle interventions combining CCR or IFCCR with structured behavioral support and regular physical activity reliably improved eating disorder psychopathology, with improvements in diabetes-related distress largely mediated by weight loss and improvements in glycemic control. These interventions offer psychologically and clinically meaningful benefits beyond metabolic outcomes. They support a complementary view of chronic disease management by addressing psychological well-being alongside metabolic and clinical outcomes. Future studies should explore mechanistic pathways, scalable delivery, long-term maintenance, inclusion of additional psychological measures, and personalized approaches to maximize benefit across diverse patient populations.
