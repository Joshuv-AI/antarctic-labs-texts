A Family at War—Against Tropheryma whipplei: A Case Series
Authors: Daina Lasaitiene, Marie Kebke, Anna Grankvist, Ahmed Abdi, Niels Wagner, Karin Bergman, Thomas Beck-Friis, Anders Lundqvist
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 29-year-old woman (case 1) with a 2-year history of arthralgia, pericarditis, fever, weight loss, and elevated inflammatory parameters was admitted to Södra Älvsborg Hospital in Borås, Sweden, in January 2023. Severe thrombocytopenia and anemia were remarkable findings at admission. Transthoracic echocardiography showed vegetations on the aortic valve indicating endocarditis. Initial blood cultures were negative. Diagnostics were expanded with polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing in blood for Tropheryma whipplei and other unusual pathogens. The PCR in blood was positive for T. whipplei, warranting further investigation. Both positive periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining and positive PCR of duodenal biopsy confirmed a diagnosis of Whipple's disease (WD). In addition to WD-associated endocarditis/pericarditis, the patient was diagnosed with bilateral multifocal chorioretinitis, which is a possible manifestation of the disease. WD became suspected even in close relatives, the patient's father and her homozygotic twin, who both had similar manifestations such as pericarditis, arthritis/arthralgia, and gastrointestinal symptoms. Another relative, a sister to the father, presented with gastrointestinal symptoms and arthralgia. “Definitive” WD was confirmed in case 2 and case 3 and “possible” WD in case 4.

Tropheryma whipplei is a gram-positive bacterium widely distributed in the environment. Not all individuals carrying the agent develop infection [1]. In fact, Whipple's disease (WD) is rare, with an incidence of about 1 to 3 cases per 1 000 000 people in Europe [2]. It typically affects middle-aged Caucasian males [3], and primary infections can manifest as symptomatic episodes resembling acute infectious illnesses and, in most cases, lead to bacterial clearance. However, in some instances, the bacteria persist within the host, leading to chronic disease or asymptomatic carriage. Classical WD disease presents with gastrointestinal symptoms like abdominal pain, diarrhea, weight loss, and arthralgia; however, many organ systems may be affected [4]. The rarity of WD, its broad spectrum of symptoms, and the absence of specific clinical features contribute to the challenge of diagnosis, often resulting in delayed recognition.

Here, we report 4 patients in the same family with a delay of several years between the emergence of symptoms and the diagnosis of WD, aiming to raise awareness of this unusual, potentially lethal disease.

CASE 1

A 29-year-old woman came to the emergency department in January 2023 because of a generally impaired condition. She had a history of several years of fatigue, abdominal symptoms, and pain in the chest, shoulders, and neck, as well several months of unexplained anemia and thrombocytopenia (Figure 1).

At initial admission in March 2021, tests showed iron deficiency anemia and elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP). Arthralgias, but not arthritis, and fresh blood in the stool were present. Antinuclear antibodies (ANAs), extractable nuclear antigen antibodies (ENAs), anticitrullinated protein antibodies (ACPAs), and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCAs) were negative, whereas HLA-B27 was positive. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the sacroiliac joints and spine was normal. Results of various examinations are presented in Figure 1. At the beginning of 2022, the diagnosis remained unclear. Persisting shortness of breath and reduced physical capacity resulted in cardiac MRI that revealed pericarditis. She was treated with NSAIDs, colchicine, prednisolone, and anakinra, without clinical effect.

At the end of 2022 (Figure 1), the patient was diagnosed with vaginal bleeding explained by severe thrombocytopenia. Thrombocytopenia was treated with high-dose prednisolone and intravenous immunoglobulin.

At admission in January 2023, tests showed a hemoglobin level of 60 g/L, thrombocytes of 12 × 109/L, CRP of 83 mg/L, ESR of 70 mm/h, and elevated NT-proBNP of 1760 ng/L. Echocardiography showed, unexpectedly, vegetations on all 3 aortic cusps (Figure 2). The national care program of endocarditis in Sweden was followed, and after blood culturing, treatment with benzylpenicillin and tobramycin was initiated. Negative blood cultures led to the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) in blood including unusual pathogens, with a positive result for T. whipplei. The diagnosis was confirmed by PCR analysis of tissue sampling from the duodenum having with corresponding histopathological findings. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) revealed no inflammation, and PCR for T. whipplei was negative. Clinical and laboratory features responded initially to treatment with benzylpenicillin, which was later changed to piperacillin-tazobactam because of a relapse of symptoms, including fever. One month later, the patient was dismissed from the hospital with oral treatment with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP/SMX).

Three additional weeks of intravenous ceftriaxone were initiated due to a relapse of symptoms the following month. At this time, the patient experienced impaired vision in the right eye, along with diplopia and photophobia. Ophthalmological examination in March 2023 revealed bilateral keratoconjunctivitis sicca and left anterior uveitis. Cortisone and atropine drops in the left eye were administered. Follow-up 4 days later showed partial regression in the anterior uveitis. Follow-up a week later showed a bilateral multifocal chorioretinitis, thought to be related to WD (Figure 3). The left anterior uveitis had subsided. Follow-up in April 2023 showed total regression of fundoscopic findings. Of note, the patient described a burning sensation in both eyes and photophobia in February 2021, but no ophthalmologist consultation was performed then.

Whipple's disease. Ocular fundoscopic imaging showing multifocal chorioretinitis in both eyes, taken in March 2023, case 1.

Treatment with intravenous ceftriaxone was planned for another 6 months. At 5-month follow-up, she was in good health, with blood tests being completely normalized. In September 2023, echocardiography showed remaining vegetations on aortic valves and a moderate degree of aortic insufficiency. In October 2023, ceftriaxone was changed to oral treatment with doxycycline plus hydroxychloroquine (HCQ). Two years of successful treatment ended in March 2025.

CASE 2

The father of case 1 was diagnosed with psoriatic arthritis in 2019. A 59-year-old White man with a family history of psoriasis, a history of hypertension, and >20 years of fleeting joint pain, he had been previously examined by 3 different rheumatologists (Figure 4).

Having been treated with methotrexate (MTX) for 3 months, he was diagnosed with rhinovirus infection, and MTX was paused. Soon thereafter, he developed fever, cough, and dyspnea. Examination revealed exudative pleuritis, pericarditis, and heart failure, with an ejection fraction (EF) of 45%. Tests showed elevated CRP and ESR. ACPA, rheumatoid factor (RF), ANA, ENA, and ANCA were negative. Treatment with high-dose prednisolone was started, with a later addition of adalimumab for immune-related polyserositis. Further investigation with cardiac MRI and cardiac catheterization revealed constrictive pericarditis, and endomyocardial biopsy showed focal endocardial and myocardial fibrosis of unknown origin. PAS staining was negative. During the subsequent 1.5–2 years, he was treated with various combinations of immunosuppressants for psoriatic arthritis and constrictive pericarditis. Due to worsening of heart failure and constrictive pericarditis, he was investigated for WD because his daughter had been recently diagnosed with WD. The patient reported weight loss of 10 kg over 2 months and intermittent night sweats. Tests showed a PCR that was positive for T. whipplei from feces but PCR from blood, saliva, and urine were negative, indicating carriership. Transesophageal echocardiography ruled out endocarditis. PAS staining of gastric biopsy did not support the diagnosis of WD. However, positive PCR from duodenal biopsy and knee synovial fluid confirmed WD. Normal CSF composition and negative PCR excluded WD-associated engagement of the nervous system.

Intravenous ceftriaxone was started. Within a few days, the patient experienced improvement, with lessening of arthralgia and fatigue and improved inflammatory parameters. Following 2 weeks of intravenous treatment, the patient was discharged with oral TMP/SMX. Side effects due to oral treatment began 2 weeks later, with itching rash leading to change of treatment to ceftriaxone for a week, changing thereafter to doxycycline and HCQ. Normal histomorphology of the duodenum with negative PAS staining on the duodenal biopsy, as well as negative duodenal PCR for T. whipplei, resulted in discontinuation of successful 1-year antibiotic treatment. The latest PCR tests for T. whipplei from feces, urine, and saliva were negative.

CASE 3

Beginning in 2019, the monozygotic twin to case 1 visited the Department of Infectious Diseases several times due recurrent fever and slightly elevated CRP and ESR (Figure 5). The patient complained of arthralgias for the past 2 years, as well as irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). She experienced weight loss, fatigue, and headache. Chronic infection was assessed as unlikely after investigation. ANA, ENA, ANCA, ACPA, and RF were negative. In 2020, at her first visit to a rheumatologist, she described fluctuating fever, intermittent arthralgia moving from joint to joint for periods of 2–3 days, and swollen joints and lymph nodes. Arthritis was, however, not evident, and inflammatory parameters were normal. She was diagnosed with palindromic rheumatism, and an initially successful treatment with HCQ was initiated. On her own initiative, she chose to discontinue HCQ treatment after 1–1.5 years due to a fibromyalgia-like condition and did not return to the rheumatologist until 2023 for a diagnostic arthrocentesis as part of an investigation for possible WD.

In 2023, the patient self-referred to the Department of Infectious Diseases as both her monozygotic twin (case 1) and father (case 2) had been recently diagnosed with WD. The patient described fibromyalgia- and palindromic rheumatism–like symptoms. Although a duodenal biopsy did not show histomorphological changes specific to WD, the final WD diagnosis was based on positive PCR tests from saliva, feces, and synovial fluid samples, as well as positive PCRs from duodenal and ileal biopsies. CSF was negative for WD PCR. A 2-week treatment with ceftriaxone was initiated, followed by TMP/SMX. Development of exanthema resulted in a change of TMP/SMX to a year-long effective treatment with doxycycline and HCQ. At present, she is well, apart from myalgia.

CASE 4

In December 2023, a 52-year-old woman, the sibling of case 2, had her first appointment at the Clinic of Infectious Diseases. She had made a self-referral because of longstanding ailments that could possibly be explained by WD. Persistent arthralgia and hypermobility of several joints, especially the hands and knees, were present. The patient had bowel problems dating back several decades, including flatulence and loose stools, daily at times. Her symptoms had been diagnosed as IBS. Chronic thrombocytopenia with platelet counts of 50–150 ×109/L, judged to be idiopathic or related to medication use, was present. Tests showed normal CRP and ESR. Thrombocytes were in the lower normal range. PCR samples from saliva, urine, and feces were positive for T. whipplei. Further, PCR from duodenal biopsy was positive for WD, but histomorphological changes were not present. In January 2024, the patient was scheduled for a knee arthroscopy to repair a previously diagnosed meniscus tear. PCR tests from meniscus tissue and synovial fluid were negative for WD. Definitive WD diagnosis was not confirmed due to the absence of duodenal PAS positivity and positive extraintestinal PCR samples. In February 2024, the patient began treatment for WD with ceftriaxone for 14 days, followed by TMP/SMX planned for 1 year.

At follow-up 6 months later, the patient reported reduced frequency of diarrhea and reduced general joint pain. CRP and ESR remained normal. TMP/SMX treatment was prolonged for the final 6 months and successfully discontinued thereafter. T. whipplei PCR tests from feces, urine, saliva, and blood were negative in 2 consecutive sampling sessions following the end of treatment.

DIAGNOSTIC PROCEDURES

Molecular Detection

Bacterial DNA was extracted from samples of different body sites using the MagLEAD Extraction Robot (Precision System Science [PSS], CA, USA) and the MagDEA Dx SV Isolation Kit I (PSS) according to the manufacturer's instructions, as described earlier. All samples were subjected to a specific PCR for T. whipplei amplifying a 160-bp fragment of the 16SrRNA gene of T. whipplei. After PCR amplification, the fragments were visualized on 2% agarose gel. Diagnostic confirmation was performed with a broad-range PCR targeting 766 bp of the 16sRNA gene for the samples from sterile locations, blood, CSF, and synovial fluid. Sequencing analysis was performed using the basic local alignment search tool (BLAST) [5, 6].

Histopathology

Paraffin-embedded tissue from the small bowel was examined with hematoxylin and eosin for histopathologic features. To detect microorganisms within the tissues, the Giemsa, gram (Brown-Brenn and Brown-Hopps), Periodic-Acid Schiff, Grocott-Gomori, Warthin-Starry, Gimenez, and Ziehl-Nielsen stains were performed.

DISCUSSION

Herein we describe 3 confirmed cases and 1 possible case of WD in a Swedish family, where WD was diagnosed following national guidelines for the management of blood culture–negative endocarditis (BCNE) in the first twin sister. Due to persistent rheumatological symptoms, reminiscent of case 1, WD was also diagnosed in case 2. A self-referral to the Clinic of Infectious Diseases, once again because of persistent rheumatological and gastrointestinal symptoms that were reminiscent of the 2 other relatives’ symptoms, speeded up the establishment of the “definite” WD diagnosis in case 3 and “possible” WD in case 4. A case series of WD in the same Swedish family raised the question of a possible underlying genetic predisposition for WD.

Indeed, genetic predisposition to WD has been confirmed by a French group of researchers in 2018, who described an underlying IRF4 haploinsufficiency in 4 members of a French family, all of whom were diagnosed with WD [7].

Globally, WD is a rare disorder, with most reports published in North America and Europe. The overall incidence of WD is about 1–3 in 1 million people [2]. The overall prevalence of WD was reported to be 9.8 cases per 1 million people in the United States [8]. Since the beginning of 2015, 30 cases of WD have been diagnosed in Sweden, with a population of around 10 million people. WD could have a higher incidence, and it is probably underdiagnosed due to wide array of manifestations and low index of suspicion.

WD can occur at any age, but it most often occurs during the fifth decade [9]. The mean age of onset of symptoms is 55. It is much more frequent in males than in females, with a ratio of 4:1 [2]. Our case series demonstrates that WD must be suspected in unclear clinical situations, even in young patients, when arthralgias/arthritis, gastrointestinal symptoms, and systemic inflammatory reactions are present or the patient does not respond, or gets worse, during ongoing treatment with antirheumatic immunosuppressants.

Penetration of family history in obscure cases is of vital importance and can speed up establishment of the correct diagnosis. The organism seems to be soil-dwelling, which explains the increased prevalence in farmers [2]. The most important risk factors for T. whipplei infection include exposure to contaminated soil, close contact with carriers of the organism or patients with WD, and poor living conditions [10]. While WD is rare, it is associated with a wide range of systemic presentations. Classic symptoms include arthralgias, diarrhea, and weight loss. Importantly, 15% of patients with WD do not have classic symptoms [11].

Cardiac Manifestations of Whipple's Disease

Cardiovascular symptoms were described in up to 19% of patients with WD [12]. Cardiac involvement in WD can be variable, with the most important manifestations being endocarditis and pericarditis [9]. A retrospective study of Tison et al. on 68 patients with WD demonstrated endocarditis in 4.4% and pericarditis in 8.8% of patients [13]. In a smaller retrospective study, Duss et al. reported 3 cases of endocarditis among 22 patients with confirmed WD [14]. In BCNE, an important role of WD is increasingly recognized, where T. whipplei accounts for up to 6% of BCNE [15, 16]. Mortality due to endocarditis was reported in 9.4% (12 out of 127) of patients with WD in a systematic review by Iannou et al. [17]. An interesting case of a patient with refractory arthritis presenting with stroke related to endocarditis was published in 2023. The diagnosis of WD was established by positive Tropheryma PCR after aortic valve surgery [18].

Constrictive pericarditis is a rare clinical finding in patients diagnosed with WD [19]. To our knowledge, constrictive pericarditis in case 2 is 1 of 10 reports in the world describing constrictive pericarditis related to WD. The most recent case report of pancarditis, including constrictive pericarditis, was published in German in 2023; a postmortem diagnosis of WD was made in a 69-year-old man who died of acute heart failure [20].

Rheumatic Manifestations of Whipple's Disease

Intermittent and migratory oligo- or polyarthritis is a common presenting symptom, preceding the diagnosis by a mean of 5–10 years [12]. Caillet Portillo et al. described a cohort of 73 French patients with various rheumatic diagnoses who had inadequate response to disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) and who were later diagnosed with WD. Arthralgia and arthritis were present in 98.6% and 86.3% of patients, respectively. Large-joint involvement was present in 90.3% of patients, where polyarticular pattern was evident in 56.5% of patients. DMARDs were stopped in 94.2% of patients after the start of treatment for WD [12]. WD can be unmasked or accelerated by immunosuppressive therapy, typically given for presumed rheumatic disease, in some cases with severe complications such as sepsis or disseminated T. whipplei infection [21]. Indeed, 3 of 4 members of the Swedish family had had joint symptoms for several years. The father's symptoms, such as recurrent fever, night sweats, and profound weight loss later, were unmasked by continuous immunosuppressive therapy for several years. WD should be suspected in cases of inflammatory arthritis, spondylarthritis, palindromic rheumatism, and other rheumatic disorders unresponsive to DMARDs and in cases where immunosuppressive therapy appears to worsen the course of suspected rheumatic disease with recurrence of fever, as in case 2 [12, 22].

Hematological Manifestations of Whipple's Disease

Thrombocytosis, in addition to anemia and neutrophilia, is described in up to 26% of patients with WD [13]. Thrombocytopenia and pancytopenia, however, are rare and described in only a few case reports [23–27]. Thrombocytopenia may have several different origins in patients with WD. Thrombocytopenia can be the consequence of splenomegaly with hypersplenism, or it can reflect the involvement of bone marrow in patients with WD. Thrombocytopenia was present in 2 of 4 cases. In case 1, thrombocytopenia was severe, and it required treatment with intravenous immunoglobulin, whereas in case 4 it was mild and asymptomatic. In case 1, thrombocytopenia could have possibly been aggravated by treatment with colchicine and/or anakinra, the medications with thrombocytopenia as 1 of the known, if extremely rare, potential adverse side effects. In case 4, asymptomatic thrombocytopenia was present for several years. Treatment of WD with antibiotics resulted in normalization of platelet count in both cases.

Ocular Manifestations of Whipple's Disease

Ophtalmological symptoms are present in 5.5% of patients with WD [12]. Uveitis is the most often reported ocular manifestation in the most recent studies, and the frequency varies between 2.9% and 18% in patients with WD [13, 14].

Case 1 presented with severe ocular involvement including keratoconjunctivitis sicca, anterior uveitis, and bilateral multifocal chorioretinitis. An argument could be made that the ocular symptoms were a consequence of immune reconstitution syndrome (IRIS) rather than a result of the infection and WD. IRIS is characterized by exaggerated and dysregulated inflammatory responses that can occur because of reconstitution of adaptive or innate immunity. Indeed, IRIS, including inflammatory orbitopathy, has been described in patients with WD [28]. IRIS was diagnosed in about 10% of patients with WD in a German cohort study on 142 patients with WD [29]. IRIS should be considered in patients with WD in whom inflammatory symptoms recur after effective treatment. We hypothesize, though, that ocular manifestations may have been related to WD in our patient because she had ocular symptoms even before antibiotic exposure in February 2021. Unfortunately, no ophthalmologist consultation was performed either in February 2021 or when antibiotic treatment was prescribed in March 2023. To our knowledge, our case 1 is the third case in the literature reporting bilateral multifocal chorioretinitis in a patient with WD [29–31].

CONCLUSIONS

By presenting 3 confirmed, and 1 possible, cases of WD in a Swedish family, we highlight the potential negative effects of delayed diagnosis. WD is still challenging to identify clinically due to various atypical extraintestinal manifestations and a low index of suspicion. Penetration of family history in obscure cases can be of critical importance and can speed up establishment of the correct diagnosis. A genetic predisposition to the disease must be suspected in this family, and we may consider genetic study of our patients in the near future.
