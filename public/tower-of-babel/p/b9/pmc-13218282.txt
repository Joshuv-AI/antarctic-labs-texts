Safety assessment of the substance l‐aspartic acid, N‐benzoyl‐, disodium salt for use in plastic food contact materials
Authors: EFSA Panel on Food Contact Materials (FCM), Claude Lambré, Riccardo Crebelli, Maria João da Silva, Konrad Grob, Ester Heath, Evgenia Lampi, Maria Rosaria Milana, Marja Pronk, Mario Ščetar, Georgios Theodoridis, Els Van Hoeck, Nadia Waegeneers, Ronan Cariou, Laurence Castle, Emma Di Consiglio, Roland Franz, Eric Barthélémy, Remigio Marano, Gilles Rivière
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe EFSA Panel on Food Contact Materials assessed the safety of l‐aspartic acid, N‐benzoyl, disodium salt to be used as a nucleating agent in polypropylene at up to 0.07% w/w. Final articles are intended for single and repeated use in contact with all food types. The migration of the substance was detected in ethanolic simulants up to 0.03 mg/kg food. The Panel excluded genotoxicity concerns for the substance based on in vitro studies. Migration of impurities and reaction/degradation products was either not detectable or below 0.05 mg/kg food, except for sodium benzoate and sodium l‐aspartate. The first migrated at 1.5 mg/kg food; the latter was below the limit of quantification of 0.224 mg/kg food in acetic acid. Both substances do not raise safety concerns being, respectively, the sodium salt of benzoic acid, FCM No 116, authorised without restrictions, and a salt of aspartic acid. For benzamide, given its thresholded mechanism of indirect genotoxicity as a PARP inhibitor, a sufficient margin of exposure exists to rule out concern at the observed migration levels. Four remaining impurities and reaction products were assessed using read‐across from the substance and in silico tools, and did not raise concerns for genotoxicity. The Panel concluded that the substance is not of safety concern for the consumer, if (i) it is used at up to 0.07% w/w in polypropylene homo‐ and co‐polymers to manufacture food contact materials for single and repeated use; (ii) final articles are in contact with all types of food, excluding infant formula, human milk and water that could be used to reconstitute infant formula; (iii) final articles are used for storage up to and above 6 months at room temperature and below, including hot‐fill conditions and/or heating up to 121°C for maximum 2 h; and (iv) the migration of the substance does not exceed the 0.05 mg/kg food.Keywords: benzamide; FCM substance No. 1099; food contact materials; l‐aspartic acid, N‐benzoyl‐, disodium salt; NIAS; reaction products; safety assessment

INTRODUCTION

Background and Terms of Reference

Before a substance is authorised to be used in food contact materials (FCM) and is included in a positive list, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA)'s opinion on its safety is required. This procedure has been established in Articles 8, 9 and 10 of Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004 1 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 27 October 2004 on materials and articles intended to come into contact with food.

According to this procedure, the industry submits applications to the competent authorities of Member States, which transmit the applications to the EFSA for evaluation.

In this case, EFSA received an application from the Irish competent authority (Food Safety Authority of Ireland), requesting the safety evaluation of the substance l‐aspartic acid, N‐benzoyl‐, sodium salt (1:2), FCM substance No 1099. The request has been registered in EFSA's register of received questions under the number EFSA‐Q‐2024‐00487. The dossier was submitted by Exponent International on behalf of ADEKA Corporation.

According to Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004 of the European Parliament and of the Council on materials and articles intended to come into contact with food, EFSA is asked to carry out an assessment of the risks related to the intended use of the substance and to deliver a scientific opinion.

Methodologies

The assessment was conducted in line with the principles laid down in Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004 on materials and articles intended to come into contact with food. This Regulation requires applicants to submit an application accompanied by a technical dossier containing the information specified in the guidelines for the safety assessment of a substance to be published by EFSA. In practice, the technical dossier should contain the information required in the EFSA Note for Guidance for the preparation of an application for the safety assessment of a substance to be used in food contact materials (EFSA AFC Panel, 2008).

The methodology is based on the characterisation of the substance that is the subject of the request for safety assessment prior to authorisation, its impurities and reaction and degradation products, the evaluation of the exposure to those substances through migration and the definition of minimum sets of toxicity data required for safety assessment.

To establish the safety from ingestion of migrating substances, the toxicological data indicating the potential hazard and the likely human exposure data need to be combined. Exposure is estimated from studies on migration into food or food simulants and considering that a person may consume daily up to 1 kg of food in contact with the relevant FCM.

As a general rule, the greater the exposure through migration, the more toxicological data is required for the safety assessment of a substance. Currently there are three tiers with different thresholds triggering the need for more toxicological information as follows:

In case of high migration (i.e. 5–60 mg/kg food), an extensive data set is needed.

In case of high migration (i.e. 5–60 mg/kg food), an extensive data set is needed.

In case of migration between 0.05 and 5 mg/kg food, a reduced data set may suffice.

In case of migration between 0.05 and 5 mg/kg food, a reduced data set may suffice.

In case of low migration (i.e. < 0.05 mg/kg food), only a limited data set is needed.

In case of low migration (i.e. < 0.05 mg/kg food), only a limited data set is needed.

The above tiered approach to toxicity testing applies to all migrating substances.

For impurities, oligomers and reaction and degradation products which migrate into foods, non‐testing methods may be taken into account to assess the genotoxic potential (EFSA CEF Panel, 2016).

In case of applications on mixtures of natural origin, the EFSA CEP Panel updated the risk assessment of ‘wood flour and fibres, untreated’ and set criteria for future applications of materials from plant origin as additives for plastic food contact materials (EFSA CEP Panel, 2019). This was completed by an EFSA Technical Report on principles that could be applicable to the safety assessment of the use of mixtures of natural origin (EFSA, 2023a), which details and sets specific data requirements.

Finally, relevant cross‐cutting documents from the EFSA Scientific Committee 5 should be followed.

More detailed information on the above data requirements is available in the respective above‐mentioned EFSA documents.

The assessment was conducted in line with the principles described in the EFSA Guidance on transparency in the scientific aspects of risk assessment (EFSA, 2009).

ASSESSMENT

According to the applicant, the substance l‐aspartic acid, N‐benzoyl‐, sodium salt (1:2) 6 is intended to be used as a β‐nucleating agent in the production of polypropylene (PP) homo‐ and co‐polymers to manufacture FCM for single and repeated use articles in contact with all types of food, including mineral water, such as cups, bottles and food storage containers. The final FCMs may be used for up to 2 hours at temperatures up to 121°C, followed by long term storage (> 30 days) at room temperature and below. The maximum intended use level is 0.07% w/w.

The substance is not intended to be used in articles that will be brought into contact with infant food or formula and human milk.

The substance has not been evaluated in the past by the SCF or EFSA.

Physical and chemical properties 8

The substance is soluble in water (between 299 and 346 g/L at 20°C), in 3% acetic acid and 10% ethanol (> 300 g/L), but sparingly soluble in 95% ethanol (0.1 g/L) and practically insoluble in miglyol (< 0.01 g/L). Since the substance is a powder, the presence of small particles should be established in accordance with the ‘Guidance on Technical requirements for regulated food and feed product applications to establish the presence of small particles, including nanoparticles’. Toxicological data 11

The migration of l‐aspartic acid, N‐benzoyl‐, disodium salt was below 0.05 mg/kg food and was assessed in accordance with the EFSA Note for Guidance (EFSA AFC Panel, 2008). The applicant submitted a bacterial reverse mutation assay (Ames test) and an in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test, following the EFSA Scientific opinion on genotoxicity testing strategies applicable to food and feed safety assessment (EFSA Scientific Committee, 2011). In addition, an in vitro chromosomal aberration test and an in vivo micronucleus test in mice were provided.

Apart from BzNa (see Section 3.1.3), six impurities and reaction products were assessed. The migration of L‐AsNa was below the LOQ (0.224 mg/kg food) and does not raise safety concerns, as it is a salt of aspartic acid, which is an amino acid and a normal component of human proteins. Migration of the other five compounds was either below 0.05 mg/kg food or not detected at the LODs, always below 0.05 mg/kg food. BzAm was assessed with in silico data and literature studies, while the remaining four (Imp1, Imp2, Et1 and Et2) were assessed with read‐across from the substance and in silico data, all for their genotoxicity potential.

l‐aspartic acid, N‐benzoyl‐, disodium salt

Bacterial reverse mutation assay

The test item consisted of 99.6% l‐aspartic acid, N‐benzoyl‐, disodium salt and 0.1% of benzamide. It was tested in the bacterial reverse mutation assay (Ames test) with Salmonella Typhimurium strains TA98, TA100, TA1535, TA1537 and Escherichia coli WP2 uvrA. The study was performed in compliance with GLP principles, following the OECD Test Guideline (TG) 471 (OECD, 2020). The test material was dissolved in distilled water and evaluated in two separate experiments using the preincubation procedure. Both assays were performed in the absence and presence of metabolic activation by phenobarbital/β‐naphthoflavone‐induced rat liver S9 fraction (S9‐mix). In the first experiment, the following concentrations were tested: 5000 μg/plate, the maximum recommended concentration, and five lower ones (1250, 313, 78.1, 19.5 and 4.88 μg/plate). In the second experiment, 5000 μg/plate and four lower concentrations (2500, 1250, 625 and 313 μg/plate) were evaluated; an additional lower concentration (156 μg/plate) was tested only in TA100 without S9 mix. All concentrations, as well as negative (vehicle) and positive controls, were evaluated using triplicate plates.

No precipitation of the test substance was observed. No reduction of the background growth of auxotrophic bacteria, indicating a bacteriotoxic effect, was observed in any tester strain and tested concentration in both experiments. A decrease in the number of revertants below the historical negative control range, suggestive of a toxic effect, was only observed in TA100 in the first experiment at the highest dose in the absence of metabolic activation. No increase in revertant colonies more than twice the negative control, or treatment‐related increase in revertants, was observed in both experiments in any tester strain, with or without metabolic activation. Both vehicle and positive controls were within the respective historical control limits.

The results of the study are considered negative, i.e. the test item did not induce gene mutations under the conditions of the study. Based on the EFSA's ‘Harmonised approach for reporting reliability and relevance of genotoxicity studies’ (EFSA, 2023b), the Panel considered the study reliable without restrictions and the results of high relevance.

In vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test

The test item consisted of 99.6% l‐aspartic acid, N‐benzoyl‐, disodium salt and 0.1% of benzamide and was tested in the in vitro micronucleus assay using the human lymphoblastoid cell line TK6. The test was performed in compliance with GLP principles and according to the OECD TG 487 (2023). The test item was dissolved in distilled water. In the absence of evidence of toxicity in a range‐finding test, the test item was evaluated at the maximum recommended concentration of 2000 μg/mL and at two lower ones (1000 and 500 μg/mL). In the short treatment, cells were exposed for 3 h in the absence and presence of metabolic activation by phenobarbital/β‐naphthoflavone‐induced rat liver S9 fraction (S9‐mix), followed by 21 h of recovery; in the continuous treatment, cells were exposed for 24 h in the absence of S9 mix. Micronucleated cells were scored in 1000 cells from duplicated cultures (2000 in total) at all concentrations, as well as for negative and positive controls. The toxicity of treatment was evaluated comparing the cell count (RCC, relative cell count) and growth rate (RPD, relative population doubling) in treated and negative control cells.

No evidence of cytotoxicity and no precipitation of the test item were observed at the end of the incubation period. The frequency of micronucleated cells in treated cultures was not significantly different from that in negative controls in any treatment condition. The frequencies of micronucleated cells in negative and positive controls were within the historical limits.

The results of the study were considered negative, i.e. the test item did not induce the formation of micronuclei under the conditions of the study. The Panel considered the study reliable without restrictions and the results of high relevance.

In vitro chromosomal aberrations test

The test item (99.7% pure l‐aspartic acid, N‐benzoyl‐, disodium salt) was tested in a chromosomal aberration test in human lymphocytes in vitro. The test was performed in compliance with GLP principles and according to the OECD TG 473 (2016a). The test item was dissolved in minimal essential medium and evaluated in a range of concentrations determined in a preliminary toxicity test on the basis of mitotic index (MI) inhibition. In the short treatment, cells were exposed for 4 h in the absence and presence of metabolic activation by phenobarbital/β‐naphthoflavone‐induced rat liver S9 fraction (S9‐mix), followed by 20 h of recovery; in the continuous treatment, cells were exposed for 24 h without S9 mix. In the absence of evidence of toxicity, in the short treatment, cells were evaluated for metaphase analysis at the maximum recommended concentration of 2000 μg/mL and at two lower ones (1000 and 500 μg/mL). In the continuous treatment, the MI was decreased by 42%, 57% and 44% at the three highest concentrations (500, 1000 and 2000 μg/mL): on this basis, 1000 μg/mL was selected as maximum concentration for metaphase analysis; two lower concentrations (500 and 250 μg/mL) were also evaluated. For each concentration, structural chromosomal aberrations and polyploidy were evaluated from coded slides in 300 metaphases (150 per duplicate cultures); 600 metaphases were scored for negative controls.

No precipitation of the test item was observed at the end of the incubation period. The frequency of cells with structural chromosomal aberration and polyploidy in treated cultures was not significantly different from negative controls in any treatment condition. The frequencies of chromosomal aberrations in negative and positive controls were within the historical limits.

The results of the study were considered negative, i.e. the test item did not induce the formation of structural chromosomal aberrations and polyploidy under the conditions of the study. However, the Panel noted that in the continuous treatment, the maximum recommended concentration of 2000 μg/mL could also have been evaluated, as at this dose the MI inhibition (44%) was still below the maximum recommended level. On this basis, the Panel considered the study reliable with restrictions and the results of limited relevance.

In vivo micronucleus test

The test item consisted of 99.6% l‐aspartic acid, N‐benzoyl‐, disodium salt and 0.1% of benzamide and was tested in the mammalian erythrocyte micronucleus test in Crl:CD‐1 mice. The test was performed in compliance with GLP principles and according to the OECD TG 474 (2016b). The test item was dissolved in distilled water and administered by gavage twice at 24 h interval to groups of five young adult mice (7 weeks old). Given the absence of sex‐related toxicity in the preliminary toxicity test, only males were treated in the main experiment. The test item was administered at the maximum recommended dose of 2000 mg/kg body weight (bw) and two lower doses of 1000 and 500 mg/kg bw. Twenty‐four hours after the second administration, animals were killed and bone marrow smears prepared for microscopic examination. Micronuclei were scored in 4000 polychromatic erythrocytes (PCE) per animal. As a measure of toxicity of treatments to bone marrow, the PCE/total erythrocyte (TE) ratio was evaluated in 500 TE per animal.

No clinical signs, body weight reduction or decrease in the PCE/TE ratio were observed in treated animals compared to negative controls. The frequency of micronucleated PCE (MNPCE) in the groups of treated mice was not statistically different from the concurrent control group (conditional binomial tests). However, the mean MNPCE in the high‐dose group was slightly above the 95% historical control limit based on the latest 20 experiments (0.140% vs. 0.136%). Therefore, not all conditions for a clearly negative result, as indicated in OECD TG 474 (2016b), were fulfilled in this study. In this case, according to TG 474 (2026), expert judgement should be applied to evaluate the biological relevance of the result. Considering the absence of a dose–response relationship and the interanimal variability of results, the borderline (< 1.7‐fold) increase in mean MNPCE frequency observed in the high‐dose group is of questionable biological relevance.

According to the evaluation criteria of the OECD TG 474, demonstration of bone marrow exposure – in addition to the lack of induction of micronuclei at the maximum recommended or applicable dose – is requested to consider a test substance as clearly negative and the results as relevant for the in vivo hazard identification. Without confirmation of bone marrow exposure, potential genotoxic effects at sites other than those modelled by bone marrow (e.g. the site of first contact) are still possible and cannot be ruled out. In the present study, no evidence of bone marrow toxicity nor any other proof of systemic exposure was observed. Therefore, the negative results do not, on their own, demonstrate the lack of in vivo clastogenic or aneugenic activity of the test item. However, based on the negative results obtained in the in vitro micronucleus test (see Section 3.2.1.2), the Panel concluded that no in vivo clastogenic/aneugenic activity is anticipated. Therefore, no follow‐up with an in vivo micronucleus test is required to complete the genotoxicity assessment of the substance.

Conclusion on the genotoxicity potential of the substance

The Panel concluded that the available results of the in vitro genotoxicity studies allow to rule out concern for the genotoxicity of l‐aspartic acid, N‐benzoyl‐, disodium salt.

Impurities and reaction products

In silico data from the OECD QSAR Toolbox were provided for all five impurities and reaction products and from Derek Nexus for BzAm, Imp1 and Imp2. Literature data were also provided for BzAm.

No genotoxicity is anticipated for Imp1, Imp2, Et1 and Et2, based on read‐across from the substance l‐aspartic acid, N‐benzoyl‐, disodium salt, taking into account their structural similarity and the available negative experimental data on the substance. The in silico assessment of the molecules indicated no alerts for genotoxicity and hence confirmed the indications of the read‐across.

BzAm is an inhibitor of the enzyme poly‐(ADP‐ribose)‐polymerase (PARP), which is involved in various mechanisms of DNA repair. In the EFSA's ‘Re‐evaluation of saccharin and its sodium, potassium and calcium salts (E 954) as food additives’ (EFSA FAF Panel, 2024), the FAF Panel reported that ‘genotoxicity studies on BzAm indicate co‐mutagenic and genotoxic potential, with the enhancement and delayed repair of MMS‐induced single strand breaks, the enhancement of MNNG‐induced UDS in vitro, the induction of sister chromatid exchange (SCE) in rodent cells in vitro and the formation of micronuclei in erythropoietic cells of mice treated orally’.

BzAm was negative in an Ames test in the presence and absence of rat or hamster S9 (Zeiger et al., 1988). No alerts for DNA binding and in vitro or in vivo mutagenicity were identified in the OECD QSAR Toolbox in BzAm, except for an alert for protein binding through acylation, which is consistent with the biological activity of BzAm as PARP inhibitor. The Panel noted that, as PARP is an abundant nuclear protein (about 106 molecules per cell), DNA damage secondary to PARP inhibition is considered a thresholded mechanism, which allows the application of a margin of exposure approach (Yamamura et al., 2015).

The Panel noted that the test item evaluated in the in vivo micronucleus test (Section 3.2.1.4) contained 0.1% of BzAm; thus, at the administered top dose of 2000 mg/kg bw, animals received 2 mg/kg bw of BzAm. Although this study is considered inconclusive for the genotoxicity assessment of the substance owing to the absence of demonstrated bone marrow exposure, the Panel noted that such systemic exposure to BzAm is anticipated based on its predicted bioavailability (OECD QSAR Toolbox). Consequently, the observed absence of micronucleus induction at the highest tested dose can be used as reference point for the risk characterisation of BzAm, given its indirect, thresholded mechanism of genotoxicity. The Panel calculated that the margin between the no‐effect oral dose of BzAm in the in vivo micronucleus assay (2 mg/kg bw) and the human oral exposure resulting from its migration into food (up to 0.03 mg/kg food, i.e. 0.0005 mg/kg bw) is sufficiently large to accommodate possible uncertainties regarding the extent of systemic exposure to BzAm under the test conditions. Therefore, this margin is considered sufficient by the Panel to rule out concern for indirect genotoxic effects associated with BzAm migration into food.

DISCUSSION

The substance is a powder and its solubility in water is far above 60 mg/L. Therefore, no additional assessment for the fraction of small particles is needed.

At all‐time points, the migration of the substance, its impurities and reaction products, except for the BzNa and the l‐AsNa, into the tested food simulants was either individually below 0.05 mg/kg food or below the respective LODs (always below 0.05 mg/kg food).

The Panel considered the negative results of the available in vitro genotoxicity studies as highly relevant for the assessment of the substance and concluded that the substance does not raise concern for genotoxicity.

BzNa is the sodium salt of the benzoic acid, FCM No 116, authorised without restrictions, and l‐AsNa is a salt of aspartic acid, a normal component of human proteins. Both substances do not raise safety concerns.

Based on in silico data and read‐across, the Panel concluded that the other identified impurities and the reaction products formed under the reported conditions, Imp1, Imp2, Et1 and Et2, do not raise a concern for genotoxicity.

BzAm lacks structural alerts for DNA damage and genotoxicity and was negative in the Ames test provided. However, genotoxic effects in vitro and in vivo have been reported because of the inhibitory activity of BzAm on the DNA repair enzyme PARP. As this indirect mechanism of BzAm genotoxicity is considered thresholded, the Panel applied a margin of exposure approach for its safety evaluation. Based on the negative results obtained in the in vivo micronucleus test with a test item containing BzAm as minor component, a sufficient margin exists with respect to the human exposure associated with the migration of BzAm into food. This margin allows to rule out concern for indirect genotoxic effects. Hence, the FCM Panel concluded that the migration of BzAm is not of concern at the assessed use level of the substance (0.07% w/w).

The applicant excluded the use of the final PP articles in contact with infant formula, human milk and water that could be used to reconstitute infant formula. Therefore, this use was not considered in this evaluation.

CONCLUSIONS

The FCM Panel concluded that the substance l‐aspartic acid, N‐benzoyl‐, disodium salt is not of safety concern for the consumer if:

it is used at up to 0.07% w/w in polypropylene homo‐ and co‐polymers to manufacture food contact materials for single and repeated use; and

it is used at up to 0.07% w/w in polypropylene homo‐ and co‐polymers to manufacture food contact materials for single and repeated use; and

the final articles are in contact with all types of food, excluding infant formula, human milk, and water that could be used to reconstitute infant formula; and

the final articles are in contact with all types of food, excluding infant formula, human milk, and water that could be used to reconstitute infant formula; and

the final articles are used for storage up to and above 6 months at room temperature and below, including hot‐fill conditions and/or heating up to 121°C for maximum 2 hours; and

the final articles are used for storage up to and above 6 months at room temperature and below, including hot‐fill conditions and/or heating up to 121°C for maximum 2 hours; and

the migration of the substance does not exceed 0.05 mg/kg food.
