Fibula single-tunnel versus fibula double-tunnel for both anterior talofibular ligament and calcaneofibular ligament reconstruction: a biomechanical comparison
Authors: Jingyue Gan, Haifeng Liang, Dongtai Li, Yiran Yan, Zhongxun Liu, Yan Chen, Bo Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnatomic ligament repair surgery, despite being a tremendous technical challenge in treating chronic lateral ankle instability patients, remains the gold standard for this condition. The fibula single-tunnel and double-tunnel techniques are effective for intraoperative reconstruction of the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) and calcaneofibular ligament (CFL). However, which approach is more appropriate is a subject of debate.MethodsTwelve fresh frozen cadaveric ankles were randomly divided into 2 groups of 6 specimens: fibula single-tunnel reconstruction group and fibula double-tunnel reconstruction group. The specimens were tested for ankle joint laxity on the plane radiographs with 150 N anterior drawer force and 150 N varus stress force. The anterior talar displacement and talar tilt angle were measured at states of intact, cut and reconstruction of both ATFL and CFL. Then, the reconstructed specimens were loaded to ultimate failure to determine the strength and stiffness of each construct.ResultsThere were no significant differences between the two groups in the anterior talar displacement and talar tilt angle at the states of intact, cut and reconstruction of both the ATFL and CFL. There were no significant differences between the two groups in the ultimate load failure and stiffness after reconstruction of both the ATFL and CFL.ConclusionsIn patients undergoing ligament reconstruction surgery for ankle instability, both single-tunnel reconstruction of the fibula and double-tunnel reconstruction of the fibula were able to achieve better results. There was no significant difference between these two surgical procedures in our study. The long-term survival of the ankle joint after both techniques remains to be studied.Keywords: Chronic ankle instability, Anterior talofibular ligament, Calcaneofibular ligament, Biomechanical, Ligament reconstruction

Introduction

Most ankle sprains can achieve satisfactory curative effects through conservative treatment; however, 20–40% of severe ankle sprains develop into chronic lateral ankle instability [1]. These patients require surgery, for which, there are various surgical methods [2–5] mainly including anatomical repair technology with modified BrostrÖm as the gold standard [6], anatomical reconstruction of lateral ankle ligaments and non-anatomical reconstruction procedures. Due to their alteration of normal ankle and subtalar joint biomechanics, non-anatomical reconstruction procedures are associated with complications—such as limited ankle motion and intrinsic muscle imbalance—and are therefore no longer recommended [5]. Although the modified Broström technique is widely accepted as the gold standard for anatomical repair of chronic lateral ankle instability, it may be insufficient in certain patient populations. Specifically, patients with poor or insufficient ligament remnants, prior failed repairs, high physical activity demands, or generalized ligamentous laxity may benefit more from anatomical reconstruction [4, 5].

As the location of the ATFL and CFL on the talar and calcaneus were relatively constant, there was less controversy about the selection of ligament insertion sites in the talar and calcaneus. Nevertheless, there are many controversies over the insertion site of ATFL and CFL on the fibula [7–9]. Therefore, there are many disputes about the selection of graft insertion sites in the fibula when reconstructing the ATFL and CFL. Some surgeons selected the insertion site for the ATFL on the fibula as the common tunnel location of both the ATFL and CFL for reconstruction and, drilled a single fibular tunnel for reconstruction [10–13] while others selected the independent insertion site of the ATFL and CFL on the fibula as the tunnel location for reconstruction and, drilled two fibular tunnels for reconstruction [3, 14–17]. Although both fibula single-tunnel reconstruction and fibular double-tunnel reconstruction have achieved satisfactory curative effects [14, 15, 18, 19], there is no such research into a biomechanical comparison of the two reconstruction techniques. Therefore, the present study compared the biomechanical differences between the two techniques and provided a biomechanical basis for clinical application for chronic ankle instability.

Materials and methods

Specimens

Twelve fresh-frozen cadaveric ankle specimens with an average age of 46.2 ± 10.0 years (range, 31 to 60) were used in this study. Specimens were excluded if they had prior ankle ligament tears, surgery or any evidence of prior ankle injury by direct inspection. The criteria for inclusion in cadavers was age older than 20 and younger than 65 years, and no history of cancer as a cause of death. The cadavers were kept at −20℃ and thawed at room temperature for 24 h before use.

Study design and setting

Fresh frozen cadaveric ankle specimens were collected for follow-up, and all procedures were performed by two orthopedic surgeons trained in ligament reconstruction surgery.

Specimens were randomly divided into two groups of 6 specimens:

Group I: fibula single-tunnel for both ATFL and CFL reconstruction and, Group II: fibula double-tunnel for both ATFL and CFL reconstruction.

Plane radiographs were taken under 150 N anterior drawer force (for sagittal plane displacement) and 150 N varus stress force (for coronal plane tilt), in accordance with previous biomechanical studies simulating clinical stress levels [20]. The anterior talar displacement and talar tilt angle were measured at the states of intact, cut and reconstruction of both ATFL and CFL. Then, the reconstructed specimens were loaded to ultimate failure to determine the strength and stiffness of each construct.

Surgical procedure

For all specimens, a curvilinear incision was made posterior to the fibula, extending from the talar attachment of the ATFL to the distal posterior fibula (Fig. 1A). Dissection proceeded through skin and subcutaneous tissues, taking care to identify the inferior extensor retinaculum. Once identified, this structure was retracted, exposing the underlying ATFL. The capsular interval between the anterior inferior tibiofibular ligament and the ATFL was identified and opened, exposing the lateral trochlea of the talus. Then, the peroneal tendon sheath was split along the direction of peroneal tendon with a No. 11 blade, exposing the peroneal tendon and dislocating it anteriorly to clearly expose the CFL (Fig. 1B). Next, we exposed the insertion sites of ATFL and CFL on the fibula, talar and calcaneus. We completely cut off both the ATFL and CFL 0.5 cm apart from the insertion site to the lateral malleolar tip, to cause ankle instability (Fig. 1C-D). When exposing the peroneal tendon, we harvested the anterior half of the peroneus longus tendon about 14.0 cm long as the autograft source for the reconstruction. Both ends of the graft were woven by 2.0 Ethibond (Ethicon, USA) (Fig. 1E).

The fibula single-tunnel reconstruction: the talar tunnel, with a diameter of 7.5 mm and a depth of 20 mm, was drilled on the anterior margin of the talar body at the ATFL attachment area. The fibular tunnel of 5 mm was drilled at the footprint of the ATFL attachment (Fig. 1F). The calcaneal tunnel, with a diameter of 7.5 mm and depth of 20 mm, was drilled at the CFL attachment area. One end of the woven anterior half of the peroneus longus tendon was fixed into the talar tunnel with a 7.0 mm bioabsorbable interference screw (CONMED, USA). Afterwards, the central part of the graft was pulled 10.0 mm into the fibular tunnel and fixed with an Endobutton (Smith & Nephew, USA) on the retro malleolar side of the fibula. The other end of the graft was inserted into the calcaneal tunnel and was tensioned with the ankle in plantigrade position and the subtalar joint in a neutral position. The free end of the graft was fixed into the calcaneal tunnel with a 7.0 mm bioabsorbable interference screw (CONMED, USA) (Fig. 1G).

The fibula double-tunnel reconstruction: the talar tunnel, with a diameter of 7.5 mm and depth of 20.0 mm, was drilled on the anterior margin of the talar body at the ATFL attachment area. Two fibular tunnels with a diameter of 4.0 mm were drilled at the footprint of the ATFL and CFL attachment. The two tunnels were drilled in parallel 45°to the longitudinal axis of fibula (Fig. 1H). The calcaneal tunnel, with a diameter of 7.5 mm and depth of 20 mm, was drilled at the CFL attachment area. The woven anterior half of the peroneus longus tendon was passed through the proximal fibular tunnel and the distal fibular tunnel and, one end of the graft was fixed into the talar tunnel with a 7.0 mm bioabsorbable interference screw (CONMED, USA). The other end of the graft was inserted into the calcaneal tunnel and tensioned with the ankle in the plantigrade position and the subtalar joint in a neutral position. The free end of the graft was fixed into the calcaneal tunnel with a 7.0 mm bioabsorbable interference screw (CONMED, USA) (Fig. 1I). It should be noted that for specimens with double-banded ATFL, we drilled the fibular tunnel at the footprint of the superior-band attachment.

Radiographic measurement

All radiographs were obtained using the same radiographic technique and a calibrated radiopaque marker was placed on each film (Fig. 2A). Radiographic measurements of anterior displacement and varus tilt angle were independently performed by three orthopedic surgeons. Each surgeon conducted measurements on all specimens under the three test conditions (intact, post-ligament transection, and post-reconstruction). The average of the three measurements was used for statistical analysis. To assess interobserver reliability, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated using a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement. Anterior displacement was measured as the perpendicular distance from the posterior lip of the tibial plafond to the posterior aspect of the talar dome on lateral radiographs under 150 N anterior drawer stress, as previously described by Lee et al. [20] and Karlsson et al. [21]. (Fig. 2A). Varus tilt angle was measured between the articular line of the tibial plafond and the talar dome [20] (Fig. 2B). Each radiograph was measured three times by different examiners. The mean measurements from three examiners were used for analyses.

Radiological measurements of the anterior drawer test and varus stress test. A The talar anterior displacement was measured. B The varus tilt angle was measured. Hexagram points to the calibrated radiopaque marker, diameter = 25.0 mm

Plane radiographs with anterior drawer force and varus force were taken using the Telos device (1) after exposing the ATFL and CFL, (2) after cutting off the ATFL and CFL, creating instability of the ankle, and (3) after surgical reconstruction of the ATFL and CFL. The lateral ankle radiographs of the ankle joints were examined under 150 N anterior drawer force in 10° plantar flexion, and the anterior-posterior ankle radiographs were examined under 150 N varus force. The anterior displacement and the tilt angle were measured using the PiViewSTAR PACS software system.

Biomechanical testing

Specimen preparation

Before biomechanical testing, the tibia and all overlying soft tissues were removed. Only the distal fibula and reconstructed ATFL and CFL were preserved. Each specimen was secured onto a customized wooden jig with a steel strap over the dorsum of the foot. The foot was placed in 20° inversion and 10° plantar flexion, to replicate the position of the ATFL (Fig. 3A). A 5.0 mm diameter Steinmann pin was used to make an 80.0 mm deep guiding hole from posterior to anterior into the calcaneus to rigidly fix the hind foot (Fig. 3B). A 5.0 mm diameter Steinmann pin was drilled medial to lateral through the proximal fibula, which was mounted to the load actuator applied by a dynamic testing machine (Bose Electroforce, USA) (Fig. 3A).

Ultimate load testing

The biomechanical testing was measured by the dynamic tensile testing machine (Bose Electroforce, USA). The ligament was gradually preloaded to 15 N for 10 s and, then the load was held at 15 N for 5 s to remove any potential creep. The ligament was pulled to failure at a rate of 20 mm/min. The load and displacement data were noted and the failure mode was recorded immediately after the load to failure. The ultimate load was defined as the maximum load value from the load-displacement curve. Stiffness was calculated as the slope of the linear region of the load-displacement curve corresponding to the steepest straight-line tangent to the curve.

Statistical analysis

The difference of anterior talar displacement and the talar tilt angle between the fibula single-tunnel reconstruction group and the fibula double-tunnel reconstruction group was assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test. The difference in ultimate load failure and stiffness between the two groups was analyzed using one-way ANOVA. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS statistics, version 25.0(IBM, Armonk, New York). A P value of <0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Radiographic measurements

To validate the reliability of radiographic measurements, interobserver agreement was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). The ICCs for anterior displacement and varus tilt angle across the three conditions (intact, post-transection, and post-reconstruction) ranged from 0.992 to 1.000, indicating excellent measurement consistency. After exposing both the ATFL and CFL of the ankle, the mean anterior displacements at 150 N in groups I and II were 5.9 ± 1.4 mm (range 4.7–7.8 mm) and 5.4 ± 0.9 mm (range 4.8–7.1 mm), respectively. The mean varus angles at 150 N in groups I and II were 1.9°± 0.4° (range, 1.4°−2.5°) and 2.4°± 0.6°(range, 1.8°−3.2°), respectively. After cutting off both the ATFL and CFL, the mean anterior displacements at 150 N in groups I and II were 10.5 ± 1.9 mm (range 8.9–13.4 mm) and 9.7 ± 2.2 mm (range 6.8–13.6 mm), respectively. The mean varus angles at 150 N in groups I and II were 11.1°± 2.3°(range 8.3°−14.7°) and 10.8°± 1.9° (range 8.1°−13.5°), respectively. There was no statistically significant difference in the mean anterior displacement or varus tilt angle between the two groups in the intact state or after transection of both the ATFL and CFL.

After the surgical reconstruction of both the ATFL and CFL, the mean anterior displacements at 150 N in groups I and II were 5.7 ± 1.3 mm (range 4.1–7.5 mm) and 5.4 ± 1.2 mm (range 4.3–7.4 mm), respectively. The mean varus angles at 150 N in groups I and II were 2.4° ± 1.2° (range 1.2°−4.0°) and 3.5° ± 1.7°(range 1.2°−6.4°), respectively. There was no statistically significant difference in mean anterior displacement or varus tilt angle between the two groups after reconstruction of both the ATFL and CFL (Figs. 4 and 5).

The magnitude of instability had decreased after the surgical reconstruction in both groups. The decrease was also compared between the two groups. The anterior displacements at 150 N in groups I and II decreased by 4.8 ± 1.4 mm (range 3.1–6.9 mm) and 4.4 ± 2.4 mm (range 1.9–8.8 mm), respectively. The varus angle at 150 N in groups I and II decreased by 8.6°± 2.6° (range 6.2°−13.5°) and 7.3°± 2.3° (range 4.0°−10.2°), respectively. In load-to-failure testing, the mean ultimate loads in Groups I and II were 117.8 ± 23.5 N (range, 92.2–158.8 N) and 119.4 ± 28.1 N (range, 92.5–166.4 N), respectively. The mean stiffness in Groups I and II was 8.6 ± 1.5 N/mm (range 6.7–10.9 N/mm) and 12.8 ± 5.1 N/mm (range 6.6–20.5 N), respectively. There was no statistically significant difference in the mean ultimate loads and mean stiffness between the two groups (Fig. 6) (Tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

Anatomical reconstruction of the lateral ankle ligaments has evolved to address cases where direct repair is not feasible due to poor tissue quality, failed prior repair, or generalized ligamentous laxity. Recent studies have introduced techniques that anatomically reconstruct both the ATFL and CFL using separate fibular tunnels, aiming to restore the native ligament orientation and tension [22–24]. In the present study, we biomechanically compared fibular single-tunnel and double-tunnel reconstruction techniques in a cadaveric model. Our results demonstrated that both methods effectively restored anterior and varus stability after ligament transection, with no significant differences observed between the two techniques. These findings suggest that single-tunnel reconstruction may offer biomechanical equivalence to the double-tunnel method while potentially reducing surgical complexity and tunnel-related complications.

The theoretical basis of fibula single-tunnel reconstruction is that the ATFL and CFL have a fiber connection between the fibula insertion, and the theoretical basis of fibula double-tunnel reconstruction is that ATFL and CFL have independent fibular insertion [7–9]. It was also found that there are many bundle types of ATFL: single type with an isolated band, double type with a divided superior band and inferior band and, multiple type (triple type), with a separated superior, middle and inferior band [25, 26]. It was known that superior ATFL band has anatomically larger diameters than the inferior band in double banded ATFL or multiple/triple banded ATFL, and plays a more important role when the ankle is damaged under plantar flexion and inversion condition [3, 7, 19, 27–29]. Therefore, we selected the fibular insertion of the superior ATFL band as the location in the fibula single-tunnel reconstruction. For the fibula double-tunnel reconstruction, we selected the independent fibular insertion of ATFL and CFL as the location of the two separate tunnels. As far as we know, there is no appropriate standard for the direction of the fibular tunnel. Michels F used spiral CT-scan images of the ankle to measure fibular tunnels in different directions and finally recommended drilling an oblique fibular tunnel because it allows for a longer tunnel and retains more of the surrounding bone [24]. In our study, both the single-tunnel reconstruction group and the double-tunnel reconstruction group used the fibular tunnel at 45° to the longitudinal axis of the fibula. The two fibular tunnels were arranged in parallel in the double-tunnel reconstruction group. If the two fibular tunnels are not arranged in parallel, but intersect with the posterior fibular wall to become a common tunnel exit, the thickness of the bone walls of the two tunnels tends to decrease gradually. In the biomechanical test, the thinner tunnel bone wall easily causes bone wall fracture due to the graft tension, which leads to tunnel failure. Our study confirmed this scenario. Even if we arranged the two tunnels in parallel, and the bone wall between the two tunnels had a predetermined thickness, in our biomechanical tests, 5 of the 6 specimens were presented the bone wall fracture between the two tunnels, which led to the failure of the graft.

Recent studies have explored the biomechanical interplay between lateral ankle ligaments and the distal tibiofibular syndesmosis. Finite element models suggest that the ATFL and CFL may contribute to syndesmotic stability under certain loading conditions [30–32]. Conversely, syndesmotic structures may also influence fibular positioning and thereby affect lateral ligament function. While our cadaveric setup focused on isolating lateral ligament function via the anterior drawer and varus stress tests, future investigations incorporating full weight-bearing or dynamic simulation may better elucidate these multidirectional interactions.

Radiographic measurements showed no statistically significant difference in mean anterior displacement or varus tilt angle between the two groups after reconstruction of both the ATFL and CFL. It means that both fibula single-tunnel reconstruction and double-tunnel reconstruction can reconstruct ankle stability. Although the insertion of ATFL and CFL on the fibular side is independent, we still use the insertion of ATFL on the fibular side as the common insertion of ATFL and CFL for reconstruction. The results shows that there was no significant different from those of the fibula double-tunnel group. Lee et al. analyzed the postoperative locations of the fibula tunnels through 3-dimensional computed tomography (3D-CT) after anatomic ligament reconstruction and showed that patients would gain good results in postoperative ankle stability if the fibula double-tunnel for ATFL and CFL were approximately 52% and 20% of the way from the fibular tip [23]. Park et al. performed a cadaver study, indicated that the location of the fibula single-tunnel for clinical application was the inferior 40% of the distance from articular tip of the lateral malleolus to inferior tip of the lateral malleolus [33]. It can be further speculated that during fibula single-tunnel reconstruction, similar results may occur when the graft insertion is located between the ATFL and CFL attachment. Therefore, we put forward the concept of “safe zone“ (Fig. 8), that is, the result of fibula single-tunnel reconstruction is not different from that of fibula double-tunnel reconstruction when the graft insertion is located in the “safe zone”. The “safe zone” ranges from the ATFL fibula side insertion to the CFL fibula side insertion (including the ATFL insertion, but not including the CFL insertion). Since the CFL insertion is located at the distal end of the fibula, the limited tunnel length and thickness may cause the potential risk of tunnel wall fracture. However, this hypothesis needs to be validated in further experiments.

In the cases of fibula single-tunnel reconstruction, 4 cases of graft failure in biomechanical tests occurred at the sites of tendon bone bonding interfaces in the calcaneus tunnel, and 2 cases occurred at the site of the tendon bone bonding interface in fibular tunnel. It can be seen that the failure site of the single-tunnel reconstruction group usually occurred at the site of the graft bone bonding interface in the calcaneus tunnel (Fig. 7A). In the double-tunnel reconstruction group, the failure site usually occurred on the fibula side, and the fracture of the bone wall between the two tunnels was the most common (Fig. 7B). Although the final failure site of the two groups was different, there were no significant differences in the ultimate failure load tests, which confirmed that either the single-tunnel reconstruction or the double-tunnel reconstruction can obtain ankle joints initial firm fixation.

In the current study, we used the anterior half of the peroneal longus tendon (AHPLT) as the graft. As the dynamic stable structure of the lateral side of the ankle joint, the posterior foot and the lateral arch of the foot, some scholars [18, 34–38] indicate that peroneal longus tendon (PLT) plays an important role in the function of the foot and ankle. In a cadaveric study, Zhao, J and Huangfu X indicated that only removal of the AHPLT on foot and ankle function was minimal; compared with the hamstring tendon, removal of the AHPLT does not need to change the surgical position, which is more convenient for the surgeon [39]. Park et al. confirmed that the removal of the AHPLT had no significant effect on the stability of the ankle joint and the strength of the tendon itself [40]. In the present study, the diameter and length of the AHPLT met the requirements of the ligament reconstruction, and the ultimate failure load and stiffness of the graft were basically consistent with the results of a previous study [39]. It was found that although there was no significant difference in stiffness between the single-tunnel reconstruction and the double-tunnel reconstruction, the mean value of stiffness of the double-tunnel reconstruction was larger than that of the single-tunnel reconstruction. This means that the ability of graft to resist deformation under external forces is stronger in the double-tunnel group than in the single-tunnel group. In addition, it indicates that the graft in double-tunnel reconstruction may be more robust than that in single-tunnel reconstruction when the ankle suffers accidental violence in the early postoperative stage. Although there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups, a larger sample size would have allowed for more precise results.

This study has some limitations. Firstly, the study was the time zero study without the healing effect of the ligament. It is likely that the graft strength will be improved as the ligament and tissue goes through the maturation progress. Secondly, the sample size for each group may be considered as small at 6; however, the statistical analysis defined a reasonable confidence interval. Thirdly, the average age of the cadavers was older than that of patients with typical lateral ankle sprains and requiring surgical stabilization. The tissue quality in these individuals may not be as good as one would expect in patients presenting with lateral ankle instability. Further, using the peroneal tendon as a graft is a limitation, since different tendon grafts may yield different stability results. Moreover, due to fact that the thickness of peroneal tendon autografts varies between specimens, we employed bone tunnels and screws of uniform diameter across all cases to standardize the study protocol - the approach that might potentially affect stability test outcomes.

Conclusion

The first finding is that the radiographic measurement of ankle instability showed no significant difference between the fibula single-tunnel reconstruction groups and the fibula double-tunnel reconstruction groups. The second finding is that the load failure and stiffness also showed no significant differences between the two groups. The ankle lateral ligament reconstruction with fibula single-tunnel can provide as much as the initial stability as fibula double-tunnel.
