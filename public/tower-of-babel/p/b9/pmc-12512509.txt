The association between dietary consumption of amino acids and the risk of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease: a case-control study
Authors: Asieh Mansour, Mohammad Abdollahi, Maryam Mirahmad, Soudabe Motamed, Atie Sadat Khorasanian, Seyed Hossein Mirlohi, Hossein Poustchi, Elaheh Amini, Farnaz Tavakoli, Mohammad Reza Mohajeri-Tehrani, Sayed Mahmoud Sajjadi-Jazi, Azita Hekmatdoost
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFew studies, with inconsistent results, have been conducted to examine the effect of protein and amino acid consumption on non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Therefore, this study aimed to assess the relationship between dietary intake of amino acids or groups of amino acids and the risk of NAFLD.MethodsThis case-control study included 171 participants with NAFLD and 730 controls from Tehran, Iran. A validated Food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) with 168 items, was used to evaluate dietary information. Odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated by regression models, adjusted for potential confounders including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, physical activity, history of diabetes mellitus, and total energy intake.ResultsThe mean ± standard deviation (SD) age of participants was 43.26 ± 13.9 years. Intake of total protein and all amino acids was significantly higher in patients with NAFLD than in the control group (P < 0.001). Increased risk of developing NAFLD compared to the reference quartile was observed in the highest quartiles of dietary isoleucine (OR, 4.72; 95%CI, 1.57–14.19), tyrosine (OR, 5.11, 95%CI, 1.73–15.05), threonine (OR, 3.47; 95%CI, 1.16-10.33), and valine (OR, 4.51; 95%CI, 1.45–14.02) intake. Subgroup analysis by sex revealed that in the females, the OR for NAFLD were 0.36 (95%CI, 0.13–0.98) among those with the highest intake of non-essential amino acids, and 2.78 (95%CI, 1.02–7.50) among those with the highest intake of essential amino acids compared to those in the first quartile. However, there was no significant trend among male cases.ConclusionConsumption of specific amino acids might be associated with odds of NAFLD.

Introduction

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a condition characterized by fat accumulation in the liver, which is not caused by heavy alcohol consumption or other medical causes [1]. The prevalence of NAFLD has been reported to be about 25% throughout the world [2]. The highest prevalence was reported among people with obesity (80–90%), hyperlipidemia (90%), and diabetes mellitus (30–50%) [3].

Two types of NAFLD have been defined; nonalcoholic fatty liver (NAFL), with no hepatocyte damage and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), with evidence of inflammation and injured hepatocytes. NASH may cause fibrosis and may increase the risk of progression to cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma [1, 4].

Obesity, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and metabolic syndrome are the metabolic complications associated with NAFLD [5]. It is suggested that oxidative stress and inflammation are the mediators of metabolic disorders and the initiation of NAFLD [6, 7].

Moreover, unhealthy lifestyle factors are key to the development of NAFLD [3]. The components of an unhealthy lifestyle include reduced physical activity and excessive calorie intake [8]. Although nutrients are parts of a diet and are not consumed separately, some nutrients are known to have an important role in the presence of NAFLD as triggers of oxidative stress and inflammation [9, 10]. For instance, dietary protein and specific amino acids are the suspected dietary factors for hepatocyte fat accumulation and the risk of NAFLD [11]. Additionally, increased levels of circulating branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) and aromatic amino acids (AAAs) in patients with NAFLD have been reported by several studies [12–15]. However, other studies have found no effect or even contradictory effect of protein or amino acid intake on NAFLD [16–18]. The observed controversy can be attributed to some extent the source of the protein, such as animal or vegetable, and its amino acid content [19, 20]. For instance, in a study with 1128 participants, vegetable protein was negatively associated with NAFLD [20]. Evidence has also shown that high intakes of animal protein is associated with NAFLD in overweight elderly individuals [21]. Some other literature represents the relationship between intake of red meat and processed meat with higher risk for NAFLD [22, 23].

Since very few studies have examined the effect of protein consumption on NAFLD, a definitive statement regarding its effect on subjects with NAFLD cannot be made at this time. Considering the conflicting results of studies that examined the association between the protein or amino acids intake and the risk of NAFLD, in the present study we aimed to address the potential association between the intake of amino acids or groups of amino acids and NAFLD.

Methods

This case-control study was carried out among 171 subjects diagnosed with NAFLD and 730 controls. The participants were recruited from two hepatology clinics in Tehran. Candidates underwent a liver Ultrasound by a radiologist within one month prior to participating in the study. The participants were diagnosed with steatosis in hepatic ultrasound and underwent further examination by Fibroscan® (EchoSense, France, 5 MHz). The inclusion criteria for cases were recently diagnosed NAFLD whining previous month by ultrasound and the diagnosis was confirmed by fibroscan using controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) score ≥ 263 dB/m [24].

The convenience sampling method was applied to include the cases. Controls were subjects who were randomly selected from the same clinic patients with available ultrasound within previous month showing no evidence of hepatic steatosis. Data collection from both cases and controls was conducted at the same time. The participation rates for cases and controls were 94% and 98%, respectively. Written informed consent was obtained from cases and controls. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent revisions, and research ethics approval was gained from the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1401.214).

Dietary intake was assessed using a validated 168-item semi-quantitative Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ), administered via face-to-face interviews by trained nutritionists [25]. Daily, weekly, or monthly intakes and also frequency and portion size of each food item were asked from participants and converted to daily intakes. The Nutritionist IV software (First Databank, Hearst Corp, San Bruno, CA, United States) was used to calculate the intake of dietary nutrients. The software analyzes the FFQs and links each item to its corresponding amino acid composition. The total daily intake of protein or each amino acid was calculated by multiplying the portion size and frequency of each food item by its amino acid or protein content and then summing them across all foods to estimate the total daily intake of protein or each amino acid. Each amino acid intake was computed as the percentage of total protein intake. Amino acids were also categorized based on two main factors: essentiality and chemical structure. Essential amino acids are those that the body cannot synthesize and must be obtained through diet. These amino acids include methionine, phenylalanine, tryptophan, histidine, leucine, threonine, valine, isoleucine, and lysine. In contrast, non-essential amino acids can be synthesized by the body and include alanine, arginine, aspartic acid, cysteine, glutamic acid, glycine, proline, tyrosine, and serine. Additionally, amino acids are grouped by their chemical structure into BCAAs (leucine, isoleucine, and valine) and AAAs (phenylalanine, tryptophan, tyrosine). Exclusion criteria were as follows: the subjects with at least 10% incomplete dietary questionnaires and those whose reported energy intake ranged out of 500–5000 kcal/d [26].

Demographic and clinical data (e.g., age, sex, medical history, smoking status) were collected using structured questionnaires. Physical activity was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire and calculated based on metabolic equivalent task (MET) scores [27]. All baseline data and questionnaires were collected by trained researchers to ensure consistency and accuracy in data collection. Body weight and height were measured by trained researcher using calibrated scales and wall-mounted stadiometers, respectively. Measurements followed standardized procedures and were recorded to the nearest 0.1 kg and 0.1 cm, with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using STATA v.12 software (StataCorp LLC.). Characteristics of the participants were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequency and percentages for categorical variables. To compare the baseline characteristics and dietary intakes between cases and controls, t-test was applied for continuous variables and chi-square test for categorical variables.

Amino acid intake values were categorized into quartiles based on cut points among controls. The odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were estimated using multivariable logistic regression models to assess the association between the quartiles of the various amino acid intakes and NAFLD. Logistic regression models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), cigarette smoking status, physical activity, history of diabetes mellitus, and total energy intake. When the linear trend (p for trend) was not statistically significant, non-linear associations were also evaluated using restricted cubic splines and likelihood-ratio tests. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of cases and controls are presented in Table 1. The mean ± SD age of cases and controls were 42.84 ± 11.9 and 43.37 ± 14.4 years, respectively. Mean BMI was significantly higher among NAFLD patients compared with the control group (36.30 ± 11 vs. 27.90 ± 4.5 kg/m2, P < 0.001). Sex distribution of participants was not similar (P < 0.001). 57.3% of cases were male, compared with 37.7% males among controls. NAFLD cases were more smokers (95.3% for cases vs. 13.8% for controls) and had lower physical activity compared with the controls (P < 0.001). Diabetes mellitus was more prevalent in cases compared with controls (22.1% for cases vs. 8.6% for controls; P < 0.001). No significant difference was found in the mean calorie intake of cases and controls.

Table 3 evaluates the association between different types of amino acid intake (dietary BCAAs, AAAs, essential, and non-essential amino acids) and the risk of NAFLD. Higher intake of BCAAs was linked to an increased risk of NAFLD in the total population, with a statistically significant trend (P = 0.036). However, this association was not significant when analyzed by sex. Intake of AAAs showed no significant association with NAFLD in either the total population or by sex. For essential amino acids, a trend toward increased NAFLD risk was observed, especially in women, where the highest quartile of intake (Q4) was significantly associated with higher odds of NAFLD (OR = 2.78; 95%CI: 1.02–7.50), although the overall trend did not reach statistical significance. Individuals in the third quartile of non-essential amino acid consumption had 79% lower odds of developing NAFLD (OR = 0.21; 95%CI: 0.07–0.64). The trend across other quartiles did not reach statistical significance (P for trend = 0.067). Among females, this inverse association was statistically significant, with all intake quartiles showing substantially lower odds of NAFLD compared to the lowest quartile (with ORs of 0.38 [95%CI: 0.15–0.94] for Q2, 0.20 [95%CI: 0.06–0.61] for Q3, and 0.36 [95%CI: 0.13–0.98] for Q4, relative to Q1; P for trend = 0.038).

Table 4 demonstrates the association between intake of specific amino acids and NAFLD risk. The model showed that participants whose dietary isoleucine levels were in the highest quartile were 4.72 times more likely to have NAFLD than those in the lowest quartile (OR, 4.72; 95%CI, 1.57–14.19). Additionally, the highest quartile of dietary tyrosine intake increased the OR of NAFLD to 5.11 (OR, 5.11, 95%CI, 1.73–15.05). The risk of NAFLD was 3.47 times higher in patients at the top quartile of threonine consumption (OR, 3.47; 95%CI, 1.16–10.33). There was a 4.51-fold risk of NAFLD in those in the highest quartile of dietary valine consumption (OR, 4.51; 95%CI, 1.45–14.02). Moreover, significant increasing trends were observed in NAFLD odds across higher quartiles of the dietary isoleucine (P-trend = 0.002), tyrosine (P-trend = 0.002), and valine (P-trend = 0.016). The consumption of remaining amino acids showed no significant association with NAFLD risk.

Discussion

In this case-control study among Iranian adults we found that compared with control group, patients with NAFLD had significantly higher intakes of total protein and amino acids, despite comparable mean calorie intake of the two groups. In accordance with our results, some studies concluded that patients with NAFLD have a diet with a higher content of protein [21, 28, 29].

The quality of protein, including amino acid content and its source, has a significant role in promotion of a healthy diet [30]. Previous studies have shown that the intake of AAA, BCAA, and sulfur amino acids (SAA) was positively associated with fat accumulation in the liver [30] and odds of NAFLD [31]. Our study found that higher dietary intake of tyrosine, threonine, and two amino acids from BCAA (i.e. isoleucine, and valine) was associated with higher odds of NAFLD. Consistent with our result, a Japanese cohort study reported that plasma levels of isoleucine, leucine, valine, tyrosine, and alanine are strongly correlated with dyslipidemia and variables related to visceral obesity and diabetes mellitus [32]. Another epidemiological study found that several dietary amino acids including those which were reported in the current study (i.e. isoleucine, valine, threonine, tyrosine), as well as lysine, alanine, methionine, histidine, and aspartic acid are positively related to triglycerides changes [33]. Baseline plasma valine levels were also reported to predict future risk of liver fat accumulation [34].

However, the results were contrary in a study on elderly subjects. A community-based study conducted in China concluded that higher intakes of AAA, threonine, lysine, and valine were associated with a lower risk of NAFLD in participants aged 65 and over [35]. This discrepancy may be due to differing genetic and environmental factors as well as varying dietary requirements across age groups. A higher protein intake in elderly patients with hepatic steatosis may be required to maintain muscle mass, prevent sarcopenia [36], and improving insulin sensitivity, and NAFLD severity [37, 38]. In our previous study, we also found that low amino acid intake in older adults was linked to an increased risk of mortality [39].

We found an increasing trend in NAFLD odds across higher intakes of BCAAs. In the same way, other studies suggested that increased dietary intake of BCAA from both animal and vegetable resources are associated with liver injury [40].

Some observational studies have reported elevated levels of circulating amino acids in adults with NAFLD/NASH [41–43]. In addition to BCAAs, elevated levels of AAA, alanine, glutamate, and lysine as well as decreased levels of glycine and serine have been reported among patients with NAFLD [44]. It has been proposed that high intakes of AAA, BCAA, and SAA can damage the liver possibly through oxidative stress, which is known as one of the main causes of NAFLD development [6]. In the study of Goffredo et al., BCAA levels were positively correlated with intrahepatic fat content, independent of insulin resistance or obesity [34]. Another study reported that serum BCAA and AAA levels were higher in NAFLD than the healthy control group and were positively correlated with liver fat accumulation [43]. Previous literature has also shown increased levels of essential amino acids including BCAAs, phenylalanine [45], methionine, and non-essential amino acids including serine, glycine, glutamate, glutamine, aspartate, asparagine, and alanine in plasma samples of NASH patients [46]. In another study using Mendelian randomization method, genetically predicted higher alanine and lower glutamine levels were associated with higher risk of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) [47]. This difference in findings may be attributed to the distinct methodologies. Mendelian randomization uses genetic variants to assess the causal effect of lifelong exposure to circulating metabolites, making it less susceptible to confounding from lifestyle factors. In contrast, our observational approach assesses dietary intake via an FFQ, which is subject to recall bias and confounding. Thus, a lack of association with dietary intake does not rule out a causal role for the circulating metabolites themselves. Our results should be interpreted as complementary to those of Mendelian randomization studies, and further research is needed to clarify these associations.

Present study showed that increased dietary essential amino acids intake in females was associated with significantly increased ORs for developing NAFLD. We also found that dietary non-essential amino acids may provide beneficial effects against NAFLD in females. However, such associations were not evident among male participants. Grzych et al. also reported a sex-dependent association between serum BCAA levels and NAFLD severity. While serum BCAA levels increased with the severity of NAFLD in females, they tended to decrease in males [48]. Future studies with larger sample sizes are required to provide further information.

One mechanism suggested for the effect of BCAA on liver injury is that it can activate adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPKα2) and stimulate adipose lipolysis and subsequently elevate free fatty acids (FFA) levels in the plasma and then increase fat droplet accumulation in the liver [40]. BCAA can also inhibit the conversion of FFA to triglyceride and hinder autophagy through activating the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway [40]. Increased hepatic fat accumulation and de novo synthesis of lipids in response to continuing consumption of a diet with high levels of BCAA has been reported by Solon-Biet [49]. It also has been suggested that increased plasma BCAAs might result in insulin resistance in the liver through stimulating gluconeogenesis [50] or sustained activation of the mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) signals, which promotes the inhibitory serine phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS-1) [51].

The current study has some advantages including a relatively large sample size and high participation rate that decrease inter-individual response bias. The second advantage of this study is performing fibroscan to detect the disease, which is the best diagnostic approach for the assessment of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis, after liver biopsy (the gold standard in diagnosing NAFLD) [52]. Moreover, the patients with NAFLD were recently diagnosed with the disease and therefore, they were unlikely to change their dietary habits to manage their disease. Cases and controls were selected from the same clinics and are probably matched in term of socio-economic status.

The limitations of the study include using FFQ for evaluating dietary intake, as the results may be affected by measurement error and recall bias. Another limitation is that there are unknown risk factors for the initiation of NAFLD that cannot be measured or adjusted and might affect the observed association. Moreover, we observed the associations between higher intakes of certain amino acids (such as valine) and increased NAFLD risk were accompanied by wide confidence intervals. This imprecision, likely due to limited sample size, introduces uncertainty regarding the true magnitude of the associations and limits the reliability and immediate clinical applicability of our findings. Therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution and validated in larger, prospective studies. Finally, like any other case-control study, the current study is susceptible to recall and selection bias.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that higher dietary intakes of specific amino acids (tyrosine, threonine, isoleucine, and valine) and BCAAs are associated with an increased risk of NAFLD. The associations for different amino acid groups appear to be sex-specific. In females, higher intake of essential amino acids was linked to an increased risk of NAFLD, whereas non-essential amino acids demonstrated a protective effect. In the total population, a relationship was observed for non-essential amino acids, with moderate consumption being associated with a reduced risk of NAFLD. The public health implication of these findings is that dietary guidance for NAFLD should consider specific amino acid profiles, which may need to be sex-specific. However, because our study is observational, future prospective and interventional studies are required to confirm these associations and establish causality.
