Multiform-based Yi Jin Jing exercise against osteoarthritis of the knee: study protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Zehao Hu, Guohua Chen, Qiuxuan Huang, Zhanxin Li, Peixin Huang, Xiangmin Li, Wenyon Lv, Chuangjian Lin, Danzi Chen, Zenan Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionKnee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a chronic osteoarticular disease characterized by degenerative lesions of articular cartilage, with clinical manifestations of knee pain, stiffness, and limited mobility. Patients with KOA often experience lower limb muscle strength decline and weakened joint stability due to pain, further exacerbating functional disabilities. Yijinjing, a traditional Chinese fitness qigong, features stretching and gentle movements, emphasizing the coordination of limb movements and breathing. This study innovatively combined traditional Yijinjing with Baduanjin and Wuqinxi to design a modified Yijinjing training program including three postures: upright, sitting, and recumbent. The program adopts a stepped training model, adjusting training postures according to patients’ knee joint mobility, and designs targeted movements for common symptoms of KOA, aiming to provide personalized rehabilitation options for KOA patients with different severities.Methods/designIn this study, a single-blind randomised controlled trial was used and a randomised list was generated using a computerised random number generator and opaque sealed envelopes for group allocation were prepared.Strict blinding was followed and outcome evaluators were blinded until the end of the experiment, when participants would not know which group they were included in.The study was conducted on patients aged between 40 and 75 years, with stable medical conditions, who had not practised Yi Jin Jing regularly in the past.These patients were randomly divided into the following 3 groups: (1) Conventional medication group (CG); (2) Traditional Yi Jin Jing exercise group (TG); (3) Modified Yi Jin Jing exercise group (MG); the control group received only conventional medication, whereas the TG and IG groups exercised for 1 h per day on top of the conventional medication for 3 months. One day per week was taken and all groups, except the control group, underwent 1 day of volitional supervised training. WOMAC was the primary outcome variables; the HSS knee score, Time Up and Go Test Active and passive knee mobility and the SF-36 scale served as secondary outcome indicators.DiscussionThe present study may result in a new and widely accepted, easy-to-use, targeted and adaptable exercise prescription for Yi Jin Jing.The prescription encompasses three forms: upright, seated, and recumbent positions, along with stepped exercise patterns and targeted movements. This comprehensive approach is designed to accommodate the varying stages of KOA and the individual conditions of patients, and it is anticipated to address the shortcomings of conventional knee rehabilitation and traditional Yi Jin Jing practices.Trial registrationInternational Traditional Medicine Clinical Registry Platform, ITMCTR2024000234. Registered on 30 July 2024.

Backgroud

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a progressive osteoarthritic disease caused by a variety of factors that lead to degenerative cartilage lesions and secondary osteophytes in the knee joint [1], which are often manifested clinically as knee swelling, pain, stiffness, as well as knee deformity and dysfunction [2].With the changes in the living patterns of the general public, the incidence of KOA has shown a gradual increase in recent years, in which the incidence rate of people over 60 years of age is as high as up to 78.5%, which has a great impact on the quality of life of the patients [3], and has now become one of the major public problems worldwide [4].Currently, treatments for osteoarthritis of the knee include a variety of methods, and in addition to knee-related surgical treatments, other treatments for KOA can be broadly categorised into pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments [5].Surgical treatment is often used as the final option for patients with osteoarthritis of the knee due to its huge healthcare expenditure and reduced quality of life after surgery [6, 7].Commonly used pharmacological treatments for osteoarthritis of the knee, mainly including analgesics, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and corticosteroid injections [8].Although these drugs are effective in reducing pain and improving limb function [9], the safety of their treatment has been controversial [10].Studies have shown [11]that prolonged administration and intra-articular injection of these drugs may cause adverse effects such as gastrointestinal discomfort, multi-organ failure, pain, and swelling.In contrast, a green, non-pharmacological intervention is relatively safe and effective [12].The Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) has now proposed that the preferred treatment for osteoarthritis of the knee is conservative rather than surgical [13], in which exercise therapy in the conservative treatment has been strongly recommended by the OARSI, the American College of Rheumatology (ACR), and the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) [14].More and more studies have shown [15] that traditional Chinese gong fu have a certain degree of safety and efficacy in the prevention and treatment of osteoarthritis of the knee, and more and more doctors are recommending their application to osteoarthritis of the knee.

Traditional Chinese gong fu is a gentle, universally applicable aerobic breathing exercise with isometric contraction, improved stability of the body and flexibility [16].Research has shown that Chinese traditional gong fu is a low to medium intensity exercise with universal applicability, especially suitable for middle-aged and elderly people; it has a positive impact on relieving knee pain, stiffness and other symptoms, and relevant studies have shown that after a period of Chinese traditional gong fu exercise, the muscle strength of the patient’s limbs, the flexibility and stability of the body have been improved to a certain extent, and the quality of life has been significantly improved [17].Today, these Chinese traditional gong fu are gradually spreading all over the world and are now being applied to a wide range of clinical conditions.

Yi Jin Jing [18] is a traditional Chinese martial art that has been passed down from ancient times. Its movements are stretching, soft and well-proportioned, featuring rigidity and flexibility, movement and static. Compared with other traditional gong methods, the movements of Yi Jin Jing are designed to include a combination of limb movements and spinal movements, with special emphasis on spinal rotation, flexion and extension, as well as static and dynamic movements, to ensure the comprehensiveness and diversity of the movements. In addition, during the process of movement, particularly when focusing on coordinating movement with breath, it is essential to integrate these elements effectively. This integration helps to smooth the flow of chi through appropriate movements, thereby maximizing both the physical and mental health of the human body. Furthermore, due to the prolonged and soothing nature of traditional Chinese gong fu, it is easy for practitioners to learn and practice, resulting in a high level of adherence to the practice.Through Yi Jin Jing exercises, muscle strength can be strengthened, limb mobility can be improved, body coordination can be enhanced, and can be used as an alternative to exercise rehabilitation exercises for osteoarthritis of the knee.Although many studies have demonstrated the efficacy of traditional Chinese exercise feats in the prevention and treatment of osteoarthritis of the knee [16], little attention has been paid to the issue of body position for exercise workouts in patients with KOA.Throughout the course of KOA, pain is often the earliest to appear and accompanied throughout. Joint pain inhibits nerve and muscle function, leading patients to adopt a sedentary or supine lifestyle to avoid discomfort while walking. Over time, this predictably results in significant muscle contraction, joint stiffness, and a decline in overall quality of life [19].In addition, there is another issue worth noting regarding why many patients with KOA are prone to recurrent attacks, which is a question that merits further consideration. The pathogenesis of KOA involves several factors, including heavy labor, obesity, aging, and trauma, all of which contribute to knee cartilage damage [20]. In the early stages of KOA, the articular cartilage may still appear intact; however, the hydrophobic binding capacity of the extracellular matrix is altered [21]. This change prevents the cartilage from withstanding significant joint forces, leading to cartilage damage and altering the biomechanical properties of the knee joint [22]. As the disease progresses, the function and integrity of the articular cartilage, meniscus, and infrapatellar fat pads gradually deteriorate. The production of inflammatory factors and the release of inflammatory mediators, such as interleukins (IL-4, IL-8, and IL-15) and tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α), exacerbate the pathology of the articular cartilage. Additionally, the onset of quadriceps weakness, ligament laxity around the knee joint, and abnormal joint force lines, along with an uneven distribution of muscle power, contribute to underlying pathological factors [23]. Although treatment may alleviate symptoms, the poor lifestyle habits of KOA patients often remain unchanged, perpetuating abnormal joint stress, muscle power distribution imbalances, and alterations in the longitudinal axis of the lower limb force lines. Currently, there are fewer applications of Yi Jin Jing in KOA. Most of the changes in muscle strength of the extensor muscle groups in KOA are more significant than those of the flexor muscle groups, which may be related to the selective inhibition of extensor and flexor motor neurones, and this potentially suggests that the differences in distribution of the muscle groups in all directions around the knee should be balanced. This also potentially suggests [29] that the distribution of the muscles around the knee should be balanced to maintain the stability of the knee joint, the coordination of the muscle groups, and the longitudinal axis of the lower limb.Britt Elin Øiestad conducted further research and found [30] that exercise can significantly increase the levels of metabolites such as H+, lactate, adenosine monophosphate, and other substances in muscle cells. Additionally, exercise promotes the secretion of growth hormone by the pituitary gland.The high oxygen consumption by muscle tissues during exercise facilitates the transformation of muscle fibers, increasing the proportion of type I muscle fibers. Methods

Study design

This study was a single-blind, randomized controlled clinical trial comparing three groups: (1) a conventional drug group, (2) a traditional Yi Jin Jing group, and (3) a modified Yi Jin Jing group. All subjects were recruited from the Department of Orthopaedics and Traumatology III at Shantou Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, affiliated with Guangzhou University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. The study protocol was registered with the International Traditional Medicine Clinical Registry Platform on July 30, 2024 (ITMCTR2024000234). Additionally, the study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Shantou Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Guangzhou University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, on May 11, 2024, with approval number 20,230,406. The study is currently in the patient recruitment phase, which started on June 1, 2024, and is expected to end on December 31, 2025. The first participant was enrolled on June 15, 2024. As of the manuscript submission date (June 24, 2025), a total of 70 patients have been recruited. Data analysis has not yet commenced, and the results are anticipated to be published in the winter of 2027.All participants provided written informed consent.

Subjects will be assigned according to a computer-generated table of random numbers and randomly assigned to one of three groups by an independent individual who is blind to the purpose of the experiment and the recruitment process. We will use sealed opaque envelopes to hide the randomly assigned numbers in order to perform blinding. As the intervention in this study is delivered in a motorised manner, it is not possible to complete the blinding for participants and therapists, so we have introduced an outcome assessor to maintain blinding. The time points of the study are detailed in Fig. 2 and the study process is detailed in Fig. 3.The design of this study followed the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines [36].

Study setting and recruitment

The study will be conducted at the third Department of Bone and Trauma of Shantou Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine affiliated to Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine. Relevant recruitment information will be publicised through several social media and municipal advertisements. Through formal and informal invitations, participants’ names, ages, telephone numbers and other relevant information will be registered for further screening. All participants willing to take part in this study will receive our leaflet, which will detail the advantages, rights, benefits, responsibilities and duties of the participants in order to effectively inform the participants. We will invite all participants to complete the test screening at the hospital by telephone in the first instance, and relevant baseline and physical examination information will be recorded in a timely manner for participants who meet the inclusion criteria.Recruitment will continue until the desired sample size is reached.

Sample size

The WOMAC score for joint function and pain is an important indicator for assessing the efficacy of knee osteoarthritis. In the WOMAC score, the minimum difference between groups was 2 points with a baseline standard deviation of 8 points. Given a two-tailed 0.05 and 80% power, the minimum sample size for each group was 50. with an expected shedding rate of 10%, to compensate for the expected loss of participants, an adjusted sample size of 55 per group was available and 165 patients could be recruited.

Participants

All stable KOA patients will be recruited by the third Department of Bone Injury of Shantou Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine affiliated to Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine.Potential participants will be screened through the hospital’s clinical database to determine whether they are eligible for the study by telephone or face-to-face contact. All participants will be assessed by an assessor who is unaware of the purpose and significance of the experiment after signing the consent form. Participants who meet the relevant eligibility criteria will have their demographic, anthropometric and questionnaire information recorded and will be randomly allocated to groups.

The diagnosis of KOA will be based on the criteria established by the ACR [37] and the diagnostic recommendations of the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) [38]. The inclusion criteria for this trial are as follows:

Randomization and concealed allocation

Prior to the baseline assessment, participants will be randomly assigned to three groups: CG, MG, and TG. The allocation sequence will be distributed a priori using ‘learning randomisation’, with a random number generator ( https://www.randomizer.org/) online random number generator, and either participating in the subject recruitment assessment or non-participating investigators. Hidden allocation will be achieved through sequentially numbered, sealed and opaque envelopes kept in a secure cabinet by specially authorised staff, who will be the only ones allowed to open these envelopes.

Blinding

Outpatient physicians (C.G.H and H.Q.X) will perform the initial assessment of outpatients and independently enter and collect information. They will double check the information and correct any errors in a timely manner. The treating physicians (H.Z.H and H.P.X) will perform the initial assessment and group assignment without knowledge. The statistical analysis team will blind the subgroup assignments and the study, in addition to the statistics and analyses of the intervention data, which will be performed independently by multiple physicians. Blinding will occur at the completion of the trial or at the occurrence of a serious adverse event.

Intervention

Regular verbal and visual instruction will be provided to all participants to standardise their movements and enhance understanding. Depending on the specific needs of each experimental group, we will provide the appropriate intervention instruction and require all group members to participate. We also ask all participants to record information about the content of the movements, the duration of the exercise, the intensity of the exercise, the exercise venue, and their personal feelings during or after the exercise. Control group

Members of the CG group will continue to receive their regular medication without any exercise intervention. Following the conventional practices of Western medicine, Meloxicam tablets (produced by Shanghai Boehringer Ingelheim Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd., production batch number: H20020217) are combined with Glucosamine Hydrochloride tablets (produced by Hong Kong Macao Pharmaceutical Manufacturing Co., Ltd., production batch number: HC20160008). Meloxicam tablets are prescribed at a dosage of 7.5 mg per tablet, with a recommended intake of 1 tablet per day, taken orally for a duration of 14 days. Glucosamine Hydrochloride tablets are dosed at 0.75 g per tablet, with a recommended intake of 2 tablets per day—1 tablet in the morning and 1 tablet in the evening—taken orally for a period of one month.

Traditional Yi jin Jing exercise group

TG participants were taking their regular medication in addition to a 3-month exercise programme intervention.The frequency of exercise for the TG was 1 session per day. Specialists from the hospital will form a steering committee to provide weekly training and education sessions to TG participants, with each session lasting 60 min and consisting of the following: (1) a 10-minute warm-up; (2) a 40-minute training session of traditional ekphrasis exercises; and (3) a 10-minute relaxation stretching and cooling-down exercise. Patient monitoring and patient safety are used in the same way as MG.

Modified Yi jin Jing exercise group

MG participants will continue to receive their currently prescribed medications in addition to a three-month exercise program. The MG exercise program consists of one session per day. A steering committee of hospital specialists facilitates weekly training and education sessions, each lasting 60 min. Each session includes (1) 10 min of warm-up exercises, (2) 40 min of modified Yi Jin Jing exercise training, and (3) 10 min of relaxation stretching and cooling exercises. Exercise is conducted safely under the verbal and visual supervision of a physician, with exercise intensity and heart rate monitored to prevent accidents and ensure a smooth trial.

In addition, unlike the traditional Yi Jin Jing workout, the Modified Yi Jin Jing workout adopts a stepped exercise pattern that reflects the characteristics of its various asanas. The three asanas can be interchanged based on knee flexion activity, which serves as the assessment index for conversion. When knee flexion exceeds 80°, the vertical Yi Jin Jing exercise will be performed for three months. If knee flexion is between 40° and 80°, the seated Yi Jin Jing will be utilized. Furthermore, if a patient’s knee flexion increases to over 80° during the treatment period, they may transition to the vertical position on the first day of the following month. The last day of each month will be designated for assessing knee joint flexion. If knee flexion falls below 40°, the horizontal Yi Jin Jing will be employed. Additionally, during the treatment period, if a patient’s knee flexion reaches 40° or exceeds 80°, they can switch to the seated or vertical position on the first day of the next month, with knee flexion activity assessed on the last day of each month. In each movement of the exercise, the modified Yi Jin Jing starts from the pathogenesis of KOA and combines the most common symptoms of clinical KOA patients, such as pain in all directions of the knee joint and difficulty in squatting, to design each movement in a targeted way. This allows patients to focus on the exercise movement according to their own symptoms, and the characteristics of the movement therapy are detailed in Fig. 7. Advice to stay active

Throughout the duration of the trial, participants in both the trial and control groups will be asked to attend an educational course related to the treatment of KOA in order to maintain their interest and motivation. The course will focus on presenting the positive effects of exercise therapy on KOA.All participants are required to attend and register for two online or offline classes per month. The teaching physicians (H.Q.X and H.Z.H) will actively encourage all participants to complete the course and keep detailed records of each individual’s progress. The aim of the course is to motivate participants to take a more active part in the initiation of the trial and the completion of the relevant indicators to facilitate the smooth running of the trial and the collection of data.

Adherence to interventions

In order to ensure that all subjects complete the test, we will organise telephone interviews with experts at least once a week and the number of questions answered will be measured by treatment adherence. We will set the adherence index to be greater than or equal to 85%, i.e. subjects in the CG will be required to answer the questions at least 11 times. For the rest of the intervention group, adherence will be quantified by the number of supervised training sessions organised by the hospital that they attend and the number of daily exercise logs completed. Again, adherence requires completion of at least 85% of the sessions or logs.

Outcome measurement

For participants who completed baseline enrolment, efficacy observations were made at week 0, weeks 4, 8, and 12 before and after treatment, and at 12 weeks of follow-up. Primary prognostic indicators include WOMAC. Secondary outcome measures will include the HSS knee score, the Time Up and Go Test, Active and passive knee mobility and the SF-36 scale.

Outcomes

Primary Efficacy Outcome

The primary efficacy outcome of this study is the WOMAC scale [39]. The WOMAC scale is an internationally recognized assessment standard for KOA, comprising five components: pain, symptoms, activities of daily living, sports function, and quality of life, with a total of 24 items. A higher score indicates more severe KOA symptoms and functional disabilities. Efficacy assessments will be conducted by independent evaluators blinded to group assignments, and participants are required to select the score that best reflects the severity of their symptoms based on their actual condition.

Secondary Efficacy Outcomes

HSS knee score: The function of the knee joint was assessed using the HSS Knee Rating Scale before and after treatment. The total score was 100 points, 30 points for pain, 22 points for function, 18 points for mobility, 10 points for muscle strength, 10 points for knee flexion deformity, and 10 points for stability; excellent: greater than 85 points; good: 70–84 points; medium 60–69 points; and poor: less than 59 points. The higher the score, the better the knee function.Active and passive knee mobility: Knee mobility was assessed before and after treatment through both active and passive knee flexion. Normal knee flexion ranges from 0 to 150°, while normal knee extension ranges from 0 to −10°. A completion time between 10 and 19 s indicates a mild abnormality, suggesting the ability for independent activity. A completion time of 20 to 29 s reflects a moderate abnormality, indicating unstable mobility. A completion time exceeding 30 s is classified as a severe anomaly, signifying a significant activity disorder. Generally, a shorter completion time indicates better functional mobility.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions

After obtaining a signed informed consent form from the participant, we will ask for consent and conduct the experiment and will take into account the individual wishes of the participant. If the participant does not agree and refuses to participate in the experiment, we will respect his/her opinion, postpone or modify the intervention and report the specific reasons. In the event of an adverse event or emergency, we will immediately stop the trial and take proactive measures to provide appropriate treatment and care for patients who experience adverse events during the trial.

Statistical analysis

After collecting all the research data, SPSS v.27.0 software package was used for data analysis. The significance level was established at 0.05, and the limit of confidence interval was set at 95%. A descriptive analysis of the baseline characteristics of the sample will be performed. All quantitative variables will be analyzed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and the Lilliefors correction to determine whether they obey the normal distribution. Normality data are expressed as mean ± SD and skewness data are expressed as median and interquartile intervals. Depending on the normality of the data distribution, the initial homogeneity between the groups was analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal-Wallis. Based on the results of homogeneity and normality analyses, comparison of baseline differences between groups will be performed using one-way ANOVA or non-parametric tests, and intra-group differences will be evaluated using paired T-tests or non-parametric tests. Chi-square tests were used to detect differences between categorical variables.

Subgroup analyses

Based on previous statistical analyses of knee function test results, disease severity may be a potential factor contributing to differences in outcome indicators. Therefore, we considered performing subgroup analyses accordingly. Further subgroup analyses were performed by sitting, standing, and prone positions to determine whether the therapeutic effect of this exercise therapy on KOA varied by body position.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the review conclusions. The systematic review process involves considering several decision nodes for sensitivity analyses, such as methodological flaws, small-scale studies, and missing data. Following the recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook, sensitivity analyses consist of two steps: the first step includes all significant meta-analysis research, and the second step incorporates studies known to be eligible. A summary table presenting the findings of the sensitivity analyses will be provided. The review process will also address the risk of bias based on the results of the sensitivity analyses.

Safety

All exercise must be completed under the supervision of a designated physician and requires simultaneous monitoring of the patient’s heart rate. Once the patient’s heart rate reaches 80 per cent of their reserve heart rate, they will be asked to pause the exercise, continue to monitor their heart rate and may continue to participate in the trial until their heart rate stabilises. If the patient feels any discomfort, they can request an interim test and receive the most prompt treatment. In the event of an emergency, such as chest pain, fracture, strain, dyspnoea, nausea or dizziness, the patient will be asked to discontinue the trial immediately. The experimenter will record the patient’s condition and make a definitive judgement.

Data collection and management

We will begin with baseline data collection and a baseline assessment prior to randomisation. All data and results will be recorded on paper in a uniform format and converted to electronic files by a dedicated research assistant. Subsequently, a second research assistant will re-enter the same data and compare it with the data entered by the first research assistant to verify the completeness of the data and to detect and correct errors in a timely manner. When challenging situations are encountered, the data specialist and project manager will discuss and resolve the problem.

In order to manage the data efficiently and ensure confidentiality, we will identify individual subjects only by their trial identification number. Collected data will be converted into electronic format and stored on a password-protected server to protect the confidentiality of the data, and the corresponding paper files will be stored in locked filing cabinets. Only authorised researchers will have access to the data. Each participant’s identity will be hidden and identified with a unique number to prevent statisticians from discovering personal information and group assignments. The result will be a set of data that prevents any personal data from being displayed to ensure confidentiality. The examiner will supervise the measurement of the questions.

Discussion

Knee pain and impaired mobility are significant symptoms of KOA. As the condition progresses, many patients experience varying degrees of deterioration in limb function, muscle atrophy, and alterations in the force distribution of the lower limbs. Consequently, restoring limb function and realigning the lower extremity force distribution to a balanced state is essential in the treatment of knee osteoarthritis, as it plays a crucial role in improving clinical outcomes.

This study presents several notable features. Firstly, the research intervention involved a modified version of Yi Jin Jing, which was innovatively developed in comparison to the traditional Yi Jin Jing. This modified version includes two new postures—sitting and lying—that are not found in the traditional practice. These options allow patients with severe knee impairments, particularly those who are bedridden for extended periods, to select the most suitable posture for rehabilitation, a flexibility not offered by other exercise therapies. Secondly, in addition to enhancing overall balance and coordination, the three asanas of the improved Yi Jin Jing specifically incorporate movements beneficial to the knee joints, drawing from traditional Chinese gongfu methods such as Ba Duan Jin and Wu Qin Xi. This approach addresses the distribution of muscle groups in the lower limbs and balances the force lines, ensuring that the targeted exercises not only contribute to health maintenance but also effectively alleviate pain and dysfunction in patients with KOA. Thirdly, the movement design of the improved Yi Jin Jing is rooted in the pathogenesis of KOA and is tailored to the most common symptoms experienced by clinical KOA patients. Each movement is designed with specific targeting in mind, allowing patients to focus on exercises that correspond to their individual symptoms. This targeted approach enhances the treatment’s focus while adhering to the general principles of rehabilitation. Fourth, the modified Yi Jin Jing incorporates various postures—sitting, lying, and standing—resulting in an exercise mode that differs from conventional practices. It employs a stepped exercise model, with knee mobility serving as the primary assessment index. Based on changes in mobility throughout the treatment period, patients can transition from lying to sitting, and from sitting to standing. This stepped approach offers patients a more optimal exercise program tailored to their needs. Fifth, we conducted subgroup analyses of patients with varying degrees of illness to investigate potential differences in the therapeutic effects of sitting, lying, and standing postures. The goal was to identify the best combination of options to optimize therapeutic outcomes. Sixth, multiple indicators were utilized to comprehensively assess the pre- and post-treatment status of stable KOA patients, with particular emphasis on limb motor function. Finally, this study was conducted in strict accordance with CONSORT guidelines [41] to explore the therapeutic effects of modified Yi Jin Jing on patients with stable KOA.

Nevertheless, our study still has several limitations. Firstly, all participants were recruited from a single medical center, and thus the extrapolation of results to populations with different geographical locations, cultural backgrounds, and levels of medical resources may be subject to variations. The acceptance of traditional Chinese exercises is influenced by regional cultures, yet this study did not systematically evaluate the impact of urban-rural differences, educational levels, and awareness of traditional medicine on intervention adherence, which may undermine the generalizability of the conclusions. Secondly, exercise therapy, particularly traditional Chinese exercises, imposes requirements on participants’ physical fitness and psychological status. For instance, factors such as participants’ physical fitness levels and the coordination between breathing and movements may affect the final therapeutic efficacy, and such differences may introduce individual heterogeneity in treatment outcomes. Thirdly, due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and therapists was not feasible, which might amplify the improvement in subjective indicators through the expectation effect. Although independent outcome assessors were employed, they could hardly completely isolate information contamination between groups, thereby affecting the integrity of the study and the retention rate of participants, leading to early withdrawal of some samples from the study. Finally, given that this study focuses on exercise interventions in a home setting, patient compliance may pose challenges. To ensure that patients can perform exercises according to the established plan, the research team will conduct regular communication and supervision through remote video calls and telephone calls, carry out weekly educational courses, and perform regular assessments to evaluate the effectiveness of the exercise intervention.

The primary objective of this study is to examine the effects of modified Yi Jin Jing on limb motor function and symptoms in patients with KOA. This will be achieved through a clinical trial that evaluates three different forms of modified Yi Jin Jing—vertical, seated, and horizontal—as stability interventions for individuals with KOA. Additionally, we will assess the impact of a single protocol that includes traditional Yi Jin Jing and conventional medication, as well as the advantages and disadvantages of a combined protocol. The findings are anticipated to provide clinicians and therapists with a straightforward, safe, and effective new approach for KOA rehabilitation.
