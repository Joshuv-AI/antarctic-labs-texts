A qualitative study exploring factors associated with the accuracy of recording malaria rapid diagnostic test results in two high-burden regions of Côte d’Ivoire
Authors: Orphée M A Kouakou, Valérie A Bédia-Tanoh, Annie Arnzen, Abibatou Konaté-Toure, Saadjo Sow, Antoine M Tanoh, Michael Humes, Kevin Griffith, Kim A Lindblade, William Yavo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile the expanded use of malaria rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) in Sub-Saharan Africa has improved testing rates, healthcare workers do not always adhere to results or report them accurately. Because misrecording RDT results compromises malaria surveillance, this study identified factors associated with providers’ guideline adherence and accurate RDT reporting in in two high-burden regions in Côte d’Ivoire.MethodsThis qualitative study was embedded within a mixed-methods evaluation of RDT reporting and recording accuracy called the Malaria RDT Capture and Reporting Assessment (MaCRA). Healthcare workers performed RDTs for patients with suspected malaria and recorded interpretations in registers. RDTs were photographed and independently read by an external panel; agreement was measured using Cohen’s kappa. Sixteen healthcare workers (eight high agreement, eight low agreement) with ≥ 75 recorded results were purposively selected across regions and districts. Semi-structured interviews in French covered RDT knowledge, perceived self-efficacy for performing and interpreting RDTs, perceived risks, subjective norms, guidelines, and health system context. Blinded analysts coded transcripts in NVivo using deductive and inductive approaches to compare themes between groups.ResultsPerceptions of RDT reliability, particularly confidence in negative results for symptomatic patients, was the primary differentiator between groups. Low-agreement participants more frequently feared false-negative results and severe consequences of failing to provide antimalarials, contributing to prescribing antimalarials after a negative RDT. Several described deliberate misrecording to align registers with treatment decisions, including changing negative results to positive or omitting antimalarial documentation to avoid scrutiny. Conversely, some high-agreement participants documented these cases as “presumed” malaria rather than falsifying results.ConclusionsDifferences in guideline adherence and RDT recording were driven by risk perceptions and confidence in results rather than a lack of procedural knowledge, guideline access, or supply constraints. Interventions should target clinical decision-making for RDT-negative fevers through supportive supervision, expand non-malarial diagnostic options, and strengthen documentation systems to record non-adherence transparently and without incentivizing misreporting.

Background

Malaria remains endemic in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), with the World Health Organization (WHO) African Region alone accounting for around 95% of cases worldwide, estimated at 265 million cases in 2024 [1]. Thanks to a combination of strategies to reduce the burden of malaria in this region, deaths due to malaria fell from 804,000 in 2000 to 579,000 in 2024 [1].

Reducing the number of cases and deaths from malaria requires appropriate case management, which is based on parasitological confirmation of malaria in patients with suspected infection. WHO recommends antimalarial treatment only for patients who test positive by rapid diagnostic test (RDT) or microscopy, and further evaluation for patients who test negative to determine the cause of their febrile illness, without prescription of antimalarial drugs [2].

Although all malaria-affected countries in SSA include the WHO recommendation in their national guidelines, over-prescription of antimalarial drugs to people with a negative RDT test is widespread [3–9]. Reported determinants of prescription non-adherence include health system constraints (for example, diagnostic or antimalarial stock-outs, inadequate training and supportive supervision, and limited storage capacity), socio-economic considerations (such as perceived risks and consequences of delayed treatment in settings with limited financial resources), and political and historical factors (including limited acceptance of health reforms by providers). In addition, perceptions of treatment failure and the absence of alternative diagnostic options for febrile illness have been cited as reasons for prescribing antimalarials to RDT-negative patients [8, 10]. Prescription adherence is also heavily influenced by trust in the RDT itself. Healthcare workers may disregard results due to documented RDT limitations, including a limit of detection that excludes very low-density infections [11], parasite deletions of genes that encode the histidine-rich protein 2 (HRP-2) antigen [12], and RDTs that detect only a single species [13]. Additionally, the persistence of HRP-2 antigen post-treatment can lead to prolonged false positives, further undermining provider confidence in RDT results [13, 14]. Overall, prior evidence suggests that even when healthcare workers are capable of following guidelines, contextual constraints including system-level factors such as supplies and stock, workload, and supportive supervision, can shape how RDTs are used and how results are acted upon [10].

In malaria-affected countries in SSA, accurate malaria surveillance depends on correct recording of parasitological test results in health facility registers. However, healthcare workers may misrecord RDT results purposefully to align register results with their clinical treatment decisions. In a mixed-methods observational study conducted in Benin, Côte d’Ivoire, Nigeria and Uganda, the accuracy of recording and reporting of malaria RDT results was assessed [15]. Negative RDT results were more frequently recorded as positive than the reverse, a pattern suggestive of deliberate misrecording rather than random error. This paper presents findings from the qualitative component of that study, focusing on determinants of provider prescription adherence to, and recording of, RDT results in the study healthcare facilities.

Methods

Study sites

Côte d’Ivoire is administratively divided into 33 health regions and 113 health districts. Two health regions, Kabadougou and Mé, were selected for this study based on their reported high levels of malaria transmission. Mé is located in southern Côte d’Ivoire, where transmission is high (200–299 cases per 1000 population per year [ppy]) and perennial. Kabadougou (≥ 300 cases per 1000 ppy) is located in northern Côte d’Ivoire, where transmission is more seasonal, with incidence peaking during the long rainy season (approximately 6–8 months) (Tanoh M, pers. comm.). In Côte d’Ivoire, between 92 and 100% of malaria infections are caused by Plasmodium falciparum [16, 17].

This study was conducted in public primary healthcare facilities where malaria diagnosis relied solely on RDTs. In these facilities, RDTs are routinely performed by nurses, midwives, and orderlies. Facilities performed an average of 3 to 15 RDTs per day during the study period. All RDTs used in public health facilities during the period of this study had a single test line that detected P. falciparum HRP-2 [12]. The two most common RDT products were AdvDx Malaria Pf (Advy Chemical, Mumbai, India) and First Response Malaria Pf (Premier Medical Corporation Ltd, Gujarat, India) [18].

Within each region, two districts were purposively selected for the study and all primary healthcare facilities that reported an average of at least 50 RDTs per month for at least nine months for each of the two previous years were eligible. Healthcare facilities were divided into four strata based on the medians of high/low patient volume (based on the number of outpatient visits) and high/low average RDT positivity rate (TPR) for 2021–2022. In each stratum in each district, one healthcare facility was randomly selected. In total, 16 healthcare facilities were selected for the initial study, with eight from each region and four from each district.

Study design

Over a five-month period, all RDTs performed in participating health facilities were photographed and linked to the corresponding entry in the health facility register and to the healthcare worker who recorded the result [18]. RDT photographs were independently interpreted by an external panel of trained RDT readers, who recorded their results for each cassette. Healthcare workers who had conducted at least 75 RDTs during the period were eligible for inclusion in the qualitative component. Agreement between eligible healthcare workers and the external panel was quantified using Cohen’s kappa and healthcare workers were categorized into terciles based on their kappa scores. Details related to measurement of agreement can be found in a previous publication [18]. Based on the concept of saturation, the number of healthcare workers selected for in-depth interviews was 16 [19].

Purposive sampling was used to select eight healthcare workers from the highest and lowest terciles, ensuring an equitable distribution between the two regions and four districts. Selection of healthcare workers was conducted by an investigator (KAL) outside Côte d’Ivoire using anonymized identification numbers, and in-country research staff were blinded to participants’ agreement category until coding had been completed.

Data collection

Sociologists on the research team developed a semi-structured interview guide to examine individual factors (namely healthcare workers’ knowledge of RDT administration, perceived self-efficacy to perform and interpret RDTs, perceived risks associated with relying on RDT results for case management, subjective norms surrounding the use and reporting of RDT results), and health system factors (i.e. supplies and stock, workload and timing of RDT completion, supportive supervision, influence of perceptions on compliance with RDT results in processing and reporting) that could affect accurate recording and reporting. Using this guide, sociologists conducted in-depth interviews with selected healthcare workers in French, which were audio-recorded using a digital recorder.

Data analysis

Analysis was conducted by two sociologists who were not involved in data collection. Interviews were transcribed, imported into NVivo 14, and coded line by line using a pre-established coding manual (deductive approach). Codes were added to capture emergent themes not covered in the initial codebook (inductive approach). Throughout coding, analysts were blinded to participants’ agreement category (high versus low) and to which participants were in the same group. The analysis identified and interpreted key themes and compared findings between healthcare workers with high and low agreement. Theme organisation was guided by a conceptual framework developed from the literature and behavioural theories (Rational action theory, Theory of Planned Behavior) [20]. Results are presented according to the framework’s main domains: individual, guideline, and health system characteristics. Specific quotes from participants that were chosen for this report were translated into English using an online translator (DeepL, DeepL SE, Cologne, Germany) and then verified for accuracy by a bilingual staff member. Back-translation was performed.

Ethical considerations

All healthcare workers provided written informed consent to link their personal data to the RDT results they recorded. Healthcare workers selected for in-depth interviews provided additional written informed consent. Ethical approval for the protocol was obtained from the Comité National d’Ethique des Sciences de la Vie et de la Santé in Côte d’Ivoire (N° 057–23/MSHPCMU/CNESVS-km/ April, 26, 2023) and the WCG (formerly Western, Cary, NC USA) institutional review board (IRB) (No. 20231373).

Results

There were 107 healthcare workers with at least one RDT observation in the dataset, and 38 who performed at least 75 RDT measurements during the study period. The healthcare workers in the lowest tercile had kappa scores between 0.32 and 0.80, while those in the highest tercile had scores between 0.88 and 0.97.

All 16 invited healthcare workers agreed to participate. Recruited from 13 of the 16 participating health facilities, the sample included seven orderlies, seven nurses, and two midwives (5 female, 11 male). Among participants in the high-agreement group, two were orderlies and six were nurses. In the low-agreement group, five were orderlies, two were midwives, and one was a nurse. Two healthcare workers (one midwife and one orderly) had less than 1 year of experience delivering malaria control services; four had between 1 and 4 years of experience (three nurses, one orderly), and 10 had 5 years or more (five nurses, one midwife, and 4 orderlies). Professional experience tracked closely with level of agreement: five of the eight healthcare workers in the high-agreement group had ≥ 5 years of experience, compared with three out of eight in the low-agreement group. Because this qualitative analysis focused strictly on participant perspectives and experiences, data from all 16 individuals were included.

Individual-level factors

Healthcare workers’ knowledge of RDT administration

Healthcare workers accurately described the key procedural steps for performing and reading RDTs, regardless of agreement category. Most participants defined an invalid test as one without a control line, with one exception in the low-agreement group.

When asked how long to wait before reading results, responses varied. Several participants, more commonly in the low-agreement group, described variable or incorrect waiting times, suggesting uncertainty around timing. For example:

“Normally, as I was saying, it’s from the minute you started, if it’s been too long it’s 15 minutes, often 5 minutes, the result is out, 10 minutes the result is out, 3 minutes the result is out, normally it depends on the person’s blood, and then on the buffer, too...Yes, all malaria RDTs I think it’s like that.” (Low agreement)

Several participants in the high agreement group mentioned following the instruction manual to determine the time required:

“I read the RDT leaflet when we open a new box and follow the instructions: the number of drops of reagent, the time needed to obtain the result. After taking all this information, I clean the patient’s middle finger, let it dry, prick his finger using the lancet, collect the blood and place it in the well indicated on the cassette, put in the number of drops indicated by the manufacturer and wait the time indicated in the leaflet and read the result.” (High agreement)

Perceived self-efficacy to perform and interpret RDTs

Most participants reported confidence in their ability to interpret RDT results and did not describe major difficulties. One participant in the low-agreement group reported uncertainty when test lines were faint and described seeking confirmation:

“Often, we see RDTs with very fine lines, so we’re in doubt whether it’s positive or negative. When it’s like that, I take the nurse’s advice.” (Low agreement)

Perceived risks associated with relying on RDT results for case management

Participants in both agreement groups described advantages of RDTs, but perceived risks were raised more frequently by low-agreement participants. Concerns centred on possible false-negative results in symptomatic patients, and the potential consequences of withholding antimalarial treatment:

“… for example: You see a patient who has all the signs of malaria and his RDT is negative. A few days later, when you find the patient, it’s not a pretty sight; it’s as if you’ve done a poor job.” (Low agreement)

“ If you want to rely on RDT results, you give the patient a paracetamol and tell him to come back afterwards, he’s not going to come back.... This can lead to death if you’ve told the patient that it’s not malaria. He’s going to stay at home, he could die.” (Low agreement)

Some high-agreement participants exhibited advanced knowledge, including the limitations of RDT sensitivity at low parasite density and potential for RDTs to miss non-falciparum infections:

“With experience, too, we have understood that when the patient also has malaria, if the level of Plasmodium is also not high, it is often difficult for the RDT to show us the right result. An example: you do someone’s thick blood smear and maybe he has 40 trophozoites, but often you do the RDT it can be negative. But that doesn’t mean he doesn’t have malaria.... There is also the fact that RDT does not detect other types of plasmodia. What is widespread is the RDT which detects Plasmodium falciparum while there are 4 types of plasmodia. If a person has the other types of plasmodia in the body, the RDT will not be able to detect it, and the person still has malaria.” (High agreement)

Overall, confidence in RDTs to detect malaria varied across participants, including within agreement groups. Some participants described high trust in RDTs based on personal experience:

“When the RDT is positive, it’s malaria. When we start malaria treatment, on the third day, the patient comes back to us, cured, hence the effectiveness of the RDT...The result of the RDT itself is a compass. When we were starting out in medicine, we used to treat malaria on the assumption that maybe it wasn’t malaria, and you’d give medicine, but now we have a compass, we have an element that says if you do this and it’s positive, it’s malaria, when you do the RDT and it’s positive, the treatment that follows is 100% cured, that’s the first factor of effectiveness for me, of the RDT.” (High agreement)

“In my career as a civil servant, I’ve seen that RDTs have saved many cases and enabled good treatments to be carried out. All RDTs where malaria was suspected were always positive. After the RDT and the treatment, the results gave me confidence in the RDT. It is an effective and accurate diagnostic tool.” (Low agreement)

Subjective norms surrounding the use and reporting of RDT results

Most healthcare workers noted that the choice of whether to treat a patient following an RDT was entirely their own, uninfluenced by colleagues or patients:

“Yes, I’ve already had this case... I didn’t do the treatment! No one comes to tell me how to provide care. I am responsible for what I do and full responsibility lies with the person who stamped the prescription. I handle my cases myself; I don’t make my prescriptions according to anyone’s recommendations.” (Low agreement)

“Yes, they [the patients] come often, they even ask for the ACTs, and we say we cannot give the ACTs. At that point, I tell them, to give the ACTs, you first have to have a fever: you come, I register you, I do your RDT, if it’s positive, I give it to you, if it’s negative, I am looking further, otherwise I don’t give it.” (High agreement)

The only exception was a healthcare worker in the low-agreement group who mentioned that the nurse may accede to patients’ wishes:

“Yes! They [the patients] say that when they take the tablets, in a month or a week, the same illness comes back, and we have had cases here. There are children who, when you give them the tablets, do not drink it, they vomit, but they have to drink it. But when they finish the three injections, they get better. So, the nurse gives them the injections and then gives them the tablets... When he does not give the injections, the patients criticize him. They say he is not competent.” (Low agreement)

Guideline-related factors

Participants in both agreement groups reported that guidelines were available, understandable, and useful for diagnosis and case management:

“We have had the directives for a long time. Here we have just rehabilitated and it was only after our last training that we were given the new directives. The last guidelines we were given was at the last training in 2023. And we have a document. Each department has a document where the guidelines are located.” (High agreement)

“When we went to the Cascade Hotel for the training, we received the instructions.” (Low agreement)

“Yes, we understand very well.” (High agreement)

“Indeed, everything is clear.” (Low agreement)

System-level factors

Supplies and stockouts

Participants in both agreement groups generally described adequate availability of RDTs and required consumables:

“The material is there; everything is available.” (High agreement)

“When we find we’re almost out of stock, we make the order we don’t wait. We make sure we’re never out of stock.” (Low agreement)

However, some participants reported periods of RDT or artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) stock-outs and described reverting to presumptive management when diagnostic capacity was limited:

“If, so! there aren’t any at the moment, but there are the ACT. At one point even the ACT were finished; but we haven’t had any RDTs for almost a month now, and we’re now basing our treatment on signs. So, if there’s no RDT, I base my treatment on signs, but there are ACTs”. (High agreement)

“Currently we have RDT, normally there is a drug shortage.” (Low agreement)

Participants also described limited availability of diagnostic options for further evaluation and the use of referral or external testing:

“We don’t have any other materials but we refer to the symptoms the patient presents...if it’s necessary to refer we do.” (Low agreement)

“Apart from the RDT, we don’t have anything else to test for malaria. But we can ask for a biological test, such as a thick blood smear. But this is done in Madinani [a larger city in the region].” (High agreement)

Workload and timing of RDT completion

Most participants reported that patient volume did not compromise their ability to perform RDTs:

“Enough time...Anyway it’s fine, it’s fine with me. Well, I mean I feel comfortable, I have no problems, everything’s going well.” (Low agreement)

“Whatever the season...at any time, there are no problems, we have enough time to carry out the RDTs.” (High agreement)

One participant described how workload could lead to reliance on clinical signs:

“Yes, because there are often a lot of patients and when I’m alone, there are several tasks at the same time... I take 30 minutes per person... It’s tiring, the others are impatient and start to sulk; This panics the healthcare worker who is forced to rely on the signs to move forward.” (Low agreement)

Supportive supervision

Most participants reported some supervision related to RDTs. Descriptions suggested that supervision often focused on test performance, and sometimes extended to broader case management:

“Yes, the district does sporadic supervision. When we receive supervisors, they sit down and look at us: when we have a case of malaria, they try to see how the case management is done; from the interrogation to the RDT technique and even to the prescription of medication. If they notice any shortcomings, they let us know so that we can improve”. (Low agreement)

“I was saying that we have received RDT execution supervision visits, but these are district visits... they attend to see how we use the RDTs and if there are any small failures after the patient has left, we try to correct them.” (High agreement)

“He makes corrections on my performance of the RDTs. For the reading of the RDT, since we did not follow the 15-minute waiting time, he advised me a lot on this.” (Low agreement)

Some participants, all in the low-agreement group, reported no specific supervision on RDT performance:

“I don’t remember if there was any supervision of RTD execution. In any case, I’ve never been supervised specifically on RTD execution.” (Low agreement)

Influence of perceptions on compliance with RDT results in processing and reporting

Participants described different approaches to managing patients with malaria-like symptoms and a negative RDT result. Some, more often in the low-agreement group, reported providing antimalarial treatment while pursuing referral or further evaluation:

“When the RDT is negative and the patient shows signs of malaria, I treat the malaria at the same time.” (Low agreement)

“Normally, we treat malaria... it’s true that we’ve done the RDT and the result is negative, but we treat it first, now we give the person an examination report, he goes to Odienné [a nearby town where there are health establishments with a better technical platform] to have more examinations there.” (Low agreement)

Others, more often in the high-agreement group, reported avoiding antimalarial treatment when the RDT was negative and pursuing alternative diagnoses or further testing:

“But we don’t treat a patient with a negative RDT! We treat other pathologies...before, we worked in ignorance, we worked on presumption... If the patient has all the signs of malaria and the RDT is negative, it’s not malaria...there’s a national protocol that we follow. If the RDT is positive, I treat him, if it’s not positive, I direct my diagnosis elsewhere...I can ask for a thick blood smear, he’ll go and do it in Odienné and send me the result.” (High agreement)

However, even among high-agreement participants, treatment decisions were described as potentially shaped by perceived patient financial constraints:

“Once the RDT is negative, you yourself see signs in the patient that show that no, the patient has malaria. But on the RDT it’s not displayed, so you see he has fever, he has altered taste, when we do the RDT it’s always negative, so we give him a three-day treatment, or, if he has the means he’ll do a blood test to see.” (High agreement)

Participants in the low-agreement group only described two approaches when prescribing antimalarials despite a negative RDT: altering the recorded test result, or recording the negative result while omitting the antimalarial treatment from documentation:

“I mention in my register that the RDT is positive, knowing that it’s negative on the RDT, so that I can do the treatment; because you can’t report a negative RDT, straight away the supervisors will ask you what you based the [clinical] management on... So, we make the RDT positive to [be able to provide] case management.” (Low agreement)

“You can’t have a negative RDT, then mention in the negative register that you gave an antimalarial! It’s just not possible. However, in our own way, we will treat, as we say, the other symptoms, but the antimalarial we give will not be mentioned, because this could be prejudicial to us.” (Low agreement)

One high-agreement participant described how a healthcare worker he was supervising had misrecorded RDT results:

“Often, I take an example: The guy is so tired: RDT done? He writes yes. Positive or negative? He marks negative, but he gives malaria treatment. But when we do supervisions, at the time we make the reports, we see these cases. We ask him: How can you treat malaria when the RDT is negative? He answers: I was tired. Here we know each other. He justifies: It was positive, I wanted to mark positive but I marked negative. We correct it.” (High agreement)

High-agreement participants who reported treating malaria despite a negative RDT described documenting this as “presumed” malaria:

“In the report, it says ‘presumed’. It is at this level that ‘presumed malaria’ is used to justify ACTs taken from the stock.” (High agreement)

Discussion

This qualitative study was embedded within a mixed-methods evaluation of malaria RDT recording and reporting accuracy in four countries, which found that negative RDT results were more frequently recorded as positive than the reverse, a pattern suggestive of deliberate misrecording rather than random error [18, 21]. In Côte d’Ivoire, healthcare workers were purposively selected from the highest and lowest terciles of agreement with an external panel to identify why some recorded RDT results more accurately than others. In-depth interviews were designed to clarify whether knowledge, perceptions, or contextual factors differed between the two groups and could plausibly explain observed differences in RDT recording accuracy.

Across both agreement groups, healthcare workers accurately described the key steps required to conduct and interpret an RDT, and they reported that guidelines were both available and understandable. Health system context, including the availability of RDT supplies, workload, and the presence of supervision, was also described as broadly similar across groups, despite reports of episodic stock-outs and variable supervision. These findings suggest that behavioral differences, such as misreporting RDT results, were not primarily driven by a lack of guideline access or routine commodity shortages.

Instead, the most salient differences between groups related to perceptions of RDT reliability, particularly confidence in negative results among patients with malaria-like symptoms. Low-agreement healthcare workers more frequently described the risk of false-negative results and severe consequences if malaria were missed. Under these conditions, clinical judgement and risk avoidance tended to override RDT findings, leading to antimalarial treatment despite a negative test. Similar concerns about the consequences of non-treatment after a negative test have been reported in other settings, where perceived unreliability of RDTs contributes to prescription non-adherence [8, 10, 22]. Conversely, participants who expressed greater trust in RDTs described positive prior experiences with RDT-guided case management and more often articulated guideline-concordant responses to negative tests, including pursuing alternative diagnoses or referral for further testing. Findings consistent with this pattern have been described previously, including perceptions of RDTs as a tool that supports more accurate diagnosis and reduces treatment errors [10].

The interviews further suggested that providers often use their clinical judgment and disregard RDT results in favor of their own prescribing decisions. Even among some high-agreement participants, treatment of malaria after a negative RDT was described when symptoms were perceived as highly suggestive of malaria, particularly when patients lacked resources for additional testing. This aligns with prior work indicating that prescription non-adherence to RDT results can coexist with beliefs in exceptions, including perceived treatment failure, low density malaria infections, or other circumstances in which RDTs are considered insufficient [10]. Such exceptions may be reinforced in primary care settings where diagnostic options for non-malarial febrile illness are limited. In this study, participants commonly reported few alternatives beyond referral or off-site microscopy testing. However, because similar constraints were described by both agreement groups, limited diagnostic alternatives alone did not explain differences in their accuracy of recorded RDT results.

A key contribution of this study is the explicit linkage between case management decisions and recording practices. Several participants in the low-agreement group, but none in the high agreement group, described how they either modified register entries themselves, or omitted documentation of antimalarial treatment, when they treated malaria despite a negative RDT. These practices were explained as responses to anticipated scrutiny and potential consequences if registers showed antimalarial prescriptions for RDT-negative patients. This mechanism provides a plausible behavioural pathway by which non-adherence of antimalarial prescription to RDT results can directly degrade surveillance data quality, and is consistent with the broader study’s finding that negative results were disproportionately misrecorded as positive [18]. In contrast, some participants in the high-agreement group described documenting malaria as “presumed” in the register when antimalarials were prescribed to a patient who tested negative, suggesting that documentation norms, accountability structures, and available register categories may shape whether prescription non-adherence becomes visible in routine data.

There are several implications for these findings. First, strengthening technical competency alone is unlikely to fully resolve prescription non-adherence or misrecording where behaviour is driven by risk perceptions, low confidence in negative results, and fear of accountability for guideline deviations. Second, supervision may need to extend beyond observation of RDT technique to include supportive discussion of decision-making for RDT-negative febrile patients, including when referral is indicated and how to manage uncertainty. Third, surveillance quality may be improved by documentation systems that allow transparent recording of prescription non-adherence without penalising providers, while still enabling monitoring of deviations from guidelines. Finally, improving access to diagnostics for common non-malarial causes of fever could reduce reliance on malaria testing and treatment and may increase confidence in withholding antimalarials when RDTs are negative.

This study has limitations. Agreement with the expert panel reflected concordance on RDT interpretation, not necessarily adherence to the recorded result in treatment decisions. Findings derive from a small, purposive sample of healthcare workers in two high-transmission regions and may not be generalisable to all settings in Côte d’Ivoire. Social desirability bias may also have influenced reports of guideline use, supervision, and recording practices. Nevertheless, the analytic design, including blinded coding and comparison of high versus low-agreement groups, supports the credibility of the themes identified.

Conclusions

In primary healthcare facilities where the study was conducted, differences in prescription adherence to RDT results and recording practices were driven more by provider perceptions than by reported access to guidelines or routine availability of RDT supplies. Limited confidence in negative RDT results, particularly when clinical signs suggested malaria, contributed to presumptive treatment and, for some providers, deliberate misrecording or incomplete documentation. In the public health facilities where these problems arise, efforts to improve malaria case management and surveillance should therefore extend beyond training on RDT procedures and commodity availability. Interventions should address provider risk perceptions, strengthen confidence in negative results through supportive supervision (emphasizing ethical issues and the implications of reporting erroneous data) and clinical decision support for febrile illness, expand diagnostic options for non-malarial causes of fever, and adapt recording systems to enable transparent documentation of prescription non-adherence without perversely incentivising misreporting.

Healthcare facilities must provide an environment that fosters a culture of accountability, which healthcare workers are expected to have initially acquired during their foundational training. This focus on accountability should be reinforced through continuing professional education and formative supervision, ensuring that healthcare workers fully understand the clinical and epidemiological implications of deviating from guidelines.
