Novel 4-quinolone derivative inflicted cytotoxicity via intrinsic apoptotic pathway activation on human metastatic triple-negative breast cancer cells
Authors: Nursyuhada Azzman, Muhammad Shoaib Ali Gill, Nurshariza Abdullah, Denisse A. Gutierrez, Armando Varela-Ramirez, Laura A. Sanchez-Michael, Jose A. Lopez-Saenz, Mohamad Nurul Azmi Mohamad Taib, Syed Adnan Ali Shah, Renato J. Aguilera, Nafees Ahemad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Quinolones are a class of compounds that have shown promising potential as anticancer agents. However, the current quinolones in clinical trials are currently hampered by their efficacy issues. Therefore, we designed and synthesized novel 4-quinolone-3-carboxamide derivatives 14–67. All the synthesized compounds were initially screened for their cytotoxicity activity using the MTT assay method and evaluated for their anticancer activities against leukemia and breast cancer cells using the Differential Nuclear Staining (DNS) assay. Six screened compounds exhibited low CC50 inhibitory effects against breast cancer cell lines, where compounds 24, 25, 52, and 65 also showed good activity (CC50 = 6.99–11.17 μM) against the leukemia cell line (Jurkat). In vitro assessment showed that the most active compound, 65, exhibited significantly higher growth inhibition activity (CC50 = 7.10–83.2 μM) in breast cancer cell lines, with less cytotoxicity observed in non-cancerous cells (MCF-10 A). The most active analog, compound 65, triggered apoptosis via membrane depolarization and caused cell cycle arrest at the S phase in the MDA-MB231/LM2–4 cell line. In conclusion, the outcomes indicate that compound 65 exhibits the potential to be a potent and effective anticancer agent, making it an excellent candidate for further research and development.

Introduction

Cancer is the second leading cause of death in the United States, after heart disease [1]. Approximately 2,041,910 new cancer cases and 618,120 cancer deaths are estimated to occur in the United States in 2025 [2]. Breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer in women worldwide [3]. It is estimated that by 2025, 316,950 women will be diagnosed with invasive breast cancer, with 59,080 new cases of non-invasive, ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) [4]. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) has the poorest prognosis among breast cancer subtypes, leading to recurrence and metastasis. TNBC patients have a higher mortality rate after a decade [5], necessitating the development of novel drugs targeting TNBC cells, particularly those targeting metastatic cells.

Quinolones have significantly contributed to advancing scientific knowledge in the past two decades through their diverse biological activities, increasing researchers’ interest across various disciplines [6]. Quinolones and their derivatives are shown to be effective as therapeutic scaffolds; moreover, as anticancer agents, leading researchers to propose modifications in their synthesis and research. These include synthetic methodology such as metal-catalyzed synthesis, metal-free or green synthesis, multicomponent one-pot or microwave irradiation, and additional post-modification techniques [7]. Moreover, quinolone derivatives have demonstrated significant promise as potent anticancer agents by specifically targeting and inhibiting specific enzymes such as topoisomerase enzymes (Top I/II), G-quadruplex (G4), tubulin polymerization, protein kinases, phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K), histone deacetylases (HDAC) and pathways in cancer cells [8] therefore preventing the growth and multiplication of cancer cells.

At present, four quinolone derivatives have demonstrated significant anticancer efficacy against various targets in clinical trials (Table 1). Following the success of these compounds entering clinical trials, more quinolone derivatives have been synthesized as anticancer agents. For instance, a quinolone derivative, Compound 1 (Fig. 1) previously tested against several cancer cell lines, was found to have good potential as a VEGFR-2 inhibitor due to the bi-aryl urea moiety structure, which is pivotal for interaction with amino acids at the active site of VEGFR-2. Compound 1 showed higher potency with an IC50 of 1.6 μM against HepG2, which is 5.24-fold higher than Sorafenib (IC50 = 8.38 μM) [9].

Forezi et al. found that compounds 2 and 3 exhibited cytotoxic effects on the gastric cancer cell line (ACP-03), with IC50 values of 1.92 and 5.18 μM, respectively. Both compounds showed no cytotoxic effect on non-cancerous fibroblasts at 20 μM. In contrast, the control drug, Doxorubicin showed toxicity towards the normal cells (MRC-5) at an IC50 of 0.274 μM [14]. The molecular docking analysis revealed an interaction between the carbonyl group located in the 4-quinolone nucleus of derivatives 2 and 3 with the GLN778 residue of the Top II enzyme. Furthermore, the structure-activity relationship (SAR) analysis demonstrated that the decrease in molecular volume of these derivatives correlates with the stabilization of the 4-quinolone-3-carboxamide/topoisomerase II complex, especially when interactions are limited to the GLN778 residue [14]. Nucleoside quinolones such as 4, 5, and 6 were also found to exert anticancer activity against leukemia cancer cell line (K562) [15].

Recently, Machado et al. supported the idea of nucleoside quinolone, synthesizing eleven novel acyclic nucleoside phosphonate derivatives of the 4-quinolone-3-carboxamide. These derivatives showed good potential as anticancer agents with two compounds showing low IC50 values of 13.2 and 20.4 μM against breast cancer cell line (MCF-7) [16]. Additionally, several more quinolone derivatives exhibited effective cytotoxicity on breast cancer cell lines, such as MDA-MB-231 [17], MCF-7, MDA-MB-453, [18] MDA-MB-468 [19]. Therefore, it is postulated that the 4(1H)-quinolones-3-carboxamide scaffold could be helpful for breast cancer studies. Several studies support the anticancer potential of 4(1H)-quinolone derivatives with chloro groups at position C-6 and N-1 substitutions. [20]. Among them, compound 7 displays selective cytotoxic activity in the HepG2 cell line (IC50 ≤ 1.0 μM), exhibiting apoptosis and cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase detected in a dose-dependent manner [20]. To improve the cytotoxic activities, Zhang et al. synthesized and found compounds 8 and 9 that demonstrated the highest potencies against several cancer cell lines with IC50 between 3.5 and 6.8 μM [21].

As a result, we decided to design novel 4-quinolone-3-carboxamide derivatives for the development of new anti-cancer agents in breast cancer. Based on the pharmacophore from previous studies, the synthesized compounds might improve cancer treatment by significantly enhancing biological activity. The synthesized compounds were also assessed for their ability to inhibit the growth of breast cancer and leukemia cell lines, where the most active compound was examined for its ability to induce apoptosis, membrane depolarization, and inhibition of cell cycle activity in breast cancer cells. To better understand the potential target, immunocytochemistry and confocal analysis of microtubules were performed.

Design rationale for anticancer agents using 4-quinolone-3-carboxamide scaffold

It is believed that the substitution at the N-1 position of quinolone can exhibit potent cytotoxicity and may induce apoptosis in cancer cells [22,23]. Interestingly, a few studies supported that modifications at the N-1, C-5 or C-7 positions can have significant effects on potency and pharmacokinetic profiles [24–26]. Although large hydrophobic N-1 substituents have yet to be fully investigated, it was postulated that aromatic moieties substituted at the N-1 position enhanced the hydrophobic interaction with DNA’s major groove [27] and inhibited topoisomerase enzymes by making interaction with the tyrosine active site [28]. It is important to note that a few 4-quinolone derivatives in clinical trials, Quarfloxin and Pidnarulex, possess a carboxamide, while Vosaroxin has carboxylic substituent at the C-3 position. Furthermore, all of these three compounds had modifications made at the N-1 position of the quinolone structure.

Based on the studies of quinolone derivatives, we found that some 4-quinolone compounds with better bioactivity have aromatic groups at the C-3 position of the quinolone ring [9,14]. This might increase hydrophobic interactions and π–π stacking within the active site of target proteins, potentially enhancing binding affinity and specificity [14]. According to a review of the literature, the replacement of a short alkyl group at the N-1 position reduces the antiproliferative activity while larger substituents such as such as benzyl or (3-phenyl)propyl enhance the activity due to π -π interactions with the target. Furthermore, the replacement of esters with carboxamide at the C-3 position was found to improve the potency of the compound. In contrast, the inclusion of a flexible amino side chain containing 3 methylene units at the N-amide group augmented its anticancer activity [29].

Although a few studies supported that substitution of esters, carboxylic acids, and carboxamide groups at the C-3 position can have a significant impact on the enzyme binding properties [30,31], Batalha et al. found that the carboxamide group at the C-3 position enhanced the antiproliferative activity compared to substitution of ester and carboxylic groups. In addition, substitution of the phenoxy group at the C-6 position was also found to improve the antiproliferative efficacy significantly [29]. Therefore, 4-quinolone-3-carboxamide was selected as the scaffold to study. By employing molecular hybridization, anilines or benzylamines were substituted at the C-3 position. In contrast, only benzyl amines substituted at the N-1 position of the quinolone skeleton with a 4-phenoxy substitution at the C-6 position (Fig. 2). The bioactivity of the quinolone derivatives was evaluated in vitro against several breast cancer cell lines and a human non-cancerous breast cell line (MCF-10 A). Additionally, the mechanism by which these compounds exert their anticancer effects was investigated, aiming to identify potent and safe anticancer agents or potential drug candidates.

Results and discussion

Chemistry

The general synthetic routes of 4-quinolone derivatives, 12,14, 15–20, 22–24, and 26–67 are outlined in Schemes 1 and 2. Condensation of 4-phenoxy aniline with Diethyl 2-(ethoxy methylene) malonate (DEEM) yielded diethyl 2-((4-phenoxyphenyl) amino) methylene) malonate (12). Compound 12 was then converted into ethyl 4-oxo-6-phenoxy-1,4-dihydroquinoline-3-carboxylate (13) upon heating with diphenyl ether (PhOPh) and at high temperature (240–250 °C) for 45 min by Gould-Jacob cyclization reaction and filtered using ethyl acetate (EtOAc). Subsequently, 13 was treated with 1 M NaOH (aq) and ethanol under reflux to yield 4-oxo-6-phenoxy-1,4-dihydroquinoline-3-carboxylic acid (14). Moreover, compound 13, upon condensation with various 4-substituted anilines in DMF using MW at 220 °C for 45 min, yielded compounds 15–20 as depicted in Scheme 1.

Subsequently, compound 13 was treated with 4-substituted-benzyl bromide and converted into N-1 substitution, ethyl 1-(4-substituted-benzyl)-4-oxo-6-phenoxy-1,4-dihydroquinoline-3-carboxylate (44-c) in DMF containing catalytic potassium carbonate and sodium iodide. Compounds 22–24 were subsequently treated with aqueous sodium hydroxide in ethanol to produce 25a-c. Ethyl chloroformate (ECF) was used to convert compounds 22–24 into an intermediate, (ethyl carbonic) 1-(4-substituted-benzyl)-4-oxo-6-phenoxy-1,4-dihydroquinoline-3-carboxylic anhydride (25a-c). Following this, compound 25a-c was reacted with 4-substituted anilines or benzyl amines using DMF and TEA to yield compounds 26–67 as shown in Scheme 2. All the synthesized compounds were characterized using 1H, 13C NMR, and MS techniques.

Biological activity

The synthesized compounds were compared with the reference drug doxorubicin for antiproliferative activity using 3-(4, 5-dimethylthiazolyl-2)-2, 5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay against the breast cancer cell line (MCF-7) at 24 and 48 h. The IC50 values for compounds that exceeded 500 μM at both times of exposure were eliminated from further testing. Among the tested compounds, only thirteen compounds showed moderate activities for this primary screening against MCF-7, with compounds 12, 14, and 45 failing to display antiproliferative activity at a concentration of lower than 200 μM at both times of treatment, as depicted in Table 2. Compounds 61 and 65 had antiproliferative activities against the MCF-7 cell line with IC50 values lower than 12 μM. Following the results from the MTT assay, all the compounds were further tested using the Differential Nuclear Staining (DNS) assay to determine the cytotoxic effects of the compounds against several types of breast cancer cell lines.

The DNS assay identified six 4(1H)-quinolone derivatives with cytotoxic activity against breast cancer and T cell leukemia cells

The compounds were initially tested using the DNS assay against non-adherent (Jurkat) and adherent (MDA-MB-231) cell lines for 72 h to screen the best compounds for further evaluation (Table 3). Out of the thirteen compounds examined, six compounds (24, 25, 46, 50, 52, and 65) were found to display significant cytotoxic activity against Jurkat and MDA-MB-231 cell lines with cytotoxic concentration (CC50) values between 4.6 and 42.7 μM (Table 3). Moreover, 46, 50, 52, and 65 exhibited selective cytotoxic activity against the MDA-MB-231 cell line with CC50 values lower than 8 μM. Interestingly, compound 65, bearing a 4-trifluoromethyl benzylamine at the N-1 position and a 4-benzyl amine substituent in position-3, was the most potent compound. It should be noted that most of the compounds show less toxicity towards normal breast cells (MCF-10 A). Furthermore, most of the breast cancer cell lines tested using 65 demonstrated CC50 values below 22 μM except for MDA-MB-468 and T47D. The most sensitive breast cancer cell lines to 65 were the MDA-MB-231 and its metastatic derivative MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 triple-negative breast cancer cells with CC50 values of 7.10 μM and 11.65 μM, respectively (Table 4).

Compound 65 displayed 2.7 times greater inhibitory activity in cancer cells when compared with a non-cancerous cell line, MCF-10 A (CC50 = 18.64 μM). It is important to note that the MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 cell line was chosen not only for its low CC50, but for its biological relevance as a metastatic TNBC model. This lung-homing derivative of MDA-MB-231 was developed through in vivo selection for enhanced metastatic potential to the lungs [32]. In addition to increased glycolysis, altered serine biosynthesis, and decreased mitochondrial ATP production, MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 cells exhibit metabolic adaptations specific to the lung microenvironment [32]. This makes MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 an excellent model for studying metastatic behavior and drug response in TNBC. Additionally, the parental MDA-MB-231 line is commonly used in cancer research due to its aggressive, invasive phenotype as well as its triple-negative status, which is similar to the clinical challenges present in TNBC [32,33]. Using the MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 variant, a more organ-specific metastatic profile can be assumed, increasing the clinical relevance of the findings.

Jurkat cells (CC50 = 6.985 μM) were found to exhibit even higher sensitivity in comparison to all the cancer cell lines. This may be due to their rapid proliferation and limited DNA repair capacity, which make them more vulnerable to quinolone derivatives that target topoisomerases and disrupt DNA synthesis and cell signaling [30]. In contrast, breast cancer cells often possess resistance mechanisms such as drug efflux and enhanced DNA repair, reducing their susceptibility [34]. These results suggested that the substitution of CF3 at N-1 substitution in the benzylamine N-1 position is superior to Cl and F groups against the breast cancer cell lines. However, it is notable that substituting the F group at the same position may increase the cytotoxic potency only in the MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 cell line.

We selected the CC50 from the 65-treated non-cancerous human epithelial breast (MCF-10 A) cell line to determine cancer-type selectivity and calculate the selective cytotoxicity index (SCI). Table 5 depicts the SCI of the 65 against a panel of breast cancer cells compared with non-cancerous cells. The SCI was calculated by dividing the CC50 value obtained from normal breast cells (MCF-10 A) by the CC50 value obtained from cancerous cells. A higher SCI value indicates greater selectivity of compound 65 towards cancer cells over normal cells. Compound 65 displayed significant selectivity against various tumor types, including the MDA-MB-231 (SCI = 2.6) and MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 (SCI = 1.6). However, this selectivity varied significantly with other breast cancer lines, ranging from 0.2 for MDA-MB-468 and T47D to 0.9 for MCF-7 and HCC70, respectively (Table 5). An SCI value below 1 indicates poor selectivity, suggesting that the compound is more toxic to normal cells than to cancer cells, which is undesirable in anticancer drug development [35].

Although compound 65 exhibited a relatively low CC50 value of 11.65 μM in metastatic triple-negative MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 cells, the selection of this cell line for mode of action studies was based not only on its sensitivity to the compound but also on its established relevance as a clinically representative model of metastatic triple-negative breast cancer. The MDA-MB-231 and its derivative lines, including LM2–4, are widely recognized as prototypes for studying aggressive and metastatic forms of triple-negative breast cancer [36,37]. These cell lines have been extensively used in preclinical research due to their mesenchymal features, metastatic potential, and genetic alterations such as mutations in RAS pathway effectors, which are commonly associated with claudin-low and basal-like breast cancer subtypes [37]. The LM2–4 variant, in particular, is a lung-selective metastatic derivative of MDA-MB-231 and has been extensively used in preclinical models to evaluate therapeutic responses in metastatic TNBC [38]. Moreover, MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 is a crucial cell line for mode-of-action studies in metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), as it represents the aggressive, invasive, and metastatic features of this challenging disease [39].

Compound 65 induces mitochondrial depolarization in MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 cells

The electrical potential across the inner membrane, known as mitochondrial transmembrane potential (ΔΨm), is pivotal for healthy mitochondria and cells. A reduction in ΔΨm is a critical event usually observed and linked to the intrinsic apoptosis pathway. At the same time, the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential is associated with the overproduction of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS), causing oxidative stress [41]. To determine if 65 affects the well-being of mitochondria and plays a role in apoptosis, we analyzed the ΔΨm (mitochondrial membrane potential) of MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 cells exposed to 65 for a duration of 5 h (Fig. 3). Cells treated with 65 were stained with the polychromatic fluorescent reagent JC-1 and analyzed with flow cytometry. When a loss of ΔΨm potential occurs, the JC-1 fluorescence emission signal changes from red to green. Both fluorescence signals can be detected and quantified using FL2 and FL1 detectors via flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 3A, MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 cells exposed to compound 65 at the CC50 exhibited a significant amount of mitochondrial depolarization (30.52 %) when compared to the vehicle (DMSO) control (p < 0.05) with 13.34 % of JC-1 monomers. As expected, H2O2 treatment (48.97 %) resulted in a significant percentage of cells with depolarized mitochondria. Thus, our results indicate that 65 induces mitochondrial depolarization and activation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway.

Compound 65 induces cell death via apoptosis as determined by PS externalization

The mechanism of cell death induction by compound 65 was investigated by measuring phosphatidylserine (PS) externalization through flow cytometry after 48 h of exposure to MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 cells. The 48 h CC50 of 65 (33.11 μM) and twice the CC50 (66.22 μM) were used for these analyses as DNS assay data can be retrieved only at 48 h due to high cytotoxicity at 24 h that made the reading unavailable. Our data show a significant increase in apoptosis in 65-treated cells compared to vehicle-control-treated cells (1 % DMSO, p <0.05, Fig. 4A). Untreated and H2O2-treated samples were also included as negative and positive controls, respectively. Cells displaying PS externalization were identified as early apoptotic (Annexin V-FITC positive), and cells with compromised cytoplasmic membranes (Propidium iodide positive) and externalized PS were classified as late apoptotic cells. Percentages shown in Fig. 4B represent total apoptosis values resulting from the sum of both early and late apoptosis (Fig. 4).

Compound 65 disrupts cell cycle progression by arresting cells in S phase

An analysis of the cell cycle profile was conducted to investigate the effects of compound 65 on MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 cells. The cells were treated with CC50 (33.11 μM) and CC30 (19.87 μM) concentrations for 48 h. These concentrations were determined based on cytotoxicity data obtained using the DNS assay, which distinguishes viable and non-viable cells through nuclear staining techniques [42]. The percentage of viable cells was calculated and plotted against compound concentration to generate a dose-response curve. From this curve, the concentrations that resulted in 50 % and 30 % reductions in cell viability were identified as CC50 and CC30, respectively. After treatment, the cells were harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry to assess changes in cell cycle distribution and to further characterize the cellular response to compound 65 under sub-lethal conditions. It is crucial to highlight that excessive DNA fragmentation of cell subpopulations (sub-G0-G1; hypodiploid) is seen when using a concentration higher than CC50 in this assay, which may severely affect the accurate determination of the cell cycle phases (gates). Following the application of the nuclear isolation media-DAPI (NIM-DAPI), the intensity of the DAPI-DNA signal was measured using flow cytometry to determine the total amount of DNA in the cells. In comparison to the solvent control (DMSO; p < 0.001, Fig. 5C), cells treated with low concentrations of 65 (CC50 or CC30) showed a notable cell cycle arrest in the S (hyperdiploid) phase. Ethylene oxide (EtO) at a concentration of 80 μM was used as a control group in the cell cycle analysis based on previous findings demonstrating that sub-millimolar doses can transiently stimulate DNA synthesis and promote S-phase accumulation without inducing cytotoxicity. This concentration was specifically chosen to avoid extensive glutathione depletion, which has been linked to cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [43], thereby ensuring that any observed effects are due to regulatory modulation rather than oxidative stress or cell death.

Cytoskeleton cell structure is not disrupted by compound 65

Based on the previous reported studies on 4-quinolone-3-carboxamide derivatives, it was predicted that this series of new compounds could be potential tubulin disruptors, thus, the effect of these drugs was evaluated in the cell cytoskeleton by immunocytochemistry and confocal analysis. The potential impacts of Compound 65 on the cytoskeleton were analyzed by assessing the integrity of microfilament (F-actin) and microtubule (tubulin) using an immunocytochemistry staining protocol and confocal microscopy after the exposure of MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 cells with Compound 65 for 4 h [44,45]. Cells treated with Compound 65 (CC50 and CC50 2×), DMSO, Vinblastine, and untreated samples were included as controls. Digital confocal microscopy pictures were obtained by fixing, permeabilizing, and immunostaining MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 cells with α-tubulin-Alexa-488 antibodies (microtubules), phalloidin-Alexa-568 (F-actin), and DAPI (nucleus). Several images were obtained from each treatment, and the morphological alterations of both microfilaments and microtubules were rigorously studied. Fig. 6 illustrates that the tubular configuration of microtubules is preserved and comparable between the 65 treatments and the untreated and DMSO controls. Moreover, no alterations were observed in actin fibers or microfilaments following exposure to 65. These results demonstrate that compound 65 does not disrupt the cell microtubule and/or microfilament structure.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the results from this study indicated that compound 65 has excellent potential as an anticancer drug for treating TNBC. It demonstrated potent cytotoxicity against seven breast cancer cell lines with higher selectivity towards TNBC cell lines with low CC50’s. Compound 65 disrupted the cell-cycle progression in the metastatic MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 cell line and induced apoptotic DNA fragmentation and S phase arrest. Further analysis showed that 65 induced mitochondrial depolarization, activating the intrinsic apoptotic pathway to initiate its cell death mechanism in metastatic TNBC cells. Moreover, it was also determined that 65-treated MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 cells exhibited PS externalization in a dose-dependent manner, supporting that its cytotoxic effect is via apoptosis. These findings provide rational insight for further evaluating 65 as an anti-breast cancer agent in an animal model. The discovery of 65 establishes a foundation for developing analogs with better activity against TNBC and other types of cancer cells.

Experimental section

General chemistry

Unless otherwise noted, all reagents in the experiments were bought from commercial suppliers and applied to the experiments without further purification. The 4-phenoxy aniline (CAS 139–59–3) and Diethyl ethoxymethylene malonate (CAS 87–13–8) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Milwaukee, WI, USA). All benzylamines and anilines were also purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Milwaukee, WI, USA except 4-(trifluoromethoxy)benzylamine (CAS 93919–56–3) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA).

General procedures

All synthesized compounds were characterized by 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and were confirmed via LC/MSD with an Agilent 1200 series system. Reagents were obtained from commercial suppliers and used without further purification. The NMR spectra were recorded either at 300 MHz on a Bruker Fourier 300 spectrometer or at 500/600 MHz on a Bruker Avance III, using TMS as internal standard and CDCl3 or DMSO-d6 as solvent, and chemical shifts (d) were expressed in ppm. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) was performed on silica gel-coated aluminum plates and viewed under UV light. The compounds’ melting points were determined using the Stuart SMP40 apparatus without correction. The Mass spectrometry was performed using either an Agilent Technologies LCMS 6520 or Agilent Single Quadrupole LCMS G6120B systems, while the Microwave reactions were carried out with a Monowave 400 reactor, 850 W (Anton Paar, Austria).

Biological evaluation

Cell lines and culture conditions

The MCF-7 cell line was used to screen compounds with anticancer activity via MTT assay. Following this, the triple-negative breast adenocarcinoma cell line, MDA-MB-231, and the acute T-cell leukemia cells, Jurkat, were used as primary models in the DNS assay for adherent and non-adherent cancer cell lines, respectively [46]. Following this primary screening, the potent compounds were tested against a panel of breast cancer cell lines. Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; CORNING, Corning, NY, USA), supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin, was used for the following cell lines: breast carcinomas: MCF-7; breast adenocarcinoma: MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 and MDA-MB-468 cell lines. The MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 cell line is a lung-metastatic variant derived from MDA-MB-231 cells through two rounds of in vivo selection in CB17 SCID mice, involving orthotopic injections, surgical resection of primary tumors, and isolation of lung metastases [40]. Genotypic analysis confirmed its human origin and lineage. The cell line was kindly provided by Dr. Munoz and used without additional authentication testing. The non-cancerous breast epithelial cells, MCF-10 A, were cultured in DMEM F/12 media containing 10 % FBS, 100 U/mL of penicillin, and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin. The MCF-10 A cells were supplemented with 0.5 μg/mL of hydrocortisone, 10 μg/mL of insulin, and 20 ng/mL of epidermal growth factor (EGF). RPMI-1640 culture medium (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) supplemented with 100 U/mL of penicillin and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin (Lonza, Walkersville, MD, USA) was used for the following cell lines: breast carcinomas, T47D, HCC70, and HCC1419. The RPMI-1640 medium was supplemented with 10 % heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS: Hyclone). All the cell lines in this study were grown consistently at 37 °C and in a 5 % CO2 humidified atmosphere.

MTT (4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) assay

A cell proliferation assay was carried out using 96-well microtiter plate cultures and MTT staining. Cells were grown in a DMEM medium containing 10 % (v/v) fetal bovine serum and 100 mM streptomycin. Cultures were propagated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2. Cells in the logarithmic phase of growth were diluted to a density of 10,000 cells/mL in culture medium and treated with various concentrations of compounds 12,14, 15–20, 22–24 and 26–67 in 96-well plates for 24 h and 48 h in final volumes of 200 μL. Then 20 μL MTT (5 mg/mL) was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for an additional 4 h. After carefully removing the medium, the precipitates were dissolved in solubilization solution dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and shaken mechanically for 2 min. Finally, the absorbance values at a wavelength of 570 nm were taken on a spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices SpectraMax iD3 multimode microplate absorbance Reader, USA) with a reference filter of 650 nm. IC50 values were calculated using a percentage of growth versus the untreated control. Doxorubicin was used as a positive control, and DMSO was used as the solvent control. The final concentration of DMSO in the growth medium was 1 % (v/v) or lower, a concentration without effect on cell replication. Three independent determinations were used to obtain each experimental point in these experiments. Cell viability (%) was acquired using the following eq. [47]: %CellViability=MeanODsample×100MeanODblank

The cells were counted using a hemocytometer by placing 15–20 μL cell suspension between the hemocytometer and cover glass. The number of cells was counted in the four outer squares and divided by four to find the mean number of cells per square. The number of cells per square × 104 = the number of cells/mL of suspension.

Cell viability analyses

The viability of non-adherent cells was assessed using PI exclusion dye and flow cytometric assay before the preparation of any experimental multi-well plate [48]. Using a fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) tube, the cell pellet was resuspended with PBS containing a final 5 μg/mL PI concentration. The samples were subsequently analyzed using flow cytometry (Gallios, Beckman Coulter, Miami, FL, USA) by comparing them to an unstained cell sample. Cells positive for PI were considered non-viable, and only samples with a viability of 95 % or higher, which indicates healthy cells, were used [49].

Differential nuclear staining (DNS) assay

The cytotoxicity of the experimental compounds was evaluated using the DNS assay as a secondary screening for hit validation [42] The DNS assay was conducted as follows: on the first day, 10,000 cells were seeded in 100 μL of culture media and placed in each well of a 96-well plate format (Greiner Bio-One, Austria). The following day, cells were treated with different concentrations of compounds ranging from 3.125 μM to 100 μM. In addition, three types of controls were included: vehicle (1 % v/v DMSO), untreated cells, and a positive control for cell death (1 mM H2O2). A 72-h incubation period followed after adding the treatments to the cells. Two hours before the end of the incubation period, the cells were stained with Propidium Iodide (PI) (MP Biomedicals, Solon, OH, USA) and Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) at a final concentration of 5 μg/mL for each dye. This screening strategy utilizes two DNA-intercalating agents, red (P.I.) and blue (Hoechst), to obtain images under two fluorescent channels and determine the percentage of live/dead cells. Hoechst dye is permeable to all cells and is used to label the whole cell population. Conversely, PI only stains the nuclei of dead cells whose membranes have been compromised. Two hours after the staining, image acquisition was performed with the multi-well plate reader Image Xpress Pico automated bioimager system (Molecular Devices; San Jose, CA). Four contiguous images (2 × 2 image montage) were captured from each well with a 4× objective for each fluorescence channel. Image acquisition and analysis were performed by using Cell Reporter Xpress software (Molecular Devices) to estimate the percentage of live and dead cells from each well.

Cytotoxic concentration of 50 % (CC50) and selective cytotoxicity index (SCI)

The Selective Cytotoxicity Index (SCI) was calculated as follows: SCI = CC50 of non-cancerous cells / CC50 of cancer cells. The SCI values of one or less are suggestive of a lack of selective cytotoxicity of an experimental drug in killing cancer cells. In contrast, SCI values higher than one indicate the selective cytotoxicity of an experimental drug, which kills cancer cells preferentially over non-cancerous cells [53]. The CC50 values, which represent the concentration at which chemical 65 kills 50 % of the cell population, were determined using the cytotoxicity percentage obtained from a secondary screening using a linear interpolation equation (https://www.johndcook.com/interpolator.html, accessed on May 10, 2024). Most research employed CC50 and 2 X CC50 concentrations to evaluate the dose-response effects of compounds on cells. In addition, the CC50 was measured after 72 h of incubation, as many studies were carried out during this time frame. The selective cytotoxicity index (SCI) values indicate the capacity of the compounds to selectively kill the cancer cells while avoiding substantial harm to normal, non-cancerous cells. To determine this SCI value, the CC50 of the non-cancerous cell line was divided by the CC50 of the cancerous cell line [50].

Annexin V-FITC/PI assay

The apoptosis/necrosis assay was employed to identify the mechanism of cell death induced by 65 [20]. One hundred thousand cells (MDA-MB-231/LM2–4) per well in 1 mL of culture media were seeded in 24-well plates. After overnight incubation at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 environment, the cells were treated on the subsequent day with 65 CC50 and 2× CC50, 1 mM H2O2 (positive control for cell death), 1 % v/v DMSO (vehicle control) for 24 h using the Annexin V-FITC/PI kit (Beckman Coulter, Miami, FL, USA). Cells were harvested and collected into flow cytometry tubes and centrifuged at 262 X g for 5 min. After removing the supernatants, each tube was gently resuspended with a 100 μL of an ice-cold mixture containing Annexin V-FITC and PI. The tubes were kept on ice in the dark for 15 min for incubation before 300 μL of ice-cold 1× binding buffer was added. Using flow cytometry (Gallios, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), the samples were analyzed immediately, collecting approximately 10,000 events per sample. The data collected were analyzed using Kaluza 1.3 software (Beckman Coulter).

Mitochondrial membrane depolarization assay

MitoProbe JC-1 assay kit (Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR, USA; M34152) was used to assess the mitochondrial membrane potential of 65-exposed cells to evaluate whether 65 causes cell death via the intrinsic apoptosis pathway. MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 cells were plated in 24-well plates at a density of 100,000 cells per milliliter of culture media. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C overnight. The following day, cells were treated with 65 CC50 (5.52 μm), 2× CC50 (11.04 μm), 1 % DMSO as a vehicle control, and 1 mM H2O2 as a positive control for dead cells. The cells were exposed to 65 for 5 h at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 atmosphere. All the cells were collected into FACS tubes and centrifuged at 262 X g for 5 min. The supernatant was decanted, and pellets were resuspended in 500 μL of PBS containing 5 μL of the JC-1 fluorescent reagent (50,6,60-tetrachloro-1,10,3,30-tetraethylbenzimidazolylcarbocyanineiodide) and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. Following the incubation period, 1 mL of PBS was added to each sample, and the cells were centrifuged again at 262 X g for 5 min. The supernatants were decanted, and the pellets were resuspended in 500 μL of PBS before being analyzed by flow cytometry. Each sample consisted of 10,000 events/cells that were collected and evaluated. The JC-1 dye is retained inside the mitochondria by the population of cells with high mitochondrial membrane potential, resulting in aggregates that emit a red fluorescence signal. This JC-1 aggregates, however, separates into monomers when the mitochondria depolarize, producing a green fluorescent signal. The 5-h treatment duration used for JC-1 assay was based on preliminary optimization experiments and is supported by literature demonstrating that extended exposure (up to 4–5 h) to mitochondrial stressors such as CCCP can induce measurable mitochondrial depolarization without compromising cell viability [51].

Analysis of the cell cycle progression

The effect of 65 on the cell cycle profile was assessed by using flow cytometry to quantify the total cellular DNA content. MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 cells were seeded in a 24-well plate format and treated with low quantities of 65 (CC30 and CC50) for 48 h. The cytotoxic concentration 30 % (CC30) is a theoretical concentration calculated based on the CC50 of 65 by using linear interpolation software (https://www.johndcook.com/interpolator.html). The rationale behind employing small doses of the experimental chemical is to minimize severe DNA fragmentation or cell death, thereby facilitating the identification of cell cycle arrest at any phase. Control treatments were included, as previously stated. The cells were collected and spun in a centrifuge, and the resulting cell pellets were mixed with 100 μL of culture medium. Subsequently, 200 μL of nuclear isolation media (NIM)-DAPI solution (Beckman Coulter) was introduced to each sample, and measurements were obtained using the Gallios flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). The total DNA content of each cell can be measured by fixing, permeabilizing, and staining the cell nuclei with the NIM-DAPI solution. To establish a clearly defined cell cycle distribution profile, 20,000 events were recorded for these investigations. The FL9 detector was employed to collect the fluorescent signal emitted (~461 nm) by DAPI-DNA complexes. This method eliminates the need to remove cellular RNA since the DAPI-RNA complexes emit a fluorescence signal with a longer wavelength (~500 nm).

Analysis of the compound 65 influence on the cytoskeleton organization

This initiative utilized metastatic triple-negative breast cancer MDA-MB-231/LM2–4 cells and a confocal microscope [40] (LSM 700; Zeiss; New York, NY). The black, thin, and transparent flat-bottom 96-well tissue culture-treated imaging microplates (BD Falcon; Cat. No. 353219) were also used. This microplate permits short working distance objectives (40×, 63×, and 100×) to capture subcellular details necessary for this series of experiments. Cells were seeded at a density of 5000 in 100 μL of medium per well. Then, cells were processed essentially, as previously detailed [44,45]. After exposing the cells for 2 and 4 h to compound 65 at CC50 (33.11 μM) and 2× CC50 (66.22 μM) they were fixed, permeabilized, and blocked. The controls included in these experiments were untreated, solvent (1 % DMSO), and Vinblastine-treated cells (3 μM); the latter was included as a positive control. Next, cells were stained with 50 μL per well with a solution containing 5 μg/mL of DAPI, 0.156 μM of Alexa Fluor 568-Phalloidin (Invitrogen), and 0.5 μg/mL of Alexa Fluor 488-anti-α-tubulin monoclonal antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rochester, NY). All the staining phases were executed in the dark to minimize photo-bleaching effects [52]. Lastly, the cells were washed three times, using 200 μL of the permeabilization solution as earlier described [44]. High-resolution digital confocal images from triple-stained fluorescent samples were acquired from three fluorescent channels: Alexa-488, Alexa-568, and DAPI. An EC Plan-Neofluar 40× / 1.30 oil DIC objective was used consistently with a pinhole adjusted to 1-Airy Unit (AU) per channel. For the acquisition and analysis of confocal images, the ZEN 2009 software was utilized (Zeiss).

Statistical analysis

Every single data point plotted represents at least three independent measurements. The results are depicted as the average (or mean) of multiple measurements, along with their corresponding standard deviations (SD) and error bars, representing experimental variability. The statistical significance of the similarities between two experimental samples was determined using a two-tailed Student’s t-test (http://studentsttest.com/), and a P-value equal to or less than 0.05 (<0.05) was considered significant.
