From viral drift to tumor drift: why cancer immunotherapy needs B cells and dynamic adaptation
Authors: Zheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrent T-cell-based immunotherapies—chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cells, checkpoint inhibitors, and neoantigen vaccines—share a fundamental vulnerability: they target fixed antigens while tumors continuously evolve. Like influenza viruses, cancer cells undergo antigenic drift, remodeling their surface antigen landscape and rendering static therapies eventually ineffective. Drawing on our recent proof-of-concept study and establishing the foundation in a mouse model of cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, we propose a paradigm shift: treat cancer as a moving target. By periodically harvesting tumor cells, immunizing healthy donors, and returning the resulting antibody-rich serum, this approach generates polyclonal antibodies that dynamically match the tumor’s evolving antigenic profile. We articulate three interconnected principles underlying this strategy: the threshold principle (immune control operates below a critical tumor burden), the drift principle (antigen change renders fixed targeting futile), and the B-cell advantage (healthy donors generate antibodies that tolerant hosts cannot). We outline key experiments to test these principles directly and discuss translational paths, including off-the-shelf antibody banks and combination with T-cell therapies. This article argues that borrowing adaptive strategies from infectious disease epidemiology could establish a new framework for cancer immunotherapy—one built on B-cell responses, dynamic matching, and continuous adaptation.Keywords: B cell, Antibody, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy succeeds, yet tumors evolve faster than therapies adapt.1 Like influenza viruses, cancer cells continuously remodel surface antigens—rendering fixed-target T-cell therapies ineffective. Our recent proof-of-concept study establishes the foundation and tested whether serum from healthy mice immunized with autologous tumor cells could reduce tumor burden in a mouse model of cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (mCSCC).2 Drawing on this work, we propose an alternative paradigm borrowed from infectious disease: treat cancer as a moving target. By periodically harvesting tumor cells, immunizing healthy donors, and returning the resulting serum, this approach generates polyclonal antibodies that dynamically match the tumor’s evolving antigenic profile. We articulate three core principles—threshold, drift, and B-cell advantage—and outline key experiments to test this framework.

The chase that T cells cannot win

Cancer immunotherapy has been built overwhelmingly on T cells. Checkpoint inhibitors unleash them. Chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cells re-engineer them. Neoantigen vaccines train them. This focus is understandable: T cells are powerful, specific, and capable of immunological memory. But they share a vulnerability that the field has only recently begun to confront in earnest. T cells are precise, and precision is a liability.

When a tumor expresses a fixed antigen—CD19, for example—CAR-T cells can eliminate every cell bearing that target. The result is often dramatic. But within months, tumor cells lacking CD19 emerge.3 The therapy, exquisitely designed to recognize one protein, is rendered blind. This is not an exception; it is the rule. Across nearly every form of T-cell-based immunotherapy, antigen loss, lineage switching, and immune editing are now recognized as primary mechanisms of resistance.4 5 The field’s response has been to broaden the target: bispecific antibodies, multi-target CARs, and personalized vaccines encoding up to 34 neoantigens.6 But these remain static strategies. They select a set of targets—whether 1 or 34—and lock onto them. Meanwhile, the tumor continues to evolve, selecting for clones that have shed or mutated the targeted antigens. The chase continues, but T-cell-based therapies are built to be left behind.

What oncology can learn from infectious disease

Infectious disease immunology long ago solved a similar problem. Seasonal influenza vaccines are updated annually because the virus’s surface antigens drift.7 Convalescent plasma therapy for emerging viral variants works by capturing antibodies generated against the current circulating strain.7 The principle is simple: when the pathogen changes, the therapy must change with it.

Why has oncology not adopted this logic? Perhaps because we have been focused on the wrong lymphocyte. T cells recognize intracellular antigens presented on major histocompatibility complex; they are ideal for detecting viral infections but are constrained by the very specificity that makes them powerful. B cells, by contrast, recognize surface antigens directly. Their product—antibodies—can be harvested, transferred, and, crucially, updated as the target changes. This is not a one-time intervention but a series of treatments designed to keep tumor burden below a critical threshold.

This is not a new idea. In 1973, Nature published a report showing that serum transfer could slow tumor growth in rats.8 But the field moved toward T cells, and serum therapy was largely abandoned. Three principles: Threshold, Drift, and B Cells

Our recent proof-of-concept study establishes the foundation tested whether serum from healthy mice immunized with autologous tumor cells could reduce tumor burden in a mouse model of mCSCC. The results—tumor reduction and reversal of molecular markers—were encouraging.2 In reflecting on the mechanism, we arrived at three principles that together form a coherent framework.

Principle 1: The Threshold. Tumor immunity is not all-or-nothing. The immune system can contain small tumors, even in tolerant hosts, but fails once a critical burden is exceeded. In our study, serum treatment reduced tumor volume but did not eliminate it entirely—consistent with a “reset” mechanism that pushes the burden below the threshold, allowing endogenous immunity—T cells, natural killer cells, macrophages—to contribute.

Principle 2: The Drift. Tumor cells do not remain static. They mutate, adapt, and change the very antigens that immunotherapies target. In our study, 3 weekly serum treatments were more effective than a single treatment, hinting that the tumor’s antigenic landscape shifted during therapy.

Principle 3: The B-Cell Advantage. A tumor-bearing host has been chronically exposed to tumor antigens, inducing tolerance. The same antigens, introduced into a healthy host, are perceived as foreign and provoke a robust immune response. Healthy mice injected with mCSCC cells generated antibodies that, when transferred, could recognize and suppress tumors in tolerant hosts—a repertoire gap that T cells cannot fill.

Testing the principles: A roadmap

Our published study provides proof of concept and establishes the foundation. The next phase must address three questions. First, does antigenic drift occur? We propose testing whether serum generated against early tumor cells binds less efficiently to later tumor cells using flow cytometry. Second, does updating improve outcomes? Comparing static serum versus serum updated weekly would reveal whether periodic re-immunization yields superior tumor control. Third, is it the antibodies? Purifying IgG from serum and comparing efficacy with whole serum, IgG-depleted serum, and non-immune serum would clarify the specific contribution of antibodies.

A call to rethink

The dominance of T cells in cancer immunotherapy has been justified by their power. But power without adaptability is a brittle weapon. Tumors evolve; therapies that cannot evolve with them will eventually fail. We are not arguing against T cells—we are arguing that T cells alone are insufficient, and that the field has neglected B cells, which are uniquely suited to address antigenic drift. The strategy we propose—periodic sampling, immunization of healthy donors, and serum transfer—is not a return to 1970s serum therapy. It is a modern reconceptualization grounded in principles of antigenic drift, immune thresholds, and the untapped potential of humoral immunity. In infectious disease, the shift from static to adaptive strategies happened decades ago. In oncology, it is time to do the same.
