Moving beyond oral gavage: micropipette-guided drug administration (MDA) as a 3R refinement for voluntary oral drug delivery in rodents
Authors: Frida A. M. Nilsson, Olga Krzyzaniak, Macsmeila Dietrich, Sarah Steiner, Laura Kämpfen, Paulin Jirkof, Urs Meyer, Pål Johansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Micropipette-guided drug administration (MDA) is a voluntary oral drug delivery method for rodents designed to minimise stress and the risk of injury, in contrast to traditional oral gavage, which remains the most widely used technique. By allowing animals to voluntarily consume drugs without restraint, MDA improves animal welfare while maintaining experimental accuracy and represent a refinement with the 3R framework. The method has been applied across multiple research fields, including neuroscience, oncology, infections, and behavioural studies. As interest in alternatives to gavage increases, preserving the principles of voluntary intake and minimal restraint is essential to maintain the refinement benefits of MDA and ensure reliable scientific outcome.

Micropipette-guided drug administration (MDA) is a non-invasive method of oral drug delivery in rodents that was originally designed for chronic (> 4 weeks, once per day) per os treatments in early adolescent mice1,2. It is grounded in voluntary intake and minimises procedural stress1,3,4, especially as an alternative to oral gavage1,3, but also to more invasive parenteral drug applications4. Briefly, in MDA, animals remain unrestrained, move freely on the cage grid or within the cage5 and approach a researcher-held pipette to consume a palatable substance1,2. Traditional oral administration techniques, such as oral gavage, are widely used in preclinical research but are increasingly recognised as stress-inducing and invasive procedures1,2,4,6,7,14–17. Gavage (Fig. 1A) involves physical restraint and insertion of a feeding needle or tube, which can cause discomfort, aspiration and tissue injury, particularly when repeated over multiple days or weeks7. This technique not only affects animal welfare but can introduce confounding physiological and behavioural stress responses that jeopardise data quality. These concerns are especially relevant, for example, in studies of inflammation, neurodevelopment and behaviour, where stress itself is a biological variable of interest. Moreover, developing MDA represents a step towards more translatable animal research as it mimics human oral administration. MDA across research fields with a 3R impact

MDA is emerging as a refinement minimising the above-mentioned limitations of oral gavage in specific and chronic drug delivery in general. By enabling safe, voluntary, palatable consumption of small-volume drug solutions, MDA better resembles the animal’s normal activity and behaviour, reduces the need for restraint and provides a scalable alternative that integrates easily into common experimental procedures. Importantly, MDA aligns with the 3Rs, especially with the refinement principle, which has become a central element of legislation and responsible animal experimentation. MDA therefore directly reduces the severity of oral administration procedures, when the alternative is oral gavage. Our team has demonstrated MDA’s impact in mouse models, with clear improvements in animal welfare, while maintaining reproducibility2,4,15,18. MDA has been successfully applied in a wide range of research areas, including neural disorders1,4,18, behavioural neuroscience4,15, hormone treatment for conditional gene expression17, oral glucose tolerance testing3, infections and vaccine research19, but also pain treatment and oncology (unpublished data: O. Krzyzaniak et al and F. Nilsson et al.). Schematic illustrations of MDA and oral gavage as well as images of MDA-treated mice are shown in Fig. 1. Further images and a demonstration video of MDA can be found at https://www.rodentmda.ch.

The growing base of evidence and the method’s practicality have positioned MDA as a strong candidate for broad implementation. However, ensuring methodological fidelity, particularly the aspect of voluntary intake, is essential for maintaining its aimed benefits. Below, we outline the key principles of the MDA, detail its successful dissemination and emphasise the importance of safeguarding its refinement attributes as interest in the method increases.

MDA principles and benefits

Voluntarism and freedom of movement

Core to MDA is the principle of voluntarism. Animals are gently trained to consume drug solutions directly from a pipette without restraint. Figure 1B illustrates the typical posture of a voluntarily participating mouse during MDA, while Figure 1C-E show the same in live settings. A detailed video walkthrough of the MDA method is available at https://www.rodentmda.ch/resources/teaching-resources. The procedure occurs in the home environment with the animal not having to leave the cage’s environment and actively approaching the pipette held by the experimenter. Importantly, no physical immobilisation is used (Fig. 1C). This approach minimises procedural stress, eliminates the risk of aspiration or oesophageal injury and supports natural behaviour. In some situations, mice may be lightly restrained by tail-holding (Fig. 1D), but even then, they take the drug voluntarily and can move away from the pipette if they choose. Alternatively, the animal can receive the drug while being gently cupped the researcher’s hand (Fig. 1E). In all situations, voluntarism depends on the reward from receiving the drug in a palatable vehicle. We typically use sweetened condensed milk, but other vehicles can be employed. For bitter or otherwise unpalatable drugs, MDA may be more challenging, and further work is needed to identify effective masking agents or reformulations to ensure successful voluntary consumption.

MDA has reduced severity grading across multiple fields

MDA, as described, has been successfully applied in a wide range of research areas. Across these studies, MDA has consistently reduced severity grading from severity degrees (SD) 1–2 (in gavage) to SD0 according to the Swiss SD classification (i.e., no (SD0), slight (SD1), moderate (SD2) and severe (SD3) constraint). This SD reduction represents a tangible improvement in animal welfare without compromising scientific outcomes.

Tailored implementation for robust adoption

Adaptation to strain-, sex- and compound-specific variables is possible using customised standard operating procedures (SOPs) and vehicle formulation adjustments2. Supplementary materials available online provide templates for SOPs adapted to different strains and compounds (https://www.rodentmda.ch). Training animals for MDA is feasible in most laboratory settings and can be integrated into standard husbandry routines with minimal disruption2,15. Of note, the success of MDA can be influenced by the taste of the drug, so thorough palatability testing is essential for each new substance to promote voluntary consumption. While sweetened condensed milk is commonly used as a vehicle2,20, alternative excipients may be necessary if initial acceptance is low.

Clarifying methodological integrity of MDA

We welcome growing international interest in MDA and appreciate that recent publications are helping to raise awareness of alternatives to gavage3,17,20. However, it is critical to emphasise that the key refinement of MDA lies in its non-restrained, voluntary design. In the work of Cruz Lebron and co-workers, drug delivery was conducted with animals restrained in dorsal recumbency by one researcher, while a second researcher administered the drug via pipette20. This protocol, while differing from gavage, does not follow the original MDA principles of voluntary intake and freedom from restraint. In addition, the administered volume (0.1 mL) substantially exceeds the established standard of 0.03-0.05 mL (equivalent to 1.2-2 mL/kg) in mice1,2,4,15,18, posing potential welfare risks such as aspiration and stress, particularly when animals are restrained and forced to swallow. Of note, the standard administration volume in rats is 0.5 mL/kg. While Cruz Lebron and co-workers did not claim their procedure was voluntary, the use of the term "MDA" may inadvertently lead to confusion regarding best practices. We believe that it is important to preserve the integrity of the MDA method as a refinement tool and recommend that future uses of the term be aligned with its original, voluntarism-based design.

Conversely, other groups have independently applied the principles of MDA without explicitly using the term. For example, Buch and co-workers described the voluntary oral administration of tamoxifen to spatiotemporally control gene expression in Cre/loxP mice, an approach that aligns closely with MDA’s refinement goals17. We view such work as further evidence of MDA’s broader relevance and encourage consistent terminology to support clearer recognition and dissemination of best practices.

Conclusion

MDA represents a validated, humane, and scalable alternative to oral gavage. Its strength lies in its animal-centred approach, grounded in voluntary behaviour and scientific rigor. As the method gains visibility, it is essential to uphold the defining principles that make MDA a true refinement. We invite researchers, institutions and policymakers to explore our video and training materials to support correct and effective implementation (www.rodentmda.ch). We encourage the research community, animal welfare authorities and policymakers to recognise and promote the original MDA approach to maximise its ethical and scientific impact.
