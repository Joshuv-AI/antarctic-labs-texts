Exploring pension access and health-seeking behavior among older adults in South Kerala: A mixed-methods study
Authors: Angitha Anilkumar, Betsy A. Jose, Sherin Susan Paul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:India has overtaken China as the most populous country, causing an increase in the proportion of older adults. 8.6% of India’s population is olde adults and is projected to rise to 19% by 2050; in Kerala, the proportion is already 12.6%. Financial insecurity is a major determinant of poor health and inequitable healthcare access among the elderly. Social security measures such as pensions play an important role in influencing health-seeking behaviour, particularly in primary care settings. This study assessed the prevalence of pension coverage among community-dwelling older adults, examined their health-seeking behaviour, and healthcare utilization pattern.Methods:A mixed-methods community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Mallapuzhassery Panchayat, Kerala, from March 2021 to July 2022 among 124 adults aged ≥65 years selected using simple random sampling. Data were collected using an interviewer-administered semi-structured questionnaire. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS. Associations were assessed and p <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Qualitative data were collected through purposive in-depth case studies among participants who experienced a major health event in the preceding year and analyzed using content analysis.Results:Pension coverage was observed in 82.3% of participants, predominantly through non-contributory social pensions. Possession of a below-poverty-line ration card was the only significant predictor of pension receipt. Pensions met health-related expenses for 65.3% of participants. Good health-seeking behaviour was observed in 60.4%. Government health facilities were the primary source of treatment (48.4%) and medicines (54%). Pension amount explained 43.4% of the variation in monthly medication expenditure.Conclusion:Social pensions enhance access to primary healthcare among older adults. Strengthening social security measures and addressing socioeconomic determinants are essential to improve health-seeking behaviour in an ageing population.

Introduction

India, having the highest population in the world, is experiencing ‘population ageing,’ a dynamic demographic trend occurring, as our civilization ages. The average proportion of elderly in India was 8.6% as per population survey and is proposed to reach 19% by 2050, but in Kerala, it was 12.6%.[1] According to estimates, Kerala’s elderly population is expected to reach 8.3 million by 2026.[2] The demographic changes, urbanization, and nuclearization of family made elderly to find their own monetary needs. Inadequate financial resources are a significant contributor to poor health irrespective of age, but basic income guarantees financial security and improved access to goods and services, thus fostering positive health outcome.[3] Financial barriers are a problem to the old aged, but it severely affects older women as they are dependent on their family, particularly in India.[4] The way people perceive their own illness, along with various factors such as sex,[5] education and financial situation,[6] living arrangements, access,[7] quality of services,[8] and type and severity of illness[9] may also formulate the health-seeking pattern of the society. Today, there is an urgent need to promote old-age monetary schemes.[10] Financial stability can be considered as an indicator of better health.[311] This may be directly considered in the case of elderly, where the only income will be from sources of pension provided by government/private institutions or their retirement plans. The theme for World Health Day 2025 is “Global Action for Universal Health,” emphasizing the commitment to ensure that everyone has access to health services without financial hardship.[12] Thus, in our state of Kerala, where the health seeking behavior pattern and health indicators are comparable to international standards, this study will provide a better understanding about the problems faced by the older population regarding their finances from various sources and its relationship toward seeking health service.

Methodology

A cross-sectional study, upon receiving the Research and Ethics Committee approval(Approval number PIMSRC/E1/388A/50/2021), was conducted among 124 elderly aged >65 years in Mallapuzhassery panchayat in Kerala between March 2021 and July 2022 using simple random sampling technique, through an interviewer-administered semi-structured questionnaire after getting consent. We used SPSS 25 for statistical analysis to identify the prevalence of pension coverage and Chi-square test to find the association between categorical variables and pension availability, and binary logistic regression was done to identify the possible predictors. For identifying the relationship between health-seeking behavior and different variables, a linear regression model was used. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A qualitative study was done to find out the missing factors from the findings of the quantitative part and to determine the patterns of health-seeking behavior among elderly in a rural setting in South Kerala having had a major disease event in the past 1 year. In-depth interview with five participants identified through purposive sampling gave an insight into how the people respond to different aspects if there is a medical incident in their life or within family and later content analysis was done. Three primary care givers were interviewed for participants who could not speak for themselves.

Results

General characteristics

The mean age of the sample was 73.04 ± 7.35 years (ranging 65–96 years). Out of the 124 participants, 63.7% were young (65 to 74 years) older adults, 27.4% were middle (75 to 84 years) older adults, and 8.9% were old (≥85 years) older adults. Among the 124 participants, 50 (40.3%) were males and 74 (59.7%) were females; 77 (62.1) were married, 45 (36.3%) were widowed, and 2 (1.6%) were single; 85 (68.5%) have a 3-generation family, 34 (27.4%) have a nuclear family, and the rest 5 (4%) live with their extended family; 20 (16.1%) have a yellow ration card, 53 (42.7%) have a pink ration card, 16 (12.9%) have a blue card, and 35 (28.2%) have a white card (pink and yellow are below poverty line families; white and blue are above poverty line families), and 110 (88.7%) were not employed at the time.

Economic background

Of the 124 study participants, the prevalence of those who receive some kind of pension was 102 (82.3%) and the rest 22 (17.7%) do not receive any pension [Figure 1].

Of the 124 participants, 73 (58.9%) did not have any insurance, 43 (34.7%) were insured with KASP (Karunya Arogya Suraksha Padhathi), 2 (1.6%) had MEDISEP (MEDical Insurance for State Employees and Pensioners), 3 (2.4%) were dependent on their children, 1 (0.8%) was insured with ECHS (Ex-Servicemen Contributory Health Scheme), and 2 (1.6%) had private insurances. When asked why they had not planned for economic security, 108 gave a variety of reasons, including limited income 54 (50%), nonpermanent employment 29 (26.7%), and lack of knowledge 6 (5.6%).

Health-seeking behavior

Among the total 124 participants, 48.4% went to primary care, while 26.6% received treatments in tertiary-care government hospitals, and 54.4% got services from public and 45.2% received private hospital services; only 13.7% got all their medicines free of cost. For the 107 participants who spend money on medicines, the median expenditure was Rs 1500 (ranging Rs 100–30,000). Among the 43 (34.7%) who said they cannot meet their health expenditure with their income, 51.2% were males, 74.4% were married, 67.4% belong to three generation or extended family, 62.8% were BPL, 72.1% were receiving social pension, 44.2% had government and 51.2% had no insurance, and 4.7% did not have any morbidity, and their health expenditure per month ranged from 200 to 12,000 rupees. Distribution of participants on the different ways of meeting expenses is shown in Figure 2.

Distribution of participants on the different ways of meeting expenses

While considering the reason for visiting the hospital, among the 124 total participants, 100 (80.6%) were for chronic diseases, 41 (33.1%) for minor accidents, and 18 (14.5%) for surgery. For delaying the care they needed, ever, 81 (65.3%) said they never did unlike the rest 43 (34.7%) delayed at least once in life, and 99 (79.8%) did not postpone treatment.

Of the 124 participants, 60.4% had good and 39.6% had poor health-seeking behavior, respectively. Among the 25 participants who postponed treatment in the past one year of the study, a significantly large proportion 88% did it because of lack of money [Figure 3]. A statistically significant association (P < 0.005) was noted only with participants having APL ration-card and good health-seeking behavior. However, there was no significant association between age, gender, family type, employment, insurance status, presence of morbidity, and pension. A statistically significant strong positive correlation using Spearman’s rank order correlation was identified between 102 persons’ amounts of pension received per month and duration of pension availed in months (correlation coefficient, rs = 0.323 and P = 0.001), 102 persons’ pension amounts received per month and amount spent on medication per month (correlation coefficient, rs = 0.214 and P = 0.031), 102 persons’ pension amounts received per month and health-seeking behavior (correlation coefficient, rs = 0.225 and P = 0.023), and number of morbidities the participants suffer from and the amount spent on medicines per month (correlation coefficient, rs = 0.502 and P = 0.01).

Distribution of participants based on the reason for postponement of medical treatment in the past 1 year

y = 0.32 (±0.074) x + 796.4

That is, the total variation in the amount spent on medication per month can be explained by the pension amount received per month by 43.4%, which is considerably large.

Discussion

Among the study population, 82.3% of the people in the elderly category receive one or the other type of pension under social security. This is in contrast from the findings of the study in India ‘Changing needs of old people in India 2021,’[10] where the coverage was 28.64%, which may be comparable to developed nations. The understanding about the demographic shift in the population leading to a significant increase in the number of elderly, especially in Kerala, where the average proportion is proposed to reach 19% by 2050, may be the reason why the government may have introduced provisions to up-bring the care for elderly in the past 10 years.[1]

In our study, among the married participants, 75.3% were receiving pension, while 64.7% belonged to nuclear family, that is, living alone or with spouse. More than half of the individuals, 65.3%, can meet their health needs with pension, and the rest depend on their family members. According to the National Sample Survey of India conducted in 2004 on the condition of the aged, 17.7% and 21.7% of the total participants received statutory and social pension, respectively. Among those married, 19.4% received pension; for those living alone, it was 10.7%, and for those living with spouses, 36.4% received pension. 23% of population were not dependent on anyone for their economic needs.[6] Of the total participants in our study, 11.2% employed and 78.6% receive any type of pension. This implies that even though they receive a monthly benefit, they require another source of income to meet their needs. A contrasting trend is seen in other parts of India. In the research ‘Financial security for India’s elderly population,’[13] the number of employed persons on usual basis was 60%, of which 82.7% was in the unorganized sector. This may be because of the improved economic situation in Kerala.

Out of the total participants, around 41.1% have some kind of health insurance, either government (36.3%) or private (4.8%). According to BKPAI Kerala report, 2011,[14] only 4.4% of the elderly received medical health insurance from the government and 0.9% from other sources. Although in a study in 2016 on the elderly of Kerala, 57.7% were enrolled under Comprehensive Health Insurance Scheme. In a study following the COVID-19, in 2019, 18.9% of the elderly had health insurance in India.[15] This diversity can be explained by the introduction of insurance schemes by central and state governments for those in the economically weaker sections. In October 2024, regardless of income, the Government proposed Ayushman Vaya Vandana Card to all senior citizens above the age of 70 under Ayushman Bharat to provide free health coverage. It can be noted that most persons, 97.7%, receiving social pensions also benefit from government insurance programs, and those who are qualified for other social security measures provided by the government or other institutions make extensive use of health-care facilities. Previously, there was the RSBY scheme (Rashtriya Swasthya Bima Yojana-established for workers in the unorganized sector because of the need for medical care and hospitalization and even for their family members, a recognized health insurance for providing protection to poor)[16] by the central government that later got upgraded to KASP (Karunya Arogya Suraksha Padhathi, a scheme run combinedly by Central and State Government, where the premium is shared in the ratio 60:40, providing a health cover of Rs. 5 lakhs per family per year for secondary and tertiary care hospitalization to poor families)[17] by the government of Kerala. Another scheme the participants of this study used was ECHS (Ex-Servicemen Contributory Health Scheme, which provides medical care to Ex-servicemen pensioner and their dependents through a network of ECHS polyclinics, government, and empaneled private hospitals across the country).[18] In the article from Arunachal Pradesh,[19] it can be seen that some form of economic security did bring about better tribal elderly health outcomes. This variation may be because of the changes happening in society because of improved quality of life. Another reason may be people were reluctant to spend money on health when they were not ill.[20] The benefit of pooling resources for healthcare may not be known to many.

Factors associated for getting pension were studied; a statistical significance was found with age >75 years, females, unmarried, living in a non-nuclear family, and bearing a BPL card. However, while looking for the predictors of getting pension, families having a BPL ration card was the only factor found statistically significant. This may be because of the benefits received from the monthly Indira Gandhi National Old Age Pension Scheme (IGNOAPS)[19] from the Central Government to BPL residents >65 years. The majority of our participants satisfied the criterion, maybe because most of the population in our country still exist as below poverty line families.

Coming to the health-seeking behavior pattern of the study participants, for the primary place of care, 60 (48.4%) make use of government and 33 (26.6%) of private hospitals. The primary place for acquiring medicines for 54.4% was from the government, followed by 45.2% from private hospitals. 17 (13.7%) got all their medicines free-of-cost, 100 (80.6%) visited the hospital for chronic diseases, 41 (33.1%) for minor accidents, and 18 (14.5%) for surgery. Similar patterns are seen in in Puducherry,[21] where 51.7% of them were utilizing government health care. The private and missionary hospitals were selected because of the accessibility and the availability of drugs.[22] From a study in Shimla, 60.7% use government;[23] this may be due to availability of only government facilities, which is also seen in a study from Nepal.[24] Also, elderly living with family mostly prefer private hospitals to public healthcare.[25] The difference may be due to the severity of illness at the time of interview and the educational status of the participants along with well-functioning primary health-care systems in Kerala and the fact that the latter study included inmates of old age homes.

In our study, while finding out association between health-seeking behavior and various factors, having an APL ration card was found statistically significant with good health-seeking behavior, but a comparative study from Shimla shows that more elderly living in urban areas were seeking healthcare as compared with their rural counterparts. But gender, literacy, income, and marital status were not found to be related.[23] However, in a study conducted in Ethiopia, early age (60–65), formal education, family support in illness, knowledge when to visit, and accessibility to health facility were major predictors.[20] In another study, functional limitations served predictors of healthcare use.[26] A study in Maharashtra concludes that the average health expenditure was more among those who were >75 years of age, employed, educated, and above the poverty line, irrespective of gender.[27] Two studies from rural Bangladesh[528] and in Dharan, Nepal,[24] found that, poverty is a major determinant of health-seeking behavior among elderly. The way people perceive their illness (feeling that it is not an emergency, just a disease of old age) may be the reason for these types of disparities among different populations. While estimating the linear-regression model for health-seeking behavior, it was found that the variation in the amount spent by the participants on medication per month was dependent on the amount of pension they receive rather than the morbidities. This shows the economic factor is deciding the health of an individual. There are similar findings in studies like ‘Wealth and Health Behavior: Testing the Concept of Health Cost’[29] and from BMC Public Health.[30]

The intention of the qualitative study to verify the results as well as recognize any missing components in the first phase of the study was found supporting the quantitative part. From the case studies of the lower economic strata, the healthcare is sought in the public sector because of financial constraints. Similar trends may be seen in other studies where most people go the institutions of public sector and become satisfied with their care[31] when they themselves opined about problems of these institutions[32] like seen in different studies from Kerala,[25] Puducherry,[21] Shimla,[23] and Kenya.[22] But interestingly, the two cases from the upper strata chose differently and case 3 chose a place where care was given free of cost. Some of the patient’s relatives chose a health-care facility they thought would provide the best care, even though it was far from their home, whereas others sought care from the facility that provided the required medicines and care with schemes she was entitled to. Economic improvement will tend people to spend more on health-care; this was also found in studies in Albania[33] and Kerala[25] but not in Uttar Pradesh.[34] The place of care is determined by the opinions of family members. People living with family tend to have a better health-seeking behavior pattern. This can be seen from studies from Ethiopia[20] and Turkey.[35] Utilization of schemes provided by agencies depending upon their eligibility can be appreciated in this study. This can also be seen in a different study from Kochi.[36] Similar to the findings of second case study, most elderly are reluctant to get adequate medical care if the health condition is not severe and consider that as a part of natural ageing process as in Ethiopia.[20] Beliefs and religious customs were also a determining factor for health-seeking behavior in our study. Similar trends were also seen in other studies.[19]

Conclusion

This study concludes there is good coverage of social assistance schemes among the older population. From the lower economic strata, elderly tend to use public facilities, whereas those belonging to the upper strata prefer places that provide quality care that stays within their budget. In most cases, individuals feel a sense of self-actualization regarding their financial condition and tend to choose their healthcare consumption accordingly rather than the personal medical need or the quality of the services provided in contrast to the WHO’s Universal Health Coverage theme to ensure that everyone has access to adequate health services without financial restrictions. To improve the health status of the elderly in the state, it is vital that the authorities propose schemes that increase the economic empowerment of elderly and systematically strengthen the quality of health services. Extensive research is required for finding the patterns of usage of health systems along with the various barriers society faces to rectify it accordingly.
