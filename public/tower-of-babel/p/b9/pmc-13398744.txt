Functional constipation in children and young adults with Prader–Willi syndrome
Authors: Melinda J. Pierce, Isabella Niu, Lusine Ambartsumyan, Parisa Salehi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPrader–Willi Syndrome (PWS) is characterized by hyperphagia, endocrinopathies, and gastrointestinal abnormalities. Clinical concerns about constipation and fecal incontinence (FI) are common, but no studies to date have clear data on functional defecation disorders in children with PWS. This study aims to describe the prevalence of functional constipation (FC) in children with PWS seen at Seattle Children's Hospital between 2019 and 2023.MethodsParental perception of stooling was collected with a survey designed to correlate with the Rome IV criteria for FC. A retrospective chart review, including survey answers, was done for 69 patients ages 1–21 years who completed the initial survey between 2019 and 2023. FC status was assigned according to responses. Results were collected in an online secure REDCap database, and statistical analysis was done in STATA statistical software.ResultsApproximately 17% of the population reported symptoms consistent with FC. Those with FC trended older and had higher rates of laxative use. Growth hormone and levothyroxine use were similar between groups. Parents of patients with FC were more likely to identify their child had constipation, though half did not. Lastly, 35% of patients had FI after established toilet training, though it was not always associated with FC at the time of the visit.ConclusionsRates of constipation in the pediatric PWS population are nearly 80% higher than in the general population, with high rates of FI. The accuracy of parental reports of constipation may be unreliable without reviewing symptoms. This study highlights the importance of parent and provider education and recognition of functional defecation disorders in children with PWS.

INTRODUCTION

Prader–Willi Syndrome (PWS), a genetic condition due to the absence of expression of the paternal alleles in the 15q11.2q13 region, has a reported incidence of 1:15,000 to 1:30,000 births worldwide. PWS is characterized by a number of unique multi‐system characteristics, including hyperphagia, endocrinopathies, intellectual disability, and neurobehavioral disturbances. 1 Constipation and fecal incontinence (FI) are commonly encountered in children with PWS. 2 It is hypothesized that low muscle tone, diets that are low in fluid and fiber, rectal evacuation dysfunction, slower gut transit time, and behavioral comorbidities all increase the risk for constipation in this population. However, to date, there are no studies describing the prevalence and characteristics of functional defecation disorders in children with PWS.

Functional defecation disorders, such as functional constipation (FC) and FI, affect children of all ages with a worldwide prevalence of 0.5%–32% (pooled prevalence of 9.5%) and 0.8%–4.1%, respectively, depending on the criteria used. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 These diagnoses may make up nearly a third of all referrals to outpatient gastroenterology (GI) practices in children under age 4 years and are associated with a negative impact on quality of life. 9 , 10 The Rome IV criteria are symptom‐based diagnostic criteria used to classify functional gastrointestinal disorders, including FC and functional non‐retentive FI. 11

Functional defecation disorders are relatively common in many disorders that have hypotonia as a common feature. Although constipation is part of anticipatory guidance for multidisciplinary management of individuals with PWS, it is not mentioned in the most recent American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) Clinical Report on Health Supervision. 2 , 12 Furthermore, there are no studies describing the prevalence and characteristics of functional defecation disorders in children with PWS. A search in PubMed for the terms “constipation” and “Prader–Willi Syndrome” yielded only four relevant published papers related to functional defecation disorders in children with PWS. These included two case studies, one case series, and one brief mention in a pediatric surgery journal article about surgical technique. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 These papers presented a total of five pediatric cases with rectal prolapse in the setting of rectal picking and presumed constipation. 13 , 15 , 16 The last case report was of a teenager with refractory constipation who underwent anorectal manometry and rectal sensation testing and was found to have dyssynergic defecation with rectal hypersensitivity. 14 Unfortunately, the only study in PWS patients aiming to characterize constipation further was conducted in 2012 in 21 adults with PWS by Kuhlmann et al. 17 This study found a high prevalence of constipation in adults with PWS compared to the general population (40% vs. 11%) and, similarly, adults with PWS had a slow colonic transit time based on imaging compared to the general population (24% vs. 2%–4%).

This study is a retrospective chart review reporting the prevalence and characteristics of functional defecation disorders in a cross‐sectional population of children with PWS who were seen at the multidisciplinary PWS clinic at Seattle Children's Hospital (SCH) between 2019 and 2023.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the SCH Institutional Review Board (IRB) with a waiver of Informed Consent and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) authorization.

Participants

The study participants included patients between ages >1 and <21 years with a genetic diagnosis of PWS who were seen in the SCH PWS Clinic between 2019 and 2023 and whose parents had completed a clinic‐created bowel questionnaire. The questionnaire included questions about frequency of stooling, fecal and urinary incontinence, characteristics of stool, and other parental perceptions about stooling habits.

Medical records were reviewed to obtain demographic and clinical data, including genetic subtypes, nasogastric/gastric tube use for feedings, documentation of rectal picking, urinary incontinence, medication use, and history of GI evaluation within the 2 years prior to survey completion. Genetic subtypes were categorized as deletion, non‐deletion, and other. Non‐deletion included maternal uniparental disomy (mUPD), imprinting center defect, and positive methylation for PWS and negative testing for deletion.

Procedure

Parents and/or primary caregivers of patients with PWS were asked to complete a questionnaire about the youth's bowel habits prior to scheduled clinic visits as part of routine clinical care through the SCH PWS Clinic. The bowel questionnaire was designed to address specific symptoms associated with the Rome IV diagnostic criteria for FC, in addition to urinary incontinence, relationship between bowel and bladder symptoms, the effect on quality of life, and medication use. The pertinent questions addressed frequency of stooling, presence/frequency of stool incontinence, stool retention/posturing, pain with stooling/hard stool, and size of stool. Answers to the bowel questionnaire were collected and saved into REDCap electronic data capture tool hosted at the University of Washington. REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) is a secure, web‐based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies, providing 1) an intuitive interface for validated data capture; 2) audit trails for tracking data manipulation and export procedures; 3) automated export procedures for seamless data downloads to common statistical packages; and 4) procedures for data integration and interoperability with external sources. Retrospective assignment of presumed FC was done through categorical coding of the survey results. Patients were considered to have FC if two or more of the criteria associated with Rome IV classification were reported by the caregiver on the pre‐clinic stool questionnaire.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with STATA statistical software for data science. Patients were divided into those who were assigned to have FC and those who were not. As some questionnaires were incomplete, percentages are reported only from the number of provided answers. Comparisons for age were done with a Student's t test for mean comparison. For all patients who had completed an initial questionnaire about bowel habits, comparisons were made for medication use, clinical pathology, GI involvement, and parental perception between those who were categorized as FC and those who were not. Comparisons of categorical variables were calculated with Fisher's exact test, given the small number of patients who met any individual criteria in any category.

Of the 69 patients, 12 (17%) were categorized as having FC based on questionnaire answers. Those with FC were older (mean age 14 years 8 months vs. 6 years 8 months; p = 0.005), though the difference was not statistically significant for female patients. Use of growth hormone and levothyroxine was seen in 75% and 19% of patients, respectively. There was no statistically significant difference in usage between the groups. Rates of rectal picking and urinary incontinence were similar between groups.

The percentage of patients with obesity skewed higher in those with FC. Logistic regression for risk of FC showed an odds ratio of only 1.03 for obesity that was not statistically significant when controlling for age and sex. All patients stooled at least three times a week, and 23/69 (33%) reported hard or painful stools, and 26/69 (38%) reported large stools for age.

Toilet training followed developmental expectations overall, with 48/59 (80%) patients at least 2 years of age and 46/54 (85%) of those at least 4 years of age reported to be toilet trained. This means 46 of the 69 total patients (67%) were at least 4 years old and reported to be toilet trained (10 with FC, 36 without FC). Of this group, 16 (35%) had parental report of stooling accidents or FI at least once a month. This represented 40% of the individuals with FC in this subgroup and 33% of the individuals without FC. The majority had FI less than once a month, and no one reported FI frequency >3 times a month.

Laxative use

Seventeen (28%) patients were on sennosides and/or polyethylene glycol 3350 (PEG) laxatives at the time of survey completion. Treatment with combination of sennosides and PEG was more common in children with FC (50%) than those without (5%). This difference was not statistically significant.

Parent perceptions

Parent perceptions were also included in the patient survey. Parents of patients with FC were more likely to report their child had constipation, though this did not reach statistical significance. Half of the parents did not identify constipation in their child despite reporting symptoms consistent with FC. A greater percentage of parents of a child with assignment of FC reported hard stools, and that stooling affected their quality of life compared to parents of children without FC.

DISCUSSION

This is the first study to report the prevalence of FC in children and young adults with PWS. In the SCH cohort, FC occurred at a rate two times higher than is seen in the general population, and FI was quite common (17% vs. 9.5% and 35% vs. 1%–4%, respectively). 3 , 5 , 8 , 19 , 20 In the only available similar study done in adults with PWS, 40% of patients were reported to have FC as assessed by Rome III, which supports this study's observed trend that constipation increases with increasing age. 17 Our results would place the prevalence of FC at a higher rate than that of severe scoliosis in the same population. 12

Apart from age, there are no clinical characteristics in this study that seem to distinguish between children with and without FC. Data from the general population are mixed on the association between obesity and FC, and while in this study, those with FC trended towards higher body mass index (BMI) for age, the difference did not reach statistical significance. 4 , 21 This suggests that individuals with PWS are at risk of developing functional defecation disorders regardless of BMI classification. Instead, constipation should be addressed in all children with PWS as part of the broader, multifactorial phenotype. Parental perception of constipation was not a reliable measure of clinical symptoms, and the higher frequency of stooling suggests that surveillance questions in this population should instead focus on the more common features such as hard or very large stools.

Rates of urinary complaints and concerns about negative impact of stooling on quality of life were statistically similar between groups. Rectal picking is a common symptom in PWS, which is atypical in other populations with hypotonia. It is reported in one study to occur in 6% of patients. 22 There were four cases in older children in our population (7%) and a trend toward greater incidence in those with symptoms of FC, though there was no statistical difference. This was likely due to the smaller numbers.

Most of the patients categorized as having FC were not followed by a gastroenterologist (GI) provider. The use of sennoside alone as a laxative was more common in those seen by GI, primarily amongst patients whose symptoms were not consistent with FC at the time of the survey. Most of these patients were followed by GI for other primary diagnoses, such as oropharyngeal dysphagia. It is possible that adequate treatment of their underlying constipation was the reason these patients did not have reported symptoms of FC, given that over half of those seen by GI who did not have survey responses consistent with FC had parents who reported constipation. This is in comparison to parental report of constipation for one in seven of those without FC who were not followed by GI. The combination of sennoside plus PEG was seen more commonly in those with FC, but that did not correlate with having a GI specialist directing treatment. While this indicates the need for intensive treatment, it may be done by other providers in place of referral to GI. It is also possible that the under‐reporting of constipation symptoms by parents contributes to the lower rates of referral to GI clinics and overall underutilization of GI services.

There are several limitations to our study. It was a retrospective study in a limited geographic location with a relatively small sample size that was predominantly female. The small sample size may have affected the power of the study and, therefore, the ability to find significant differences despite trends. Although limited to only one site, the SCH PWS clinic is a referral center for the entire Pacific Northwest Region and, therefore, is representative of the social, ethnic, and economic demographics of the region. However, it should be considered that regional diet, climate, and activity may further limit the generalizability of the results. Finally, much of the relevant data was derived from caregiver perceptions rather than self‐report, and caregiver bias and the subjective nature of the questionnaire may lead to inaccurate reporting. The questionnaire does not specify toilet training status, so estimates are provided based solely on data available in clinical documentation. In addition, the Rome Criteria were based on symptoms for a neurotypical population, and special populations such as PWS will have physical, physiological, and behavioral differences that will skew responses. The Rome IV criteria also include a physical exam criterion of large rectal stool burden, which was not assessed in this study, though inclusion of this criterion would only be expected to result in even higher reported rates of FC. Despite these limitations, this study gives important insight into the prevalence and characteristics of defecation disorders in individuals with PWS.

CONCLUSIONS

This study shows a higher prevalence of FC and FI in children and young adults with PWS compared to the general population. Further studies are needed at this time, and these should include larger numbers of patients to better characterize the incidence, prevalence, and specific features of defecation disorders in the PWS population, including adults. Longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate treatment options for the treatment of FC in individuals with PWS with attention to safety and efficacy. More studies are needed on the impact of functional defecation disorders and their treatment on serious comorbidities like feeding difficulties, vomiting, and gastric rupture, especially as newer medications to treat obesity and hyperphagia are coming to the clinical setting and may impact feeding and stooling behaviors. This study serves as a baseline estimate of FC and FI in the pediatric PWS population.
