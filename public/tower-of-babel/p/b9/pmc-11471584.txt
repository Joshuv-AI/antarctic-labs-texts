Shock response regulation effects and nonlinear dynamic model of lead-cone waveform generator
Authors: Chengwu Liu, Yiming Wu, Jingjing Wen, Bin Wu, Feng Xu, Kangbo Yuan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLead-cone waveform generator (LCWG) is one of the crucial components in shock tests to generate final-peak saw-tooth shock pulses which are widely used to evaluate the safety and reliability of structure/equipment under shock environment in laboratory. With aim to generate a desired final-peak saw-tooth waveform and develop its dynamic model, it is necessary to investigate the working mechanism of LCWG in impact conditions. In this work, a series of customized shock tests based on LCWGs were performed by using a drop test machine (DTM). Meanwhile, the regulation effects of several core characters, including the aspect ratio (h/2R) of LCWG, the impact velocity, and the impact mass, on the generated final-peak saw-tooth waveforms are studied systematically. Experimental results showed that the overall geometric shapes of LCWGs varied from cone to truncated cone during impact processes, accompanying with local non-uniform deformation near the contact position. The correlation analysis indicated that peak acceleration of the generated final-peak saw-tooth waveform increased linearly with the increasing impact velocity, the impact mass, but decreased exponentially with elevated aspect ratio of lead-cone; the pulse duration of the generated final-peak saw-tooth waveform exhibited nonlinear increase trend with the increase of aspect ratio, but was not sensitive to the changes of impact velocity and impact mass. Subsequently, a nonlinear dynamic model of LCWG to predict the final-peak saw-tooth waveform was established by incorporating the geometric deformation model of the lead-cone during impact process and the mechanical constitutive model of the lead. The proposed model considered main parameters of the LCWG in shock tests, including the impact mass, the impact velocity, the geometric dimensioning of the LCWG, and the mechanical properties of lead. The predicted waveforms based on the established model agreed well with the experimental shock pulses, which provided a basis design for shock test.Keywords: Lead-cone waveform generator, Final-peak saw-tooth waveform, Shock test, Nonlinear dynamic model, Shock pulse prediction

Introduction

Shock environment is defined as a transient change in velocity field and widely exists in various engineering fields, such as automobile crashing, space equipment launching, and electronics dropping [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. In the shock environment, systems/equipment may undergo sudden energy inputs which would result in large deformation or extreme stress, and even eventually damage or failure. Therefore, various elastoplastic materials and structures are designed and utilized as the shock isolation parts of systems/equipment to prevent damage/failure [6,7]. Examples include elastoplastic steel dampers, which enhance structural strength and reduce seismic responses in buildings [8], bumper beam systems in automobiles designed to absorb kinetic energy during collisions [9], and elastoplastic shock isolators to protect precision satellite devices during separation [10]. Due to the irreversible deformation during energy absorption, elastoplastic materials are widely applied in shock isolation.

To further evaluate reliability and enhance the shock resistance of systems/equipment, three mainstream methods, namely numerical simulation, analytical modeling and experimental testing, are commonly used to analyze their responses in shock environment [[11], [12], [13]]. In terms of numerical simulation, Kezmane et al. [14] analyze the impact response of concrete slab with elastoplastic supports by finite element method and obtain possible failure modes. Marzbanrad et al. [15] compare the stress distribution and energy absorption behavior of different elastoplastic bumpers in simulation model. Similarly, García-Pérez et al. [16] evaluate the capability of space instruments to withstand severe shock environments by numerical simulation. As for analytical dynamic model, Chiaia et al. [17] establish a three-degrees-of-freedom model to simulate the elastoplastic supports response under impact load. Li et al. [18] calculate the maximum shock response of structure based on single-degree-of-freedom model. Although numerical and analytical model can predict shock response, experimental testing remains the only way to obtain accurate results. Therefore, a series of stringent shock tests should be conducted in advance to ensure the safety of the systems/equipment [19]. Besides, relevant shock test standards (e.g. MIL-STD-810, ISO-16750, JESD22-B110, etc.) have been developed by several standardization bodies (e.g. MIL, IEC, JEDEC, etc.) as evaluation criteria for specimen, and these specimens must satisfy the requirements of these standards before practical applications [20,21]. Among these standards, one common-used specification for shock tests is the acceleration-time history which is characterized by the peak value and duration of the shock pulse. As shown in Fig. 1, three classical acceleration-time histories, including the half-sine waveform, the trapezoidal waveform, and the final-peak saw-tooth waveform, are mainly used as the shock test waveform for reliability assessment [22].

Three classical acceleration-time histories for shock tests.

Generally, shock test machines (STMs) generate these classical shock pulses by using an impact mass (drop table/projectile) to collide with the specific waveform generators (e.g. rubber, lead-cone, pneumatic cylinder, etc.) [23]. Obviously, an appropriate waveform generator is the crucial component to generate satisfactory shock pulses, and, thusly, the investigation on the impact response mechanism of different waveform generators has attracted great attention. At present, many literatures for waveform generators concentrates on the dynamics models of rubbery material which have been applied extensively in various shock tests [[24], [25], [26]]. Also, some empirical models based on nonlinear springs and dampers for rubber waveform generators (RWGs) are developed and proved to be capable of predicting the half-sine shock pulse accurately in shock tests [[27], [28], [29]]. Additionally, a waveform generator combining the rubber and electromagnet has been developed to improve the accuracy of half-sine shock pulse and corresponding dynamic model has been established for the prediction of the shock response, which inspires the research of novel waveform generator [30]. In terms of the trapezoidal waveform, various electronic components, such as driving disk, mobile phones, and laptops, are tested under trapezoidal shock pulses to estimate the structural reliabilities of devices [31,32]. In early stages, a piecewise linear model for trapezoidal waveform generator (TWG) is proposed to describe the response of pneumatic cylinder when considering it as a linear spring [33]. Recently, Wen et al. optimize the structure of pneumatic cylinder to remove the residual response of TWG and obtain a more satisfactory trapezoidal waveform [34]. As for final-peak saw-tooth waveform, related researches show much interests on the designing methods of waveform generators to meet requirements of shock tests. Liang proposes a method to reverse-design the shape of the lead-cone based on required shock pulses [35]. However, this method ignores the incompressibility of lead, which causes errors to some extent. Besides, Zhu and Zhang et al. derive expressions to predict the peak acceleration and duration of final-peak saw-tooth waveform from geometric features of LCWG [36,37]. With respect to the simulation, Yang and Liang et al. consider the shape factor and mechanical properties of lead and investigate the pulse regulation of LCWG by finite element method [38,39]. Although, the simulation method considers the shape factor and mechanical properties of LCWG, the complex geometric modeling and large calculation-cost, increase the difficulty of practical guidance for shock tests.

In summary, the main deficiencies in the previous research methods on waveform generator are as follows.

(1)Although the final-peak saw-tooth waveform is primarily produced by most elastoplastic materials or structures due to their unrecoverable deformation under impact loads, there is a lack of systematic research on the mechanism and modeling of this final-peak saw-tooth waveform.

As mentioned above, it is essential to propose a dynamic modeling method that incorporates both the constitutive model and geometric factors of waveform generator, particularly for elastoplastic waveform generators, to analyze and predict shock responses. This efficient dynamic model has broad applications in predicting the shock responses of elastic-plastic structures, such as the seismic resistance of building/bridges, vehicle collisions, and high-speed impacts on aerospace structures [[40], [41], [42]].

As a crucial shock pulse of shock test standards, final-peak saw-tooth waveform is usually used to simulate inelastic impact of equipment/system which does not rebound after collision. Due to large plasticity and mechanical mold-ability, lead is employed as the raw material of waveform generator to generate final-peak saw-tooth waveforms. Additionally, as shown in Fig. 2, the lead waveform generator is usually processed into a cone shape to control the contact area with the impactor. However, different from the RWG and TWG, the LCWG can only be used one time because of irreversible plastic deformation of lead-cone after collision [43]. In addition, the manufacture of LCWG at high temperature (400∼500°C) would produce lead gasification which threatens the health of the experimenters [44]. All these process defects of LCWG result in serious trouble during shock tests. To reduce the manufacturing cost of LCWG and improve the efficiency and accuracy of shock tests, it is necessary to analyze the shock pulse regulation of LCWG systematically; more importantly, establish a direct and clear dynamic model of LCWG to accurately predict the final-peak saw-tooth waveform before shock tests.

A typical final-peak saw-tooth waveform and LCWG.

This work aims to obtain the shock pulse regulation of final-peak saw-tooth waveform and develop a novel nonlinear dynamic model of LCWG during shock tests. Firstly, a series of direct drop tests of LCWG are carried out and the generated final-peak saw-tooth waveforms are analyzed qualitatively and systematically. The correlation analysis includes the impact mass, the impact velocity, and the aspect ratio of LCWG. Meanwhile, the deformation processes of LCWGs during the drop tests is observed by a high-speed photography to reveal the geometric deformation mechanism. Later, mechanical properties of lead under different strain rates are obtained from quasi-static compression tests through an MTS universal testing machine. The strain rate effect on yield stress of lead is described by Cowper-Symonds model. After that, a nonlinear single-degree-of-freedom dynamic model is established by considering both the geometric deformation model of lead-cone and the mechanical model of lead comprehensively. Lastly, the predicted shock pulses can be obtained by solving the dynamic model in time dominate with Runge-Kutta directly.

Material and experiment

Materials

The waveform generator studied in this work is made of lead (99.9% Pb, 0.1% Sb and Sn). The lead is heated to above 400°C, then poured into different conical molds to manufacture lead-cones with different dimensions. To take the geometric parameters into account in shock tests and dynamic model, several LCWGs with different dimensions are prepared for direct drop tests and the shape factor of lead-cone is defined as the aspect ratio (h/(2R)), where h and R are the initial height and bottom radius of LCWG respectively. The specimen groups for drop tests are listed in Table 1 and all the specimen possesses the same bottom radius (R=15mm). In addition, compression tests are also conducted to study the mechanical properties of lead at different strain rates. The lead is cut into cylinder as the specimens, and both end faces of the specimens are polished to guarantee the low-roughness and high-flatness. According to the test requirements, the recommended length and diameter of specimens are ∅7×20mm for quasi-static compression tests [45].

Drop test setup

A drop test machine (DTM) is prepared to study the shock response of LCWG and mainly consists of a rigid base and drop table, two guide columns, and a control system for raise/brake/release. As shown in Fig. 4, the operation of DTM is: (1) initially, the drop table is elevated to a certain height and braked; (2) then, the control system releases the table and drops it along with guide columns to impact a waveform generator placed on the base; (3) lastly, a high precision acceleration sensor installed on the table is used to collect the shock pulse during the collision. A buffering system, which is made of average-distributed hydraulic dampers and air springs, are placed on bottom of the base to reduce the shock effect on subgrade. Moreover, to observe the deformation process of LCWGs, a high-speed camera is employed to capture their deformation-time history at a speed of 20000fps.

Drop tests of LCWGs.

Based on the prepared LCWGs, parameterized correlation analysis for final-peak saw-tooth waveform is conducted by involving three variables: the impact mass (mass of the drop table), impact velocity, and aspect ratio of the LCWG. The drop table in DTM is made of aluminum alloy with mass distribution from 20∼50kg, and the initial impact velocity (v0=2gH) of drop table can be calculated approximately according to its drop height (H), where g represents the gravity. Thus, the different characteristics of shock pulse are determined by coupling effects of the impact mass, the impact velocity, and the aspect ratio of the LCWG. Results and analysis

After a series of necessary data processing, such as signal conversion, filtering, and truncation, the shock acceleration-time history of the drop table and relevant characteristics (peak acceleration Amax and pulse duration D) are obtained for the further analysis in this section. The effect of main parameters, including the impact mass, impact velocity, and the aspect ratio of the LCWG, on the acceleration-time history, the peak acceleration, and the pulse duration are discussed systematically. According to the definition in JEDEC [46], as shown in Fig. 5, the maximum acceleration is defined as the peak of the shock pulse and the duration is defined as the time interval between 10% the peak acceleration during both the rise and the decay portions. For further analyzing the acceleration-time history, the pulse durations of the rise (trise) and the decay (tdecay) are also defined respectively in Fig. 5. More importantly, the deformation rate-time history of each drop test is calculated from dividing the initial height (h) of LCWG by compressive velocity gradient (defined as the integral of the rise duration of acceleration-time history) [47]. The analysis of compressive deformation rate of LCWG is to determine the strain rate range of the lead, which is helpful for the establishment of the subsequent dynamic model.

Drop impact process

To analyze the impact process between the drop table and the LCWG; illustrate the generation mechanism of the final-peak saw-tooth waveform, the impact characteristics of groups 1 and 12 are demonstrated in Fig. 6 simultaneously as the example. It can be noticed that the Amax and D of acceleration-time history are affected by the aspect ratio of LCWG under the same impact mass and velocity. Qualitatively, the Amax decreases with increasing aspect ratio, but D shows the opposite trend. Combining the acceleration-time history and the compressive deformation processes of LCWGs captured by high-speed camera (in Table 3), the overall pulses are mainly divided into two stages: (1) the initial nonlinear stage where acceleration increases slowly; (2) the rapid linear decreasing stage behind the peak acceleration. The long duration for rising acceleration and the short duration for decaying acceleration together form typical final-peak saw-tooth waveform. In the first stage, the initial compression occurs as the top of LCWG contact with drop table, where the contact area increases from zero resulting in minimal interaction force on the drop table. Then, the contact area increases gradually with the increasing compressive displacement, leading to gradual rising acceleration response of the table. When the compression reaches to maximum (t4), the acceleration response increases to the peak correspondingly. After that, the second stage begins as peak acceleration achieves. There is a slight rebound of the drop table can be found at t5, which means that abrupt unloading of table and its acceleration response decay approximate linearly (in Fig. 6 (a)). In addition, the slight vibration, named as the residual response, can be observed after attenuation of acceleration, which is caused by free response of table and should be neglected compared with primary response.

The researches on the stress wave propagation in material/structure indicated there is a local stress non-equilibrium during a short time of initial contact due to the mis-matched wave impedance. After multiple times of reflection and transmission of stress wave, the stress equilibrium and global response can be achieved [48,49]. During the compression, the local stress of initial contact mainly results in the local damage of LCWG, especially at the top of lead-cone. Later, the plastic damage would continue to accumulate with the increasing compressive displacement at the stage of global response. Therefore, it is necessary to analyze the process of initial contact for the further understanding of global response of LCWG.

From Eq. (5), it can be found that contact stresses depend on the impact velocity, wave impedance and contact aera. Generally, the LCWG with small aspect ratio produce large contact aera of LCWG (Aw), which leads to large contact stress. This explains that the initial acceleration response of group 1 is higher than the acceleration of group 12. Moreover, the initial contact aera, Aw, is extremely small due to the cone, leading to large concentrated stress on the top of lead-cone. Thus, it can be deduced that local yield of LCWG has occurred at the initial impact due to the small yield strength of lead [50].

With further compression, the local plastic deformation region of LCWG rapidly expands to the whole region to achieve stress equilibrium, and global response consists of plastic deformation at the top and the elastic deformation at the bottom. Some studies on the mechanical properties of pure lead or Pb-based alloy have demonstrated that mechanical behavior exhibit large plasticity (high elongation and ductility) [51,52]. Moreover, the stress-strain curves of lead under quasi-static uniaxial loading shows short stage of linear elasticity within the strain range of 2%, followed by long stage of plastic flows [53,54]. As a face-centered cubic (FCC) metal, the plastic deformation of lead occurs more easily due to easier cross-slip of grade [55]. Therefore, it can be assumed that the plastic deformations of LCWG are the primary deformation forms during the stage of global response. In terms of the rebounded drop table, it may come from the slight elastic recovery of the compressed LCWG.

It is known that the compressive strain rate of drop table/hammer is not a constant, which affect the mechanical response of material/structure during the whole process [56]. Based on the calculation method for compressive rate of the impact drop hammer in Ref. [57], the deformation strain rate-time history of LCWG can be approximately deduced from Eq. (6). Fig. 7 presents the compress strain rate-time history of group 1 and 12, respectively. Where, the maximum compressive strain rate of group 1 is above 180s−1, which is about 4.5 times higher than that of group 12. Because the mechanical properties of lead exhibits strongly strain rate strengthening effect, the acceleration response of LCWG under higher strain rate (group 1) will become higher [53]. However, the corresponding pulse duration of group 1 will be shorter due to the same impact energy. Another important deduction is that the nonlinear rise duration of acceleration-time history may affect by both variable contact aera of LCWG and compressive strain rate, which is significant for subsequent establishment of dynamic model.

Drop test results

Aspect ratio effect

To investigate the aspect ratio effect on the of LCWG on the generated shock pulse, the experimental results of five groups with the same impact mass and impact velocity are analyzed. The key parameters of the results, such as the peak acceleration (Amax), the pulse duration (D), the rising duration (trise), the decaying duration (tdecay), and compressive strain rate (ε˙), are plotted in Fig. 8, Fig. 9, and listed in Table 4, simultaneously. In Fig. 8, Amax decreases with the increasing aspect ratio generally, and, Amax is about 220g when aspect ratio of LCWG equals to 0.3 which is near to 4 times higher than that of group 13. On the contrary, D increases obviously as the aspect ratio increases. This phenomenon can be explained from two aspects. On the one hand, the LCWG with small aspect ratio produce large initial contact area (Aw) with the drop table at the initial impact due to its geometric characteristics, resulting in large contact force and high acceleration response. On the other hand, the LCWG with small aspect ratio possess small height (h), correspondingly, the compressive distance of LCWG is limited and short, which leads to narrower pulse duration and high compressive strain rate under the same impact velocity. As shown in Fig. 9, the maximum strain rate of LCWG with minimum aspect ratio (0.3) reaches to 300s−1, which is about 5 times of the LCWG with the aspect ratio of 1.5. The strain rate strengthening effect will contribute to higher acceleration response of drop table. Therefore, the shock pulse of the LCWG with smaller aspect ratio possess higher peak acceleration but narrower pulse duration (D) under the same impact energy due to its geometric deformation regulation and strain rate strengthening effect.

These can also be attributed to the rapid changes of contact area and strain rate effect of lead. In the whole compression process, the contact areas for all the LCWGs are varied from nearly zero to the maximum. As mentioned above, the contact area and compressive strain rate of LCWG with smaller aspect ratio are larger than those of the LCWG with bigger aspect ratio within the whole compression, thus, leading to sharp rise of the acceleration-time history. As for pulse duration, the increasing trend is approximately linear with increasing aspect ratio, shown in Fig. 8 (b). It can be commonly assumed that pulse duration is determined by the compressive distance of LCWG. Due to the small contact area of LCWG with high aspect ratio, it produces the weak contact force to the drop table, forming larger compressive distance and pulse duration finally. Meanwhile, there is no obvious fluctuation on the decay duration, which indicates that the rebound velocities of the drop tables are similar. It is known that the elastic recovery (rebound) of LCWG mainly depends on its material properties, especially Young's modulus. Consequently, the nearly unchanged Young's modulus of lead material under the identical impact velocity leads to the similar effect on rebound of drop table [53].

Impact velocity effect

To determine the impact velocity effect on shock pulse, the experimental results of groups 4∼7 are compared, as shown in Fig. 10. With the same impact mass and aspect ratio of LCWG, the impact velocity is positively correlated with the Amax, but there is a negative correlation between the impact velocity and D. These can be attributed to the coupling effect between geometric deformation mechanism of LCWG and strain rate strengthening of lead. In general, the high impact velocity will produce large contact aera with impactor in a short time, which forms large contact force and finally leads to high peak acceleration. Meanwhile, high impact velocity corresponds to high compressive strain rate. As shown in Fig. 11, the initial compressive strain rate increase nearly twice as the impact velocity elevates from 2.42 to 4.85m/s, which leads to strain rate strengthen of LCWG during the whole compression. Thus, the contact force would increase rapidly and form sharp rise stage of acceleration-time history. As for the decreased pulse duration, it can be explained that the large contact force produces a large deceleration for the drop table so that the velocity of table rapidly reduces to zero and the pulse duration becomes small although the initial velocity of table is high.

Impact mass effect

The experimental results of groups 6 and 8∼10 are shown in Fig. 12 to study the impact mass effect on the shock pulse. It can be found the Amax increases linearly, while the D decreases slightly with the increase of the impact mass, which is due to the high impact kinetic energy for large impact mass. When the impact mass increases, the impact kinetic energy also increases, and it means the LCWG needs to undergo greater deformation to absorb energy. Therefore, the compressive distance and contact aera of LCWG increases more easily as the impact mass increases, where the contact force for the deformation resistance of LCWG also increases sharply, leading to the high peak acceleration. In addition, the decreased pulse duration is mainly determined by compressive time of LCWG. Similarly, due to the contact force of drop table increases as the impact mass elevated, the attenuation of the drop table velocity is more obvious, resulting in decreased pulse duration. However, the change of pulse duration with different impact mass is not significant. This is because the initial impact velocity of drop table is the same, thus, the compressive strain rate for each group is also similar, as shown in Fig. 13. Under the same strain rate, the effect of strain rate strengthen is not obvious and the increase of contact force caused by strain rate effect will also be suppressed. In the end, the pulse duration is hardly affected by impact mass. Moreover, the similar initial rise stage of acceleration-time history in Fig. 12 (a) can also demonstrate that initial compressive processes is not influenced by impact mass.

Mechanical behavior characteristics and strain rate effect

According to the experimental results above, all the acceleration-time history exhibits the long duration for nonlinear growth to peak, then rapid linear decay within short duration, which is not only related to the geometric features of LCWG but also the mechanical behavior of the lead, especially strain rate strengthening effect. Therefore, it is necessary to analyze the mechanical properties of pure lead under uniaxial compression. Fig. 14 (a) presents the true stress-strain curves of pure lead under low and intermediate strain rates, which are originated from the quasi-state compression and Ref. [43], respectively. As a ductile FCC metal, all the mechanical responses of lead at different strain rates shows obvious elastic-plastic features. For example, the range of linear elastic stages is limited and short compared with the whole plastic strain range, and the continuous strain hardening on flow stress in plastic stage is significant. Moreover, the influence of strain rate on the flow stress at various strain can be observed easily, as displayed in Fig. 14 (b). As the strain rate increases from 0.001 to 5s−1, the relationship between strain rate and flow stress is approximately linear, especially at the range of quasi-state. This is owing to that the improvement of magnitude of strain rate produces more remarkable strengthening effect. However, with the further increase of strain rate (above 575s−1), the flow stress increases slowly due to the instability at intermediate strain rates, i.e. 575s−1 and 2000s−1.

Nonlinear dynamic model of LCWGs

In section 3, the relationships between the shock pulse, compressive strain rate and experimental variable parameters (aspect ratio of LCWGs, impact velocity and impact mass) have been analyzed qualitatively. Although these analyses can provide approximate estimation and engineering guidance for drop shock tests, the whole final-peak saw-tooth waveforms cannot be predicted precisely. Therefore, it is necessary to establish a dynamic model of LCWGs for the accurate prediction of the entire acceleration-time history.

Model description

Geometry assumption

As analyzed above, the changes of contact area (Aw) between LCWG and drop table is a key factor for the nonlinear shock response within the compression. Moreover, the plastic deformation of lead-cone is predominated form due to the slight elastic stage of lead. Generally, the strain partial tensors mainly determinate the shear and plastic deformation of materials, which will not affect their volume, thus, it can be assumed that the volume of LCWGs remains a constant during the compression [58]. In section 3, the initial impact velocity of drop table is evaluated roughly by free drop regulation and the compressive strain rate-time history is calculated approximately by Eq. (6). It is known that there are two main factors affecting the initial impact velocity, the velocity loss derived from the friction of guide column and different height of LCWG due to its aspect ratio. Therefore, to describe the initial compressive strain rate effect more accurately, the relationship between the losses of initial impact velocity, drop height of table and aspect ratio of LCWG in shock test system should be established quantitatively.

It can be observed that the predicted acceleration-time histories agree well with the experimental results, which indicates that the dynamic model possesses the ability to accurately describe the compressive behaviors of LCWGs. From Table 9, the minimum of Rd2 is 0.93, which indicates the whole shapes of acceleration-time history coincide well. Moreover, the prediction errors of peak acceleration (Amax) and pulse duration (D) are less than 4.04% and 4.91%, respectively. Nevertheless, there are some local deviations between prediction and experimental ones. For example, nearby the peak acceleration, the predicted value is a little lower than the experimental value and the slight deviations of acceleration decay can also be observed. There are two statements to account for the predicted acceleration deviations. One is that the geometric deformation process of LCWG under shock tests is assumed ideally in the dynamic model. In fact, the shape of lead-cone cannot keep ideally conical during compression due to the local deformation on its top. Besides, the piecewise deformation hypothesis, especially at the rebound stage, can result in discontinuity of the acceleration pulse. The other is the simplified material model of lead. In fact, the flow stress is related not only to strain rate but also to its strain. Although the strong nonlinearity of yield stress has been developed by considering the strain rate effect, the degree of nonlinearity is still not enough to describe the stress change of LCWGs during the compression process. Additionally, the deviations of duration are more obvious than peak acceleration. As shown in Fig. 22(a–g, j), the rise durations of predicted pulse are much higher than experimental results, which can be explained by the insufficient nonlinearity of yield stress and contact area. In addition, the Young's modulus of lead can also be influenced by strain rate slightly, which has a certain effect on duration decay. These deficiencies in the proposed dynamic model are worthy for further investigation, and will be improved in our subsequent research.

Conclusions

In this study, shock tests and uniaxial compressions on LCWG to are conducted directly to explore the mechanism of generating final-peak saw-tooth waveforms. The generation and regulation effect of acceleration-time history on aspect ratio of LCWG, impact velocity and mass of drop table are observed and analyzed systematically. In addition, a nonlinear dynamic model is developed to describe and predict the acceleration-time history. In this model, the geometric characteristics of LCWG during compression and the strain rate-dependent mechanical model of lead are incorporated simultaneously. In the end, the predicted shock pulses based on the dynamic model are compared with the experimental results, which shows a good agreement. The main findings can be summarized as follows.

(1)The compressive process of LCWG can be mainly divided into two stages: the compression to maximum deformation and slight rebound. During the initial contact between the LCWG and the drop table, the local plastic deformation on the top of LCWG plays a dominated role, and this plastic response is maintained to its maximum deformation. In the rebound stage, there is a slight displacement, which is related to the elasticity of the lead material.

In the rebound stage, there is a slight displacement, which is related to the elasticity of the lead material.

(2)The correlation analysis of drop impact pulse reveals that aspect ratio of LCWG, impact velocity and mass of drop table has significant effect on the peak acceleration and duration of pulse. In detail, the aspect ratio of LCWG is negatively correlated with peak acceleration but positively correlated with duration. There is a positive correlation between impact velocity and peak acceleration, while the duration exhibits a decreasing trend with impact velocity. As for the mass effect, the peak acceleration improves with the increasing impact mass but the duration is not sensitive to the changes of the impact mass. These relationships can provide guidance for the approximate adjusting of shock tests.
