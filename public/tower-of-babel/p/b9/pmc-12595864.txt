Genome-wide identification and expression analysis of the PtrUGT gene family in Populus trichocarpa
Authors: Lingyun Liu, Gaixia Yang, Shijie Wang, Chunyu Wang, Lijiao Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
UDP-glycosyltransferases (UGTs) were widely distributed in plants and played crucial roles in mediating glycosylation reactions associated with metabolic pathways. Although the UGT gene family has been characterized in numerous plant species, a systematic analysis in Populus trichocarpa still requires further refinement. In this study, 204 PtrUGT genes were identified through genome-wide analysis, revealing significant variations in protein length, molecular weight, and isoelectric point. Chromosomal mapping revealed an uneven distribution across all 19 chromosomes, with chr16 exhibiting the highest gene density. Furthermore, tandem duplication events were identified as the primary drivers of gene family expansion. Synteny analysis of P. trichocarpa identified 266 paralogous PtrUGT gene pairs, with significant enrichment on Chr16, which were highly conserved among closely related woody plants. Phylogenetic classification grouped the PtrUGTs into 19 distinct subgroups (A-S), with subgroup-specific motif conservation and gene structures. Promoter analysis uncovered abundant cis-regulatory elements associated with light, methyl jasmonate, abscisic acid, and stress responses, indicating functional diversification among the PtrUGT genes. Both RNA-seq and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analyses revealed tissue-specific expression patterns and stress-responsive regulation, with certain PtrUGTs showing significant induction under drought, salt stress, or insect herbivory stress. Subcellular localization analysis revealed that the stress-responsive PtrUGT198 was present in both the nucleus and the cytoplasm. This study provides a systematic characterization of the PtrUGT family, offering valuable insights for identifying genes related to stress resistance and facilitating molecular breeding strategies in poplar.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07572-w.

Introduction

Glycosyltransferases (GTs) are enzymes that catalyze the transfer of glycosyl groups from donor molecules, such as nucleotide sugars, to acceptor molecules. According to the glycosylation site of the receptor molecule, GTs are classified into four types: C-GTs, O-GTs, S-GTs, and N-GTs [1, 2]. GTs form a superfamily that is systematically divided into 138 families in the CAZy database (https://www.cazy.org/GlycosylTransferase-family, accessed in 2025). GT1 is the largest family within the GT superfamily and is also referred to as the UGT family due to its use of UDP-glucose as the glycosyl donor [3]. In plants, the N-terminal region of UGT gene family members exhibits high sequence diversity, while the C-terminal region contains a highly conserved 44-amino acid sequence known as the Plant Secondary Product Glycosyltransferase (PSPG) box [4]. The binding domains for the sugar acceptor and sugar donor are located in the Rossmann-like folds of the N-terminal and C-terminal regions, respectively. Members of the UGT gene family share ≥ 40% amino acid sequence similarity, while subfamily members share ≥ 60% similarity [5].

In recent years, with the continuous completion of plant genome sequencing projects, the UGT gene family has been identified in numerous species, including Arabidopsis thaliana (107 members) [6], Oryza sativa (215) [7], Zea mays (147) [8], Gossypium hirsutum (274) [9], Manihot esculenta (121) [10], Acer rubrum (249) [11], Nilaparvata lugens (20) [12], Platycodon grandiflorus (107) [13], and Meconopsis wilsonii (26) [14]. Recent studies have revealed that UGT enzymes mediate diverse biochemical processes critical for plant metabolism and stress responses. For example, Heterologous expression of the StUGT178 gene in Arabidopsis demonstrated that StUGT178 exhibits a significant response to salt treatment and enhances salt tolerance by regulating antioxidant enzyme activity and reducing H₂O₂ accumulation [4]. Silencing CsUGT87A1 reduced drought resistance and IAA levels in tea plants, while heterologous expression of this gene enhanced drought tolerance and promoted IAA accumulation in Arabidopsis, demonstrating its role in improving drought resistance through IAA homeostasis regulation [15]. CRISPR/Cas9 knockout of GmUGT enhanced soybean resistance to cotton bollworm and beet armyworm by altering flavonoid content and defense gene expression, while overexpression increased susceptibility [16]. Overexpression of UGT75L28 in transgenic poplar lines leads to decreased petiole lignification, whereas its knockdown produces the opposite effect [17]. Overexpression of NtUGT217 in N. attenuate increased the accumulation of specific types of flavonoids, including quercetin-3-O-glucoside, quercetin-3-O-rutin, and kaempferol-3-O-rutin [18]. Ectopic overexpression of ATUGT74E2 enhances seed germination and regulates stress tolerance through abscisic acid (ABA) signalling in rice [19]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that the UGT gene family, widely conserved across plant species, plays crucial roles in stress adaptation, secondary metabolism, growth regulation, and hormonal homeostasis through diverse biochemical mechanisms.

Poplar is a deciduous tree genus distributed worldwide and is widely cultivated in China due to its rapid growth, strong adaptability, and economic benefits [20]. Moreover, poplar is widely recognized as a model for studying woody plants, being the first perennial woody plant to have its entire genome sequenced [21]. Given the well-documented roles of UGTs in plant growth, development, and stress responses [16, 22, 23], a comprehensive genomic survey and systematic characterization of the UGT gene family in P. trichocarpa were conducted, encompassing analyses of gene structure, protein features, chromosomal localization, synteny, and promoter cis-regulatory elements. Additionally, transcriptomic profiling and qRT-PCR were performed to investigate tissue-specific expression patterns and stress-responsive regulation under both biotic and abiotic conditions. The subcellular localization of UGT198 was further determined. Our findings offer a fundamental gene resource and theoretical foundation for the functional studies of UGT genes in poplar.

Materials and methods

Identification of PtrUGT gene family in P. trichocarpa

Two methods, BLAST and hmmsearch, were utilized to identify the members of the UGT family in P. trichocarpa. The genome sequence, annotation, CDS, and protein sequences for P. trichocarpa (version 4.1) were retrieved from Phytozome (https://phytozome.jgi.doe.gov/). UGT protein sequences from A. thaliana served as queries for a BLASTP search against the P. trichocarpa protein database. An e-value threshold of 1e-5 was established to identify putative UGT protein sequences, thereby facilitating the selection of initial candidates for the PtrUGT genes in P. trichocarpa. Additionally, the PSPG domain (PF00201) hidden Markov model (HMM) from Pfam (http://pfam.xfam.org/) [24] was employed for hmmsearch screening. Redundant candidates were eliminated, and domain validation was conducted using InterPro (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/), Pfam (http://pfam.xfam.org/), and the Conserved Domain Database (CDD) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi). Sequences that lacked the PSPG structural domain or exhibited incomplete PSPG domains were manually excluded from the dataset. This comprehensive approach ultimately resulted in the identification of the UGT gene family members in P. trichocarpa. All PtrUGT protein sequences were uploaded to the DeepLoc 2.0 web server (http://localizer.csiro.au/) for subcellular localization prediction [25].

Chromosome localization and synteny analysis

Chromosome localization mapping of the PtrUGT family members was performed using MapChart software [26]. Genome sequence files for P. trichocarpa, P. euphratica, A. thaliana, O. sativa, and E. robusta were compared and analyzed using the One Step MCScanX-Super Fast tool within TBtools software [27]. Based on the findings of this comparative analysis, synteny map was conducted using MCScanX software with default parameters to identify syntenic relationships and potential gene duplications among the selected species.

Phylogenetic analysis

Multiple sequence alignment of UGT protein sequences from P. trichocarpa and A. thaliana was conducted using MUSCLE v3 [28]. The phylogenetic tree was constructed utilizing the neighbor-joining (NJ) method based on biological evolutionary distances in MEGA X software, with a bootstrap value set to 1000 [29].

Gene structure and motif prediction

Using the GFF files and amino acid sequences of the P. trichocarpa genome, the Gene Structure View function in TBtools [27] was employed to visualize the gene structure of PtrUGTs. Conserved motifs were analyzed using the online MEME suite (http://meme-suite.org/tools/meme) [30].

Promoter

Promoter cis-acting element analysis

The 2000 bp sequence located upstream of the start codon of the UGT genes was retrieved from the P. trichocarpa genome. Subsequently, the analysis of promoter cis-acting elements was conducted using the PlantCARE tool (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [31].

Transcriptome analysis of UGT gene family expression

The expression patterns of the UGT gene family in poplar were analyzed using public transcriptome data from various tissues, including three-cell-type (fibers, vessels, rays), as well as shoot, root, leaf, xylem, phloem, vessel, and fiber (SRX1740285-SRX1740305). Additionally, responses to various hormone treatments, such as SA and JA, were examined (SRX5181815-SRX5181841), along with abiotic stresses including salt, drought, heat and cold (ERR1864411-ERR1864437), pathogen responses (SRR12371687-SRR12371698), and treatments for the brassy willow beetles (Phratora vitellinae) (SRR8424223-SRR8609265). All original RNA-seq data were obtained from NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). TopHat was employed to align RNA-seq reads to the genome of P. trichocarpa (version 4.1), and the Cufflinks package was used to analyze differentially expressed genes based on fragments per kilobase million (FPKM) values [32]. The heatmaps representing gene expression were created and visualized using TBtools software [27]. Details regarding the experimental materials, treatment concentrations, and durations can be found in Yang et al. (2023) [33].

Plant materials and stress treatments

The ‘741 poplar’ (P. alba × [(P. davidiana) + (P. simonii)] × P. tomentosa) was selected as the experimental material for the stress experiments. The seedlings were cultivated in a greenhouse under controlled conditions of 25 ± 2 °C temperature, light intensity ranging from 1500 to 2000 lx, and a 14-h light/10-hdark cycle. Upon reaching a height of 12–15 cm, the seedlings underwent stress treatments. For abiotic stress studies, the seedlings were subjected to irrigation with 200 mM NaCl and 6% PEG6000. Leaf samples were collected 24 h after the stress application. In the insect feeding experiment, Hyphantria cunea larvae were allowed to feed on the leaves, and leaf samples were collected at 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, and 12 h post-feeding. The 0-h time point served as the control (CK) in these stress treatments. Each treatment included three biological replicates. Following collection, the samples were promptly flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis.

qRT-PCR assay

Genes exhibiting higher expression levels in the transcriptome were selected, and specific primers for the chosen PtrUGTs were designed using Primer 5.0 software. This was done to analyze the expression patterns of the UGT gene family across different tissues, under salt and drought stress conditions, and at various times points during insect feeding. RNA was extracted from the samples using a Polysaccharide Polyphenol Plant Total RNA Extraction Kit (TIANGEN; DP441). cDNA synthesis was performed using the FastKing RT Kit (with gDNase) (TIANGEN; KR116), following the manufacturer’s instructions. For qRT-PCR, SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche) was used, with the reaction mixture containing 10 µL of 2 × FastReal qPCR PreMix (SYBR Green), 1 µL of cDNA, 0.6 µL of forward primer (10 µM), 0.6 µL of reverse primer (10 µM), 0.4 µL of 50 × ROX Reference Dye, and 7 µL of ddH2O, all measured using the Agilent Mx3005P PCR system. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 15 s. Expression levels were calculated using the ΔΔCT method to determine the fold change (Fold change = 2−[ΔΔCT]). ACT7 was utilized as an internal reference gene for normalization of gene expression data. Each experiment consisted of three replicates, and the primers were listed in Table S1. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, with a significance level set at P < 0.05.

Subcellular localization

The UGT198 gene, which lacking a stop codon, was cloned and inserted into the pBWA(V)HS-ccdb-GLosgfp vector that had been digested with BsaI and Eco31I endonucleases to create the pBWA(V)HS-UGT198 fusion vector. This fusion vector was then transformed into competent E. coli cells and spread on a kanamycin-resistant plate, which was incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. Ten single colonies were selected for PCR amplification and sequence validation. The correctly constructed plasmid was then transfected into Agrobacterium tumefaciens (EHA105). Subsequently, Agrobacterium was scraped from the solid Petri dish with an inoculation loop, combined with 10 mL of YEB liquid medium, and incubated at 170 rpm for 1 h. The mixture was then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 4 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The bacterial pellet was resuspended in the remaining liquid, and the OD600 was adjusted to approximately 0.6. Robustly growing Nicotiana attenuata seedlings were selected, and the resuspended Agrobacterium suspension was injected into the lower epidermis of the N. attenuata leaf blade using a syringe with a deneedled tip. The injected N. attenuata plants were then placed under low light conditions and incubated for 2 days. The labelled N. attenuata leaves were collected to prepare slides, which were subsequently observed and photographed under a laser confocal microscope.

Results

Genome-wide identification and characterization of PtrUGT gene family in P. trichocarpa

Both BLAST and hmmsearch were employed to identify the UGT family genes. After validating the conserved domains and applying manual filtering, a total of 204 PtrUGTs (designated as PtrUGT1 to PtrUGT204) were identified. The results revealed significant variation in the amino acid sequence length, molecular weight, and isoelectric point among the PtrUGT members (Table S2). For instance, PtrUGT87 encodes only 177 amino acid residues, corresponding to a protein molecular weight of 20.01 kDa, whereas PtrUGT92 encodes 862 amino acid residues with a molecular weight of 96.73 kDa. Additionally, the isoelectric points of the PtrUGTs ranged from 4.83 to 8.54. The aliphatic index varied from 72.09 to 102.55. Among the 204 PtrUGT proteins, 171 had a grand average of hydropathicity value below 0, indicating that the majority of PtrUGT proteins are hydrophilic. Furthermore, 199 PtrUGT proteins exhibited a transmembrane domain count of 0, suggesting that most of them are non-membrane proteins. Subcellular localization prediction revealed that the vast majority of PtrUGT proteins were localized in the cytoplasm. In addition, PtrUGT92 was targeted to the nucleus, PtrUGT126 was localized in the endoplasmic reticulum (Table S2).

Chromosome distribution of PtrUGT genes

Gene duplication serves as a crucial mechanism driving functional gene divergence [34, 35]. In this study, we identified 150 tandemly duplicated PtrUGT gene pairs (Fig. 1, Table S3). To visualize the distribution of PtrUGTs in the poplar genome, a chromosomal localization map was constructed. As shown in Fig. 1, the 204 PtrUGTs are unevenly distributed across the 19 chromosomes. Notably, chr16 harbors the highest density of PtrUGTs, with 45 genes, followed by chr6 (28 PtrUGTs), chr17 (21 PtrUGTs), and chr1 (20 PtrUGTs). Additionally, chr9 contains 16 PtrUGTs, while chr 4 and chr7 each have 13 PtrUGTs. Chr2, chr10, and chr14 each contain 7 PtrUGTs, and chr12 harbors 5 PtrUGTs. Chr3 has 4 PtrUGTs, while chr5, chr13, chr15, and chr18 contain 3 PtrUGTs each. Chr8 and chr11 each have 2 PtrUGTs, and only a single PtrUGT is located on each of scaffold_25 and scaffold_1865.

Syntenic analysis of PtrUGT genes in intra- and inter-species comparisons

Genome-wide synteny analysis identified 266 paralogous PtrUGT gene pairs in P. trichocarpa, including 116 segmental duplication gene pairs and 150 tandem duplication gene pairs (Figs. 1 and 2, Table S3). Paralogous PtrUGT gene pairs on chr16 accounted for 63.91% (170/266) of the total, with segmental duplication pairs comprising 29.32% (78/266) and tandem duplication pairs 34.59% (92/266). The PtrUGT genes on Chr16 had a linear relationship only with the PtrUGT genes on Chr06, Chr07, and Chr14 (Fig. 2b, Table S3). The duplicated genes on Chr16 mainly originated from the A and M subfamilies, with both members of each pair belonging to the same subfamily. In contrast, paralogous PtrUGT gene pairs on other chromosomes made up 36.09% (96/266), including 14.29% (38/266) segmental duplication pairs and 21.80% (58/266) tandem duplication pairs (Fig. 2a, Table S3). In the pairs of tandemly and segmentally duplicated genes, there were 56 genes related to chr16, accounting for 35.22% of the total; whereas there were 103 genes that are completely unrelated, making up 64.78%. These results indicated a significant enrichment of PtrUGT gene pairs associated with chr16, suggesting that chr16 may play an important role in the amplification and functional diversity of PtrUGT genes.

Phylogenetic analysis of PtrUGT family genes

To explore the evolutionary relationships, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the amino acid sequences of UGTs from P. trichocarpa and A. thaliana. The results revealed that the PtrUGTs were classified into 19 subgroups (A-S). Subgroup A, comprising 44 genes (21.6% of total), was the largest group, followed by subgroup M with 31 genes (15.2%). Subgroup I contained only one gene (PtrUGT156, 0.5%). Subgroups E, L, and P each included 14 PtrUGTs (6.9%), while subgroups J and K each contained 9 PtrUGTs (4.4%). Subgroup R comprised 8 PtrUGTs (3.9%), and subgroups F and Q each had 7 PtrUGTs (3.4%). Subgroups B, C, G, and O each contained 6 PtrUGTs (2.9%), while subgroup H included 5 PtrUGTs (2.5%). Subgroup S consisted of 4 PtrUGTs (2.0%), and subgroups D and N each contained only 2 PtrUGTs (1.0%) (Fig. 4). Each subgroup contained UGT genes from both P. trichocarpa and A. thaliana, indicating their conserved evolutionary origins. The classification of PtrUGTs into 19 subgroups with varying gene numbers suggests diverse evolutionary dynamics and functional specialization within the P. trichocarpa UGT gene family.

Conserved motifs and gene structure analysis

To investigate the characteristics of PtrUGT members, we analyzed conserved motifs and gene structures, together with the phylogenetic tree (Figs. 5 and 6). Phylogenetic analysis grouped the PtrUGT genes into distinct groups, with members of each group sharing highly similar motif compositions and gene structure (Fig. 6a). Conserved motif analysis revealed that most PtrUGT proteins contained comparable motif types and arrangements, with motif 4 predominantly located at the C-terminus and motif 10 frequently found at the N-terminus. Motifs 1, 2, 3, 7, and 9 were present in all subfamilies; motif 4 occurred in every subfamily except J. Motif 5 was restricted to subfamilies A, B, D, H, G, F, Q, P and R and was absent from all others. Notably, subfamily A contains the largest repertoire of conserved motifs: motif 6 was found in every member of subfamily A and only sporadically in PtrUGT91/199 of subfamilies D and L, indicating that motif 6 was a diagnostic signature of subfamily A. Motif 8 was missing from subfamily F but present in all other subfamilies, whereas motif 10 was found in all subfamilies except S and R (Fig. 6b). Exon-intron structure analysis revealed considerable variation in exon numbers among PtrUGT genes, ranging from 1 to 20. The majority of PtrUGT genes (112, 54.9%) contained a single exon, while 85 (41.7%) and 4 (2.0%) genes possessed two and three exons, respectively. Notably, PtrUGT92 exhibited an exceptional configuration with 20 exons, whereas PtrUGT13 and PtrUGT86 possessed 5 and 4 exons, respectively. Within each subfamily, PtrUGT genes generally maintained conserved exon-intron architectures, though exceptions were observed. For instance, while most members of subfamily A featured two exons, PtrUGT59 uniquely contained three exons (Fig. 6c). The conservation of similar gene structures and motif distributions among different UGT subfamilies suggested their close evolutionary relationships, while the observed structural variations within subfamilies might indicate functional diversification among these members.

Cis-acting element analysis of the PtrUGT promoters

Analysis of PtrUGT promoter regions using PlantCARE identified 23 distinct cis-acting elements associated with diverse biological processes, including phytohormone signalling and responses to biotic/abiotic stresses (Fig. 7a). Quantitative assessment revealed these elements comprised: light-responsive elements, MeJA-responsive regulatory elements, anaerobic induction elements, and response elements for ABA, gibberellin (GA), and salicylic acid (SA), along with stress-responsive elements related to defense mechanisms, drought, and low-temperature adaptation (Fig. 7b). Ten element types occurred with frequencies exceeding 100 instances, including light response (2,469), MeJA-response (538), anaerobic induction (464), ABA-responsive (416), GA-responsive (158), SA-responsive (117), zein metabolism regulation (116), drought-inducible (110), auxin-responsive (109), and defense and stress response (107) elements. Notably, PtrUGT41 uniquely harbored dehydration-, low temperature-, and salt stress-related cis-elements, while light-responsive elements were conserved across all family members. Collectively, the predominance of light- and hormone-responsive elements over stress-responsive elements suggests PtrUGTs may primarily participate in stress adaptation through light-mediated regulation and hormone signalling pathways.

Expression patterns of PtrUGTs in different tissues

The expression patterns of PtrUGTs across various tissues (three-cell-type, shoot, root, leaf, xylem, phloem, vessel, and fiber) were analyzed using public transcriptome data. The expression patterns of PtrUGT genes varied across different tissues (Fig. 8). In particular, genes with significantly elevated expression were predominantly concentrated in leaves and roots, and were evenly distributed across all 19 subfamilies (A-S). Notably, every member of subfamily N exhibited markedly high expression in both leaves and roots. PtrUGT107, PtrUGT181, PtrUGT182, and PtrUGT87 exhibited relatively low expression levels across all tissues. The highest number of genes with elevated expression levels was observed in roots, followed by leaves. Among these, 22 genes showed high expression levels in both roots and leaves, including PtrUGT109, PtrUGT134, PtrUGT133, PtrUGT120, PtrUGT126, PtrUGT94, PtrUGT147, PtrUGT148, PtrUGT158, PtrUGT191, PtrUGT108, PtrUGT34, PtrUGT90, PtrUGT122, PtrUGT129, PtrUGT8, PtrUGT32, PtrUGT136, PtrUGT23, PtrUGT25, PtrUGT43, PtrUGT58. PtrUGT128 and PtrUGT172 exhibit higher expression levels in the xylem. In the phloem, PtrUGT109, PtrUGT134, PtrUGT132, PtrUGT64, PtrUGT174, PtrUGT19, PtrUGT42, PtrUGT137, PtrUGT24, PtrUGT57, PtrUGT110, and PtrUGT28 showed elevated expression levels, with PtrUGT137, PtrUGT24, PtrUGT57, PtrUGT110, and PtrUGT28 also displaying high expression levels in roots. Additionally, PtrUGT174, PtrUGT19, and PtrUGT42 exhibited higher expression levels in shoot. Genes like PtrUGT54, PtrUGT119, PtrUGT154, PtrUGT112, PtrUGT111, PtrUGT139, PtrUGT102, PtrUGT131, PtrUGT178, and PtrUGT149 showed higher expression levels in vessels, with PtrUGT119 also exhibiting elevated expression in roots and PtrUGT149 in leaves. PtrUGT177, PtrUGT127, and PtrUGT93 demonstrated high expression levels in fibers, with PtrUGT177 and PtrUGT127 also showing elevated expression in roots.

Expression patterns of PtrUGT genes under hormone stress treatments

Most PtrUGT genes were upregulated under both SA and JA hormone stresses and were evenly distributed across all 19 subfamilies (A-S), with no evident subfamily-specific expression patterns. Among them, PtrUGT56, PtrUGT57, PtrUGT61, PtrUGT60, PtrUGT58, PtrUGT25, PtrUGT100, PtrUGT156, PtrUGT101, PtrUGT172, PtrUGT198, PtrUGT197, PtrUGT81, PtrUGT91, PtrUGT106, PtrUGT35, PtrUGT36, PtrUGT22, PtrUGT170, PtrUGT181, and PtrUGT98 genes exhibited the highest expression levels at 2 h under JA treatment. Following this, PtrUGT70, PtrUGT81, PtrUGT18, PtrUGT122, PtrUGT171, PtrUGT83, PtrUGT19, PtrUGT95, and PtrUGT176 genes showed the highest expression levels at 6 h under JA treatment. PtrUGT194, PtrUGT144, PtrUGT118, and PtrUGT117 genes exhibited the highest expression levels at 12 h under JA treatment. PtrUGT64, PtrUGT65, PtrUGT155, PtrUGT151, PtrUGT69, and PtrUGT72 genes showed the highest expression levels at 24 h under JA treatment, indicating their involvement in the JA biosynthesis-related pathways. Discussion

Glycosylation was recognized as a ubiquitous modification reaction in plants and served as a crucial mechanism for maintaining cellular metabolic homeostasis, a process primarily catalyzed by UGTs [5, 36]. Recent studies demonstrated that plant UGTs played essential roles in multiple biological processes, including seed size, disease and pest resistance, drought and salt stress responses, temperature adaptation, hormone signalling, and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites [11, 37–41]. To date, the UGT gene family has been identified in multiple plant species [6, 13, 40, 42]. In this study, a total of 204 PtrUGT genes were identified in the P. trichocarpa genome. These genes were found to be unevenly distributed across all 19 chromosomes and were phylogenetically classified into 19 distinct groups (A-S), whereas 134 PeUGT genes identified in P. euphratica were classified into 16 major groups (A-P), with UGT genes within the same group exhibiting similar structural characteristics [43]. In addition, 191 PtrUGT genes were reported by Rehman et al. (2022) in the P. trichocarpa genome, which were distributed across all chromosomes except chromosome 20 and were phylogenetically classified into 16 major groups (A-P) based on reference UGT genes from 22 A. thaliana sequences [44]. In contrast, the variations observed between the two studies on the same species can be primarily attributed to the different methodologies used for gene-family identification. Rehman et al. (2022) employed the PopGenie database, constructed an HMM for the PSPG-box motif within UGTs, and identified UGT sequences via BLAST [44]. In comparison, the current study used BLASTP and hmmsearch to query the P. trichocarpa genome, followed by validation of the conserved domains using Pfam (PF00201) and manual curation. These methodological refinements account for the observed differences in gene numbers. Additionally, the divergence in phylogenetic classification was suggested to stem from differences in the species included in the analysis, the phylogenetic tree construction software that was used, and subjective criteria that were applied during the classification process. These methodological and data refinements provide a more refined and comprehensive landscape of the PtrUGT gene family.

Gene structure analysis revealed that 96.6% (197/204) of PtrUGT genes were found to contain only 1–2 exons in P. trichocarpa. This structural characteristic has also been observed in other plant species, including A. rubrum (80.7%, 249/200 ArUGT genes) [11], Arachis hypogaea (77.7%, 269/208 AhUGT genes) [45], P. euphratica (86.57%, 116/134 PeUGT genes) [43], and Solanum tuberosum (94.9%, 295/280 StUGT genes) [4], all exhibiting similar 1–2 exon structural features. These data indicated that while the proportion of UGT genes with 1–2 exons varies among different species, their gene structures demonstrate remarkable conservation across species. The exceptionally high percentage of 1–2 exon PtrUGT genes in P. trichocarpa may reflect selective pressures or functional adaptations during the evolutionary process of this species [11]. Notably, phylogenetically closely related PtrUGT subfamily members also showed highly similar conserved motif arrangements, further supporting that motif distribution patterns, together with gene structural characteristics, provide robust evidence for the phylogenetic classification of PtrUGT genes.

Gene duplication has been recognized as a key mechanism driving the expansion and evolution of the UGT gene family [46, 47]. P. trichocarpa possessed 204 UGT family members, whereas P. euphratica possessed 134 UGT family members [43], indicating that the UGT gene count in P. trichocarpa was significantly higher than that in P. euphratica and may have resulted from gene duplication. Notably, distinct expansion patterns of UGT gene families have been observed among different plant species. For instance, P. grandiflorus primarily relies on 32 pairs of tandemly duplicated genes for family expansion [13]. In contrast, Gossypium species exhibit divergent expansion mechanisms: the allotetraploid G. hirsutum is predominantly driven by 132 segmental duplication events, while the diploid G. arboreum and G. raimondii show a preference for tandem duplication-mediated gene amplification [48]. Genome-wide duplication (21 events) and segmental duplication (25 events) have been identified as the primary duplication events contributing to the expansion of the LuUGT gene family in Linum usitatissimum [49], whereas Acer rubrum exhibits both tandem (21 events) and segmental (66 events) duplication patterns [11]. These findings collectively demonstrate significant interspecies variation in duplication preferences during UGT family expansion, potentially reflecting species-specific genomic duplication adaptation strategies shaped by long-term evolutionary processes. The present study reveals that the expansion of the P. trichocarpa UGT gene family is predominantly driven by tandem duplication, highlighting the dominant role of this mechanism in the evolutionary trajectory of PtrUGT genes. Additionally, this contrasted with the significantly higher proportion of segmental duplications in P. trichocarpa reported by Rehman et al. (2022) [44], and was consistent with the result in P. euphratica, where tandem duplications were the main source of UGT gene evolution [43].

Tandem duplication played an important role in the expansion of UGT gene families across species, but a higher proportion of tandem duplicates did not necessarily correspond to a larger expansion. For example, P. euphratica had 134 members with 65 tandem-duplicated gene pairs (58.56%) and 46 segmental-duplicated gene pairs (41.44%) [43]; P. grandiflorus had 107 members with 32 tandem-duplicated gene pairs (86.49%) and 5 segmental-duplicated gene pairs (13.51%) [13]; S. tuberosum had 295 members with 108 tandem-duplicated gene pairs (82.44%) and 23 segmental-duplicated gene pairs (17.56%) [4]. In this study, P. trichocarpa contained 204 UGT members with 150 tandem-duplicated gene pairs (56.39%) and 116 segmental-duplicated gene pairs (43.61%). This indicated that the UGT gene expansion in P. trichocarpa was at least partly associated with tandem duplication, but it was not the most pronounced across all species. Although tandem duplication contributed to expansion, a higher tandem proportion did not necessarily imply a larger expansion; the overall extent of expansion was influenced by multiple factors, including genome size, historical whole-genome duplication events, gene loss, functional redundancy, and selective pressure.

The number of gene family members was quantified across ten species, revealing that subfamilies A, D, E, G and L accounted for a larger share (Fig. S1). The subfamily with the highest representation varied among species, indicating that each species exhibited a unique subfamily evolutionary pattern. In P. trichocarpa, subfamilies A and M were the most numerous, whereas in P. euphratica subfamilies E and G predominated [43]. In P. trichocarpa, 63.91% of duplicated gene pairs involved Chr16, indicating that Chr16 played an important role in PtrUGT gene expansion; and the duplications on Chr16 largely originated from the A and M subfamilies, suggesting that gene duplication events drove the expansion of the A and M subfamilies. UGT genes participate in essential biosynthetic and metabolic processes in plants, so their expansion may reflect adaptation to different environmental pressures or to meet physiological demands under specific ecological conditions [1–3]. The high frequency of gene duplication events on Chr16 may have driven local PtrUGT expansion and the accumulation of metabolic diversity, with potential adaptive significance under environmental challenges. Furthermore, the tandemly duplicated PtrUGT genes on Chr16 showed differential expression across tissues and in response to stress. Even within the same subfamily, pairs of tandem duplicates and individual members within the same gene pair displayed distinct expression patterns. These expression differences provided clues for potential subfunctionalization or neofunctionalization rather than simple redundancy, but further functional validation was needed to confirm their impact on specific metabolic pathways.

Wang et al. (2023) performed whole-genome comparative analyses of UGT genes between Fagopyrum tataricum and six representative species (three dicots and three monocots). The analysis identified 24 collinear gene pairs between F. tataricum and Setaria italica, but only one pair with O. sativa [50]. Jin et al. (2022) performed a homologous sequence comparison of Forsythia suspensa FsUGT83A1 with UGT83A1 orthologs in A. thaliana, Pistacia vera, N. attenuata, Vitis vinifera, and Olea europaea. The highest amino acid sequence similarity was observed with O. europaea, indicating a close evolutionary relationship within the same family [51]. In our study, inter-species synteny analyses revealed that P. trichocarpa was most closely related to P. euphratica within the genus Populus. An et al. (2025) conducted comparative synteny analyses between P. euphratica and both A. thaliana (a model eudicot) and P. trichocarpa (a closely related woody species), and the results also concluded that P. euphratica was evolutionarily closer to P. trichocarpa than to A. thaliana [43], which was fully consistent with our findings. Additionally, Zhang et al. (2021) performed a genome-wide characterization of Quercus robur, identifying light-responsive elements, phytohormone-responsive elements, and stress-related cis-elements in the 2000 bp promoter regions upstream of UGT genes [52].These findings are highly consistent with the results of the present study, which also detected numerous light- and hormone-responsive elements in the promoter regions of UGT genes. This suggests that UGT genes may be coordinately regulated by multiple metabolic pathways.

Tissue-specific expression patterns of UGT genes were found to vary across plant species [53]. In Phoebe bournei, the PbUGT genes exhibited differential expression levels, with the highest average expression observed in leaves, followed by stem xylem, stem bark, root xylem, and root bark [54]. Similarly, in A. thaliana, UGT72B1 showed preferential expression in young stem tissues, particularly in the xylem [55]. Song et al. (2019) identified 120 CcUGT genes in Cajanus cajan and analyzed the tissue-specific expression of 11 representative genes, revealing higher transcript levels in leaves compared to other tissues [56]. Additionally, in Panax ginseng, most PgUGT genes displayed elevated expression in leaves and roots relative to buds and stems [57]. Rehman et al. (2022) observed high expression of most UGTs in flowers, roots, and leaves, with several subfamily members strongly up-regulated under drought [44]. An et al. (2025) reported that leaves exhibited the most pronounced transcriptional changes under drought stress [43]. Tissue-specific expression may also be influenced by temporal or stress-related factors. Data obtained at the specific time and under the specific conditions used in this experiment indicated that most PtrUGT genes were highly expressed in roots and leaves.

The promoter regions of PtrUGT genes contained abundant hormone- and stress-related cis-regulatory elements, with most genes showing distinct temporal expression patterns under various stress treatments including SA, JA, salt, drought, cold, heat, and pathogen exposure. In addition, most members of the M subfamily were significantly upregulated under hormone stress and prolonged low-temperature stress, whereas most members of the J subfamily showed increased expression under insect attack but exhibited a collective decrease under drought stress, indicating that PtrUGTs displayed partially subfamily-specific expression patterns. Previous studies demonstrated that Arabidopsis UGT71C3 contributed to pathogen defense through MeSA glycosylation [58], while UGT71C5 modulated drought response via ABA glycosylation [59]. Similarly, tomato UGT73B3/B5 and Arabidopsis UGT76B1 were activated during Pseudomonas syringae infection, suggesting their involvement in SA-JA signalling crosstalk [60, 61]. These findings collectively suggested that PtrUGT genes might play crucial roles in plant stress adaptation by mediating hormone metabolism and signal transduction pathways. Multiple studies have demonstrated that UGT genes played a crucial role in plant defense against insect herbivory. In tea plants, the UGT89AC1 gene was strongly induced by Ectropis grisescens infestation, mediating quercetin glycosylation to enhance insect resistance [38]. The rice UGT gene Os07g32020 regulated naringenin synthesis, a key flavonoid precursor, contributing to insect resistance [62]. In soybean, GmUGT88A1 (located at Rscn16) encoded an isoflavone-7-O-glucosyltransferase activated by GmMYB29, promoting isoflavone glycoside accumulation for broad-spectrum SCN resistance while improving seed traits [63]. CRISPR/Cas9 knockout of GmUGT (Glyma.07g110300) in soybean enhanced resistance to Helicoverpa armigera and Spodoptera litura by disrupting flavonoid metabolism without compromising agronomic traits [16]. Similarly, in poplar, we observed that insect feeding significantly induced the expression of genes such as PtrUGT198, which might participate in insect defense by regulating secondary metabolite biosynthesis. An et al. (2025) reported that qRT-PCR validation of 5 candidate PeUGT genes in leaves, roots, and stems showed strong concordance with RNA-Seq profiles, and over-expression of PeUGT110 in Arabidopsis alleviated drought-induced damage [43]. However, we went further by intersecting multiple heat-map datasets to select 15 significantly up-regulated candidates. qRT-PCR confirmed that all 15 genes were markedly more highly expressed under herbivory than under CK, NaCl, or PEG6000 treatments. Among them, PtrUGT198 exhibited the strongest response. Nevertheless, we noted some discrepancies between the transcriptomic and qRT-PCR analyses, which might be attributed to variations in poplar cultivars, differences in stress treatment durations/concentrations, and the use of distinct insect species across experiments. These findings highlighted the diverse mechanisms by which UGT gene family members modulated insect resistance, providing valuable targets for poplar breeding efforts aimed at enhancing pest resistance.

In Hippophae rhamnoides, approximately 92% of the HrUGT proteins were localized in the cytoplasm [64], while about 78.2% of the 124 JrUGT proteins in Juglans regia were primarily localized in the cytoplasm [65]. These findings were consistent with our results: the majority of PtrUGT proteins were cytoplasmic, with PtrUGT198 also predicted to be cytoplasmic. However, transient expression assays in tobacco demonstrated that PtrUGT198 was predominantly localized in both the nucleus and the cytoplasm. The discrepancy between the predicted results and experimental observations could be attributed to condition-dependent subcellular transport and post-translational modifications. Additionally, the Arabidopsis glycosyltransferase UGT87A2 affected flowering time by regulating the flowering repressor FLOWERING LOCUS C, and its protein product localized to both the cytoplasm and nucleus [66]. A study by Xie et al. (2022) found that cells transfected with the LuUGT175-GFP gene exhibited fluorescence signals in both the cytoplasm and nucleus [49]. These findings were consistent with the localization of UGT198 in the nucleus and cytoplasm in this study, suggesting that GTs might have been involved in intracellular metabolic regulation through glycosylation.

Conclusions

In the current study, the UGT gene family in P. trichocarpa was comprehensively characterized through analyses of chromosomal localization, synteny relationships, gene structure, phylogenetic relationships, and promoter cis-elements. Notably, the investigation expanded the stress response spectrum to encompass abiotic stresses (salt, drought, cold, and heat), biotic stresses (pathogen infection and herbivore attack), and hormone treatments (SA and MeJA). Furthermore, the expression patterns were experimentally validated by qRT-PCR analysis, and the subcellular localization of UGT198 was determined. To advance our understanding of PtrUGT functions in poplar growth and stress responses, future studies should employ transgenic approaches including overexpression constructs, targeted gene knockouts using CRISPR/Cas9, and transcriptional activation systems. Systematic functional characterization of these PtrUGTs will provide valuable genetic resources for developing improved poplar varieties with enhanced stress resistance through molecular breeding approaches.
