Phase I crossover study of DNA‐protein kinase inhibitor peposertib in healthy volunteers: Effect of food and pharmacokinetics of an oral suspension
Authors: Andreas Becker, Axel Krebs‐Brown, Claudia Vetter, Tanja Reuter, Almudena Rodriguez‐Gutierrez, Xiaoli You, Michael Lissy
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeposertib is an orally administered inhibitor of DNA‐dependent protein kinase. We evaluated the effect of food on its pharmacokinetics, and examined the pharmacokinetics of an oral suspension (OS) of disintegrated tablets, in a phase I, open‐label, crossover three‐period study (NCT04702698). Twelve healthy volunteers were randomized to one of six treatment sequences. They received a single dose of peposertib 100 mg as film‐coated tablets under fasted or fed conditions (“tablet fasted” or “tablet fed”) or as an OS under fasted conditions (“OS fasted”), with washout between treatments. Using healthy volunteers was possible because, despite its mechanism of action being suppression of DNA repair, peposertib has shown no genotoxic effect in animals. A mild food effect was observed with peposertib tablets. Fed‐to‐fasted ratios were: area under the curve from time 0 to time t (AUC0–t), 123.81% (90% confidence interval [CI]: 108.04, 141.87%); AUC from zero to infinity (AUC0–∞), 110.28% (90% CI 100.71, 120.77%); and maximum concentration (C max) 104.47% (90% CI: 79.15, 137.90%). C max was delayed under fed conditions (median time to maximum concentration [T max] was 3.5 h [tablet fed] vs. 1 h [tablet fasted]). OS‐to‐tablet (fasted) ratios were: AUC0–t, 124.83% (90% CI: 111.50%, 139.76%); AUC0–∞, 119.05% (90% CI: 104.47, 135.67%); and C max 173.29% (90% CI: 135.78, 221.16%). Median T max was 0.5 h (OS fasted) versus 1 h (tablet). All treatments were well‐tolerated in healthy volunteers. Peposertib tablets can be taken with or without food; if combined with chemotherapy or radiotherapy, the delay in C max must be considered to optimize the chemo‐ or radiosensitizing effect. The peposertib OS form represents an alternative route of administration in patients with specific cancers causing dysphagia. However, the OS form should be part of future dose optimization strategies in relevant settings.

INTRODUCTION

Physical or chemical agents that generate breaks in DNA are widely used in the treatment of cancer, with the aim of inducing cell cycle arrest and/or apoptosis. Ionizing radiation is particularly associated with DNA double‐strand breaks (DSBs), 1 , 2 which are the most cytotoxic type of DNA damage, and can lead to cell death if left unrepaired. 1 , 2 Hence, a complex surveillance and signaling network, the DNA damage response, has evolved to maintain genome integrity by repairing DNA damage. 2 , 3 , 4

Two major mechanisms, homologous recombination and non‐homologous end joining (NHEJ), are used to repair DNA DSBs, 3 , 4 and a key enzyme in the NHEJ repair process is DNA‐dependent protein kinase (DNA‐PK). DNA‐PK consists of a heterodimer of two proteins, Ku70 and Ku80, and a catalytic subunit, 5 , 6 , 7 is recruited to DNA DSBs, and is activated through autophosphorylation. Upon activation, it recruits the NHEJ machinery to enable DNA repair. 8 , 9 Therefore, inhibition of DNA‐PK might increase the therapeutic effect of radiotherapy (RT) and chemotherapy (CT), as this would prevent repair of therapy‐induced DNA DSBs. 10

Peposertib (formerly M3814) is a potent and selective, orally administered, ATP‐competitive inhibitor of DNA‐PK that is currently being developed as an anticancer agent as it targets tumor cell growth and survival by inhibiting the NHEJ. 10 Preclinical pharmacology studies have shown that M467 (O‐demethylated peposertib) is the major circulating metabolite of peposertib and that it shows measurable DNA‐PK inhibition. Peposertib is a Biopharmaceutics Classification System Class II compound with low, pH‐dependent solubility. It was initially developed as a capsule formulation and was subsequently modified to a film‐coated tablet. The tablet formulation has a 75% greater bioavailability compared to capsule. The first‐in‐human study of peposertib used the capsule formulation and derived the recommended phase II dose to be 400 mg b.i.d. 11 Peposertib plus RT has been evaluated in locally advanced rectal cancer (plus capecitabine; NCT03770689) 12 and in advanced head and neck tumors (with or without cisplatin; NCT02516813). 13 Ongoing trials are evaluating combination regimens containing peposertib in locally advanced pancreatic cancer (NCT04172532), advanced/metastatic solid tumors, and hepatobiliary malignancies (plus avelumab; NCT04068194), and in newly diagnosed MGMT unmethylated glioblastoma or gliosarcoma (NCT04555577).

Food intake is known to increase the intragastric pH, and can potentially result in delayed gastric emptying, stimulation of bile flow, altered intestinal metabolism, and increased splanchnic blood flow. 14 As these factors may affect the rate and extent of absorption of peposertib, the impact of food on the pharmacokinetics of peposertib needs investigation. Further, dysphagia is common among patients with cancer, and especially among those with head and neck cancers. Administration of peposertib as an oral suspension (OS) of disintegrated tablets orally or via a feeding tube could overcome swallowing difficulties. Accordingly, the pharmacokinetics of the OS form of peposertib were also assessed.

Therefore, this phase I study (NCT04702698; EudraCT 2020‐004187‐26) was designed to evaluate whether food intake affects the pharmacokinetics of peposertib when given as a film‐coated tablet, and whether the pharmacokinetic profile and exposure are different when peposertib is administered in OS form. It was possible to conduct this study with healthy volunteers because peposertib was not mutagenic in animals in a standard battery of genotoxicity tests, even though its mechanism of action involves suppression of DNA repair.

METHODS

Study design

This study was registered as NCT04702698 and was conducted at one center in Germany between January 14, 2021, and March 3, 2021. It was a phase I, open‐label, randomized, crossover three‐period, six‐sequence study to investigate the pharmacokinetics of peposertib following single‐dose administration of 100 mg to healthy participants. Peposertib was administered in three different ways: as a film‐coated tablet under fasted conditions (“tablet fasted”); as a film‐coated tablet under fed conditions following a high‐fat, high‐calorie meal (“tablet fed”); or as an OS of disintegrated tablets under fasted conditions (“OS fasted”). In all cases, two 50 mg peposertib tablets were used to make up the 100 mg dose.

Twelve healthy participants were randomized on day 1 of period one to one of six treatment sequences (Figure S1). Each participant was assigned a unique three‐digit number which encoded their assignment to one of the treatment sequences, according to the randomization schedule. There were two participants per sequence and all participants were exposed to all three treatments, but in a different order.

Participants were screened from day –28 to day –2 and admitted to the study site on day –1. They remained as inpatients at the study site under medical supervision until discharge ~48 h after dosing (day 3) in each period. The washout period between peposertib administrations was at least 7 days. Serial blood samples were collected throughout the inpatient period (day –1 to day 2) for safety (hematology, biochemistry, and coagulation) and pharmacokinetic assessments. The safety‐follow‐up/end‐of‐treatment visit was on day 3 of the last treatment period, prior to discharge from inpatient stay. On day 6 of the last treatment period, an additional safety follow‐up was conducted via televisit.

This study was performed in compliance with International Council for Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol and other relevant documents were reviewed and approved by an institutional review board/independent ethics committee before study start. Participation was voluntary and all participants provided their written informed consent.

Participants

Healthy men or women aged 18–45 years inclusive, with no history of clinically relevant gastrointestinal disease, no prescription or over‐the‐counter medication intake within 2 weeks or five half‐lives (whichever is longer) prior to first study intervention administration (except paracetamol [up to 2 g/day]), or potentially confounding comorbidities, or clinically relevant conditions that could interfere with the study conduct or interpretation of results were eligible to participate. Key exclusion criteria consisted of clinically relevant conditions that could interfere with the study conduct or interpretation of results. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are shown in Table S1.

Objectives and end points

The first primary objective was to determine and compare the pharmacokinetic profile of a single dose of peposertib 100 mg administered as a film‐coated tablet under fasted and fed conditions. A second primary objective was to determine and compare the pharmacokinetic profile of a single dose of peposertib 100 mg administered as a film‐coated tablet or an OS of disintegrated tablets, both under fasted conditions.

Secondary objectives were to evaluate the safety and tolerability of a single dose of peposertib 100 mg administered as a film‐coated tablet under fasted and fed conditions or as an OS of disintegrated tablets under fasted conditions. Tertiary objectives were as follows: to determine additional pharmacokinetic parameters of peposertib administered as described in the preceding sentence; to determine the excretion of peposertib and its major metabolite M467 in urine; and to characterize the pharmacokinetics of M467 after a single dose of peposertib as a tablet (fasted and fed conditions) or an OS of disintegrated tablets (fasted conditions).

The primary pharmacokinetic end points were area under the curve (AUC) from time 0 to time t (AUC0‐t) where t was the last sampling time at which the concentration was at or above the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ), AUC from time 0 extrapolated to infinity (AUC0‐∞), and the maximum concentration (C max) of peposertib, all calculated from concentration data over the period 0 to 36 h [AUC0‐36] postdose. It is to be noted that AUC0‐36 was not equal to AUC0‐t, even when the sampling scheme went up to 36 hours in both cases. For AUC0‐t, the AUC was calculated only up to the last sample above the LLOQ; every sample thereafter was considered as missing. AUC0‐36 considered the next sample (after the above‐mentioned LLOQ sample) as 0.

These end points were also assessed for M467. Further pharmacokinetic end points for both peposertib and M467 included AUC from zero to 24 h (AUC0–24h), time to maximum drug concentration (T max), time prior to the first measurable (non‐zero) concentration (t lag), and terminal half‐life (t 1/2). Apparent total body clearance of the drug, and apparent volume of distribution during the terminal phase were also determined for peposertib.

The quantification of peposertib and its metabolite M467 in plasma were performed using validated assay methods. Plasma samples for pharmacokinetic analysis were collected at predose and 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 3.5, 4.0, 5.0, 6.0, 8.0, 12.0, 24.0, and 36.0 h after dosing on day 1 in each period.

Pharmacokinetic parameters were derived using noncompartmental methods with the validated computer program Phoenix WinNonlin (version 7.0; Certara, L.P.). SAS version 9.4 was used to calculate the molecular‐weight‐corrected metabolic ratios (MRs) and renal clearance (CLR).

Safety end points were as follows: the occurrence of treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs) and serious TEAEs; the occurrence of TEAEs by severity according to the Qualitative Toxicity Scale selected from the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (version 5.0, November 2017); and absolute values and changes from baseline in laboratory tests, vital signs, and 12‐lead echocardiogram (ECG; assessed on day 1 of each treatment period at predose, and at 1, 4, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h postdose).

The MRs of M467 versus peposertib for AUC0‐t, AUC0‐24h, AUC0‐∞, and C max were also determined, as were the relative amounts of peposertib and M467 excreted in urine (A e0‐24h, A e0‐24h%) and CLR of both compounds.

Statistical analyses

For determining the sample size, limited data were available on the pharmacokinetics of peposertib after a single dose of 100 mg using the film‐coated tablet. Assuming intrasubject coefficients of variations (CVs) of 30% for AUC0‐tlast, AUC0‐∞, and C max, it was calculated that 12 evaluable participants would provide adequate precision for estimating the ratios (fed vs. fasted, or OS vs. tablet).

The pharmacokinetic analysis set consisted of all participants who received at least one dose of active peposertib and provided at least one recorded postdose value. The safety analysis set consisted of all participants who were administered any dose of any study intervention. Statistical analysis was descriptive; no hypothesis tests were performed.

Pharmacokinetic parameters based on noncompartmental analysis were calculated for peposertib and its metabolite M467. Two linear mixed models with condition (fasted/fed and tablet/OS), period, and sequence as fixed effects and subject within sequence as a random effect, were applied to log‐transformed primary endpoints C max, AUC0‐tlast, and AUC0‐∞ based on the pharmacokinetic analysis set. Treatment differences for fed versus fasted and OS versus tablet on the log scale were estimated for the parameters together with their 90% confidence intervals (CIs). Point estimates and CIs were back‐transformed to the original scale for presentation.

Relative bioavailability of OS of disintegrated tablets (fasted conditions)

Under fasted conditions, the concentration‐time courses showed a C max value of ~70% higher for the OS compared with the tablet (Figures 1a and 2a, Table 2). The extent of exposure (AUC) was about 19%–25% higher for the OS fasted compared to the tablet fasted (Table 3).

Variability of AUCs was comparable between the OS fasted and tablet fasted (values between 62.1% and 70.8%), whereas the variability of C max was higher for the OS fasted (75.1%) versus the tablet fasted (60.5%; Table 2).

C max was reached earlier with the OS (median T max 0.5 h [min; max 0.5; 2.0] than with the tablet (median T max 1.0 h [min; max 0.5; 3.0]; Table 2). Half‐life of peposertib given as an OS geometric mean 3.79 h, GeoCV% 79.9.

Urinary excretion and renal clearance (all treatments)

The relative amount of the total dose of peposertib excreted in urine was very low, with geometric mean values of Ae0‐24h% ranging from 0.076 to 0.21% (Table S2). The CLR of peposertib was also very low, with geometric mean values of between 0.050 and 0.309 L/h (Table S2).

Safety

Three single doses of 100 mg peposertib, administered as two 50 mg tablets under fasted or fed conditions or as an OS of two disintegrated 50 mg tablets under fasted conditions, were well‐tolerated. No deaths, serious adverse events, other significant TEAEs, or discontinuations due to TEAEs occurred. Two of the 12 participants experienced three TEAEs: oropharyngeal pain and oral herpes in one participant, and musculoskeletal stiffness in another participant. All TEAEs were mild (grade 1). No TEAEs were considered related to peposertib. No clinically relevant changes over time were observed for standard laboratory parameters, vital signs, or ECG parameters.

DISCUSSION

In this food effect and pharmacokinetic study, consumption of a high‐fat, high‐calorie meal had only a mild effect on peposertib exposure following a single dose of 100 mg, but with a delayed C max. The AUCs for the OS form of peposertib 100 mg and film‐coated tablets were comparable, but a higher C max was seen with the OS form. The concentration‐time profile of the metabolite, M467, mirrored that of peposertib, with the metabolic ratios of exposure in the range of 33%–42% of the parent drug. The observed pharmacokinetic profile of M467 was independent of peposertib treatment, and no relevant additional accumulation is expected at steady‐state for once‐daily or twice‐daily dosing. Overall, single doses of peposertib 100 mg were well‐tolerated by healthy participants when administered either as tablets or in OS form.

Peposertib shows a significant margin of selectivity for DNA‐PK over its closest family members, phosphoinositide 3‐kinase α, ‐β, ‐δ, and ‐γ, and is inactive against a broad panel of PKs. 10 As functional DNA‐PK confers a degree of resistance to RT and CT by repairing therapy‐induced double‐strand breaks, 15 peposertib could increase the sensitivity of tumor cells to these treatments. Indeed, peposertib has been shown to sensitize multiple cancer cell lines to ionizing radiation. 10 Furthermore, administration of oral peposertib plus ionizing radiation to two xenograft models of human cancer strongly potentiated the antitumor activity of the radiation and led to complete tumor regression at non‐toxic doses. 10

Traditionally, anticancer drug development focused on chemotherapeutics, and these could not be administered to healthy volunteers due to ethical concerns over their toxic effects. However, the current immunomodulatory and molecularly targeted drugs are rarely genotoxic and have better safety profiles than chemotherapeutics, and can be administered to healthy volunteers. 16 Furthermore, the use of healthy volunteers instead of patients in clinical pharmacology studies (e.g., those evaluating food effects, drug–drug interactions, and thorough QT trials), is advantageous because recruitment can be faster and dropout rates are lower, rich sampling is easily possible, sampling can be more precisely timed, difficult pre‐analytical procedures are possible, and costs can be lower. 17 , 18 There is also lower risk of confounding by comorbidities or concomitant medications. Notably, using healthy participants removes the ethical concern of administering subtherapeutic doses to patients with disease when studies of curative or palliative intent may be more appropriate. 16 , 19 In the absence of RT or CT, the constitutive level of DNA‐PK is low in healthy volunteers and DNA‐PK mediated repair of double‐strand DNA damage is considered negligible. Additionally, as the half‐life of peposertib is short at ~5.5 h, 11 any risk to healthy volunteers, due to unexpected DNA damage upon peposertib exposure, was considered to be within acceptable limits of clinical pharmacology trials. These advantages have to be weighed against the fact that the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of the drug may not be identical in healthy volunteers and patients, in some cases, and that engagement of the drug target may have different safety consequences in patients. 16 Nonetheless, the case for using healthy volunteers is strengthened if the preclinical efficacious dose is equal to or less than the no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) and the drug is non‐genotoxic. Overall, healthy volunteers are being increasingly enrolled in pharmacological trials of drugs in oncologic indications for the development of small molecule therapeutics. 16

Nonclinical safety data showed that peposertib was not mutagenic or genotoxic in a standard battery of genotoxicity tests (see Data S1: Nonclinical Safety for more details). Specifically, genotoxicity testing included a bacterial reverse gene mutation assay, a chromosomal aberration test, a hypoxanthine‐guanine phosphoribosyl transferase gene mutation test, and a rodent micronucleus study. Observations from general toxicity studies indicated reversibility and monitorability of target organ effects, and, as expected, these were later demonstrated to be manageable in the first‐in‐human study. 11 The main target organs of peposertib toxicity, as identified by 4‐week and 13‐week repeat‐dose toxicity studies in rats and dogs, are the lymphatic system, the male and female reproductive systems, and the epithelia, including skin and mucosae of the digestive tract. An embryofetal developmental study in rats showed that peposertib induced fetal malformations, increased number of fetal skeletal variations, and resulted in maternal toxicity. Hence, women of child‐bearing potential were excluded from this clinical study and contraceptive measures were in place for male participants.

Peposertib as monotherapy demonstrated modest efficacy in a phase I dose‐escalation trial among unselected patients (n = 31) with advanced solid tumors. 11 Dose selection for the current study was based on safety data from the above‐mentioned trial 11 and on preliminary safety data from three other clinical trials that are currently investigating peposertib in oncological indications (NCT02516813, NCT03770689, and NCT03724890). A comparison of the observed human exposure at 100 mg/patient to the exposure at NOAEL in dogs (the more sensitive species) yielded an animal/human exposure ratio (safety margin) of 3.7 and 4.8 for AUC and C max, respectively. Additionally, as no dose‐limiting toxicities were observed when patients were repeatedly exposed to 400 mg twice daily dose level of peposertib as a capsule for a duration of 21 days, this was selected as the recommended phase II dose. 11 The safety margin between this dose and the dose planned to be administered in healthy participants was estimated to be eight‐fold (AUC) and four‐fold (C max) at the time of study initiation. In the current study, healthy volunteers were exposed to only a single 100 mg dose of peposertib per treatment period, which further minimized any safety risk associated with multiple dosing. The use of this dose level is further substantiated by the fact that, after the current study was initiated, findings from a population pharmacokinetic analysis indicated higher exposure levels for the peposertib tablet compared to the capsule formulation, with an average 75% estimated increase in AUC and C max.

As the current study found no marked effect of food on peposertib exposure, tablets can be taken with or without food. However, when combined with CT and RT, peposertib administration without food should be considered as C max occurs earlier and has a smaller range under these conditions. Additionally, when timing is key (i.e., synchronized exposure of CT/RT and peposertib), administration in the fasted state would help ensure that C max is achieved in a more predictable timeframe.

As the AUCs of the OS form and the tablet were comparable, the OS form represents a useful alternative in patients with dysphagia, which occurs frequently in certain cancers. However, because C max was higher after administration of the OS form, dosing and schedule should be part of the future dose optimization strategies in the relevant population and combination settings.

A potential limitation of this study is the fact that only male participants were included, although the protocol allowed for the inclusion of female participants; any potential variability resulting from sex differences would therefore not have been detected.

CONCLUSION

This food effect study showed that a single dose of 100 mg peposertib can be taken with or without food. Administration without food should be considered if combined with CT and/or RT to ensure a more predictable time to reach C max. Peposertib can also be administered in OS form to alleviate discomfort in patients with dysphagia. However, dose optimization strategies in relevant settings are needed for the OS form.
