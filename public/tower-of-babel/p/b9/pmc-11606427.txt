DEAD‐box RNA helicase RH20 positively regulates RNAi‐based antiviral immunity in plants by associating with SGS3/RDR6 bodies
Authors: Zhiyan Wen, Rujian Hu, Qinglin Pi, Dingliang Zhang, Jiangning Duan, Zhen Li, Qian Li, Xiaoyun Zhao, Meng Yang, Xiaofei Zhao, Deshui Liu, Zhen Su, Dawei Li, Yongliang Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryRNA silencing plays a crucial role in defending against viral infections in diverse eukaryotic hosts. Despite extensive studies on core components of the antiviral RNAi pathway such as DCLs, AGOs and RDRs proteins, host factors involved in antiviral RNAi remain incompletely understood. In this study, we employed the proximity labelling approach to identify the host factors required for antiviral RNAi in Nicotiana benthamiana. Using the barley stripe mosaic virus (BSMV)‐encoded γb, a viral suppressor of RNA silencing (VSR), as the bait protein, we identified the DEAD‐box RNA helicase RH20, a broadly conserved protein in plants and animals with a homologous human protein known as DDX5. We demonstrated the interaction between RH20 and BSMV γb. Knockdown or knockout of RH20 attenuates the accumulation of viral small interfering RNAs, leading to increased susceptibility to BSMV, while overexpression of RH20 enhances resistance to BSMV, a process requiring the cytoplasmic localization and RNA‐binding activity of RH20. In addition to BSMV, RH20 also negatively regulates the infection of several other positive‐sense RNA viruses, suggesting the broad‐spectrum antiviral activity of RH20. Mechanistic analysis revealed the colocalization and interaction of RH20 with SGS3/RDR6, and disruption of either SGS3 or RDR6 undermines the antiviral function of RH20, suggesting RH20 as a new component of the SGS3/RDR6 bodies. As a counter‐defence, BSMV γb VSR subverts the RH20‐mediated antiviral defence by interfering with the RH20–SGS3 interaction. Our results uncover RH20 as a new positive regulator of antiviral RNAi and provide new potential targets for controlling plant viral diseases.Keywords: proxitome profiling, antiviral RNA interference, barley stripe mosaic virus, γb viral suppressors of RNA silencing (VSR), DEAD‐box RNA helicase RH20, suppressor of gene silencing 3 (SGS3)

Introduction

Plants employ various mechanisms to defend against virus infections (Wu et al., 2024). Among them, RNA silencing is a highly conserved gene regulatory mechanism involving small RNAs, which constitutes a crucial defence against viral invasion (Baulcombe, 2022; Guo et al., 2019; Yang and Li, 2018). In plants, endonuclease Dicer‐like (DCL) proteins recognize and bind double‐stranded RNA or secondary structured RNA derived from viral genome replication, cleaving them into 20–30 nucleotide (nt) virus‐derived small interfering RNAs (vsiRNAs). These vsiRNAs are loaded into Argonaute (AGO) proteins to form RNA‐induced silencing complexes (RISCs), which then guide the associated RISCs to target viral genomic DNA/RNA sequences and suppress viral replication (Ding, 2010; Guo et al., 2019; Lopez‐Gomollon and Baulcombe, 2022). Furthermore, the protein complexes including RNA‐Dependent RNA Polymerase 6 (RDR6) and Suppressor of Gene Silencing 3 (SGS3) amplify the effect of gene silencing by generating a large number of secondary siRNAs, thereby reinforcing and/or maintaining the silenced state of the target RNA (Kumakura et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2010). The DCLs, AGOs, RDR6 and SGS3 proteins mentioned above constitute key components of the antiviral RNAi pathway and have been extensively studied.

As the main proteins involved in siRNA biogenesis, the DCLs/AGOs/RDR6/SGS3 proteins often function by forming complexes with other proteins. For instance, SGS3 and Silencing Defective 5 (SDE5) cooperatively recruit RDR6 to specific RNAs, a process essential for siRNA amplification in Arabidopsis (Yoshikawa et al., 2021). Additionally, several newly identified proteins, including Reduced Dormancy 5 (RDO5) and Antiviral RNAi Regulator 1 (VIR1) (Liu et al., 2022), Aminophospholipid Transporting ATPase 2 (ALA2) and its homolog ALA1 (Guo et al., 2017) and Antiviral RNAi‐Defective2 (AVI2) (Guo et al., 2018), have been reported to be involved in siRNA production. Moreover, Barely Any Meristem 1 (BAM1) was found to be indispensable for the cell‐to‐cell spread of RNAi (Rosas‐Diaz et al., 2018). However, most of the reported positive/negative regulators of antiviral RNAi have been identified in the model plant Arabidopsis using forward genetic approaches. Whether there are new components involved in the antiviral RNAi process in other plants beyond those reported in Arabidopsis remains to be fully explored.

During the long‐term ‘arms race’ between viruses and their host plants, viruses have evolved viral suppressors of RNA silencing (VSRs) to counteract RNAi‐based antiviral immunity (Burgyán and Havelda, 2011; Díaz‐Pendón and Ding, 2008; Jin et al., 2021). VSRs are comprised of virus‐encoded replicases, coat proteins (CPs) or movement proteins, among others. Different VSRs disrupt the host's RNAi by targeting various components of the antiviral RNAi pathway, such as DCL or AGO proteins, to maintain survival of the virus within the host cells (Csorba et al., 2015; Li and Wang, 2019). In fact, over the past 20 years, VSRs have proven to be valuable tools for identifying and analysing the RNA silencing pathway (Baulcombe, 2022). Barley stripe mosaic virus (BSMV) is a positive single‐stranded RNA virus with a genome consisting of tripartite RNAs designated as α, β and γ. Among them, the γb encoded by RNAγ is a multifunctional protein (Jiang et al., 2021) that can suppress dsRNA‐induced RNAi, exhibiting strong VSR activity (Bragg and Jackson, 2004). Moreover, phosphorylation modification can enhance the VSR activity of the γb protein (Zhang et al., 2018). However, to date, the molecular mechanisms underlying how the γb protein exerts its VSR function remain elusive.

In this study, we employed a TurboID‐based proximity labelling (PL) approach to identify the interactome of γb VSR in the context of BSMV infections of Nicotiana benthamiana. We identified a previously uncharacterized protein named DEAD‐box ATP‐dependent RNA helicase 20 (RH20) that interacts with γb and plays an antiviral role in BSMV infection. We demonstrated that RH20 positively regulates antiviral RNAi by acting as a new component of SGS3/RDR6 bodies. In turn, BSMV γb disrupts the interaction between RH20 and SGS3 to undermine RH20‐mediated defence response. Our findings enrich the regulatory network of RNAi‐mediated antiviral immunity, elucidating a hitherto unknown mechanism by which the γb protein exerts its VSR function, aiding in our understanding of the defence and counter‐defence processes between viruses and hosts during their long‐term coevolution.

Results

Proxitome profiling of BSMV γb VSR

To identify the interactome of γb in the context of BSMV infections, we constructed BSMVγb‐TurboID vectors by fusing the TurboID biotin ligase to the C‐terminus of the γb as depicted in Figure 1a, and then infiltrated N. benthamiana leaves with Agrobacterium carrying the BSMVγb‐TurboID construct. Meanwhile, leaves were infiltrated with Agrobacterium carrying the Citrine–TurboID construct in the context of BSMV infection, serving as the control group (Figure 1a; Method S1). Immunoblot analysis confirmed the detection of γb‐TurboID fusion protein along with biotin‐modified protein bands (Figure 1b), suggesting that the fusion of TurboID to the γb has little, if any, effect on BSMV infection in local leaves, and efficient biotinylation of proteins proximal to γb occurs. Protein enrichment together with liquid chromatography tandem‐mass spectrometry (LC‐MS/MS) analysis was then conducted following the experimental design outlined in Figure 1c.

After protein identification via LC‐MS/MS and subsequent bioinformatics analysis (Figure 1c), a total of 4091 proteins were identified across all three biological replicate experiments in the BSMVγb‐TurboID group (Figure 1d; Figure S1a; Table S1; Method S1). Among these, 131 proteins (including 128 host proteins) were identified as enriched interactors, meeting a threshold of P < 0.05 and a fold change (BSMVγb‐TurboID/Citrine) of ≥1.5 (Figure 1d; Figure S1b; Table S2). Notably, BSMV‐encoded triple gene block protein 1 (TGB1), αa and CP, previously validated to interact with γb (Jiang et al., 2020; Yue et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2017), were identified (Figure 1d), underscoring the confidence of our γb proxitome‐derived MS data set.

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and interaction network construction were conducted based on the significantly enriched proteins. Results indicated that approximately one‐third of these proteins are associated with RNA processes, including RNA transport, binding, splicing and unwinding of secondary RNA structures, while others are involved in translation, transport and responses to various stresses (Figure 1e; Table S3). Notably, within the proteins associated with RNA processes, the DEAD‐box family proteins were prominently enriched in the γb proxitome (Figure 1f; Tables S4 and S5). Among them, RH20 is highly conserved across different plant species, featuring eight domains responsible for ATP binding, helicase activity and RNA binding (Figure S2). DEAD‐box helicases play diverse roles in RNA turnover (Li et al., 2023; Linder and Jankowsky, 2011). Importantly, the RH20 ortholog in Drosophila was reported to be associated with the RISC complex (Ishizuka et al., 2002), suggesting a potential role of RH20 in the RNA‐silencing pathway. Considering γb's established role as a VSR, we proposed that γb might target RH20 to suppress antiviral RNAi. To investigate this hypothesis, we then focus on RH20 in our following studies.

γb interacts with RH20 in vivo and in vitro

To test whether RH20 interacts with γb, we amplified the NbRH20 coding sequence from N. benthamiana cDNA and cloned it into the bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) vectors. The results revealed that both combinations, RH20‐YFPn and γb‐YFPc, as well as RH20‐YFPc and γb‐YFPn, exhibited evident reconstituted fluorescence signals, forming puncta of varying sizes in the cytoplasm (Figure 2a). Moreover, similar to BSMVγb‐TurboID, we introduced YFPn into the C‐terminus of the γb open reading frame (ORF) in the binary BSMV vector to generate BSMVγb‐YFPn. Co‐expression of BSMVγb‐YFPn and RH20‐YFPc in N. benthamiana leaves also resulted in observable reconstituted YFP signals (Figure 2a).

To determine the interaction regions between γb and RH20 proteins, we initially modelled the structures of γb and RH20 proteins using AlphaFold2 and predicted their potential interacting domains. Results indicated that the BM domain of the γb protein likely mediates their interaction (Figure S3a). We introduced mutations in amino acids 25 and 26 (RK) within the BM domain of the γb protein to QN (25RK26→25QN26), which were previously shown to disrupt the RNA binding and VSR activity of γb (Donald and Jackson, 1996). BiFC experiments demonstrated that the γbBM26 mutant failed to interact with the RH20 protein (Figure 2a). Consistent results were obtained when employing firefly luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) to evaluate the γb–RH20 interaction (Figure 2b; Figure S3b), and protein expression in the BiFC and LCI assays were confirmed via immunoblot analysis (Figure S4a,b).

Considering the RNA‐binding abilities of both the RH20 protein and the γb protein, we aimed to eliminate the potential influence of RNA mediation on the interaction between these proteins in vivo. To achieve this, we introduced a mutation at a highly conserved arginine residue (Position 428) of the RH20 protein to lysine (R428K) (Figure S5), thereby disrupting the RNA‐binding capability of RH20 (Bond et al., 2001). Co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) analysis revealed that the RH20R428K mutant not only retained its binding ability to γb but also exhibited even stronger binding compared with the non‐mutated RH20 protein (Figure 2c,d), thereby excluding the possibility of RNA bridging the interaction between RH20 and γb in vivo.

Furthermore, GST‐γb and RH20‐6×His or their respective variants GST‐γbBM26 and RH20R428K‐6×His were expressed in E. coli and purified, followed by a GST pull‐down assay. The results revealed that γb specifically binds to either RH20 or the RH20R428K mutant but not to the GFP control (Figure 2e). In contrast, the γbBM26 mutant exhibited remarkably reduced binding ability to RH20 protein compared with wild‐type γb protein (Figure 2f), consistent with the results obtained from the BiFC and LCI assays (Figure 2a,b). These results indicate that γb interacts with RH20 protein in vivo and in vitro, and the amino acids 25RK26 of γb play a crucial role in determining its interaction with RH20.

RH20 plays an antiviral role in BSMV infection

To investigate the role of NbRH20 in BSMV infection, we cultivated transgenic plants with knocked down (RH20‐KD) and knocked out (RH20‐KO) RH20 expression (Figure S6). Knockdown or knockout of RH20 did not have a significant effect on plant growth (Figure S6a). We then inoculated BSMV onto RH20‐KD and RH20‐KO transgenic N. benthamiana plants via agroinfiltration, with non‐transgenic (NT) plants serving as controls. The results showed that, at 10 days post inoculation (dpi), symptoms of leaf chlorosis and curling induced by BSMV infection were more pronounced in systemically infected leaves in either RH20‐KO or RH20‐KD plants than in the NT control plants (Figure 3a). Immunoblot analysis revealed a significant increase in BSMV accumulation in both inoculated leaves (Ino.) at 2.5 dpi (Figure 3b) and systemically infected leaves (Sys.) at 10 dpi (Figure 3c) compared with NT controls. These results indicate that RH20 negatively regulates BSMV infection.

To further characterize the function of RH20 in BSMV infection, we observed the subcellular localization of RH20. Confocal microscopy analysis showed that, in the absence of BSMV infection, RH20 exhibited clear nuclear localization with faint cytoplasmic fluorescence. In contrast, upon infection of wild‐type or movement‐deficient BSMV (RNAα + RNAγ), NbRH20 not only remained localized to the nucleus but also exhibited significantly enhanced fluorescence in the cytoplasm (Figure 3d; Figure S7a). Further nuclear/cytosolic fractionation assays consistently indicated a noticeable shift in the cytoplasmic/nuclear distribution of RH20 after BSMV infection as evidenced by a higher cytoplasmic/nuclear ratio compared with control leaves (EV) without virus infection (Figure 3e; Figure S7b). These results indicate that NbRH20 tends to localize more to the cytoplasm in response to BSMV infection, prompting the question of whether the alteration in NbRH20's cytoplasmic localization is necessary for its anti‐BSMV function.

To address this question, we fused nuclear localization signal (NLS) and nuclear export signal (NES) peptides (Li et al., 2018) to the N‐ and C‐termini of RH20 protein, respectively. Confocal analysis confirmed that fusion with NLS and NES peptides restricted RH20 protein localization to the nucleus and cytoplasm, respectively (Figure 3f). When we transiently co‐expressed NLS–RH20 and RH20–NES with BSMV in N. benthamiana leaves, with untagged RH20 and EV as controls, immunoblot analysis revealed that RH20 overexpression significantly inhibited BSMV infection compared with the EV control (Figure 3g,h), consistent with RH20's negative role in BSMV infection (Figure S7c). In contrast, expression of NLS–RH20 failed to inhibit BSMV infection, while RH20–NES retained the ability to suppress BSMV infection, albeit not as effectively as untagged RH20 (Figure 3g,h). These results suggest that cytoplasmic localization of the RH20 protein is indispensable for its anti‐BSMV function.

Previous results have demonstrated that RH20R428K loses its RNA binding (Bond et al., 2001). We then evaluate the effect of RH20R428K expression on BSMV infection, and immunoblot analysis showed that transient overexpression of RH20R428K failed to suppress BSMV infection, similar to the EV control (Figure 3i,j). Collectively, these results underscore the necessity of cytoplasmic localization and RNA‐binding activity of RH20 for its defensive role against BSMV infection.

RH20 positively regulate antiviral RNAi

Considering the association of the RH20 ortholog in Drosophila with the RISC complex (Ishizuka et al., 2002), we propose that RH20 may confer resistance to BSMV infection by participating in the antiviral RNAi pathway. To investigate this hypothesis, we co‐expressed the γb protein and (positive) sense GFP (sGFP) (Zhang et al., 2018) with RH20, RH20R428K or EV in N. benthamiana leaves, respectively. The combination of EV and sGFP served as the control. At 3 dpi, infiltrated leaves were observed under ultraviolet (UV) light. Results showed that the combination of EV and sGFP exhibited the weakest fluorescence, while the combination of γb and sGFP displayed the strongest fluorescence (Figure 4a), consistent with γb protein being a typical VSR (Bragg and Jackson, 2004). Conversely, when γb and sGFP were expressed in the presence of the RH20 protein, the GFP fluorescence was remarkably reduced compared with that in the absence of RH20 protein, and the fluorescence produced by the combination of γb, sGFP and RH20R428K was intermediate between that of the combinations of γb + sGFP, and γb + sGFP + RH20 (Figure 4a). Immunoblot analysis of sGFP protein accumulation corroborated the fluorescence observations (Figure 4b). These results suggest that the RH20 protein likely promotes RNAi, thereby reducing the accumulation of GFP protein.

To further investigate the relationship between RH20 and the RNAi pathway, we transiently expressed sGFP in the leaves of NT, RH20‐KO and RH20‐KD transgenic plants, respectively. At 3 dpi, observations under UV light revealed a significantly higher fluorescence intensity of sGFP in the leaves of both RH20–KO and RH20–KD plants compared with the control NT plants (Figure 4c). Immunoblot analysis of sGFP protein accumulation yielded results consistent with the fluorescence observations (Figure 4d). We further analysed the accumulation of sGFP‐derived small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) in RH20–KO plants. Northern blot analysis demonstrated that the knockout of NbRH20 significantly reduced the accumulation of sGFP‐derived siRNAs compared with the control NT plants, analogous to the reduced siRNA levels observed in transgenic plants with knocked down RDR6 (Figure 4e). Moreover, when mutant BSMVBM26, with abolished γb VSR activity (Donald and Jackson, 1996), was used to infect the three different plant lines mentioned above, consistent results were obtained. Reduced levels of virus‐derived small interfering RNAs (vsiRNAs) were observed in either RH20–KO or RDR6i plants compared with the control NT plants (Figure 4f).

Furthermore, we conducted small RNA deep sequencing on BSMV‐infected RH20‐KO and NT N. benthamiana plants. We found that 77% of the siRNAs matched the BSMV genomic RNA (Figure 4g), with 21 and 22 nt vsiRNAs being predominant (Figure 4h). When comparing the reads of BSMV‐derived vsiRNAs between RH20‐KO and NT, we observed a decrease in the total vsiRNA abundance after RH20 knockout (Figure 4i), consistent with our northern blot analysis of vsiRNA results (Figure 4f). Furthermore, the knockout of RH20 resulted in a reduction in the abundance of 21 nt vsiRNAs relative to the wild type (Figure 4j), suggesting a potential link between the decreased levels of 21 nt vsiRNAs and the enhanced accumulation of BSMV in RH20‐KO N. benthamiana plants (Figure 3a–c). Collectively, these results indicate that RH20 negatively regulates BSMV infection by promoting RNAi‐based antiviral immunity.

RH20 interacts with SGS3/RDR6 bodies

Since the RH20 positively regulates antiviral RNAi, the next question is how about the molecular mechanisms underlying this. To address this question, we reanalysed the LC‐MS/MS data obtained from the PL assay. Interestingly, SGS3, known to play a crucial role in the RNAi pathway, was identified in the γb proxitome, despite exhibiting a fold change of 1.3 in the BSMVγb‐TurboID group (Table S1). This suggests a potential association between the SGS3 protein and either RH20 or the γb protein. We then tested whether RH20 interacts with NbSGS3 by BiFC and Co‐IP, and the results showed that RH20 interacts with the NbSGS3 protein in vivo (Figure 5a,b; Figure S4c). Since we failed to purify NbSGS3 protein, as an alternative, we purified the C‐terminal domains of AtSGS3 by procaryotic expression (Tong et al., 2021). Despite the amino acid sequences of SGS3 proteins from A. thaliana and N. benthamiana sharing only about 50% identity, they exhibited a notable structural similarity (Figure S8a,b). This implies functional conservation of SGS3 proteins across different plant species, which is supported by the interaction between RH20 from N. benthamiana and SGS3 from A. thaliana to some extent (Figure S8c). We then conducted a GST pull‐down assay involving RH20 and truncated AtSGS3 proteins (Figure S8d), and the results revealed a direct interaction between RH20 and AtSGS3 (Figure 5c).

As SGS3 always works together with RDR6 and forms cytoplasmic SGS3/RDR6 bodies (Kumakura et al., 2009), we then examined whether the NbRH20 protein is spatially associated with SGS3/RDR6 bodies. Confocal microscopy analysis of RH20–RFP, NbSGS3–BFP and NbRDR6–GFP showed their co‐localization in the cytoplasm, forming small florescent puncta (Figure 5d). Moreover, when further marking SGS3/RDR6 bodies by co‐expressing AtSGS3–YFPn and AtRDR6–YFPc, RH20 protein was also observed to localize to these bodies (Figure 5e). Furthermore, we transiently coexpressed the RH20, NbRDR6 and NbSGS3 proteins in N. benthamiana leaves followed by Co‐IP analysis. The results showed that NbSGS3 co‐immunoprecipitates with both NbRDR6 and RH20 (Figure 5f). These results strongly suggest that RH20 contributes to antiviral RNAi through interaction with SGS3/RDR6 bodies.

To further elucidate the genetic relationship between RH20 and SGS3/RDR6 bodies, we transiently overexpressed RH20 protein in non‐transgenic N. benthamiana plants as well as in plants where the NbRDR6, NbDCL2/4 or NbAGO2 genes were knocked down (Figure S6), followed by BSMV inoculation via agroinfiltration. Immunoblot analysis showed that RH20 protein can inhibit BSMV accumulation in NT N. benthamiana plants, whereas its ability to inhibit BSMV was compromised in RDR6i, DCL2/4i and AGO2i N. benthamiana plants (Figure 5g). It deserves our attention that in these three transgenic N. benthamiana lines where the key genes in the RNAi pathway were down‐regulated, both the RH20 protein level and the BSMV TGB1 accumulation were higher than those in control wild‐type plants (Figure 5g), which is consistent with previous reports showing that knocking down key genes in the RNAi pathway increases plant susceptibility to virus infections (Garcia‐Ruiz et al., 2010; Jaubert et al., 2011; Mourrain et al., 2000; Wang et al., 2010). These results suggest that RH20 protein primarily relies on the RNAi pathway to exert its inhibitory effect on BSMV. Furthermore, we employed tobacco rattle virus (TRV)‐induced gene silencing to down‐regulate the expression of NbSGS3 in NT or RDR6i N. benthamiana plants. Compared with non‐silenced N. benthamiana plants inoculated with the empty TRV‐based VIGS vector (TRV::00), immunoblot analysis showed that silencing NbSGS3 alone or NbSGS3 and NbRDR6 together resulted in the loss of NbRH20's ability to inhibit BSMV infection (Figure 5h). The down‐regulation of NbSGS3 and NbRDR6 genes was confirmed by qRT‐PCR (Figure 5i). These results underscore the genetic dependence of RH20 on SGS3/RDR6 bodies for its antiviral activity.

BSMV γb subverts RH20‐mediated defence by interfering with RH20–SGS3 interaction

To elucidate the biological significance of the interaction between γb and RH20, we conducted transient co‐expression experiments where RH20 protein was expressed alongside wild‐type BSMV, a mutant BSMVmγb lacking functional γb protein, and a mutant BSMVBM26 with impaired interaction between γb protein and RH20 (Figure 2), respectively, via agroinfiltration into N. benthamiana leaves. The results revealed that overexpression of RH20 led to approximately a 26% reduction in the accumulation of wild‐type BSMV. In contrast, the accumulation of BSMVmγb and BSMVBM26 was reduced by approximately 63% and 68%, respectively, upon RH20 overexpression (Figure 6a). This underscores that RH20 exerts a more pronounced inhibition of BSMV when the interaction between γb and RH20 is disrupted. These findings suggest that BSMV may exploit the γb protein to target RH20, thereby neutralizing the antiviral effects mediated by RH20.

As RH20 interacts with and depends on NbSGS3 to defend against BSMV infection (Figure 5h), and γb also interacts with RH20 (Figure 2), we hypothesized that γb might disrupt the interaction between RH20 and SGS3. To test this hypothesis, we conducted a LCI assay. The results revealed that co‐expression of SGS3‐nLUC and cLUC‐RH20 yielded a significant fluorescent signal. However, in the presence of BSMV, the interaction between SGS3 and RH20 was markedly impaired, resulting in a significant reduction in the fluorescent signal in comparison with the EV control (Figure 6b). Conversely, infection with BSMVmγb and BSMVBM26 showed higher fluorescent signal generated by the SGS3–RH20 interaction than that of BSMV (Figure 6b). Additionally, co‐expression of SGS3–nLUC and cLUC–RH20 with γb or γbBM26 proteins showed that γb significantly inhibited the SGS3–RH20 interaction, while γbBM26 protein had minimal effect on their interaction (Figure 6c). Immunoblot analysis validated the protein expression in the LCI assay (Figure 6d,e). Furthermore, in vitro competitive GST pull‐down assays consistently demonstrated that γb inhibits the binding of RH20 protein to the CC domain of AtSGS3 in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 6f). Taken together, these results indicate that BSMV γb interferes with the RH20‐NbSGS3 interaction to counteract RH20‐mediated defence.

A general role of RH20 in RNAi‐based antiviral immunity

To investigate whether the RH20 protein also exerts antiviral functions during the infection process of other viruses, we agroinoculated three other RNA viruses, including GFP‐tagged turnip mosaic virus (TuMV) (Lellis et al., 2002) and cucumber mosaic virus (CMV) (Zhang et al., 2021), as well as beet black scorch virus (BBSV) (Gao et al., 2022), into transgenic N. benthamiana plants with knocked‐out (RH20‐KO), knocked‐down (RH20‐KD) and overexpressed (RH20‐OE) RH20 expression (Figure S6), respectively. Symptom observation revealed that knocking out or knocking down RH20 enhanced the susceptibility of N. benthamiana to these viruses, as manifested by an earlier onset and more severe systemic symptoms following viral infection compared to NT control (Figure 7a). Conversely, overexpression of RH20 delayed the onset of systemic symptoms induced by viral infection and mitigated symptom severity (Figure 7b). Immunoblot analysis of the accumulation levels of the corresponding viruses in infected N. benthamiana plants corroborated the symptom observation results (Figure 7c–h). These results suggest that RH20 exhibits broad‐spectrum antiviral activity during plant virus infection.

Discussion

In this study, we employed the BSMV γb protein as the bait, aiming to elucidate the molecular mechanism by which γb exerts VSR activity through identification of its interacting proteins. Using TurboID‐based PL approach, we characterized the proxitome of γb VSR and identified a new interacting partner of γb, the RH20 protein. Upon BSMV invasion of plant cells, BSMV replication generates dsRNA, triggering host RNA silencing. Dicer‐like proteins (DCLs) then cleave dsRNA into vsiRNAs, which are loaded into AGO proteins to form the RISC, leading to the cleavage of viral genomes and the generation of virus‐derived aberrant RNAs (abRNAs). Concurrently, BSMV infection induces increased cytoplasmic distribution of RH20 protein and its recruitment to SGS3/RDR6 bodies (siRNA bodies), facilitating SGS3/RDR6‐dependent vsiRNA amplification, thereby enhancing RNAi‐mediated antiviral defence (Figure 7i, left panel). With the ongoing of virus infection, BSMV expresses large amounts of γb VSR protein, which targets SGS3/RDR6 bodies (siRNA bodies) and competitively interferes with the interaction between RH20 and SGS3 proteins in a dose‐dependent manner, thus impairing vsiRNA amplification to counteract RH20‐mediated virus resistance and ultimately maintaining normal viral infection (Figure 7i, right panel). Our findings elucidate at least one molecular mechanism by which the BSMV γb protein exerts VSR activity and unveil a previously unknown function of the RH20 protein by linking it to the RNA silencing pathway.

Defining the proximal interaction networks of γb VSR

Currently, the identification of new regulators in the plant antiviral RNAi pathway often involves forward genetic screens in Arabidopsis, using CMV defective in RNAi suppression (Guo et al., 2017, 2018; Liu et al., 2022), or traditional Co‐IP combined with LC‐MS/MS analysis of known RNA silencing pathway proteins such as Hyponastic Leaves 1 (HYL1) (Li et al., 2021). VSRs typically act as pathogenicity determinants for viruses. Nevertheless, over the past two decades, VSRs have provided valuable materials for identifying and analysing RNA silencing (Baulcombe, 2022). Recently, the TurboID‐based PL technique has been widely used for analysing transient/weak interactions or the interaction partners of membrane‐associated proteins (Branon et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2019, 2023a). Proteins identified using this technique can largely reflect true protein–protein interaction events within living cells. To date, there have been no reports of using PL technology to analyse constituents of the plant RNAi pathway.

Here, we identified multiple candidate γb‐interacting proteins using the PL approach, including BSMV TGB1, CP and αa proteins, which have been validated to interact with γb (Jiang et al., 2020; Yue et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2017), along with numerous RNA binding proteins (Figure 1; Tables S1 and S2). Besides the DEAD‐box family protein RH20 investigated in this study, we also identified several proteins related to RNA metabolism pathways (Figure 1e,f; Table S3), known to be closely associated with virus infection or antiviral defence. These include Decapping 5 (DCP5) protein, a component of processing bodies (P‐bodies) (Hoffmann et al., 2022), U6 snRNA‐associated Sm‐like protein Lsm4 (Wang et al., 2021) and proteins functioning in the nonsense‐mediated mRNA decay pathway, such as Regulator of nonsense transcripts 1 (UPF1) and 5′–3′ exoribonuclease 4 (XRN4) (Li and Wang, 2019; Wu et al., 2023), although the fold change of XRN4 was slightly below the 1.5 cut‐off. Additionally, we found polyadenylate‐binding proteins, which were reported to be involved in viral translation (Iwakawa et al., 2012), and mRNA stability regulators including m6A readers known as evolutionarily conserved C‐terminal region (ECT) 2, 5, and 6 (Martínez‐Pérez et al., 2023) (Figure 1; Table S1). These findings suggest, on the one hand, that γb, as a classical VSR with RNA‐binding ability, likely regulates multiple aspects of RNA metabolism to achieve optimal virus accumulation, highlighting its crucial roles in the coevolution between BSMV and plant hosts. On the other hand, they also imply that the identified RNA‐binding proteins in the γb proxitome might potentially engage in crosstalk or interact with each other.

Furthermore, in addition to the RH20–SGS3–RDR6 module studied here, we also identified other RNAi pathway components such as double‐stranded RNA‐binding protein 2 (DRB2) (Fátyol et al., 2020) and Argonaute 2 (AGO2) (Harvey et al., 2011) proteins in the γb proxitome (Figure 1; Table S1), suggesting that γb might target other components of the RNAi pathway to suppress antiviral RNAi. Collectively, the γb VSR proxitome data obtained in our study underscore the distinct advantages of using viruses to investigate the RNA‐silencing pathway and provide important resources for further exploration of the regulatory network of antiviral RNAi.

Molecular characterization of RH20 in host‐virus interaction

The DEAD‐box RNA helicases constitute the largest family of RNA helicases, functioning in nearly all aspects of RNA‐related processes (Bourgeois et al., 2016; Linder and Jankowsky, 2011; Sloan and Bohnsack, 2018). DEAD‐box helicases are named for their highly conserved amino acid sequence D‐E‐A‐D (Asp‐Glu‐Ala‐Asp) within the Walker B motif (Linder et al., 1989) (Figures S2 and S5a). Proteins from the DEAD‐box RNA helicase family play diverse roles in plant growth, development and responses to various biotic and abiotic stresses (Li et al., 2023; Pandey et al., 2020). Recent studies reveal their involvement in forming biomolecular condensates such as P bodies and dicing bodies (D‐bodies), enabling plants to respond to various stresses (Li et al., 2024). For instance, Arabidopsis RH6, RH8 and RH12 interact with the zinc‐finger protein SERRATE and promote the formation of dicing bodies through liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS) (Li et al., 2021). Interestingly, Brassica napus RH6 was found to localize to cytoplasmic P bodies in response to salt stresses (Zhang et al., 2023b), suggesting that the same DEAD‐box RNA helicase may target different biomolecular condensates in response to different stresses. RH20, as a member of the DEAD‐box RNA helicase family, is evolutionarily conserved in plants and animals, its Drosophila homolog of p68 RNA helicase (Dmp68) is required for efficient RNAi (Ishizuka et al., 2002). However, how RH20 regulates RNAi remains elusive to date. In this study, we demonstrated that RH20 associates with SGS3/RDR6‐containing condensates, known as SGS3/RDR6‐bodies (Figure 5), to facilitate siRNA production (Figure 4). Indeed, recent research indicate that SGS3 promotes the formation of SGS3/RDR6‐bodies formation through LLPS, and LC‐MS/MS analysis revealed the presence of multiple DEAD‐box RNA helicase family proteins in SGS3/RDR6 bodies, including the RH20 protein focused on in our study (Tan et al., 2023), further substantiating RH20's association with SGS3/RDR6‐bodies to promote RNA silencing.

The functions of DEAD‐box helicase family proteins vary in viral infections (Taschuk and Cherry, 2020; Ullah et al., 2022). For instance, some viruses such as TBSV and potyviruses can co‐opt various DEAD‐box helicase proteins to promote their infections (Huang et al., 2010; Kovalev and Nagy, 2014; Li et al., 2016, 2021). In contrast, DEAD‐box helicases such as DDX17‐like protein RH30 can effectively inhibit virus infection by impeding the assembly of the virus replication complex (Wu and Nagy, 2019). Regarding RH20, an earlier study demonstrated that Dbp2p, a protein homologous to AtRH20, can be exploited by TBSV to facilitate virus infection (Kovalev et al., 2012). This appears to contradict the antiviral role of RH20 observed in our study. One possible explanation for this discrepancy could be that we investigated the role of RH20 in virus infection using the plant system instead of yeast. We also noted that although RH20–NES retains its ability to inhibit BSMV infection, this ability is reduced compared with the wild‐type RH20 (Figure 3g,h). The possible reason is that while cytoplasmic localization of RH20 is essential, it is not sufficient for RH20 to fully exert its antiviral activity.

Suppression of antiviral RNAi by VSR

Similar to pathogens such as bacteria, which inhibit pattern‐triggered immunity by secreting various effectors (Dou and Zhou, 2012), during long‐term arms race between plants and viruses, viruses have evolved diverse VSRs to counteract host antiviral RNAi. These VSRs can target one or multiple components of the RNA silencing pathway, such as DCL and AGO proteins, to undermine RNAi‐mediated defence (Lopez‐Gomollon and Baulcombe, 2022). Several VSRs encoded by viruses such as turnip crinkle virus, rice tungro bacilliform virus, turnip mosaic virus, potato virus A, sweet potato chlorotic stunt virus, plantago asiatica mosaic virus and tomato yellow leaf curl virus interact with SGS3 to disrupt RNAi (Cheng and Wang, 2017; Glick et al., 2008; Liu et al., 2023; Naresh et al., 2023; Okano et al., 2014; Rajamäki et al., 2014; Weinheimer et al., 2016). In this study, we found that the γb VSR targets the positive regulator of SGS3/RDR6 bodies, RH20, to impede RNAi, further indicating that VSRs can dampen antiviral RNAi by affecting multiple layers of RNAi pathway. Considering RH20's broad‐spectrum antiviral activity, it would be interesting to investigate whether other VSRs besides γb can target RH20 to suppress RNAi.

In summary, our studies indicate that RH20 promotes antiviral silencing by enhancing the accumulation of vsiRNAs. RH20 exerts antiviral effects during multiple virus infections (Figure 7), further supporting the notion that RH20 may serve as a novel component of the antiviral RNAi pathway, alongside SGS3 and RDR6. Additionally, besides influencing vsiRNAs accumulation, our deep sequencing analysis of siRNAs suggest that RH20 also plays a role in regulating the accumulation of miRNAs (Figure S9). Considering the association of RH20 with SGS3/RDR6 bodies, we speculate that RH20 might modulate the amplification of abRNA, the phase separation of SGS3, or other processes related to SGS3/RDR6 bodies. Further precise functional characterization of RH20 will clarify and underscore its significant role in RNA silencing pathways, thus providing new targets for designing antiviral strategies.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Seeds of the transgenic N. benthamiana DCL2/4i plants were kindly provided by Dr. Kriton Kalantidis (Institute of Molecular Biology and Biotechnology, Heraklion, Greece) (Dadami et al., 2013). Seeds of the transgenic N. benthamiana RDR6i were kindly provided by Dr. Huishan Guo (Institute of Microbiology, Chinese Academy of Sciences). All transgenic plants and wild‐type N. benthamiana plants were grown in a greenhouse maintained at 23 °C, with a 15/9 h light/dark photoperiod.

Samples preparation for proximity labelling (PL) assay

Leaf samples for PL experiments were prepared according to previously reported methods (Zhang et al., 2023a). Briefly, leaves of 4–6‐week‐old N. benthamiana plants were infiltrated with Agrobacterium mixtures containing the corresponding plasmids. At 64 h post inoculation (hpi), the leaves were infiltrated with 200 μM biotin (in 10 mM MgCl2) followed by incubation for 8 h at room temperature (RT). Subsequently, the leaves were snap‐frozen and stored at −80 °C until use. Each experimental group consisted of three biological replicates.

Proteins were initially extracted from leaf powder using RIPA buffer and desalted with Zeba spin column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 89893). After quantification via Bradford assay, equal amounts of total protein and streptavidin beads (Invitrogen, Cat. No. 65001) were incubated at 4 °C for 16 h. The streptavidin beads were sequentially washed with buffer I (2% SDS [w/v]), buffer II (50 mM HEPES, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1% deoxycholic acid [w/v], 1% Triton X‐100) and buffer III (10 mM Tris‐HCl, 250 mM LiCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1% deoxycholic acid [w/v], 1% IGEPAL‐630 [v/v]), followed by two washes in 50 mM Tris‐HCl (pH 7.5) and six washes in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate. After the final wash step, the streptavidin bead pull‐down samples were frozen and stored at −80°C. All solutions and buffers utilized for streptavidin pull‐down experiments were freshly prepared.

On‐bead trypsin digestion and TMT labelling

Biotinylated proteins immobilized on streptavidin beads were treated with 5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) and 20 mM iodoacetamide (IAM), and then digested overnight at 37°C using 1 μg of trypsin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. NO. 90058). Peptides were extracted using a 10 000 MWCOHY ultrafiltration products of VI‐VACON 500 (Sartorius Stedim Biotech GmbH, Cat. NO. VN01H02). Following quantification via Pierce quantitative colorimetry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. NO. 23275), the peptides were dissolved in 50 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) (Sigma, Cat. NO. T7408) and assessed for quality using Thermo Q‐Exactive high‐resolution MS (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA).

For TMT labelling, 20 μg of each sample was supplemented with 50 mM TEAB to standardize the volume. Subsequently, 40 μL of TMT 10‐plex Label Reagent dissolved in acetonitrile (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 90066) was added, and the reaction mixture was incubated at RT for 2 h. Next, 20 μL of formic acid was added to each tube and incubated at RT for 15 min to terminate the labelling reaction. After quenching, 5 μL of TMT 10‐plex‐labelled reactions was combined, and the proportions of different labelled components in the mixture were calculated using Mascot (version 2.4; Matrix Science, London, UK) and Scaffold software (Version 3.6.5, Proteome Software, Portland). Based on the calculated proportions, equal amounts of peptides from different samples were mixed and dried using vacuum centrifugation at 45°C. A gradient elution program was employed by utilizing a 20 mM aqueous solution of ammonium acetate (pH 10.0) and a 20 mM acetonitrile solution of ammonium acetate (pH 10.0) through a C18 stationary phase (2.1 mm, 100 mm, 2.6 m, Kinetex, Phenomenex, Catalog NO. 00D‐4462‐AN). Fractionation was conducted using High‐Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) system (HClass bio, Waters, Milford, MA).

Data analysis

Hierarchical clustering of peptides and score sum matrices of γb proximal proteins was conducted using Gene cluster 3.0, and the results were visualized with Java Tree View. The resulting heatmap illustrates significant enrichment fold changes (γb/Citrine) ≥1.5.

Proteins in the γb proximal protein–protein interactions (PPI) network were primarily selected based on BH test truncation with a significance threshold of P < 0.05 and normalized fold change (γb/Citrine) ≥1.5, which represents high confidence. A subset of Arabidopsis orthologs corresponding to N. benthamiana protein inputs was identified, and their interactions were predicted using the STRING online platform. The minimum interaction score required was set to 0.4, indicating medium confidence. Visualization of the information was performed using Cytoscape program (version 3.9.1) to illustrate the PPI network.

Volcano plots or GO dot plots illustrating grouped pathways of significantly enriched proteins were generated using the RStudio software and the R programming language. GO term analysis was conducted using prediction and enrichment tools available on the online agriGO v2.0 platform (http://10.2.42.17/agriGO/). The data utilized for this analysis were obtained from predicted one‐to‐one associations with Arabidopsis, and the homologues were retrieved from the N. benthamiana GO term annotation database.

Generation of transgenic N. benthamiana plants

Transgenic N. benthamiana lines, including RH20‐KO, RH20‐KD, RH20‐OE and AGO2i, were generated through Agrobacterium‐mediated leaf disc transformation. RH20‐KO and RH20‐KD lines were obtained by transforming N. benthamiana plants with Agrobacterium carrying BGK01–RH20 and pMDC32–RH20i, respectively. Guide RNAs for knocking out RH20 were designed using the online CRISPR website (http://crispr.hzau.edu.cn/CRISPR2/). A 300 bp segment from the RH20 gene was selected using the online VIGS tool. RH20‐OE lines was generated by transforming N. benthamiana plants with Agrobacterium containing pGWB14‐RH20. AGO2i lines was generated by transforming N. benthamiana plants with Agrobacterium containing pMDC32‐AGO2i. RH20 and AGO2 knockdown was examined by qRT‐PCR, while the knockout of RH20 was confirmed by sequencing the genomic DNA fragments encompassing the guide RNA‐targeted regions. Verification of RH20 overexpression was conducted through immunoblot analysis (Figure S6d).

Reverse transcription‐quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from N. benthamiana leaves using TRIzol (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer's instructions. qRT‐PCR was conducted as previously described (Liu et al., 2012). Briefly, 3 μg of purified RNA was treated with DNase I (Takara) to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. Reverse transcription was performed using a reverse transcription mix (Mei5bio) to synthesize cDNA. qPCR analysis was performed using the Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Mei5bio) in the Bio‐Rad CFX96 Real‐Time PCR Detection System (Bio‐Rad). The Protein Phosphatase 2A (PP2A) gene was used as the internal control. Data analysis was performed using Bio‐Rad CFX Manager. Primers used for qRT‐PCR are listed in Table S6.
