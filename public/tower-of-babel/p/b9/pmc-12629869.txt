Breaking barriers: The cGAS‐STING pathway as a novel frontier in cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Yuheng Yan, Ximin Tan, Bin Song, Ming Yi, Qian Chu, Kongming Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSince its discovery, the cyclic GMP‐AMP synthase (cGAS)‐stimulator of the interferon gene (STING) signaling pathway has been considered a pivotal component of innate immunity and a promising target for cancer immunotherapy. Beyond its canonical role in pathogen defense, accumulating evidence has demonstrated that the cGAS‐STING pathway critically regulates diverse cellular processes, including cellular senescence, autophagy, cell death, and tumor immunosurveillance; therefore, dysregulation of this pathway correlates with the pathogenesis and progression of various human diseases, ranging from autoimmune and inflammatory disorders to cancer. Herein, we reviewed the regulatory mechanisms and cellular functions of the cGAS‐STING pathway, highlighting its essential role in maintaining immune homeostasis. We systematically discussed the dual roles of the cGAS‐STING pathway in cancer immunity, in which it triggers both antitumor and immunosuppressive effects. Finally, we summarized the recent advances and challenges in therapeutic strategies targeting the cGAS‐STING pathway and discussed the next generation of therapies, including nanomaterials, antibody‐drug conjugates, engineered bacteria, alternative strategies, optogenetic approaches, and combination strategies. We hope that our efforts will advance the understanding of the fundamental principles of innate immune recognition and response, and provide novel directions for improving the clinical outcomes of cGAS‐STING‐targeted therapies.Keywords: cancer immunotherapy, innate immunity, cGAS‐STING signaling pathway

INTRODUCTION

In 1989, immunologist Charles A. Janeway Jr. proposed that the immune system discriminated between ‘self’ and ‘non‐self’ molecules through pattern‐recognition receptors (PRRs), enabling specific and appropriate immune responses against pathogen‐associated molecular patterns [1]. In 2008, the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)‐resident stimulator of interferon genes (STING) was identified as a critical mediator of type I interferon (IFN‐α/β) responses, activating IFN regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) and nuclear transcription factor‐kappa B (NF‐κB) transcription pathways to orchestrate antiviral immunity [2]. Yet, the primary PRR responsible for detecting pathogenic DNA remained elusive until 2013, when Zhijian Chen et al. discovered cyclic GMP‐AMP synthase (cGAS) as the crucial sensor that recognizes microbial and endogenous DNA [3, 4]. Upon DNA binding, cGAS synthesizes the second messenger, 2’3’‐cyclic GMP‐AMP (cGAMP), which binds and activates STING, triggering rapid and potent IFN‐mediated immune responses [5].

Over the past decade, research on the cGAS‐STING pathway has revealed its activation mechanisms, regulatory networks, and diverse cellular functions, marking landmark advancements in innate immunity (Figure 1). Beyond its canonical role in antimicrobial defense, cGAS‐STING also regulates cellular senescence, autophagy, lysosomal biogenesis, cell death, and antitumor immunity [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Abnormal cGAS activation by self‐DNA drives aberrant immune responses and contributes to the pathogenesis of a wide range of autoimmune and inflammatory diseases [12, 13, 14, 15]. STING is considered a promising target for antitumor immunotherapy because of its essential role in stimulating antitumor immunity [16]. Intriguingly, chronic STING activation in chromosomally unstable tumors promotes tumorigenesis and metastasis through downstream signal reprogramming and immunosuppressive mechanisms [17, 18, 19, 20]. Moreover, the clinical outcomes of STING agonists are disappointing, which may partially be due to poor membrane permeability [21], systemic toxicity [16], and resistance mechanisms [19, 22]. To overcome these barriers, recent years have witnessed rapid progress in cGAS‐STING‐targeted therapies such as nanomaterials, antibody‐drug conjugates (ADCs), engineered bacteria, alternative strategies, and combination strategies, which offer promising avenues for cancer treatment.

This review systematically summarized recent regulatory and functional advances in cGAS‐STING as a cornerstone of innate immunity. By discussing the dual roles of the cGAS‐STING pathway in cancer immunity, we hope to provide new insights for next‐generation cGAS‐STING‐targeted therapies.

STRUCTURAL AND FUNCTIONAL INSIGHTS INTO cGAS‐STING ACTIVATION

Initially characterized as a cytosolic DNA sensor, cGAS binds to microbial double‐stranded DNA (dsDNA) (such as from viruses and bacteria), extracellular self‐DNA from dead or tumor cells, and self‐DNA released from damaged mitochondria or nuclei (Figure 2A) [23]. However, cGAS is primarily sequestered by nucleosomes under physiological conditions, preventing aberrant activation and maintaining immune homeostasis [24, 25]. cGAS is structured as a cGAS/DncV‐like nucleotidyltransferase domain composed of a C‐terminal domain containing three DNA‐binding sites (A, B, and C) and an unstructured N‐terminal domain [26] (Figure 2B). Upon encountering cytosolic dsDNA, cGAS engages the DNA at sites A and B to form a 2:2 cGAS‐dsDNA complex. This interaction induces conformational rearrangements that structure the catalytic site, enabling the conversion of ATP and GTP to cGAMP, a cyclic dinucleotide (CDN) featuring 2’‐5’ and 3’‐5’ bonds between AMP and GMP (Figure 2A and C) [27, 28].

Functioning as an endogenous second messenger, cGAMP triggers the ER‐resident adaptor, STING, a transmembrane protein featuring four helical domains and a cytosolic region responsible for ligand binding and signal transduction [5, 29] (Figure 2D). cGAMP binding induces structural rearrangements in STING, facilitating oligomerization from dimers to tetramers/higher‐order oligomers and triggering downstream cascades (Figure 2A) [5]. Following activation, STING undergoes coat protein complex (COP)II‐mediated vesicular trafficking, relocating sequentially from the ER to the ER‐Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC) and the Golgi apparatus [8]. In the Golgi, TANK‐binding kinase 1 (TBK1) is recruited to the C‐terminal domain of STING. Subsequent TBK1 autophosphorylation and STING phosphorylation at serine (S) 366 (human) or S365 (mouse) in the C‐terminal tail (CTT) enables IRF3 activation and nuclear translocation, initiating the transcription of type I interferons (IFNs) and interferon‐stimulated genes (ISGs) [30, 31]. Simultaneously, STING activates the NF‐κB pathway, upregulating proinflammatory cytokines including IFN‐β, tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), interleukin (IL)‐1β, and IL‐6 [32, 33, 34]. However, the mechanism of STING‐mediated NF‐κB activation remains poorly understood, including whether this process requires TBK1 or its homolog IκB kinase ε (IKKε), and whether NF‐κB interacts directly with the STING signalosome or indirectly [35, 36].

Ubiquitination, SUMOylation, and ISGylation

Ubiquitination plays an essential role in regulating the activation and stabilization of both cGAS and STING. Lysine (K) 48‐, K62‐, and K27‐linked polyubiquitinations, by E3 ubiquitin ligases, such as tripartite motif‐containing protein 7 (TRIM27) [37] and ubiquitin‐protein ligase E3C (UBE3C) [38], inhibit cGAS activity by impairing its DNA‐binding capacity and promoting proteasomal degradation. In contrast, monoubiquitination mediated by TRIM56 and TRIM41 enhances cGAS dimerization, DNA‐binding ability, and cGAMP synthesis [39, 40]. The dynamic balance of these modifications is further regulated by deubiquitinating enzymes, such as ubiquitin‐specific proteases, which promote cGAS stabilization and activation by removing inhibitory ubiquitin chains [41, 42]. Similarly, STING activity is precisely controlled by ubiquitination and deubiquitination that influence oligomerization, intracellular trafficking, protein stability, and interactions with TBK1 (Table 2).

Likewise, SUMOylation and ISGylation critically regulate cGAS‐STING activity via E3 ligases. For example, TRIM38‐mediated SUMOylation of both cGAS and STING enhances their stability and promotes early phase antiviral interferon responses, whereas SUMO‐specific protease 2 (SENP2)‐mediated deSUMOylation facilitates their degradation during the later stages of infection to prevent excessive inflammation [43]. Additionally, the E3 ubiquitin ligases, ariadne RBR E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 1 (ARIH1) [44] and HECT and RLD domain containing E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 5 (HERC5) [45], catalyze the ISGylation of cGAS, further potentiating its antiviral activity.

Phosphorylation

Phosphorylation, one of the most fundamental and extensively studied PTMs, regulates both cGAS enzymatic activity and STING signaling function (Tables 1 and 2). For instance, mitotic kinase cyclin‐dependent kinase 1 (CDK1)‐cyclin B complex phosphorylates human cGAS at S305 (S291 in mice) during mitosis to prevent cGAS‐mediated aberrant self‐DNA sensing during cell division, and protein kinase B (AKT)‐mediated phosphorylation at the same residue negatively modulates antiviral immune responses following viral DNA stimulation [46, 47]. In contrast, type 1 phosphatase (PP1) counteracts these inhibitory effects by dephosphorylating cGAS and restoring its catalytic capacity for cGAMP synthesis [46]. Similarly, phosphorylation exerts multifaceted regulatory effects on STING oligomerization, translocation, and downstream activation. TBK1 phosphorylates STING at S366 (human) or S365 (mouse) in the CTT and promotes IRF3 recruitment and downstream signaling [30, 31], whereas UNC‐51‐like kinase (ULK1)‐mediated phosphorylation at the same residue paradoxically suppresses IRF3 activation, demonstrating the complex, context‐dependent nature of the phosphorylation‐mediated regulation of STING [48].

Palmitoylation

Palmitoylation, a dynamic lipid modification catalyzed by zinc finger and DHHC motif‐containing (ZDHHC) palmitoyltransferases, is a critical regulatory mechanism for both cGAS and STING activity (Tables 1 and 2). Palmitoylation of cGAS at the cysteine residues (C404/405) enhances its dimerization and potentiates downstream immune responses [49]. In contrast, the lysophospholipase‐like 1 (LYPLAL1)‐induced de‐palmitoylation of cGAS attenuates cGAS activity, and the inhibition of LYPLAL1 promotes an inflammatory response and enhances the efficacy of programmed death‐1 (PD‐1) blockade therapy [49]. Similarly, palmitoylation plays an indispensable role in STING activation, as evidenced by impaired antiviral responses observed in STING variants with C88 and C91 mutations that prevent proper palmitoylation [50].

Acetylation

Aspirin was discovered to directly acetylate cGAS at specific lysine residues (K384, K394, and K414), resulting in the suppression of cGAS‐mediated immune responses in autoimmune diseases [51]. Subsequent mass spectrometry analyses revealed the complex site‐specific effects of cGAS acetylation, showing that acetyl‐mimetic mutations at K384 and K414 inhibited cGAS activity, whereas K198 acetylation enhanced antiviral responses [52]. Further studies identified lysine acetyltransferase 5 (KAT5) as a positive regulator that promotes cGAS activation through N‐terminal acetylation (K47, K56, K62, and K83) [53]. The acetylation regulatory network extends to STING: Beclin‐1 binds to the C‐terminal domain of STING and induces K338 acetylation, triggering autophagy‐mediated degradation [54] (Tables 1 and 2).

Other PTMs

Recent studies have revealed several novel regulatory mechanisms of how PTMs regulate the cGAS‐STING pathway (Tables 1 and 2). Among these, alanyl‐tRNA synthetase 1 (AARS1) and AARS2 mediate cGAS lactylation, thereby modulating its phase separation properties and impairing its DNA‐sensing capacity in response to metabolic changes [55]. Another recently discovered modification involves the poly(ADP‐ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1)‐mediated PARylation of cGAS at aspartic acid 191 (D191), which occurs following viral‐induced genomic instability and subsequent PARP1 translocation to the cytoplasm, effectively suppressing antiviral responses by interfering with cGAS‐DNA interactions [56]. Additionally, alkylation of STING at specific cysteine residues (C65, C71, C88, and C147) inhibits STING‐mediated secretion of inflammatory factors [57]. Collectively, these multilayered PTM networks enable the precise spatiotemporal control of cGAS‐STING signaling.

Epigenetic modifications of cGAS‐STING signaling

Epigenetic modifications exert multilayered control over the cGAS‐STING pathway activity. At the transcriptional level, promoter hypermethylation of cGAS and STING genes leads to transcriptional silencing, promoting an immunosuppressive microenvironment and facilitating tumor progression [58, 59, 60, 61, 62]. Notably, cancer‐associated mutations in isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1) drive epigenetic silencing of cGAS by inducing its hypermethylation [63]. N6‐methyladenosine (m6A) RNA methylation mediated by methyltransferase‐like 3 (METTL3) exerts opposite effects by stabilizing cGAS and STING mRNAs, thereby potentiating inflammatory responses under sterile conditions, such as fibrosis [64]. At the post‐translational level, epigenetic modifications regulate cGAS activity via protein methylation. The methyltransferase, SUV39H1, catalyzes cGAS methylation and promotes its chromatin sequestration, thus impairing cGAS‐mediated antitumor immunity in colorectal cancer [65]. Suppression of cGAS can be reversed by methionine deprivation, which has therapeutic potential for colorectal cancer treatment [65]. Similarly, protein arginine N‐methyltransferase (PRMP5) induces cGAS methylation at the conserved arginine 124 residue (R124), thereby inhibiting its DNA‐binding ability and suppressing the antiviral response [66]. In summary, epigenetic modifications of the cGAS‐STING pathway often result in loss of gene function and promotion of tumor progression.

Mechanisms to avoid abnormal self‐DNA recognition

The activation of cGAS is strictly dependent on its interaction with dsDNA, structure [27], form [28], and length [67], which determine the efficiency of DNA sensing and binding. However, cGAS lacks pathogen specificity during the recognition of dsDNA. Therefore, additional regulatory mechanisms are required to prevent erroneous inflammation caused by the recognition of self‐DNA by cGAS.

Deoxyribonucleases (DNases)

DNases maintain cellular DNA homeostasis by constitutively degrading potentially immunostimulatory cytosolic DNA. Three repair exonucleases (TREXs) and DNase II have emerged as the primary physiological suppressors of aberrant cGAS activation. The abnormal expression of ISGs triggered by TREX1 deficiency can be abrogated by the suppression of cGAS [68], unequivocally establishing the cGAS‐STING pathway as the dominant mediator of this autoimmune response. TREX1 exhibits critical immuno‐oncological functions, and its DNA‐degrading activity facilitates tumor immune evasion, a discovery that has spurred significant interest in developing TREX1 inhibitors as novel immunotherapeutic agents [69, 70]. Furthermore, the related exonuclease, TREX2, is another key negative regulator of the cGAS‐STING pathway, and its DNA‐scavenging activity similarly suppresses antitumor immunity [71, 72, 73]. These findings collectively position DNases as central players in maintaining immune tolerance and that their dysfunction leads to either autoimmune pathology or cancer progression.

Cellular compartmentalization

Under physiological conditions, dsDNA is strictly compartmentalized within the nucleus and mitochondria to prevent the inadvertent activation of the cGAS‐STING pathway. However, various pathological processes can disrupt this spatial regulation through three principal mechanisms: (1) Micronuclei formation: genome instability arising from mitotic errors, chronic inflammation, DNA damage, or malignant transformation, which leads to the generation of micronuclei. These nuclear envelope‐encapsulated DNA aggregates serve as potent platforms for cGAS activation, with their frequency correlating with disease severity in cancer and autoinflammatory disorders [74, 75, 76]; (2) Cytosolic accumulation of chromatin fragments: Cellular senescence and oncogenic transformation cause nuclear envelope integrity loss, releasing chromatin fragments into the cytosol [10, 77]. Moreover, metastatic tumor cells exhibit particularly pronounced nuclear envelope ruptures during extracellular matrix traversal, exposing nuclear dsDNA to cGAS [78, 79]; (3) Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) release: Oxidative stress or permeability transition pore opening disrupts mitochondrial membrane integrity, causing packaging and subsequent cytosolic release of defective mtDNA and activation of cGAS‐STING signaling [80, 81].

Sophisticated mechanisms govern cGAS nuclear sequestration and its controlled release into the cytoplasm, which are critical for maintaining immune homeostasis [24, 25]. Structural studies reveal that cGAS is constitutively anchored to nucleosomes through specific interactions between its DNA‐binding domain and the nucleosome acidic patch, mediated by evolutionarily conserved residues (R236, R255, R353, and K354); their mutations liberate cGAS from nucleosome sequestration and make it accessible to self‐DNA [24, 25, 82, 83, 84]. Notably, the expressions of nuclear localization and export sequences regulate cGAS cellular distribution and catalytic activity [85, 86, 87, 88].

Regulation of cGAMP transmission and degradation

cGAMP serves not only as an intracellular second messenger that activates STING [4], but also as an immunotransmitter between cells (Figure 3) [89, 90]. cGAMP was initially reported to transfer through gap junctions to bystander cells, amplifying antiviral immunity [89]. Later, cGAMP was found to be incorporated into viral particles [91, 92] or secreted in a soluble form [93] to deliver immune signals to non‐adjacent cells. Nonetheless, it remains unclear how anionic hydrophilic cGAMP or CDNs permeate the cellular phospholipid bilayer. In recent years, several exporters and importers have been shown to mediate cGAMP transport across cell membranes. For example, cGAMP is recognized and forms a ligand‐bound structure with ATP‐binding cassette subfamily C member 1 (ABCC1), which mediates cGAMP export and restricts the cell‐intrinsic interferon response [94, 95]. Additionally, leucine‐rich repeat‐containing 8A (LRRC8A) heteromeric channels are widely expressed cGAMP transporters that mediate both the import and export of cGAMP [96, 97]. Human cathelicidin antimicrobial peptide LL‐37 also acts as a cGAMP transporter by binding to cGAMP and delivering it to target cells [98]. Also of note, the reduced folate carrier solute carrier family 19 member 1 (SLC19A1) was identified as a major importer of CDNs into target cells [99, 100]. Other importers, including P2X7 receptor (P2X7R) [101] and LRRC8A [96, 97], have also been identified. However, the expression profiles, structural features, and substrate specificities of the cGAMP transporters require further investigation.

In addition, ENPP1, ENPP3, and SMPDL3A degrade cGAMP and negatively regulate its cellular transport. Several negative regulators modulate the cleavage of cGAMP and its interactions with cGAMP transporters. Ecto‐nucleotide pyrophosphatase phosphodiesterase 1 (ENPP1) is an extensively studied cGAMP hydrolase, which is expressed on cancer cells to eliminate extracellular 2’3’‐cGAMP in the tumor microenvironment (TME) and inhibit STING‐mediated antitumor immune response [93, 102, 103, 104]. ENPP1 is also carried by tumor‐derived exosomes to hinder the infiltration of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells into the TME and block the interaction of cGAMP with the importer LL‐37 [105]. Recently, other cGAMP hydrolases have also been characterized, like sphingomyelin phosphodiesterase acid‐like 3A (SMPDL3A) [106] and ENPP3 [107]. It remains unknown whether there are hydrolases that cleave intracellular cGAS, or whether the enzymatic activity of cGAMP hydrolases can be stimulated under other distinct conditions.

Regulation of STING transport and trafficking

The transport, trafficking, and degradation of STING are tightly orchestrated to maintain immune homeostasis (Figure 4). STING translocation from the ER to the ERGIC is the rate‐limiting step for STING activation, which relies on the assembly of the ARF GTPases‐COPII complex‐STING transport complex [8, 108]. Several positive and negative regulators are involved in this process. For example, STING ER exit protein (STEEP) facilitates STING trafficking by stimulating phosphatidylinositol 3‐phosphate (PI3P) production and inducing ER membrane curvature [109]. Moreover, translocon‐associated protein beta (TRAPβ) serves as an essential positive regulator mediating STING translocation across the ER membrane [2, 110]. The COPII vesicle outer layer consists of SEC24 homolog, COPII coat complex component (SEC13) and SEC31, whereas SEC24, SEC23, and secretion‐associated Ras‐related GTPase 1 (SAR1) form the inner layer [8, 111, 112]. Notably, the Ca2+ sensor stromal interaction molecule 1 (STIM1) is a negative regulator of STING, retaining it in the ER and subsequently attenuating the IFN response [113]. The precise regulatory mechanisms of these cofactors in modulating STING trafficking warrant further investigation.

STING termination is mediated by the ubiquitin‐proteasome system, retrograde transport, and the autophagic endolysosomal system (Figure 4). STING can be recognized by E3 ubiquitin ligases, such as TRIM29 [114, 115] and TRIM13[116], and subsequently tagged with ubiquitin molecules, thereby facilitating its recognition by the proteasome and degradation into short peptide fragments. In the basal state, the SEL1L‐HRD1 protein complex, the most conserved branch of ER‐associated degradation, ubiquitinates and recruits nascent STING for proteasomal degradation [117]. In contrast, TOLLIP stabilizes STING and prevents its degradation through direct interaction [118]. Upon translocation to the Golgi apparatus and the activation of downstream molecules, STING signaling is terminated through recycling and lysosomal degradation. The retrograde transport of STING from the Golgi to the ER, which requires COPI and the adapter molecule surfeit locus protein 4 (Surf4) to form the STING/Surf4/COPI complex, is essential for the tightly control of IFN signaling [119, 120, 121, 122]. The GTPase ADP‐ribosylation factor 1 (ARF1) was also confirmed to facilitate COPI vesicle‐mediated the Golgi‐to‐ER retrieval of STING [123]. Lysosomal degradation refers to the process by which STING is translocated into lysosomes via the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT)‐mediated autophagy [124]. In this process, the ESCRT machinery recognizes ubiquitinated STING, encapsulates it into intraluminal vesicles, and facilitates its direct engulfment and degradation by the lysosomal membrane, thereby terminating immune signal transduction. In addition, some cofactors control the lysosomal degradation of STING. For instance, the C‐terminal domain of phosphorylated STING interacts with adaptor protein complex 1 (AP‐1), facilitating its incorporation into clathrin‐coated vesicles for lysosomal degradation [125]. This termination process requires additional components, including GCC2, RAB GTPases (e.g., RAB14), and the lysosomal protein Niemann‐Pick disease type C1 protein (NPC1) [124, 126, 127]. The spatiotemporal dynamics and intensity of STING signaling are regulated by complex mechanisms, with dysregulated activation being pathogenically linked to tumorigenesis and autoimmune diseases.

CELLULAR FUNCTIONS OF cGAS‐STING PATHWAY

Antimicrobial defense

Since its discovery, cGAS has been shown to indiscriminately recognize virus‐derived dsDNA and trigger interferon‐dependent antiviral immunity [3]. As research progresses, the antiviral spectrum of cGAS is not only limited to DNA viruses, but also to RNA viruses [128, 129] and fungal pathogens [130]. In addition, although STING‐IFN‐mediated antiviral immunity is initiated through S365 phosphorylation in the CTT of STING, mice harboring an S365A mutation in STING are still protected against herpes simplex virus‐1 (HSV‐1) infection, indicating the IFN‐independent activity of STING [131, 132]. Transcriptomic analysis revealed widespread IFN‐independent STING activity in macrophages, particularly T cells, which contribute to antiviral immunity [131]. However, the IFN‐independent mechanism of STING‐induced resistance to HSV‐1 remains unresolved, and it has been proposed that autophagy or an autophagy‐like process might be involved [132]. In addition, pathogens have developed various mechanisms to antagonize cGAS or STING activity. For example, the essential varicella zoster virus tegument protein, open reading frame 9 (ORF9), antagonizes cGAS activity to produce cGAMP [133]. A recent study illustrated that the immune evasion mechanism is highly conserved between animal and bacterial viruses, as animal poxvirus proteins that suppress cGAS‐STING activity exhibit structural homology with bacteriophage Acb1 proteins, providing an evolutionary basis for the immune evasion of viruses [134].

Autophagy and lysosome biogenesis

It has been widely acknowledged that the cGAS‐STING pathway induces IFN‐I production in a TBK1‐/IKK‐dependent manner to defend against foreign pathogens, while a growing body of evidence demonstrates that cGAS‐STING can also trigger noncanonical autophagy and promote lysosome biogenesis, thereby facilitating the clearance of cytoplasmic DNA and intracellular pathogens [8, 9]. These functions are regarded as ancestral roles of the cGAS‐STING pathway, which likely evolved prior to the emergence of IFN‐I signaling (Figure 5) [8, 9]. Upon activation by cGAMP, STING interacts with light chain 3 (LC3) and induces LC3 lipidation, a canonical step in autophagy [8, 135, 136, 137, 138]. Autophagy regulators, including WD repeat domain, phosphoinositide‐interacting protein 2 (WIPI2), autophagy related 5 (ATG5), vacuolar ATPase (V‐ATPase), and ATG16L1, are indispensable for STING‐induced LC3 lipidation and autophagosome formation [8, 136, 137, 138]. Mechanistically, STING competes with PI3P for binding to the FRRG motif of WIPI2, which recruits ATG16L1 and ATG5 to single‐membrane perinuclear vesicles and induces V‐ATPase‐dependent LC3 lipidation. STING‐induced autophagy is essential for the clearance of cytosolic DNA and viruses and the termination of IFN‐induced inflammatory responses [8, 137].

STING‐induced noncanonical autophagy and lysosome biogenesis. The cGAS‐STING‐mediated noncanonical autophagy involves regulators, including WIPI2, ATG5, V‐ATPase, and ATG16L1, which induce LC3 lipidation. STING also inhibits canonical autophagy through competing with PI3P for binding to WIPI2. STING has recently been shown to function as a proton channel. The increased proton flux and pH in the Golgi apparatus and acidic vesicles, followed by STING activation, are essential for LC3 lipidation. Also, the lipidation of the GABARAPs abrogates the inhibition of mTORC2 on the transcriptional activity of TFEB, TFE3, and MITF, thus promoting lysosomal biogenesis. The non‐canonical autophagy and lysosome biogenesis are essential for cell survival and host defense and considered the primordial function of the cGAS‐STING pathway, prior to the emergence of STING‐NF‐κB/IRF3‐IFN‐dependent functions in antimicrobial defense and immune surveillance (recently adapted functions). Recently, emerging evidence has revealed the critical role of STING as a proton channel [139, 140, 141, 142, 143, 144]. Increased proton flux and pH in the Golgi apparatus and liposomes following STING activation are essential for LC3 lipidation and inflammasome activation, triggering non‐canonical autophagy [139, 140]. Furthermore, proton channel activity is essential for STING‐induced lipidation of ATG8 proteins, especially gamma‐aminobutyric acid receptor‐associated proteins (GABARAPs), which abrogate the phosphorylation and inhibition of microphthalmia/transcription factor E (MiT/TFE) transcription factors (TFEB, TFE3, and MITF) by rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), thereby stimulating lysosome biogenesis [142, 143, 144]. In mammalian cells, TFEB and its paralogs serve as master regulators of lysosomal biogenesis, with their activity being tightly controlled by mTORC1 [9, 145, 146, 147, 148]. Under nutrient‐rich conditions, activated mTORC1 phosphorylates TFEB at the lysosomal surface and causes cytoplasmic retention of TFEB, whereas upon nutrient deprivation, mTORC1 inactivation leads to TFEB dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation [145, 146, 147]. Within the nucleus, TFEB drives lysosomal biogenesis by upregulating the expression of lysosome‐related genes [9, 148]. Functionally, enhanced lysosomal biogenesis promotes the clearance of cytoplasmic DNA and invading pathogens and plays an essential role in cellular antibacterial and antiviral innate immune defenses [143].

Cellular senescence

Cellular senescence is characterized by stable and irreversible cell cycle arrest and secretion of senescence‐associated secretory phenotypes (SASPs), which are essential for senescence‐induced growth arrest, tissue repair, and immune surveillance [149, 150, 151, 152]. The cGAS‐STING signaling pathway has been considered a critical participant and regulator of cellular senescence (Figure 6) [10, 77, 153, 154]. It was discovered that chromatin fragments released from intact nuclei in primary cells during senescence can be recognized by cGAS and trigger proinflammatory SASPs [10, 77, 153]. DNA damage [155], oxidative stress [156], replicative stress [157], oncogenic signals [158], and mitochondrial dysfunction [20, 159, 160] also drive the abnormal activation of cGAS‐STING and trigger cellular senescence. Retrotransposable elements (e.g., long interspersed nuclear element‐1, LINE‐1) [161, 162, 163] and endogenous retroviruses (e.g., human endogenous retrovirus K) [164] can become transcriptionally unlocked to transcribe RNA intermediates and are subsequently reverse transcribed to generate cDNAs, eliciting cGAS‐STING‐mediated SASPs in senescent cells. In DNA damage‐induced senescent cells, nuclear cGAS promotes tripartite motif‐containing protein 41 (TRIM41)‐mediated ubiquitination and degradation of open reading frame 2 protein (ORF2p), thereby inducing senescence‐associated repression of LINE‐1 retrotransposition to maintain genome stability [165]. Additionally, several protective mechanisms exist to sustain the cGAS sensing of cytoplasmic DNA and preserve the proinflammatory output of SASPs. Thioredoxin reductase 1 (TXNRD1), a cytoplasmic chromatin fragment‐localized protein, interacts with cGAS to positively regulate its enzymatic activity, thus driving SASP and inflammation [166]. In senescent cells, the expressions of DNases (both DNase II and TREX1) are significantly downregulated, and DNA fragments accumulate in the cytosol, thereby stimulating cGAS‐STING activation [167]. The activity of the transcriptional coactivators, Yes‐associated protein (YAP) and transcriptional co‐activator with PDZ‐binding motif (TAZ), is significantly suppressed during physiological aging in stromal cells because YAP/TAZ directly regulates lamin B1 and actin‐related protein 2 (ACTR2) transcription, thus maintaining nuclear envelope integrity and preventing the release of nuclear DNA fragments to be sensed by cGAS [168].

Some non‐canonical pathways are involved in the cGAS‐STING‐mediated induction of SASPs (Figure 6). For example, upon STING‐mediated activation during innate DNA sensing, IRF3 interacts with retinoblastoma (RB) in the nucleus to block CDK4/6‐mediated RB hyperphosphorylation and the activation of downstream early 2 factor (E2F) transcription factors, thus promoting hepatic stellate cells toward senescence [169, 170]. Also intriguingly, after receiving proinflammatory signals from cGAMP, STING activates protein kinase RNA‐like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK), which then mediates the phosphorylation of eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF2α) to establish an inflammatory‐ and survival‐preferred translation program that is essential for cellular senescence and organ fibrosis [171]. Furthermore, a recent study has also demonstrated that mtDNA released by senescent cells was transferred by extracellular vesicles to myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (PMN‐MDSCs), the immunosuppressive activity of which was subsequently enhanced via cGAS/STING/NF‐κB or cGAS/STING/PERK/NF‐κB signaling pathway [20]. This study highlights the crosstalk between cGAS‐STING‐driven cellular senescence and immune surveillance. Moreover, the distinct DNA sources and signaling pathways involved in cGAS‐STING‐mediated aging and senescence require further characterization.

Cell death

cGAS‐STING signaling orchestrates cell death, including apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and autophagy, through IFN‐dependent or ‐independent mechanisms, dynamically shaping cell fate during infection and injury.

Apoptosis

STING activation drives apoptosis in cancerous T cells and B cells [172, 173]. The direct interaction and co‐translocation of IRF3 and Bax to the mitochondria trigger the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway [174]. In contrast, cGAS‐STING activation is also a consequence of apoptosis, during which the mitochondria undergo outer membrane permeabilization, and BAK/BAX macropores allow the release of mtDNA, thus stimulating cGAS‐STING‐mediated innate immune signaling [175, 176] and SASP [159]. Apoptotic caspases, including caspase‐3 (human) and caspase‐7 (mouse), play negative roles in mtDNA‐triggered immune responses by mediating the cleavage of cGAS, STING, and IRF3 [175, 177].

Necroptosis

Necroptosis is initiated by the receptor‐interacting protein kinase 1 (RIPK1)‐RIPK3 mixed lineage kinase domain‐like protein (MLKL) axis to induce inflammatory cell death [178, 179]. cGAS‐STING‐mediated IFN and TNF drive cell necroptosis by promoting MLKL expression and oligomerization [180, 181, 182, 183]. cGAS activation suppresses oncogenic proliferation by inducing Z‐DNA binding protein 1 (ZBP1)/RIPK3/MLKL‐mediated necroptosis [184]. PERK/STING/RIPK3 axis induces necroptosis and cognitive dysfunction [185]. Conversely, RIP3/MLKL‐induced necroptosis promotes the transcription of the proapoptotic factor PUMA, which stimulates the release of mtDNA and triggers activation of the cGAS‐STING pathway [186]. This evidence supports a positive feedback loop between cGAS‐STING signaling and necroptosis.

Pyroptosis

There is also a feedback loop between the cGAS‐STING pathway and pyroptosis [187]. On one hand, cGAS‐STING activates nucleotide‐binding oligomerization domain, leucine‐rich repeat‐, and pyrin domain‐containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome [188, 189, 190, 191, 192] and absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2) inflammasome [193, 194, 195, 196] in response to viral infection and mitochondrial oxidative stress, resulting in robust interferon response and immune activation [192]. In contrast, the key components of pyroptosis are the positive or negative regulators that influence the activity of the cGAS‐STING pathway. For example, gasdermin‐D (GSDMD) and GSDME both facilitate the release of mtDNA and trigger cGAS‐STING activation [197, 198]. Deficiency of NLRP3 [199, 200] or AIM2 [201, 202] activates the STING‐type I IFN signaling pathway, indicating the negative role of these inflammasomes in regulating cGAS‐STING activity.

Ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is a distinct form of cell death characterized by iron reliance and lipid peroxidation [203, 204, 205]. The activation of STING signaling directly or indirectly promotes ferroptosis by regulating ferroptosis‐related factors, including glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4), and solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), ultimately resulting in aggravated tissue damage [206, 207, 208]. In addition, chemotherapy‐induced mitochondrial stress activates the STING‐mediated DNA sensing pathway, subsequently promoting lipid peroxidation and ferroptotic cell death by bypassing the interferon response [209]. Intriguingly, deficiency of GPX4, a critical enzyme that antagonizes lipid peroxidation and sustains redox homeostasis, inhibits cGAS‐STING activation by promoting STING carbonylation at C88 [210].

THE DUAL ROLES OF cGAS‐STING SIGNALING IN CANCER

During the malignant transformation of cells, errors in chromosome segregation [211] and alterations in the DNA damage response, such as defective mismatch repair [74, 212, 213, 214], activate cGAS‐STING in the TME. The role of cGAS‐STING signaling in tumorigenesis and progression is extremely complicated and is partially discussed below.

The antitumor role of cGAS‐STING signaling in the TME

The cGAS‐STING pathway regulates immune cell differentiation, infiltration, and function while also influencing cancer cell death and metabolism (Figure 7).

Myeloid cells

STING in myeloid cells predominantly drives IFN‐dependent signaling, which has been extensively studied. As the most potent antigen‐presenting cells (APCs) in tumor immunotherapy, dendritic cells (DCs) contribute to the capture, processing, and presentation of tumor antigens to activate CD8+ T‐cell responses [215]. The recruitment and functionality of DCs depend on both cell‐autonomous and microenvironmental cGAS‐STING signaling following the engulfment of DNA‐damaged cells [216, 217, 218]. On one hand, the accumulation of cytosolic dsDNA in cancer cells leads to the activation of the cGAS‐STING inflammatory cascade, which is responsible for the intratumoral infiltration and antigen presentation of DCs [219]. On the other hand, dsDNA from cancer cells is transferred to the cytoplasm of basic leucine zipper ATF‐like transcription factor 3 (BATF3)‐dependent DCs through tumor‐derived exosomes, which then activate STING‐dependent IFN production and upregulate costimulatory molecules in DCs, ultimately stimulating CD8+ T cell responses [220, 221, 222]. In regressing tumors, the activation state of CD11b+ conventional DCs with the expression of ISGs and presentation of MHC class I complexes has been observed [223].

Tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs) are categorized into two subsets: proinflammatory M1 and anti‐inflammatory M2 TAMs [224, 225]. STING activation in the TME was confirmed to promote TAM polarization into M1‐like proinflammatory macrophages as well as to optimize phagocytic activity and apoptosis‐inducing effects [226, 227, 228, 229]. In addition, activation of the cGAS‐STING pathway can drive the conversion of M2 to M1 TAMs [230, 231] and inhibit M2 TAM polarization [228]. Also of note, the STING‐dependent induction of IL‐18 and IL‐1β in macrophages also promoted the antitumor effect of NK cells through the upregulation of costimulatory signals 4‐1BB ligand/4‐1BB [232].

Studies indicate that neutrophils exhibit negligible STING expression, which may serve as a protective mechanism against excessive immune activation [233]. Using a mouse model with neutrophil‐specific expression of the STING gain‐of‐function mutant N153S, researchers have demonstrated that STING overexpression triggers IFN‐independent systemic inflammation, marked by significantly elevated serum levels of inflammatory factors, including granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF), chemokine C‐C motif ligands 4 (CCL4), and CCL5 (RANTES) [233]. Notably, interferon‐induced Ly6E‐high neutrophils have emerged as a circulating biomarker for anti‐PD‐1 therapy response in mice. These neutrophils are induced by activation of the tumor‐intrinsic STING pathway and possess the capacity to sensitize therapy‐resistant tumors to anti‐PD‐1 treatment [234].

As distinct immune cells that exert immunosuppressive effects, myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) play an essential role in tumor progression and metastasis [235, 236]. STING signaling has been identified as a negative regulator of MDSC differentiation via the suppressors of cytokine signaling 1 (SOCS1)‐mediated inhibition of granulocyte‐macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (GM‐CSF)/IL‐6 production [237]. Treatment with low doses of c‐di‐GMP increases MDSC secretion of IL‐12, which significantly improves T cell responses to cancer vaccines [238]. STING deletion in CD11b+ brain‐infiltrating leukocytes significantly promoted the induction of MDSCs while decreasing IFN‐γ‐producing CD8+ T cells, thus decreasing the survival rate of glioma‐bearing StingGt/Gt mice [239].

Lymphocytes

Spontaneous T cell responses are crucial for tumor regression, and intrinsic cGAS‐STING activation in T cells is essential for their functionality [240, 241]. Reportedly, STING agonism augmented stem cell‐like CD8+ T cell differentiation, as well as enhanced the differentiation and antitumor role of T helper 1 (Th1) and Th9 cells by stimulating the secretion of IFN‐γ (via STING/IRF3 signaling) and IL‐9 (via STING/ rapamycin signaling), respectively [242, 243]. Loss of intrinsic STING signaling in CD8⁺ T cells inhibits their functional capacity and memory differentiation by inducing excessive hexokinase 2 (HK2)‐mediated glycolysis, causing lactate accumulation that suppresses IFN‐γ production and impairs immunotherapy efficacy [244]. Tumor‐intrinsic STING expression is closely associated with T cell exhaustion [233]. Notably, this phenomenon is clinically relevant in patients with colorectal cancer; STING expression is downregulated in tumor‐infiltrating CD8+ T cells, and the STING‐low population exhibits significant upregulation of exhaustion markers, including PD‐1, T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain containing‐3, lymphocyte activation gene 3, and thymocyte selection‐associated high mobility group box [233]. Nevertheless, the intrinsic STING of CD8+ T cells is reportedly correlated with proapoptotic and antiproliferative gene signatures, and cancer cell‐derived cGAMP limits the proliferation, survival, and function of STING‐expressing CD8+ T cells [245]. However, the physiological function of STING in T cells remains poorly understood.

To address the problem that tumor cells may evade CD8+ T cell recognition owing to the deficiency of MHC molecules or neoantigens, NK cells can mediate tumor rejection in CD8+ T cell‐resistant cancers [246]. CDN treatment‐induced IFN production activates NK cells and promotes their cytotoxic activity through direct interactions or induction of IL‐15 receptors on DCs [246]. Tumor‐derived cGAMP, which is transferred to non‐tumor cells, is essential for NK cell‐mediated killing of tumor cells [247]. Additionally, intrinsic STING signaling, which is partially triggered by tumor mtDNA recognition, maintains a reservoir of TCF‐1+NK cells [248].

cGAS‐STING signaling is also involved in regulatory T cells (Tregs)‐cell‐mediated immunosuppression. In lung cancer, Tregs establish an immunosuppressive microenvironment by inhibiting STING signaling, leading to reduced CD4+ T‐cell infiltration and enhanced metastatic progression. STING inhibition promotes GATA binding protein 3/ nitric oxide synthase 2‐mediated immunosuppression and contributes to CD8+ T cell exhaustion [249]. Similarly, in multiple myeloma, Treg‐derived TGF‐β1 directly suppresses the cGAS‐STING pathway in tumor cells, which results in downregulation of MHC class I molecules and upregulation of programmed death‐ligand 1 (PD‐L1), ultimately facilitating immune evasion [250]. These findings highlighted the central role of STING in regulating the immunosuppressive function of Tregs.

Tumor cells

During abnormal mitosis and proliferation of tumor cells, cytoplasmic fragments, and micronuclei trigger cGAS‐STING‐mediated cellular senescence and tumor regression [10, 74, 251]. The DNA double‐strand break sensor, meiotic recombination 11 protein, rescues nuclear cGAS from sequestered chromatin, and activated cGAS promotes ZBP1/RIPK3/MLKL‐mediated necroptosis of tumor cells and subsequently inhibits oncogenic proliferation [184]. The above evidence suggests that the regulation of cell death pathways is involved in the suppressive effect of cGAS‐STING in cancer cells. Independent of the IRF3/IFN‐triggered innate immune function, STING directly targets and inhibits HK2 activity to restrict tumor aerobic glycolysis, thereby functioning as a cell‐intrinsic metabolic checkpoint to promote antitumor immunity [252]. These results established an important link between the intrinsic tumor‐suppressive activity of STING and cellular metabolism.

Tumor angiogenesis

Tumor angiogenesis is indispensable for tumor growth and progression [253]. TET‐2 mediates the epigenetic upregulation of cGAS in tumor cells and produces cGAMP, which activates STING signaling in endothelial cells, promotes vascular normalization, and enhances the transendothelial migration of lymphocytes [254]. Reportedly, intraperitoneal administration of the STING agonist ADU‐S100 significantly suppressed aberrant angiogenesis and normalized tumor vessels, which recovered effector T cell function in a type‐I interferon‐dependent manner [255]. Evidence indicates that the cGAS‐STING pathway suppresses tumor angiogenesis while activating immune responses.

Pro‐tumorigenic and pro‐metastatic role of cGAS‐STING signaling

The influence of chromosomal instability (CIN)‐induced cGAS‐STING signaling on tumorigenesis and progression is highly correlated with the signal intensity, TME context, and complex regulatory mechanisms of this pathway. Acute interferon signaling exerts antitumor effects by promoting the activation of T and NK cells by APCs (Figure 8A) [256, 257, 258, 259]. Conversely, chronic cGAS‐STING activation mediates downstream signal reprogramming, induces the expression of immunosuppressive factors, and initiates a series of adaptive immune evasion mechanisms, ultimately promoting tumor immunosuppression, survival, invasion, and metastasis (Figure 8B) [18, 260].

Rewiring of downstream signaling

The dualistic effects of STING on tumorigenesis may stem from its differential signaling patterns. Evidence suggested that the TBK1‐independent non‐canonical NF‐κB pathway is an essential mediator of tumorigenesis and metastasis. In CIN‐positive malignancies, disruption of cGAS‐STING signaling preferentially compromises tumor cell viability [260]. Mechanistically, CIN augments IL‐6/STAT3 pro‐survival signaling via non‐canonical NF‐κB activation [260]. Continuous chromosome segregation errors activate the cGAS‐STING pathway and downstream TBK1‐independent non‐canonical NF‐κB signaling, which drives tumor metastasis [211]. Additionally, chronic STING stimulation induces a transition from proinflammatory interferon signaling to the ER stress pathway [17, 18]. Reportedly, chronic STING stimulation via STING agonists significantly weakened interferon response (called “interferon tachyphylaxis”), while the ER stress signals were activated, leading to an immunosuppressive TME (enriched with M2 TAMs, dysfunctional T cells, and MDSCs) and promoting cancer cell invasion and metastasis [18]. The depletion of ER stress sensors, including inositol‐requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1), PERK, or activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6), significantly inhibited tumor metastasis in chromosomally unstable tumors [18].

Induction of immunosuppression

Low levels of chronic type I interferon can lead to the upregulation of immunosuppressive factors, such as immune checkpoints and indoleamine 2,3’‐dioxygenase (IDO; catabolizes tryptophan to kynurenine), which dampens immune responses and promotes cancer cell immune escape [261, 262]. After STING activation, cell‐intrinsic PD‐L1 expression is significantly upregulated within tumor monocytes, which acts as a major barrier to treatment resistance to STING agonists [19]. Pretreatment with toll‐like receptor 2 (TLR2) promotes the interaction between TNF receptor associated factor 6 and STING, inhibits IFN‐I signaling, and reduces PD‐L1 expression [19]. Notably, chromosomally unstable tumors also develop resistance mechanisms via upregulating ENPP1 expression, which catalyzes cGAMP to GMP and AMP [93, 263]. Furthermore, AMP can be hydrolyzed by NT5E (also known as CD73) into the immunosuppressive metabolite adenosine, which promotes tumor progression [263].

Notably, the cGAS‐STING pathway is involved in immune evasion in TME. It was recently uncovered that mtDNA released from senescent cells via extracellular vesicles was selectively sensed by polymorphonuclear MDSCs in the TME, thus stimulating its immunosuppressive activity through cGAS/STING/NF‐κB signaling and promoting tumor progression [20]. Cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs) also act as immunosuppressive cells that influence the antitumor treatment response of patients with cancer [264, 265, 266]. Activation of cGAS/STING/IFN signaling in tumor cells drives SLC14A1+ CAF differentiation [267]. In addition, cisplatin promotes DNA transfer from tumor cells to CAFs, inducing the IFNβ1‐secretory phenotype in CAFs, which modulates resistance to chemotherapy [268]. Additionally, the STING/IRF3/IL‐35 axis in regulatory B cells significantly suppresses NK cell proliferation and induces resistance to SING agonist monotherapy [22]. Moreover, in breast cancer susceptibility gene 1 (BRCA1)‐mutated ovarian cancer, BRCA1 deficiency causes the transcriptional reprogramming of STING and IFN, which mediates the upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor A, thereby mediating immune resistance and tumor progression [269].

FiRST‐GENERATION OF STING agonists and challenges

CDNs

Natural CDNs produced by malignant cells or bacteria, such as cGAMP, c‐diGMP, and c‐diAMP, can traverse the cell membrane through the reduced folate carrier, SLC19A1, and activate STING in the absence of cGAS [99]. Nevertheless, the poor metabolic stability and low delivery efficacy of natural CDNs limit their application and have promoted the development of CDN‐based STING synthetic agonists (Table 3) [270, 271]. ADU‐S100 (MIW815) was the first CDN STING agonist to enter clinical trials with higher STING binding affinity and stronger IFN‐I‐inducing effect [272]. However, the clinical outcome of ADU‐S100 has been disappointing and its clinical development was terminated [21, 273]. According to a phase I dose‐escalation trial of ADU‐S100, only one out of 47 patients with advanced/metastatic cancer treated with monotherapy achieved a confirmed partial response [21]. The limited efficacy may be attributed to several factors highlighted in that study. First, due to their poor membrane permeability, ADU‐S100 as well as other CDNs (such as IMSA‐101, MK‐1454, MK‐2118, BI‐1387446, and E7766) require intratumoral (IT) administration for effective delivery. However, plasma pharmacokinetic analyses demonstrated that IT‐administered ADU‐S100 was rapidly absorbed from the injection site into systemic circulation and exhibited a short half‐life. In addition, factors such as intratumoral pressure and injection technique may lead to uneven drug distribution, potentially compromising the therapeutic effects. Furthermore, due to the multiple target lesions and heterogeneity of patients, the systemic immune response may be limited [21].

Non‐CDN small agonists

Various non‐CDN small agonists have been designed to overcome the poor membrane permeability of CDNs. An amidobenzimidazole‐based compound (diABZI) was the first non‐CDN small agonist of STING proposed in 2018 [274]. Synthetic small‐molecule STING agonists such as MSA‐2 [275] and SR‐717 [276] also show promising antitumor effects in preclinical models. However, poor oral bioavailability, latent off‐target distribution, systemic toxicity, and suboptimal delivery to immune‐rich sites such as lymph nodes restrict their clinical application [16]. Other novel small‐molecule STING agonists, including SNX281, GSK3745417, ONO‐7914, HG‐381, and KL340399, have also been reported; however, their efficacy has yet to be studied in clinical trials (Table 4).

Next Generation of STING modulators for cancer therapy

The conventional STING agonists face challenges including rapid systemic clearance, systemic toxicity, restricted administration routes, suboptimal therapeutic efficacy, emerging resistance mechanisms, and tumor heterogeneity, which restrict their clinical application [16, 21, 22]. Efforts should be made to optimize pharmacokinetics, mitigate toxicity, improve delivery routes, and investigate alternative combinations and biomarker‐guided therapeutic strategies. Novel STING agonists (such as nanomaterials, ADCs, and engineered bacteria) as well as alternative strategies, are currently under clinical evaluation (Table 4).

Nanomaterials

In recent years, the emergence of nanomaterials has profoundly revolutionized STING agonists delivery, such as lipid nanoparticles [277, 278, 279], polymeric nanocarriers [280], metal‐organic frameworks [281, 282, 283, 284], mesoporous silicon nanoparticles [285, 286], exosomes [287], hydrogels [288, 289, 290], nanogels [291], scaffolds [290, 292], and ferritin nanoparticles [293, 294]. Modification of STING agonists via precise drug delivery systems and multifunctional platforms significantly improves their water solubility, extends their half‐life, achieves remarkable region‐specific biodistribution, and maximizes therapeutic effects while minimizing adverse effects [295]. For example, a novel “shock‐and‐lock” polymeric STING‐activating nanoparticle (PolySTING) encapsulates cGAMP in a micellar matrix composed of the polymer PSC7A, ensuring specific identification by conventional type 1 dendritic cells (cDC1s) over other immune cells to mount a robust antitumor immunity and avoid STING‐mediated T cell death [296]. In addition, a polymeric nanoplatform with resiquimod (R848) and cGAMP co‐encapsulation (named M‐PNP@R@C) promotes the conversion of M2 TAMs to proinflammatory M1 TAMs and enhanced antitumor immunity [297]. The design of STING agonist‐based nanotechnologies capable of specifically activating the STING signaling pathway in DCs or TAMs effectively mitigated systemic toxicity, providing valuable guidance for future drug development.

ExoSTING is an engineered extracellular vesicle‐based STING agonist that integrates CDN into the lumen, facilitating the activation of APCs within the TME and generating systemic antitumor immunity [298]. According to phase I/II data, intratumoral injection of ExoSTING in advanced solid tumors showed tumor contraction in injected tumors as well as in distal non‐injected tumors, and no adverse reactions of DLT and grade 3 or above were observed (NCT04592484). A related nanoagonist (ONM‐501) is being evaluated in a phase I clinical trial to determine its safety and therapeutic efficacy alone or in combination with cemiplimab for tumors (NCT06022029).

Antibody‐drug conjugates

STING is ubiquitously expressed in both tumors and normal tissues; therefore, the systemic administration of STING agonists may induce cytokine storms. The development of novel drug delivery systems or ADCs based on STING agonists may prevent corresponding adverse reactions. TAK‐500 is an ADC that specifically targets STING and CCR2 and facilitates the recruitment and activation of CCR2+ myeloid cells within the TME [299]. However, a phase I/II clinical trial evaluating the efficacy of TAK‐500, with or without pembrolizumab, in locally advanced or metastatic solid tumors was terminated due to clinical futility (NCT05070247). Additionally, XMT‐2056, a HER2‐directed STING ADC, induced tumor regression in various tumor models and showed combinatorial effects with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [300]. XMT‐2056 is currently in a phase I study that has enrolled patients with advanced or relapsed HER2‐expressing solid tumors (NCT05514717). ADCs show potential for achieving precision therapy and may represent a key area for the future development of STING agonists.

Engineered bacteria

Synthetic biology is an efficient tool in drug design. SYNB1891, developed from non‐pathogenic Escherichia coli Nissle to produce CDNs under hypoxia, promotes STING activation in phagocytic ADCs and elicits robust antitumor immunity in murine models [301]. A phase I clinical trial (NCT04167137) evaluating the safety and efficacy of SYNB1891 alone or in combination with atezolizumab, in patients with metastatic solid tumors or lymphomas, demonstrated that SYNB1891 was well tolerated and showed evidence of STING pathway target engagement [302]. Bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) have garnered interest as cancer vaccines that potently stimulate immune responses [303]. Anchoring OMVs with ferrous ions loaded with STING agonists elicited promising antitumor responses in a murine model of colon cancer [304]. Nevertheless, concerns of low yield, toxicity, and unclear mechanisms of action of bacterial materials need to be resolved.

Alternative strategies

Given the clinical challenges associated with direct STING agonists, alternative strategies that efficiently and safely target the STING pathway may represent promising therapeutic breakthroughs. For instance, ENPP1 antagonists have emerged as a promising next‐generation STING modulator [305, 306]. As an ectoenzyme that degrades extracellular cGAMP, ENPP1 suppresses STING activation in the TME, and its overexpression correlates with a poor response to immunotherapy [93, 102, 103, 104]. Unlike direct STING agonists, ENPP1 inhibitors achieve in situ activation of the STING pathway by elevating extracellular cGAMP concentrations, specifically within the TME [305]. This spatially restricted action minimizes the systemic toxicity and offers a significantly improved safety profile [305]. Mechanistically, ENPP1 inhibition preserves extracellular cGAMP, which is preferentially taken up by APCs, and triggers efficient type I IFN production [305]. Furthermore, as ENPP1 is frequently overexpressed across diverse solid tumors, targeting ENPP1 represents a broader strategy for potentiating antitumor immunity [305]. To date, four candidate oral ENPP1 inhibitors (ISM5939, RBS2418, TXN10128, and SR‐8541A) have been tested in clinical trials [305]. The development of ENPP1 antagonists has provided new insights into the development of indirect STING modulators, such as ENPP3 and DNase antagonists.

Optogenetic approaches

Owing to the systemic toxicity and spatiotemporal control limitations of conventional STING agonists, optogenetic tools offer a promising approach for precise modulation of the cGAS‐STING signaling pathway [307]. For instance, light‐inducible SMOC‐like repeats (LiSmore) are light‐sensitive optogenetic devices consisting of a tandem STING CTT (amino acids 340‐379) fused with the photosensitive protein CRY2clust. Upon blue light illumination, CRY2clust forms clusters, driving STING‐CTT aggregation and subsequently inducing DC maturation, ultimately activating cytotoxic T lymphocytes [308]. Similarly, OptoSTING employs a nanobody‐CRY2 fusion system, enabling light‐inducible clustering of STING [309]. Notably, OptoSTING is the first tool capable of precisely controlling STING aggregation. Mechanistically, STING clustering is strictly dependent on ER exit, as only the cytosolic fragment (cyt‐OptoSTING) can successfully trigger type I interferon responses [309]. Structural investigations have demonstrated that upon binding to its ligand, cGAMP, STING undergoes conformational changes that induce ER membrane curvature, thereby facilitating ER escape and subsequent downstream signaling activation [310]. These structural findings provide novel insights for the rational design of next‐generation STING agonists.

Combination strategies

The limited efficacy of STING agonist monotherapy in clinical trials reflects profound tumor resistance, underscoring the need for rational combination strategies. The combination of STING agonists with ICIs shows encouraging results in preclinical models [311, 312]. For example, the combination of an anti‐TGF‐β/PD‐L1 bispecific antibody (YM101) with oral STING agonist MSA‐2 synergistically amplified immune response in multiple murine tumor models [311]. Nevertheless, the combination of the STING agonist ADU‐S100 and spartalizumab showed minimal antitumor responses in patients with advanced/metastatic solid tumors or lymphomas, with an overall response rate of 10.4% [273]. Mechanistically, it was recently discovered that STING agonists activate and expand PD‐L1‐high monocytes in the TME through the IRF3‐IFN‐I signaling axis, thereby suppressing immune responses and compromising the therapeutic efficacy of STING agonists [19]. Intriguingly, the PD‐L1 blockade failed to counteract this effect, while pretreatment with TLR2 agonist significantly improved the antitumor efficacy of STING agonists, as TLR2 activation reprogrammed STING signaling to enhance NF‐κB activation and reduce PD‐L1 expression in monocytes [19]. Additionally, the STING‐IL‐35 axis in regulatory B cells was correlated with tumor resistance to STING monotherapy by attenuating the NK‐mediated antitumor response, and the combination of STING agonists with anti‐IL‐35 blockade therapy significantly promoted tumor elimination in preclinical tumor models [22]. Notably, recent studies have increasingly focused on combinatorial approaches that integrate STING agonists with adoptive cell therapies (e.g., CAR‐transduced cell therapies [313, 314, 315, 316, 317]), which prevent the outgrowth of Ag‐loss tumor variants and elicit superior antitumor immune responses.

The combination of traditional chemotherapy and radiotherapy with STING agonists also exhibited improved treatment efficacy [318, 319, 320, 321, 322]. Chemotherapeutic agents can induce DNA damage‐mediated accumulation of cytoplasmic DNA, thereby activating the STING‐IFN response [323, 324]. Harnessing STING‐mediated antitumor immunity to bridge chemotherapy‐induced DNA damage is a promising strategy for chemosensitization. For instance, the recently identified STING agonist, carvedilol, synergizes with etoposide to promote a STING‐dependent type I IFN response and remodels the TME by augmenting NK cell infiltration, thereby potentiating chemotherapeutic efficacy [318]. Additionally, the proinflammatory effects of STING demonstrate its therapeutic potential in counteracting the immunosuppressive effects of radiotherapy, monotherapy, and overcoming radioresistance [320, 321]. Reportedly, a distinct subset of CAFs characterized by high interferon regulatory factor‐1 (IRF1) expression, termed inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), is enriched in various radiotherapy‐sensitive solid tumors [320]. Activation of the STING‐IFN‐γ signaling pathway in iCAFs drives its polarization and promotes the secretion of CCL4 and CCL5, thus enhancing radiotherapeutic efficacy [320].

Patient heterogeneity and precision therapy

Inherent genetic diversity of the STING pathway may influence the efficacy of STING agonists. Analyzed from single nucleotide polymorphism data, STING exhibits significant population‐level heterogeneity, with five major variants (R232, HAQ, R232H, AQ, and R293Q) showing distinct geographical distributions and differential responsiveness to STING agonists [325]. The development of pan‐spectrum STING agonists like c[G(2′,5′)U(2′,5′)], which show pan‐variant activity, offers a promising prospect to overcome this heterogeneity [326].

Biomarker‐guided therapy is also an essential approach for improving the responsiveness of patients to immunotherapy, particularly STING‐targeted therapies. An XCR1+STING+CXCL9+ signature in cDC1s, in which STING activation drives transcriptional programs associated with improved survival, suggests that these cells are putative biomarkers for immunotherapy stratification [296]. Future studies should aim to identify additional predictive biomarkers of treatment responses.

CONCLUSIONS AND PERSPECTIVES

The last decade has witnessed great progress in uncovering the regulatory mechanisms, biological functions, and targeted therapies of the cGAS‐STING signaling pathway. However, there are also other unresolved problems: (1) the complex regulatory mechanisms of cGAS‐STING require further exploration; (2) since more cellular functions of cGAS‐STING have been uncovered, including the recently identified role of STING as a proton channel to regulate lysosomal biogenesis, autophagy, and Golgi functions [139, 141, 144], other uncharacterized roles of cGAS and STING require further clarification; and (3) how to improve the clinical efficiency of cGAS‐STING modulators. Although STING agonists exhibited promising antitumor effects in preclinical murine models, these drugs failed to elicit effective responses in patients with cancer either as single agents or in combination with PD‐(L)1 inhibitor, according to clinical studies published to date [21, 273]. These disappointing outcomes may be attributed to the short half‐life, systemic toxicity, restricted administration routes, suboptimal therapeutic efficacy, emerging resistance mechanisms, and tumor heterogeneity [15, 20, 21]. In the future, nanomaterials, ADCs, engineered bacterial alternatives, alternative strategies (e.g., ENPP1 antagonists), combination strategies, and biomarker‐guided precision therapies may revolutionize the development of cancer immunotherapy.
