Inflammatory biomarkers are associated with renal injury and partially explain the obesity-renal injury relationship in U.S. adolescents: a cross-sectional NHANES study
Authors: Yanchao Li, Bi Wang, Yiming Yang, Dan Chen, Xiaoshan Shao, Xiaoyan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity is increasingly prevalent among adolescents and is implicated in renal injury yet its cross-sectional association with renal injury remains paradoxical. Systemic inflammatory biomarkers—including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SIRI)—may be involved in this paradoxical association. Their potential role as mediators of obesity-related renal dysfunction requires longitudinal investigation.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of 5,869 adolescents aged 12–20 years from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2009–2018 cycles. Renal injury was defined as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² or urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) ≥ 30 mg/g. Obesity was defined as BMI ≥ 95th percentile for age and sex. Using survey-weighted logistic regression and causal mediation analysis with 1,000 bootstrap replications, we assessed the associations of obesity and inflammatory biomarkers with renal injury and evaluated the mediating roles of NLR, PLR, and SIRI.ResultsAmong an estimated 32.98 million U.S. adolescents, 12.43% had renal injury. Obesity was inversely associated with renal injury (fully adjusted OR = 0.356; 95% CI: 0.231–0.548). NLR (OR = 1.190; 95% CI: 1.100–1.290) and SIRI (OR = 1.180; 95% CI: 1.060–1.310) were independently and positively associated with renal injury, whereas PLR showed no significant association. Mediation analysis revealed that NLR and SIRI showed statistical mediation of the obesity–renal injury association, accounting for 14.82% and 17.53% of the total effect, though effect sizes were modest, respectively (p < 0.001); PLR exhibited no mediating effect (p = 0.460). Associations were consistent across subgroups by sex, age, race, poverty status, vitamin D level, and sedentary behavior.ConclusionThe inverse obesity–renal injury association likely reflects hyperfiltration masking rather than true protection. Only NLR and SIRI showed significant associations with renal function indicators; PLR showed no significant association. These markers may aid risk stratification among adolescents with identified renal abnormalities, though they are not recommended as standalone screening tools.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Adolescent, Obesity, Renal injury, Inflammatory biomarkers, Mediation analysis

Introduction

Childhood obesity is recognized as one of the most significant public health challenges worldwide [1]. Children who are obese often continue to experience obesity into adulthood, which increases their risk of various adverse clinical outcomes, including kidney disease [2, 3]. Obesity is a central component of metabolic syndrome, characterized by a combination of cardiovascular risk factors such as central obesity, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and hyperglycemia [4]. There is also a link between obesity and the occurrence and progression of kidney disease, and individuals with higher BMI are at greater risk of developing proteinuria [5–7]. Furthermore, the incidence of CKD among the pediatric population has been steadily increasing [8]. There is a strong correlation between obesity and both the onset and progression of CKD [4]. Obesity-related glomerulopathy is characterized by glomerular hypertrophy, hyperfiltration, and subsequent proteinuria, representing a distinct pathway that connects adipose accumulation to progressive renal dysfunction [9, 10]. However, early detection of renal injury in this population remains challenging. Conventional indicators, such as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), may appear normal or even elevated due to compensatory hyperfiltration, thereby obscuring underlying structural damage [9].

In the early stages of kidney injury, the Urinary Albumin-to-Creatinine Ratio (UACR) and eGFR serve as complementary indicators—UACR reflecting glomerular barrier dysfunction and eGFR estimating filtration capacity [11, 12]. In the initial phases of obesity-related kidney disease, especially during adolescence, the sensitivity of these indicators may be restricted. Consequently, there is an urgent necessity to develop novel biomarkers that are easily accessible and low-cost, which can accurately reflect the pathophysiological processes occurring prior to the onset of overt proteinuria or a decline in GFR.

Growing evidence suggests that chronic low-grade inflammation is the key mechanism linking obesity to end-organ damage [13, 14]. In the obese state, adipose tissue secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines that activate systemic immune responses, subsequently leading to endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and glomerular injury [15, 16]. Previous research has established a link between chronic, low-grade inflammation and the development of obesity-related complications, including renal damage [17]. Hematological inflammatory indices obtained from routine complete blood counts, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SIRI), have emerged as practical surrogate markers for systemic inflammation due to their accessibility and ease of measurement. Despite these advances, several gaps remain in our understanding of the relationship between childhood obesity, renal injury, and inflammatory markers.

The mechanisms linking systemic inflammation to renal injury in obese children warrant further investigation. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the association of inflammatory biomarkers with obesity, and renal injury in adolescents, utilizing data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).

Materials and methods

Study population and design

This study utilizes data from the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) and employs a comprehensive cross-sectional design to investigate the relationship between inflammatory biomarkers (NLR, PLR, SIRI) and obesity, as well as renal injury, among adolescents in the United States. Additionally, it aims to evaluate whether these inflammatory biomarkers statistically mediate the association between obesity and renal injury through a complex multi-stage sampling design. The examination of interactions that affect the risk of renal injury in adolescents enhances the generalizability of the findings to a broad segment of the non-institutionalized civilian population aged 12 to 20 years. The NHANES cycles from 2009 to 2018 are specifically designed to collect nutrition and health-related data from non-institutionalized civilians across the United States [18, 19]. The NHANES study received approval from the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/irba98.htm). Specifically, written consent was acquired from all participants aged 12 years and older in the NHANES Basic Survey. Furthermore, all NHANES data released by NCHS have been de-identified to ensure anonymity during data analysis.

This analysis combined data from two NHANES cycles (2009–2010, 2011–2012, 2013–2014, 2015–2016 and 2017–2018), following NCHS recommendations, initially including 49,693 individuals (Fig. 1). The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) age under 12 years or over 20 years (42,640 cases excluded, n = 7,053 remaining), (2) missing data, for example N, BMI, UACR and serum creatinine (1,184 cases excluded, n = 5,869 remaining). The initial dataset included 5,869 participants.

Inflammatory markers

Lymphocyte, neutrophil, monocyte, and platelet counts were performed using a fully automated blood analyzer (Kurt® Dx H 800 analyzer) for complete blood counts. The NHANES Laboratory Procedures Manual (LPM) offers standardized protocols for the measurement of these biomarkers and discusses potential biases associated with these measurements. For further details, please visit [CDC NHANES Biospecimens] (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/biospecimens/serum_plasma_urine.htm).This study used SIRI, PLR, and NLR as mediating variables, where SII was counted by platelets × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count is obtained, and SIRI is obtained from monocyte count × neutrophil count/lymphocyte count [20]. PLR is derived from platelet count/lymphocyte count [21]. NLR is derived from neutrophil count/lymphocyte count [21].

Renal injury

The definition of kidney damage was an eGFR less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m² or a urine protein-to-creatinine ratio greater than or equal to 30 mg/g. Below are the steps and calculations we used to obtain eGFR and UACR:

The method for obtaining serum creatinine involves the use of the Jaffe rate method (kinetic alkaline picrate) on the DxC800 modular chemistry analyzer to measure creatinine concentrations in serum, plasma, or urine. This calibration of creatinine is traceable to an isotope dilution mass spectrometry (IDMS) reference method, ensuring that the method employed on the DxC800 is IDMS standardized. The body measures data were collected, in the Mobile Examination Center (MEC), by trained health technicians. The health technician was assisted by a recorder during the body measures examination. The participant’s age at the time of the screening interview determined the body measures examination protocol. Furthermore, when height is measured in centimeters, a bedside calculation of 0.413 multiplied by the height divided by the serum creatinine offers a reliable approximation of GFR formula [22]. Following the KDIGO 2024 Clinical Practice Guideline for the Evaluation and Management of Chronic Kidney Disease, an eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² was used to define reduced kidney function in this study [23].

Antibody-coated polyacrylamide beads effectively capture albumin from urine. The resulting complex is tagged with a fluorescein-labeled secondary antibody. Following a washing step, the fluorescence bound to the beads is measured, which is directly proportional to the concentration of urinary albumin. The outcome variable was albuminuria, defined using the Urinary Albumin-to-Creatinine Ratio (UACR), which is calculated by dividing urinary albumin (mg) by urinary creatinine (g). UACR > 30 mg/g is classified as proteinuria [23].

Obesity

Body measurements were collected by trained health technicians at the Mobile Examination Center (MEC). During the physical examination, health technicians were supported by recorders. The examination protocol was determined by the participant’s age at the time of the screening interview. Standing height was measured using a stadiometer equipped with a fixed vertical backboard and an adjustable headpiece. Participants’ weights were recorded using a digital weighing scale. Health technicians underwent standardized training and were regularly assessed through intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC > 0.90) and technical error of measurement (TEM < 0.5 cm). Participants were required to remove shoes, wear light examination gowns, and void their bladder prior to measurement. For height measurement, the head was positioned in the Frankfurt horizontal plane; waist circumference was measured at the level of the iliac crest. BMI was calculated as weight divided by height squared (kg/m²) [24]. In pediatric populations, BMI is evaluated using age- and sex-specific percentiles derived from growth charts, which differ from the categorizations applied to adults. These percentiles represent the distribution of BMI within reference populations of the same age and sex, facilitating a more accurate assessment of weight status, where a BMI at or above the 95th percentile indicates elevated weight [25].

Covariate assessment

In our study, we analyzed several potential covariates, which included age categories (12–16 years, and 17–20 years), gender (boys and girls), race (Mexican American, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and Other), Poverty-Income Ratio (PIR, categorized as < 1, ≥ 1), sedentary time (defined as < 5 h or ≥ 5 h), and physical activity (Active, Moderate, Low). Biochemical analyses were performed at the University of Minnesota Advanced Research Diagnostics Laboratory using standardized NHANES protocols. Serum creatinine was measured by the IDMS-traceable kinetic alkaline picrate method; other analytes were measured by enzymatic or photometric assays (NHANES Laboratory Manuals). All assays adhered to CLIA (1988) mandates and Westgard quality control rules, with accuracy ensured through blind split samples and 2% random retesting. Following the cutoff values established in the report released by the Institute of Medicine, we classified serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] levels in adolescents into three categories: serum concentrations of 25-hydroxyvitamin D in adolescents were categorized into three distinct levels: adequate (≥ 50 nmol/L), insufficient (30 to < 50 nmol/L), and deficient (< 30 nmol/L) [26, 27].

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed with R 4.5.1 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). Survey weights that adjust for the complex, multi-stage probability design of NHANES 2009–2018 were used for all descriptive and inferential steps. Descriptive statistics are presented as survey-weighted means ± SE or weighted percentages. Survey-weighted logistic regression (svyglm, family = quasibinomial) was utilized to initially estimate the total effect, followed by a stepwise adjustment for demographic, lifestyle, and biochemical indicators. Linear trends were evaluated using Wald chi-square tests. We investigated whether systemic inflammatory markers are associated with impaired renal function in obese adolescents and assessed their potential as statistical mediators using cross-sectional data. The causal-mediation framework implemented in the mediation package was applied with 1 000 non-parametric bootstraps. Continuous variables were imputed using the Predictive Mean Matching (PMM) method from the ‘mice’ package, with pooled estimates derived according to Rubin’s rules. All models utilized renal injury as the dependent variable and employed weighted linear regression (family = quasilinear) with a consistent set of covariates, including gender, age, race, and PIR. Each covariate demonstrated a significant association with renal injury (p < 0.05), which underscores the robustness of the findings. Additionally, to explore the potential nonlinear associations between inflammatory biomarkers (NLR, PLR, SIRI) and renal injury, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses were performed at the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles. Finally, subgroup analyses stratified by different confounding factors were also conducted.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1, with a p-value < 0.05 (two-tailed) regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Table 1 summarized the weighted characteristics of 6,987,594 NHANES participants aged 12–20 years who attended the 2009–2018 cycles, stratified by renal status. Of these, an estimated 4.10 million (weighted 12.43%) met the criteria for renal injury. Compared with the normal-renal group, adolescents with renal injury were more likely to be female (57.66% vs. 47.02%; p < 0.001) and younger (68.73% aged 12–16 years vs. 54.57%; p < 0.001). Racial distribution also differed modestly between groups (p = 0.008), whereas vitamin D status, and physical-activity level were similar across strata. Obesity prevalence was almost twofold higher in the renal-injury group (5.99% vs. 12.74%; p < 0.001), despite the overall proportion of obese participants being low. Serum phosphorus was slightly higher in the injured group (1.41 vs. 1.37 mmol/L; p = 0.003), but all other electrolytes, renal function indices, and blood cell counts were comparable. Among the inflammatory markers evaluated, only NLR was significantly higher in participants with renal injury (1.90 vs. 1.80; p = 0.034), whereas white-cell, neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, and platelet counts, as well as PLR and SIRI, showed no appreciable between-group differences.

Table 2 displayed the cross-sectional association between obesity and renal injury among 5,869 adolescents aged 12–20 years. In the unadjusted model (Model 1), obesity was associated with 56.40% lower odds of renal injury (OR = 0.436; 95% CI 0.289–0.660; p < 0.001). After adjustment for age, sex, race and PIR (Model 2), the inverse association remained materially unchanged (OR = 0.353; 95% CI 0.229–0.545; p < 0.001). Further adjustment for sedentary time, physical-activity level, vitamin D status, albumin and electrolytes (Model 3) yielded virtually identical estimates (OR = 0.356; 95% CI 0.231–0.548; p < 0.001), indicating that obesity is independently and inversely related to renal injury in this population.

Table 3 summarized the associations between inflammatory biomarkers and renal injury in 5,869 adolescents. NLR showed a steady, positive association across all models: unadjusted OR = 1.110 (95% CI 1.020–1.210; p = 0.013), and the effect was virtually unchanged after full multivariable adjustment (Model 3: OR = 1.190; 95% CI 1.100–1.290; p < 0.001). PLR was unrelated to renal injury in every model (Model 3: OR = 1.000; 95% CI 0.999–1.000; p = 0.306). SIRI was not significant in the crude model (OR = 1.090; 95% CI 0.983–1.210; p = 0.102), but became significant following adjustment (Model 3: OR = 1.180; 95% CI 1.060–1.310; p = 0.005). Thus, both NLR and SIRI—rather than PLR—were independently associated with early renal injury in this population.

Mediation analyses showed that NLR (indirect effect = 0.009, p < 0.001) and SIRI (indirect effect = 0.010, p < 0.001) each showed statistical mediation of the obesity–renal injury association, accounting for 14.82% and 17.53% of the total effect, respectively, whereas PLR carried virtually no indirect effect (< 0.001, p = 0.460) (Fig. 3). Discussion

In the NHANES sample from 2009 to 2018, which included adolescents aged 12 to 20 years, approximately 4.10 million participants (weighted 12.43%) exhibited early renal injury, defined as an eGFR of less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m² or a UACR of 30 mg/g or higher. The renal injury group consisted of a higher proportion of females, younger individuals, and non-obese participants. At the cross-sectional level, obesity demonstrated an inverse association with kidney injury (Model 3: OR = 0.356; p < 0.001). Both NLR (OR = 1.190) and SIRI (OR = 1.180) exhibited independent positive associations with renal injury, while no significant association was noted for PLR (OR ≈ 1.000). The effects of NLR and SIRI on renal injury remained consistent across stratifications by gender, age, race, PIR, vitamin D levels, and sedentary behavior, with all p-values for interaction ≥ 0.05, indicating stable results across subgroups. In the adjusted mixed mediation model, NLR and SIRI were statistically associated with 14.82% and 17.53% of the total obesity–renal injury association, respectively (indirect effect p < 0.001), while PLR showed no association (p = 0.460). Although these patterns are consistent with NLR and SIRI potentially contributing to obesity-related renal injury pathways, we emphasize that: (1) causal mediation cannot be inferred from cross-sectional data; (2) the absence of elevated SIRI in obese adolescents precludes use as an obesity screening tool; and (3) any clinical utility would be limited to risk stratification within already identified renal abnormalities, pending longitudinal validation.

eGFR and UACR serve as sensitive indicators of early kidney damage. Routine screening of high-risk populations facilitates the timely identification of subclinical renal injury, thereby enabling early intervention and the prevention of chronic kidney disease. Some studies suggest that obesity factors have a protective effect on survival rates in chronic kidney disease [28]. Most current studies suggest that obesity is an independent risk factor for the progression of kidney disease [29–31]. The prevalence of kidney disease is positively correlated with obesity [5]. In recent years, there has been a steady increase in the prevalence of kidney disease within the pediatric population, accompanied by a significant rise in the associated risk of mortality from kidney disease among children and adolescents [32, 33]. Some studies have confirmed that patients with high BMI or obesity can compensatory increase their glomerular filtration rate [34, 35]. Simultaneous monitoring of eGFR and UACR in obese adolescents is crucial for assessing renal injury and implementing early interventions. This approach represents a critical opportunity to interrupt the progression of obesity-related nephropathy.

Therefore, in the cross-sectional study, we observed an OR < 1 for the association between obesity and renal injury, which does not imply a “protective effect” of obesity. Rather, it is likely due to obesity-induced glomerular hyperfiltration masking early renal injury, leading to conventional indicators (such as UACR and eGFR) failing to sensitively reflect the true renal function status, thereby creating a statistical illusion of “pseudo-protection“ [36, 37]. Although this study employed a cross-sectional design, which precludes the establishment of causality, two recent long-term cohort studies have provided crucial corroborating evidence. A 30-year cohort study on BMI trends revealed a gradual increase in BMI among children, adolescents, adults, and middle-aged individuals, which was associated with elevated risks of UACR and albuminuria in later life [38]. Another 25-year cohort study found that adolescent overweight and obesity were significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause ESRD [39]. These longitudinal findings strongly suggest that obesity is not a protective factor for kidney health; rather, it is a significant precursor to chronic kidney injury. Coupled with the observed phenomenon of “OR < 1” in this study, it is essential to exercise caution, as this may arise from the masking effect of glomerular hyperfiltration on early renal injury at the cross-sectional time point, rather than indicating a genuine benefit.

The correlation between systemic inflammation levels and kidney health has been extensively studied [40, 41]. Inflammation plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis and progression of kidney diseases, given that the kidneys are highly metabolically active organs particularly susceptible to oxidative damage and hypoxia induced by inflammatory processes [42].Excessive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines may lead to pathological changes in the kidney [31, 42]. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that elevated levels of inflammatory mediators are closely linked to poor prognosis and an increased incidence of renal disease complications [43, 44]. Conversely, kidney injury can initiate inflammatory responses, thereby creating a self-perpetuating cycle that exacerbates disease progression [43]. These findings suggest that factors influencing systemic inflammation levels may be associated with the risk of early renal injury.

In this cross-sectional study, NLR and SIRI showed statistical evidence of partial mediation in the obesity–early renal injury association, whereas PLR showed no significant indirect effect. While the mediated effects are modest in magnitude, they may represent early inflammatory changes detectable prior to overt renal dysfunction in adolescents, a population where early intervention could alter long-term trajectories. Several explanations may account for these differential findings. First, PLR may exhibit varying sensitivity to different types of inflammatory responses. In conditions such as neonatal sepsis and pediatric inflammatory bowel disease, NLR demonstrates superior diagnostic value compared to PLR, while PLR shows better performance in thrombotic disorders [45, 46]. Second, given that the inflammatory mechanisms of chronic kidney disease involve multi-system interactions—including inflammation, microorganisms, tumor comorbidities, and cardiovascular comorbidities—relying solely on single systemic indicators, such as PLR, may not accurately reflect the complex pathological changes occurring in the local renal microenvironment [43, 47]. Finally, this study employed a cross-sectional design, focusing on early renal injury (as indicated by eGFR and UACR). Although PLR showed no statistical mediation of the obesity-early renal injury association in our cross-sectional adolescent cohort, it plays an important role in the progression of chronic kidney disease and end-stage renal disease [48, 49]. Therefore, the PLR may not demonstrate a significant association during the initial stages of kidney injury, with its potential role becoming more pronounced only when the disease progresses to intermediate or advanced stages. Future longitudinal studies integrating dynamic renal function trajectories, multi-omics profiling, and local kidney inflammation markers are warranted to clarify the context-dependent utility of PLR in kidney health assessment. Additionally, NLR and SIRI exhibited linear dose-response relationships with renal injury. These associations suggest potential utility for risk stratification among adolescents with identified renal abnormalities, though they are not recommended as standalone screening tools given the lack of mean difference in SIRI between obese and non-obese groups. Crucially, given the cross-sectional design, these findings demonstrate statistical associations only; future longitudinal cohort and multicenter studies are warranted to investigate whether these markers reflect causal pathways and to establish clinical thresholds.

The limitations of our study are as follows: The NHANES 2009–2018 survey is a one-time assessment, which prevents the establishment of temporal causality between obesity, SIRI, and renal injury, and does not capture the long-term effects of the duration of obesity and the dynamic changes in inflammatory biomarkers (NLR, PLR, SIRI) on proteinuria. The study population comprises children and adolescents aged 12–20 years, while the latency period for obesity-related glomerular injury typically exceeds 5–7 years, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true effect; consequently, future studies with longer follow-up periods are necessary to observe the occurrence of positive events. NHANES lacks data on specific renal injury etiologies (e.g., biopsy-proven glomerular diseases, ATN, or specific diabetic nephropathy). Future studies utilizing registry data with biopsy confirmation or detailed medical records are warranted to validate our findings in specific CKD subtypes. Although we have adjusted for demographic factors, lifestyle choices, and multiple biochemical indicators, some potential confounders, such as dietary sodium intake and circadian rhythms of blood pressure, were not included in our analysis.

Conclusion

Although obesity appeared inversely associated with early renal injury, NLR and SIRI were independently associated with kidney dysfunction and statistically mediated this association. These markers may help stratify risk in adolescents with renal abnormalities but are not recommended for screening obese adolescents. Longitudinal validation is needed.
