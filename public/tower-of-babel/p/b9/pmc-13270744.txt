Decision-making for ICU admission: is there a place for AI? Exploring and understanding meaning, experiences, and perspectives
Authors: Ambre Sauvage, Antoine Guillon, Marie-France Mamzer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntensive care unit (ICU) admission (or refusal) decisions are intricate, relying on the assessment of patient benefit by the intensivist, sole decision-maker, without clear guidelines. As artificial intelligence (AI) rapidly develops predictive applications in healthcare, it could serve as a valuable forecasting tool to aid intensivist decision-making. This study aimed to explore whether intensivists are in demand of an “intelligent” decision-support tool for admissions and, if so, their specific expectations.MethodsA cross-sectional qualitative study was conducted. Ten French intensivists were interviewed between February and May 2024 and their contributions were analyzed using grounded theory method.ResultsParticipants varied in experience (1–35 years), region of practice (hyper-urbanized, n = 5, or not) and familiarity with AI (n = 4). Nine and a half hours of interviews revealed that the ICU admission process is a complex undertaking, depending on many criteria and often involving unconventional decision pathways. Intensivists’ decision-making was inherently subjective, deeply tied to moral values aiming to preserve their medical integrity, though sometimes manifesting as biases that result in unfairness. Participants frequently voiced discomfort with the uncertainty in “grey areas”, that is to say poorly documented and non-extreme situations. While intensivists expected AI to counteract their biases and augment their knowledge, they also feared a loss of the humanity in decision-making to the expense of the technical element.ConclusionExploring experiences of intensivists regarding ICU admission revealed that the decision is less a matter of protocol than of nuanced, subjective assessment. AI likely has a role in supporting ICU admission or refusal decisions, provided its limitations as a purely informative statistical tool are acknowledged.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-026-01454-y.

Introduction

Intensive care units (ICUs) play a crucial role in the management of patients with one or more organ failures and whose vital prognosis is compromised. However, not all patients with (or at risk of developing) organ failure are admitted to ICU. The decision for admission requires a complex assessment of the severity of the patient’s condition, balanced against multiple factors to estimate the potential benefit of intensive care [1]. Indeed, the environment and the therapies used, often invasive, can lead to iatrogenesis capable of outweighing the expected benefits, both in terms of survival and functional outcome [2, 3]. In parallel, the resources of ICUs are inherently limited, notably by the number of beds they have, which varies [4, 5]. Therefore, in addition to the individual patient assessment, there is a need for judicious allocation of these precious limited resources.

The absence of standardized admission or refusal criteria leaves substantial decision-making latitude to intensivists, who bear full responsibility for decision that they must make quickly, often without the opportunity for multidisciplinary deliberation (e.g., on call). Although general recommendations exist [1, 6, 7], they offer few adaptable guidelines for individual situations, thus limiting their applicability in clinical practice [6, 8]. Predictive mortality scores, such as the Sepsis-related Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) [9] or the Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS) [10], are commonly used in ICU, but they cannot be used for admission decisions because, by their nature, they can only be calculated after 24 h of patient evolution in the unit. The literature, largely quantitative, highlights several criteria influencing this decision in the field, such as severity, dependence, comorbidities and age, the latter being a sometimes controversial factor [11]. Although the importance of the patient’s environment has been occasionally emphasized in the literature [12, 13], the role of contextual elements in decision-making remains underexplored [6, 11].

In parallel, the rapid rise of artificial intelligence (AI) is opening up new perspectives in the field of healthcare, particularly in ICU [14], with promising applications in prediction [15–20], therapeutics [21–23], diagnoses [24], and monitoring [25]. AI tools have already demonstrated performance at least equivalent to that of humans (e.g., in reading mammograms [26]) or even superior to conventional scores (e.g., prediction of mortality in ICU [18] and post-out-of-hospital cardiac arrest [27]). The combination of AI and medical human expertise has also shown increased potential, such as in the interpretation of chest X-ray [28].

In this context, the possibility of an AI tool capable of assisting with the decision to admit or refuse ICU admission questioned us. We chose to study this issue from the perspective of intensivists, the key players in this decision-making process. Our research aimed to determine if these professionals desired an “intelligent” decision support tool for ICU admission, and, if so, to identify their expectations regarding such a tool. To answer these questions, we wanted to explore their representations of current practices in ICU admission/refusal decisions, as well as their perceptions of AI as a potential support for this decision.

Methods

Study design and participants

The study consisted of cross-sectional qualitative research, based on interviews. The interviews were selected to capture as faithfully as possible participants’ individual representations of their experiences of ICU admission or refusal, as well as their views on the role AI could play in this context [29]. The aim was to grasp the meaning they gave to their practices through their discourse. We recruited board-certified intensive care physicians (i.e., having completed their internship) who were actively practicing in an ICU. Residents were not included because they are not responsible for decisions regarding patient admission to or refusal from the ICU in France. Non-inclusion criteria consisted of refusal to participate or lack of consent to audio recording of the interview, while the exclusion criterion was opposition to the processing of personal data. Recruitment followed a purposive sampling strategy aimed at capturing, as closely as possible, the diversity of practices and perspectives among French intensive care physicians. Participants were selected based on their level of seniority, hierarchical position, region of practice (one hyper-urbanized and one non-hyper-urbanized region, with differing population demographics and healthcare resources), and their level of familiarity with AI (either physicians with formal training in AI or strong involvement in research and teaching in the field, versus those without such exposure).

The physicians were contacted individually by e-mail, with an attached information letter about the study, offering them to take part in a face-to-face interview at a location of their choice. A reminder was sent to those who had not replied after the first message. If the face-to-face interview was not possible for them, a videoconference was scheduled.

Data collection

The study took place from February to May 2024 in three French cities from two different regions.

Each participant completed a single individual interview, which was recorded using a voice recorder (with written consent). The interviews were conducted by a public health resident familiar with intensive care practice. The discussions were then transcribed, respecting the pseudonymization of the participants.

The interviews were semi-structured using a grid based on bibliographical research and made up of open-ended questions giving space for free elaboration by the participant (cf. Box, and Appendix, Supplemental material 1). The relevance of the interview grid was validated through a pilot test conducted with an ICU expert prior to the study. Aspects related to the theme not included in the grid could be raised spontaneously by the participants, leading to in-depth questioning. At the end of the semi-structured interview, if certain topics had not been sufficiently covered (particularly the role of age and of the number of available beds in the decision), a clinical vignette was used to prompt further reflection (cf. Appendix, Supplemental material 2).

Data analysis

The qualitative analytical approach was inductive, drawing on the main principles of the grounded theory method, from which theory emerges from the field [30, 31]. Analysis was carried out through a coding process. Labels (codes) were assigned to the verbatim interviews. Coding was initially done vertically (interview by interview), and over time was enriched by horizontal coding (linking the interviews together). As coding progressed, meaningful labels were selected and grouped into categories, which in turn were assembled into themes. The theory emerged from the articulation between themes and categories. The research was exploratory, was not intended to be exhaustive and therefore did not aim for theoretical saturation of the data. The study received favorable opinion from the Tours University Hospital Research Ethics Committee (n° 2024 011).

Results

Study population

Ten intensive care physicians were contacted by e-mail; all agreed to participate and were interviewed. Seven were men, three were women, and their ages ranged from 29 to 64. Five were attending physicians, three were professors of medicine-attending physicians and two were chief residents. The number of years in intensive care after residency ranged from one to 35 years. Four participants were considered “young doctors” (less than 10 years’ experience), six “senior doctors”. Half of the physicians practiced in a non-hyper-urbanized area, the other half in a hyper-urbanized area. A total of four doctors were trained in AI.

Nine intensivists were interviewed face-to-face, one by videoconference. Interviews lasted between 35 and 105 min. The clinical vignette was developed for six participants.

Types of ICU admission

Most admissions narrated by the participants began with a phone call from a physician requesting the admission of a patient, either in- or outpatient. The request was assessed either solely by phone, by asking the referring doctor about the patient, or by going to meet the patient if he/she was hospitalized in the hospital where the intensivist worked. The first thing physicians declared to look for was the confirmation of the existence of an indication. All participants said that the decision to admit or refuse was taken after the assessment, and that in the case of admission, all intensive care treatments deemed necessary for the patient were implemented on arrival in the unit. However, additional complexities were reported in relation to the classic admission scheme, as described below.

Admissions with ceilings of care

An admission to ICU did not necessarily mean deploying all the technical resources available in the specialty. Depending on the patient and the situation, participants claimed that it was possible to establish limitations regarding invasive treatments, to be defined at the outset of the admission decision.

Compassionate admissions

Some admissions to the intensive care unit have been described in end-of-life situations. These admissions were justified by the fact that the support provided in the ICU was considered to be better for the patient than in conventional hospital wards, mainly due to a more favorable nursing staff/patient ratio. The existence in the ICU of more flexible rules better suited to end-of-life situations, such as the possibility of overnight visits by relatives, was also cited as a reason. Admissions to give relatives time to become aware, to prepare them for the therapeutic impasse, or for better communication between medical staff and relatives, were also described.

Admissions or refusals without decision

Some patients could be automatically admitted if they had already undergone technical procedures requiring further intensive care, such as pre-hospital intubation. Conversely, intensivists reported that referring physicians sometimes called them even after deciding on a patient’s non-resuscitation status, in order to obtain ‘validation’.

Alternative to direct admission

It was described as possible to delay admission by referring the patient to an intermediate service also able to manage emergencies. The aim was either to wait for a place to become available in the ICU, or to obtain an initial medical assessment and observe the patient’s short-term evolution before deciding to admit or refuse.

Admissions with temporary life support

Most participants explained that they regularly would admit a patient to ICU, implement all therapies, then observe the patient’s evolution before possibly reconsidering the therapeutic project (which could go up to withdrawing life-sustaining therapies).

ICU admission and refusal criteria

Indications for admission to ICU

Three elements were necessary to consider admission to ICU: the severity of the clinical condition, the need for immediate or future organ support and the reversibility of the reason for admission (Fig. 1). If the patient did not show any signs of severity, he was refused because considered ‘too well to benefit’. If, on the contrary, the patient was too clinically severe to be responsive to therapies, he was refused for ‘futility’.

Contraindications to ICU admission

The interviews highlighted absolute (sufficient in themselves) and relative (effective if part of a set of arguments) contraindications to admission to ICU, which are described in Fig. 1. Several criteria gave rise to different interpretations by the participants. Loss of autonomy, a contraindication inevitably invoked, was sometimes considered in general terms of functional dependencies of daily life, while a few intensivists focused on a detailed narrative description of social interactions, centers of interest, etc. The perception of an advanced age varied from 80 (for young doctors) to 85 years old (for senior doctors) and was described as an absolute contraindication for a minority of the participants. The comorbidities the most reported as contraindications to admission were chronic organ failure, cancer and neuroprogressive diseases. Cognitive impairments were also mentioned.

Additional influencing factors

Additional elements that could influence the decision are described in Fig. 1. Intensivists reported them as having less weight in the decision than the indications and contraindications and were mentioned in a less consensual manner. Among them, some appeared as not dependent on the patient. Thus, certain admissions were related to a desire to help young doctors in difficulty facing the patient and/or their relatives or to relieve emergency services or conventional hospitalization services (suffering from a workload too heavy compared to human resources). Sometimes the admissions were described as intended to relieve tensions with colleagues in the event of disagreement over a patient, the objective then being to give more time to reach a consensus. A low number of beds had an impact on the decision in the majority for intensivists working in non-hyper-urbanized regions, while intensivists in hyper-urbanized regions refused more frequently patients due to a lack of available beds. The number of beds available was considered bias by some of the participants because a decision based on this criterion would be funded on hypothetical future events (other future admission requests). The term ‘pretext’ has also been used, to refuse patients that they did not wish to admit, to prioritize patients for whom the service could be provided for a longer term.

Decision experience

Certainties

The participants stated that in the majority of cases (simple situations), their admission or refusal decisions were obvious, without doubt. These “simple” situations gathered all the admissions of young, autonomous persons, without comorbidities, with acute organ failure and short treatment (the typical example cited being voluntary drug intoxication), together with all the refusals of bedridden elderly persons with advance directives limiting the admission to ICU. The certainties involved in decisions to admit or refuse were justified by knowledge from scientific literature combined with contributions from personal experience. Certainties were sometimes translated into rules of conduct: “doubt benefits the patient” (if there is a doubt, the patient must be admitted) or more specifically, for some, not admitting subjects over 80 years old for invasive ventilation in the context of pneumonia.

Uncertainties

Doubts were described as occurring in three main types of admission or refusal decision situations: a lack of information available on the patient, “gray areas” in relation to patients presenting some relative but not clear contraindications, a lack of knowledge of the patient’s underlying pathology. The uncertainty was also found in the fallibility in the prognosis, which the intensivists explained in particular by the fact that they are not aware of the outcomes neither of the patients released alive from ICU nor of the patients refused. For these situations of uncertainty, the participants reported using their intuition to decide, particularly the senior doctors. This was described as a sensation indicating to them the decision to make, without however being able to identify the arguments underlying it. Sometimes the intuitive decision was followed, sometimes not.

Confidence

Confidence in the decision was described as evolving with experience. The younger doctors reported having had a lot of doubts at the very beginning of their careers but said they no longer hesitated often. The most senior doctors reported questioning their certainties more and more frequently.

A subjective decision

The physicians described the impossibility of perfect and systematic consensus between intensivists on admission or refusal decisions. These differences reflected subjectivity in the perception of a person’s autonomy and quality of life, as well as the perception of what is acceptable in terms of care and sequela (what is ‘reasonable’).

Necessary for medical integrity

Subjectivity was perceived by the participants as the vector of their humanity which was considered essential to the doctor-patient relationship. They claimed that it was through subjectivity that they were able to feel empathy and sympathy for the patient. The situations recounted by the intensivists highlighted a fine and personal granularity in the narrative search for information about the patient, particularly his or her lifestyle or personality. Subjective appreciation of the individual’s singularity enabled to respect the patient’s autonomy as much as possible, and thus to be as close as possible to his or her wishes. Subjectivity was also seen as useful in capturing and interpreting the implicitness in the information received from the patient, relatives or other doctors. The physicians’ subjectivity was also used to implement a holistic medical practice, which was valued, by enabling them to consider the extra-medical dimensions (sometimes not palpable, such as feeling) of situations to take into account the “whole project”. Finally, the use of personal sensitivities and convictions was described as necessary to know how to “do the least harm”.

Responsible for inequity

Many participants reported projecting their own personal expectations, what they would have liked for themselves or their loved ones, onto the patients, even if they admitted that this was not fair. They also described being influenced in their decisions, mostly in favor of admission, when they or their loved ones identified with the patients. The direct influence of emotions and affects was also discussed through affinities (for the patient, his or her relatives or the referring doctor) and through the pressures that can be brought to bear by patients’ families or colleagues. Finally, two types of thinking loops were highlighted in relation to decisions to admit or refuse patients to ICU, both of which were subject to the emergence of biases. In situations of uncertainty, participants expressed a degree of intuition in their decisions, which they described as a sensation indicating the decision to be taken, without being able to identify the underlying arguments. The presence of cognitive biases was also reported by intensivists, particularly the availability bias.

Intensivists’ perceptions of AI as a decision support tool for admission/refusal to the ICU

Among the participants, four said they were in favor of AI technology to help the decision-making process of admission or refusal to ICU, five were neutral and one was opposed to it. They spontaneously perceived the tool as delivering prognostic survival scores.

The participants expected AI to act as a safeguard against errors related to human subjectivity and fallibility (fatigability, distractibility…), as well as to increase their knowledge regarding the outcomes of patients. AI was also perceived by some physicians as relieving the stress of the decision.

The intensivists unanimously expressed fear regarding the technical validity of an AI tool for decision support, in terms of quality and reliability of the information from the database, choice of variables studied, selection bias and risk of errors. The risk of reproduction of unconscious human biases, for example racist or sexist, by the algorithm was highlighted. The fear of ending up with a immovable system which would not integrate changes in practices was expressed as well. The physicians were also worried about losing the human aspect of their decision-making practice and losing a medicine considering the singularity of patients. No participant considered AI as a tool that could replace the doctor in decision-making. However, the question of the loss of decision-making power, via the influence of the tool on the human’s decision, was raised. Finally, on a societal level, economic and environmental fears about AI have been expressed, in terms of overconsumption of resources but also in terms of institutional twisting of the tool to integrate financial or ecological criteria in order to decide to refuse or admit a person to ICU.

Discussion

The narrative of the professionals highlighted several aspects influencing the decision-making process for admission to ICU.

Moral values underlying subjectivity

The subjectivity of the intensivists was omnipresent throughout the decision-making process, and a strong ambivalence has been highlighted regarding its place, presented simultaneously as an advantage and a disadvantage. We were able to highlight that the doctors’ subjectivity reflected underlying moral principles. This subjectivity was sometimes asserted as their strength because it was structured by values guaranteeing medical integrity. It would also enable a phenomenological approach to patient assessment, leading to a global representation of the situation, which in turn would lead to practicing better medicine. This representation, also described in a previous study as the “general reflection of the patient” plays a major role in the decision [32]. Conversely, the subjectivity of the intensivists was sometimes asserted as their weakness because it carried cognitive biases responsible for inequalities.

There is little literature that has explored the decision-making process regarding the role of moral values. The involvement of non-purely medical criteria in the decision is poorly documented, and these factors are not well identified and understood by physicians [33]. Medical decision-making is described as mostly based on a benefit-risk scale that only includes medical arguments and as not individualized to the patient’s singularity [32]. Through empirical works conducted with intensivists, a foundation of values necessary for an ethical decision of admission or refusal to intensive care has been described in the literature: explicability, transparency, and fairness [33], trust, respect, honesty, and respect for patient autonomy (although rarely observed in practice) [32]. However, to our knowledge, no prior work had highlighted the articulation of moral values in the actual observed practices of admission decisions in ICU. Some authors have proposed tools to support ethical reflection on moral dilemmas at the patient’s bedside, such as the MORAL balance framework developed by Harvey and Gardner [34], which provides a decision-making grid grounded in Beauchamp and Childress’ four ethical principles. Intensivists are likely to benefit from such clinical ethics tools, as they can offer valuable guidance in complex decision-making situations. While ICU admission decisions are theoretically grounded in a multidisciplinary decision-making process, in practice they are frequently made by a single intensive care physician, particularly during night shifts or emergency situations, and often under severe time constraints. It is this situated, individual decision-making process—rather than the formal frameworks themselves—that constitutes the focus of our qualitative analysis.

Two types of subjectivity were in fact described by the intensivists in our study: one that is assumed and asserted, carrying moral values perceived as positive; one with a negative value, structured by cognitive biases and personal affects, which would be desirable to eliminate. This explains why the participants also proved to be ambivalent regarding their expectations of AI. They expressed the desire to free themselves from their subjectivity via the technical tool but were nonetheless concerned that an AI would remove the non-technical elements of the decision in favor of purely scientific elements, for fear of losing their humanity. By neutralizing their subjectivity, the participants intended to eliminate the inequalities created by their personal biases related to misconceptions and stereotypes, of which.

From the perspective of an AI created by humans, the objectives aimed for by the physicians seem difficult to achieve. Whether the algorithm is predetermined by the engineers or whether it is self-learning from practices collected in medico-administrative databases, the tool itself would risk reflecting their own biases, or even creating new ones [35, 36]. Selecting data to analyze and integrate in order to quantify reality also appears difficult to achieve exhaustively. These choices inevitably introduce an exclusion of potentially essential information, a paradox reminiscent of Gödel’s incompleteness theorems, as recalled by Wernly et al. [37]. This limitation is particularly significant regarding the specificities of human cognition, the “humanity” described by the participants, such as contextual awareness and the consideration of the implicit aspects, which frequently escapes algorithmic modeling.

However, the subjectivity of physicians making admission or refusal decisions to the ICU inherently leads to variability in choices among intensivists, among departments, but even for the same intensivist depending on external factors [38–40]. An AI tool could offer a more standardized framework to the decision-making process to reduce discrepancies potentially responsible for inequalities, while maintaining the human intervention necessary for multifactorial integration and the understanding of the individual case.

Implicit knowledge

In our study, the participants expressed discomfort related to decisions for which the observable medical criteria were not sufficient to guide them. To address these situations identified as ‘grey areas’, they had to rely on their intuition, which they sometimes found difficult to follow because of a strong need for a decision based on explicit and justifiable criteria. This result, which shows the difficulty for intensivists in articulating the arguments and assessing the importance to give to their intuition in their admission decision, aligns with what was described in previous literature [32].

The intuition described was considered not very legitimate by the participants. For them, the intuitive feeling was felt as an aspect of their subjectivity that would serve to compensate for a lack of subtlety in reasoning, in relation to a lack of formalized empirical evidence. This way, although they implicitly and informally carry out a prognostic evaluation throughout their decision-making processes, the term “prognosis” does not appear at all in their discourse as a criterion that they can evaluate. In fact, similarly to what has been shown in oncology [41] and in general medicine [42], the intuitive strategy comes into play in the decision when data is lacking or when the situation proves too complex to fit into the categories of recommendations.

However, physicians’ intuitions are a reflection of their tacit knowledge, which largely stems from their experience [43, 44]. The knowledge derived from clinical experience is usually considered as favorably contributing to the quality of decisions and the resulting care, and its benefits are demonstrated in practice. For instance, in diabetes management within general medicine, it has been observed that experienced clinicians make decisions more effectively by applying personal established routines [45]. However, the same authors also pointed out a risk of inertia (“autopilot”) as well as a possible decrease in the reflexivity of physicians in their judgments (fewer challenges to their thinking). Indeed, despite the probable benefits of involving practical knowledge in medicine, any subconscious process involving automatic thought patterns is prone to errors and cognitive biases [46], and medical decision-making is subject to many [47, 48], including in ICU [49]. This shortcut thinking system has, moreover, been shown to be rather ineffective in real life for assessing patient severity (ICU admission prediction) [50].

Consistent with their fear of the uncertainty associated with their feelings of intuition, the intensivists in our study expected an AI to enable them to increase their knowledge. Such a tool would, however, risk missing an entire segment of tacit knowledge that couldn’t be codified. For some authors, attempting to reduce all knowledge to explicit statements, without considering the social and personal dimensions, would be a mistake [44, 51]; notably due to the importance of accounting for the complex nature of phenomena and adopting an individualized approach to patients [41], thereby acknowledging their singularity. Average, statistical results are indeed not always relevant for a unique patient, and complex ethical questions requires human judgment [37], especially in ICU, a specialty filled with moral dimensions [52].

Nevertheless, no one is requesting AI to replace human decision-makers. While preserving human adaptability and nuanced judgment, an AI technique could find its place in this context as a pure documentation tool (like a score among the other scores), allowing for the estimation of probabilities of (un)favorable events occurring. The tool could thereby act as a safeguard in case of the involvement of the physician’s personal biases leading to false beliefs or simple errors. It could prove all the more useful to intensivists as it could serve as support regarding their tacit knowledge to bolster their argumentation when they have difficulty translating it explicitly and thus making it actionable. Notably, research in cardiac surgery has demonstrated that statistical models outperform clinician intuition regarding predictive accuracy when dealing with non-extreme situations [53] (similar to gray areas). For all these reasons, it might be possible to imagine a judicious blend of technological capabilities and human practical wisdom, consistently putting patient individuality first.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Our study focuses on exploring and understanding meaning, experiences, and perspectives rather than measuring numerical data or testing hypotheses. It explores a diverse sample in terms of profiles, on sociodemographic levels, professional experiences, but also awareness of AI technology. While the sample size is small from a quantitative standpoint, it is appropriate and methodologically justified for a qualitative study. Qualitative research aims to generate an in-depth understanding of meanings, experiences, and decision-making processes rather than statistically generalizable results. Our participants were purposively selected intensivists directly involved in ICU admission and refusal decisions, ensuring high relevance and expertise. This approach allowed us to capture nuanced, context-sensitive perceptions that would not be accessible through larger, survey-based designs.

All physicians who were approached agreed to participate, regardless of their professional profile or level of involvement with AI, and the views collected were diverse, encompassing both favorable and unfavorable positions regarding the integration of AI. Despite the limited number of participants, the purposive sampling strategy enabled the inclusion of contrasting geographical and institutional contexts within France. Although conducted within a specific national context, the findings of this study are likely transferable to other high-income countries. These settings share similar constraints in critical care, notably the structurally limited availability of ICU beds [54], which necessitates trade-offs and triage decisions irrespective of healthcare financing models. While ethical frameworks may vary across cultures, high-income countries largely converge around common principles such as beneficence and fairness. Local organizational factors may influence how these principles are implemented; however, the core decision-making criteria—clinical severity, prognosis, and expected benefit—remain broadly comparable across contexts. The consistency of our findings with those reported in the international literature further supports their external validity [6, 11–13]. Finally, although healthcare systems rely on different funding models, issues related to financing are typically addressed upstream of the clinical decision-making processes examined in this study, rendering these processes largely independent of funding constraints. Although generalizability remains limited due to the small sample size, this exploratory study generated rich and sufficient qualitative material to address the research objectives. These findings represent an initial step and underscore the need for further studies involving larger and more diverse populations.

The choice to conduct interviews proved particularly appropriate to capture the participants’ narratives and delve deeper into ambivalences or elements that were sometimes implicit, as well as to address aspects we had not considered. Finally, the method, by its nature, only allowed us to explore the intensivists’ statements (assumed practices), and not directly the real practices. In the same way, certain topics could not be addressed in the study because they were not spontaneously raised by participants. For example, the case of patients who may be eligible for organ donation was not addressed, even though this decision may be based on complex interactions between various factors.

Conclusion

Our study revealed that the ICU admission is a complex decision tree with atypical patterns in real life setting. The subjectivity of the intensivists was omnipresent throughout the decision-making process. Given these discussions, it seems unlikely that IA will ever fully replicate this uniquely human activity. Instead, AI could be developed as a purely informative, statistical tool to assist intensivists in making balanced decisions about ICU admission or refusal.
