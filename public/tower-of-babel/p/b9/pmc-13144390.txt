MOF-mediated H4K16ac is critical for blastocyst formation in sheep by shaping promoter accessibility and transcription
Authors: Liqin Wang, Baobao Chen, Xiaowei Chen, Haonan Chen, Lu Gan, Ying Chen, Nasser Ghanem, Mingtian Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extending culture to blastocysts in vitro enhances embryo selection efficiency and implantation success in mammals, yet the epigenetic mechanisms underlying blastocyst formation remain unclear. Here, we investigate the role of histone acetyltransferase MOF-mediated H4K16ac during sheep blastocyst formation. We find dynamic changes in H4K16ac distribution during development, with a significant increase from the 8-cell stage to blastocyst and stage-specific enrichment in promoters. Inhibition of MOF activity reduces blastocyst formation and induces widespread transcriptional dysregulation and loss of H4K16ac at 3044 genomic peaks. Genes with these peaks are down-regulated and enriched for pathways critical to blastocyst formation. Notably, the lost H4K16ac peaks show reduced chromatin openness and lower RNA polymerase II (Pol2) enrichment, demonstrating the essential role of MOF in RNA Pol2 occupancy during blastocyst formation. Our findings indicate that MOF-mediated H4K16ac is critical for ovine blastocyst formation by promoting chromatin accessibility and RNA Pol2 occupancy at promoters, thereby facilitating appropriate transcriptional programs.

Introduction

Within the first 5 to 6 days after fertilization and prior to implantation, blastocysts form, characterized by significant morphological changes and the first major cell fate decision. This process leads to the diversification of the early embryo into two distinct cell populations: the inner cell mass (ICM) and the trophectoderm (TE) in humans, cattle, pigs, and sheep1–4. In assisted reproductive technologies (ART), particularly in vitro fertilization (IVF), extending embryo culture to the blastocyst stage has become common practice to improve embryo selection and implantation rates in humans1,5,6. These and subsequent studies demonstrate that blastocyst formation is critical for early embryo development in mammals7,8.

The formation of the blastocyst is orchestrated by a complex interplay of epigenetic modifications that dictate cell fate decisions and differentiation. During blastocyst formation, the ICM and TE develop distinct epigenetic profiles that reflect their distinct developmental fates and gene-expression programs. For example, trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 4 (H3K4me3) forms bivalent domains with H3K27me3 at developmental genes in mouse blastocyst9, and knockdown of H3K4me3 demethylases KDM5B reduces the overall blastocyst rate10. H3K27me2/3 is detected in both ICM and TE, but is more predominant in the ICM in mice2,11,12, and knockdown of KDM6B impairs blastocyst formation in cattle13. Additionally, RNA N4-acetylcytidine (ac4C) modification also contributes to blastocyst formation. Cui et al. reported that N-acetyltransferase 10 (NAT10) regulates the transition of morula-to-blastocyst by catalyzing ac4C14, and Wang et al. reported that ac4C in Nop2 is required for the morula-to-blastocyst transition in mice [15]. These and subsequent studies demonstrate that epigenetic remodeling is essential for blastocyst formation11,15.

H4K16 acetylation (H4K16ac) is a histone modification established by Males Absent on the First (MOF), also known as lysine acetyltransferase 8 (KAT8) or MYST1. H4K16ac plays a pivotal role during early embryonic development through multiple interconnected mechanisms, as evidenced by conserved functions across species, including Drosophila, mouse, and humans. In mouse embryonic stem cells (ESCs), H4K16ac marks active genes and enhancers16, and in mouse skin, MOF has been shown to establish H4K16ac to govern mitochondrial functions by regulating gene promoters17, suggesting fine-tuned regulation of MOF and H4K16ac. During Drosophila embryogenesis, maternal MOF complexes deposit H4K16ac in oocytes, which is preserved intergenerationally through fertilization to establish chromatin accessibility for RNA Polymerase II (Pol2) recruitment during major zygotic genome activation (ZGA)18,19. In mice, MOF-deficient embryos fail to develop beyond the expanded blastocyst stage and are essential for ICM cell survival20. Additionally, KAT8-mediated H4K16ac is essential for sustaining trophoblast self-renewal and proliferation via regulating CDX2 in humans21.

Sheep are docile ruminants widely used for wool and meat production, and their similar size and embryonic development to humans make them ideal experimental animal models for studying human diseases and optimizing therapeutic strategies. However, due to the limited availability and non-expandable nature of embryos, characterizing and mapping the epigenetic landscape during early embryonic development remains challenging in mammals. Therefore, the landscape of H4K16ac and the potential roles of MOF-mediated H4K16ac in early sheep embryo development remain poorly elucidated.

In the present study, we describe the global profiling of H4K16ac in sheep oocytes and early embryos using Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation (CUT&Tag) and reveal the essential roles of MOF-mediated H4K16ac in establishing chromatin accessibility for RNA Pol2 recruitment during sheep blastocyst formation.

Results

To map the stage-specific distribution of H4K16ac in promoters, we analyzed H4K16ac enrichment in sheep oocytes and early embryos. Using k-means algorithms, promoter H4K16ac profiles in oocytes and the early embryos was classified into three distinct clusters: cleavage-specific accumulation before ZGA (cluster 1, n = 8638), including NR5A2, KLF17, PADI6, and SOX17; cleavage-specific accumulation after ZGA (cluster 2, n = 4476), such as FMN2, KMT2C, and ZFP69; blastocyst-specific accumulation (cluster 3, n = 11,348), including TFAP2C, YBX1, CDX2, GATA3, and YAP1, suggesting that these regions are related to blastocyst formation. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis indicated that promoter H4K16ac in cluster 1 was deposited for genes involved in calcium ion transport, cell fate commitment, and embryo organ morphogenesis. Promoter H4K16ac in cluster 2 was deposited for genes enriched in cell junction assembly, regulation of hemopoiesis, and regulation of lipid metabolic process. Promoter H4K16ac in cluster 3 was deposited for genes involved in ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis, ncRNA/mRNA processing, RNA splicing, chromosome segregation, and DNA replication (Fig. 1D). Collectively, these results indicate that promoter H4K16ac accumulation is associated with gene expression for diverse biological functions during early embryonic development, particularly in blastocyst formation.

Impaired blastocyst formation in MOF-inhibited embryos

To reveal the role of H4K16ac during sheep early embryo development, we first analyzed the expression of MOF using IF. In the sheep oocyte, MOF was clearly observed and down-regulated post-fertilization. After zygotic genome activation (occurs at the 8-/16-cell stage), the signal of MOF was gradually increased and reached its highest level in the blastocysts (Fig. 2A, B), consistent with the high H4K16ac signal in the blastocyst as revealed by CUT&Tag (Fig. 1D). To inhibit the catalytic activity of MOF, we cultured the embryos with MG149 supplement. Survival rate assessment revealed no toxicity for the inhibitor treatment at concentrations up to 94 μM (Supplementary Fig. S1). We proceeded with 47 μM MG149 in subsequent experimental procedures. Surprisingly, the percentage of embryos that reached the blastocyst stage was significantly decreased in MOF-inhibited embryos compared with that of DMSO-treated embryos (37.4% vs. 60.8%, p < 0.01). Additionally, 42.67% of MOF-inhibited embryos were arrested at the 8-/16-cell stage, compared to only 4.23% of DMSO-treated embryos (p < 0.01, Fig. 2C, D). Because MG149 is a dual inhibitor of KAT5 and MOF, another MOF inhibitor, MC4171, was added to BO-IVC. As expected, MC4171-treated zygotes also showed decreased blastocyst rate compared with the DMSO-treated group (Supplementary Fig. S2). H4K16ac is correlated with elevated gene expression by changing RNA Pol2 occupancy

To explore the mechanisms underlying MOF-mediated blastocyst formation, we next investigated chromatin accessibility and RNA Pol2 distribution when MOF was inhibited, as RNA Pol2 is recruited to the promoter to initiate transcription when the chromatin is accessible. As expected, the up-regulated genes and genes with gained H4K16ac peaks showed higher chromatin openness (Fig. 6A, B). Moreover, H4K16ac and RNA Pol2 were co-occupied in the promoter regions, and in regions with more enriched H4K16ac showed stronger RNA Pol2 signal (Fig. 6C, D). Of note, regions with a gain of H4K16ac signal showed more RNA Pol2 enrichment than that of H4K16ac loss regions (Fig. 6E). Examination of individual loci showed that the HSPH1 promoter were enriched with H4K16ac and RNA Pol2, and was highly expressed in the blastocysts (Fig. 6F). We next investigated the dynamic change of H4K16ac and chromatin accessibility in these regions. The MOF inhibition-induced H4K16ac loss region showed less H4K16ac signal from the 2-cell embryos to the morula compared with the blastocyst, while the MOF inhibition-induced H4K16ac gain region showed more enrichment of H4K16ac in the oocytes (Supplementary Fig. S4A–D). Meta-plot revealed higher chromatin accessibility in MOF inhibition-induced H4K16ac loss region at the blastocysts (Supplementary Fig. S4E, F). Our data suggest that these regions were specifically chromatin opened and acetylated for blastocyst formation in sheep.

Discussion

High blastocyst formation rates and high-quality blastocysts are critical for successful implantation in mammals, particularly in ART. In the present study, we describe the global profiling of H4K16ac in sheep oocytes and early embryos and reveal important roles of MOF-mediated H4K16ac in blastocyst formation by remodeling chromatin accessibility to regulate RNA Pol2 distribution. Since a recent study revealed that H3K4me3 is a potential target for the improvement of ART-derived embryos22, we further inferred that MOF-H4K16ac signaling could also serve as a potential biomarker of blastocyst quality.

Dynamic remodeling of histone modifications underpins chromatin reprogramming during mammalian pre-implantation development2,23,24. The present study provides the first genome-wide atlas of H4K16ac in the ovine embryos. In sheep blastocysts, H4K16ac peaks were distributed across seven genomic region categories, with the majority falling within intergenic regions, introns, and promoters. Consistently, Taylor et al. reported a similar distribution of H4K16ac peaks in mouse ESCs16, arguing that H4K16ac is conserved across mammals. They also found H4K16ac enrichment around the transcriptional start site, consistent with our data. Moreover, we revealed stage-specific H4K16ac signatures that demarcate developmental transitions, and the progressive narrowing of H4K16ac to promoters of lineage-affiliated genes suggests that this modification serves not merely as a permissive open signal, but as a stage-specific licensing mark that anticipates ensuing transcriptional demands. Indeed, integrated multi-omics revealed a quantitative relationship between promoter H4K16ac and transcription. Similarly, Taylor et al. reported that H4K16ac is correlated with gene expression in mouse ESCs but not in neural progenitor cells16, suggesting cell-type-specific distribution and roles of H4K16ac25–27. This relationship between promoter H4K16ac and transcription could be explained by the strong association between H4K16ac and open chromatin, as concomitant ATAC-seq demonstrated that H4K16ac gain was tightly coupled to increased chromatin accessibility. Consistently, this relationship has been reported in Drosophila oocytes18,28, human acute myeloid leukemia cells26, and mouse ESCs16,29,30.

MOF is a member of the MYST family of histone acetyltransferases. During blastocyst formation, stronger MOF staining was observed in ICM cells. However, dysregulation of ICM-specific genes in sheep blastocysts after MOF inhibition occurred, consistent with previously published data during Drosophila embryogenesis28. In addition, Thomas et al. reported that the absence of MOF led to poorly developed ICMs, degenerated TEs, and nonexpanded blastocoelic cavities20. These studies and our data demonstrate the essential role of MOF in ICM pluripotency. Besides its acetyltransferase activity, Li reported that MOF directly binds to pluripotency-associated genes26, including Nanog, Oct4, and Sox2, and specifically regulates the NANOG transcriptional network in mouse ESCs, and ablation of MOF leads to loss of ESC self-renewal29. Bi et al. found MOF sustains trophoblast stem cells self-renewal and proliferation via regulating CDX2 in humans21. Therefore, MOF could also directly bind to pluripotency-associated genes to regulate blastocyst formation in sheep, which needs further investigation in future studies.

Transcription initiation and elongation were controlled by RNA Pol2 occupancy at promoters31–33. During early embryo development, RNA Pol2 is preferentially loaded to future gene targets before ZGA, where it later engages in full transcription elongation during the major ZGA34. In the present study, we found correlations between promoter H4K16ac and RNA Pol2 occupancy, and elevated RNA Pol2 binding in H4K16ac gain regions. It is highly tempting to hypothesize that MOF might recruit RNA Pol2 to H4K16ac regions. Unfortunately, we were unable to generate RNA Pol II CUT&Tag data from MOF-inhibited blastocysts. Previous studies revealed that acetylated H4 is known to be recognized by TAF135, a subunit of RNA Pol2 transcription initiation complex, and TAF1 was reported to bind NSL complex of MOF36. Therefore, inhibition of MOF disrupts higher-order chromatin compaction, facilitating RNA Pol2 initiation and elongation.

In summary, we delineate a MOF-mediated H4K16ac program that regulates chromatin accessibility, RNA Pol2 recruitment, and lineage-specific gene expression in the ovine embryo. Disruption of this pathway uncouples transcriptional fidelity from developmental timing, leading to blastocyst failure. These findings establish H4K16ac as an epigenetic checkpoint that safeguards the transition from cleavage to blastocyst in sheep, and as a potential biomarker of blastocyst quality in ART.

Methods

In vitro maturation

In vitro maturation (IVM) was performed as previously described3,37. Ovaries from adult sheep were collected at the Hualing abattoir in Urumqi, Xinjiang. Follicular fluid from large (>3 mm in diameter) follicles on the ovarian surface was aspirated with a 10 mL syringe containing 2 mL of oocyte collection fluid. Cumulus-oocyte complexes (COCs) with more than two layers of compact cumulus cells and a dense, homogeneous cytoplasm were collected. For IVM, the culture medium (TCM-199 supplemented with 10% FBS, 0.05 IU/mL LH, 0.05 IU/mL FSH, 24.2 mg/L sodium pyruvate, 1 g/mL 17β-estradiol, 10 ng/mL EGF, and 0.1 mM cysteamine) was pre-equilibrated at 38.5 °C in 5% CO2, 95% air, and saturated humidity for 3 h before use. COCs were then transferred to a 100-μL IVM medium droplet in groups of 20 and cultured at 38.5 °C under 5% CO2, 95% air, and saturated humidity for 20–22 h. After maturation, MII-stage oocytes were collected for subsequent in vitro fertilization (IVF).

In vitro fertilization

Sheep IVF was performed as previously described4,38. Briefly, 20 mature oocytes were transferred to a 100-μL microdrop of BO-IVF medium (#71004, IVF Bioscience, Falmouth, UK) overlaid with mineral oil. Frozen semen from one ram (#SF0216) was thawed and washed twice by centrifugation at 328 × g for 5 min in 1 mL BO-SemenPrep (#71003, IVF Bioscience). Capacitated spermatozoa were then added to the microdrops and cultured for 16 h at 38.5 °C under 5% CO2 and saturated humidity. Fertilized zygotes were transferred to BO-IVC medium (#71005, IVF bioscience) for further development. At defined time points, the 2-, 4-, 8-, and 16-cell stage embryos, as well as the MII stage oocytes, morulae, and blastocysts, were collected for CUT&Tag library preparation.

Inhibition of MOF in sheep embryos

To investigate the role of MOF in early embryonic development, the histone acetyltransferase inhibitor MG149 (S7476, Selleck, Shanghai, China) was added to BO-IVC medium (#71005, IVF bioscience) at a final concentration of 47 μM. Embryos were cultured from the zygote stage to the blastocyst stage with MG149 or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) treatment. Embryonic developmental status was assessed at 72 h and 168 h post-fertilization. To exclude broader HAT inhibition or off-target effects, we used a second MOF inhibitor, MC4171 (HY-153604, MCE, Shanghai, China), to treat the zygotes with concentrations of 4 μM, 8 μM, 40 μM, and 80 μM.

Immunofluorescent staining

Immunofluorescent (IF) staining was performed as previously described39. Briefly, embryos were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 15 min at room temperature (RT), then permeabilized in 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min at RT. After blocking in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h at RT, embryos were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody anti-MOF (ab200660, 1:100 dilution; Abcam). Following washes in blocking solution, embryos were incubated with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (ab150160, 1:200 dilution, Abcam) for 1 h at RT. The embryos were then stained with 1 mg/ml 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 5 min at RT, mounted on a glass slide, and observed using an LSM710 laser scanning confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) under consistent acquisition parameters.

CUT&Tag library preparation and sequencing

Oocytes and embryos were prepared as follows: 150 oocytes, 120 2-cell-stage embryos, 100 4-cell-stage embryos, 75 8- and 16-cell-stage embryos, 50 morula embryos, and 40 expanded blastocysts per stage. Three replicates were performed for each stage. The CUT&Tag library was constructed as previously described in our published paper4. Following removal of the zona pellucida, embryos were incubated overnight at 4 °C with either anti-H4K16ac (ab109463, 1:100; Abcam) or RNA Pol2 (ab300575, 1:100; Abcam). Then, embryos were incubated with ChiTag goat anti-rabbit IgG (N269-01A, Novoprotein) for 1 h at RT and exposed to pAG-Transposome containing 1% digitonin for 1 h at RT, followed by tagmentation for 1 h at 37 °C. DNA was purified using tagment DNA extract beads and subjected to 15 cycles of PCR amplification with i5 and i7 primers. The resulting libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus (Annoroad Gene Technology Company).

CUT&Tag analysis

CUT&Tag analysis was performed as previously described in our published paper4. Briefly, Bowtie2 software (v2.5.1) was employed for the alignment of paired-end reads against the sheep reference genome oviAri4, with the application of parameters as follows: --end-to-end --very-sensitive --no-mixed --no-discordant --phred33 -I 10 -X 1000. Subsequent removal of PCR duplicates was accomplished using the Picard tool (v2.27.5) under its default parameter settings. For subsequent downstream analytical procedures, read counts derived from merged replicate BAM files were normalized to the reads per kilobase of bin per million reads mapped (RPKM) metric through the use of deepTools (v3.5.1). To visualize the signals of H4K16ac and RNA Pol2 in the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) browser, the bamCoverage function in deepTools was used to generate RPKM values based on 100-bp windows, while metaplots were constructed using the computeMatrix and plotProfile functions integrated into the same software. MACS2 was used to call H4K16ac peaks with the following parameters: -B, -f BAMPE, --nomodel, -nolambda, -q 0.05, and --keep-dup all. Meanwhile, the bdgdiff function of MACS2, run with default parameters, was applied to identify peaks showing loss or gain. Peak annotation was implemented with the ChIPseeker package (v1.38.0) in R software, and Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was carried out in R environment with the assistance of prcomp and the clusterProfiler package (v4.10.1).

ATAC-seq library construction and sequencing

ATAC-seq libraries were prepared utilizing the Hyperactive ATAC-Seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (TD711-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). In summary, thirty blastocysts, prepared in three replicates, were incubated in ice-cold lysis buffer comprising 10% NP-40, 10% Tween 20, and 1% Digitonin for five minutes on ice. Subsequently, the blastocysts were transferred to a mixture containing 18.5 μL ddH2O, 10 μL 5× TTBL, 4 μL TTE mix V5, along with 10% Tween 20 and 1% Digitonin, and incubated at 37 °C for thirty minutes. The reaction was halted by adding 5 μL of 5× TS stop buffer. DNA extraction was performed using ATAC DNA Extract Beads, followed by library amplification through the addition of 5 μL N5XX primer, 5 μL N7XX primer (TD202, Vazyme), 10 μL 5× TAB, and 1 μL TAE, with eighteen cycles of PCR. Post-amplification, libraries were purified using ATAC DNA Clean Beads and prepared for next-generation sequencing.

ATAC-seq analysis

Paired-end reads were aligned to the sheep reference genome oviAri4 utilizing Bowtie2 with the specified parameters: --end-to-end, --very-sensitive, --no-mixed, --no-discordant, --phred33, -I 10, and -X 1000. PCR duplicates were eliminated employing Picard with default settings. For subsequent analyses, read counts were normalized to RPKM using deepTools, with merged replicate BAM files. The generation of metaplots was performed using the computeMatrix and plotProfile functions in deepTools.

RNA library construction and sequencing

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was conducted as detailed in our prior publication. In brief, five blastocysts were lysed directly, with three replicates conducted per group. The lysate was utilized to prepare RNA libraries employing the Smart-seq2 methodology. cDNA was fragmented using dsDNA fragmentase (M0348S, NEB) by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by size selection using the supplied sample purification beads. Fragments within the 150–300 bp range were employed for library construction. The libraries were sequenced using paired-end sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform by Annoroad Gene Technology Corporation.

RNA-seq analysis

Raw sequencing reads were trimmed to eliminate adaptors and low-quality bases utilizing fastp (v0.23.4). Reads passing quality control were aligned to the sheep reference genome (oviAri4) using hisat2 (v2.1.0). All aligned reads were subsequently sorted and indexed with samtools (v1.9). Transcript quantification was carried out using featureCounts (v2.0.1), and gene expression levels were normalized employing fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped fragments (FPKM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with default parameters in the DESeq2 package (v3.11) in R, considering genes with an absolute log2 fold change greater than 1 and an adjusted p-value less than 0.05 as significant DEGs.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed in the R programming language. Data are presented as mean values ± SEM (standard error of the mean). Statistical comparison was performed using Fisher’s exact test, the Wilcoxon test, or the Student’s t-test. Statistical significance, sample size, and the number of replicates were determined as indicated in the Figure legends. P values < 0.05 were considered significant (∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001).

Data visualization

Data visualization was primarily performed using R programming language, including the Bioconductor software packages. Phylogenetic trees, principal component analysis (PCA), heatmap, and boxplot were generated using ggtree (v3.17), ggrepel (v0.9.3), pheatmap (v1.0.12), and ggplot2 (v3.3.2), respectively. H4K16ac signals at specific regions were visualized using the IGV browser.
