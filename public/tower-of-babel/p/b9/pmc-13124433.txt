Efficacy of scalp acupuncture for post stroke dysphagia: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Li Wang, Chao Zhang, Ruiyun Yu, Haihuan Yang, Xiaocheng Wang, Yuanyuan Wang, Linjing Song, Dayong Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Stroke persists as a paramount global health priority, maintaining persistently elevated incidence and mortality metrics across epidemiological registries. Post-stroke dysphagia, serving as a critical determinant of functional prognosis, imposes multidimensional burdens spanning aspiration pneumonia risk, nutritional compromise, and psychosocial well-being. This neurogenic deglutition disorder has consequently catalyzed clinical innovation in rehabilitation modalities, with scalp acupuncture emerging as a promising neuromodulatory intervention.Methods:We conducted a systematic search of 8 databases for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) assessing scalp acupuncture treatment for post-stroke dysphagia. The primary outcomes included clinical efficacy, while the secondary outcomes comprised the Kubota Water Swallowing Test (WST), standardized swallowing assessment (SSA), videofluoroscopic dysphagia scale (VDS), and adverse events. Rigorous evaluation of potential biases within the selected studies was performed by the Cochrane Handbook (version 5.1.0) for systematic reviews of interventions. Heterogeneity among studies was addressed through sensitivity analysis.Results:Based on data derived from 20 RCTs involving 1278 participants, the meta-analysis revealed significant clinical effective rate in the application of scalp acupuncture for post-stroke dysphagia [odds ratio = 4.45, 95% confidence interval (CI) (3.04, 6.51), P < .01], improvement in the Kubota WST [mean differences (MD) = −0.82, 95% CI (−1.04, −0.59), P < .00001], SSA [MD = −4.01, 95% CI (−4.59, −3.42), P < .00001], VDS [MD = −5.75, 95% CI (−8.33, −3.17), P < .0001], in comparison with the conventional rehabilitation protocols. Only one study reported adverse events.Conclusion:Current evidence suggests that scalp acupuncture therapy may be an effective measure for post-stroke dysphagia.

1. Introduction

Globally ranked as the second leading cause of mortality, stroke is surpassed only by ischemic heart disease. Within China’s adult population, it represents the foremost etiology of both disability-adjusted life years and premature death, thus imposing substantial socioeconomic burdens on affected individuals, familial networks, and healthcare infrastructure.[1,2] As a prevalent neurological sequela, dysphagia is characterized by deglutition coordination, elevated aspiration risk, and subjective swallowing obstruction, which significantly contributes to stroke-related mortality and complication-driven hospitalization. According to relevant research data, 50 to 67% of stroke patients suffer from dysphagia, and the mortality rate among stroke patients with dysphagia is 5.4 times higher than that of patients without dysphagia.[3]

Currently, rehabilitation protocols such as electrical stimulation, magnetic stimulation, and various forms of swallowing training have been incorporated into the adjuvant treatment of dysphagia. However, while initial findings are promising, the consistency of pertinent research remains to be verified.[4]

Therefore, some studies have shifted their focus to other interventions, like acupuncture, which has been widely used in the treatment of stroke and related symptoms as one of China’s traditional therapies.[5] Simultaneously, acupuncture has been acknowledged and integrated into behavioral measures in recent guidelines, supported by evidence of moderate quality, which reflects an increasing acceptance of its potential within the realm of adjunctive interventions.

Scalp acupuncture, a specialized form of acupuncture, also referred to as cranial needling therapy, is a therapeutic modality that involves precise needle insertion at specific cephalic acupoints or cortical projection zones. This technique exerts its therapeutic effects through neuromodulation of cerebral hemodynamics and metabolic homeostasis in corresponding cortical regions.[6]

Currently, research on scalp acupuncture is predominantly focused on headache and hemiplegia.[7,8] Given the gradual accumulation of clinical evidence supporting the benefits of scalp acupuncture in treating post-stroke dysphagia,[9] it is necessary to conduct a comprehensive systematic review of scalp acupuncture for post-stroke dysphagia, including the latest clinical research and multiple outcome measures. Such a review not only promises to offer deeper insights into the treatment of post-stroke dysphagia but also has the potential to broaden the scope of scalp acupuncture application, providing additional inspiration for neuroscience research.

This study aims to conduct a meta-analysis of clinical RCTs on scalp acupuncture for the treatment of post-stroke dysphagia, to provide more reliable evidence-based decision-making for the rehabilitation of patients with post-stroke dysphagia.

2.1.3. Protocol

The protocol has been registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD42023487127).

2.2. Research methods

2.2.1. Search strategy

To ensure the reliability and comprehensiveness of the clinical data, 8 databases were retrieved from their inception up to February 2025, yielding all languages. The database includes: English literature database: PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science; Chinese literature database: China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Database (WANFANG), the Chinese Science and Technology Journals Databases (VIP), Chinese Biomedical Literature Database (CBM). Search terms like “Stroke,” “Deglutition Disorder,” “Dysphagia,” “Acupuncture,” “Randomized controlled trial” and “RCT” were used to identify potential studies. Further eligibility of the search studies and any related studies that may be missing were determined by a manual review of the reference lists of the studies.

2.2.2. Records screening and data extraction

All retrieved records were imported into EndNote X9 for management and de-duplication. The titles and abstracts of the retrieved studies were reviewed by Song L., and the unrelated articles were excluded according to the established inclusion criteria. Two reviewers (Yang H. and Yu R.) extracted relevant data from the provided records, encompassing several key elements, which included: fundamental details about the incorporated study such as publication year, primary author, and title; participant-related information, the sample size, etc; specific content of the interventions including the type of the therapy, duration, etc; outcomes; and adverse events.

2.2.3. Risk of bias assessment

Based on the risk bias assessment tool provided in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (version 5.1.0), 2 reviewers (Wang Y. and Wang X.) evaluated the quality of the included literature. The evaluation contents mainly covered the following aspects: random sequence generation, allocation sequence concealment, double-blind, study outcome blindness, outcome data integrity, selective reporting and other bias.

2.3. Statistical treatment

The Revman 5.3.0 and Stata 16.0 software were used for the statistical analysis. The odds ratio (OR) was calculated for binary variables in included studies, the mean difference (MD) or standardized mean difference was calculated for continuous variables, and 95% confidence interval (CI) was used to represent the effect size of the 2 variables within the interval. The heterogeneity of the included studies was assessed by the Cochrane Q-test and I2 statistics before data merging. If there was no obvious heterogeneity between studies (P > .1, I2 < 50%), the heterogeneity was considered good and a fixed-effect model was selected for Meta-analysis; if there was significant heterogeneity between studies (P < .1, I2 > 50%), a random effects model was selected for meta-analysis. To investigate sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses stratified by intervention type (specifically, with or without electrical stimulation) were conducted using RevMan 5.3.0. Additionally, Stata 16.0 was employed to perform sensitivity analysis via the “leave-one-out” method to verify robustness, as well as to assess publication bias (using funnel plots, Egger test, and Begg test) for outcomes including 10 or more studies.

By applying to several databases, we identified 592 potentially relevant studies, of which 572 articles were removed due to duplicate or irrelevance on the basis of title and abstract. 3.3. Risk of bias assessment

Based on the inclusion criteria, 20 articles were retrieved and subjected to a quality assessment according to the procedural steps. Among the 20 included studies, one study[10] was grouped numerically, thus classified as high risk. 11 studies[13,15–18,20,21,23,26–28] clearly stated the use of the random number table method for randomization, one study[19] utilized SPSS software for randomization, were evaluated as low risk for random sequence generation. The remaining 7 studies[11,12,14,22,24,25,29] only mentioned the term “random” without providing specific details on the method, resulting in an unclear risk judgment. For allocation concealment, 4 studies[12,15,23,27] explicitly described concealment measures and were rated as low risk, while the remaining studies provided insufficient information, leading to an unclear risk rating. In terms of performance and detection bias (blinding), one study[16] explicitly reported blinding of participants, personnel, and outcome assessors, indicating a low risk of bias. Conversely, one study[22] did not implement blinding for any parties, resulting in a high risk judgment. Two studies[27,28] reported blinding of outcome assessors only (low risk for detection bias), while the blinding status for the remaining studies was unspecified (unclear risk). Six studies[15,16,19,23,26,27] reported dropouts or loss to follow-up. Among them, one study[16] provided detailed reasons for the attrition, with balanced dropout rates between groups; thus, it was deemed low risk. The other 5 studies were classified as high risk due to unexplained loss to follow-up or high attrition rates. Finally, all included studies were rated as low risk for selective reporting and other biases, as no evidence of selective outcome reporting or other significant sources of bias was identified. 3.4. Primary outcomes (clinical efficiency)

Among the literature selected for this study, 16 articles[10–19,21,23,25,27–29] reported the clinical OR of post-stroke swallowing disorder employing a fixed-effects model for the meta-analysis (prior heterogeneity test [P = 1.00, I2 = 0%] indicated no significant heterogeneity among studies). The data collected from the studies revealed a notable clinical treatment effect for scalp acupuncture therapy in patients with post-stroke dysphagia, with statistically significant differences [OR = 4.45, 95% CI (3.04, 6.51), P < .01] (see Fig. 3).

Forest plot for the meta-analysis of clinical efficiency.

3.5. Secondary outcomes

3.5.1. WST

13 articles[10,11,13,16,17,19–23,27–29] reported the scoring results of the Water Swallowing Test (WST) assessment for continuous variable data, all utilizing a scoring system ranging from 1 to 5. After conducting a heterogeneity test (P < .0001, I2 = 78%), significant heterogeneity was found among the studies. Consequently, a random-effects model was observed for the meta-analysis. The results indicated that the treatment group demonstrated statistically significant superiority over the control group in the WST scores [MD = −0.82, 95% CI (−1.04, −0.59), P < .00001] (see Fig. 4).

Forest plot for the meta-analysis of WST score. WST = Kubota Water Swallow Test.

3.5.2. SSA

In 8 articles,[13,15,21,22,24,26,28,29] the impact of acupuncture treatment on the SSA scores of post-stroke dysphagia patients was documented. Following a preliminary heterogeneity test (P < .00001, I2 = 89%), it was observed that there was substantial heterogeneity among the studies, for which a random-effects model was selected for the meta-analysis. The results demonstrated that the improvement in swallowing function in the treatment group was superior to that of the control group [MD = −4.01, 95% CI (−4.59, −3.42), P < .00001] (Fig. 5).

Forest plot for the meta-analysis of SSA score. SSA = standardized swallowing assessment.

3.5.3. VDS

The VDS score results were revealed in 2 articles.[20,25] Due to the homogeneity exhibited in the study (P = .84, I2 = 0%), a fixed-effects model was applied for the meta-analysis. The results indicated that the VDS scores in the treatment group were superior to those in the control group [MD = −5.75, 95% CI (−8.33, −3.17), P < .0001]. Please refer to Figure 6 for details.

Forest plot for the meta-analysis of VDS score. VDS = videofluoroscopic dysphagia scale.

3.6. Adverse events

One study[20] reported adverse events, with 6 cases occurring in the acupuncture group (2 cases of vomiting, 1 case of nausea, and 3 cases of diarrhea), and 8 cases in the control group (1 case of stomachache, 2 cases of vomiting, 2 cases of nausea, and 3 cases of diarrhea).

3.7. Investigation of potential heterogeneity

Given the significant statistical heterogeneity observed in the primary pooled analyses (I2 > 50%, P < .01), we conducted sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis to evaluate the robustness of the results and to explore potential sources of variation.

3.8. Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis of the WST/SSA scoring index was conducted using the one-at-a-time exclusion method with Stata 16.0 software. One study was sequentially excluded, and the remaining studies were combined for meta-analysis. As shown in the Figure 7, even after the exclusion of most studies, the combined results of the remaining studies remained statistically significant, indicating no considerable changes, which suggests high stability of the research analysis results.

Sensitivity analysis. (A) WST. (B) SSA. SSA = standardized swallowing assessment, WST = Kubota Water Swallow Test.

3.9. Subgroup analysis

To investigate the sources of heterogeneity for WST and SSA, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by the type of concomitant intervention: swallowing rehabilitation, electrical stimulation, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, and body acupuncture. Subgroup analysis of the WST (Fig. 8) revealed substantial heterogeneity in the therapeutic effects of scalp-body acupuncture combination for post-stroke dysphagia (I2 = 89%, P < .01). Similarly, persistent residual heterogeneity was observed across various combination therapies in the SSA subgroup analysis (Fig. 9). This phenomenon is likely attributable to 2 key methodological characteristics of acupuncture interventions: Treatment individualization: The unique neuroanatomical targeting strategy in acupuncture, where acupoint selection is inherently personalized based on meridian theory and symptom patterns[30]; Operator-dependent variability: Significant inter-practitioner differences in therapeutic rationale and point localization techniques, as documented in acupuncture standardization studies.[31]

Forest plot of subgroup analysis by interventions on WST score. WST = Kubota Water Swallow Test.

Forest plot of subgroup analysis by interventions on SSA score. SSA = standardized swallowing assessment.

Notably, the clinical diversity arising from these factors exceeded the stratification parameters predefined in our protocol, suggesting the need for advanced multivariate adjustment models in future trial designs.

3.10. Publication bias

In order to assess publication bias for outcomes (involving a study quantity of ≥10), funnel plots (Fig. 10) were utilized to indicate the basic distribution of related studies on both sides of the baseline. Regarding clinical efficiency (Begg test, Pr > |z|= 0.753 > 0.05; Egger test, P = .821), WST (Begg test, Pr > z|= 0.583 > 0.05; Egger test, P = .156), it can be considered that there is no significant publication bias in the study results.

4. Discussion

Modern medical studies have shown that swallowing pattern-generating neural circuits are located in the ventrolateral region of the medulla head and that stroke-related lesions in the cortical hemisphere, subcortical control circuit or in the brainstem may lead to dysphagia.[32] The function of the cerebral cortex has a certain refraction relationship with the corresponding scalp site.[33] The neurostimulation protocols developed by Huang et al[20] and Zhou et al[13] research group strategically targeted acupuncture loci neuroanatomically correlated with primary motor cortex and somatosensory association areas. Appropriate stimulation can activate the internal neurons in the plane parallel to the brain surface, which in turn affects the corresponding cerebral cortex function.[34] In the investigation conducted by Liu S et al,[26] diffusion tensor imaging tractography revealed fractional anisotropy values in region of interest demonstrated significant increases in the primary motor cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, parieto-occipital cortex, insular cortex, and posterior limb of the internal capsule (P < .05) following scalp acupuncture intervention. Stimulating the acupoints on the head has been purported to enhance cerebral blood supply, provoke activity within the cerebral cortex, foster neurogenesis and cellular proliferation in the central nervous system, as well as modulate the distribution of blood flow and neurochemical substances.[35] In Ling’s neurovascular study,[18] scalp acupuncture intervention significantly increased cerebral perfusion parameters, with cerebral blood flow and cerebral blood volume demonstrating marked elevation compared to pretreatment baselines within the intervention cohort (P < .05). Furthermore, the treatment group exhibited superior cerebral blood flow (22.47 ± 2.26 vs 19.54 ± 2.13 mL/100 g/min) and cerebral blood volume (2.25 ± 0.26 vs 1.96 ± 0.22 mL/100 g) metrics relative to control counterparts (P < .05).

Additionally, several notable novel findings have emerged from our investigation. Firstly, the intervention combining scalp acupuncture with repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation demonstrated superior efficacy in reducing SSA scores (effect size = −6.95; 95% CI [−8.70, −5.20]), significantly surpassing combinations with electrostimulation or swallowing rehabilitation. Secondly, the combination of scalp acupuncture and body acupuncture exhibited more pronounced improvements in WST. Thirdly, therapeutic efficacy evaluation revealed enhanced therapeutic outcomes when needling sites were concentrated in motor cortex regions and somatosensory areas.

Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting our findings: Insufficient data precluded a comprehensive meta-analysis of all eligible trials, including the study by Jin et al,[36] which reported positive effects of scalp acupuncture on post-stroke dysphagia; The methodological quality of some RCTs remains to improve, and the failure to demonstrate the process of allocation concealment and blinding, which leads to the low level of evidence; Although Begg and Egger tests indicated no statistically significant publication bias, visual inspection of the funnel plots revealed an asymmetric distribution. This implies that potential bias cannot be entirely ruled out, as negative outcomes are less likely to enter the public domain. Such selective reporting may have inflated effect estimates, particularly among smaller studies with methodological limitations and/or absence of registered null-result trials; The included RCTs have heterogeneity in drinking water experiment scores and SSA scores in depression fields. Despite a comprehensive investigation of potential heterogeneity sources, it is important to note that confounding statistical outcomes due to heterogeneity may not be eliminated; Only one trial reported adverse events and few safety data were exhibited, so follow-up studies are needed to further examine the risk of scalp acupuncture treatment; Although no language restrictions were imposed, it is noteworthy that the included studies were exclusively conducted in China, which may introduce language or regional bias. More high-quality RCTs are still needed to provide more robust evidence for evidence-based medicine in the future.

5. Conclusion

20 articles were included in this review, including 1278 patients. The results suggest that the treatment of post-stroke swallowing disorder appears to be beneficial for the clinical efficacy, WST, SSA, and VDS scores in patients with post-stroke dysphagia. However, due to the high risk of bias and heterogeneity among the included RCTs, these findings require confirmation through rigorously designed, large-scale, and multi-center RCTs.
