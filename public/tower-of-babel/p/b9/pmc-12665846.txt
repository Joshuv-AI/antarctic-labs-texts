Left Ventricular Pseudoaneurysm Following Atrioventricular Groove Disruption in a Patient With Native Valve Endocarditis
Authors: Boateng Kubi, Nikhil Panda, Brian Kinsman, Eriberto Michel, Thoralf Sundt, Antonia Kreso
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeft ventricular (LV) pseudoaneurysm is a rare and life-threatening sequela of atrioventricular (AV) groove disruption.Case SummaryA 73-year-old woman with giant cell arteritis on immunosuppressive therapy presented with dyspnea. Blood cultures revealed Enterococcus faecalis native mitral and aortic valve endocarditis with severe mitral and aortic regurgitation. She underwent bioprosthetic aortic and mitral valve replacement. History and Physical Examination

A 73-year-old woman with a history of giant cell arteritis, on immunosuppressive therapy (tocilizumab and prednisone), and deep vein thrombosis, on apixaban, presented to an outside hospital with severe dyspnea. Initial evaluation with computed tomography pulmonary angiography revealed pulmonary edema and no evidence of pulmonary embolism. Blood cultures grew Enterococcus faecalis, and a transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrated vegetations on the aortic and mitral valves consistent with infectious endocarditis, with severe aortic and mitral regurgitation (MR). She was transferred to our hospital and, given her presentation with florid heart failure, was scheduled for urgent surgical management.

One day after discharge from that admission, she returned to the emergency department with an asymptomatic conversion pause after atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response. On examination, her vital signs were notable for a heart rate of 92 beats/min, a blood pressure of 110/59 mm Hg, and an oxygen saturation of 99% on 2 L of oxygen. Cardiac examination revealed a regular rhythm without murmurs on auscultation. No peripheral edema was noted, and her lungs were clear to auscultation bilaterally.

Past Medical History

The patient carried a diagnosis of giant cell arteritis, for which she was receiving immunosuppressive therapy with tocilizumab and prednisone. She also had a history of ischemic colitis and asthma managed with medical therapy and deep vein thrombosis managed with apixaban.

Differential Diagnosis

On initial presentation of the patient with symptoms of heart failure, positive blood culture results, and echocardiographic findings of aortic and mitral valve vegetations, the diagnosis of infective endocarditis was made. On her readmission, the differential diagnosis included bradyarrhythmia secondary to iatrogenic atrioventricular (AV) block, progressive heart failure, and persistent endocarditis. Ultimately, the discovery of the LV pseudoaneurysm at the AV groove on cross-sectional imaging offered the definitive diagnosis.

Investigations

During her index operation, initial investigations included a computed tomography pulmonary angiography, which ruled out pulmonary embolism; transthoracic echocardiogram, which raised concern for infective endocarditis; and transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE), which demonstrated severe MR and aortic regurgitation (Videos 1 and 2). Blood culture results were positive for E. faecalis, confirming the diagnosis of infectious endocarditis requiring surgical management.

After her initial surgical course, she presented with paroxysms of rapid atrial fibrillation terminating in prolonged conversion pauses (Figure 1). The electrocardiogram on arrival showed sinus rhythm with premature atrial complexes. The chest radiograph demonstrated clear lung fields with small bilateral pleural effusions. Given the concern for sinus node dysfunction, she was evaluated by electrophysiology for potential leadless pacemaker placement. A cardiac-gated computed tomography angiography (CTA) was recommended for preprocedural planning. CTA revealed a 5.4 × 3.4 × 2.4 cm serpentine pseudoaneurysm arising from the left posterior AV groove with a 10-mm defect size immediately lateral to the mitral annulus (Figure 2). Intraoperative TEE confirmed the presence of the pseudoaneurysm and noted that both prostheses were well seated with no paravalvular regurgitation (Videos 3 and 4).

Management

During her index hospitalization, the patient was treated with intravenous diuretics for heart failure and antibiotics targeting enterococci. Given the acuity of MR and AR coupled with her severe symptoms, she underwent urgent aortic valve replacement and mitral valve replacement. In the operating room, she was found to have friable tissue with extensive vegetation on all leaflets of the aortic valve and both anterior and posterior mitral valve leaflets, extending into the subvalvular apparatus (Figure 3). She underwent bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement with a 23-mm Inspiris RESILIA valve (Edwards Lifesciences) and mitral valve replacement with a 31-mm Epic Plus mitral valve (Abbott). She experienced severe bleeding from the posterior aspect of the heart, necessitating venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation for circulatory support and delayed chest closure. Patch repair of AV groove bleeding was not attempted because of her severely friable tissue quality.

When she returned with the LV pseudoaneurysm, the operative approach was through the fifth interspace using a left posterolateral thoracotomy. Femoral arterial and venous cannulation was used for cardiopulmonary bypass, and she was cooled to 28 °C to achieve fibrillatory arrest. Exposure of the AV groove and pseudoaneurysm was achieved by opening the pericardium longitudinally, preserving the phrenic nerve. The large pseudoaneurysm sac was identified in the posterior aspect of the LV, with the neck of the pseudoaneurysm located at the AV groove (Figure 4). Exploration confirmed a small defect caudal to the mitral valve prosthesis, through which blood was entering the pseudoaneurysm. Given the small size of the defect, a patch was deferred, and primary closure was achieved using felt reinforcement and multiple pledgeted horizontal mattress sutures. These sutures were meticulously placed from the healthy myocardium, passing through the pseudoaneurysm sac, the sewing ring of the mitral valve prosthesis, and back through the sac into the surrounding healthy myocardial tissue. Once hemostasis was confirmed, the pseudoaneurysm sac was oversewn. Post-repair, TEE demonstrated complete closure of the pseudoaneurysm with no residual flow (Video 5). The patient was weaned off bypass, and the thoracotomy incision was closed in layers.

Outcome and Follow-Up

After the successful repair of the LV pseudoaneurysm, the patient was extubated on postoperative day 0 and developed a slow junctional rhythm requiring temporary pacing. By postoperative day 3, she was transferred out of the intensive care unit. On postoperative day 6, she underwent placement of a leadless atrial pacemaker complicated by postprocedure stress cardiomyopathy, evidenced by a reduced ejection fraction (31%) on repeat echocardiography. She was managed with guideline-directed medical therapy.1 Her clinical status gradually improved, and she was discharged back to inpatient rehabilitation on postoperative day 13. At her most recent follow-up, she was living independently with no recurrence of the pseudoaneurysm and a recovered ejection fraction of 60% on echocardiography.

Discussion

Persistent bleeding in the posterior aspect of the heart after mitral surgery is concerning for AV groove disruption. Risk factors for AV groove disruption include heavy vegetation burden, annular tissue destruction, and underlying fragility from infection or immunosuppression. In such high-risk cases, prophylactic reinforcement of the posterior annulus (such as patch placement) to reduce tension on the AV groove can be considered. Implanting smaller prosthetic valves may also reduce mechanical stress on the region. Intraoperatively, excessive rotation or elevation of the heart can exacerbate AV groove injury and should be avoided. When AV groove disruption is suspected, the typical approach is to re-arrest the heart, explant the prosthesis, and patch repair the posterior annulus. Given our patient’s frail tissue, our atypical strategy avoided a potentially high-risk operation.

Pseudoaneurysms of the LV are rare but life-threatening complications of cardiac surgery, often occurring due to myocardial tissue fragility.2,3 In this patient, the combination of immunosuppressive therapy and severe infective endocarditis likely contributed to her weakened myocardial tissue, predisposing her to pseudoaneurysm formation after AV groove separation. LV pseudoaneurysm is typically diagnosed through advanced imaging modalities such as TEE or CTA, which can delineate the false aneurysm sac. This sac is formed when cardiac rupture is contained by adjacent pericardium or scar tissue in the absence of healthy myocardial tissue.4 This patient’s pseudoaneurysm was identified after a new conduction abnormality, but other symptoms such as dyspnea, heart failure, or murmurs can occur.5

Surgical repair of the LV pseudoaneurysm is the definitive treatment but can be technically challenging when located near the AV groove.6 Repair can be performed from an internal (endocardial) approach through the left atrium or from an epicardial approach without incising the heart chambers.7 An internal approach offers superior visualization of the subannular structures and allows simultaneous correction of any associated intracardiac abnormalities. This method is particularly advantageous if there is suspicion of subvalvular disruption or compromise of the implanted prosthesis.7 In contrast, an epicardial approach, as used in our case, can be performed without disrupting the cardiac chambers, reducing the risk of injuring the implanted prosthesis or the left circumflex coronary artery. By accessing the pseudoaneurysm directly through a left thoracotomy, the need for a repeat sternotomy and extensive adhesiolysis can be potentially avoided. Limitations of the epicardial approach include inadequate exposure of the subannular apparatus, which in our case did not require exploration. In addition, although the left thoracotomy permits good visualization, options for aortic cross-clamping and cardioplegia delivery are limited. For this patient, the thickened epicardial tissue around the pseudoaneurysm was strong enough to secure pledgeted horizontal mattress sutures, ensuring a durable closure without risking extensive manipulation or injury to the coronary sinus.

Conclusions

This case report underscores the importance of recognizing and managing AV groove disruption and LV pseudoaneurysm, a rare but potentially fatal complication of valve surgery in patients with friable cardiac tissue. Early recognition, accurate diagnosis, and timely surgical intervention are essential for favorable outcomes.
