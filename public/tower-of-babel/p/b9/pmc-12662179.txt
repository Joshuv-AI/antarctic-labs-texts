Estimating cognitive decline in longitudinal studies with high mortality and a long interwave period: A comparison of approaches and considerations around integrating end‐of‐life interview data
Authors: Emma Nichols, Alden L Gross, Eleanor Hayes‐Larson, Erik Meijer, Lindsay C Kobayashi, Jinkook Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONSelective attrition due to interwave mortality can lead to bias in studies of cognitive decline. Long interwave intervals exacerbate challenges.METHODSUsing the two waves of the Longitudinal Aging Study in India–Diagnostic Assessment of Dementia (LASI‐DAD) (N = 3546, from 2017–2019 to 2022–2024), we compared approaches to account for interwave mortality in estimating cognitive decline across age groups, including inverse probability weighting, joint modeling, and imputation of cognition before death using end‐of‐life interview data.RESULTSAll approaches produced steeper estimates of cognitive decline compared to estimates that did not account for interwave mortality. Imputations combined with joint modeling led to the steepest estimates of decline in the 60 to 69 and 70 to 79 age groups (22.0% and 35.9% steeper than the base model not considering mortality, respectively).DISCUSSIONFindings showcase the importance of accounting for interwave mortality and highlight the utility of imputation combined with joint models for studies with long interwave periods.Highlights All approaches to accounting for interwave mortality led to steeper estimates of cognitive decline than approaches ignoring mortality.We developed adjustments to end‐of‐life cognition data to account for potential reporting bias.Imputations based on end‐of‐life data helped account for cognitive decline after the last study interview.Combining imputations from end‐of‐life data with a joint model led to the steepest estimates of cognitive decline in two of the three age groups.Accounting for interwave mortality and considering cognitive decline between waves is important in studies with long interwave intervals. Keywords: cognition, dementia, longitudinal, measurement, mortality, selection bias

BACKGROUND

Expected trends in population aging are projected to lead to a tripling in the number of individuals impacted by dementia in the coming decades. 1 Epidemiologic research on cognitive aging can further our understanding of the causes and consequences of dementia and serves a critical role in informing the design of prevention strategies. However, measuring and studying cognitive aging in epidemiologic research is challenging. Cross‐sectional studies using cognitive functioning as the study outcome may risk conflating variation in cognition due to dementia with variation in cognition due to education or other factors that can influence cognitive performance independently of neurodegenerative diseases. 2 , 3 , 4 In contrast, longitudinal studies can provide measures of within‐person cognitive decline, which are more closely related to brain aging, 5 , 6 and may control for differences by education and other factors that affect the measurement of cognitive performance but may be less likely to affect the measurement of cognitive change. 7

In longitudinal studies, estimates of cognitive change would ideally be based on visits at least every 2 to 3 years, allowing close monitoring of the study sample over time. However, such short interwave intervals are not always feasible, particularly when data collection requires large‐scale field operations in logistically challenging environments. For example, interwave intervals for new nationally representative studies using the Harmonized Cognitive Assessment Protocol (HCAP) across countries including the USA, 8 Mexico, 9 England, 10 India, 11 and Europe 12 tend to be 4 to 6 years following the initial wave of data collection. 13 These long interwave intervals pose challenges given higher rates of attrition due to mortality, especially in lower‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) with high background mortality rates among older adults.

When those who are more likely to experience cognitive decline are also more likely to drop out from the study, this can lead to bias in estimates of cognitive decline. When attrition is differential by both the exposure and the outcome, it can also introduce bias in studies of risk factor associations. Common approaches to account for interwave mortality, such as inverse probability weighting (IPW) and joint models for longitudinal and time‐to‐event data, can help account for potential bias in substantive analyses. 14 , 15 These methods have been applied in dementia research, but existing applications have largely focused on studies with annual or biannual visits. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 Longer interwave intervals may lead to worse model performance because these approaches fail to capture cognitive change that may occur during the longer time interval between the last study visit and death. In studies with long interwave intervals, incorporation of data on cognitive status before death from end‐of‐life interviews with family members or friends of deceased respondents may help address this issue.

In this study, we used HCAP data from the two waves available from the Longitudinal Aging Study in India – Diagnostic Assessment of Dementia (LASI‐DAD) to develop a novel approach to imputing cognitive status before death using data from end‐of‐life interviews, accounting for potential reporting biases. We further compared the use of these imputations to existing approaches that account for interwave mortality (e.g., IPW and joint models) and an approach combining the imputations with a joint model in a study setting with high interwave mortality and a long interwave interval.

METHODS

Study sample

The LASI‐DAD is a substudy of the LASI, which includes a nationally representative sample of over 73,000 respondents 45 years and older. 11 LASI collects information on a broad range of demographic, socioeconomic, and health related factors, 19 whereas LASI‐DAD focuses on cognitive aging and dementia. LASI‐DAD is also nationally representative and includes adults 60 years and older. LASI‐DAD administered an adapted version of the HCAP protocol, adjusting the included cognitive tests where needed given cultural factors as well as low levels of literacy and numeracy. LASI‐DAD initially sampled 4096 participants for its baseline wave from 2017 to 2019. One follow‐up wave (2022 to 2024) has been conducted. All respondents provided informed consent (written or thumbprint). Study protocols were approved by the Indian Council of Medical Research (2202‐16741/F1) and collaborating institutions (Appendix S1).

To simplify analyses, given our goal of comparing approaches to account for mortality‐related attrition, we first excluded those who were lost to follow‐up between LASI‐DAD waves for non‐mortality reasons (N = 550; N = 225 lost to follow‐up and N = 325 refusals), yielding a final sample size of N = 3546 (flowchart in Appendix S2).

Measurement of cognition and daily functioning

The HCAP battery used in the LASI‐DAD study included assessments of memory, executive functioning, language, and visuospatial functioning (list in Appendix S3). Confirmatory factor analysis was used to estimate latent general cognitive functioning separately at each wave while considering adjustments to the battery between waves to improve performance. 20 This approach results in separate scores at each wave, calibrated to be on the same scale.

The HCAP battery also included an informant interview, in which a family member or friend provided information on the cognitive functioning of the respondent in the context of everyday activities. The informant interview included the Jorm Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly (IQCODE) 21 and the informant portion of the Blessed Dementia Scale, divided into questions on Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) (e.g., toileting, dressing) and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADLs) (e.g., coping with money, remembering shopping lists). 22

Measurement of death and cognition before death

LASI‐DAD conducted end‐of‐life interviews with family members or friends after respondents died to collect information on the date of death (details in Appendix S4) and informant‐reported cognition prior to death using the IQCODE and Blessed ADL and IADL scales. Informant questionnaires on cognition before death asked about respondents’ status 1 month prior to death to avoid the potential influence of delirium and other factors associated with terminal decline. 23

Measurement of other variables

To reduce risk of bias (e.g., age heaping 24 ) in self‐reported age, age at baseline was calculated from self‐reported month and year of birth and interview date. In models to estimate inverse probability weights and to impute cognitive functioning, we considered a range of variables on demographics, socioeconomic status, health behaviors, and health conditions (full list in Appendix 4).

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive statistics to characterize the sample overall and separately for those who died between waves and those who participated in wave 2. The primary analysis aim was the comparison of approaches for the consideration of mortality (Section 2.5.3). Prior to this comparison, we developed imputations for cognition before death (Section 2.5.2). The development of these imputations required describing and applying adjustments to the end‐of‐life data (Section 2.5.1).

Challenges with end‐of‐life data and development of data adjustments

We characterized the end‐of‐life data by comparing informant‐reported cognition data from the end‐of‐life interviews to prior observations of informant‐reported cognition in wave 1. We examined the proportion of respondents who improved versus declined on these informant measures across different groups defined by the interval of time between the wave 1 interview and death (we assigned the date as 1 month prior to death, given this is the time for which informants report on cognitive status).

We assumed that any increases in performance on informant measures versus wave 1 interviews, especially for short time intervals between wave 1 and death were suggestive of reporting bias. To account for this possibility, we developed adjustment terms to correct for reporting bias. Adjustment terms represent the hypothetical model‐implied difference in, for example, IQCODE score, between informant reports for someone still alive versus after death. To estimate adjustment terms, we used linear regression models to predict the difference between informant reports in wave 1 and informant reports from end‐of‐life data for those who died between waves as a function of a smooth spline (natural cubic spline with three degrees of freedom) on the time difference between wave 1 and death. Models also adjusted for age, gender, and informant relation at both wave 1 and exit interview (spouse/child[‐in‐law] or grandchild/other relative/other/missing), as prior research showed that informant characteristics could impact informant reports. 25 We used these models to predict the difference between wave 1 informant reports and end‐of‐life informant reports for a time difference of 0, representing the hypothetical scenario where these two informant reports (one while the respondent was alive, one after they died) refer to an individual's status at the same time and therefore should be the same in the absence of reporting bias. This prediction allowed us to estimate adjustment terms that isolated differences in informant reports due to their perspective reporting on a decedent rather than due to true change over time following disease progression. Adjustment terms for the Blessed IADLs scale were based on a smaller sample size (N = 556), due to the introduction of this scale in the second phase of wave 1 data collection.

We developed four versions of end‐of‐life data based on the estimated adjustment terms. To apply the adjustments, we subtracted the estimated adjustment terms from the scores on the end‐of‐life informant reports. The four data versions were as follows: (1) no adjustment for bias in end‐of‐life interview data, (2) adjustment for bias in IQCODE data from the end‐of‐life interviews, (3) adjustment for bias in IQCODE and Blessed (IADLs) data from the end‐of‐life informant interviews, and (4) adjustment for bias in the IQCODE, Blessed (IADLs), and Blessed (ADLs) informant scales.

Development of imputations for cognition before death

We developed a prediction model to impute cognition before death using data from the end‐of‐life interview. The most important predictors were the end‐of‐life informant reports, as they were the only source of data with direct information about cognitive functioning before death. We also included a small set of additional demographic predictors (Appendix S4) in addition to wave 1 cognitive status and informant gender, relation (spouse/child[‐in‐law] or grandchild/other relative/other/missing), and frequency of contact (co‐resident/daily/several times per week to once per week/1 to 3 times per month or less).

We used data from wave 2 to estimate the prediction model, given the availability of all predictors of interest (i.e., wave 1 measures and informant reports from wave 2) as well as contemporaneous measures of cognition. We then applied this prediction model to the four versions of the end‐of‐life data that use different bias adjustments to account for potential reporting bias in end‐of‐life informant reports of cognition. The application of this prediction model to the four versions of end‐of‐life data resulted in four different sets of imputations. To ensure complete data on predictors of interest, we used multiple imputation with chained equations (MICE) 26 (m = 15 imputed datasets) prior to estimating and applying the prediction model to impute cognitive functioning before death (flowchart in Appendix S5).

Comparison of different approaches for considering mortality

To compare different approaches to considering mortality in analyses of decline, we used a simple case study – the estimation of cognitive decline stratified across three age groups (60 to 69, 70 to 79, and 80+). All models used the previously estimated cognitive factor scores from confirmatory factor analysis as the outcome of interest. We examined three age groups to account for non‐linear decline with age and to observe how differences in the background mortality rates would impact the implemented approaches. We used an unweighted, unadjusted generalized estimating equation (GEE) model with an exchangeable correlation structure to estimate a base model without accounting for or considering interwave mortality. We visualized model predictions with raw data using spaghetti plots. Though we chose to rely on GEE models as the single base model condition, we acknowledge that mixed‐effects models are also a common modeling approach for longitudinal data, with a slightly different structure and interpretation. 27 , 28 However, we show that estimates from mixed‐effects models are similar to those from GEE models (Appendix S6) and therefore choose to present only GEE estimates in primary analyses for simplicity.

We compared our base model for estimating cognitive decline by age group to results from four different approaches to accounting for interwave mortality:

IPW: We used a GEE model with an exchangeable correlation structure and IPW for attrition due to mortality. 16 We included a wide range of variables in IPW models, including demographics (e.g., age, state, gender), socioeconomic indicators (e.g., education, per‐capita consumption quintile), health status indicators (e.g., depressive symptoms, systolic blood pressure), and indicators of cognitive status (e.g., wave 1 general cognitive functioning, clinical dementia rating) (full list in Appendix S4). Details of the IPW modeling process are available in Appendix S7.

IPW: We used a GEE model with an exchangeable correlation structure and IPW for attrition due to mortality. 16 We included a wide range of variables in IPW models, including demographics (e.g., age, state, gender), socioeconomic indicators (e.g., education, per‐capita consumption quintile), health status indicators (e.g., depressive symptoms, systolic blood pressure), and indicators of cognitive status (e.g., wave 1 general cognitive functioning, clinical dementia rating) (full list in Appendix S4). Details of the IPW modeling process are available in Appendix S7.

Joint models: We used joint models for longitudinal change in cognition and survival, which link two submodels (mixed‐effects regression with random intercepts and a survival model) through the use of the current value of the longitudinal cognitive decline process as a linear predictor in the survival model. 29

Imputation: We used a GEE model with an exchangeable correlation structure, further including additional data on imputed cognition from end‐of‐life interviews for those who died. We estimated models with versions of imputations with varying adjustments of informant reports as described in Section 2.5.2.

Imputation: We used a GEE model with an exchangeable correlation structure, further including additional data on imputed cognition from end‐of‐life interviews for those who died. We estimated models with versions of imputations with varying adjustments of informant reports as described in Section 2.5.2.

Joint models with imputed data: We added imputed data from end‐of‐life interviews to the longitudinal cognition submodel in the joint modeling framework. Here, we selected the no‐adjustment and IQCODE‐adjusted versions of imputations as the two imputation versions best supported by the available evidence and theoretical explanations of potential reporting bias patterns.

Joint models with imputed data: We added imputed data from end‐of‐life interviews to the longitudinal cognition submodel in the joint modeling framework. Here, we selected the no‐adjustment and IQCODE‐adjusted versions of imputations as the two imputation versions best supported by the available evidence and theoretical explanations of potential reporting bias patterns.

Given the number of models and comparisons included in the analysis, we focused the interpretation of findings on broad‐scale patterns across models rather than individual model results. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.1. We used mice for multiple imputation 26 and JM for joint modeling. 29

RESULTS

Study sample

The LASI‐DAD sample in this analysis included 3546 older adults (mean age: 69.8, standard deviation [SD]: 7.6), and approximately half the respondents were women (53.4%) (Table 1). Almost half of the sample had no formal education between (49.7%) and 66.4% were from disadvantaged caste groups. Of the 3546 respondents, 980 (27.6%) died between waves. At wave 1, those who died were older (died: 73.9 years [SD 8.9] vs observed in wave 2: 68.3 [SD 6.4]) and had lower cognitive scores (died: −0.4 SD units [SD 1.0] vs wave 2: 0.1 [SD 0.9]) as well as greater informant‐reported impairment (e.g., IQCODE – died: 3.6 [SD 0.6] vs wave 2: 3.4 [SD 0.4]). Those who died between waves died an average of 2.6 years following their wave 1 interview, whereas wave 2 visits were an average of 4.6 years after wave 1.

Cognitive decline by age group

Without accounting for interwave mortality, we estimated the rate of cognitive decline as 0.020 SD units/year (95% confidence interval [CI] from 0.014 to 0.025) for those 60 to 69 years of age, 0.029 SD units/year (95% CI 0.021 to 0.038) for those 70 to 79 years of age, and 0.054 SD units/year (95% CI 0.036 to 0.072) for those 80+ years of age (Figure 1).

End‐of‐life data and imputations of cognition

Comparing informant reports from wave 1 to informant reports from end‐of‐life data, a sizable portion of informants reported improvements in the end‐of‐life interviews (i.e., less impairment), though findings differed across scales (Figure 2). Overall, the proportion of respondents with improvements varied from 9.5% for the Blessed ADLs to 39.6% for the Blessed IADLs and 54.9% for the IQCODE. There was also variation by the timing of death relative to the wave 1 interview, with a greater proportion with improvements and a lower proportion with worsening impairment for deaths closer in time to wave 1 interview. The extrapolated prediction of the difference between end‐of‐life and wave 1 reports happening at the same time (i.e., time elapsed of 0), which we interpret as the potential bias due to conducting informant reports after death, was −0.23 points for the IQCODE and −0.14 points for Blessed IADLs, though this difference was not statistically significant for Blessed IADLs. Although models suggested a bias toward greater versus less impairment in end‐of‐life data (0.23 units) for Blessed ADLs, this difference could alternatively be explained by sharp expected increases in impairment or disability immediately prior to death and may not be captured well by the modeled extrapolation.

Compared to those who survived to wave 2, those who died had lower cognitive functioning and steeper declines in cognition based on imputed cognition prior to death, although uncertainty was large for those who died, and confidence intervals overlapped (Figure 3). There were some differences across imputation versions with different adjustments, though the magnitude of differences was small. For example, among those aged 70 to 79 years, the estimated cognitive slope among those who died was −0.052 (95% CI −0.077 to −0.027) for imputations with no adjustment, −0.061 (95% CI −0.085 to −0.037) for imputations after adjusting the IQCODE, −0.062 (−0.088 to −0.037) for imputations after adjusting the IQCODE and Blessed IADLs, and −0.057 (−0.084 to −0.031) for imputations after adjusting the IQCODE and Blessed IADLs and ADLs.

Comparison of approaches accounting for interwave mortality

There was meaningful variation across different models accounting for interwave mortality (Table 2, Appendix S8). Across imputation versions, applying the IQCODE adjustment only led to the greatest estimates of cognitive decline. Adjustments from applying the joint model and imputations separately were similar, but combining the two approaches led to larger estimates of decline. Across all approaches, though there was some variation across age groups, the inverse probability weighted estimates (60 to 69: 9.3%; 70 to 79: 25.1%; 80+: 42.2%) and the joint model with IQCODE‐adjusted imputations (60 to 69: 22.0%; 70 to 79: 35.9%; 80+: 36.2%) had the greatest strengthening of slope estimates compared to the base model that did not account for interwave mortality. However, the variability of estimates, as quantified by the percentage increase in the width of the confidence intervals for a given estimate compared to the base model, was largest for IPW‐based estimates (60 to 69: 4.7%; 70 to 79: 25.6%; 80+: 26.7%) and lower for other approaches, such as imputation with IQCODE adjustment (60 to 69: 5.1%; 70 to 79: 2.6%; 80+: 2.2%). Though there were increases in variability when combining imputations with the joint modeling framework, the percentage increase in the width of the confidence interval was still considerably smaller than with IPW for the 70 to 79 age group (IPW: 25.6%, joint model + imputations with IQCODE adjustment: 14.1%) and 80+ age group (IPW: 26.7%, joint model + imputations with IQCODE adjustment: 6.5%).

DISCUSSION

In a high mortality setting with a long interwave interval, accounting for interwave mortality between the first two waves produced steeper estimates of cognitive decline across all approaches considered, though the magnitude of differences varied by age group and modeling approach. While patterns in informant reports of cognition from end‐of‐life data suggested potential reporting bias, the incorporation of imputations based on these data led to steeper estimates of cognitive decline, with only minor variations depending on the choice of adjustment. In the 60 to 69 and 70 to 79 age groups, which include the majority of LASI‐DAD respondents, the combination of using imputations with a joint model led to the steepest estimates of cognitive decline.

Our general finding that accounting for interwave mortality led to steeper estimates of cognitive decline is in line with prior research on the application of methods such as IPW and joint modeling. 15 , 16 , 30 Though prior simulation research directly comparing joint models with IPW‐based estimates found that joint models more accurately estimated cognitive decline than IPW‐based approaches, the number of predictors included in IPW estimation was limited. 15 In comparison, current analyses included a large range of predictors in IPW models and were therefore more likely to account for potential proxies for unmeasured factors associated with both cognitive decline and survival.

Few existing studies used imputation approaches to account for bias due to interwave mortality, and methodological differences make comparisons challenging. Research in the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities Study used multiple imputation to impute cognition before death for those who died during a 12‐year gap in data collection. 31 Using informative auxiliary data from telephone assessments of cognition, hospital records, and a proxy‐based clinical dementia rating score, estimates of cognitive decline were stronger after imputing cognition 6 months before death among those who died, 31 aligning with our results. In contrast, multiple imputation efforts in the Virginia Cognitive Aging Project showed that imputation of cognition for who left the study for any reason did not impact estimates of cognitive change, though only a small proportion of the sample died and these imputations were based only on prior cognitive performance, age, and personality characteristics. 32

In the current study, imputations of cognitive functioning were primarily based on informant reports of cognitive and daily functioning before death from end‐of‐life interviews. However, quantitative evidence that more respondents improved than declined on some informant scales during the time between wave 1 and death raised concerns about the potential for reporting bias. Prior research has suggested that informants may be able to report on the presence or absence of chronic diseases or mobility difficulties after death, but there may be greater potential for reporting bias in more subjective assessments of cognitive and mental health symptoms after death. 33 , 34 , 35 This aligns with our finding that evidence of bias was stronger for informant scales with more subjective assessments (e.g., IQCODE) than for the Blessed ADLs scale, which assesses more concrete limitations on dressing or bathing. Evidence from cognitive psychology suggests that processes around memory are impacted by emotion and experiences with bereavement, 36 and more subjective evaluations may be more susceptible to such memory effects. Though one study on dementia symptom scales found no evidence of systematic bias comparing family caregivers with nurses, this small‐scale (48 participants) study was conducted in the Netherlands, and differences in cultural attitudes between the Netherlands and India are likely important. 37 Cultural norms around respect for elders in India 38 may have a greater impact on reports after death in this context, particularly when informants are children or children‐in‐law.

In the current study, we addressed the potential for reporting bias by estimating adjustments based on the model‐implied difference in reports for the hypothetical scenario where wave 1 reports and end‐of‐life reports occurred at the same time. Due to uncertainty about which scales should be adjusted, we computed a range of estimates based on unadjusted data and different sets of adjustments. We generally found that estimates of cognitive decline did not vary substantially across adjustments. However, because the computed adjustment was not statistically significant for Blessed IADLs and that the positive adjustment for Blessed ADLs may be due to sharp increases in impairment in the months prior to death that are not well captured by model extrapolation, we feel that adjusting the data only for potential bias in the IQCODE is most appropriate. Based on this evaluation, we included only two versions of imputations in the combined joint modeling and imputation approach: imputations without any adjustments and imputations adjusted for IQCODE. Future research should seek to further refine the informant questionnaires to assess whether adjustments to the questions or protocol could produce more accurate information.

In comparing different methodological approaches for considering interwave mortality, it is important to understand potential differences in the interpretation of estimates from alternative models. IPW‐based approaches such as the one implemented in the current study reweight survivors to represent the full population. This approach has been criticized in the literature for effectively creating a hypothetical immortal population. 39 , 40 However, under additional assumptions, prior work showed that IPW‐based estimates were closely tied mathematically to the survival average causal effect, or the effect of the exposure for individuals who would have survived regardless of exposure status. 41 In contrast, subject‐specific joint models estimate “partly conditional” effects, or effects conditional on respondents being alive, even when dropout is conditional on the true longitudinal trajectory up until the survival time. 40 , 42 This difference in interpretation may help explain observed differences in estimates for our IPW and joint modeling approaches. However, both IPW and joint modeling approaches are biased if common causes of the longitudinal process and survival routinely emerge following the last observation (e.g., a stroke following the last visit that led to cognitive decline and death before the next wave), which may be the case in studies with long interwave intervals, such as LASI‐DAD. Unlike IPW or joint modeling approaches, in which accounting for mortality alters the interpretation of the estimate or population to which it applies, the imputation‐based approach seeks to address bias by estimating decline after the last study observation but prior to death. By combining imputation with joint modeling, it is possible to combine all the benefits of the joint model with an approach to account for cognitive decline after the last study observation.

Findings from the current study highlight the importance of paying careful attention to the implications of mortality for estimates of cognitive change, particularly in studies with high mortality and long interwave intervals. Given differences in interpretation across analysis methods, 14 , 43 careful consideration should be given to choosing the most appropriate estimand, though comparisons of effect estimates across methods may also be informative. Our finding that the combination of imputation from end‐of‐life interviews with joint modeling resulted in the steepest estimates of the decline for the majority of the sample suggests that the combination of these two approaches may be particularly fruitful for use in future research. Nonetheless, findings also highlighted limitations with reports from end‐of‐life interviews and emphasize the need for additional research exploring ways to improve the quality of this information.

Limitations of the current study should be considered. First, because the analysis used cohort data from the LASI‐DAD study, true underlying rates of cognitive decline in the population were unknown. In comparisons, we assumed that methods producing steeper rates of cognitive decline better accounted for mortality given the close link between cognitive decline and death. 44 , 45 While using LASI‐DAD data allowed for the description and development of approaches to address realistic challenges, such as the potential reporting biases in end‐of‐life data, future work using simulation analysis could complement the current results by exploring the performance of the included approaches under different assumptions about the underlying structure of associations and the available data. We note that our adjustments for potential reporting biases in end‐of‐life data were based on theoretical considerations and data for validation was not available. Instead, we used comparisons across four potential adjustments to convey the potential sensitivity of results to different choices in modeling adjustment terms. Measurement error in self‐reported data (e.g., birth date used to calculate age, ADLs) could also lead to bias but would be less likely to impact comparisons between methods. Additionally, the LASI‐DAD study currently has only two waves of data, making the estimation of decline more difficult and more susceptible to the effects of measurement error at either time point. 46 Further extensions of the current work should evaluate comparisons with a larger number of waves and longer follow‐up.

Additionally, the current analysis focuses on the estimation of rates of cognitive decline, but the extent to which comparisons across approaches observed in the current analysis may hold in studies focused on the more common goal of estimating the association between an exposure and cognitive decline is unclear. Prior research comparing methods that do not account for mortality with IPW and joint models has largely found that differences between the different approaches tend to be small, 15 , 47 , 48 , 49 with some exceptions. 16 However, future research should explore these comparisons in settings with long interwave intervals and high mortality and should incorporate imputation‐based approaches into comparisons as well. Furthermore, we simplified models by excluding those who left the study for non‐mortality reasons; additional research could also consider integrating adjustments to account for other sources of attrition.

Overall, our findings showcase the importance and challenges associated with accounting for interwave mortality in studies with high mortality rates and long interwave intervals. Differences in interpretation warrant careful attention when choosing an appropriate analysis method for a specific research question, 14 , 43 though in some cases comparisons of effect estimates across methods may also be informative. While we highlighted the possibility for reporting biases in end‐of‐life data, results also illustrated the potential utility of these data in imputation‐based approaches to account for mortality, either on their own or in conjunction with joint modeling.
