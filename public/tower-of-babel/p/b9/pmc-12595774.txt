Moral conflicts in home-based care: a meta-synthesis of qualitative research
Authors: Qi Fan He, Xiao Yan Chen, Jing Yan Cai, Lei Wang, Fang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs the global population continues to age, home-based care has gained increasing popularity as a way to address the growing need for long-term care among older adults. At the same time, healthcare and social care providers working in home care settings are experiencing a rise in ethical conflicts. This qualitative meta-synthesis aims to explore the ethical conflicts experienced by healthcare and social care providers in home-based care, offering insights and guidance for resolving moral dilemmas in this setting.MethodsQualitative systematic review utilizing meta-ethnography. A systematic search was conducted across four databases (Web of Science, Embase, MEDLINE, and CINAHL) from their inception through April 2025. Two reviewers independently screened articles against the eligibility criteria, appraised them using the JBI critical appraisal tools, and extracted qualitative data in duplicate. 16 studies met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed using thematic synthesis.ResultsThe sixteen included studies were synthesized into three main themes and seven subthemes. The three main themes were Paternalism versus Respect for Autonomy, Blurred Boundaries, and Shortcomings of the Health System.ConclusionOur findings reveal that healthcare and social care providers experience diverse forms of moral conflict during home care; these experiences jeopardize their physical and mental well-being and urgently call for targeted support and intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-025-01303-4.Keywords: Moral conflict, Home-based care, Meta-synthesis, Healthcare providers, Nurses

Introduction

By 2050, the proportion of people aged 60 years and older worldwide is projected to nearly double, rising from 12.0% in 2015 to 22.0% [1]. This demographic shift has redirected the focus of care from hospitals and long-term care facilities to home and community settings [2]. Compared with institutional care, domiciliary care offers several advantages, including greater autonomy and the ability for individuals to remain in their own homes, which more closely reflects their values and preferences [3]. However, the growing reliance on home care also exposes healthcare and social care providers to a range of ethical conflicts and moral dilemmas, such as patient privacy issues, assuming greater responsibilities, and managing complex relationships with patients and their families. These ethical challenges can contribute to compassion fatigue among healthcare professionals, ultimately compromising the quality of patient care and emerging as a critical issue in healthcare [4, 5].

In circumstances of ethical conflict, individuals may encounter what Jameton terms “moral distress,” a state of emotional and psychological turmoil arising when a person recognizes the ethically appropriate course of action but cannot carry it out due to various constraints [6]. Building on Carnevale’s conceptual and moral analysis of suffering [7], moral suffering can be understood as a broader construct that not only encompasses moral distress but also includes the profound experiences of anguish arising from moral conflict, the violation of personal values, and threats to one’s sense of meaning in life. In this framework, moral conflicts constitute the situational context, whereas moral suffering and moral distress represent the outcomes of confronting such conflicts. As the definition of moral distress has evolved over time, this paper adopts the definition proposed by Julia Petersen [8], which is “psychological response to morally challenging situations.”

In previous review, we found only two studies examining ethical challenges in home-based care. However, one [9] was outdated and thus provided limited reference value. The other review included both qualitative and quantitative research [3]. Qualitative research enables a profound exploration of the subject, revealing the root causes behind people’s behaviors, beliefs, and attitudes [10]. Given this, a qualitative research design appears more suitable for investigating ethical conflicts in home-based care.

Therefore, this meta-synthesis aims to consolidate existing qualitative research on ethical conflicts in home care settings, providing evidence-based insights and practical references for frontline practitioners and policymakers.

Methods

Aims and design

The aim of this meta-synthesis is to systematically synthesize the qualitative evidence regarding the experiences of healthcare and social care providers facing ethical conflicts. The conduct and reporting followed the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis) guidelines [11]. The systematic review protocol was registered at PROSPERO with identifier number: CRD420251022946.

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted in the Web of Science, Embase, MEDLINE, and CINAHL databases to identify relevant articles. To guide the formulation of the research question and ensure consistency, we applied the PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) framework. In this review, the Population was defined as healthcare and social care providers involved in home-based care; the Intervention referred to home-based care; the Comparison was not applicable, as the review synthesized qualitative evidence without a control group; and the Outcomes focused on experience of moral conflict. The search was initiated in January 2025 and updated in April 2025. For a detailed description of the search, please refer to the supplementary appendix. Furthermore, the references of the identified literature were reviewed to find potential studies.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) The study population comprises healthcare and social care providers; (2) The phenomena of interest were the experiences of moral conflicts in home-based care among healthcare and social care providers; (3) Studies that applied qualitative methods, including phenomenology, case studies, ethnography, narrative research, grounded theory, case studies.

The exclusion criteria included the following: (1) Unavailability of the full text; (2) Studies only reporting on patients and from nursing homes or hospitals; (3) Review articles, commentaries, repeated or overlapping publications, other secondary material articles; (4) Articles published other than English.

Quality appraisal of included studies

Two researchers independently assessed the methodological quality of the selected studies based on the JBI Quality Evaluation Criteria for Qualitative Research (2020 edition) [12]. This assessment involved ten criteria, each categorized as “yes,” “no,” “uncertain,” or “not applicable.” Any conflicts during this process were settled through either consultation between the two researchers or by involving a third researcher for discussion. To enhance the comprehensiveness and scope of the meta-ethnography synthesis, all studies were incorporated in the data synthesis regardless of their quality ratings [13].

Data extraction and analysis

Two authors independently collected the general characteristics of the studies using an identical prefabricated form. After the data collection was completed, the two versions were compared to ensure the accuracy of the information gathered. Specifically, the following information was extracted: author, year, country, purpose, methodology, participants, data collection and analysis, and results. Any inconsistencies during the extraction process were adjudicated by a third researcher. Analytical themes introduce the findings from the relevant original studies and provide new in-depth interpretations and insights. Thomas and Harden’s three-stage thematic and content analysis approach were used to synthesize the data of the included studies [14]. The process of data synthesis was conducted by four authors. First, the researchers systematically reviewed and repeatedly read the original literature and coded. In the second stage, following line-by-line coding, researchers began to organize the codes into relevant domains to construct descriptive themes. Descriptive themes are direct descriptions of the findings from the original studies, closely connected to the raw data, and strive to maintain fidelity to the original research. During this stage, researchers look for connections between codes and combine them into broader themes. Finally, the researchers repeatedly checked the descriptive themes and identified new concepts, understandings or assumptions. At this stage, analytical findings were generated that presented the key findings of the primary articles and provided more comprehensive explanations of this field. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion. To minimize potential bias, regular meetings were held to ensure the rigor of the data synthesis results.

Ethical considerations

The data presented in this review are entirely sourced from previously published studies and do not involve any new experiments on animals or humans. Therefore, this review does not require ethical approval.

Synthesis of study findings

This review explores the information from the sixteen included studies, a total of three analytical themes and seven subthemes were identified.

Theme One: Paternalism versus Respect for Autonomy

This theme comprises two sub-themes highlighting how health-care and social-care providers necessarily entail some infringement on patient autonomy, even when motivated by harm prevention. Striking a balance between paternalism and respect for patient autonomy represents a pervasive ethical dilemma in their practice.

Preventing harm

For many nursing staff, achieving a balance between respecting patient autonomy and preventing harm presents a considerable challenge, particularly for individuals experiencing cognitive decline who lack full awareness of the risks associated with certain behaviors [15, 28].

…Mrs. I said she did not need to wash as she had been up early and lay in bed because she was cold. The professional caregiver doubted this but said she felt it would be “disrespectful” if she insisted on following her to the bathroom to help her wash [28].

Furthermore, healthcare and social care providers are responsible for providing safe and competent care to minimize risks to patients and their families. However, ethical tensions arise when this obligation to ensure safety conflicts with patients’ decisions to live with certain risks [19].

I do know that I can make decisions about the patients and if they don’t like them, they won’t.

do it. … You have to get to know that about people, and live with the risk, what they’re.

able to live with.

Reluctant patient

The most common ethical challenge was patients refusing or resisting care. This created a dilemma for staff, who had to balance respecting patient autonomy with providing beneficial and non-harmful care [8, 20, 22, 23, 28]. This frequently caused moral suffering among healthcare professionals, who struggled to honor patients’ care decisions while simultaneously questioning their competence to make informed treatment choices.

“And I was really concerned. I said are you eating and you know she wasn’t keeping her doctor appointments. But she was seeing a naturopath and I really had my queries about this natural doctor.” [23].

Similarly, family members may sometimes refuse treatment on the patient’s behalf [25, 29]. One participant described working with a family having a documented history of abuse and dysfunction. Despite the family’s reluctance to accept the care plan, they were expected to ‘try’ to keep the man within community care. This expectation generated considerable anger and despair among the nursing staff.

There seems to be this interesting dynamic where the situation almost must get bad enough for caregivers to get help [25].

Theme two: blurred boundaries

The second theme emphasizes various relational issues between healthcare and social care providers and those they care for. They often encounter the dilemma of balancing the maintenance of professional boundaries with the establishment of emotional connections with the care recipients. Moreover, healthcare and social care providers are sometimes perceived as intruders into patients’ privacy, necessitating the establishment and maintenance of trust-based relationships with them.

Conflict between emotional involvement and professional roles

Four studies [8, 16, 21, 29] documented instances where nursing staffs developed overly familiar relationships with patients, compromising their ability to maintain professional distance. This undermined the objectivity and boundaries required in nursing practice, as emotional investment made it challenging to uphold these professional standards.

“…You build a relationship with them to [ensure they] trust you and know that they are safe with you, but at the same time they have to understand that you are there doing a job. You’re not there as their friend although you are friendly towards them.” [21].

Maintaining an appropriate professional balance between necessary closeness and respectful distance becomes especially challenging when patients feel lonely and place substantial demands on nursing staff. Some of them often feel obliged to address issues that go beyond their nursing responsibilities, which often leads to moral stress [8].

…. Perhaps, I became less professional, but I felt I should be there for him and help him in the.

best possible way. He was always on my mind [29].

Respecting or transgressing the right to privacy

Our findings suggest that in some cases [16, 20], nurses and social care providers feel like intruders on patients. When patients decline to open the door, them invoke their professional authority to persuade patients to grant entry—a practice that several participants regarded as ethically inconsistent with the patient’s right to privacy.

To give an injection to the patient in his own home is something I really don’t want to do. I feel that I intrude when I come to give that injection. It is difficult [for the patient] to say ‘no’ to psychiatric personnel. [16]

Theme three: shortcomings of the health system

The third theme reveals a dilemma where inequitable resource allocation, overall scarcity, and certain regulations prevent patients from receiving the assistance they need. Even though healthcare and social care providers recognize the unreasonable of this situation, they feel powerless to change it.

Burdensome tasks

Two studies documented nurses’ exposure to inequitable work arrangements. Nurses reported feeling exploited by their employer due to the prioritization of economic objectives over their well-being (e.g., consolidation of work tours) [18]. Additionally, nurses are not only required to spend a significant amount of time on documentation but also expected to participate in the financial decision-making of the nursing organization, balancing profitability with patient needs [8]. These cumbersome tasks increase their moral distress.

But then. very often we just can’t keep to the plan because unforeseen things come up. So you spend more time and then you’re delayed for the other jobs [18].

Scarcity of resources

In three studies [17, 23, 25], inadequate resources emerged as a contributing factor to healthcare providers’ distress. Some participants highlighted frontline care shortages, which frequently caused delayed care or unmet support needs. The value conflicts resulting from resource scarcity often triggered their emotional suffering.

But hearing someone say, “I’m at the end of my rope and I don’t know what to do”, and not having anything that I can really do to help them… It makes me want to cry on a daily basis for clients…So the caregivers feel so helpless. [25]

Moreover, constrained budgets and staffing shortages can lead to inequitable resource allocation, allowing the most vocal patients to secure a disproportionate share of available services.

If they are to get the same—the same type of help and at the same time—it may well be that I give priority to those whose family members shout loudest. [17]

Restricted by rules

Two studies [18, 24] documented that healthcare providers navigate complex regulatory requirements, creating tension between compliance protocols and individualized patient care. These studies report that home-based care is determined by administrative decisions, which outline the specific services provided to each patient. Consequently, these written directives constrain nursing practice, making it rigid and task-oriented, with minimal opportunity to address patients’ personal and unique needs.

Nursing is a continuous process. It’s contradictory to make an administrative decision for six months in advance for this patient… [18].

When institutions implement monitoring technologies to enhance productivity, quality, and safety, it often results in reduced autonomy for caregivers and diminished control over their work [27].

Digital aids for supervision can be good. However, many people are alone, and the home care service is the only visit you get for a whole day. Is it right for that person to talk on a screen, or does it require a human visit? [27]

Discussion

This review explores and synthesizes 16 qualitative studies on home-based care to summarize the global status, experiences, and needs regarding moral conflicts in home-based care. All relevant information was consolidated into three main themes and seven subthemes, highlighting the forms of moral conflict and their contributing factors. These factors include whether paternalistic approaches were employed, the nature of the caregiver–patient relationship, and shortcomings within the healthcare system. This study provides a comprehensive exploration of the moral conflicts occurring in home care, aiming to offer meaningful insights for addressing these conflicts and improving the working environment for frontline caregivers.

All included studies originated from Europe and North America, with most conducted in the Nordic countries. This concentration is largely attributable to cultural values, comprehensive welfare systems, and strong policy support [17]. In the Nordic context, self-reliance and independence are highly valued. Remaining at home enables older adults and other care recipients to preserve their daily routines, sustain community connections, and maintain a sense of self-determination [30]. In Norway and Sweden, nurses and other professionals involved in home care are primarily employed and funded by municipalities through publicly financed healthcare systems [31]. In Canada, home care is financed through the publicly funded provincial healthcare systems, although the organization and delivery of services vary considerably across provinces [32]. In all three countries, home care professionals operate within universal healthcare systems where funding is primarily derived from taxation, and services are designed to ensure equitable access. Possible reasons for the paucity of related studies in Asia, Africa, and Latin America may include limited research funding, the lower prioritization of ethics and psychological research within research agendas, and challenges associated with research infrastructure and international collaboration.

Paternalism versus respect for autonomy

The results of this review report that, healthcare and social care providers encounter difficulty in balancing respect for patients’ autonomy with benevolent care practices. Patients with diminished cognitive and volitional capacities often struggle to determine their best interests, and their impaired decision-making abilities place healthcare and social care providers in a dilemma.

Moreover, providing care in a dignified manner is crucial, as failing to do so may signal to patients that they are losing control over their own lives. This finding aligns with the results of prior research [33, 34]. Preserving patient autonomy has long presented challenges in hospital settings [35, 36], but the issue is even more pronounced in home-based care. This phenomenon stems from the perception of the home as a private realm essential for preserving patients’ personal identity—a space where they can freely exercise self-determination [37]. Consequently, home health caregivers must adapt flexibly to patients’ established routines within this personal domain—despite lacking environmental authority—to preserve patient autonomy.

As home health caregivers typically work independently and often lack established support systems to address the ethical challenges described in this review [38], it is imperative for policymakers to create new learning opportunities and provide support for these professionals.

Blurred boundaries

The findings from this review indicate a conflict between healthcare and social care providers’ personal emotions and their professional roles. They are aware that, as professionals, they must maintain clear and objective boundaries.

However, their emotional involvement makes it difficult for them to do so. Two predominant perspectives exist: some contend that affection constitutes an appropriate ethical dimension of social care, while others maintain that forming friendships with care recipients crosses professional boundaries [20]. The former group maintains that friendship should be part of professional relationships, as transcending professional boundaries to engage in unreserved emotional exchange with patients enhances job satisfaction [39, 40]. The latter group argues that excessive emotional involvement may cause them discomfort and even reduce the quality of care.

Our findings also reveal privacy infringements that are specific to patients with dementia or chronic psychiatric disorders. Previous research has reported that respecting the wishes of individuals with dementia may, in some situations, entail accepting a certain level of risk to preserve aspects of life they hold valuable. However, when that risk becomes excessive, honoring their preferences is no longer feasible [41]. In summary, it is necessary to place greater emphasis on clarifying the affective facet of caregiving and on understanding how it shapes nursing practice.

Shortcomings of the health system

The synthesis of arguments in this review highlights that a scarcity of human resources, high care costs, and unreasonable regulations contribute to moral distress among healthcare and social care providers. Structural constraints within the healthcare system are also a major source of distress for healthcare and social care providers. Nursing practice often becomes overly formalized and task-driven, leaving little opportunity to provide care consistent with professional ethical standards. Compounding this problem, staff shortages and rigid scheduling prevent patients from receiving the level of care that healthcare providers deem appropriate—a situation the staff themselves perceive as painful. These pressures have worsened in the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic, accelerating the exodus of frontline personnel [42, 43]. The resulting workforce deficit further compresses their rest periods and forces them to choose between their professional responsibilities and their family lives. In summary, these healthcare and social care providers’ experiences of moral conflict underscore the imperative for health system transformation. Such transformation requires a multifaceted approach. First, organizational structures and policies should be established to support ethical reflection and dialogue among healthcare professionals. Second, training and psychological support programs need to be integrated to help professionals cope with moral suffering. Finally, ensuring adequate human resources is essential to reduce the structural causes that give rise to moral conflicts.

Limitations

Several factors should be taken into account when interpreting our findings. First, as all studies were conducted in Europe and Canada, the generalizability of this review’s findings is limited; caution is warranted when applying the results to countries in Asia, Africa, and Latin America. Secondly, an exclusive focus on English-language articles may have caused us to overlook important research published in other languages and could have introduced selection bias. Finally, most of the included studies focused on nurses, while research involving social care providers was scarce. This reflects a lack of knowledge of production in this area. Future studies should pay greater attention to the moral distress of social care professionals.

Conclusion

This review systematically synthesizes qualitative studies on experiences of moral conflict in home care. The findings reveal the diverse nature of these conflicts, which arise from factors such as the home environment, the physical condition of the care recipients, and the conditions within the healthcare system. Moral conflict poses significant risks to the physical and mental well-being of healthcare and social care providers, underscoring the need for targeted support and intervention. This study offers essential insights for improving care quality and developing effective strategies to address these conflicts.
