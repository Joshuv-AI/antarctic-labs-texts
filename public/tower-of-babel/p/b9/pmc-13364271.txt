A single-institution retrospective review of patients treated with single-fraction 28 Gy SBRT to lung tumors
Authors: Benson Wan, Vanessa Di Lalla, Tanner Connell, Neil Kopek, Marie Duclos, Bassam Abdulkarim, Sergio L. Faria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSingle-fraction stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) has been used to treat lung tumors. However, there is heterogeneity in the prescribed dose. We performed a single-institution, retrospective review to assess the outcomes associated with single-fraction 28 Gy SBRT to cancers in the lung.Materials and methodsA chart review was performed on all patients treated with 28 Gy in 1 fraction to the lung in our institution between February 2022 and April 2023. Patient and tumour characteristics were obtained. Outcomes pertaining to local control, and acute and late toxicities occurring after treatment were obtained from radiological reports and patient charts.Results130 patients were reviewed and analyzed. The median follow-up in this cohort was 22 months. 81% of the patients were treated for a primary lung tumour. Estimated Kaplan–Meier 1- and 2-year local control rates were 95.7% (primary: 97.9%, metastases: 89.1%) and 86.9% (primary: 87.6%, metastases: 76.3%), respectively. 21% of patients experienced acute grade 1–2 toxicities, most commonly fatigue and temporary chest wall pain. 18% of patients experienced late grade 1–2 toxicities, most commonly asymptomatic pneumonitis and chest wall pain. There was no reported grade 3 or higher acute or late toxicities.ConclusionsOur review supports the safety and value of 28 Gy delivered in one fraction. Prospective trials with long-term follow-up are needed to define the role and optimal dose of single-fraction ablative SBRT to lung tumors.

Introduction

Stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) is widely used in the treatment of lung tumors. Candidates for ablative SBRT range from medically inoperable patients with early-stage primary lung cancer to those with lung metastatic disease [1]. Existing literature describes various SBRT regimens for lung tumors, including limited data with single-fraction doses ranging from 15 Gy to 34 Gy [2].

Variations in single-fraction doses exist due to the desire to balance tumour control with treatment- related toxicities. However, to date, there is no consensus on the optimal dose. The aim of this review is to present our institution’s experience with single-fraction 28 Gy SBRT for lung tumors, and to discuss the available data supporting single-fraction dose regimens.

Materials and methods

We conducted a retrospective review of patients who received single-fraction 28 Gy SBRT for lung tumors at the McGill University Health Centre between February 2022 and April 2023. Patients were excluded from the review if they did not undergo follow-up imaging, either thoracic computed tomography (CT) or positron emission tomography (PET), or were not seen in follow-up in our department.

All patients underwent four-dimensional CT (4D-CT) simulation without intravenous contrast. Simulation images were fused with diagnostic CT or PET imaging. Contouring included the gross tumor volume (GTV), an internal target volume (ITV) based on the 4D-CT, and a planning target volume (PTV) generated by applying a 5 mm expansion margin. All treatments were delivered using a Varian Linear Accelerator with coplanar 6 MV flattening-filter-free photons and on-board cone-beam CT for patient positioning verification. The mean beam-on time was approximately 5 minutes. Dose coverage requirements and constraints are summarized in Table 1.

For this analysis, lesions were categorized by their anatomical location: chest wall (CW) if in contact with or within 2 cm of the chest wall, Central if within 2 cm of the proximal bronchial tree, heart, esophagus, mediastinum, or pericardial pleura, or Middle if classified as neither CW nor Central.

Patient demographics, tumor histology, dosimetric and contouring data, outcomes, and radiological reports were collected from local and provincial electronic health record systems. Local progression was determined based on radiology reports from follow-up CT or PET imaging. Acute toxicities (less than or equal to 120 days post-treatment) and late toxicities (more than 120 days post-treatment) were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5. Follow-up appointments typically occurred within 4 months after treatment, with subsequent visits scheduled at the treating physician’s discretion.

Survival statistics and Kaplan–Meier curves were generated using SPSS Statistics (IBM). Patients were censored at the time of their last follow-up or reported scan.

Results

A total of 130 patients were included in this retrospective review. In total, 146 lesions were treated, as some patients received SBRT for multiple nodules. Of the 130 patients, 105 were treated for primary lung tumors (67 without histologic confirmation, treated based on imaging and clinical diagnosis), 2 for recurrent lung tumors, and 23 for metastatic lesions. The median follow-up was 22 months (range: 0.2–33.7). Table 2 summarizes the patient demographics and characteristics.

The median PTV of the analyzed lesions was 14.06 cc (range: 1.63–91.15). The lesions were situated in the following lung lobes: LLL (23), LUL (37), RLL (31), RML (4), RUL (50), and 1 lesion spanned the RUL/RML. By anatomical location, there were 94 CW, 49 Middle, and 3 Central lesions.

At the time of analysis, 89% (n = 116) of patients showed no local progression, while 11% (n = 14) had evidence of progression. Kaplan–Meier estimates demonstrated a local control rate of 95.7% (97.9% primary, 89.1% metastatic) at 1 year and 86.9% (87.6% primary, 76.3% metastatic) at 2 years (Fig. 1).

Local progression occurred in 16 tumors in 14 patients. The median PTV of those lesions was 22.4 cc (range: 1.63–66.49). Twelve were CW lesions and four were Middle. Histologies included: lung adenocarcinoma (38%), colorectal (19%), leiomyosarcoma (13%), neuroendocrine bowel (6%) and clinically diagnosed primary lung (25%).

No grade 3 or higher acute or late toxicities were observed. Grade 1 or 2 acute toxicities occurred in 21% of patients and late toxicities occurred in 18% of patients. The most common acute toxicities were fatigue (grade 1: 12%; grade 2: 1%), chest wall pain (grade 1: 7%; grade 2: 2%), and pneumonitis (grade 2: 2%). Other acute events (1%) included nausea, dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, and dermatitis. Five percent of patients experienced multiple acute toxicities. The most frequent late toxicities were pneumonitis (grade 1: 9%; grade 2: 1%) and chest wall pain (grade 1: 7%). Three patients (3%) experienced pathological rib fractures attributable to SBRT. The median PTV for lesions associated with acute toxicity was 19 cc (range: 5.03–50.4) while the median PTV for lesions associated with late toxicity was 14.57 cc (range: 5.16–53.4).

Discussion

Single-fraction SBRT is advantageous for both patients and the healthcare system, offering shorter treatment times and potential cost savings compared with multi-fraction regimens [3]. However, evidence for single-fraction ablative SBRT in lung cancer remains limited.

Two phase II randomized trials published in 2019 compared single- vs. multi-fraction SBRT in lung tumours. The RTOG 0915 trial compared 34 Gy in 1 fraction (BED10) = 149 Gy) with 48 Gy in 4 fractions (BED10 = 106 Gy) in 94 patients with medically inoperable early-stage, peripheral non-small cell lung cancer. After a median 4-year follow-up, no significant differences were found in survival or grade 2 or higher toxicities [4]. The second phase II study performed in the USA compared 30 Gy in 1 fraction (BED10 = 120 Gy) with 60 Gy in 3 fractions (BED10 = 180 Gy) in 98 patients, also with non-operable peripheral non-small cell lung cancer, showing equivalent toxicity, local control, progression-free, and overall survival, with better quality-of-life outcomes favoring the single-fraction arm [5].

The Trans-Tasman Radiation Oncology Group compared 28 Gy in 1 fraction vs. 48 Gy in 4 fractions in 90 patients with 133 lung oligo-metastases. After a median 5.4-year follow-up, no significant differences were found in local control or toxicities [6].

Our findings, based on 130 patients treated with single-fraction 28 Gy SBRT, demonstrate favorable local control and good tolerability, with no grade 3 or higher toxicities. Limitations of the present review include short follow-up duration, lack of histology in some patients, reliance on radiological reports and its retrospective design.

Current evidence suggests that single-fraction doses between 28 Gy and 34 Gy are generally safe and achieve satisfactory control when used for lung tumours [7]. However, when extrapolating from intracranial stereotactic radiosurgery where single fraction 18–24 Gy is often used, doses below 28 Gy may suffice in treating select lung tumours [8]. The practicality of 27 Gy single-fraction SBRT was examined with one pilot study for 30 centrally located lung tumors, and reported no grade 2 or higher toxicities and a 97% local control rate at a median follow-up of 12.7 months [9]. The ongoing SABR-COMET-10 trial will further assess SBRT’s clinical and quality-of-life outcomes for patients with 4–10 oligo-metastases, including lung lesions, allowing single-fraction dosing of 20 Gy (BED10 = 60 Gy) [10].

Conclusions

Single-fraction SBRT for lung tumors is a practical option for patients, particularly for those who live far from the treating hospital, which often is the case in our institution. Our single-institution review supports the safety and value of 28 Gy delivered in one fraction in lung cancer. However, more evidence, including long-term follow-up and prospective trials, is required to further define the role of ablative single-fraction SBRT in lung tumors.
