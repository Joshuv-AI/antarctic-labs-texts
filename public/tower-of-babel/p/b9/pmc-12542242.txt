LGBTIQ + people in palliative and end-of-life care contexts in Switzerland: a scoping review
Authors: Michael J. Deml, Clément Meier, Léïla Eisner, Tabea Hässler, Gaé Colussi, Jolanda Elmers, Philip Larkin, Claudia Gamondi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ContextDespite being a high-income country with universal health insurance coverage, disparities persist for LGBTIQ + people’s mental health, substance use, sexual health, and physical health in Switzerland. Less research attention and public health efforts have focused on their palliative and end-of-life care (PEOLC) needs. This scoping review’s objective is to explore, describe, and map the evidence related to PEOLC contexts for LGBTIQ + people in Switzerland.MethodsWe conducted a scoping review in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews and adhered to PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Inclusion criteria were defined using the Population-Concept-Context framework. We systematically searched peer-reviewed and grey literature published in English, German, French, and Italian in databases including APA PsychInfo, CINAHL, Cochrane, Embase, Google Scholar, Medline Ovid ALL, ProQuest, Web of Science, and various Swiss institutional repositories. Screening and data extraction were facilitated using Covidence, with titles and abstracts independently reviewed by at least two authors. Extracted data were organized to identify themes regarding patients’ (anticipated) PEOLC needs, social support networks, healthcare professional (HCP) competencies, and existing initiatives or policies in Switzerland.ResultsOur search yielded 454 potential references, from which 35 were included after screening and eligibility assessments. The majority (n = 32, 91.4%) were grey literature sources, reflecting limited peer-reviewed empirical research. Nine references presented empirically collected data directly related to LGBTIQ + populations and their social support or HCP experiences in ageing, palliative, or end-of-life contexts. Findings highlighted significant health disparities, elevated chronic disease prevalence, and persistent fears of discrimination among ageing LGBTIQ + people, negatively impacting healthcare access and engagement. Social networks primarily composed of chosen families underscored vulnerabilities related to informal caregiving and decision-making in PEOLC contexts. HCPs were generally reported as lacking sufficient competencies and preparedness for providing culturally sensitive and inclusive care. Initiatives designed to promote awareness-building and inclusion remain fragmented, underscoring the need for coordinated efforts to address gaps in care provision for LGBTIQ + people in Switzerland.ConclusionsA stronger empirical basis is needed to effectively respond to PEOLC needs for LGBTIQ + people in Switzerland. Collaborative research efforts will lay the groundwork to build trust and capacity between people, communities, and healthcare professionals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-025-01874-4.

Background

In recent years, we have witnessed political will and social movements pushing for wider-scale equality for LGBTIQ + people (lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, intersex, queer, and other sexual/gender minorities), both in Switzerland and several countries across the globe. For example, in January 2022, those wishing to change their official (binary) gender in Switzerland were able to do so through a simplified process without going to court or undergoing medical examinations [1]. Another recent example emanates from marriage equality being legally recognized through a referendum in 2021 and going into effect in July 2022 [2]. Despite these advances, in 2022, the Swiss parliament opted not to ban non-consensual surgeries on intersex children [3]–a serious violation of their physical integrity–, despite recommendations from organizations such as the World Health Organization [4], Physicians for Human Rights [4], the American Academy of Family Physicians [5], and the United Nations [6], which called for deferring medically unnecessary surgeries until the child can participate in the decision [7]. Meanwhile, in many regions—including parts of Africa, Asia, Eastern Europe, United States—progress has stalled or regressed [8]. These legal and political trends reflect broader global patterns of polarization over human rights, with LGBTIQ + issues becoming increasingly contentious, both internationally and domestically [9, 10].

Furthermore, notable health disparities remain for sexual and gender minorities. Sexual minority members are people with a minority sexual orientation, such as homosexual (gay, lesbian), bisexual or pansexual people. Gender minority members are people with a minority gender identity such as trans people or people whose sex characteristics differ from those typically expected of men or women (i.e. intersex people). It is important to acknowledge that gender minorities can also be sexual minorities, as sexual orientation and gender identity are distinct concepts.

Researchers have gathered evidence on health inequalities between LGBT people and the general population in Switzerland and found important negative health outcome discrepancies in terms of mental health, suicide attempts, substance use, sexual health, and physical health [11, 12]. In the LGBT Health Survey, which was administered to 2,064 people throughout Switzerland in 2021, 67.6% of participants reported having experienced discrimination or violence on the basis of sexual orientation and/or gender identity at least once in their lives, with a striking 26.6% reporting discrimination or violence in healthcare settings [11]. Additionally, the Swiss LGBTIQ + Panel’s 2023 annual report, involving 2,812 participants—comprising 1,825 sexual minority members, 648 gender minority members, and 339 cisgender heterosexual endosex people (hereafter, cis-heterosexual people)—highlighted pronounced mental health disparities among LGBTIQ + people compared to their cis-heterosexual counterparts [12]. Gender minorities were identified as especially vulnerable. Given that gender minorities reported more experiences of discrimination—and considering the well-established link between discrimination and health disparities [13]—these findings are not surprising.

Like many other high-income countries, Switzerland faces an aging population, with longer life expectancies and anticipated growing needs for healthcare services, particularly for the need for complex care for cases of multimorbidity, the chronically ill, and for palliative care [14]. Among the general population, these needs are expected to be exacerbated by more people living alone, family members and potential caregivers not living close to patients, and potential family caregivers not being able to combine informal caregiving and employment [15]. Many LGBTIQ + people grew up in a time when sexual orientation, gender identity, and/or intersex status were widely stigmatized, pathologized, or even criminalized [16], leading to chronic adversity and enduring invisibility. Furthermore, many may have faced rejection or marginalization from their families of origin, highlighting the importance of found or chosen families for LGBTIQ + people’s social support networks [17, 18].

It is difficult to know with certainty how many people in Switzerland are LGBTIQ + . This is in part due to the variables of sexual orientation and gender identity not being systematically included in national census data collection or in large population-scale studies. The Swiss Health Observatory, for example, produced a synthesis report focusing on regularly collected, representative data about health equity in Switzerland, noting a lack of long-term, representative data on issues faced by LGBTIQ + populations [19]. On the basis of European figures and studies in surrounding countries, approximating that 5–10% of the Swiss population belongs to a sexual or gender minority, estimates of the LGBT population aged ≥ 65 years in Switzerland are between 85,000 and 170,000 people, with predictions that these numbers will increase to 135,000 and 270,000 by 2045 [20].

In aging societies with higher burdens of disease and complex care needs, it is important to know what social support might be available as a potential resource for informal caregiving, particularly through family structures’ potential social capital (i.e. resources) [21, 22]. Older LGBTIQ + people are more inclined to live alone, which increases their vulnerability to social isolation [23, 24]. Furthermore, LGBTIQ + people’s family structures likely differ from those of cis-heterosexual people, who tend to rely on families of origin for support and care as they age or face illness. Older LGBTIQ + people may rely more on their families of choice, including friends, partners, and ex-partners [25]. To our knowledge, little is known about LGBTIQ + people’s social support networks in aging, illness, and palliative contexts in Switzerland, although respondents in the 2022 Swiss LGBTIQ + Panel Survey reported feeling most supported by friends and other LGBTIQ + people, which ranked overall higher as sources of support than family [26].

International research on PEOLC contexts for LGBTIQ + populations has become more inclusive over time, with earlier published literature focusing on the cancer experiences of gay men and lesbian women, with little mention of bisexual people, and no evidence published for transgender people [27]. Since then, bisexual and transgender representation in the research literature has slightly increased [28], but other sexual and gender minorities, such as asexual and intersex people, remain underrepresented [18]. In addition to studies focusing on cancer, more recent research has focused on LGBTIQ + patient and caregiver experiences with dementia and cognitive impairment [29–31]. Scholarly interest on these topics continues to increase, as evidenced by a recent special collection of research in the fields of LGBTQS2 + (2S: two-spirit) ageing, health, and palliative care [32].

Systematic and scoping reviews on research outside of Switzerland have highlighted the importance of social support and inclusion of families, biological and chosen, in health and PEOLC decisions and noted fears of discrimination and lack of trust in healthcare institutions [18, 28, 33]. These reviews also identified low knowledge and preparedness for end-of-life contexts and the need for increased economic and legal support in navigating healthcare systems [18, 28, 33]. Other studies have shown how LGBTIQ + patients in PEOLC situations may potentially experience loss or erosion of identity, fear of rejection, neglect, or disrespect by care staff [34, 35]. Previous negative interactions with health professionals and health institutions throughout their lives may result in feelings of distrust, eliciting reluctance to seek care [18, 28, 36, 37].

Other international research on LGBTIQ + people’s PEOLC experiences focuses on the healthcare professionals (HCPs) providing this care. A focus group study among hospice care team providers in the USA demonstrated how many participating HCPs viewed sexual and gender minority status as private, irrelevant for PEOLC care provision, and not something for which care needed to be tailored; study authors noted how participants were overall unaware of specific LGBTIQ + needs for PEOLC [38]. Another USA-based study surveyed 865 palliative HCPs, with 53.6% of participants reporting that sexual minority patients were more likely to receive discriminatory care in their institutions and 64.3% responding that transgender patients were more likely to experience discrimination than non-transgender patients [39]. Despite consensus about training needs in this area, proper training and standardized competencies for HCPs to meet LGBTIQ + needs for appropriate and respectful PEOLC have not been developed [40], although there are noteworthy reference materials [41].

What little is known regarding PEOLC needs and experiences among LGBTIQ + populations in Switzerland has not yet been systematically collected and synthesized. The objective of this scoping review is therefore to explore, describe, and map the evidence related to their PEOLC contexts.

Methods

In line with our scoping review protocol [42], we adhered to the JBI scoping review methodology [43] and used the PRISMA-ScR checklist for Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analysis extension for Scoping Reviews [44] (Appendix 1). To define the inclusion criteria for references to review, we applied the Population-Concept-Context framework, as recommended by Peters et al. (2020).

Inclusion criteria

Population/types of participants

This scoping review has considered references that focus on LGBTIQ + people in Switzerland who are in palliative care or end-of-life settings.

Concept

The concept guiding the review has four overarching components which are derived from the literature we have cited in the introduction section of this article. The inclusion of item four, existing initiatives, will serve as a foundation for future participatory action research in this field. These components include:LGBTIQ + patients in PEOLC settings, including ageing contexts, anticipation of future needs, wishes and preferences, health statuses, diseases, and care settingssocial support for LGBTIQ + populations via social networks, including families and chosen familiesPEOLC HCP experiences, perceptions and competences around provision of care for LGBTIQ + populations, as well as available tools for such caremapping of existing initiatives, interventions, education, awareness building, and policies: LGBTIQ + groups, associations, and networks, and potential resources for education and awareness campaigns

Context

The context includes palliative care settings and end-of-life contexts (i.e. clinical hospital settings, palliative care units, hospices, community-based palliative care, long-term care institutions, and institutions that care for people in end-of-life settings) and people in LGBTIQ + people’s social networks who may play a role in home care at the end of life.

Types of studies and references included in the search

In our search, we included peer-reviewed literature, including qualitative, quantitative and/or mixed methods studies and systematic and scoping reviews that pertain to LGBTIQ + populations in palliative care or end-of-life contexts in Switzerland. As we expected to find few peer-reviewed references, we expanded the search to include grey literature, such as reports, unpublished PhD and master’s theses, clinical and practical guidelines, health policy, and websites. We included references in English, as well as German, French, and Italian, which are Switzerland’s three main languages.

Search strategy

We followed the three-step search strategy recommended by JBI [45] to identify relevant references, with a search strategy based upon the above-defined study Population-Concept-Context. First, we conducted a preliminary search in the Cochrane Database for Systematic Reviews, Embase, Epistemonikos, and for existing studies on the topic. Second, to identify research articles, a systematic search strategy was designed by a medical librarian (JE) from the Lausanne University Medical Library, using a combination of controlled vocabulary terms and free text terms covering the overarching concepts of the search: LGBTQIA + and palliative care and end-of-life care. The search strategy (Appendix 2) was adapted into search strings for a range of databases: APA PsycInfo Ovid, CINAHL with Full Text EBSCO, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews Wiley, Embase, Google Scholar, Medline Ovid ALL, ProQuest Dissertations and Theses, and Web of Science Core Collection.

The search strategies were peer-reviewed by a second medical librarian before the searches were first conducted on April 2, 2024. Since no other researchers have conducted a scoping or systematic review on this topic in Switzerland, we did not set a start date and searched among all references available until the date our searches were conducted. Additional searches were run on May 9, 2025, with no additional references retained for review.

All references identified through the database searches were downloaded into EndNote 20. Duplicate records were removed using a fully automated AI-based deduplication solution, Deduklick [46]. Study authors then searched for grey literature in institutional repositories of Swiss Universities and Institutions for Higher Education, Google, Google Scholar, and websites of Swiss health organizations and LGBTIQ + associations and networks. Duplicates were removed in EndNote 20. Third, the study team manually examined reference lists of selected references for inclusion in the scoping review.

Study selection, screening, and data extraction

Study selection, screening, and data extraction was facilitated by use of Covidence [47]. Study authors piloted the screening on 25 retained reference titles and abstracts and discussed discrepancies. After pilot screening, at least two authors screened all titles and abstracts for inclusion eligibility. Subsequently, at least two authors conducted a full-text examination to determine which references would be included for final data extraction. All discrepancies were resolved upon discussion between a minimum of two authors. We customized the data extraction template on Covidence to extract the following from the retained references: author(s), year of publication, title, aims/objectives, language of reference, study design, type of reference, and brief descriptions and summaries of data related to the population, concept, and context. MJD extracted data in English and French, and GC extracted data in German. Extracted data were compiled into a Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmont, WA) spreadsheet, allowing for the description and identification of common themes and gaps.

Results

Our literature search resulted in a total of 454 potentially eligible references. After removing duplicates, screening, and full-text review, we extracted data from 35 references. Most references were excluded because they did not: focus on PEOLC contexts, include information about LGBTIQ + populations, or provide information specific to Switzerland. Overview of retained references

Of the 35 retained references, 3 (8.6%) were peer-reviewed publications, including a qualitative study on HIV-affected families in German-speaking Switzerland [48], a focused literature review of the specific needs of older gay men and lesbian women [49], and a book chapter about issues LGBTI people face in healthcare settings [50]. The remaining 32 (91.4%) references represent grey literature, including: 8 newspaper or magazine articles (print and online) [51–58], 7 reports [59–65], 1 PhD dissertation [66], 1 Master’s thesis [67], 3 Bachelor’s theses [68–70], 4 reference materials for professionals [71–74], 3 video recordings of round table panel discussions concerning sexuality, ageing, and housing for LGBTIQ + people in old age [75–77], 3 website pages detailing ageing-related activities within LGBTIQ + associations [78–80], 1 biographical book about a woman mourning the death of her partner [81], and a series of podcasts about ageing and older queer people in Switzerland [82]. In terms of language, 18 (51.4%) references were in German only, 12 (34.3%) in French only, 2 (5.7%) in both German and French, 2 (5.7%) in German, French, and Italian, and 1 (2.9%) in English.

Not all references present empirical data collected from LGBTIQ + people, their families, health institutions, associations, or health professionals. By this, we mean data collected with specific aims or objectives by using scientific methods such as surveys, questionnaires, or qualitative interviews. References presenting such evidence (n = 10; 28.6%) include 6 reports [60–65], 1 peer-reviewed article [48], 1 PhD dissertation [66], 1 Master’s thesis [67], and 1 Bachelor’s thesis [69]. Data were collected with mixed-methods [61–64, 67], qualitative [48, 66, 69], and quantitative [65] approaches. Sample sizes range from 7 to 2,064 people, with references presenting data collected from LGBTIQ + people [61, 65, 69], LGBTIQ + people’s (chosen) family members [48, 66], older age populations [64], the general population [65], and HCPs [60, 62, 66, 67]. In terms of representation of LGBTIQ + sub-populations, the 10 empirical references refer to lesbian (n = 8) [48, 60–62, 64–67], gay (n = 9) [48, 60–62, 64–67, 69], bisexual (n = 8) [48, 60–62, 64–67], transgender (n = 6) [60–62, 64–67], intersex (n = 4) [60–62, 65], queer (n = 2) [61, 67], and asexual (n = 2) [65, 66] people, with 1 reference only referring to “sexual orientation” [63].

Overview of empirical references

LGBTIQ + patients in PEOLC settings

Synthesis of empirically collected data

Whereas no empirical references explicitly refer to palliative care for LGBTIQ + people, our anticipatory understanding of their potential PEOLC needs brought us to focus on what findings reveal about ageing contexts, anticipation of future needs, health statuses, (co)morbidities, and care settings. Three references [64, 65, 69] present such data collected from LGBTIQ + people with potential implications for PEOLC.

Krüger et al. [65] conducted a study commissioned by the Swiss Federal Office of Public Health (FOPH), drawing upon data from the Swiss Health Survey (ESS) (2012 and 2017) and a specifically designed national online survey (“LGBT Health”; 2021). Findings demonstrate significant health disparities among LGBT people, with 39% of trans/nonbinary and 22% of cisgender LGB people rating their overall health as poor or very poor, compared to 12% in the general population. Chronic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, or obesity were also more prevalent among LGBT respondents (33%) than among their cis-heterosexual counterparts (24%). In their study, access to healthcare is notably influenced by discrimination, as evidenced by 27% of LGBT participants reporting experiences of discrimination within healthcare settings, and 16% avoiding medical services entirely due to mistrust or fear of insensitive treatment.

Borkowsky [64] conducted a national online survey targeting the general population aged ≥ 80 years to investigate their living conditions and experiences of discrimination. Of 835 respondents, 67 (8%) reported being lesbian, gay, bisexual or other (LGB +). Although only 3% of the overall sample reports having experienced discrimination based upon sexual orientation or gender identity, approximately 37.5% of the LGB + respondents reported such discrimination. Summermatter [69]’s qualitative findings from 11 homosexual men (50–79 years of age) echo fears of such discrimination in long-term care facilities in older age. He reports how approximately half of respondents expressed worries about being patronized, discriminated against, or considered outsiders due to their sexuality.

Synthesis of non-empirical references

Of the references not presenting original, empirically collected data from LGBTIQ + people, 17 provide information relevant to implications for them as patients in PEOLC in Switzerland. This includes 7 newspaper or magazine articles (print and online) [51–54, 56–58], 4 reference materials for professionals [50, 71–73], 2 video recordings [75, 76], 1 peer-reviewed focused literature review [49], 1 podcast [82], 1 report [59], and 1 biographical book [81].

Among these references, we identified several transversal themes with implications for LGBTIQ + people as patients in PEOLC settings. These themes include the persistent invisibility of LGBTIQ + identities and needs in healthcare settings [49, 50, 52, 56–59, 72, 75, 82], discrimination [49, 50, 59, 72, 73], and institutional challenges (i.e. lack of resources and competing resources) and inadequate staff awareness [51, 52, 54, 59, 72, 76]. Some references point to data about heightened (co)morbidities throughout the life course, such as increased risks for hypertension, diabetes, mental health struggles, certain cancers, HIV and HIV-aggravated conditions, and substance abuse, among LGBTIQ + populations [49, 50, 59, 72, 73].

Social support for LGBTIQ + populations

Synthesis of empirically collected data

Similar to the previous section, no empirical references explicitly referenced social support for LGBTIQ + populations in Swiss palliative settings. We therefore chose to synthesize relevant information about social support for LGBTIQ + populations in anticipation of their PEOLC needs. By social support, we sought to understand what references could reveal about resources available to LGBTIQ + people via their social networks, including families and chosen families. Four references [48, 61, 65, 69] present such data collected with potential implications for PEOLC contexts.

Three empirical references explicitly address how LGBTIQ + family structures and compositions may differ from cis-heteronormative family structures, increasing the likelihood of people relying more on friendship networks than biological family for support [48, 65, 69]. LGBTIQ + people’s reliance on “chosen” family networks may become more apparent in institutional settings, as evidenced by the documented desire for long-term care facilities to adapt to and recognize LGBTIQ + people’s social network compositions [61, 69]. One study examined LGBTIQ + people’s needs in retirement and care facilities, hospitals, and in home-care settings, with people’s preferences around institutionalized care including proximity to family and friend groups as an important factor [61].

Spirig [48]’s qualitative study with HIV-affected families demonstrates how multiple forms of stigmatization, such as HIV/AIDS-, sexuality-, and drug use-related stigmatization, may play important roles in the family dynamics of those living with HIV. The study describes different levels of implication from “close family members,” “ambivalent family members,” and “distanced family members.”

Data presented from Krüger et al. [65] highlight disparities in terms of social support and loneliness between LGBTIQ + and non-LGBTIQ + people, with additional differences between LGBTIQ + subgroups. Comparatively less trans and nonbinary people reported high levels of social support (25.6%) than LGB people (45.5–47.9%), with people ≥ 50 years old statistically less likely to have medium to high levels of social support. Similar results were found in terms of loneliness; 46.2% trans and nonbinary people reported feeling sometimes or very often lonely, compared to 36.9% among cisgender bisexuals, 26.1% among cisgender gay men, and 21.4% cisgender lesbian women. The effects of loneliness were compounded by income; those who had a salary of less than 4,500CHF/month were significantly likely to report feeling loneliness.

Synthesis of non-empirical references

Of the references not presenting original, empirically collected data, 19 provide information about social support for LGBTIQ + people that is relevant for PEOLC settings in Switzerland. This includes 7 newspaper or magazine articles (print and online) [51–56, 58], 4 reference materials for professionals [50, 72–74], 2 video recordings [76, 77], 2 websites [79, 80], 1 peer-reviewed focused literature review [49], 1 podcast [82], 1 report [59], and 1 biographical book [81].

Among non-empirical references, many echo the above-described issues of differential social support networks and resources LGBTIQ + people. References describe how older LGBTIQ + people are more likely to live alone or have limited family networks, underscoring vulnerabilities in informal caregiving during end-of-life care, where reliance on chosen families rather than traditional kinship structures can lead to legal and institutional barriers in medical decision-making [49, 56, 59, 72, 73, 76, 77, 79]. One report emphasizes how there are historical reasons why LGBT seniors may have less resources as they age. With no legal recognition for same-sex partnerships in Switzerland until 2007, couples have had challenges related to inheritance, surviving partner pension benefits, and transfer of wealth [72].

Fear of social isolation and rejection within ageing contexts is another notable theme. Such concerns were particularly present in media coverage around initiatives regarding long-term care facilities and home care designed specifically for ageing queer populations [51–53, 73, 76]. Although some reporting presents debates around fears of ghettoization of queer populations in LGBTIQ + -specific long-term care facilities [76], other references call attention to the needs for supportive spaces and strong social support. Several initiatives include addressing social support among ageing LGBTIQ + people as part of their objectives [51, 54, 79, 80].

Healthcare professional experiences, competences, and tools

Synthesis of empirically collected data

Of the 5 references which report empirically collected data related to healthcare professional provision of PEOLC care to LGBTIQ + people, 1 specifically focused on specialized palliative care [67]. The others focused on health training institutions’ inclusion of curriculum specific to LGBTI and HIV + and AIDS issues [60], awareness of needs specific to LGBTIQ + people and people living with HIV + or AIDS in inpatient and home care settings [62], LGBTIQ people’s expectations in retirement, care, and hospital facilities, home care, and nursing schools [61], and older people’s sexuality in nursing homes from the perspectives of professionals and residents’ children [66]. These references included 3 reports [60–62], 1 PhD dissertation [66], and 1 Master’s thesis [67].

In a study commissioned by multiple LGBTIQ + associations (Pink Cross, LOS, TGNS, InterAction, QueerAltern), 246 participants responded to a survey aimed at understanding LGBTIQ + people’s needs in retirement and care facilities, hospitals, home care, and clinics in general [61]. Most respondents considered HCPs to be “not at all” or “moderately” prepared to provide care to LGBTIQ + and HIV + people. Qualitative findings point to concerns about HCPs being afraid of providing certain care tasks, inadequate training, long-term care facilities being less prepared than hospitals, and major differences between cities and rural areas. It was also important for respondents to have specific institutional guidelines about acceptance for LGBTIQ + populations, which is something they would consider when making choices around care facilities and care in general.

Data collected from HCPs or those with management positions in healthcare institutions reflect the lack of HCP preparation and training reported in the survey mandated by multiple LGBTIQ + associations. One study among 7 people with leading functions (i.e. managing nurses or medical specialists) in palliative care institutions suggests that HCPs in Swiss specialized palliative care institutions perceive their competence in supporting LGBTIQ + patients to be limited and, despite being open, do not consider the topic to be especially important [67]. One study of 115 HCPs highlights shortcomings in training regarding ageing LGBTI and HIV + populations, with education on these themes markedly absent from most nursing and social care curricula [60]. Similar findings were reflected in a study including 65 employees of institutions providing home care for older patients, with results showing low levels of knowledge of LGBTIQ + -specific needs and a lack of institutional guidelines and information materials [62].

Synthesis of non-empirical references

Of the references not presenting original, empirically collected data, 15 provide information related to healthcare professional provision of PEOLC care to LGBTIQ + people. This includes 5 reference materials for professionals [50, 71–74] (of which 1 is a peer-reviewed book chapter [50]), 3 newspaper or magazine articles (print and online) [51, 52, 56], 3 video recordings [75–77], 1 peer-reviewed focused literature review [49], 1 website [80], 1 report [59], and 1 Bachelor’s thesis [68].

We synthesized the content of the 5 references materials designed for professionals in their provision of care or services to LGBTIQ + people. Each of the references underlines the significance of addressing and overcoming stigmatization and discrimination within healthcare settings to improve care quality for LGBTIQ + people, particularly older adults. Common themes include the necessity of fostering inclusive communication practices and increasing visibility and acknowledgment of diverse sexual orientations and gender identities within care institutions [72, 73] and homecare settings [74]. Some documents emphasize the critical importance of educating and sensitizing professionals to effectively meet the unique health needs of LGBTIQ + populations, which include increased risks for mental health issues, social isolation, and healthcare discrimination [50, 72]. Another recurring aspect is the special attention required for LGBTIQ + people’s social support networks, as many rely more heavily on chosen families and friendship circles, potentially facing greater risks of loneliness and isolation compared to their cis-heterosexual peers [50, 72, 73].

The non-empirical references provided similar content to what was presented in the 5 reference materials for HCPs. Additional topics in the non-reference material include the role of age-related stigma and societal taboos surrounding sexuality in shaping older LGBTIQ + people’s health experiences and their reluctance to disclose identity in care contexts [49, 52, 75]. Many references mention lack of institutional will, lack of preparedness, and limited HCP training as factors reducing quality of care for ageing LGBTIQ + people [49, 51, 56, 59, 68, 75–77]. Others stress the importance of direct LGBTIQ + community involvement in developing healthcare guidance and tools, underscoring the need for bottom-up approaches that reflect lived experience and community-defined priorities [59, 80].

Existing initiatives, interventions, awareness building, and policies

In total, 17 references allude to ongoing initiatives or interventions concerning awareness building and policies relevant to PEOLC for LGBTIQ + people. This includes 6 newspaper or magazine articles (print and online) [51–53, 55, 56, 58], 3 websites [78–80], 3 reference materials for professionals [71, 72, 74], 2 videos [76, 77], 1 report presenting empirically collected data [61], 1 non-empirical report [59], and 1 Bachelor’s thesis [74].

Many of the references in this category present information related to services specifically designed with ageing LGBTIQ + people in mind. Retirement homes and care facilities specifically tailored for LGBTIQ + people and their partners was a major topic in many references [51–53, 55, 56, 58, 70, 76, 79], with most coverage concerning the anticipation and implementation of Switzerland’s first LGBTIQ + older adult care home in Zurich. This initiative is the result of a collaborative partnership between the association queerAltern, an association promoting the social life of ageing queer people, and the municipality of Zurich as part of its Strategies Zurich 2035 (German: Strategien Zürich 2035). One reference mentioned the existence of specialized mobile teams providing homosexual patients with at-home care in Zurich [74].

Following an exploratory study into issues facing ageing LGBT people [59], the city of Geneva collaborated with Association360, a group dedicated to recognizing sexual and gender diversity, in initiating the project LGBT Seniors (French: Projet ainé-e-s LGBT) to develop inclusive practices and community events for older LGBT people and public services, institutions, and associations [72, 79]. The Canton of Vaud has the aging initiative Aging2030 (French: Viellir2030), which includes the Senior LGBTIQ + Project (French: Projet Seniors LGBTIQ +) [80]. Launched in August 2023, Senior LGBTIQ + aims to propose community events, open spaces for dialogue, create a warm atmosphere, heighten awareness around sexuality and gender identities among older populations, and train professionals about this group’s diverse needs. One reference described a meeting between QUEER INSEL, a professional network for the employees of Inselspital, the university hospital in Bern, and queerAlternBern, where the needs of older queer people receiving hospital care were discussed [78]. One newspaper article described how a working group was commissioned in Biel/Bienne to address the topic of queer ageing, particularly in retirement home contexts [56].

Non-governmental organizations have also been proactive in spearheading initiatives to collect empirical data around specific topics for LGBTIQ + communities with relevance for PEOLC. Such efforts represent an interest coming from Switzerland’s major LGBTIQ + organizations for topics related to ageing and care. For example, in 2015, Pink Cross, Switzerland’s national umbrella organization of gay and bisexual men, mandated the Institute of Ageing at the University of Bern to conduct a study on the teaching curriculum of health training institutions in nursing and care related to LGBTI + people and people living with HIV or AIDS [60]. Around the same period, Pink Cross, LOS (Lesbian Organization Switzerland), and TGNS (Transgender Network Switzerland) formed a committee (German: “Fachgruppe Alter”; French: “Commission Senior”) to focus on issues specific to older LGBTI populations. This committee commissioned a study to investigate the awareness of inpatient and outpatient care, nursing facilities, and training institutions regarding the needs of older LGBTI people and those living with HIV or AIDS [62]. In 2019, the committee then involved InterAction (a key organization advocating for the rights and well-being of intersex people) and QueerAltern for the administration of an online survey designed to measure LGBTIQ 1) people’s expectations toward old age care institutions, hospitals, clinics, home care, 2) teaching curriculum of health training institutions in nursing and care related to LGBTI + people and people living with HIV or AIDS, and the requests for the committee itself [61].

Discussion

Our findings illustrate a disconnect between the lived experiences and expectations of LGBTIQ + people in Switzerland and the preparedness of healthcare institutions and professionals to support them in PEOLC contexts. Across the reviewed empirical references, participants consistently described fears of discrimination, a lack of institutional visibility, and structural barriers to care—particularly for trans, non-binary, bisexual, and older people. Social support emerged as both a protective factor and a source of vulnerability, with reliance on chosen families often complicating access to care and decision-making. Healthcare professionals were frequently described as lacking training, guidance, and confidence in addressing LGBTIQ + needs, despite expressing general openness. These themes resonate with international research and recommendations [32, 83, 84], emphasizing the importance of inclusive spaces, community involvement, increasing awareness and providing training opportunities to HCPs, and addressing discriminatory policy and practices, particularly in institutional settings.

This scoping review highlights several significant gaps in the literature concerning LGBTIQ + people’ needs and experiences in PEOLC contexts in Switzerland. Our findings indicate that there is limited peer-reviewed empirical research explicitly addressing the PEOLC needs of LGBTIQ + populations. Most available data were collected through grey literature and mandated by LGBTIQ + organizations, suggesting a strong community-driven awareness but a substantial lack of engagement from researchers and policy makers. Despite this, numerous initiatives have emerged from within the LGBTIQ + community itself, including dedicated community events and care facilities specifically tailored to the needs of LGBTIQ + people. These efforts underscore the importance of recognizing and supporting community expertise while also emphasizing the need for collaborative research initiatives bridging health systems, regional disparities, and linguistic divides within Switzerland.

Furthermore, there is a marked underrepresentation of certain sub-populations within the LGBTIQ + community in existing studies. Although research has expanded to include more bisexual and transgender perspectives, there remains limited visibility for asexual, pansexual, nonbinary, intersex, and other minority identities. This uneven representation potentially contributes to rendering certain people invisible in the existing evidence and to insufficient awareness of the specific health challenges they may face. This lack of visibility may negatively impact policy advocacy and health equity efforts by perpetuating gaps in awareness and support for these sub-populations. Future research efforts in Switzerland should systematically include variables related to the diversity in sexual orientation and gender identities to address this invisibility and to better inform inclusive health policies and practices.

The higher prevalence of chronic diseases, such as hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and mental health conditions among LGBTIQ + people, signals an increased need for targeted PEOLC services. Barriers stemming from fear of discrimination, lack of trust in healthcare institutions, and anticipated or past negative experiences potentially delay or prevent timely access to necessary care. Additionally, reliance on chosen families rather than traditional kinship structures creates complexities in decision-making and caregiving responsibilities at the end-of-life stage, requiring sensitive and culturally competent approaches from healthcare professionals.

Findings underscore a strong need for targeted training and the development of appropriate tools to enhance HCP competences in supporting LGBTIQ + people in PEOLC settings. This need is reflected both in studies with HCPs, who reported feeling poorly prepared to care for LGBTIQ + people, and in studies with LGBTIQ + participants, who perceived a lack of awareness and understanding among healthcare providers. The collective insights from this scoping review suggest that substantial improvements in education, professional development, and institutional policy are required to bridge the existing gaps in knowledge and preparedness among healthcare providers. Future research agendas should include the development and evaluation of educational interventions aimed at integrating cultural humility in terms of LGBTIQ + people’s needs into health curricula. For instance, collaborative approaches involving healthcare institutions, academic stakeholders, and LGBTIQ + community organizations can foster sustainable changes in professional training. For healthcare institutions, recognizing historical and systemic harms inflicted upon LGBTIQ + populations within healthcare settings represents one avenue for fostering safer, equitable PEOLC environments.

When it comes to questions of equity in Switzerland, particularly in the domain of health, it is important to consider questions of language. The retained references reflect the dominance of both German- and French-language materials in Switzerland, with only 1 reference available as a translation in Italian and none in Romansh (an official language of Switzerland spoken by a small minority of people). Future efforts should make efforts to consider language diversity in Switzerland and represent this in the publication of research, reports, reference materials for professionals and information distributed to lay people. Additionally, reliance on digital sources such as websites, podcasts and videos, while valuable, highlights potential disparities related to the digital divide among older populations. This might restrict access to support and information for aging LGBTIQ + individuals, necessitating inclusive outreach strategies that also utilize more traditional, accessible formats.

Limitations

Despite these insights, this review has certain limitations. Primarily, both the reliance on grey literature and the paucity of peer-reviewed publications limit the generalizability of findings to all LGBTIQ + people and HCPs providing PEOLC care in Switzerland. Similarly, given the reliance on grey literature in Switzerland, our search strategy might not have allowed us to find various reports or references from all regional governmental structures and LGBTIQ + associations, indicating potential for selection bias. Additionally, the scoping nature of the review means that quality appraisal and meta-analysis of evidence were not undertaken. Future systematic reviews and primary research efforts, including quantitative studies and qualitative explorations, are needed to robustly capture and address the diverse experiences of LGBTIQ + populations in Swiss PEOLC settings.

Conclusions

This scoping review has presented the limited evidence on PEOLC provision for LGBTIQ + patients, their (chosen) families, HCPs who provide their care, and existing initiatives seeking to address their specific needs in Switzerland. Overall, available evidence for the Swiss context demonstrates the many challenges LGBTIQ + populations face that are relevant for the provision of PEOLC care, including disparities in health and healthcare access, concerns about discrimination or stigmatization, and social support networks that may differ from cis-heteronormative kinship compositions. The lack of available peer-reviewed literature and limited empirically collected data present important knowledge gaps. Several of Switzerland’s well-established LGBTIQ + organizations have already shown interest in topics relevant to PEOLC care for LGBTIQ + people. As such, future research should involve the inclusion of these organizations, the communities they represent, and HCPs providing PEOLC care to establish a stronger evidence base around the PEOLC needs and care provision for LGBTIQ + people in Switzerland.
