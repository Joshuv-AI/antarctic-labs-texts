Cerebellar–Basal Ganglia Dysconnectivity in Schizophrenia: Insights into Motivational Deficits
Authors: Xuan Wang, Xiao-dong Guo, Xin-lu Cai, Bing-jie Huang, Yi Wang, Cheng-cheng Pu, Xin Yu, Simon S Y Lui, Raymond C K Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and HypothesisMotivational deficits are core negative symptoms of schizophrenia (SCZ), which have been linked to disruptions in reward network. Recent evidence suggests the cerebellum’s role in motivational and hedonic processing. This study examined its connectivity with the reward network in SCZ and hypothesized that decreased connectivity would be found in SCZ patients and correlated with severe negative symptoms.Study DesignThis study employed a cross-sample validation approach using 2 independent cohorts (Sample 1: NSCZ = 62, NHC = 61; Sample 2: NSCZ = 53, NHC = 55). Resting-state functional connectivity was assessed using network-based analysis to identify disrupted subnetworks, followed by seed-based connectivity analysis to localize specific connections. Effective connectivity was assessed using spectral Dynamic Causal Modeling (DCM) for inferring the directional influences of abnormal connectivity related to amotivation or anhedonia.Study ResultsNetwork-based analysis in Sample 1 identified a disrupted subnetwork between the cerebellum (lobules VI, VIIb, VIII) and basal ganglia (putamen, caudate, pallidum) in SCZ, with cerebellar–pallidal connectivity associated with amotivation. Seed-based analysis in Sample 2 revealed reduced putamen/caudate-lobule VI connectivity, correlating with amotivation and anhedonia symptoms in SCZ. Spectral DCM indicated reduced excitatory input from cerebellum to the basal ganglia in Sample 1, but such results could not be replicated in Sample 2.ConclusionsOur findings highlighted the role of cerebellum–basal ganglia connectivity in the pathophysiology of SCZ, particularly in relation to amotivation and anhedonia. This pathway may be a putative target for neuromodulation to ameliorate negative symptoms of SCZ.

Introduction

Schizophrenia (SCZ) is a severe and debilitating mental disorder. Amotivation and anhedonia constitute the motivation dimension of SCZ’s negative symptoms,1,2 which exacerbates SCZ-associated cognitive impairments,3 leads to poor prognosis4 and hinders functional recovery.5 Effective treatments for these symptoms are limited, and the underlying mechanisms for amotivation and anhedonia remain unclear.6

Amotivation and anhedonia have been closely linked to dysfunctions in the reward processing system,7 which involves multiple brain regions, particularly the mesocorticolimbic system. The striatum plays a key role in reward prediction and prediction error,8,9 while the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) encodes reward value.10 The anterior cingulate cortex is involved in effort-reward evaluation,11 the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) contributes to executive control,12 and the hippocampus stores reward-related information.13 While these regions have been extensively studied in SCZ patients, recent evidence further implicates that the cerebellum is linked to motivation and hedonic processing.2,14

Although traditionally the cerebellum has been associated primarily with motor functions, accumulating evidence supports its essential role in cognition, emotion regulation, and motivational processing.15–17 The cerebellum also connects functionally to the core regions of reward network, including the striatum18,19 and the prefrontal cortex.20,21 Animal studies have demonstrated that neuromodulation of the cerebellum can alter reward signals in the basal ganglia, affecting motivational and hedonic behaviors.22 It is plausible that cerebellar dysfunction is one of the neural mechanisms for amotivation and anhedonia in SCZ patients.

The cerebellum is involved in the pathophysiology of SCZ, particularly through its functional connectivity with the cerebrum. The classic unitary model of SCZ posits that clinical symptoms may stem from “cognitive dysmetria,” a breakdown in neural coordination associated with alterations in the cortico-cerebellum-thalamic-cortical (CCTC) circuit.23,24 Empirical research has supported abnormal cerebellar activation in SCZ patients during cognitive and emotional processing,25–27 as well as dysconnectivity within the CCTC circuit.28 For example, a network of 84 nodes of CCTC circuit was identified in SCZ, which may serve as a state-independent neural signature that could distinguish SCZ patients from healthy individuals across different illness states,29–31 and even predict the development of symptoms.32 Furthermore, neuromodulation studies also suggested that stimulating cerebellar regions may modulate their functional connectivity with cortical or subcortical regions, and alleviate negative symptoms.33–37

While most previous studies on cerebellar connectivity of SCZ have adopted large-scale, data-driven approaches, the complex and uneven connectivity patterns between the cerebellum and cortical/subcortical regions pose significant challenges in pinpointing specific pathways linked to particular symptoms. Instead, this study focused on amotivation and anhedonia, aiming to elucidate their neural mechanisms from the perspective of the cerebellum and its functional connectivity with the reward network in patients with SCZ. We conducted a resting-state functional connectivity analysis, leveraging predefined regions of interest (ROIs) in the cerebellum and reward network to identify abnormal subnetworks and connectivity and examined their associations with clinical symptoms. Additionally, we assessed the directionality of impaired connectivity—reflecting effective connectivity—using Spectral Dynamic Causal Modeling (DCM). To enhance the robustness of our findings, we employed a 2-sample validation approach.

Building on the anatomical and functional connectivity between the cerebellum and the mesocorticolimbic regions,19,38,39 as well as the CCTC model of SCZ,23,24 we hypothesized that SCZ would exhibit decreased functional connectivity between the cerebellum and the reward network, which would correlate with more severe amotivation and anhedonia. Furthermore, we proposed that effective connectivity between these regions would be characterized by reduced cerebellar input to the reward network in SCZ patients. By elucidating the neural mechanisms underlying amotivation and anhedonia in SCZ, this study aims to identify potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for future interventions.

Methods

Participants

This study utilized two samples. Sample 1 comprised 62 SCZ patients and 61 age- and gender-matched HCs recruited from Peking University Sixth Hospital and the surrounding communities. Sample 2, which constituted part of the neuroimaging dataset published elsewhere,40 included 53 SCZ patients and 50 HC recruited from Castle Peak Hospital Hong Kong. All participants were Asian, and all SCZ participants met the diagnostic criteria according to DSM-IV Axis I Disorders.41–43 Eligibility criteria are detailed in Supplementary Methods, and demographic information for the 2 samples is provided in Table 1.

Measures

Clinical symptoms were assessed using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS)44 and the Scale of the Assessment of Negative Symptoms (SANS).45 Amotivation and anhedonia symptoms were assessed using the domain scores of avolition-apathy and anhedonia-asociality of SANS respectively. Participants’ IQ was estimated using the short form of the Chinese version of the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale.46

Image Acquisition

All participants underwent brain scanning in 3-T MRI scanners, acquiring anatomical T1-weighted images and blood oxygenation level dependent signals during the resting state. The scanning sequences were acquired with a sufficiently large field of view to ensure full coverage of both the cerebrum and cerebellum for most participants.47 Detailed scanning parameters can be found in Supplementary Methods.

Image Preprocessing

The anatomical images and resting-state functional MRI (rsfMRI) data were preprocessed using the robust preprocessing pipeline integrated into fMRIPrep 20.2.6.47 Anatomical preprocessing included intensity correction, skull stripping, brain tissue segmentation, and normalization to Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space. Preprocessing of rsfMRI signal involved head motion estimation, slice timing correction, coregistration, and normalization. Independent component analysis for automatic removal of motion artifacts (ICA-AROMA)48 was employed to identify noise components. Following Parkes et al.’s quality control workflow,49 1 SCZ patient in Sample 1 was excluded due to excessive head movement (>20% of the individual’s data were marked as head movement outliers). Further noise reduction was conducted in CONN toolbox v21.a,50 including smoothing with a 6 mm full-width at half maximum Gaussian kernel, regressing out outlier volumes, irrelevant brain issues (ie, white matter and cerebrospinal fluid), head-motion indicators (ie, Friston 12 parameters and 10 noise components of ICA-AROMA),48,49 and applying a bandpass filter (0.01–0.1 Hz) with linear detrending. Detailed preprocessing steps can be found in Supplementary Methods, and the workflow of data analysis workflow can be found in Figure 1.

In this study, we employed both functional and effective connectivity analyses to examine abnormal neural interactions. Functional connectivity refers to the temporal correlation between brain region time series, reflecting neural synchrony or coupling.51 Effective connectivity, estimated using spectral DCM, models neural interactions in the frequency domain by fitting the cross-spectral density to infer connectivity direction.52,53 We first identified key regions with abnormal functional connectivity in SCZ and then applied spectral DCM to assess the directionality of these alterations.

Traditional Functional Connectivity

Network-based Analysis.

Based on Haber and Knutson’s proposed model of the reward network,54 we identified 18 ROIs using the Harvard-Oxford atlas, including the bilateral nucleus accumbens, caudate, putamen, pallidum, thalamus, amygdala, hippocampus, OFC, and middle frontal gyrus. Additionally, 26 cerebellar ROIs were selected from the Automated Anatomical Labeling atlas.55 The final set of 44 ROIs was defined using CONN’s built-in atlas, and the mean signal from each ROI was extracted to construct the 44 × 44 functional connectivity matrix.

Group-level comparisons between SCZ and HC participants were performed using a Generalized Linear Model (GLM), controlling for age, gender, and mean framewise displacement (FD).56–58 Network-based statistics (NBS) was applied to identify disease-related subnetworks through nonparametric inference, grouping ROI-to-ROI connections into connected subnetworks.59 Suprathreshold connections were first identified using a connection-level threshold of P < .001, followed by network-level significance testing with false discovery rate (FDR)-correction (pFDR-corrected < 0.05). The same approach was used to examine subnetworks associated with amotivation and anhedonia symptoms in patients with SCZ, using a GLM while accounting for age, gender, and mean FD. This step integrated theory- and data-driven methods to identify abnormal subnetworks in patients from a broad perspective. However, NBS identifies networks with statistically significant differences as a whole, without implying that every individual connection is engaged. To further investigate the specific connections driving these network-level effects, we conducted a follow-up seed-based analysis using key regions from the NBS results.

Seed-based analysis.

Seed-based functional connectivity analyses were conducted to localize specific regions and coordinates exhibiting abnormal connectivity. Given the fine-grained functional organization of the cerebellum, which does not always align with anatomical subdivisions, we selected reward network regions from the identified subnetwork as seed regions to determine the cerebellar coordinates with altered connectivity. The mean signal from these reward network regions was extracted and correlated with whole-brain voxels using Pearson correlation, followed by Fisher Z transformation to enhance normality. Group-level functional connectivity differences between SCZ patients and HCs were then assessed using a GLM, controlling for age, gender, and mean FD, with statistical significance set at cluster-level pFDR-corrected < 0.05 and voxel level P < .001. This analysis identified cerebellar regions where patients with SCZ exhibited altered functional connectivity with reward-related areas compared to healthy controls.

To localize reward network coordinates, the identified cerebellar loci were used as reference points. Spherical ROIs (6 mm radius) were generated, and their mean signals were extracted to identify reward network regions with altered connectivity. This step was solely for coordinate mapping and did not involve independent statistical inference.

Correlation analysis between functional connectivity and symptoms.

We examined the relationship between abnormal functional connectivity and amotivation and anhedonia symptoms in patients with SCZ using Spearman’s rank correlation, controlling for age, gender, and mean FD in a partial correlation model. We applied the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure (q < 0.05) for multiple comparisons.

Cross-sample validation.

To enhance the robustness of our results and avoid data reuse bias (double-dipping), we first conducted a network-based functional connectivity analysis in Sample 1 to identify abnormal subnetworks. These subnetworks were then tested in Sample 2 using seed-based functional connectivity and symptom correlation analyses to assess their reproducibility. In Sample 1, the same analyses were conducted without statistical inference, solely to characterize the identified abnormal connectivity and guide subsequent DCM model specification.

Effective Connectivity Analysis Based on Spectral DCM

To further investigate the functional connectivity abnormalities associated with amotivation or anhedonia, we examined their directionality using effective connectivity analyses based on Spectral DCM.60 Unlike the traditional DCM, Spectral DCM employs a deterministic model that predicts cross-spectral densities based on a physiologically plausible framework of coupled neuronal fluctuations.60 This approach is specifically designed for rsfMRI, as it models spontaneous neuronal activity and its underlying effective connectivity without requiring task-related inputs.60 It could explain the effective connectivity between observed hemodynamic responses, and is applicable to psychiatric research.61,62 Following prior studies62 and the guidelines provided in the SPM manual,63 we identified brain regions exhibiting abnormal connectivity and extracted preprocessed signals from 6 mm-radius spheres centered on these coordinates. By fitting cross-spectral density, effective connectivity would be simulated, revealing both causal effects between regions and self-inhibition effects within regions at the individual level (measured in Hz). Analyses were conducted using the DCM module in SPM 12.63 Effect values were extracted and compared between SCZ and HC participants using independent sample t-tests.

Since effective connectivity analysis differs from traditional functional connectivity in its physiological basis, computational framework, and informational output, we performed Spectral DCM independently in both Sample 1 and Sample 2. Importantly, DCM regions in each sample were derived from its own functional connectivity analysis, preventing model specification bias.

Results

Network-based Analysis

Network-based analysis in Sample 1 found connectivity differences between patients with SCZ and HC. Two disrupted subnetworks in the SCZ group were identified (see Figure 2A). “Subnetwork 1” contained 62 connections involving 19 regions, primarily reflecting abnormal connectivity between the striatum (putamen, caudate), pallidum, and cerebellum (eg, lobules VI, VIIb, VIII), as well as altered connectivity within cerebellum itself (Network Mass = 1 076.48, pFDR < 0.001). “Subnetwork 2” consisted of 18 connections involving 8 regions, with disruptions between OFC and limbic regions (ie, hippocampus and amygdala; Network Mass = 335.28, pFDR = 0.004). Seed-based Analysis

Whole-brain functional connectivity of core regions in the reward network.

According to the network-based analysis results, we identified the putamen, caudate, and pallidum as core regions of the reward network and used them for seed-based whole-brain functional connectivity analysis. To validate these findings, we conducted whole-brain functional connectivity analyses in Sample 2, using the identified regions as seed points to localize cerebellar regions exhibiting abnormal connectivity in patients.

For the right putamen, results revealed a significant reduction in functional connectivity with the left cerebellar lobule VI in the patient group (peak coordinates: (−22, −64, −24), t = 4.60; cluster-level pFDR-corrected = 0.006; voxel-level P < .001; cluster size = 122 voxels; see Figure 3 and Table S3). Additionally, decreased connectivity was observed between the right putamen and the right middle frontal gyrus (peak coordinates: (42, 4, 44), t = 4.27; cluster-level pFDR-corrected = 0.04; voxel-level P < .001; cluster size = 78 voxels). Conversely, increased connectivity was found between the right putamen and the left caudate nucleus (peak coordinates: (−18, 8, 16), t = −4.78; cluster-level pFDR-corrected < 0.001; voxel-level P < .001; cluster size = 186 voxels) as well as the right thalamus (peak coordinates: (28, −22, 0), t = −4.82; cluster-level pFDR-corrected = 0.05; voxel-level P < .001; cluster size = 68 voxels). For the left putamen, we only found a significantly increased connectivity with right caudate in patients (peak coordinates: (−18, −12, 24), t = −4.68; cluster-level pFDR-corrected = 0.003; voxel-level P < .001; cluster size = 144 voxels).

In Sample 2, SCZ patients exhibited significantly reduced functional connectivity of the left cerebellar lobule VI with both the putamen and caudate nucleus. These associations remained significant after further adjusting for illness duration and medication dosage (amotivation: Spearman’s ρ = −0.43, P = .002; anhedonia: Spearman’s ρ = −0.32, P = .025). Effective Connectivity Analysis Based on Spectral DCM

Based on the correlation analysis results in Sample 1, we conducted effective connectivity analyses using spectral DCM for the right caudate-left cerebellar Crus I. Model 1 examined the connectivity between right caudate (centered at (16, 18, 8)) and left Crus I (centered at (−28, −44, −36)), revealing a significantly reduced excitatory effect from the cerebellar Crus I to the caudate in SCZ patients compared to HCs (t = 2.45, df = 121, P = .016). Given the similar direction and trend-level association observed between the right pallidum and vermis IV&V in the symptom analysis, as well as its relevance to cerebellar–basal ganglia communication, we included this pathway in Model 2. This model examined the connectivity between the right pallidum (centered at (22, 4, 8)) and vermis IV&V (centered at (−2, −62, −12)), and found reduced excitatory input from the cerebellar vermis IV&V to the pallidum in SCZ patients (t = 2.11, df = 121, P = .037; see Figure 4C). In Sample 2, we focused on the effective connectivity between the right putamen (centered at (26, 40, −8)) and the left cerebellar lobule VI (centered at (−24, −62, −24)), but we did not find any significant difference between the 2 groups.

Discussion

In this study, we examined the functional and effective connectivity between the cerebellum and the reward network in SCZ using a stepwise approach. Network-based analysis in Sample 1 first identified abnormalities within a cerebellar–basal ganglia subnetwork, including the putamen, caudate, and pallidum. Whole-brain functional connectivity analysis in Sample 2, using basal ganglia regions as seeds, revealed reduced connectivity between the left cerebellar lobule VI and both the putamen and caudate. Notably, putamen–lobule VI dysconnectivity was associated with greater amotivation and anhedonia severity. In Sample 1, altered connectivity between the caudate and left Crus I also showed a robust association with amotivation. In contrast, the pallidum–vermis IV&V connection demonstrated a similar pattern, though such association was less pronounced. Further spectral DCM analysis in Sample 1 indicated diminished excitatory input from Crus I and vermis IV&V to the basal ganglia, though this finding did not replicate in Sample 2.

The putamen, caudate, and pallidum exhibited the most pronounced abnormal connectivity to the cerebellum in SCZ patients. As key components of the basal ganglia, these regions are essential for reward processing, motor control, and cognitive regulation.64,65 The putamen and caudate together constitute the dorsal striatum, which receives dopaminergic projections from the substantia nigra in the midbrain.66 These 2 regions maintain information about the rewarding outcomes of actions67 and play a direct role in action selection and initiation related to reward pursuit.68,69 The pallidum, receiving input from the striatum, serves as a major output hub for limbic signals.65 Besides, the pallidum codes pleasurable experiences,70 regulates motivation intensity, and influences reward-seeking behaviors.71 Previous studies on SCZ have shown that these regions are abnormally enlarged,72,73 while their functional activity was reduced.74–76 This suggested that functional connectivity disruptions of these areas may be crucial to the pathophysiology of SCZ, particularly in relation to deficits in motivation and hedonic experience.

We identified cerebellar lobules VI, VIII, Crus I, and the vermis IV&V as core regions with decreased connectivity to the reward network in patients with SCZ. While vermis IV&V is a part of anterior lobe, lobules VI, VII, and Crus I, located in the posterior cerebellum, also known as the “cognitive cerebellum.”77 Unlike the anterior part, the posterior cerebellum lacks spinal input and direct connections with sensorimotor cortex,35 but connects to regions of higher-order cognitive functions, such as the prefrontal cortex, posterior parietal cortex, superior temporal gyrus.35 Meta-analyses have suggested that the posterior lobe, particularly lobules VI and Crus I, is involved in working memory, spatial tasks, executive functions, and emotional processing.78 Damage to these regions might disrupt cognitive loops with cerebral association cortices, leading to “cerebellar-cognitive-affective syndrome.”79 Previous studies have reported volume reduction of lobules VI, VIII, and Crus I in SCZ patients, linked to neurological soft-signs80 and executive function impairments.81 Abnormal functional connectivity between these regions and cortical/subcortical areas has also been widely observed in SCZ.82,83 These findings underscored the significant role of posterior cerebellum in the cognitive and emotional deficits seen in SCZ.

We found reduced functional connectivity in patients with SCZ between the dorsal striatum (caudate in Sample 1, putamen in Sample 2) and posterior cerebellum (Crus I in Sample 1, lobule VI in Sample 2), as well as between the pallidum and vermis IV&V, which was significantly correlated with the severity of amotivation symptoms. Animal fiber-tracing studies have shown the physiological connectivity between the cerebellum and basal ganglia,19,38 while human research has provided evidence of functional synchronization between these regions. For example, an event-related fMRI study found that the coactivation of basal ganglia and cerebellar regions was correlated with temporal difference prediction errors during appetitive conditioning with a pleasant taste reward.67 Additionally, a study of 228 healthy adults reported robust resting-state functional connectivity between cerebellar lobules V, VI, VIIb, VIIIa, and the pallidum/putamen, as well as between Crus I, Crus II, and the caudate,84 which aligns with the disrupted patterns observed in SCZ in our study. Previous studies in SCZ patients without hallucinations85 and in first-episode SCZ patients86 also reported similar reductions of functional connectivity and associations with impaired cognitive functions. Therefore, these findings suggested that abnormal cerebellum–basal ganglia connectivity may serve as a critical neuropathological mechanism in SCZ. Regarding the association between these abnormal functional connections and motivation-related symptoms, based on the functions of these regions, we propose that weakened functional connectivity between the basal ganglia and the cerebellum may impair the integration of reward-related information, hinder the selection of approach-related actions, and ultimately contribute to the development of amotivation and anhedonia.69,87

Spectral DCM analysis revealed that the abnormal cerebellum-basal ganglia connectivity in SCZ patients was mainly characterized by reduced excitatory input from the cerebellum to the basal ganglia. As mentioned before, SCZ is associated with volume reduction of cerebellum, particularly in the posterior lobe,81,88 which may suggest a reduced number of neurons or a decrease in synaptic density,89 both of which might weaken the output of signals. The “internal model” suggests that the cerebellum acts as a monitor, forming an inner framework to represent the environment and mental processes, via its connections with the cerebrum. Therefore, the cerebellum provides predictive signals that help adjust mental and behavioral responses.90,91 Decreased cerebellar projections to the striatum may weaken these predictive signals, and thus reduce the phasic activity in dopaminergic neurons.92 This reduction may lead to decreased encoding of reward-related actions in the dorsal striatum, contributing to amotivation. Similarly, reduced predictive signals from the cerebellum to the pallidum may impair motor function.93 Therefore, cerebellar neuromodulation may serve as an effective intervention for these symptoms, as suggested by clinical trials showing improvements in negative symptoms following cerebellar neuromodulation.94

This study has several limitations. First, the MRI scanning sequences used in our 2 samples were not specifically designed for the cerebellum. Future studies should optimize the spatial resolution, coverage, and radiofrequency pulse of the MRI sequences to better capture cerebellar signals. Second, we administered the SANS, a first-generation negative symptom scale, while second-generation scales (eg, CAINS) may be more effective for assessing negative symptoms. Additionally, this study inferred functional connectivity during the resting state, without examining changes during reward processing. It remained unclear whether functional connectivity abnormalities would be related to abnormal reward processing. Lastly, the results of Sample 1 were not fully replicated in Sample 2, possibly related to the differences between the samples (eg, Sample 1 had the more severe amotivation symptoms). Future research should study the longitudinal changes in cerebellum–basal ganglia connectivity, and their relationship with symptom progression to understand the temporal dynamics in SCZ.

Conclusions

Our findings highlighted the significant role of disrupted cerebellum–basal ganglia connectivity in SCZ. The reduced functional connectivity between the dorsal striatum and posterior cerebellum, and between the pallidum and cerebellum vermis IV&V, appeared to contribute to the amotivation symptoms in SCZ. Spectral DCM analysis suggested that weakened excitatory input from the basal ganglia may underlie these connectivity deficits. Our work provided additional insights regarding the neural mechanisms for amotivation in SCZ patients, and supported that the cerebellum-basal ganglia connectivity may serve as a potential target for therapeutic interventions.
