Can IFRS adoption mitigate earnings management in an emerging market?
Authors: Abdelnaser M Mohamed Amer, Asil Azimli, Muri Wole Adedokun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractResearch on the impact of the International Financial Reporting Standard (IFRS) mandate on the quality of financial reporting produced mixed findings. Several researchers showed that the IFRS mandate depresses earnings management since it improves comparability, and the principle-based approach offers limited alternatives for managers in reporting. Others have shown that it provides flexibility for managers in institutionally and legally weak countries. We examine whether the IFRS mandate depresses earnings management practices in South Africa. Using a sample of 206 firms listed on the Johannesburg Securities Exchange (JSE) from 2000 to 2010, our findings show that the IFRS mandate significantly reduces earnings management. Our findings contribute to the ongoing discussion about whether high-quality standards effectively mitigate earnings management practices and protect stakeholder rights in developing countries.Keywords: IFRS, Earnings management, Performance match discretionary accruals, Income smoothing, PCSE

Introduction

The International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) provides a standardized reporting framework to ensure accountability, efficiency, and transparency in global capital markets. IFRS helps harmonize financial reporting practices in different countries to enhance comparability. There has been widespread adoption of the IFRS, which is now used in more than 120 countries and is gradually becoming the global standard for the preparation of financial statements of public companies [1]. Despite this increasing adoption of IFRS, only 21 in 54 African countries mandated IFRS, while five countries permitted it due to a lack of local accounting standards [2].

Due to the diverse accounting traditions and institutional differences among countries, the widespread adoption of IFRS is possible, given its inner flexibility and principle-based approach to accounting standards [3]. Extending the use of IFRS across different countries is expected to enhance the financial assessment of investors and analysts due to standardized reports offering better comparability. It is further noted that the use of IFRS has several economic benefits, such as a lower cost of capital [4,5], increased institutional investments, capital flow, and liquidity [6,7], broader investor base [8], and higher economic growth [9]. Further, IFRS adoption significantly depressed earnings management (EM) practices in most of the advanced countries [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. However, Bryce et al. [10] reported that IFRS does not mitigate earning management in Australia. Malofeeva [16] shows that bigger firms engage more in EM in Russia. However, only limited studies have examined the impact of IFRS adoption in Africa. For example, Klish et al. [17] identified a reduction in EM for IFRS adopters in the Middle Eastern and North African (MENA) region. Adedokun et al. [18] identified significant differences in EM before and after adopting IFRS in Nigeria.

Users of accounting information expect financial reports to reflect accurate information about the reporting entity. Barth et al. [19] show that voluntarily adopting IFRS reduces earnings management practices. According to Carmona and Trombetta [3], IFRS is a fraud deterrent, given the flexibility offered by its principle-based nature. Daske et al. [20] classified voluntary IFRS adopters into two groups: firms that significantly modified their reporting practices after adoption (serious adopters) and firms that continued using their regular reporting standards (label adopters). Their findings show that serious adopters realized higher economic benefits. However, in the case of mandatory adoption, the intentions of firms may be different. For instance, Christensen et al. [21] examined the impact of IFRS adoption on several reporting quality measures (i.e., earnings management, timely loss recognition, and earnings management). They reported increased quality in financial reports of firms that voluntarily adopted the IFRS before 2005 in Germany. They did not find the same effect for mandatory adopters. Accounting quality is reported to have a stronger connection to incentives than standards [22,23].

Previous studies also show that a lack of institutional quality, legal system, enforcement, and control mechanisms may lead to differences in applying IFRS principles across adopting countries [24]. Adopting IFRS has limited economic benefits in countries with weak enforcement [25], poor institutional (e.g. Ref. [26]), and governance [27] settings. These examinations of IFRS's economic and reporting outcomes primarily focus on developed countries, with little attention on developing countries. Ball [28] argued that uneven adoption of IFRS standards may lead to different economic and quality outcomes across countries. Nobes' [29,30] survey revealed considerable differences across jurisdictions and exact IFRS versions adopted, attributing this to translating IFRS standards into national language and concepts. Accordingly, there is a need to examine the accounting quality outcomes of adopting IFRS in developing countries.

The South African Institute of Chartered Accountants (SAICA) began to harmonize IFRS with the South African GAAP (SA-GAAP) in 1995 and IFRS adoption (SA-IFRS) in 2005; both SA-GAAP and SA-IFRS are closely aligned. This study uses a sample of 209 firms listed on the Johannesburg Stock Exchange (hereafter JSE) from 2000 to 2010. We have excluded the transition year, i.e., 2005, and limited our sample to five years for the pre- and post-IFRS periods to limit the probable impact of other changes on findings. The main questions we tried to address in this study are: Is there a difference in earnings management practices under the SA-GAAP and SA-IFRS? Accordıngly, we contribute to the extant literature as follows: (a) assessing the existence of possible differences in ‘income-smoothing’ or ‘managed earnings’ between the SA-GAAP and SA-IFRS, and (b) evaluating the impact of SA-IFRS on EM measures.

Our study differs from the previous studies in two main aspects. Most previous studies considered EM measures using only the Jones-based approach [15,16,31]. These measures are limited because they do not control for the effects of firms' performance on accruals. We use the performance-matched discretionary accruals-based Kothari et al. [32] measure to proxy EM. The measure enhances the consistency of inference from EM [32]. We offer distinctive evidence on the direction (i.e., upward/downward) and the extent (i.e., large/small) of EM based on performance matching on return on assets. Second, most previous studies use either OLS pooled or fixed effect without verifying the samples’ cross-sectional dependence. We apply an approach that resolves the heteroskedasticity issue inherent in most panels by using a sandwich estimator based on the Panel Corrected Standard Error (hereafter PCSE), whose estimates are always efficient and consistent [33]. Third, previous studies mainly focused on developed countries to examine the impact of the IFRS mandate on earnings quality (e.g., Ref. [30]; Franzen & Weiβenberger, 2018, among others). Amidu and Issahaku [34] examined the impact of the IFRS mandate on the reporting quality of financial firms across African countries. Our study is one of the first to focus on a country from Africa that has the most liquid financial market and the highest market capitalization. Given the impact of institutional, political, and legislative settings on economic and financial reporting quality outcomes of adopting IFRS, our study adds to the literature on the benefits of IFRS adoption in developing countries.

The findings of this study have significant implications for the accounting field. They provide evidence of the mitigating role of IFRS adoption on earnings management, indicating a decrease in earnings manipulations among JSE firms. Moreover, the study identifies the influence of firms’ operating cash flow, return on equity, and sales growth on earnings management practices. These findings underscore the importance of policymakers' regular assessment of firms' reported financial information to safeguard investors' funds and the integrity of the capital market.

Following the Introduction, Section 2 reviews the relevant literature, Section 3 discusses the data and empirical methodology, Section 4 presents the empirical results, and Section 5 concludes the study.

Literature

Theoretical background

The practice of EM is nuanced and complex, involving managers' attempts to use personal judgment to adjust financial statement numbers, impacting a firm's underlying performance and making their firms look more appealing to various stakeholders. Brennan [35] emphasizes that not all EM is fraudulent, but it is a practice that many stakeholders are against due to its potential to distort financial reports. Adedokun et al. [18] consider it a significant practice that may cause financial loss to creditors and shareholders. Walker [36] notes that EM involves discretion over accounting principles to make a natural financial or economic choice that impacts underlying earnings measures and economic events. Beneish [37] explains both the opportunistic and information perspectives on EM, adding to the complexity of the topic.

Some studies propose considering EM based on the income smoothing hypothesis [[38], [39], [40], [41]]. The motivation for income smoothing remains unclear. The literature identifies two conflicting perspectives. The first encourages smoothing as an invaluable practice for firms in accounting earnings. In contrast, the other considers smoothing as a self-centered manipulation of earnings by managers to withhold accounting information [40]. This debate adds an intriguing layer to the understanding of EM. Gassen et al. [38] discussed two theories of income smoothing. The first viewed income smoothing as a channel for disclosing private future earnings information. This assumes that smoothing plays a signaling role in efficient capital markets. The second is that income smoothing is a manipulation tool managers use to mislead market participants about future earnings. Li and Richie [39] found that smoothing firms have higher credit ratings, lower cost of debt, and lower cost of capital. Udayakumara and Weerathunga [41] revealed higher earnings smoothing due to IFRS adoption in Sri Lanka, indicative of higher EM.

Research on EM is based on agency theory, a theoretical framework following an ideologically driven characterization of human nature. It assesses the relationship between management (agent) and shareholders (principal), according to which the principal engages the agent to employ essential measures to maximize shareholders' wealth. This contractual relationship raises questions about human nature and behavior, prompting thoughtful reflection. The manager aims to maximize profit, even at the expense of shareholders. The managers pursue self-interest, including stealing silver unless incentivized [42]. If information asymmetry exists, an agent may pursue self-interests unfavorable to the principal. This is because the pay-off structure of the claims of stakeholders is fundamentally different. Aligning different claims and interests is crucial to avoid potential stakeholder conflicts. The model characterizes ‘rational people as despicable and selfish. Existing evidence shows that human nature is more multifaceted than allowed for by economic rationality assumptions and that the behaviors of most people are not driven solely (or primarily) by self-interests ([43]; Mercier & Sperber, 2017).

Adopting a new set of principle-based standards may alleviate these problems by deterring managers from discretionary judgemental actions in reporting financial information. This is due to the increased integration of financial markets and increased globalization of financial markets. Our assumption is based on two key reasons. First, the principle-based IFRS restricts accounting choices, limiting managerial discretion [44]. Second, the globalization and integration of financial markets mean that increased comparability of accounting information prepared under IFRS may compel managers to reduce reporting interventions, judgemental applications, and manipulations [45,46].

Following the previous studies on reporting quality, we have adopted various earnings management measures to proxy reporting quality (e.g., Ref. [47]; Franzen & Weiβenberger, 2018; [30,48], among others). Specifically, we utilized discretionary and absolute discretionary accruals to proxy earnings management following the previous studies (e.g., Urgin et al., 20127; [16]).

Literature review

Previous studies showed that shifting to IFRS produces mixed results on EM practices ([9,49,50]; Isaboke; [12,51,52]). While several reported that adopting new standards does not significantly impact EM [10,15,16,53]. Others reported the economic benefits of IFRS adoption [4,7,12,52]. The European region, the pioneer of IFRS, has been the focus of IFRS adoption studies [11,49,52,54,55]. These studies have explored different aspects of IFRS adoption and its impact on EM, providing a comprehensive understanding of the topic. For instance, Dimitropoulos et al. [54] examined the effects of IFRS on 101 European firms from 2001 to 2008, finding that IFRS contributed less to EM. Ugrin et al. [55] further demonstrated that the relationship between IFRS and EM is not uniform across European countries, with EM being relatively higher after adoption.

The impact of adopting IFRS has been studied in other countries as well. In North America, Martínez [56] analyzed the impact of the convergence to IFRS on the accounting quality of 75 firms in Mexico from 2010 to 2011. They found a significant decrease in EM. Kousay [15] explored the impact of adopting a new set of standards on EM for 791 firms in Canada over the period 2000 and 2018 and found no direct influence on EM. Using a sample of 200 firms, Bryce et al. [10] reported a positive impact of IFRS adoption on accounting quality in Australia between 2003 and 2008. Abuda and Rudiawarni [53] examined 169 firms in Indonesia from 2010 to 2012. They discovered higher EM during the post-IFRS period. Baig and Khan (2016) examined how IFRS impacted EM for 100 Pakistan firms from 2001 to 2009; they concluded that EM may be less pervasiveness. Udayakumara and Weerathunga [41] investigated the impact of the IFRS on EM in 157 Sri Lankan firms and reported a higher level of EM. Awan (2022) examined the impact of IFRS and accounting quality on banking sector efficiency in Pakistan and found a significant positive correlation between discretionary weights and IFRS but a significant negative correlation between IFRS and earnings per share. They established that adopting IFRS enhanced efficiency and optimized its audit quality. In Africa, Adedokun et al. [18] examined the impact of IFRS on accrual-based EM for 125 Nigerian firms from 2003 to 2020; they showed a decrease in EM. Mensah [31] investigated the same issue in Ghana and reported a lower EM, measured by the Modified Jones’ discretionary accruals method, during the post-IFRS period. Odoemelam et al. [57] examined the relationship between IFRS and EM for 101 Nigerian firms and concluded that IFRS resulted in higher EM. Yeboah and Yeboah [58] examined 181 South African (SA) firms between 1998 and 2012. They found that the implementation of IFRS resulted in improved accounting quality. Some studies report financial report manipulations due to a weak institutional environment and implementation of the IFRS in less developed countries [14,58]. Others showed that the IFRS mandate increases earnings quality in countries with stronger investor protection but not others ([10,15,31]; Wan et al., 2013). Furthermore, several firms (e.g., LeisureNet, Masterbond, and Regal Bank) were accused of reporting scandals [59], which have resulted from some magnitude of earnings mismanagement, amongst other things. The situation motivates us to evaluate how IFRS adoption impacts managed earnings dynamics amongst the JSE firms.

Previous studies indicate that IFRS adoption does not guarantee better economic outcomes without developed institutions, enforcement, and legal support [46,60]. Further, Callao and Jarne [61] reported higher earnings management across European financial firms, attributing this to greater flexibility under the IFRS regime. Tandeloo and Vastrealen (2011) examined the extent of earnings management under IFRS and pre-IFRS regimes in Germany. Their findings are similar to earnings management practices under two accounting regimes. In contrast, Zéghal et al. [62] report lower earnings management, thus higher reporting quality, following the IFRS mandate in France. Aubert and Grudnitski [63] report lower earnings management in 20 European countries following the IFRS mandate. Leventis et al. [64] report lower earnings management for European banks following the adoption. Eiler et al. [65] examined the impact of IFRS on earnings management practices in Mexico. They found that firms with cross-listings in the U.S. were engaged less in earnings smoothing after adopting the new standards, highlighting the importance of institutional setting. Tsipouridou and Spathis [66] examine earnings management practices in Greece during pre- and post-IFRS periods. Their findings show that IFRS had no impact on the level of earnings management, attributing this finding to the unique institutional setting in Greece, where there are strong bonds with auditors and reporting entities. Using the Mexican sample, Pelucia-Grecco et al. (2014) showed that the strength of the regulatory environment limits managers in engaging in earnings management, while IFRS adoption had a limited impact. According to the findings of Wang and Campbell [67] from China, state ownership limits managerial opportunistic reporting behavior. However, IFRS did not improve the reporting quality, measured by the extent of earnings management.

Given the mixed findings on the impact of IFRS adoption on EM, specifically in the African region where the institutions are not established, the question of whether IFRS adoption offers benefits needs further attention. Our study aims to fill this gap in the literature. We consider EM based on the performance-matched discretionary accruals. This measure enhances the consistency of inference from EM. It allows for the capture of distinctive evidence of both the ‘direction' and ‘extent' of EM-based on performance matching on return on assets. Our study may provide valuable insights for future studies in this field.

Methodology

Data source and sample selection

The data is sourced from the McGregor BFA database and Eikon Datastream. We start to construct our sample considering all listed companies in the JSE for which annual data is available. Initially, we exclude firms with missing data required to calculate essential variables used in estimations. This left us with 206 firms, corresponding to 59.88 % of the total market capitalization of JSE firms from 2000 to 2010. Furthermore, we exclude the transition year that IFRS mandated for all South African firms jointly with Europe (i.e., 2005) to limit the impact of other factors that may influence earnings management practices over the more extended period [5,27,68]. Thus, we used five years for the pre-and five years for the post-IFRS periods in our estimations. Table 1 presents the breakdown for firms over the sample period. We decided to measure earnings management as the variable of interest since it is one of the most critical reporting quality measures [47].

Model

Different aspects of EM required to evaluate the working hypotheses are tested as highlighted: (1) difference in the variability of change in net income residuals, which indicates the stability of reported earnings, (2) ratio of the standard deviation of change in net income to standard deviation of cash flow change, which measures the extent to which earnings are managed, (3) directional values of performance-matched discretionary accruals, which show the direction of earnings management. DAC, and (4) absolute values of |DAC| [16,48,55]. The first and second measures are income smoothing [19,54]; the third and fourth measures are discretionary accruals (DAC) and their absolute value. DAC captures the direction (i.e., increase or decrease) in earnings manipulations, while |DAC| measures the extent of managerial discretion on earnings manipulation [[69], [70], [71], [72]].

Initially, we conducted two tests to examine if IFRS adoption affects EM in South Africa. Specifically, the first set of tests analyzes the impact of adoption on income smoothing. Following Barth et al. [19] and Dimitropoulos et al. [54], we measure income smoothing from the change in net income residuals, which is based on two measures: (1) The difference in the variability of change in net income residuals [ΔNIσe1], which is obtained from the pooled OLS of equation (1), separately for pre- and post-IFRS period. Then, we perform the Wilcoxon test on the difference in standard deviation (σ) of the residuals. The prediction is that managers apply no discretion to smooth earnings. Hence, income is expected to be volatile and fluctuate over time when the accounting reporting standard does not permit discretion and flexibility. If the variability of ΔNIi,t is lower after IFRS adoption, it means that the new set of standards mitigates income smoothing, and vice versa [19,49,71,72].

Furthermore, we assessed the ratio of the standard deviation of the annual change in net income (ΔNIi,t) to the standard deviation of annual change in cash flows (ΔCFOi,t). This measure is limited by confounding effects by factors such as economic settings and incentives to switch to IFRS [19]. Dimitropoulos et al. [54] suggest using the ratio of standard deviation of change in net income (ΔNIσe1) to standard deviation of cash flow change (ΔCFOσe2) residuals from the pooled OLS regressions as given in equation (2) and (2′):

ΔNIi,t is change in net income scaled by the lagged total assets of firm i in year t; ΔCFOi,t is the annual change in cash flow from operations scaled by the lagged total assets of firm i in year t; SIZEi,t is the log of the firm's total assets for in year t; LEVGi,t is total liabilities to total assets ratio; GROWi,t is the percent change in sales for firm i in year t; BIG4 is a dummy assigned with a value of 1 if the BIG4 audits the firm, and 0 otherwise, and ROAi,t is the return on assets for firm i in year t. EM metric (EMi)=ΔNIσe1/ΔCFOσe2.

When ΔNIσe1<ΔCFOσe2, the spread of net income change should be less than the spread of change in operating cash flows, providing a lower positive value for the given accounting regime. If volatility of ΔNIσe1 is higher than that of ΔCFOσe2, EM is lower in the post-IFRS period [19,54]. Ahmed et al. [73] noted that when managers apply accruals to smooth the volatility in cash flows, the standard deviation of the change in net income (ΔNIσe1) would be typically less than the standard deviation of the change in cash flows (ΔCFOσe2). Intuitively, the smaller the value of this ratio, the greater the income smoothing.

We also evaluate any differences in managed earnings between the SA-GAAP and SA-IFRS. We apply the Welch t-test to test if the directional values of DAC and |DAC|, are equal for SA-GAAP and SA-IFRS. A statistically significant difference indicates differences in EM practices under two accounting regimes, local GAAP versus IFRS.

For the empirical estimation of the model, we start by testing if the pooled regression (PR) approach is appropriate. The Breusch-Pagan LM test is used to confirm whether the panel analysis is required to continue with pooled OLS by comparing the result of a pseudo PR of (3), (4) with the Random Effects (RE). We compare the results to evaluate the best model based on the power of each explanatory variable (t-tests), the predictive power of the overall model (Pseudo/Adjusted-R-square), and the joint significance of explanatory variables (F-test).

Despite the previous findings that IFRS adoption does not guarantee improved accounting quality in countries with weak institutions and enforcement [46,60,65], our findings align with those who reported lower earnings management following the adoption of IFRS (e.g., Ref. [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]). Several researchers offered possible reasons for the tendency of managers to engage less in earnings management under the IFRS regime. For instance, according to Carmona and Trombetta [3], IFRS is a fraud deterrent, given the flexibility offered by its principle-based nature. Consistent with this, Ashbaugh and Pincus [44] argued that the principle-based IFRS restricts accounting choices, limiting managerial discretion. Further, according to Van Tendeloo and Vanstraelen [45] and Jeanjean and Stolowy [46], increased comparability brought by a uniform set of standards, integration of financial markets, and cross-country investments may compel managers to reduce reporting interventions and manipulations.

The coefficient estimate for the IFRS is negative (−0.099) and significant (p-value = 0.002) at 1 % for the DACi,t model. Thus, adopting the IFRS reduced performance-matched discretionary accruals, confirming the findings in Table 5, Table 6. This result is consistent with the prediction that IFRS adoption significantly reduces earning management for JSE-listed firms. This finding corroborates those of De-Moura et al. [5], Malofeeva [16], and Ugrin et al. [55]. Several channels can explain the reason for the negative impact of IFRS on EM. First, since IFRS standardizes the reporting practices with uniform rules and guidelines, it would be more difficult for managers to manipulate earnings. Second, improved disclosures under the IFRS principle make it harder for managers to engage in EM activities. Third, improved comparability under IFRS standards makes it harder for managers to engage in EM since reports become comparable under the IFRS regime [45,46]. This can make identifying outliers and irregularities easier and thus discourage managers from engaging in EM. In addition, the results show that return on equity, operating cash flow, and sales growth are negatively related to EM, implying that managers of companies with more robust sales and profits are less likely to manage earnings. Finally, IFRS adoption often comes with more regulations, making manipulating financial statements harder.

The signs for the categorical control variables that measure the extent of a firm's accountability to directors (γ10), nepotism or reliance on professional management positions (γ11), corporate ethics (γ12) are all insignificant. The interactions with IFRS-adoption γ14, γ15 and γ16, were all significant EM predictors. The coefficient for the BIG4 is positive and significant, as well as consistent with apriori; hence, it confirms the findings of previous studies that weak performance leads to greater EM [13,19]. The coefficient of the audit firm size is small and not significant, corresponding to findings by Bozec [76], which shows that with IFRS, firms are monitored more by financial analysts, who serve as external controllers that limit EM.

The absolute performance-matched discretionary accruals model |DAC|i,t, δ1 which measures the influence of adoption on EM is negative and significant. This indicates that IFRS reduces the magnitude and extent of earnings manipulation even when proxied by alternative measures. The mean value of discretionary accruals decreased by −0.415 in the IFRS period. Consistent with the findings from Equation (3).

When we compare our findings with those of existing studies, our findings are contrary to those of Bryce et al. [10], Kousay [15], and Malofeeva [16], who showed that IFRS does not reduce EM. On the other hand, our findings are in line with the studies of Baig and Khan (2016), Dimitropoulos et al. [54], Martínez [56], Mensah [31], and Yeboah and Yeboah [58], who reported that IFRS adoption helps to alleviate the EM problem. In general, our findings support the findings of studies that reported higher reporting quality following adopting IFRS despite the weaker institutional setting. We offered several reasons for this in the spirit of Carmona and Trombetta [3], who argued that IFRS is a fraud deterrent, Ashbaugh and Pincus [44] who argued that the principle-based IFRS restricts accounting choices, limiting managerial discretion\ and Van Tendeloo and Vanstraelen [45] and Jeanjean and Stolowy [46], who argued that higher comparability of uniform set of standards depresses managers intentions to engage in earnings management practices.

Sensitivity test

We estimate equations (5), (6) to check for the sensitivity and robustness of our findings. ρ8 and θ8 are now the estimates for IFRSi,t for the discretionary accruals |DAC|. In our estimation of the PCSE models, we implement a recursive approach to mitigate the endogeneity problem and check the robustness of our results. The PCSE approach addresses some specific issues that may arise in panel data. Since observations are distributed across times and different firms, problems such as heteroskedasticity, autocorrelation, and cross-sectional dependence of error terms are common in panel data sets [77]. By adjusting standard errors of the coefficient to these known problems, PCSE provides more reliable estimates because traditional standard errors assume independent and identically distributed errors [78]. Thus, unlike most previous studies, we correct for issues of cross-sectional dependency, autocorrelation, and heteroskedasticity in the panel.

The results are reported in Table 8 (for the DAC models) and Table 9 (for the |DAC| models). We run eleven different models for each of DAC and the |DAC|. The Chi-square (χ2) tests indicates a probability greater than 5 % (Untabulated) for each model estimation, which confirms our random effects approach for the PCSE estimations. For both variants of the performance-matched discretionary accruals, the coefficient of the post-adoption (ρ8 and θ8) are negative and significant, consistent with the main findings. The magnitude of influence slightly increases as other control variables are added recursively. Therefore, our findings are robust against different estimation procedures.

Conclusions

We examine whether the IFRS mandate limits managers’ earnings management behavior in South Africa. Our sample period is from 2000 to 2010, excluding the transition year, covering five years for the pre-adoption and five years for the post-adoption periods. We consider all listed firms in the JSE with non-missing data, corresponding to 206 firms. Using several earnings management measures, including proxies for income smoothing and discretionary accruals, our findings show that the IFRS mandate depresses managerial earnings management practices in South Africa. Our findings are robust against alternative estimation methods that control for some known problems associated with panel data sets, such as heteroscedasticity, autocorrelation, and cross-correlations of error terms across observation entities.

Our study relates to a strand of literature that examines the impact of different accounting standards on reporting quality. Although this issue has been investigated in numerous studies for developed countries, evidence related to developing countries, specifically in Africa, is scarce. Given the importance of institutional settings, legal systems, and enforcement mechanisms in realizing the benefits of reporting quality by changing accounting standards, it is essential to extend our understanding of developing markets. Accordingly, the main contribution of our study is that it documents the significant impact of the IFRS mandate on earnings management in South Africa, a developing market. Further, given our sample setting, our findings are also related to studies that offered possible mechanisms to explain the impact of IFRS adoption on reporting quality. For instance, some studies show that IFRS adoption may not lead to improved financial reporting in institutionally weaker settings [46,60,65]. However, our findings show that IFRS can depress earnings management practices in South Africa, providing support for the arguments that IFRS may be a fraud deterrent [3], principle-based IFRS restricts accounting choices [44], and increased comparability deters managers from engaging in earnings management [45,46].

Considering these results, policymakers should consider transitioning to the IFRS regime. Further, they may use our findings to enhance reporting quality in developing countries. They may also improve the enforcement of disclosures to improve transparency, especially in accounts prone to earnings management. For instance, by enhancing disclosure requirements and enforcement, policymakers may promote transparency in financial reports and enable various stakeholders to make more informed decisions. Also, developing a robust monitoring and enforcement mechanism to ensure compliance with the IFRS may help increase reporting quality, reveal the potential of principle-based standards, as shown in the previous literature, and limit managers from engaging in earnings management practices. Additionally, we recommend the implementation of robust internal control mechanisms for practitioners. This will enhance adherence to IFRS, acting as an early detection system to limit earnings management practices. Practitioners should also cultivate a corporate culture to promote ethical reporting practices and transparency.

Our study has some limitations, offering future research opportunities. First, only mandatory IFRS adoption based on the regulator's official implementation is considered. Issues surrounding voluntary adoption by individual firms before the official mandate in 2005 were ignored entirely [2]. The analysis in developing markets can be expanded to cover voluntary adopters. This will enable us to learn more about managerial intentions on utilizing IFRS in different institutional settings. Second, we did not complete a separate test on detecting earnings management practices at the firm level. We follow reports and prior evidence for the country that EM exists [59]. Hence, we compute two measures of EM and confirm how these complementary control variables affect each. Future research may wish to implement further improvement to calculate and detect the level of EM or degree of financial statement fraud using alternative methods, including the distribution of ratio approach, Beneish M-scores, and others. Third, we follow proponents that firms manipulate earnings linearly. Some research questions the linear motivation for EM by supposing that discretionary accruals follow a non-linear dynamic [79,80]. This has not been tested for the performance-matched discretionary accrual adopted for this study. Future research may assume non-linear dynamics for the performance-matched discretionary accrual if detection tests confirm the relation.
