Efficacy and Safety of Antiplatelet Therapy After Endovascular Aortic Repair: A Target Trial Emulation
Authors: Ki Hong Choi, Danbee Kang, Jihee Nam, Take Kyu Park, Myung Gyu Song, Tae‐Seok Seo, Woo Jin Yang, Seung‐Hyuk Choi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe optimal antiplatelet regimen after standard endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) in patients without atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease remains uncertain. Clinical guidelines offer limited recommendations, and data specific to this population are sparse. This study aimed to compare the efficacy and safety of single versus dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) in patients undergoing standard EVAR for abdominal aortic aneurysms without established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.MethodsThis study emulated a target trial using the Korean National Health Insurance Service database, which includes the entire Korean population. We identified all patients who underwent standard EVAR between January 2003 and September 2023 and excluded those with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or no antiplatelet therapy. Patients were categorized by discharge medication into single antiplatelet therapy or DAPT groups. The primary cardiovascular efficacy end point was major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (all‐cause death, myocardial infarction, or ischemic stroke). The EVAR‐specific efficacy end point was abdominal aortic aneurysm reintervention or surgery. The safety end point was major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding. Inverse probability treatment weighting was applied to adjust baseline differences.ResultsAmong 8325 patients, 4162 (50.0%) received single antiplatelet therapy (80.6% aspirin, 19.4% clopidogrel), and 4163 (50.0%) received DAPT (aspirin plus clopidogrel). There were no significant differences between single antiplatelet therapy and DAPT in the risk of major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (inverse probability treatment weighting‐hazard ratio [HR], 1.07 [95% CI, 0.93–1.22]) or abdominal aortic aneurysm reintervention/surgery (inverse probability treatment weighting‐HR, 1.07 [95% CI, 0.92–1.24]). However, DAPT was associated with a higher risk of major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding (inverse probability treatment weighting‐HR, 1.20 [95% CI, 1.02–1.41]).ConclusionsIn patients without atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease undergoing standard EVAR, DAPT did not confer cardiovascular or procedural benefit but was associated with increased bleeding. Single antiplatelet therapy may represent a safer and equally effective antiplatelet strategy in this population.RegistrationURL: https://clinicaltrials.gov; Unique Identifier: NCT06876727.Keywords: bleeding, dual antiplatelet therapy, EVAR, real‐world data, target trial emulationSubject Categories: Aneurysm, Secondary Prevention

Endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) has become a popular treatment option for patients with abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAAs) who have suitable anatomy for EVAR due to its minimally invasive nature. 1 , 2 , 3 Previous research on aneurysm pathophysiology has highlighted the possible role of platelet activation in the development of nonocclusive intraluminal thrombus, aneurysm progression, and eventual rupture. 4 In this regard, the general guidelines of the European Society of Cardiology and those of the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association recommend the use of single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT) with clopidogrel or low‐dose aspirin as a class IIa (for the European Society of Cardiology) and class IIb (for the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association) indication for patients with complex atherosclerotic plaque in the aorta. 5 , 6 However, the efficacy and safety of the addition of a second antiplatelet agent as dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) after EVAR remain inconclusive.

The newly updated European Society for Vascular Surgery guidelines recommend considering the use of DAPT in the early postoperative period for patients at risk of bridge stent patency failure following EVAR for complex AAAs. 7 This recommendation is based on a recent Delphi expert consensus report, which suggested prescribing DAPT for up to 6 months following fenestrated/branched EVAR (f/b EVAR). 8 A recent multicenter registry further demonstrated that DAPT after f/b EVAR was associated with a lower risk of cardiovascular ischemic events and greater target vessel patency compared with SAPT. 9 However, these studies primarily involved f/b EVAR, which incorporates small‐caliber target vessels. Given the distinct anatomical and procedural characteristics of f/b EVAR, these findings may not be generalizable to standard EVAR. In addition, existing evidence on antiplatelet therapy remains limited due to the absence of randomized controlled trials, small sample sizes, heterogeneity in antiplatelet regimens, and inadequate reporting of clinically relevant outcomes, particularly in standard EVAR. 10 , 11 , 12 Importantly, the risk of complications, including bleeding, endoleaks, and reinterventions, associated with the use of antiplatelet agents after standard EVAR, remains unclear. 13 Therefore, further evidence is needed to evaluate the role of antiplatelet therapy in standard EVAR settings.

Given these uncertainties, robust clinical evidence is needed to guide antiplatelet management following EVAR. Therefore, the current study aims to evaluate the efficacy and safety of antiplatelet therapy in patients undergoing standard EVAR for AAAs without definite atherosclerotic disease using a nationwide cohort.

METHODS

We used the claims data provided by the Korean National Health Insurance Service (K‐NHIS) database. Data can only be accessed by visiting the K‐NHIS datacenter after receiving approval from the K‐NHIS data access committee. Those who want to access the data set of this study should contact the corresponding authors, who will help with the process of contacting the K‐NHIS.

Study Design and Data Sources

This study emulated a target trial using the National Health Claims database established by the K‐NHIS. We conducted this study based on a predeveloped protocol as a hypothetical randomized controlled trial (Table S1). The study followed Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines. The protocol was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (registration number: NCT06876727) before patient enrollment. The K‐NHIS database represents the entire population of Korea, 14 and all citizens are continuously enrolled unless they become ineligible due to emigration or death.

Study Participants

Among the 52 million Korean citizens included in the K‐NHIS database, we included all patients who underwent EVAR for AAA and were successfully discharged between January 2003 and September 2023 (N=16 458). Among the participants, we excluded patients who had experienced a myocardial infarction (MI; N=684), stroke (N=1567), or percutaneous coronary intervention within 1 year before the index hospitalization (N=615) or had a history of peripheral artery disease (N=874), because these patients require DAPT. Patients were also excluded if they had a major bleeding event within 1 year of admission, such as intracranial hemorrhage or gastrointestinal bleeding (N=1067), because these events could substantially influence clinical decisions on antiplatelet therapy. In practice, patients with recent major bleeding are more likely to receive SAPT or no therapy due to elevated bleeding risk, thereby introducing potential confounding by indication. Those prescribed non‐vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants or warfarin were also excluded to reduce confounding by anticoagulation effects (N=1084). Furthermore, because current clinical guidelines recommend the use of SAPT for patients with an AAA, regardless of whether they have symptomatic atherosclerotic disease, we excluded patients who were not prescribed any antiplatelet therapy (N=4669). Because study participants could meet >1 exclusion criterion, a total of 8133 patients were excluded. Consequently, the remaining 8325 patients who underwent standard EVAR were included in the final analysis (Figure S1). This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of Samsung Medical Center, Seoul, Korea (institutional review board number: SMC‐2024‐02‐035). Written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study using deidentified administrative data.

Measurement

The K‐NHIS contains comprehensive Korean health care information, including diagnoses, prescriptions, surgical procedures, length of hospitalization, length of prescription, dose, and costs. All procedures and prescriptions (mapped to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification System) are coded using domestic codes. Diagnoses are coded using the Korean Standard Classification of Diseases, Seventh Revision, and a modified version of the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10). Because the K‐NHIS routinely audits the claims, these data are considered reliable and have been used in multiple previous publications. 15 , 16

Patients were identified using procedure codes for EVAR, either the endovascular aortic repair procedure code (M6611) combined with the AAA diagnostic code as a primary or secondary diagnosis or AAA‐specific procedure codes (M6612). In this study, we restricted our analysis to standard EVAR and did not include f/b EVAR. Among these patients, those who were prescribed SAPT or DAPT at discharge from the index hospitalization for standard EVAR were classified into the SAPT group and the DAPT group, respectively.

Data on income at the time of the first screening examination were obtained from the insurance eligibility database, and income level was categorized based on whether the patient was receiving medical aid or not. The residential area at the time of the first screening examination was classified as metropolitan or rural. Health behaviors and laboratory tests were obtained from the national health screening examination database, using the most recent data from within 4 years before the index hospitalization for EVAR. Smoking habits, frequency of drinking, and amount of alcohol consumed were also collected by self‐administered questionnaires during the national health screening examination.

Comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease requiring dialysis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, congestive heart failure, liver cirrhosis, cancer, and anemia, were defined by diagnosis codes within 1 year before the index hospitalization for EVAR (Table S2). Concomitant medications included NSAIDs, steroids, proton pump inhibitors, H2 blockers, cilostazol, and statins. Long‐term use of NSAIDs and steroids was defined as prescription of these medications for at least 90 and 30 days, respectively, between 180 days before index hospitalization and 14 days after the index hospitalization. A high bleeding risk was defined by the Academic Research Consortium. 17

End Points

The primary cardiovascular efficacy end point was major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) at 1 year, which were defined as a composite of all‐cause death, MI, or ischemic stroke. Individual components of the MACCE were considered secondary cardiovascular efficacy end points. The primary AAA‐specific efficacy end point was reintervention or surgery for AAAs at 1 year. We also included acute mesenteric and acute limb ischemia, defined by ICD‐10 codes in the primary or secondary position (Table S1), as AAA‐specific secondary end points.

MI (I21–I23) was defined as the presence of diagnostic codes in the primary position during hospitalization. In a validation study, the accuracy of MI diagnosis in the K‐NHIS data was 93%. 18 Ischemic stroke was defined based on hospitalization for ischemic stroke or an emergency department visit, as well as an additional clinic visit with a primary diagnosis of ischemic stroke (ICD‐10: G45, I63, I64). Reintervention or surgery for AAAs was defined as the presence of procedure codes for EVAR or open repair surgery with an AAA diagnostic code.

The primary safety end point was major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding at 1 year. Major bleeding requiring transfusion was defined as the presence of diagnostic codes in the primary or secondary position and a record of transfusion receipt. Clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding was defined as a bleeding event that necessitated a clinic visit or hospitalization, regardless of whether transfusion was required. Intracranial hemorrhage was also considered a bleeding event and defined as hospitalization for intracranial hemorrhage or an emergency department visit (ICD‐10: I60‐62) with a primary diagnosis.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using mean±SD for continuous variables and frequency with proportion for categorical variables. For the clinical outcome analysis, the 1‐year cumulative incidence of each clinical outcome was estimated using Kaplan‐Meier curves, and differences between groups were compared using log‐rank tests. Patients were followed from discharge until the date of an event, death, or 1 year after discharge, whichever came first. We calculated hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs for the incidence of clinical outcomes using the Cox regression model, adjusting for age, sex, chronic kidney disease requiring dialysis, liver cirrhosis, cancer, anemia, use of steroids, NSAIDs, cilostazol, proton pump inhibitors, H2 blockers, and statins.

To address the potential clinical implications, we conducted a sensitivity analysis including 3321 patients who met all inclusion and exclusion criteria among 4669 patients who did not receive any antiplatelet prescription at discharge. The same definitions of end points and Cox regression model were applied to this 3‐group comparison (no antiplatelet therapy, SAPT, and DAPT).

To address potential confounding and balance baseline covariates, the propensity score was calculated using logistic regression and was then used to perform inverse probability treatment weighting (IPTW) analysis. We further adjusted for covariates with a standardized mean difference >0.2 even after IPTW. Furthermore, we conducted subgroup analyses by age, sex, diabetes, Academic Research Consortium‐High Bleeding Risk, use of proton pump inhibitors or H2 blockers, and use of statins.

All analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide (version 7.1; SAS Institute) and R version 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A 2‐tailed P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

Of the 8325 patients (mean age, 74.8 years; 84.0% men), 4162 patients (50.0%) received SAPT (80.6% aspirin and 19.4% clopidogrel), and the remaining 4163 patients (50.0%) received DAPT (aspirin plus clopidogrel) after undergoing standard EVAR (Table 1). Compared with patients who were prescribed SAPT at discharge after EVAR, those who were prescribed DAPT had similar baseline demographics (standardized mean differences <0.2), except for the use of statins.

Efficacy End Points

During the 1‐year follow‐up period, there was no significant difference in the risk of MACCE between the SAPT and DAPT groups (SAPT versus DAPT, 9.1% versus 9.4%; HR, 1.07 [95% CI, 0.92–1.24]) (Figure 1A and Table 2). The results were also consistent after IPTW adjustment (IPTW HR, 1.07 [95% CI, 0.93–1.22]). The risk of all‐cause death, MI, and ischemic stroke did not differ significantly between the 2 groups (Table 2). Compared with patients who did not receive antiplatelet therapy, the adjusted HR for MACCE was 0.91 (95% CI, 0.79–1.06) in the SAPT group and 0.97 (95% CI, 0.84–1.13) in the DAPT group (Table S3). In subgroup analyses, the results were consistently observed across all subgroups (Figure S2).

For AAA‐specific efficacy end points, the 1‐year cumulative incidence rates were 0.1% for acute mesenteric ischemia (IPTW‐adjusted HR, 0.68 [95% CI, 0.21–2.14]) and 2.2% for acute limb ischemia (IPTW‐adjusted HR, 0.84 [95% CI, 0.63–1.13]) in the DAPT group, and 0.2% and 2.5%, respectively, in the SAPT group (Table 2). The incidence of 1‐year cumulative reintervention or surgery for AAAs was 6.2% and 6.5% in the SAPT and DAPT groups, respectively (Figure 1B). Compared with the SAPT group, the IPTW‐adjusted HR was 1.03 (95% CI, 0.86–1.23) in the DAPT group (Table 2). Compared with patients who did not receive antiplatelet therapy, the adjusted HRs for AAA reintervention or surgery were 0.96 (95% CI, 0.80–1.16) and 0.99 (95% CI, 0.82–1.20), respectively (Table S3). In subgroup analyses, the results were consistently observed across all subgroups (Figure S3).

Safety End Points

For the primary safety end point, the 1‐year cumulative incidence of major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding was 7.2% and 8.8% in the SAPT and DAPT groups, respectively (Figure 1C). Patients who were prescribed DAPT at discharge after EVAR was associated with significantly higher risk of major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding compared with those who were prescribed SAPT (IPTW‐adjusted HR, 1.20 [95% CI, 1.02–1.41]; Table 2). The risk of major bleeding or intracranial hemorrhage was also higher in the DAPT group compared with the SAPT group, although no statistical significance was observed (Table 3). Compared with patients who did not receive antiplatelet therapy, the adjusted HRs for major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding were 1.01 (95% CI, 0.85–1.21) for SAPT and 1.22 (95% CI, 1.02–1.45) for DAPT (Table S3).

DISCUSSION

In this large nationwide cohort study, DAPT after standard EVAR for AAAs was associated with a higher incidence of bleeding events, but there were no differences in the risks of cardiovascular and AAA‐specific ischemic end points compared with SAPT.

In the current study, the incidence of MACCE was comparable between the DAPT and SAPT groups, suggesting that the additional platelet inhibition provided by DAPT did not lead to a meaningful clinical benefit in this population. This lack of difference may be due to the limited role of antiplatelet therapy in preventing EVAR‐related cardiovascular events. Although DAPT after f/b EVAR was associated with a lower risk of cardiovascular ischemic events and improved target vessel patency, 9 we did not observe a similar clinical benefit of DAPT over SAPT in patients undergoing standard EVAR. Based on results from randomized controlled trials focusing on the effects of antiplatelet therapy for primary prevention, 19 , 20 , 21 antiplatelet therapy is no longer routinely recommended but is instead considered beneficial only in specific patients at high cardiovascular risk and with minimal risk of bleeding. Among patients with AAAs who did not undergo surgical or endovascular repair procedures, there was no evidence supporting the effectiveness of antiplatelet therapy in reducing ischemic events, with a trend toward an increased risk of bleeding. 22 However, a meta‐analysis examining antithrombotic therapy for patients with AAAs after interventional treatment showed that antiplatelet therapy (mainly aspirin) was associated with a reduction in postintervention all‐cause mortality for AAA, but anticoagulants were associated with an increased risk of all‐cause mortality, endoleak, and reintervention. 10

The risk of MACCE, including all‐cause mortality, MI, and stroke, may be driven by systemic atherosclerotic burden or other factors not directly addressed by intensified antiplatelet therapy. In animal studies, combined aspirin and clopidogrel therapy lowered the rupture risk and inflammation in established AAAs, whereas ticagrelor combined with aspirin was effective in preventing AAA formation and reducing vascular inflammation. 23 , 24 In addition, antiplatelet agents play a crucial role in preventing stent thrombosis until the stent struts are fully reendothelialized in patients undergoing PCI. 25 , 26 However, the aorta and the coronary arteries have different anatomical and vascular remodeling processes, as well as differences in endothelial healing and hemodynamics, which may result in a lower risk of thrombosis with EVAR compared with coronary stenting. As a result, clinicians have been concerned that the role of antiplatelet therapy in preventing thrombosis after aortic stenting may be less pronounced. The current study suggests that the routine use of intensified antiplatelet therapy did not lead to a reduction in ischemic events after standard EVAR in patients with an AAA.

In contrast, DAPT was associated with a significantly higher risk of major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding events compared with SAPT. This result is consistent with previous studies in other vascular surgery populations. 27 , 28 , 29 However, because the clinical benefit was limited, the increased bleeding risk did not outweigh the potential advantages for patients with EVAR. Notably, this elevated risk was observed consistently across subgroups, including patients with a high bleeding risk. However, a nonsignificant trend favoring DAPT was observed in female patients, with wide confidence intervals. These findings should be interpreted with caution, and further studies are warranted to evaluate the potential sex‐specific effects of antiplatelet strategies after EVAR. Furthermore, several AAA‐related end points, including reintervention, acute mesenteric ischemia, and acute limb ischemia, were numerically more frequent in the SAPT group. Although these differences did not reach statistical significance, they highlight the potential need to individualize antiplatelet strategies based on a patient’s thrombotic and bleeding risk profiles. The absence of a clear ischemic benefit with DAPT, combined with its higher bleeding risk, suggests that routine use of DAPT may not be justified in the overall population. However, certain subgroups may derive greater benefit from intensified antiplatelet therapy. Future research is needed to refine patient selection criteria and explore alternative strategies that might provide thrombotic protection without substantially increasing bleeding risk.

Current guidelines provide limited evidence on antiplatelet therapy for patients undergoing EVAR. Although DAPT is mandated by guidelines for coronary artery disease after percutaneous coronary intervention, neither EVAR guideline offers clear direction on the role of DAPT after EVAR. This study adds to the growing body of evidence suggesting that DAPT does not provide additional benefit over SAPT for cardiovascular protection in patients undergoing EVAR while posing a greater risk of bleeding. Furthermore, despite lacking statistical significance, SAPT showed a numerically lower risk of MACCE (approximately a 9% relative reduction) and AAA‐related events (approximately a 4% reduction) compared with no therapy, whereas increasing bleeding risk by only 1%. Conversely, DAPT did not provide any additional benefit, but was associated with a higher incidence of bleeding. These findings suggest that SAPT may offer a clinically meaningful balance between efficacy and safety in this population. Therefore, the current results suggest that the use of SAPT is sufficient for patients with AAAs who underwent standard EVAR and do not have additional cardiovascular disease requiring mandatory DAPT.

Limitations

Some limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design introduces the potential for residual confounding and selection bias. The patient population in this study may have had different baseline characteristics and risk factors, potentially influencing the efficacy of antiplatelet therapy. However, we attempted to control this bias using IPTW based on all baseline characteristics. Second, we lacked data on some relevant clinical variables, such as aneurysm morphology or imaging‐based anatomical parameters. Given the importance of hard clinical outcomes in our study, further research is warranted. Third, compliance with the use of antiplatelet therapy was not optimal, which could have influenced the results. Further research, ideally in the form of randomized controlled trials, is needed to definitively establish the role of antiplatelet therapy in this setting and identify any subgroups that may derive greater benefit. In the meantime, an individualized approach weighing thrombotic and bleeding risks is warranted when making antiplatelet management decisions after EVAR. However, this study has several strengths, including its large sample size, nationwide scope, and the use of propensity score weighting to account for confounding.

CONCLUSIONS

This study found that DAPT did not provide additional cardiovascular benefits after standard EVAR but was associated with an increased risk of bleeding. These findings suggest that SAPT may be a safer and more effective strategy for patients with AAAs who underwent standard EVAR.
