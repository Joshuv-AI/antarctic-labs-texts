Transcatheter closure of aorto-right atrial fistula
Authors: Denizhan Ozdemir, Jair Basantes de la Calle, Hasan Jilaihawi, Moody Makar, Raj Makkar, Gavin Lewis, Jamol Uzokov, Livia Gheorghe, Alejandro Ismael Cordero, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAorto-right atrial fistulas are rare complications of cardiac surgery that can result in heart failure due to continuous left-to-right shunting. In patients with recent sternotomy, reoperation carries significant risk, making transcatheter closure a compelling alternative.Case summaryA 64-year-old man with a history of diabetes, cerebrovascular disease, and recent coronary artery bypass grafting complicated by ascending aortic dissection and repair presented with progressive dyspnoea. Prior to transfer, echocardiography suggested a cardiovascular fistula. On admission, multimodality imaging—including transthoracic and transoesophageal echocardiography and computed tomography—confirmed an aorto-right atrial fistula with elevated pulmonary artery pressures. Given the recent surgery and comorbidities, the heart team deemed the patient high risk for repeat operation and opted for percutaneous closure. Under general anaesthesia with transoesophageal echocardiographic guidance, the fistula was percutaneously occluded with a 10 mm × 7 mm Amplatzer Vascular Plug II. Post-procedural imaging confirmed successful closure without impingement on aortic valve structures. The patient was discharged in stable condition to a rehabilitation facility on dual antiplatelet therapy.DiscussionThis case highlights the role of transcatheter closure in managing post-surgical aorto-right atrial fistulas. Comprehensive imaging and echocardiographic guidance were essential for procedural success. In select patients with high operative risk, percutaneous approaches provide a safe and effective alternative to repeat surgery.Keywords: Aorto-atrial fistula, Transcatheter closure, Iatrogenic fistula, Right atrial fistula, Case report

Introduction

A 64-year-old man with recent history of coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) was admitted with acute dyspnoea. He had previously been admitted to an outside hospital for heart failure exacerbation. During this admission, the patient was haemodynamically stable with a blood pressure of 141/68 mmHg and a heart rate of 90 b.p.m. He did not have overt signs of respiratory distress, and his oxygen saturation was 98% on 2 L of oxygen. On physical examination, no jugular vein distention was noted, and heart sounds were normal with no gallops or murmurs. The examination was remarkable for residual speech slurring, a sequela of a cerebrovascular accident (CVA) following CABG. No oedema was observed in the extremities.

Case presentation

The patient’s past medical history was notable for hyperlipidaemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus, CVA, and coronary artery disease, which had been treated 1 month earlier with CABG surgery at an outside hospital. His surgery was complicated by an intraoperative dissection of the ascending aorta, which was managed with replacement and repair of the dissection. Prior to admission to our facility, the patient had been hospitalized for a heart failure exacerbation and treated with diuretics. During that admission, transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) were performed, and a cardiovascular fistula was suspected (see Supplementary material online, Videos S1 and S2). Therefore, the patient was transferred to our centre for a higher level of specialized care.

Based on the patient’s clinical presentation and medical history, the following differential diagnoses included an aortopulmonary fistula, heart failure exacerbation, and residual or recurrent ascending aortic dissection.

At baseline, the patient was anaemic with low haemoglobin and haematocrit levels (9.6 g/dL and 30.1%, respectively). A chest X-ray showed cardiomegaly and a right-sided pleural effusion. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed an ejection fraction of 56% and normal right ventricular function. No severe valvular heart disease was noted; however, mild aortic regurgitation was noted. Pulmonary artery pressure was elevated at 50 mmHg, and right atrial pressure was 15 mmHg with no enlargement. The dissection entry point was identified in the ascending aorta, and an aorta-right atrial fistula with a continuous left-to-right shunt was observed (see Supplementary material online, Videos S1 and S2). No pericardial effusion was reported. Computed tomography (CT) confirmed the presence of an aorta-to-right atrium fistula and bilateral pleural effusions (Figure 1).

Management

The heart team discussion assessed the case based on imaging findings and considering the recent cardiac surgery and clinical presentation. Our patient was deemed high risk for repeat surgery due to the recent CABG complicated by aortic dissection and CVA. A transcatheter approach was taken and performed under general anaesthesia with TEE guidance. Baseline TEE images confirmed left-to-right fistula with continuous flow and mild central aortic regurgitation (see Supplementary material online, Video S3). Intraprocedural TEE revealed maximum fistula dimension of 6 mm. At our centre, transcatheter closure device sizing is guided by TEE, with structural imagers performing detailed assessment of the defect. A standard 50% oversizing strategy is applied relative to the maximal measured diameter. In this case, the largest cross-sectional dimension of the defect was 6 mm. Accordingly, the target device diameter was calculated as 9 mm. As no 9 mm device is commercially available, a 10 × 7 mm Amplatzer Vascular Plug was selected (device diameter × unconstrained length). This approach ensured adequate sealing while minimizing the risk of embolization. We accessed right femoral artery with 8 Fr 25 cm Terumo sheath and used 6 Fr JR guide catheter to demonstrate right Sinus of Valsalva to right atrium fistula with contrast injection (see Supplementary material online, Video S3). After therapeutic anticoagulation with heparin, the fistula is crossed with Runthrough 0.014″ coronary wire with direct visualization with TEE (see Supplementary material online, Videos S4 and S5). Subsequently, a coronary microcatheter was advanced into the right atrium to facilitate the exchange for a stiff Amplatzer wire (see Supplementary material online, Video S6). This was followed by advancing the 6 Fr JR guide catheter to further intubate the fistula. Microcatheter and coronary wire were removed from right atrium, and haemodynamic tracing confirmed right atrial pressure (see Supplementary material online, Video S7). An Amplatzer vascular plug II 10 mm × 7 mm (Abbott Structural Heart, USA) was implanted to occlude the fistula under fluoroscopy and TEE imaging guidance with two discs of the plug on the atrial side and one on the aortic side (see Supplementary material online, Videos S8 and S9). There was no impingement of coronary cusp or iatrogenic worsening of aortic regurgitation (see Supplementary material online, Video S10). The arterial access was closed using the previously placed ProGlide suture-based closure device.

Intravenous protamine was administered, and the total fluoroscopy time was 10.5 min. The patient left the cardiac catheterization laboratory in a stable condition. Post-procedural TTE before discharge confirmed a well-seated Amplatzer device in the aortic root. The patient was discharged in stable condition to a rehabilitation unit with 3–6 months of dual antiplatelet therapy consisting of aspirin 81 mg and clopidogrel 75 mg daily.

Discussion

Aorto-atrial fistula (AAF) is a rare but complex pathological condition.1 A systematic review of 4519 publications identified 132 case reports after an extensive database search.1 In the majority of cases, echocardiography and angiography were utilized for diagnosis.2,3 Ascending aorta-to-right atrium fistula formation was twice as common as ascending aorta-to-left atrium fistula formation.1,2

In most cases (22.8%), endocarditis was the cause of AAF formation. Aortic aneurysms (22.1%), mainly complicated by dissected aortic walls, were the second most common cause.1,4 Congenital causes (11.8%) also accounted for a significant number of AAF cases.1,2 In 15.4% of cases, fistula formation occurred secondary to cardiac surgery.1,4

Aorto-atrial fistula was corrected via an open surgical approach in 73.5% of cases.5 In 10.3% of cases, the fistula was closed via a percutaneous intervention, while a conservative medical approach was advocated in 4.4%.2–4,6 When percutaneous closure of the fistula tract was employed, closure with an Amplatzer device was the treatment of choice (71.4%), followed by coil embolization (14.3%), covered stents (7.15%), and balloon closures (7.15%).3

In 74.3% of all studied cases, the fistula repair was successful, and patients survived the procedures.1,6 In 85.7% of cases involving percutaneous fistula correction, the repair was successful, and patients survived the procedures.1

Conclusion

Aorto-atrial fistula is a rare complication of cardiac surgery, and transcatheter approaches should be considered when the anatomy and imaging findings favour minimally invasive therapy. In the majority of percutaneous fistula corrections, the literature suggests successful repair with high survival rates.
