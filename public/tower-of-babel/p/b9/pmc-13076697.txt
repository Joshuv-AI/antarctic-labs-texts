S100A13 transcriptionally activated by SP1 facilitates osteosarcoma metastasis
Authors: Fuxing Tang, Yisheng Wang, Tao Feng, Shuheng Zhou, Xianghong Zeng, Piwei Huang, Jinhui Peng, Bowei Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteosarcoma is a highly aggressive primary bone malignancy in which distant metastasis critically determines patient prognosis. However, the molecular drivers of metastasis remain incompletely understood, making the elucidation of these drivers a research priority.MethodsWe integrated single-cell and bulk RNA sequencing data to identify cell populations associated with metastasis and to screen for candidate therapeutic targets. Bioinformatics tools predicted upstream transcriptional regulators. Functional roles and transcriptional regulation were assessed via wound-healing, Transwell, and luciferase reporter assays.ResultsSingle-cell analysis of osteosarcoma heterogeneity identified the osteoblastic subcluster C2_OB as a major contributor to metastasis. cNMF analysis defined a metastasis-associated expression program (module 2) highly active in C2_OB, within which S100A13 emerged as a novel signature gene. S100A13 was overexpressed in osteosarcoma and correlated with metastasis and poor prognosis. Functional assays demonstrated that S100A13 promoted cell migration and invasion. Mechanistically, the transcription factor SP1 directly binds to the S100A13 promoter, activates its transcription, and thereby enhances the metastatic capacity of osteosarcoma cells.ConclusionThis study identifies S100A13 as a biomarker for osteosarcoma metastasis and poor prognosis, and delineates an SP1/S100A13 axis that drives metastatic progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04750-6.

Introduction

Osteosarcoma is the most common primary malignant bone tumor, typically arising in the metaphyses of long bones and predominantly affecting children and adolescents [1]. A hallmark of osteosarcoma is its propensity for early metastasis, most commonly to the lungs. Approximately 11.4% of patients present with pulmonary metastases at initial diagnosis [2]. While the five-year overall survival rate for patients with localized disease ranges from 60% to 75% [3], it plummets to 29% in cases with distant metastasis [4]. Thus, distant metastasis is a pivotal determinant of prognosis. However, the biological mechanisms driving osteosarcoma metastasis remain poorly understood, underscoring an urgent need to elucidate its molecular drivers.

RNA sequencing has become a pivotal tool for investigating tumorigenesis and metastasis. Conventional bulk RNA sequencing provides averaged gene expression profiles but masks cellular heterogeneity. In contrast, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) resolves gene expression at the individual cell level, thereby revealing regulatory networks, cellular evolution, and interactions within the tumor microenvironment.This technology has greatly advanced research into tumor heterogeneity, metastasis, recurrence, and treatment response [5–7].

In this study, we integrated scRNA-seq datasets from two independent cohorts to characterize tumor microenvironment heterogeneity in primary and metastatic osteosarcoma. Our analysis pinpointed the osteoblastic subcluster C2_OB as strongly associated with metastatic progression.Within this subcluster, S100A13 was highly expressed and emerged as a potential biomarker for metastasis and poor prognosis. In vitro experiments confirmed that S100A13 promotes osteosarcoma cell migration and invasion. Mechanistically, we found that the transcription factor SP1 transcriptionally upregulates S100A13, thereby driving tumor metastasis. Collectively, our findings provide new insights into osteosarcoma heterogeneity and highlight the potential therapeutic relevance of targeting the SP1/S100A13 axis against metastasis.

Materials and methods

Datasets acquisition

RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data for osteosarcoma were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds/). The scRNA-seq dataset GSE152048 includes 7 primary and 2 metastatic osteosarcoma samples [6], and GSE234187 contains 1 metastatic osteosarcoma sample [8]. Bulk RNA-seq profiles were downloaded from the following GEO series: GSE19276 (44 osteosarcoma and 5 normal bone samples) [9]; GSE14359 (8 primary and 10 metastatic osteosarcoma samples) [10]; and GSE49003 (6 non-metastatic and 6 metastatic osteosarcoma samples) [11]. Additionally, RNA-seq matrix data and corresponding clinical information for 84 osteosarcoma cases were retrieved from TARGET_OS cohort via UCSC Xena database (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/).

Preprocessing and analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing data

Single-cell RNA-seq data were processed and analyzed using the Seurat package (v4.3.0) in R (v4.2.3). Low-quality cells were filtered with the following criteria: genes detected in fewer than three cells, fewer than 300 genes per cell, and mitochondrial reads exceeding 10%. Potential doublets were identified and removed using DoubletFinder (v2.0.3) [12].

The gene expression matrix was log-normalized with NormalizeData. Highly variable genes (n = 3,000) were identified with FindVariableFeatures and scaled via ScaleData. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed for dimensionality reduction, and batch effects were corrected using Harmony (v0.1.0). Cells were then clustered with a graph-based approach (FindNeighbors and FindClusters), and the resulting clusters were visualized in two-dimensional space using t-SNE (RunTSNE).

Cell type annotation

Cell types were defined using the Seurat function FindAllMarkers to identify genes that were distinctly expressed across clusters. Cell identities were then manually assigned based on the expression of canonical marker genes: myeloid (CD14, CD74, C1QA, C1QB); osteoblastic (IBSP, ALPL, RUNX2); fibroblasts (DCN, LUM, COL3A1); osteoclasts (ACP5, CTSK, CKB); tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs; NKG7, CD3D, CD3E); endothelial (EGFL7, PLVAP, VWF); pericytes (ACTA2, RGS5); chondroblastic (SOX9, PTH1R, ACAN); myocytes (MYLPF, MYL1); and mast (TPSAB1, TPSB2, CPA3).

Copy number variations(CNVs) analysis

To determine CNVs in osteosarcoma cells, we applied the inferCNV package (v1.2.1) to calculate CNV scores [13]. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), myeloid cells, and mast cells were used as the reference cell set.

CancerSEA

The functional state of S100A13 across multiple cancer types was assessed using CancerSEA (http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/CancerSEA/), a dedicated database for profiling functional states of cancer cells at single-cell resolution [14]. Specifically, we examined the correlation between S100A13 expression and 14 functional states across 25 cancer types. Correlations with |r| > 0.3 and P < 0.05 were considered significant.

irGSEA enrichment analysis

irGSEA integrates multiple enrichment analysis methods (AUCell, UCell, singscore, ssGSEA, JASMINE, and Viper) to systematically evaluate the statistical significance of target gene sets form single-cell expression data. Using the Robust Rank Aggregation (RRA) algorithm, it consolidates results from different scoring approaches to identify gene sets that are robustly enriched across methods [15].

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

Based on the median expression level of S100A13, TARGET_OS cohort were stratified into high- and low-expression groups. GSEA(v4.0.3) was then used to interrogate pathway differences between these groups. Gene sets were obtained from MSigDB (https://www.gseamsigdb.org/gsea/downloads.jsp) and the CancerSEA database [14].

cNMF clustering analysis

Consensus Non-negative Matrix Factorization (cNMF) was applied to infer gene expression programs in scRNA-seq data. Using the cNMF Python package (v1.1) [16] we ran the analysis separately on each major cell type with 50 iterations per run. All results were subsequently integrated to estimate program stability. To interpret biological relevance, each inferred program was annotated against the CancerSEA and HALLMARK gene sets.

Gene module enrichment analysis

Leveraging the metastasis-related gene modules inferred by cNMF, we applied the AddModuleScore function in Seurat to quantify the activity of these functional signatures across individual cell, thereby elucidating patterns of gene co-expression.

Survival and correlation analyses

Survival analysis was performed using gene expression and clinical data from 84 osteosarcoma samples in TARGET_OS cohort. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated with R packages survival (v3.5-5) and survminer (v0.4.9). Univariate and multivariate Cox analyses were applied to evaluate prognostic factors. Spearman’s test was used to assess gene-gene associations.

Transcription factor prediction

Cellular functions and biological behaviors are governed by complex and tightly regulated gene expression patterns, which depend on the binding of regulatory elements to promoter regions [17, 18]. To identify potential transcriptional regulators, we screened the promoter sequences of target genes using several online prediction databases, including JASPAR, hTFtarget, PROMO, CHIP-Atlas, and AnimalTFDB v4.0.

Cell culture

Human osteosarcoma cell lines (HOS, MG63, SAOS2, U2OS, and 143B) and the osteoblast cell line hFOB 1.19 were purchased from the Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Tumor cell lines were incubated at 37℃ in a 5% CO₂ incubator, whereas hFOB 1.19 was cultured at 33.5℃ under the same CO₂ conditions.143B, HOS, MG63, and hFOB 1.19 were grown in DMEM, and SAOS2 and U2OS in McCoy’s 5 A medium. The medium for 143B, HOS, MG63, U2OS, and hFOB 1.19 was supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin; SAOS2 medium contained 15% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin.

Cell transfection

siRNA and overexpression plasmids targeting S100A13 and SP1 were purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Transient transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The siRNA sequences used are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Cell migration and invasion assays

Cell migration was assessed using a scratch wound assay. Cells were grown in 6-well plates until ~ 90% confluent, after which a straight scratch was made with a 200µL pipette tip. The wells were washed with PBS and then maintained in serum-free medium. Scratch widths were measured immediately (0 h) and after 24 h of incubation.

Cell invasion was evaluated using Matrigel-coated Transwell inserts. Cells were resuspended at 5 × 10⁵ cells/mL in serum-free medium, and 100µL of the suspension was added to the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with 600µL of complete medium containing 10% FBS. After 48 h at 37 °C, cells that had invaded through the Matrigel were fixed, stained, and counted under a light microscope.

Quantitative real-time PCR(qRT-PCR)

qRT-PCR was performed with Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix(Thermo Fisher scientific, USA). Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 2. Relative quantification was normalized to the geometric mean of GAPDH mRNA levels, and fold changes were calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method.

Luciferase activity assay

HEK293T cells were seeded and cultured for 24 h, then co-transfected with a firefly luciferase reporter plasmid containing the S100A13 promoter and a Renilla luciferase control plasmid at a 10:1 ratio using Lipofectamine 2000. After 48 h, cells were washed with PBS and lysed for 15 min at room temperature. Luciferase activity was measured with a TECAN microplate reader following the instructions of the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega, #E1910).

Immunofluorescence staining (IF)

HOS cells were transfected with S100A13-siRNA for 48 h, fixed with % paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and permeabilized with 0. % Triton X-100 for 10 min. After blocking with5% BSA at 37 °C for 30 min, cells were incubated with primary antibody (mouse monoclonal antibody E-cadherin, #BF0219, Affinity Bioscience; mouse monoclonal antibody Vimentin, #BF8006, Affinity Bioscience) overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, cells were stained with fluorescently conjugated secondary antibody at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark, followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI for 5 min. Slides were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium, and images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope.

Clinical specimens

Adjacent non-tumor tissues and tumor tissues were collected from osteosarcoma patients undergoing surgery at Yulin Orthopedics Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine. All patients had a confirmed diagnosis of osteosarcoma, with or without distant metastasis. The study protocol was approved by the hospital’s Ethics Committee (No. 202504601), and written informed consent was obtained from each participant before enrollment. All procedures involving human subjects were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissue Sect. (4 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and microwave heated in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 15 min. Following overnight incubation with primary antibody (rabbit anti-human S100A13, bs-2617R, Beijing Biosynthesis Biotechnology) at 4 °C, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at 37 °C for 30 min. Color development was performed with DAB for 2 min, and counterstained with hematoxylin for 30s. After dehydration, clearing in xylene, and mounting with neutral gum, sections were photographed under a light microscope.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates, and data are presented as mean ± SD. The difference in means between two independent samples was assessed using Student’s t-test. Each survival analysis was performed using Kaplan-Meier curves and Cox proportional regression models. The correlation between the two genes was assessed using Spearman’s test. Significance levels were indicated as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001; “ns” denotes no significant difference.

Results

Single-cell transcriptomic profiles of primary and metastatic osteosarcoma

We integrated scRNA-seq datasets GSE152408 and GSE234187, retaining a total of 96,001 high-quality cells after quality control. Unsupervised clustering and manual annotation identified ten major cell types: myeloid cells, osteoblastic cells, fibroblasts, osteoclasts, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), endothelial cells, pericytes, chondroblastic cells, myocytes, and mast cells (Fig. 1A). Cell-type assignments were supported by established marker-gene expression (Fig. 1B). Using TILs, myeloid cells, and mast cells as reference, inferCNV algorithm identified large-scale CNVs in osteoblastic and chondroblastic cells. Prominent copy-number gains were observed on chromosomes 1, 7, and 8 (red), while losses were detected on chromosomes 10 and 13 (blue) (Fig. 1C). Violin plots confirm significantly higher CNV scores in osteoblastic and chondroblastic populations compared with the reference set, supporting their malignant origin (Fig. 1D). Cell-type proportions varied substantially among samples, reflecting inter-tumoral heterogeneity (Fig. 1E). Notably, osteoblastic cells were more abundant in metastatic than in primary tumors, implicating them in metastatic progression (Fig. 1F).

Transcriptional heterogeneity of osteoblastic cells

Given the central role of osteoblastic cells in osteosarcoma metastasis, we analyzed their heterogeneity at single-cell resolution. Among osteoblastic cells, we identified five transcriptionally distinct subclusters (C0–C4_OB) (Fig. 2A), each characterized by a specific set of marker genes (Fig. 2B). Notably, C2_OB was enriched in the metastatic group (Fig. 2C). irGSEA enrichment analysis using the HALLMARK gene set indicated that C2_OB was particularly active in metastasis-related pathways such as hypoxia, mTOR signaling, and TGF-β signaling, consistent with a pro-metastatic role (Fig. 2D). Further analysis with cNMF identified Module 2 as being enriched in metastasis-related pathways (Fig. 2E). AddModuleScore analysis confirmed that the Module 2 signature score was highest in C2_OB, reinforcing the pro-metastatic phenotype of this subcluster (Fig. 2F). The signature genes of Module 2 are listed in Supplementary Table 3. Among these, S100A13, previously unrecognized in this context, emerged as a signature gene within Module 2. At single-cell resolution, S100A13 expression was specifically elevated in C2_OB (Fig. 2G) and further increased in metastatic samples (Fig. 2H), suggesting it may act as a candidate driver of osteosarcoma metastasis.

High S100A13 expression associated with metastasis and poor prognosis in osteosarcoma

In the bulk RNA-seq dataset GSE19276, S100A13 expression was significantly higher in osteosarcoma tissues than in normal samples (Fig. 3A). A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve demonstrated strong diagnostic performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.977 (Fig. 3B). We next evaluated S100A13 expression in relation to clinical parameters using the TARGET, GSE14359, and GSE49003 datasets. Consistent with the single-cell findings, S100A13 levels were markedly elevated in metastatic compared with non-metastatic tumors across all three cohorts (Fig. 3C–E). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that high S100A13 expression was associated with shorter overall survival (Fig. 3F). Time-dependent ROC curves yielded AUC values of 0.690, 0.657, and 0.718 for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival, respectively, supporting its prognostic utility (Fig. 3G). Univariate Cox analysis identified both metastasis and high S100A13 expression as significant risk factors for poor survival (Fig. 3H). In multivariate Cox analysis, high S100A13 expression remained an independent prognostic factor alongside metastasis (Fig. 3I). Taken together, these results indicate that elevated S100A13 expression is strongly associated with metastasis and unfavorable prognosis in osteosarcoma, highlighting its potential as an independent prognostic biomarker and a candidate therapeutic target.

Bioinformatic prediction and experimental validation of S100A13 biological function

To investigate the biological function of S100A13 systematically, we analyzed its single-cell expression profiles across cancer types using the CancerSEA database. S100A13 expression showed significant positive correlations with the functional states of metastasis, angiogenesis, inflammation, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) (Fig. 5A). GSEA further confirmed its association with metastasis-related pathways (Fig. 5B). These findings collectively indicate an important role for S100A13 in promoting osteosarcoma metastasis.

EMT is considered a key driver of tumor metastasis, characterized by the downregulation of E-cadherin, upregulation of N-cadherin and Vimentin, and enhanced activity of extracellular matrix-degrading enzymes like MMP2 and MMP9. IF analysis demonstrated that S100A13 knockdown significantly increased E-cadherin intensity while markedly decreasing Vimentin signals (Fig. 5C). Consistently, qRT-PCR showed that S100A13 knockdown increased the mRNA levels of the epithelial marker E-cadherin, while it reduced the mesenchymal marker Vimentin and the matrix metalloproteinases MMP2 and MMP9 (Fig. 5D). These findings collectively indicate that S100A13 promotes EMT in osteosarcoma cells.

Up-regulated S100A13 promoted osteosarcoma migration and invasion

Functional assays showed that S100A13 knockdown significantly impaired cell migration and invasion (Fig. 6A), whereas its overexpression enhanced them (Fig. 6B). Taken together, these results demonstrate that S100A13 promotes the metastatic capabilities of osteosarcoma cells.

SP1 regulated S100A13 transcription by binding to its promoter

To investigate the transcriptional regulation of S100A13 in osteosarcoma, we screened several online prediction databases (JASPAR, PROMO, hTFtarget, and AnimalTFDB v4.0) for potential upstream regulators and identified SP1 as the sole candidate (Fig. 7A). According to AnimalTFDB v4.0, the S100A13 promoter region (within 2000 bp) contains 121 predicted SP1-binding sites (Supplementary Table 4). Expression analysis across the TARGET cohort revealed a significant positive correlation between SP1 and S100A13 levels (Fig. 7B), suggesting that SP1 acts as a transcriptional activator of S100A13. A dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed to verify whether SP1 directly activates the S100A13 promoter. The promoter sequence containing the predicted SP1-binding motif (CCCCTCCCCCGCCCC, located at − 147/–132) was cloned upstream of the firefly luciferase gene. SP1 overexpression significantly increased luciferase activity compared with vector (Fig. 7C), confirming direct transcriptional activation. We next transfected osteosarcoma cells with siRNA or overexpression plasmids targeting SP1. Knockdown and overexpression efficiencies were verified by qRT-PCR (Fig. 7D). SP1 silencing markedly reduced S100A13 expression (Fig. 7E), while SP1 overexpression increased it (Fig. 7F). Notably, the upregulation of S100A13 induced by SP1 overexpression was abolished when cells were co-transfected with S100A13-targeting siRNA (Fig. 7F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that SP1 transcriptionally upregulates S100A13 through direct binding to its promoter.

SP1 mediated upregulation of S100A13 promoted osteosarcoma metastasis

Previous studies have established the well-known transcription factor SP1 as a promoter of osteosarcoma growth and metastasis [19, 20]. In line with this, we found that SP1 knockdown suppressed cell migration and invasion (Fig. 8A), whereas its overexpression promoted these capabilities (Fig. 8B). To directly test whether S100A13 mediates the pro-metastatic effect of SP1, we performed rescue experiments. Notably, concomitant knockdown of S100A13 largely abolished the increases in migration and invasion induced by SP1 overexpression (Fig. 8B). Collectively, these data indicate that SP1 drives osteosarcoma metastasis, at least in part, by transcriptionally upregulating S100A13.

Discussion

Osteosarcoma exhibits considerable inter- and intratumoral heterogeneity, attributable to its varied cellular origins and intricate biological processes [21]. This heterogeneity presents major challenges for therapeutic development and contributes substantially to metastasis, recurrence, and drug resistance [22, 23]. Advances in scRNA-seq now allow this heterogeneity to be resolved at high resolution. By characterizing the tumor microenvironment in detail, scRNA-seq enables the elucidation of cellular interactions and functional diversity, offering more precise insights for diagnosis and treatment.

In this study, we performed scRNA-seq to compare primary and metastatic osteosarcoma. A distinct osteoblastic subcluster, C2_OB, was markedly expanded in metastatic lesions. Enrichment analysis linked C2_OB to hypoxia, mTOR, and TGF-β signaling—pathways frequently implicated in tumor progression. Further bioinformatic analysis predicted a pro-metastatic role for this subcluster and identified S100A13 as a key candidate gene contributing to osteosarcoma metastasis.

S100A13 encodes a small calcium-binding protein of the S100 family [24]. It has been documented to be up-regulated in several malignant tumors, including oral squamous cell carcinoma [25], non-small cell lung cancer [26], melanoma [27], where its expression often correlates with advanced disease and poor prognosis. However, its role in osteosarcoma has remained unclear. Here, we demonstrate that S100A13 is upregulated in osteosarcoma and promotes tumor cell migration and invasion, supporting its potential as a biomarker for metastasis and unfavorable outcomes.

Transcriptional regulation is a central mechanism for understanding the complex control of gene expression in tumors, providing critical insights for mechanistic studies and targeted therapy [28–30]. In this work, we identified SP1 as a principal transcriptional regulator of S100A13 in osteosarcoma. SP1 is a well-characterized transcription factor that activates gene expression by binding to GC-rich promoter motifs. It is involved in diverse cellular processes, including differentiation, growth, apoptosis, and immune evasion [31], and is often overexpressed in cancer, where it correlates with poor prognosis [32]. In osteosarcoma, SP1 has been shown, for example, to bind the SLC7A3 promoter, increasing arginine uptake and promoting cell migration [19]. Our findings establish that SP1 is overexpressed in osteosarcoma, directly binds S100A13 promoter, enhances its transcription, and thereby facilitates metastasis. These results underscore the importance of SP1 in driving S100A13-mediated metastasis and advance our knowledge of S100A13 transcriptional regulation.

In summary, this study identifies S100A13 as a potential biomarker for metastasis and poor prognosis in osteosarcoma, establishing SP1 as its key transcriptional activator that promotes metastasis by upregulating S100A13. These findings provide a novel conceptual framework and suggest potential therapeutic relevance for osteosarcoma. However, several limitations should be noted. First, functional assays were conducted mainly in the HOS cell line due to its highest S100A13 expression, validation across additional cell lines is warranted. Second, the role of the SP1/S100A13 axis requires in vivo verification. Third, direct binding of SP1 to the S100A13 promoter, beyond luciferase assay evidence, should be confirmed by chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP). Future studies should address these points through more comprehensive investigations.
