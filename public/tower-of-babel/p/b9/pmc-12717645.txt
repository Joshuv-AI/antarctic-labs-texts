Is Ischemia really the crux of the matter?
Authors: Samual Turnbull, Saurabh Kumar
Published: 2025

Catheter ablation for both idiopathic ventricular arrhythmias (VA) and those in patients with structural heart disease is an established, effective treatment. Particularly in idiopathic VAs, success rates are generally high, with favourable clinical outcomes [1]. Substantially less common than endocardial VAs, epicardial idiopathic VAs can present greater procedural challenges, particularly regarding access and the ability to map and ablate VAs being limited by epicardial fat and proximity to coronary vessels. These limitations are infamously associated with the left ventricular (LV) summit, but also to the cardiac crux, a comparably rare site of VA origin.

The cardiac crux is the inferior, epicardial region of the heart located where the atrioventricular and posterior interventricular grooves intersect, where the right atrium, left atrium, right ventricle and LV (at the posterosuperior process) meet to form somewhat of a cross. The crux is a pyramidal region, within which epicardial adipose tissue insulates the posterior descending and atrioventricular nodal arteries, typically branching from the right coronary artery. Importantly for the ablation of crux VAs, the crux also typically colocalises to where the middle cardiac vein (MCV) branches off the coronary sinus (CS).

Given the rarity of cardiac crux VAs, occurring far less frequently than outflow tract and papillary muscle VAs, or (in terms of epicardial-specific VAs) LV summit, many operators may see very few. It is therefore important that they recognise them when they do present to ensure appropriate mapping and ablation strategies are implemented.

Patients with crux VAs can present with fairly benign symptoms, such as palpitations and dizziness, but may also present with presyncope or syncope, attributable to rapid, hemodynamically unstable ventricular tachycardias (VT). These crux VTs have been reported to be catecholamine-sensitive, initiated by stressors or intraprocedurally with the assistance of isoprenaline, and such are generally considered automatic or triggered, with evidence to support re-entry sparse, though micro-reentry cannot be ruled out [2]. It is appropriate to note that crux VAs can be induced intraprocedurally with either programmed electrical stimulation or through rapid ventricular pacing.

Identification of potential crux involvement from the 12-lead electrocardiogram is critical to planning an appropriate procedural and ablation strategy. Crux VAs always have a left superior axis, frequently QS morphology in lead II and III, with lead III deeper in amplitude than lead II, and rapid transition to prominent R wave in V2 is common. Lead V1 may be indeterminant, left, or right bundle branch block-like in morphology. When attempting to differentiate between basal and apical crux, an S > R in V6 is suggestive of apical crux, and more prominent R wave in V6 suggests basal crux [3]. A maximum deflection index of greater than 0.55 % is important for differentiating the epicardial crux exit site from endocardial exits, including the tricuspid and mitral annuli and posteroseptal process [[3], [4], [5]].

Distinction from these sites is not only based on activation patterns, but the site of successful ablation, with crux VAs, by definition, ablated from within the CS or proximal 2 cm of the MCV (basal crux), the distal MCV or epicardially (apical crux) [3].

Patients experiencing with truly idiopathic crux VT can be managed without the need for an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD), though ICDs are occasionally implanted, especially when the VT is hemodynamically unstable.

In this issue of Indian Pacing and Electrophysiology Journal, Bisht and Kishor present two examples of patients with basal crux VT and ischemic heart disease (IHD) [6]. Both patients had recurrent VT in the months following percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). The first of the patients underwent PCI to the mid left anterior descending artery, and in the months following presented with recurrent VT and LV ejection fraction (LVEF) severely reduced at 25 %. The second patient underwent multivessel PCI for an inferior infarct, prior to presenting with recurrent VT and LVEF of 45 %. Activation mapping of the VT during the ablation procedure was possible for both patients, with electrograms identified −60 to −58 milliseconds pre-QRS, from the posteroseptal tricuspid annulus in the anterior infarct patient, and at the MCV in the inferior infarct patient. Unfortunately, the mechanism of the arrhythmias was not defined, however, both experienced successful ablations and demonstrated improvement to their LVEF in follow-up. Neither patient received an ICD, however, which is a major limitation of the present report. The presence of prior MI with VT, would prompt recommendation of an ICD, as most VTs are re-entrant and predicated by the presence of underlying scar. Notably, if the mechanism of VT is focal, remote from scar and ablation is successful, an ICD could conceivably be withheld after a detailed multidisciplinary discussion amongst electrophysiology colleagues, and with the patient outlining the risks and benefits, provided that VT is hemodynamically tolerated, there is certainty about absence of scar in the region of the crux (either on high density invasive endo/epicardial mapping and/or cardiac magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]), the VT is focal, and no other VTs are inducible. We have previously found a high incidence (17 %) of focal VT, often remote from scar in patients with structural heart disease [7]). Nevertheless, it would be considered "brave" not to suggest an ICD in such patients.

The authors make two notable points from these cases, firstly, that there may be a potential link between IHD and basal crux VT. The existence of patients with IHD and concomitant re-entrant VT related to nonischemic substrate remote from the infarcted region is now well established [8]. The infarcted territory in these patients can be anterior, inferior, lateral, or a combination of, however, these patients nearly always present with basal VTs that are remote from their infarct [9]. It is important to consider that the mechanism for the VTs in the patients of the present study was not defined, despite identification of prolonged fractionated electrograms that suggest a re-entrant mechanism. Crux VT is generally considered to be an automatic or triggered focal mechanism; given the relative remoteness of the patients’ infarcted regions from the crux, we should be cautious, as the authors have been, about attributing the VT directly to the ischemic scar, to avoid unnecessary empirical ablation non-arrhythmic scar.

The second point made by the authors is that ICD therapy may be reasonably withheld in these patients, where deemed appropriate, following a successful ablation. As discussed above, these patients might present a paradox of sorts: ICD implantation is recommended in patients with structural heart disease and sustained VT, however, is not recommended for patients with idiopathic VTs, due to them generally having a benign prognosis [10,11]. An LVEF of <35 % was also documented in one of the patients. Given that these patients presented in hemodynamically tolerated monomorphic VT that was successfully ablated, with improvements to the LVEF observed following ablation, it seems reasonable that a cautious, considered and individualised approach to ICD implantation was taken. It would be beneficial to examine whether optimal medical therapy was established in these patients. The timeframe between ablation and repeat echocardiogram is an important consideration also; the initial, depressed LVEF measurements were attributed to an assumed high burden of arrhythmia, but a repeat echocardiogram should be performed prior to determining to withhold ICD implantation.

Though only a single VT morphology was observed and abolished for both patients during their ablation, a repeat electrophysiology study would aid decision making in these patients with IHD, especially given the tendency of arrhythmic suppression with sedation or anaesthesia. Finally, though availability to scans can be limited, definitive determination of the presence and distribution of scar with cardiac MRI would be pivotal in decision making with these patients.

The authors should be commended for reporting these two cases of monomorphic VT originating near to or from the cardiac crux, in patients with IHD and reduced LVEF. Their work reminds us of the importance of a considered approach and shared decision making when faced with patients such as these.
