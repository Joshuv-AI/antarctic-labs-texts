Migrant status and musculoskeletal complaints in greenhouse horticulture: integrating physical and psychosocial factors
Authors: Natalia Burgos-Alonso, Pedro Monzón, Marta Rentería, Begoña Landajo, Ángel Pereira, Jesús Seco-Calvo, Lars Louis Andersen, Jon Torres-Unda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAgricultural work involves high physical demands and organizational and psychological factors that increase the risk of musculoskeletal disorders, especially in environments such as greenhouse horticulture. Migrant workers face additional vulnerabilities stemming from precarious working conditions and reduced access to preventive resources. The aim of this study was to compare functional capacity, musculoskeletal complaints, and psychosocial outcomes between migrant and non-migrant workers engaged in greenhouse horticultural production.DesignA cross-sectional study was conducted among 146 agricultural workers employed in greenhouse horticultural production in the Basque Country (Spain). Cardiorespiratory fitness, lower-limb strength, musculoskeletal complaints using the Standardised Nordic Questionnaire (SNQ) and perceived stress and anxiety were compared between migrant and non-migrant workers.ResultsMusculoskeletal complaints were lower in migrants workers in both sexes (p < 0.05). A positive correlation was identified between depressive symptoms and musculoskeletal complaints. A multivariable regression model showed that being migrant, lower depressive symptom scores, greater handgrip strength, and higher work ability were associated with lower musculoskeletal complaint levels (R2 =0.574).ConclusionMusculoskeletal complaints among greenhouse agricultural workers are not explained solely by physical workload. Migrant status, psychological well-being, muscular strength, and work ability are independently associated with musculoskeletal complaints.Keywords: Migrant health, Occupational health, Biopsychosocial models, Working conditions, Agricultural workers

Introduction

Agricultural work remains one of the most physically demanding and ergonomically hazardous occupational environments globally. The Basque Country of Spain is a region where approximately 12% of the territory is dedicated to agricultural activity and production is predominantly undertaken through small cooperative holdings. Here, manual labour continues to constitute the core of daily operations, exposing workers to repetitive movements, sustained physical exertion, and prolonged adoption of non-neutral postures (EUSTAT 2026). Such biomechanical demands are well-established precursors of musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs), which consistently rank among the leading causes of occupational morbidity, reduced functional capacity, and work absenteeism in primary-sector industries (Hoy DG et al., 2018; Fathallah FA. 2010; Holmberg et al., 2002). Empirical studies conducted in olive farming, greenhouse horticulture, and other labour-intensive agricultural settings have repeatedly documented substantial MSD prevalence associated with manual harvesting, restricted workspaces, load handling, and task repetition, underscoring the pervasive nature of these occupational risks (BarneoAlcántara M et al. 2020; Gómez-Galán M et al. 2021).

The aetiology of MSDs in agriculture, however, extends beyond physical exposures. Organisational and psychosocial factors such as extended working hours, limited mechanisation, inadequate ergonomic design, low job control, and adverse safety climate exert an amplifying effect on musculoskeletal symptomatology (Arcury TA et al. 2012; MacDonald LA et al. 2001; Mazloumi A et al. 2025; Narimoto L et al. 2012). The interaction between physical and psychosocial factors is of particular concern in greenhouse horticulture, where environmental constraints and production demands may limit opportunities for structured risk prevention.

Within this context, migrant workers constitute a subgroup facing distinct and often heightened vulnerabilities. Migrant labour is essential to the functioning of agricultural systems across Europe; yet, these workers frequently encounter precarious contractual arrangements, restricted access to occupational health resources, and limited participation in preventive programmes. Consequently, migrant workers may experience disproportionately greater ergonomic loads and psychosocial strain than their non-migrant counterparts (Bretones FD et al. 2020; Urrego-Parra HN et al. 2022). Comparable findings in other labour-intensive sectors, such as seafood processing and poultry production, have shown elevated rates of MSDs and work-related illness among migrant or foreign-born workers, reflecting broader structural inequities that shape occupational health outcomes (Cartwright MS et al. 2012; Newman KL et al. 2015; Syron LN et al. 2019).

Previous evidence has consistently documented a high burden of mental health problems among migrant agricultural workers. More generally, meta-analyses of migrant worker populations show high prevalences of depression and anxiety (Hasan et al. 2021). At the same time, the literature on musculoskeletal health describes a high prevalence of pain and musculoskeletal disorders in the agricultural sector, including among migrant populations (Tribble et al. 2015). However, the available evidence is based primarily on studies focused exclusively on migrant workers or on comparisons between different occupational groups. Comparison groups within the agricultural sector itself, comparing migrant and non-migrant groups while accounting for sex has not been analized before.

Despite increasing awareness of these disparities, comparative studies examining the physical and psychosocial health profiles of migrant and non-migrant agricultural workers within the same operational setting remain scarce. Crucially, it is unclear whether differences in musculoskeletal complaints, functional capacity, perceived stress, and anxiety are attributable to divergent occupational exposures, differing physiological conditioning, or systematic inequalities in access to healthcare and preventive interventions. Physiological assessments, including cardiorespiratory fitness and lower-limb strength, offer objective indicators of functional capacity and resilience, while validated questionnaires capturing musculoskeletal symptoms, stress, and anxiety provide important information about subjective health experiences that may not be evident in routine occupational surveillance. This study is grounded in a biopsychosocial framework, which considers musculoskeletal complaints as the result of interactions between physical capacity, psychosocial factors, and individual characteristics rather than purely biomechanical factors.

Accordingly, the aim of this study was provide to examine a comprehensive comparison between functional capacity, musculoskeletal complaints, and psychosocial factors among migrant and non-migrant agricultural workers. We hypothesized that both psychosocial and functional variables would be significantly associated with musculoskeletal complaints, beyond demographic differences.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted among agricultural workers employed in greenhouse horticultural production in the Basque Country (Spain). Greenhouse horticulture is characterised by labour-intensive tasks that commonly involve repetitive movements, manual harvesting, and prolonged awkward postures, making it an appropriate setting for assessing musculoskeletal and psychosocial risks (BarneoAlcántara M et al. 2020; Gómez-Galán M et al. 2021).

Participants

Eligible participants were contracted workers aged ≥18 years, actively employed during the data collection period and not on sick leave. Recruitment was facilitated through the regional cooperative association. This sampling strategy is consistent with previous occupational health studies conducted in the agricultural sector, where workplace-based recruitment ensures representativeness of task-related exposures [BarneoAlcántara M et al. 2020; Hoy DG et al., 2018; Narimoto L et al. 2012]. For the recruitment process, we invited partner companies to participate and sent the informed consent form to all employees. Out of approximately 300 eligible workers, 146 (48.7%) consented to participate. Although the sample was not randomly selected, the sample incluided workers from multiple companies reflecting the diversity of workforce. The data was collected between January 2021 and February 2023.

Data collection procedures

All data were collected on-site during scheduled workplace visits by an interdisciplinary research team specialising in occupational health and ergonomics. On-site assessment, rather than remote survey administration, was employed to prevent comprehension bias and enhance the completeness of the collected data (Arcury TA et al. 2012; Mazloumi A et al. 2025).

Sociodemographic and Occupational Assessment: Participants completed an ad hoc questionnaire capturing sociodemographic variables (sex, country of origin, educational level, number of children) and occupational characteristics (years of experience, weekly working hours).

Anthropometry: Body height was measured using a stadiometer (Marsden, United Kingdom) and body weight was measured using a digital floor scale (Tanita, HD-314 w, USA). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing body weight in kilograms by the square of height (kg/m²).

Cardiorespiratory fitness: Cardiorespiratory fitness was evaluated using the Ruffier–Dickson Index (RDI). The protocol requires recording heart rate (HR) before and after performing 30 squats in 45 s, allowing calculation of an index that classifies fitness levels from excellent to poor (Newman KL et al. 2015; Piquet L et al. 2000). Regarding reliability tests, Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) values are 0.19, indicating low to substantialy consistency. For this reason, it is considered a precise measure of physical fitness (Sartor F et al. 2016). HR was measured with a heart rate monitor (Polar Electro, M430, Finland) (Perkiö-Mäkelä M and Hentilä H; 2005).

Grip strength: Two types of grip strength were assessed: hand grip strength (HGS) by a hydraulic hand dynamometer (Jamar, 503051, USA) and hand clamp strength (HCS) by a pinch gauge (Jamar, USA). The procedures for body position and verbal structions were standardized according to the ASHT (ASTH 2026). Each participant completed 3 attempts with the dominant hand for both HGS and HCS tests, with a 30 s rest period between attempts. The highest values (kg) were recorded from the 3 attempts per test. The intrasubject reliability was excellent (ICC3,1: 0.91–0.97), and a strong correlation (ICC3,1: 0.90–0.98) for HGS by a hydraulic hand dynamometer (Gränicher P, et al. 2024). According to the systematic review by Szekeres et al., hand clamp strength measurement using a pinch gauge demonstrates good to excellent reliability, with intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) values generally exceeding 0.75 (Szekeres M. el atl. 2025)

Lower-limb strength: Lower-limb strength was assessed using the countermovement jump test (CMJ). Participants performed two maximal-effort jumps with arm swing permitted, and the highest jump height (cm) was retained for analysis (Markovic G et al. 2004). These results indicate that the countermovement jump test demonstrates good reliability, with strong agreement both within the same day (κ = 0.81) and between days (ICC₂,₁ = 0.88) (Karim et al. 2019). Measurements were obtained using the photoelectric system (Optojump Next, Microgate, Italy).

Musculoskeletal complaints: Musculoskeletal complaints were evaluated using the Standardised Nordic Questionnaire (SNQ). Responses are coded dichotomously (yes/no), and the total score is obtained by summing the number of body regions with reported symptoms (Kuorinka I et al. 1987). The Standardised Nordic Questionnaire has demonstrated good reliability, with test–retest agreement values (Cohen’s kappa) typically ranging from 0.60 to 0.81 (Gomez Rodriguez et al. 2020).

Perceived stress and depressive symptoms: Stress and depressive symptoms were assessed using the Goldberg Questionnaire, a validated screening tool composed of two blocks of questions: Q1 and Q2. Q1 focuses primarily on anxiety-related symptoms, while Q2 assesses depressive symptoms. Each block consists of dichotomous (yes/no) items, and higher scores within each section indicate greater levels of psychological distress (Cohen S et al. 1983; Spitzer RL et al. 2006). The Goldberg Questionnaire has demonstrated good reliability, with a mean Cronbach’s alpha of approximately 0.84 reported in a recent systematic review (Wojujutari et al., 2024).

Work Ability: Work ability was assessed using the Work Ability Index (WAI) questionnaire. The WAI is a multidimensional measure that evaluates a worker’s capacity to perform their job considering physical and mental health, job demands, and future work ability expectations. It has been widely used in occupational health research (Ilmarinen 2009). The WAI has demonstrated good reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha of approximately 0.78 reported in validation studies (Mazloumi et al., 2019).

Ethical considerations

The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of the University of The Basque Country (M10/2022/260). Participants were informed of the study’s aims and procedures and provided written informed consent. Data privacy and confidentiality were maintained in accordance with occupational health research standards (World Medical Association, 2026).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Comparisons between migrant and non-migrant workers were conducted using independent-samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and χ² tests for categorical variables. Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients were used to examine associations between musculoskeletal complaints, psychosocial variables, and functional measures. A multivariable linear regression model was used with the SNQ total score as the dependent variable to identify factors independently associated with musculoskeletal complaints. Variables were selected for inclusion in the linear regression models if they showed an association with the outcome at a significance level of p ≤ 0.20 in bivariate analyses (Hosmer DM et al. 2013; Mickey et al. 1989). A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was adopted. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), and heteroskedasticity was formally evaluated using the Breusch–Pagan test. Data were analysed using SPSS version 29.0.2.0.

Table 6 presents the regression model that explains a substantial proportion of the variance in musculoskeletal complaints (R² = 0.574), indicating that the included variables capture relevant dimensions of the phenomenon. Both Goldberg subscales (Q1 and Q2) were initially explored; however, only Q1 was included in the final regression model due to its significant association with the outcome variable. Among the predictors, psychological distress (Goldberg Q1) showed the strongest positive association, suggesting that psychosocial factors play a central role. In contrast, migrant status and handgrip strength were inversely associated with musculoskeletal complaints, pointing to potential protective factors. Self-reported work ability also emerged as a significant contributor, reinforcing the relevance of subjective functional perception. The regression models reported in Table 6 were estimated using ordinary least squares . All models were specified with SNQ as the outcome variable and included the set of independent variables described above. To assess potential multicollinearity, variance inflation factors (VIF) were computed for all predictors. All VIF values were below the commonly accepted threshold of 5, indicating that multicollinearity is not a concern. Model assumptions were examined through residual diagnostics. Heteroscedasticity was formally assessed using the Breusch–Pagan test, which did not indicate significant heteroscedasticity (BP = 2.63, df = 4, p = 0.62).

Internal consistency analyses were conducted for the main study variables. The SNQ showed good internal consistency in both migrant (α = 0.875) and non-migrant participants (α = 0.822). Grip strength measures also showed good reliability (α = 0.823 and 0.768, respectively), as did lower limb strength assessed through the Ruffier test (α = 0.700 in migrants and α = 0.842 in non-migrants). The Goldberg questionnaire showed acceptable internal consistency, although slightly lower among migrants (α = 0.66) compared to non-migrants (α = 0.771). Similarly, the Work Ability Index (WAI) showed acceptable internal consistency in both groups (α = 0.691 and 0.710, respectively).

Discussion

The present study examined associations between functional capacity, musculoskeletal complaints, and psychosocial variables among migrant and non-migrant agricultural workers employed in greenhouse horticulture. Given the cross-sectional nature of the data, the analysis identifies conditional associations rather than causal effects. The models adjust for observed characteristics to reduce confounding, but unobserved heterogeneity and reverse causality cannot be fully ruled out. The most relevant finding concerns the multivariable regression model [Table 6], in which migrant status, lower scores on the Goldberg scale, greater handgrip strength and higher self-reported work ability were independently associated with lower musculoskeletal complaint scores. These findings contribute novel evidence to agricultural occupational health research, a field traditionally focused predominantly on biomechanical risk factors.

The prevalence of musculoskeletal complaints observed in our sample is consistent with the international literature on primary sector workers. Research conducted among olive grove workers in Spain reported that 88.76% experienced some form of musculoskeletal discomfort, predominantly affecting the lumbar region, knees and upper limbs (BarneoAlcántara M et al. 2020). Similarly, in greenhouse pepper cultivation, ergonomic assessments using OWAS and RULA identified high postural risk levels, particularly involving the back and lower extremities (Gómez-Galán M et al. 2021). Our findings also align with earlier reviews identifying agriculture as one of the occupational sectors most exposed to repetitive biomechanical overload and sustained awkward postures (Bernard, C. and Tourne, M. 2007). Taken together, these studies confirm that greenhouse environments remain ergonomically demanding settings associated with elevated musculoskeletal risk. Therefore, our results are consistent with the existing literature; however, in our study, the pattern suggests that musculoskeletal complaints are not only very common but are also closely linked to psychosocial and functional factors, which supports a multidimensional interpretation of occupational risk. This reinforces the importance of adopting integrated models that incorporate both biomechanical and psychosocial dimensions of occupational exposure.

The most striking finding of the regression model is that migrant status was associated with lower musculoskeletal complaint scores. At first glance, this may appear counterintuitive, as migrant workers are often described as more vulnerable due to precarious employment conditions and reduced access to preventive services(Hargreaves, S et al. 2019; Yang, S.-Y et al. 2019). However, several plausible explanations exist.

First, this finding may reflect the healthy migrant effect (HME), a well-documented epidemiological phenomenon whereby migrants initially exhibit better health indicators than the host population, partly due to positive health selection prior to migration (McDonald, J.T. and Kennedy, S. 2004; Markides, K.S et al. 2019). Individuals who migrate for employment in physically demanding sectors may represent a subgroup with higher baseline physical resilience. Systematic reviews have confirmed that migrants frequently display better initial self-rated health and lower chronic disease prevalence compared with native-born populations, although this advantage often declines over time (Markides, K.S and Rote, S. 2019). In addition, physically demanding occupations are often affected by a healthy worker effect, whereby individuals with poorer health are less likely to enter, obtain, or maintain employment in jobs requiring sustained physical effort (Li and Sung, 1999; Shah, 2009). Therefore, the lower prevalence of musculoskeletal complaints observed among migrant workers may partly reflect health-selection processes operating both before migration and during labour market participation, rather than differences in current occupational exposures alone. Consequently, migrant status should not be interpreted as a protective factor per se, but rather as a marker potentially associated with underlying differences in health status and work-related selection mechanisms.

Second, migrant workers in our sample were, on average, younger than non-migrants. Age is a well-established factor associated with chronic pain and functional decline. In a prospective cohort study of office workers, age and prior musculoskeletal pain were associated with the development of chronic neck and low-back pain (Sihawong, R et al. 2016). Although age was considered descriptively in our analyses, interactions between age and functional variables may partially account for the association observed.

Third, cultural factors may influence pain perception and reporting behavior. Differences in cultural norms surrounding the expression of discomfort may therefore modulate symptom reporting among migrant workers. Importantly, the HME should not be interpreted as evidence of reduced occupational risk. Longitudinal research suggests that initial health advantages may erode under sustained exposure to physically demanding work and psychosocial stressors (Juárez, S.P et al. 2019; Markides, K.S. and Rote, S. 2017). Continuous monitoring of migrant worker health is therefore essential.

One of the most consistent findings in our model is the association between lower Goldberg scores and lower musculoskeletal complaint scores. The association between psychological distress and musculoskeletal pain is widely documented. In office workers, high psychological job demands were associated with chronic neck and low-back pain (Sihawong, R. et al. 2016). Likewise, elevated mental workload has been strongly associated with severe musculoskeletal disorders in industrial workers (Rodríguez-Pulido et al., 2025). These findings are consistent with the biopsychosocial model of pain, which posits that anxiety, stress and maladaptive coping increase central sensitization, reduce pain thresholds and perpetuate disability. Our results confirm that mental health is not a peripheral factor but a central determinant of musculoskeletal outcomes in agricultural workers. Additionally, literature on the ‘physical activity paradox’ suggests that prolonged occupational physical activity combined with psychosocial strain may exert adverse cardiovascular and systemic effects (Janssen, T. I. and Voelcker-Rehage, C. 2023). This further supports the need to address physical and psychosocial workload simultaneously rather than in isolation.

Greater handgrip strength was independently associated with lower musculoskeletal complaint scores. Handgrip strength is a recognized marker of global functional capacity and musculoskeletal health. Reference values demonstrate age-related decline and associations with morbidity and disability (Amaral, C.A et al. 2019). In occupational contexts, higher muscular capacity may buffer repetitive microtrauma and mechanical overload by enhancing joint stability and delaying fatigue onset. In construction workers, functional indicators have similarly been identified as factors associated with sick leave due to low-back pain (Bosman, Bosman, L.C. et al. 2018. et al. 2018). Our findings extend this evidence to agricultural greenhouse settings, its inverse association with musculoskeletal complaints may reflect greater physical resilience and tolerance to physical workload.

Higher self-reported work ability was also associated with lower musculoskeletal complaint scores. Lower work ability has been shown to be associated with increased risk of sickness absence due to low-back pain (Bosman, Bosman, L.C. et al. 2018. et al. 2018). The present findings suggest that subjective perception of work capacity may reflect underlying musculoskeletal status with reasonable accuracy. Incorporating work ability assessments into agricultural health surveillance may therefore provide an efficient screening tool for early risk identification.

The practical implications of these findings suggest that preventive strategies in greenhouse horticulture should extend beyond traditional ergonomic adjustments. Although ergonomic risk reduction remains essential, as evidenced by OWAS and RULA assessments in greenhouse tasks (Gómez-Galán M et al. 2021), a multidimensional approach is warranted, incorporating structured muscular strengthening programs, periodic functional assessments including handgrip strength, systematic psychological screening using validated tools such as the Goldberg scale, and continuous monitoring and promotion of perceived work ability. Furthermore, recent systematic evidence indicates that workplace interventions combining physical activity, education, and psychosocial support achieve more consistent health improvements than single-component programs (Zhang, S et al. 2025).

Limitations

The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether the observed associations persist over time. Self-reported questionnaires may introduce reporting bias; however, the SNQ has demonstrated adequate validity in Spanish populations (López-Aragón L et al. 2024). The small sample size, particularly for women (n = 35) and non-migrant subgroups (n = 19 men, n = 10 women), limits statistical power and the generalizability of sex-stratified analyses. Furthermore, the model’s moderate explanatory power is consistent with multifactorial pain research, as musculoskeletal complaints arise from complex interactions between biological, psychological, and organizational factors that cannot be fully captured within a single regression model. Due to the cross-sectional design, potential endogeneity between some variables (e.g., work ability and musculoskeletal complaints) cannot be ruled out, and therefore results should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that musculoskeletal complaints among greenhouse agricultural workers cannot be explained solely by physical workload. Migrant status, psychological well-being, muscular strength and perceived work ability are independently associated with musculoskeletal complaints. The findings support a biopsychosocial framework for prevention in the primary sector and highlight the importance of equity-focused interventions that preserve early health advantages while addressing long-term occupational exposures. These findings may support the development of targeted strategies aimed at improving the health and well-being of agricultural workers, particularly in migrant populations.
