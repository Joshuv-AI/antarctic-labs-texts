Gasdermin‐Mediated Pyroptosis: Novel Strategies Against Colorectal Cancer
Authors: Kaibo Guo, Yuqian Feng, Jiamin Lu, Nuerbiye Abudurexiti, Xinlei Zhou, Mengfan Wei, Song Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPyroptosis, a form of programmed cell death mediated by gasdermin proteins, has gained attention for its dual role in colorectal cancer (CRC) progression and therapy. While chronic pyroptosis‐driven inflammation can promote tumorigenesis, acute induction of pyroptosis in tumor cells offers promising antitumor effects. Understanding the mechanisms and implications of pyroptosis in CRC could lead to novel therapeutic strategies. Gasdermin proteins, particularly GSDMD and GSDME, are central to pyroptotic processes in CRC. GSDMD activation, often through NLRP3 inflammasome signaling or chemotherapeutic agents like simvastatin, induces pyroptosis and modulates immune infiltration. Conversely, GSDMC has been implicated in CRC progression under metabolic stress by recruiting immunosuppressive cells. Although frequently silenced in CRC, GSDME enhances sensitivity to chemoradiation and synergizes with immune checkpoint inhibitors by releasing immunostimulatory molecules. Inflammasomes, notably NLRP3 and AIM2, also play significant roles in CRC pathogenesis through pyroptosis and cytokine secretion. NLRP3 activation exacerbates tumor growth via inflammatory pathways, while AIM2 exerts tumor‐suppressive effects, especially in BRAF‐mutant CRC. The gut microbiota further influences inflammasome activity, with certain strains promoting chemoresistance and others enhancing antitumor immunity. Therapeutically, inducing pyroptosis synergizes with conventional therapies and immunotherapies, overcoming apoptosis resistance and resensitizing tumors. Pyroptosis releases tumor antigens and damage‐associated molecular patterns, recruiting cytotoxic lymphocytes and natural killer cells, thereby remodeling the immunosuppressive microenvironment. Pyroptosis represents a double‐edged sword in CRC, offering both challenges and opportunities. Harnessing its antitumor potential while mitigating pro‐tumorigenic inflammation requires innovative strategies. Future research should focus on elucidating the isoform‐specific roles of gasdermins, optimizing therapeutic approaches to induce pyroptosis.

Introduction

Programmed cell death (PCD), governed by specific signaling pathways, plays a crucial role in maintaining the balance between cell proliferation and death, which is essential for homeostasis [1]. Dysregulation of PCD can lead to various diseases, particularly malignancies, such as colorectal cancer (CRC) [2]. Based on their mechanisms and morphological features, forms of PCD are broadly categorized into lytic and nonlytic cell death. Apoptosis, the most extensively studied type of nonlytic cell death, is characterized by the formation of apoptotic bodies and typically occurs without the release of proinflammatory mediators or cellular contents. By contrast, pyroptosis and necroptosis are lytic forms of cell death that induce strong inflammatory responses. Pyroptosis was initially identified as a caspase‐1‐dependent process that occurs in macrophages and monocytes in response to infections or microbial toxins. Caspase‐1, an inflammatory caspase, is activated via canonical inflammasomes, whereas noncanonical inflammasomes include murine caspase‐11 and its human homologs caspase‐4 and 5, which directly detect intracellular lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [3, 4].

The gasdermin family includes gasdermin A (GSDMA), gasdermin B (GSDMB), gasdermin C (GSDMC), gasdermin D (GSDMD), and gasdermin E (GSDME, also known as deafness autosomal dominant 5), all of which share a conserved N‐terminal domain with pyroptosis‐inducing potential [5]. Cleavage by proteases, mainly caspases, eliminates the autoinhibitory effect of the C‐terminal domain, allowing the N‐terminal fragment to trigger pyroptosis. Although GSDMD contains specific cleavage sites for inflammatory caspases, other gasdermins may be activated by distinct proteases [6]. GSDMD is a member of the gasdermin family that exhibits membrane pore‐forming activity. With the identification of GSDMD as a key executor of pyroptosis, this form of cell death has been redefined as gasdermin‐dependent PCD. In the canonical inflammasome pathway, activated caspase‐1 cleaves the proinflammatory cytokines pro‐IL‐1β and pro‐IL‐18 into their mature forms, which are released via the negative charge of GSDMD pores [7]. Caspase‐1 also cleaves GSDMD at its central linker region, releasing the N‐terminal fragment that forms pores in the plasma membrane, leading to membrane permeabilization, cytokine release, and pyroptosis [8, 9, 10]. However, it is noteworthy that how GSDMA is activated has been rarely studied. GSDMA, as the dominant gasdermin in the skin, and Streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxin B cleavage of GSDMA releases an active amino‐terminal fragment that triggers pyroptosis [11, 12].

CRC is one of the most common cancers worldwide, with an estimated 1.9 million new cases and 900,000 deaths annually. The incidence of CRC is increasing, particularly in developed countries [13]. Recent studies have linked CRC to cellular pyroptosis mediated by gasdermin proteins, such as GSDMD and GSDME. Gasdermins promote membrane rupture, inflammation, and tumor progression in CRC by triggering inflammatory responses [14, 15]. Understanding this relation may provide insights into novel therapeutic strategies targeting pyroptosis in CRC treatment.

Certain anticancer agents exert their therapeutic effects on CRC through gasdermin mediated pyroptosis. Furthermore, pyroptosis‐induced inflammation enhances immune cell infiltration and alleviates immunosuppression in the tumor microenvironment (TME). Consequently, the induction of pyroptosis in CRC cells may represent a promising therapeutic strategy (Graphical abstract, Figure 1).

Signaling Pathways Involved in the Regulation of Pyroptosis

Morphologically, pyroptosis and apoptosis share certain features, including chromatin condensation and deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) fragmentation. However, the mechanisms and biological consequences of these two forms of cell death differ significantly [16]. Apoptosis is characterized by cellular shrinkage and the formation of apoptotic bodies, which are subsequently engulfed by surrounding macrophages, resulting in nonlytic and non‐inflammatory cell death. This process is tightly regulated by the caspase family of cysteine proteases [17, 18]. Specifically, initiator caspases (such as caspase‐2, ‐8, ‐9, and ‐10) are activated in response to intrinsic or extrinsic apoptotic stimuli, subsequently activating executioner caspases (caspase‐3, ‐6, and ‐7) to execute the apoptotic program [19, 20].

Pyroptosis is a lytic form of PCD characterized by cellular swelling and the formation of large membrane bubbles. Unlike apoptosis, pyroptosis is inherently inflammatory and is mediated by distinct signaling pathways (Figure 2) [21, 22]. The canonical and noncanonical inflammasome pathways are the primary mechanisms that induce pyroptosis. Additionally, certain proteases, including caspase‐3, caspase‐8, and granzymes, can cleave gasdermin proteins, promoting membrane pore formation and pyroptosis (Figure 3) [23, 24].

Schematic representation of canonical and noncanonical inflammasome pathways in pyroptosis. The canonical inflammasome pathway is initiated by sensor proteins (e.g., NLRP3, NLRP1b, NLRC4, AIM2, and pyrin) that detect pathogen‐ or damage‐associated molecular patterns through ligand‐specific interactions. For example, NLRP3 responds to diverse stimuli such as bacterial pore‐forming toxins, fungal peptides, viral dsRNA, and DAMPs (Alu RNA, ox‐mtDNA, S100A8/S100A9, Prdx1, ATP), while NLRP1b is triggered by anthrax lethal toxin, NLRC4 by bacterial flagellin or type III secretion system components, AIM2 by cytosolic bacterial/viral dsDNA, and pyrin by Rho GTPase inactivation mediated by bacterial toxins. Upon activation, these sensors oligomerize and recruit the adaptor protein ASC, which facilitates pro‐caspase‐1 recruitment and its autocatalytic cleavage into active caspase‐1. This effector protease then processes pro‐IL‐1β and pro‐IL‐18 into mature cytokines and cleaves gasdermin D (GSDMD), releasing its N‐terminal pore‐forming fragment. GSDMD‐mediated plasma membrane permeabilization enables secretion of IL‐1β, IL‐18, and HMGB1. In contrast, the noncanonical pathway bypasses ASC and sensor proteins: Cytoplasmic lipopolysaccharide (LPS) directly binds and activates human caspase‐4/−5 or murine caspase‐11, which similarly cleave GSDMD to drive pyroptotic pore formation and cytokine release, thereby converging with canonical pathway outcomes.

Caspase3/8 and granzyme‐mediated pyroptotic pathways. The virulence factor YopJ from Yersinia pestis triggers caspase‐8‐dependent pyroptosis by inhibiting TAK1, a regulatory kinase required for survival signaling. TAK1 suppression induces caspase‐8 activation, which directly cleaves gasdermin D (GSDMD) to generate pore‐forming N‐terminal fragments, driving membrane permeabilization and inflammatory cell death. Separately, caspase‐3 activated by chemotherapeutic agents cleaves gasdermin E (GSDME) to produce its cytotoxic N‐terminal domain, bypassing canonical inflammasome pathways to initiate pyroptosis. In parallel, cytotoxic lymphocytes (e.g., T cells, NK cells) induce caspase‐independent pyroptosis in tumor cells through granzyme‐mediated cleavage of gasdermins: Granzyme A specifically processes GSDMB, while granzyme B targets GSDME, both generating membrane‐disruptive fragments that execute pyroptosis independent of caspase signaling.

Canonical Inflammasome Pathway‐Induced Pyroptosis

The canonical inflammasome signaling pathway plays a central role in caspase‐1‐dependent pyroptosis. Inflammasomes are multiprotein complexes typically comprising a sensor protein, such as a pattern recognition receptor (PRR), and an adaptor protein, such as apoptosis‐associated speck‐like protein containing a caspase recruitment domain (CARD; ASC), which bridges interactions between the PRR and effector enzyme caspase‐1 [25, 26]. Among PRRs, the nucleotide‐binding oligomerization domain (NOD)‐like receptors, including NOD‐like receptor family pyrin domain‐containing 3 (NLRP3), NOD‐like receptor family pyrin domain containing 1b (NLRP1b), NOD‐like receptor family caspase activation and recruitment domain‐containing protein 4 (NLRC4), absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2), and pyrin, are the most well‐studied components of inflammasome complexes [27].

These receptors detect various external pathogens and internal damage signals, such as bacterial toxins, cytosolic double‐stranded DNA (dsDNA), and crystalline structures, triggering the formation of the canonical inflammasome complex (Figure 2) [28]. NLRP3, one of the best‐characterized inflammasomes, is activated by diverse stimuli, including bacterial pore‐forming toxins, viral ribonucleic acid (RNA), fungal peptides, and damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [29]. For instance, triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cell 2 (TREM2) deficiency exacerbates pyroptosis and impairs bacterial clearance in macrophages infected with pyogenic pathogens, whereas caspase‐1/GSDMD inhibition enhances eradication. The triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cell 2/β‐catenin axis suppresses NLRP3 activation, mechanistically linking inflammasome regulation to pyroptosis control and antibacterial responses [30]. In CRC, the DAMPs Alu RNA, oxidized mitochondrial DNA (ox‐mtDNA), S100A8/S100A9, peroxiredoxin 1 (Prdx1), and extracellular ATP each engage the NLRP3 inflammasome—Alu RNA via exosomal transfer to promote epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) [31], ox‐mtDNA as a direct activating ligand requiring new mtDNA synthesis [32], S100A8/S100A9 through toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4)/receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) signaling to exacerbate colitis‐associated tumorigenesis [33], Prdx1 by triggering pyroptosis via the NLRP3/caspase‐1/GSDMD axis to inhibit tumor growth [34], and ATP via P2X7 receptor activation to drive chronic inflammation and cancer progression [35]. NLRP1b functions as a sensor for the Bacillus anthracis lethal toxin, initiating pyroptosis through a proteolytic activation mechanism [36, 37]. Emerging models have suggested that toxin‐induced proteasomal degradation drives NLRP1b activation. Anthrax lethal factor cleavage exposes the N‐terminal degron of NLRP1b, targeting it for ubiquitin‐mediated proteasomal degradation. This posttranslational modification liberates the C‐terminal fragment, which undergoes autoproteolytic processing and subsequent oligomerization into high‐order complexes. These structural assemblies serve as catalytic platforms that facilitate caspase‐1 activation and inflammasome‐mediated inflammatory cascades [38, 39]. Similarly, NLRC4 inflammasome activation is triggered by bacterial components, such as flagellin and type III secretion system proteins [40]. AIM2 specifically detects cytosolic dsDNA from bacteria and viruses, forming an inflammasome with the ligand and ASC to activate caspase‐1 [41], whereas the pyrin inflammasome recognizes bacterial toxins that disrupt Rho GTPase activity [42, 43]. Upon sensing their respective ligands, these inflammasome receptors oligomerize and recruit pro‐caspase‐1 through CARD interactions or via ASC adaptors [44].

Activated caspase‐1 processes the proinflammatory cytokines pro‐interleukin‐1 beta (IL‐1β) and pro‐interleukin‐18 (IL‐18) into their active forms, which are then secreted. Simultaneously, caspase‐1 cleaves GSDMD and separates its N‐terminal domain, which inserts into the cell membrane to form pores. This pore formation leads to membrane permeabilization, plasma membrane rupture, and the release of cytokines and high‐mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) [45], ultimately resulting in pyroptosis [46, 47]. Previously considered a passive consequence of cell death, nerve injury‐induced protein 1 (NINJ1) forms chain‐like oligomers with kinked transmembrane helices and a hydrophobic concave face that encircles and cuts membrane patches, leading to plasma membrane rupture during pyroptosis [48]. However, the exact mechanism linking pyroptosis to NINJ1 activation remains to be fully elucidated, providing a promising avenue for further research [49].

Noncanonical Inflammasome Pathway‐Induced Pyroptosis

In the noncanonical pyroptosis pathway, caspase‐4 and caspase‐5 in humans and caspase‐11 in mice recognize and directly interact with cytoplasmic LPS from gram‐negative bacteria through their CARDs (Figure 2) [50]. This interaction promotes oligomerization and the subsequent activation of caspases. Once activated, caspases‐4, ‐5, and ‐11 cleave GSDMD and trigger pyroptosis [51, 52], a process functionally similar to caspase‐1‐mediated pyroptosis [53].

Recent in vitro studies have confirmed that GSDMD acts as the primary executor of pyroptosis initiated by the noncanonical inflammasome pathway. However, during canonical inflammasome activation, complete inhibition of pyroptosis is not achieved in GSDMD‐deficient cells, likely because of the substrate flexibility of caspase‐1 [54]. Intriguingly, in the presence of GSDMD, caspase‐1 predominantly induces pyroptosis, whereas its absence shifts the pathway toward apoptosis. This caspase‐1‐driven apoptosis is mediated by caspase‐3 activation, which can eventually cause secondary pyroptosis via GSDME cleavage [55, 56].

Moreover, GSDMD‐induced pore formation facilitates potassium efflux, which acts as a signal to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to the maturation of the proinflammatory cytokines IL‐1β and IL‐18. While caspase‐1 is crucial for processing pro‐IL‐1β and pro‐IL‐18 into their active forms, it is not required for GSDMD cleavage in the noncanonical pathway, highlighting distinct regulatory mechanisms between these pyroptotic pathways [57]. A study has shown that in caspase‐1‐deficient macrophages, GSDMD cleavage is still detectable after LPS electroporation, and this cleavage is primarily mediated by caspase‐11 [51].

Other Caspase Mediated Pyroptosis

Inflammatory caspases (caspase‐1/4/5/11) selectively proteolyze GSDMD at a conserved aspartate residue (D275 in humans) that is absent in other gasdermin family members [44]. By contrast, apoptotic caspases, such as caspase‐3, exhibit distinct substrate preferences, cleaving GSDME at its interdomain linker [58, 59]. Interestingly, GSDMD harbors two distinct cleavage sites with opposing functional outcomes, making it a substrate hijacked by competing caspases. On one hand, caspase‐8 recognizes and cleaves GSDMD at the D276 site, releasing its pore‐forming N‐terminal domain, which inserts into the plasma membrane to trigger pyroptosis, leading to cell lysis and the release of pro‐inflammatory factors. On the other hand, caspase‐3 cleaves GSDMD at the D88 site within the N‐terminal domain. This cleavage event generates a fragment that is incapable of forming functional pores, thereby inactivating GSDMD and preventing pyroptosis [60]. In addition, the emerging regulatory mechanism is that calcium influx through GSDMD pores recruits the endosomal sorting complexes required for transport III (ESCRT‐III) machinery to damaged membrane areas, initiating a membrane repair process that counteracts pyroptosis by reducing cell lysis and IL‐1β release. This reveals a negative feedback loop where cells actively attempt to survive and limit inflammation during gasdermin‐mediated pyroptosis [61]. Recent studies reveal that GSDMD activation can occur through S‐palmitoylation at Cys191, independent of its canonical cleavage by inflammatory caspases. This palmitoylation, catalyzed by zinc finger aspartate‐histidine‐histidine‐cysteine type containing 5/9 (ZDHHC5/9) and enhanced by ROS, directly mediates membrane translocation and pore formation even in cleavage‐deficient GSDMD, challenging the concept that proteolytic cleavage is the sole trigger for gasdermin family activation [62, 63].

Chemotherapeutic agents, such as doxorubicin, induce intracellular accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and activate caspase‐3, generating the GSDME‐N‐terminal fragment that permeabilizes plasma membranes and redirects apoptotic signaling toward pyroptotic execution in GSDME‐high malignancies (Figure 3) [64]. In CRC cells, lobaplatin triggers ROS elevation and c‐Jun N‐terminal kinase (JNK) activation, which promotes B‐cell lymphoma 2‐associated X protein (BAX)‐mediated mitochondrial cytochrome c (Cyt c) release, leading to activation of the caspase‐9/caspase‐3 cascade and subsequent GSDME cleavage, thereby inducing pyroptosis [65]. Another study demonstrated that apoptin‐enhanced ROS triggers BAX translocation and Cyt c release via Tom20, activating caspase‐9, which is the primary upstream caspase for caspase‐3 activation. Activated caspase‐3 subsequently cleaves GSDME, leading to GSDME‐N pore formation and lactate dehydrogenase release [66]. Furthermore, this caspase‐3‐dependent pyroptosis is recapitulated in engineered cells in which TNF‐α/cycloheximide activates caspase‐3‐mediated GSDMD processing via a synthetic caspase‐3 cleavage site [67].

Caspase‐8, traditionally recognized as a non‐inflammatory caspase, has emerged as a mediator of pyroptosis in certain pathological conditions. A notable example is Yersinia pestis infection, in which the bacterial effector protein YopJ suppresses transforming growth factor (TGF)‐β‐activated kinase 1 (TAK1), thereby triggering caspase‐8 activation [68]. Activated caspase‐8 directly cleaves GSDMD, inducing pore formation and cell death. Importantly, TGFβ‐activated kinase 1 inhibition represents a broader pathogenic strategy, as multiple pathogens exploit this mechanism to activate caspase‐8‐dependent pyroptosis (Figure 3) [69]. In addition, ROS‐initiated death receptor 6 endocytosis activates caspase‐8‐mediated cleavage of GSDMC for potential clinical application in tumor therapy [70]. These insights underscore the mechanistic complexity of pyroptosis and emphasize the necessity of deeper exploration of its context‐specific regulatory networks.

Emerging insights into cancer immunology have revealed that pyroptosis is a potent amplifier of antitumor immunity, challenging historical paradigms that attributed immune‐mediated tumor elimination solely to non‐inflammatory processes. Key cytotoxic effectors, including CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, can trigger pyroptosis in GSDMB‐expressing tumor cells via granzyme A (GzmA)‐dependent proteolytic activation of gasdermin (Figure 3) [71]. Furthermore, elevated tumor‐intrinsic GSDME expression primes malignant cells for dual immune recognition, enhancing phagocytic clearance by tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs) and sensitizing cells to CD8+ T cells/NK‐mediated cytotoxicity [72]. Notably, granzyme B (GzmB) bypasses canonical apoptotic signaling by directly cleaving GSDME at the caspase‐3 recognition site, thereby initiating caspase‐independent pyroptotic cell death (Figure 3) [73]. These granzyme‐driven mechanisms collectively establish a proinflammatory feed‐forward loop within the TME, driving robust immunostimulatory effects that may inform novel therapeutic strategies.

Overall, the interplay among caspases, granzymes, and gasdermins underscores the complexity of pyroptosis in cancer biology. This inflammatory cell death not only contributes to tumor clearance but also influences the TME, offering novel therapeutic opportunities to enhance antitumor immunity.

Role of Gasdermins in CRC

CRC is the third most diagnosed cancer (1.93 million cases) and second leading cause of cancer‐related deaths (935,000 deaths) globally, as of 2023 [74]. While high‐income countries report a declining incidence because of widespread screening, low‐ and middle‐income regions face rising mortality caused by limited healthcare access [75]. Early‐onset CRC (diagnosed in individuals younger than 50 years) has surged by 20%–30% since 2000 and is linked to obesity, sedentary lifestyles, and processed diets [76]. Survival rates differ markedly between localized disease (90% 5‐year survival) and metastatic disease (15%), highlighting the urgent need for early detection and equitable interventions [77]. Emerging research increasingly recognizes pyroptosis, a recently defined mechanism of PCD, as a critical modulator of CRC. Current evidence reveals multifaceted associations between pyroptotic signaling cascades (including gasdermin family proteins and inflammasome activation) and tumor progression pathways in CRC. Pharmacological targeting of these molecular mediators to induce controlled pyroptosis has notable therapeutic implications for CRC intervention.

The expression and function of GSDMA in CRC remain unclear, although GSDMA expression is downregulated in gastric cancer and may function as a tumor suppressor gene [78]. Recent studies have demonstrated abnormal expression and dysfunction of GSDMB, GSDMC, GSDMD, and GSDME in CRC, implying their potential role in tumorigenesis (Table S1) [79].

GSDMB and CRC

GSDMB is highly expressed in the epithelial cells of organs, such as the gastrointestinal tract, and interfaces with the external environment. Emerging evidence underscores its pathological role in enteric infections, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and CRC [80]. No significant differences in GSDMB expression were observed in the cytoplasm, cell membrane, or nucleus between CRC and normal epithelial cells. However, a study has shown that aberrantly upregulated GSDMB suppresses CRC progression by interacting with insulin‐like growth factor 2 mRNA‐binding protein 1 (IGF2BP1) and modulating dual specificity phosphatase 6 (DUSP6)‐extracellular signal‐regulated kinase (ERK) pathway, as demonstrated using in vitro cell cultures, intestinal organoids, and in vivo GSDMB transgenic mouse models [81]. Moreover, membranous GSDMB positivity detected by immunohistochemistry was predominantly observed in cases with lower TNM stages, absence of lymph node metastasis, lower histological grades, and reduced likelihood of vascular invasion, suggesting an association between GSDMB expression and lower tumor aggressiveness. Cytoplasmic GSDMB‐positive expression in CRC is associated with a favorable prognosis. The 5‐year overall survival rate of the cytoplasmic GSDMB‐positive group was 78%, significantly higher than that of the cytoplasmic GSDMB‐negative group (63%). GSDMB usually functions in CRC cells rather than immune cells, where its expression is positively associated with infiltrating GzmA+ CD8+ tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) that can induce pyroptosis via GzmA‐mediated cleavage of GSDMB, thereby modulating the tumor immune microenvironment and patient prognosis [82]. However, membranous or nuclear GSDMB positivity did not correlate with overall survival. Interestingly, in patients with CRC who received postoperative 5‐fluorouracil (5‐FU) chemotherapy, both cytoplasmic and nuclear GSDMB expression were identified as favorable predictors of overall survival [83]. Collectively, low GSDMB expression may serve as a potential predictive biomarker of poor prognosis in patients with CRC, possibly linked to reduced sensitivity to 5‐FU‐based chemotherapy; however, the precise mechanisms underlying this association require further investigation.

Despite the limited fundamental research on the role of GSDMB in intestinal inflammation and CRC, accumulating evidence has confirmed its genetic association with susceptibility to chronic mucositis. GSDMB is upregulated in IBD and orchestrates epithelial restitution and repair through pyroptosis‐independent mechanisms [84]. Experimental GSDMB knockdown substantially attenuates lytic cell death and diminishes 5‐FU chemosensitivity in CRC cells. Clinically, patients with CRC who exhibit GSDMB expression demonstrate superior therapeutic responses to 5‐FU‐based regimens, and double immunofluorescence showed that GSDMB co‐expressed with CD68 or S100A8 in stromal cells, potentially mediated by activation of the pyroptotic pathway and their immune microenvironment [83]. In general, these findings indicate that GSDMB is a promising predictive biomarker for chemotherapy efficacy, underscoring the importance of elucidating its precise pyroptosis‐related regulatory mechanisms in CRC pathogenesis.

GSDMC and CRC

Bioinformatic analysis revealed that GSDMC was the most prominently upregulated member of the gasdermin family in human CRC specimens. Comparative expression profiling demonstrated significantly elevated GSDMC levels in neoplastic tissues compared with those in adjacent normal mucosa across multiple cancer types, with particularly marked overexpression observed in CRC. Clinically, elevated GSDMC expression correlates with advanced tumor staging and significantly reduces the overall survival of patients with colon adenocarcinoma. Functional characterization studies have demonstrated that epithelial‐derived GSDMC splice variants (GSDMC2/3/4) exhibit oncogenic properties, promoting CRC cell proliferation in vitro and facilitating CRC development and spontaneous progression in vivo. Mechanistically, caspase‐6, but not caspase‐8, is a specific protease that activates GSDMC under tumor microenvironmental stress (hypoxia and glucose deprivation), triggering tumor cell pyroptosis and promoting tumor progression via C‐X‐C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CXCL2)‐dependent recruitment of myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [85]. However, conflicting roles for GSDMC in CRC progression have been reported. One study found that GSDMC promoted tumor cell proliferation during CRC development. Investigators established a CRC mouse model induced by inactivation of Tgfbr2 and Apc in the colonic epithelium, leading to adenocarcinoma formation. This unique model enhances the understanding of Tgfbr2 mutations in patients with CRC. Subsequent gene profiling analysis revealed that GSDMC expression was upregulated in CRC tissues compared with that in adjacent normal colonic tissues [86]. These findings suggest that GSDMC promotes tumor cell proliferation during CRC development and may represent a promising therapeutic target for patients with Tgfbr2‐mutated CRC.

GSDMD and CRC

The transcriptional expression of GSDMD showed no difference between CRC tissues and adjacent tissues in The Cancer Genome Atlas dataset [85]. However, contradictory findings have been reported regarding the role of GSDMD in CRC development. One study suggested that GSDMD represses inflammation‐induced colon cancer development by regulating apoptosis [87], whereas another claimed that GSDMD promotes CRC progression by inducing epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) autophosphorylation at Tyr1068 and subsequently activating downstream ERK1/2 [88]. Furthermore, GSDMD is closely associated with an imbalance in the intestinal flora. GSDMD promotes CRC development by regulating the intestinal flora to produce kynurenine [89], and the intestinal flora can also trigger CRC occurrence through NLRP3‐mediated activation of GSDMD [90]. However, in the MC38 subcutaneous CRC model, global GSDMD knockout (GSDMD −/−) C57BL/6 mice exhibited significantly accelerated tumor growth, increased tumor weight, and enhanced intratumoral infiltration of granulocytic MDSCs, accompanied by reduced CD8+ T cell and NK cell accumulation [91]. Moreover, GSDMD deficiency in CD4+ T cells impairs GSDMD‐N pore formation, reduces Ca2+ influx and interleukin‐2 (IL‐2) production, and consequently compromises CD8+ T cell‐mediated antitumor immunity, leading to accelerated CRC growth [92]. Survival analysis indicated that high cytoplasmic expression of GSDMD was an independent favorable prognostic indicator, suggesting a lower probability of distant metastasis and enhanced chemotherapy efficacy [93]. The specific mechanisms may be explained as follows: nuclear translocation of GSDMD, through interaction with poly (adenosine diphosphate‐ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP‐1), significantly inhibits DNA damage repair‐related functions and enhances CRC sensitivity to chemotherapy in a pyroptosis‐independent manner [14]. Another study showed that oxaliplatin sensitivity could be improved by GSDMD‐mediated pyroptosis [94].

GSDMD significantly modulates the cell death and proliferative processes of CRC cells, and its elevated expression presents a promising therapeutic avenue for triggering pyroptotic mechanisms to suppress tumor progression. Many approved drugs or antitumor agents, such as simvastatin, nanotoxin, ginsenoside Rh3, quercetin, secoisolariciresinol diglucoside, and luteolin, induce pyroptosis of CRC cells through GSDMD cleavage [95, 96, 97, 98, 99, 100].

Ginsenoside Rh3, a bioactive semi‐synthetic derivative isolated from the medicinal plant Panax ginseng, demonstrates significant antineoplastic potential through suppression of nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor 2 (NRF2) nuclear translocation, thereby downregulating heme oxygenase‐1 (HO‐1) expression. This process activates the NLRP3/caspase‐1 signaling axis and ultimately induces GSDMD‐mediated pyroptotic cell death, as evidenced by its potent antiproliferative effects in both in vitro CRC models and murine xenograft systems [97].

Simvastatin, a prototypical β‐hydroxy‐β‐methylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG‐CoA) reductase inhibitor used in cardiovascular risk management through suppression of cholesterol biosynthesis, has emerged as an innovative chemotherapeutic candidate. Simvastatin induces pyroptotic cell death in CRC via the ROS‐mediated caspase‐1 activation cascade, leading to GSDMD proteolysis [95]. Thus, simvastatin is a promising therapeutic modality for CRC.

GSDME and CRC

GSDME, also known as deafness autosomal dominant 5, was initially named for its association with hereditary deafness [101]. In recent years, GSDME has been recognized as one of the most common mediators of pyroptosis and is silenced in most cancer tissues because of high promoter methylation [102].

GSDME expression in CRC tissues does not differ from that in adjacent tissues [103], and no reports have established a relation between its expression and survival. Nevertheless, GSDME‐mediated pyroptosis plays an important role in CRC development and treatment. Pyroptotic epithelial cells mediated by GSDME release HMGB1, which induces tumor cell proliferation and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) expression through the ERK1/2 pathway and contributes to the tumorigenesis of colitis‐associated colorectal cancer (CAC) [104]. In contrast, the study by Zhang et al. employed transplantable tumor models, focusing on the tumor‐intrinsic role of GSDME. In these models, GSDME activates antitumor immunity by mediating pyroptosis, enhancing the phagocytic activity of TAMs, and increasing both the number and function of tumor‐infiltrating NK cells and CD8+ T cells [72]. In summary, the role of GSDME in tumor development is context‐dependent. In inflammation‐driven tumorigenesis, such as the CAC model, GSDME deficiency suppresses tumor growth by reducing inflammation‐associated pro‐tumor signals. In contrast, in immunogenic transplantable tumor models, loss of GSDME promotes tumor progression by impairing antitumor immunity. Future studies should further elucidate the regulatory mechanisms governing GSDME activation in distinct contexts and explore strategies to precisely target the GSDME pathway for therapeutic purposes.

Pyroptosis is closely associated with chemotherapeutic effects and drug resistance in CRC. GSDME mediates lobaplatin‐induced pyroptosis via the ROS/JNK/BAX‐mitochondrial apoptotic pathway, revealing how lobaplatin eradicates CRC cells [65]. Another study showed that GSDME enhances CRC cell pyroptosis and reduces resistance to irinotecan [105]. Mechanistically, circular RNA protein disulfide isomerase family A member 3 (circPDIA3)/miR‐449a/X‐box binding protein 1 (XBP1) positive feedback loop increases the expression of circPDIA3 and promotes oxaliplatin resistance by restraining pyroptosis through inhibition of palmitoylation of the GSDME‐C domain, thereby amplifying its autoinhibitory effect in CRC [106].

Regarding radiotherapy, GSDME determines CRC radiosensitivity and radiation‐induced toxicity in surrounding normal tissues through caspase‐3‐dependent pyroptosis [107]. Moreover, the long noncoding RNA nuclear paraspeckle assembly transcript 1 (LncRNA NEAT1) sponges miR‐448 to regulate GSDME expression and induces ionizing radiation‐induced pyroptosis and cell viability loss in human CRC cells [108].

Role of Inflammasomes in the Inflammatory Response of CRC Tumorigenesis

Evasion of PCD is a fundamental oncogenic characteristic in multiple malignancies. Within the spectrum of regulated cell death modalities (apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis), apoptosis typically maintains immunological silencing. This inherently non‐inflammatory nature of apoptosis underlies its role in therapeutic resistance when dysregulated in cancer, despite its extensive exploitation in antitumor treatment strategies [109, 110].

Conversely, pyroptosis is a lytic form of cell death characterized by pronounced proinflammatory signaling. Chronic inflammation is a well‐established tumorigenic driver that promotes genomic instability and disease progression—particularly by sustaining pro‐tumorigenic microenvironments that facilitate neovascularization and metastatic dissemination. However, therapeutic induction of acute pyroptosis in malignant cells produces paradoxical effects [111, 112]. Exogenous pyroptosis triggers intense inflammatory cascades within the tumor niche, generating potent antitumor immunity [113]. This mechanistic complexity positions pyroptosis modulation as a double‐edged sword in oncological pathophysiology and interventions.

NLRP3 and CRC

Among inflammasome complexes, the NLRP3 inflammasome is the most extensively investigated and best‐characterized subtype. NLRP3 demonstrates broad‐spectrum pattern recognition capability, enabling detection of diverse DAMPs and pathogen‐associated molecular patterns, which can lead to dysregulated activation under pathological conditions [114]. Studies have shown that mice deficient in NLRP3 are highly susceptible to dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)‐induced colitis due to markedly reduced IL‐18 levels leading to impaired production of the tumor suppressor interferon gamma (IFN‐γ) and defective activation of STAT1 [115]. The resulting defect in inflammasome activation compromises epithelial integrity, leading to the systemic dissemination of commensal bacteria, pronounced leukocyte infiltration, and elevated chemokine production in the colon [116, 117]. NLRP3 inflammasome activation contributes to tumorigenesis through multifaceted mechanisms, including modulation of innate and adaptive immune responses, regulation of PCD pathways (apoptosis and pyroptosis), influence on cellular differentiation, and bidirectional interactions with the gut microbiota [118]. NLRP3 expression is significantly elevated in CRC tissues compared with paired adjacent non‐tumorous tissues, and high NLRP3 expression correlates with advanced stage and reduced survival in patients with CRC [119]. Mechanistically, NLRP3 drives CRC progression by modulating the EMT and ribosomal protein S6 kinase 1‐ glioma‐associated oncogene homolog 1 (GLI1) signaling cascade, establishing this inflammasome as a promising therapeutic target [120, 121].

Azoxymethane/dextran sulfate sodium (AOM/DSS), a classic experimental proinflammatory agent, is widely used to establish animal models of IBD and CAC by activating the nuclear factor‐kappa B (NF‐κB) pathway and NLRP3 inflammasome [122]. In the AOM/DSS‐induced murine model of CAC, a 5‐hydroxytryptamine‐NLRP3 positive feedback loop was identified that sustains proinflammatory signaling within the TME, thereby driving oncogenic progression through inflammation‐dependent mechanisms [123]. Mechanistic studies have revealed that autophagy preserves the intestinal mucosal barrier, regulates inflammation, and reduces IBD progression to CRC. In AOM/DSS‐induced mouse models, autophagy exerts multimodal tumor‐suppressive effects through gut microbiota modulation that prevents pathogenic bacterial translocation, suppression of NLRP3‐driven pyroptosis and NF‐κB‐mediated proinflammatory signaling, and induction of CRC cell death. These findings suggest that enhancing autophagy may represent a novel strategy for treating IBD‐related CAC [124].

AIM2 and CRC

The AIM2 inflammasome is a critical regulatory component that suppresses CRC progression. AIM2 exhibits decreased expression across the majority of CRC cellular models. Comparative tissue analysis demonstrated markedly reduced expression of AIM2 in primary CRC lesions relative to that in matched paracancerous normal mucosa. This molecular downregulation correlates with adverse clinical outcomes, as evidenced by the association between diminished AIM2 expression and significantly poorer overall survival in patients with CRC [125]. Research indicates that in the AOM/DSS‐induced colorectal cancer model, AIM2‐deficient mice exhibited a significantly higher tumor load compared to their ASC‐deficient counterparts [126]. Mechanistically, AIM2 inhibits cellular proliferation, EMT, and migratory capacity by concurrently suppressing AKT and GLI1 signaling molecules while activating the P38 mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway [127, 128, 129]. Coordinated modulation of these intracellular cascades ultimately mediates the suppression of oncogenic processes. Attenuation of AIM2 signaling compromises intrinsic tumor‐suppressive functions in CRC.

The B‐raf proto‐oncogene, serine/threonine kinase (BRAF) V600E mutation occurs in 8%–12% of metastatic CRC cases and represents a distinct molecular subtype clinically associated with aggressive biological behavior. This alteration is characterized by two hallmark features: intrinsic refractoriness to standard chemotherapeutic agents and markedly adverse prognostic implications [130]. AIM2 expression is significantly downregulated in BRAF‐mutant CRC tissues compared with that in wild‐type counterparts. Exogenous reconstitution of AIM2 in BRAF‐mutant CRC cells profoundly suppresses neoplastic proliferation via caspase‐1‐dependent induction of programmed necroptosis. Furthermore, AIM2 demonstrates potent antitumor efficacy in patient‐derived organoids, effectively attenuating both primary tumor growth and metastatic dissemination [131]. Collectively, AIM2 constitutes a valuable biomarker and potential therapeutic target for BRAF‐mutant CRC.

Other Inflammasomes and CRC

Similar to AIM2, activation of other inflammasomes, such as NLRC4 and Nod‐like receptor family apoptosis inhibitory proteins (NAIPs), generally mediates protective responses in CAC [132]. Bioinformatic analysis has indicated reduced NLRC4 expression in both CRC tissues and cell lines. Although a study using a subcutaneous B16F10 melanoma model in Nlrc4 −/− mice on a C57BL/6J background found that Nlrc4 deficiency did not significantly affect tumor growth [133], clinically, decreased NLRC4 expression is significantly associated with lymph node metastasis in patients with CRC. A robust positive correlation was observed between NLRC4 and caspase‐1 expression in CRC specimens. Notably, concurrent downregulation of NLRC4 and caspase‐1 correlates with unfavorable survival outcomes and is associated with altered immune cell infiltration profiles in CRC cohorts [134]. Furthermore, in CRC with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, NLRC4 facilitates M2 polarization of TAMs. This mechanism promotes TAM secretion of IL‐1β and vascular endothelial growth factor, thereby establishing a tumor‐promoting microenvironment conducive to metastatic hepatic tumor growth during CRC progression [135].

NAIPs assemble into inflammasome complexes with NLRC4. NAIPs exert protective effects against tumor initiation in the colon by facilitating clearance of carcinogen‐damaged epithelial cells through mechanisms likely independent of the NLRC4 inflammasome pathway [136]. However, one study identified two differentially expressed miRNAs (miR‐145 and miR‐1) in patients with colon cancer, both of which target NAIP genes. These miRNAs exhibit tumor‐suppressive properties by directly binding NAIP transcripts, thereby negatively regulating CRC cell proliferation [137]. Therefore, the precise mechanisms and potential roles of NAIPs in CRC warrant further investigation to advance understanding of their pathophysiological significance.

Pyroptosis‐Induced Antitumor Immunity in CRC

The intrinsic resistance of tumor cells to apoptosis, combined with the immunologically silent nature of apoptotic cell death, underscores the therapeutic potential of pyroptosis induction as an innovative oncological strategy [138, 139]. GSDMB expression in CRC cells is associated with myeloid‐derived inflammatory cells (including S100A8+ immune cells and CD68+ macrophages) rather than lymphocytes within the tumor immune microenvironment [83]. Further investigation revealed that tumor cell pyroptosis and subsequent release of HMGB1 upregulated CXCL2 expression in CRC cells. The resulting CXCL2/C‐X‐C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CXCR2) signaling axis facilitates recruitment of myeloid‐derived suppressor cells to the TME, thereby establishing a pro‐tumorigenic niche [85]. Mechanical disruption of tumor cells during pyroptosis liberates tumor‐associated antigens along with DAMPs, generating dual signals that stimulate dendritic cell maturation and subsequent T‐cell priming. Concurrently, pyroptosis‐driven cytokine secretion, particularly IL‐1β and IL‐18, establishes a chemotactic gradient that promotes infiltration and activation of cytotoxic lymphocytes (CTLs) and NK cells (Figure 4) [140]. The immunogenic nature of pyroptosis is further amplified through the generation of tumor‐specific neoantigens during cellular disintegration. These neoantigens sustain immune activation by enhancing antigen‐presenting cell recruitment and epitope presentation, thereby reinforcing antitumor immunity [141]. Additionally, pyroptosis strengthens antitumor immunity by eliminating immunosuppressive components and releasing immunostimulatory mediators (Figure 5).

Therapeutic regimen‐induced CRC cells pyroptosis potentiates antitumor immunity. Multiple cancer therapies, including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and natural products, induce pyroptosis in CRC cells. Pyroptotic tumor cells release immunostimulatory damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), tumor‐associated antigens (TAAs), and proinflammatory cytokines, collectively enhancing the recruitment, phagocytic capacity, and maturation of antigen‐presenting cells (APCs). APCs process TAAs and present them via major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC II) to activate naïve CD8+ T cells, driving their differentiation into tumor antigen‐specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs). Concurrently, pyroptosis‐generated inflammatory signals within the tumor microenvironment recruit CTLs and natural killer (NK) cells. These effector cells eliminate gasdermin‐expressing tumor cells through granzyme A/B (GzmA/B)‐mediated cleavage of gasdermins, thereby perpetuating pyroptosis‐driven immunostimulation and establishing a feed‐forward loop for sustained antitumor responses.

Immunomodulatory dynamics and chronic inflammation in the CRC tumor microenvironment. The activation of NLRP3 inflammasome in intestinal epithelial cells by damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) drives chronic intestinal inflammation. This persistent inflammatory state induces gut microbiota dysbiosis, characterized by diminished abundance of beneficial commensals including Akkermansia muciniphila and Lactobacillus plantarum OC01, alongside pathogenic Escherichia coli overrepresentation. These alterations collectively establish a tumor‐permissive immune microenvironment through sustained cytokine dysregulation and epithelial barrier compromise, ultimately progressing to colorectal carcinogenesis through cumulative genomic instability. The CRC immune landscape comprises stromal cells, malignant epithelial cells, T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and macrophages. Pyroptotic immune cells induced by NLRP3 inflammasome, particularly macrophages, release interleukin (IL)‐18 and IL‐1β, which exert opposing roles in tumor progression. IL‐18 suppresses CRC growth by enhancing macrophage activity, boosting CD8+ T cell and NK cell cytotoxicity. Conversely, IL‐1β drives protumorigenic processes, including recruitment of myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), oncogenesis, metastasis, resistance to chemotherapy, and tumor progression. Concurrently, gasdermin B (GSDMB) and gasdermin E (GSDME) in CRC cells undergo caspase‐independent proteolytic activation mediated by tumor‐infiltrating cytotoxic lymphocytes. This pyroptotic pathway is selectively triggered through granzyme‐specific cleavage mechanisms: GSDMB undergoes processing by T cell‐derived GzmA, whereas NK cell‐produced granzyme B preferentially cleaves GSDME. The resulting granzyme‐induced membrane perforation potentiates immunogenic cell death, thereby bridging innate and adaptive antitumor immunity through microenvironmental crosstalk.

Pyroptosis of Cancer Cells in Antitumor Immunity

The efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors in CRC remains constrained by suboptimal tumor neoantigen levels and limited immune infiltration in most microsatellite‐stable (MSS) malignancies [142]. Pyroptosis, an immunogenic form of PCD, has the potential to enhance tumor immunogenicity and remodel the immunosuppressive TME, thereby potentiating immunotherapy responses [143]. Current research indicates that pyroptosis induction in CRC cells elicits potent antitumor immune activation (Figure 4).

The immune microenvironment is closely linked to tumorigenesis. GSDME is associated with the CRC immune microenvironment. Pyroptosis of CRC cells remodels this microenvironment by augmenting CD8+ T‐cell infiltration and cytotoxic activity while mitigating T‐cell exhaustion. On the one hand, GSDME enhances CD8+ T‐cell‐mediated antitumor immunity by damaging mitochondria and activating the cyclic guanosine monophosphate‐adenosine monophosphate synthase (cGAS)–stimulator of interferon genes (STING)–interferon‐β axis in CRC [144]. On the other hand, GSDME‐driven pyroptosis is triggered by TNF—largely produced by targeting hepcidin in CRC cells—which boosts robust immune responses against tumor antigens [145]. In addition, a direct GSDMD agonist that induces low‐level pyroptosis specifically in tumor cells converts immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” ones to prime antitumor immunity without systemic toxicity. By sparing immune cells and synergizing with anti‐programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1), this non‐canonical GSDMD activation strategy exploits controlled pyroptosis to provoke protective immunity, positioning GSDMD as a druggable target for cancer immunotherapy [146].

One study revealed that enhanced chromosome missegregation facilitates the generation of micronuclei and cytosolic release of dsDNA, thereby activating the AIM2‐mediated dsDNA‐sensing pathway. Yin Yang 2 overexpression‐induced chromosome missegregation and cytosolic dsDNA signaling trigger tumor cell pyroptosis and enhance antitumor immunotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors in both microsatellite‐instable and MSS CRC models [147]. Another study found that interleukin‐17A (IL‐17A), a cytokine predominantly secreted by γδ T cells within the CRC immune microenvironment, acts as a pivotal modulator of TME dynamics. Mechanistically, IL‐17A induces mitochondrial dysfunction and pyroptosis via the ROS/NLRP3/caspase‐4/GSDMD signaling axis, concomitantly facilitating intracellular ROS accumulation. Furthermore, IL‐17A promotes the secretion of proinflammatory mediators, including IL‐1β and IL‐18, alongside immunogenic antigens, while simultaneously enhancing CD8+ T‐cell infiltration to potentiate antitumor immunity [148].

Photodynamic therapy using IR700DX‐6T, a mitochondria‐targeted photosensitizer, has demonstrated potential for pyroptosis‐mediated immunostimulation in CRC. Mechanistic analyses revealed that light‐activated IR700DX‐6T generated ROS, which subsequently activated p38 MAPK phosphorylation and caspase‐3‐dependent proteolytic cleavage of GSDME to induce pyroptotic cell death. Notably, this modality sensitizes MSS CRC cells to PD‐1 immune checkpoint inhibition. Moreover, GSDME hypermethylation synergistically enhances therapeutic efficacy when combined with IR700DX‐6T‐mediated photodynamic therapy and anti‐PD‐1 immunotherapy [149].

Additionally, radiation‐induced GSDME‐mediated pyroptosis in CRC cells enhances antitumor immunity through NK‐cell recruitment and activation [107]. Furthermore, the pyroptosis‐associated regulator IKAROS family zinc finger 1 has been identified as a tumor suppressor that induces pyroptosis, promotes M1 macrophage polarization, and attenuates CRC progression by inactivating the janus kinase 2 (JAK2)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (STAT5) signaling axis [150]. Collectively, these findings establish pyroptosis as an immunogenic cell death modality that synergizes with immune checkpoint inhibitors to amplify antitumor responses.

Pyroptosis of Immune Cells in Antitumor Immunity

The chronic inflammatory milieu within the TME is intrinsically associated with multistep CRC carcinogenesis. Both recruited immune infiltrates and tissue‐resident immune populations critically shape the inflammatory landscape surrounding CRC cells [151]. In CRC, the dual role of immune cell pyroptosis in tumorigenesis and disease progression appears to be mediated primarily through inflammasome‐dependent signaling cascades, with pyroptotic cytokine release triggered by DAMPs and pathogen‐associated molecular patterns within the TME (Figure 5).

Emerging experimental evidence demonstrates that the NLRP3 inflammasome serves as a crucial mechanistic link between intestinal inflammatory processes and colorectal carcinogenesis in mice. Intestinal mucosal‐derived DAMPs, particularly S100A8/S100A9 heterodimers, have been identified as potent NLRP3 activators that promote both intestinal inflammatory pathogenesis and subsequent neoplastic transformation of the colonic epithelium [33]. Additionally, NLRP3 inflammasome activation in macrophages contributes to CRC cell migration and metastasis [152]. Novel recombinant oncolytic adenoviruses have been engineered to activate the NLRP3 signaling axis, promote M1‐polarized reprogramming of TAMs, augment tumor‐infiltrating effector memory/effector CD8+ T‐cell populations, and amplify antitumor responses in CRC [153].

Increasing evidence highlights the gut microbiota–immune axis as a pivotal contributor to CRC pathogenesis via the NLRP3 inflammasome. Notably, patients with CRC exhibit markedly diminished levels of Akkermansia muciniphila . Therapeutic supplementation with A. muciniphila exerts potent antitumor effects across multiple models, suppressing intestinal tumorigenesis in ApcMin/+ mice and impeding xenograft growth of HCT116/CT26 cell lines in immunodeficient hosts. Mechanistic investigations reveal that A. muciniphila preferentially enhances M1‐like macrophage polarization through NLRP3 inflammasome activation, as evidenced in both in vivo and in vitro models [154]. Another study showed that Lactobacillus plantarum OC01 administration downregulates TGF‐β expression in CRC cells, thereby attenuating M2 macrophage polarization [155]. A separate study demonstrated that Bacteroides fragilis reduces intestinal inflammation and restricts CAC development by negatively regulating the NLRP3 inflammasome and inhibiting macrophage activity [156]. Conversely, pathogenic enrichment of tumor‐resident bacteria, such as Escherichia coli , correlates with CRC progression. The carcinogenic effects of E. coli involve metabolic reprogramming through enhanced lactate production, which drives lactylation of retinoic acid‐inducible gene 1. This posttranslational modification disrupts NF‐κB binding to the NLRP3 promoter, effectively silencing inflammasome transcription. Consequently, this immunosuppressive cascade promotes regulatory T‐cell activity while impairing CD8+ T‐cell‐mediated tumor surveillance, thereby establishing a permissive microenvironment for hepatic metastasis [157].

IL‐1β is increasingly recognized as the principal downstream effector of NLRP3 inflammasome activation, serving as a critical mediator of tumor‐neutrophil crosstalk in colorectal liver metastases. Clinical analyses reveal that IL‐1β is significantly associated with immunosuppressive microenvironment formation and poor prognosis in patients with colorectal liver metastases. Mechanistic investigations demonstrate that TAM‐derived extracellular vesicles containing fibrinogen‐like protein 2 engage FcγRIIb receptors on malignant cells, initiating a phosphorylated signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (p‐STAT3)/IL‐1β positive feedback loop that amplifies cancer stemness. Moreover, therapeutic intervention with IL‐1β‐neutralizing monoclonal antibodies reduces neutrophil extracellular trap formation and potentiates anti‐PD‐1 immunotherapy responses in CRC [158].

Within the CRC TME, TAMs constitute the predominant cellular source of IL‐1β secretion. TAM depletion significantly attenuates AOM/DSS‐induced colorectal carcinogenesis. Mechanistically, TAMs potentiate colitis‐associated tumorigenesis through NLRP3 inflammasome activation, driving caspase‐1/11‐dependent IL‐1β production and release, thereby sustaining pro‐tumorigenic signaling [159, 160]. However, another study showed that IL‐1β exerts concentration‐dependent antitumor effects, with dose‐responsive escalation of therapeutic efficacy against malignant growth. Notably, cytolysin A, a gram‐negative bacterial pore‐forming toxin, potentiates LPS‐induced IL‐1β secretion in human macrophages through NLRP3–ASC–caspase‐1 signaling, subsequently exerting significant antiproliferative effects on CRC cells [161]. Additionally, systemic or myeloid‐specific STING deficiency exacerbates hepatic metastatic progression in CRC models, correlating with compromised NK‐cell‐mediated immunosurveillance, as evidenced by reduced intrahepatic infiltration and impaired cytolytic activity. Mechanistically, STING activation orchestrates NLRP3 inflammasome‐dependent IL‐1β/IL‐18 secretion in macrophages, thereby potentiating NK‐cell‐mediated tumor clearance through a cytokine‐regulated feed‐forward mechanism. Specifically, macrophage‐derived IL‐1β/IL‐18 upregulate CD137L expression on macrophages and promote CD137 expression on NK cells, establishing a coordinated co‐stimulatory axis critical for antitumor immunity [162]. Although the primary cellular source of IL‐1β is TAMs, CRC cells can also release IL‐1β upon induction of pyroptosis. Two recent studies have provided direct evidence that pyroptotic CRC cells secrete both IL‐1β and IL‐18. Mechanistically, IL‐17A induces pyroptosis in CRC cells through the ROS/NLRP3/caspase‐4/GSDMD axis, leading to IL‐1β release [148], while FAM72A knockdown promotes pyroptosis via ROS‐mediated activation of the NLRP3/caspase‐1/GSDMD pathway, which is similarly accompanied by increased IL‐1β secretion [163].

Other inflammasomes have also been reported to promote tumor progression and radiotherapy‐related intestinal mucositis. NLRC4, a component of the inflammasome complex, mediates M2 TAM polarization and processing of IL‐1β and VEGF, thereby contributing to metastatic liver tumor growth in nonalcoholic fatty liver disease but not in normal livers in CRC [135]. In addition, ionizing radiation potentiates IL‐1β secretion in LPS‐primed macrophages through p38 MAPK/NLRC4‐mediated caspase‐1 activation, thereby exacerbating CRC cell malignancy [164]. Furthermore, radiation‐induced extracellular self‐dsDNA from intestinal epithelial cells acts as an immunogenic stimulus, inducing HMGB1/RAGE‐mediated macrophage internalization. This process activates the AIM2 inflammasome pathway, provoking IL‐1β and IL‐18 release, and ultimately drives the pathological cascade underlying intestinal mucositis development [165].

Although inflammasome‐mediated pyroptosis in immune cells has been relatively well characterized, its functional implications in CRC antitumor immunity remain unclear. This mechanistic ambiguity may stem from the dualistic nature of pyroptotic immune responses in CRC pathogenesis, likely attributable to variables, such as tumor‐stage‐dependent effects, spatial heterogeneity of anatomical localization, contextual cytokine dynamics (notably IL‐1β/IL‐18 gradients), and evolving tumor microenvironmental landscapes (Figure 6).

The immunological consequences of pyroptosis exhibit biphasic characteristics dependent on activation intensity. During this lytic cell death process, pyroptotic cells discharge bioactive molecules including pro‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐1β, IL‐18) and damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that serve as alarmins to modulate immune surveillance. Chronic low‐grade pyroptosis in malignant cells establishes a tumor‐promotive niche through sustained subclinical inflammation and microenvironmental conditioning that facilitates neoplastic proliferation and metastatic dissemination. Conversely, therapeutic induction of extensive pyroptotic cascades initiates profound immunostimulatory effects. The liberation of intracellular contents in this context drives robust leukocyte infiltration and activates coordinated antitumor immunity through antigen‐presenting cell maturation and cytotoxic lymphocyte priming, ultimately culminating in tumor regression through immunogenic clearance mechanisms.

Notably, temporally distinct pyroptotic phenotypes—including acutely induced versus chronically sustained cell death—exert diametrically opposing biological effects on oncogenesis. The current consensus suggests that strategically engineered pyroptotic surges demonstrate tumor‐suppressive efficacy, particularly when targeting critical cellular subpopulations through controlled inflammasome activation. Conversely, constitutively activated, low‐grade pyroptotic signaling within neoplastic ecosystems paradoxically fuels tumorigenic progression through perpetual inflammatory priming [166, 167].

Pyroptosis‐Inducing Agents in CRC Therapy

Pyroptosis, a recently characterized form of regulated cell death, received limited scientific attention in oncology until the seminal discovery of gasdermins as its molecular executioners. Contemporary research has revealed that diverse anticancer agents or strategies, ranging from conventional chemotherapeutics to molecularly targeted compounds, mediate therapeutic effects by inducing pyroptosis in malignant cells (Table S2) [168, 169].

Current chemotherapeutic agents for CRC, including 5‐FU, oxaliplatin, and irinotecan, show limited evidence of directly triggering pyroptotic cell death. Notably, pharmacological repositioning studies have identified unexpected pyroptosis‐inducing capabilities of commercially available drugs originally developed for noncancer indications when applied to CRC models. The lipid‐lowering agent simvastatin demonstrates anticancer properties in CRC, mechanistically inducing cancer cell pyroptosis via ROS‐mediated caspase‐1/GSDMD pathway activation, while concurrently inhibiting tumor proliferation and progression in both preclinical and experimental settings [95]. When administered in combination, the essential water‐soluble micronutrients ascorbic acid (vitamin C) and arsenic trioxide synergistically induce CRC cell pyroptosis via enhanced caspase‐1 expression and inflammasome assembly [170].

In addition, various small‐molecule inhibitors induce extensive CRC cell pyroptosis. Taletrectinib (DS‐6051b/AB‐106), a novel synthetic dual c‐ros oncogene 1 (ROS1)/neurotrophic tropomyosin receptor kinase (NTRK) inhibitor, induces cytotoxicity in CRC cells through caspase‐3/GSDME‐mediated pyroptosis. PCD is achieved by modulating the SRC/AKT/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling axis [171]. Another study reported that histone deacetylase 2 (HDAC2)‐mediated histone deacetylation inhibits formation of the H3K27ac/bromodomain‐containing protein 4 (BRD4)/p‐p65 complex, leading to epigenetic silencing of NLRP3. The HDAC2 inhibitor santacruzamate A, in combination with classic antitumor agents (5‐FU or regorafenib), markedly activates pyroptosis and achieves significant therapeutic effects in CRC xenograft‐bearing animals [172]. Another study showed that the farnesoid X receptor (FXR) agonist GW4064 potentiates the oxaliplatin response in CRC through pharmacological activation of the BAX/caspase‐3/GSDME axis, executing pyroptotic cell death [173].

The strategic convergence of nanotherapeutics and molecularly targeted agents achieves synergistic antitumor efficacy while maintaining therapeutic safety through precise delivery mechanisms. The hybrid liposomal formulation HV@Btz was engineered through bortezomib encapsulation to demonstrate enhanced cellular uptake and potent cytotoxicity in CRC cells via activating caspase‐3/GSDME pathway and inducing pyroptosis. In preclinical models, HV@Btz exhibited significant tumor growth suppression in both subcutaneous and orthotopic CRC‐bearing murine systems while eliciting a robust antitumor immune response [174]. Notably, therapeutic efficacy was achieved with minimal systemic toxicity. A subsequent study engineered the protein‐based nanotherapy T22‐PE24‐H6 by integrating Pseudomonas aeruginosa exotoxin A for C‐X‐C motif chemokine receptor 4+ (CXCR4+) CRC cell‐specific targeting. This agent demonstrated preclinical efficacy in both subcutaneous and orthotopic CRC models by activating GSDMD‐mediated pyroptotic pathways while maintaining a favorable systemic safety profile [96].

In addition to conventional chemotherapeutic agents and molecularly targeted inhibitors, recent studies have identified distinct natural products with pyroptosis‐inducing potential in CRC models (Figure S1). Ginsenosides Rh3, quercetin, secoisolariciresinol diglucoside, luteolin, and OSW‐1 directly activate pyroptosis in CRC through gasdermin‐mediated pathways. Notably, structurally diverse botanical derivatives, including 2′, 4′‐dihydroxychalcone, honokiol‐magnolol‐baicalin complexes, gambogic acid, and tubeimoside I, exhibit dual‐modality effects by simultaneously triggering tumor cell pyroptosis and potentiating antitumor immunity. Ginsenoside Rh3 demonstrated potent antitumor activity against CRC through pyroptosis induction mediated by the NLRP3/caspase‐1/GSDMD pathway, with mechanistic regulation via the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3)/p53/NRF2 signaling axis in both in vitro systems and nude mouse xenograft models [97]. Similarly, the flavonoid quercetin triggers pyroptosis in CRC through never in mitosis A‐related kinase 7 (NEK7)‐dependent potentiation of the NLRP3 inflammasome‐GSDMD pathway, effectively suppressing neoplastic proliferation [98]. Another flavonoid derivative, luteolin, demonstrated a specific pyroptosis‐inducing capability through selective activation of the canonical caspase‐1/GSDMD signaling pathway [100]. Interestingly, secoisolariciresinol diglucoside initiates a dual cytotoxic mechanism involving caspase‐1/GSDMD‐mediated pyroptosis coupled with mitochondrial apoptosis via ROS‐dependent phosphoinositide 3‐kinase (PI3K)/AKT/B‐cell lymphoma 2 antagonist/killer 1 (BAK) signaling in CRC cells [99]. Notably, the botanical extract OSW‐1 from Ornithogalum saundersiae bulbs completed this mechanistic spectrum by triggering pyroptotic cell death through redox‐sensitive modulation of the ROS/NLRP3/caspase‐1 axis in CRC models [175].

Phytochemical modulation of pyroptosis and tumor immunity in CRC has also been demonstrated. The inhibitory effects of 2′, 4′‐dihydroxychalcone on CRC progression appear to be mediated through pyroptosis induction via NF‐κB/NLRP3 inflammasome suppression combined with immunological reprogramming. This dual action simultaneously enhances cytotoxic CD8+ T cell infiltration and function while reducing immunosuppressive CD11b+CD206+ macrophage populations within the TME [176]. Notably, the Huangqin Houpo decoction‐derived honokiol‐magnolol‐baicalin complex exhibited efficacy comparable to that of FOLFIRI chemotherapy. Mechanistic investigations revealed synergistic anticancer properties through GSDME‐dependent pyroptotic activation, which potentiated anti‐PD‐1 immunotherapy responses in CRC models [177]. Structural characterization identified gambogic acid, a xanthone compound isolated from gamboge resin, as a caspase‐3/GSDME pathway activator that induces tumor cell pyroptosis while favorably reshaping the immunological landscape [178]. Gambogic acid significantly increased the number of tumor‐infiltrating CD3+ T cells, CTLs, dendritic cells (DC), and CD8+CD44+CD62L effector memory T cells. Experimental analysis demonstrated that tubeimoside I inhibits pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) and subsequently promotes caspase‐3 activation and GSDME cleavage, thereby driving pyroptosis in murine CRC cells. In vivo studies showed marked tumor mass reduction accompanied by CD8+ T cell expansion and splenic M2 macrophage depletion. Further mechanistic studies revealed that tubeimoside I blocks signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6)‐mediated M2 polarization in RAW 264.7 cells while promoting proinflammatory cytokine release through pyroptotic pathway activation [179]. Collectively, these findings establish a robust foundation for the development of pyroptosis‐targeted therapeutics for CRC.

Conclusions and Perspectives

Pyroptosis, a gasdermin‐mediated lytic cell death mechanism, has emerged as a critical factor in CRC biology, bridging inflammation, tumor progression, and therapeutic responses. While chronic pyroptosis‐driven inflammation may fuel CRC development by fostering genomic instability and immunosuppressive microenvironments, the targeted induction of acute pyroptosis in tumor cells demonstrates potent antitumor effects (Figure 6). This duality underscores the context‐dependent roles of pyroptosis in CRC, which are influenced by tumor stage, gasdermin isoform expression, and microenvironmental cues [85, 104].

The gasdermin family, particularly GSDMD and GSDME, governs pyroptotic outcomes in patients with CRC. GSDMD activation via NLRP3 inflammasome signaling or chemotherapeutic agents, such as simvastatin, induces tumor cell death while modulating immune infiltration [95, 172]. Conversely, GSDMC promotes CRC progression under metabolic stress by recruiting immunosuppressive myeloid‐derived suppressor cells [85]. Although frequently silenced in CRC, GSDME enhances chemoradiation sensitivity and synergizes with immune checkpoint inhibitors by releasing immunostimulatory DAMPs [149]. These findings highlight gasdermins as promising therapeutic targets; however, their isoform‐specific roles and regulatory mechanisms require further elucidation.

Inflammasomes, notably NLRP3 and AIM2, shape CRC pathogenesis through pyroptosis and cytokine secretion. NLRP3 activation exacerbates tumor growth via IL‐1β/IL‐18‐driven inflammation and EMT [121], while AIM2 exerts tumor‐suppressive effects, particularly in BRAF‐mutant CRC [131]. The gut microbiota further modulates inflammasome activity, with pathogenic strains (including Fusobacterium nucleatum ) suppressing pyroptosis to promote chemoresistance, whereas commensals, such as Akkermansia muciniphila , enhance antitumor immunity via NLRP3‐mediated M1 macrophage polarization [79, 157].

Therapeutically, pyroptosis induction synergizes with conventional therapies and immunotherapies. Targeted drugs, natural compounds, and nanotherapeutics trigger gasdermin‐dependent pyroptosis, overcoming apoptosis resistance and resensitizing tumors [96, 97, 173]. Pyroptotic cell death releases tumor antigens and DAMPs, recruiting CTLs and NK cells, and thereby remodeling the immunosuppressive microenvironment [162]. Clinical studies highlight GSDMB and GSDME expression as predictive biomarkers for 5‐FU and anti‐PD‐1 responses [83, 149], emphasizing their translational potential.

Current preclinical research on pyroptosis in CRC faces limitations, including physiological disparities in model systems (such as cell lines, animal models, organoids, and patient‐derived xenografts, which fail to fully replicate human TMEs and immune interactions), the mechanistic complexity of pyroptosis (its cross‐talk with other pathways and dual roles in promoting inflammation and antitumor activity), and a lack of specific biomarkers. Moreover, clinical translation must address safety risks (including cytokine storms and off‐target toxicity), variability in efficacy because of tumor heterogeneity, frequent drug resistance, complexities in combination therapy regimens, and challenges in drug delivery efficiency. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop more precise models, dynamic monitoring tools, and therapeutic strategies tailored to the TME.

Future studies should focus on three core directions to address the current challenges in CRC pyroptosis research. First, to construct a clinical trial system, it is essential to integrate the complementary strengths of organoids and patient‐derived xenograft models, optimize adaptive trial designs through artificial intelligence‐driven virtual clinical trial platforms, and develop liquid biopsy‐based pyroptosis monitoring technologies to bridge the gap between model systems and authentic human TMEs. Second, biomarker research must overcome the limitations of single‐dimensional detection by leveraging spatial multiomics technologies to construct TME‐specific pyroptosis molecular maps. This should be combined with temporal correlation analyses of circulating tumor DNA methylation signatures and immune microenvironment activity markers to establish a composite biomarker system capable of predicting therapeutic responses and drug resistance risks. Third, in drug development, priority should be given to enhancing the tissue selectivity of pyroptosis inducers. This involves using computational chemistry to simulate the structure–activity relations of key pyroptosis pathway proteins (including the gasdermin family), designing tumor‐specific prodrug systems, and employing clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)‐Cas12a‐based real‐time monitoring to optimize combination therapies. Concurrent development of exosome–nanocarrier‐based targeted delivery systems will amplify localized pyroptosis effects in tumors while precisely regulating cytokine release thresholds. These integrated strategies may systematically address challenges, including therapeutic heterogeneity, off‐target toxicity, and recurrent drug resistance.

Pyroptosis is a double‐edged sword in CRC, offering both challenges and opportunities. Harnessing its antitumor potential while mitigating pro‐tumorigenic inflammation requires innovative strategies to advance CRC treatment into a new era of immune–metabolic precision medicine.
