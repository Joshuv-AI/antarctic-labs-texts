Tumor necrosis factor promotes doublecortin-like kinase 1 expression and cellular reprogramming in intestinal epithelial cells leading to neoplastic transformation
Authors: Lin Huang, Tianqi Li, Xu Huang, Xiaoxi Chen, Yuanyuan Ju, Lili Xu, Chunhua Ma, Yumei Tao, Yonghong Yang, Xiaoyun Bian, Mark M. Huycke, Xingmin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTumor necrosis factor (TNF) is a critical mediator in chronic inflammation and colorectal cancer (CRC) progression. However, whether and how prolonged TNF exposure triggers CRC is not fully understood.MethodsTNF-induced expression of Dclk1, a cancer stem cell (CSC) marker, was assessed in IEC-6 intestinal epithelial cells, murine models, and colon organoids. TNF-induced reprogramming of intestinal epithelial cells was investigated through single-cell RNA sequencing and cellular transformation assayed by xenograft tumor model.ResultsWe observed a significant increase in Dclk1 in the intestines of TNF-transgenic mice and TNF-treated IEC-6 cells. Notably, DCLK1 expression correlated positively with inflammation, TNF levels, and TNFRSF1a in human subjects. Silencing of Tnfrsf1a markedly reduced Dclk1 expression, underscoring the role of TNF-Tnfrsf1a axis in the regulation of Dclk1 expression. Furthermore, Dclk1-positive cells were significantly elevated in the intestines of TNF-treated wild-type mice, but not in Tnfrsf1a−/− mice, compared to controls. Inhibition of NFκB and c-Myc also led to a marked reduction in Dclk1 expression. Intriguingly, colonic organoids derived from TNF-transgenic mice exhibited increased numbers and sizes, whereas those from Tnfrsf1a−/− mice showed the opposite trend. Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that prolonged TNF exposure induced CSC emergence through the reprogramming of Dclk1-positive tuft cells and activation of CRC-related pathways. Critically, prolonged TNF treatment resulted in neoplastic transformation of intestinal epithelial cells, leading to xenograft tumor formation in immunodeficient mice.ConclusionsThese findings provide novel insights into how chronic inflammation drives the genesis of intestinal CSCs and fosters CRC development, highlighting potential therapeutic targets for combating colitis-associated colorectal cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02400-y.

Background

Chronic inflammation is a significant risk factor for the development of colorectal cancer (CRC), particularly in patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) such as Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis [1, 2]. Studies indicate that the incidence of colitis-associated colorectal cancer (CA-CRC) can be as high as 18% among IBD patients, although this rate has declined in recent years due to advances in therapy and improved CRC surveillance [1–3]. Despite these advancements, the precise mechanisms by which chronic inflammation promotes CRC remain poorly understood.

Tumor necrosis factor (TNF), a key inflammatory cytokine previously designated TNFα, plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of IBD and CA-CRC [4]. Two primary receptors, TNFRSF1A and TNFRSF1B, mediate TNF-induced inflammatory signaling. TNFRSF1A is predominantly linked to pro-inflammatory signaling, while TNFRSF1B, which is often activated by membrane-bound TNF, is essential for cell survival and tissue homeostasis [5, 6]. Previous research has shown that TNF contributes to CA-CRC primarily via Tnfrsf1a pathway [7]. Conversely, TNF produced by commensal-activated macrophages promotes CA-CRC by upregulating netrin-1, a key regulator of apoptosis, via the TNF-Tnfrsf1b pathway [8]. Furthermore, recent studies indicate that TNF facilitates cancer progression in breast and pancreas by enhancing cancer stemness [9, 10].

Cancer stem cells (CSCs) represent a small, long-lived population within tumors that play essential roles in tumorigenesis, heterogeneity, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance [11]. Early studies identified CSCs in CRC, particularly for tumors enriched for CD133-positive cells [12, 13]. However, the origins of these CSCs remain elusive. Lineage tracing studies suggest that CSCs may arise from transformed adult crypt stem cells, supporting a bottom-up model of CRC development [14, 15]. Conversely, there is accumulating evidence that CSCs can also emerge from differentiated cells through dedifferentiation and reprogramming during prolonged carcinogenesis [16, 17]. Inflammatory signaling has been shown to activate Wnt/β-catenin pathways, leading to cancer stem-like cells via the dedifferentiation of intestinal epithelial cells [18]. Moreover, recent findings indicate that doublecortin-like kinase 1 (Dclk1)-expressing tuft cells can serve as cancer-initiating cells in this context [19].

Dclk1 is a marker for tuft cells in the intestine and been recently identified as a marker for colorectal and pancreatic CSCs [20–22]. It promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and stemness and thus plays a crucial role in intestinal tumorigenesis [23, 24]. Elevated expression of DCLK1 has been linked to enhanced EMT and drug resistance in human CRC cells and correlates with a poor prognosis in CRC patients [23]. Additionally, high expression levels of DCLK1 can promote CRC aggressiveness by activating cyclooxygenase-2 expression and modulating inflammatory signaling within the tumor microenvironment [25]. Notably, Dclk1 is highly expressed in Enterococcus faecalis-induced CA-CRC models, where polarized macrophages produce mutagenic 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal and inflammatory cytokines such as TNF, leading to dedifferentiation and reprogramming of epithelial cells [26, 27]. DCLK1 is expressed as two distinct isoforms, either DCLK1-L or DCLK1-S, depending on the tissue types [28–30]. DCLK1-L, the longer isoform, is mainly expressed in normal intestines and regulated by β-catenin/TCF4 signaling. In contrast, the short isoform DCLK1-S is expressed in CRC and regulated by NFκB signaling [30]. Interestingly, a recent study shows that Dclk1 can promote inflammation by activating NFκB in macrophages [31]. However, how Dclk1 is regulated by inflammatory cytokines during chronic inflammation and carcinogenesis remains unclear.

In this study, we investigate the regulatory effects of TNF on Dclk1 expression and cellular plasticity. Our findings demonstrate that Dclk1 expression is significantly elevated in the intestines of human TNF-transgenic mice compared to wildtype controls. Furthermore, exposure of rat intestinal epithelial cells to TNF enhances Dclk1 expression through Tnfrsf1a signaling. TNF activates multiple CSC transcription factors, including NFκB and c-Myc, and inhibition of these factors blocks TNF-induced Dclk1 expression. Additionally, TNF facilitates colonic organoid growth and stemness, while deletion of Tnfrsf1a impedes these effects. Finally, prolonged TNF exposure induces dedifferentiation, reprogramming, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and neoplastic transformation in intestinal epithelial cells. These findings elucidate the mechanisms by which Dclk1 is regulated during chronic inflammation, providing insights into the origins of colorectal CSCs.

Methods

Cell line and treatments

The rat intestinal epithelial cell line IEC-6 [32] was obtained from the National Infrastructure of Cell Line Resource (Beijing, China). IEC-6 cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 10 µg/mL insulin (Sigma, Shanghai, China), penicillin G (100 units/mL), and streptomycin (100 µg/mL). For TNF treatment, IEC-6 cells (5 × 105) were incubated overnight in 6-well plates and subsequently treated with 100 ng/mL recombinant rat TNF (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C for 72 h. Inhibitors for NFκB (Bay-11-7082) and c-Myc (10074-G5) were purchased from Selleckchem (Shanghai, China). For long-term TNF treatment, IEC-6 cells were repeatedly exposed to 100 ng/mL recombinant rat TNF, or PBS as controls, for 72 h, resulting in a total of 10 treatment cycles. The cell lines derived from this long-term treatment were designated IEC-6TNF and IEC-6Ctrl, respectively.

Animal studies

Wildtype (WT) C57BL/6J mice and Tnfrsf1a+/− mice were obtained from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China). The Tnfrsf1a+/− mice were generated on a C57BL/6J background by deleting exons 2–5 of the Tnfrsf1a gene. Homozygous Tnfrsf1a−/− mice were produced through the inbreeding of Tnfrsf1a+/− mice and were used for experiments. Human TNF-transgenic mice were sourced from Cyagen (Suzhou, China) as a heterozygous strain [33]. All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility and were provided with regular chow ad libitum. Both sexes were used in the experiments.

To induce Dclk1 expression in vivo, 8-week-old C57BL/6J (n = 5) and Tnfrsf1a−/− (n = 5) mice were intraperitoneally injected with TNF (50 µg/kg body weight), or sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) as a control, once daily for 3 consecutive days. The mice were euthanized 24 h after the final TNF injection, and intestinal biopsies (middle portion of ileum and colon) were collected for subsequent experiments.

Xenograft tumor assay was conducted as previously reported with minor modifications [27]. In brief, 8-week-old male NOD/Scid mice (Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc., Shanghai, China) were divided into two groups: a control group with 3 mice injected with IEC-6Ctrl cells, and an experimental group with 5 mice injected with IEC-6TNF cells. Each mouse received two injections—one in each flank—containing 3.5 × 106 cells per site. Mice were necropsied after three months, and representative tumor tissues were collected and processed for subsequent staining. Additionally, DNA was extracted from fresh tumor samples, and tumor origin was confirmed by PCR amplification using primers specific to the rat Xist gene—located on the X chromosome and known to harbor several fragments missing in mice (Supplementary Table S2).

Crypt isolation and colonic organoid culture

Mouse colon crypt isolation and organoid culture were performed using the Mouse Colonic Organoid Kit (Kinlogix, Guangzhou, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, mice were euthanized, and colons excised. Colons were then cut longitudinally and sectioned into 3–5 mm pieces. Tissues were gently dissociated using the buffer supplied with the kit. The tissue fragments were digested in 20 mL of PBS containing 100 µL of the digestion solution on ice for 30 min with gentle shaking. Crypts were then separated by filtering the digestion solution through 70 μm cell sieves. The crypts were collected by centrifugation at 200 × g for 5 min and resuspended. The number of crypts was counted and then embedded in Matrigel (Corning, Shanghai, China) before being cultured in 24-well tissue culture plates with 500 µL of organoid culture medium (reduced growth factors) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. For TNF treatment, 100 ng/mL of TNF was added to the medium throughout the experimental period. The number and size of the organoids were assessed under an inverted microscope on the fifth day of treatment. Organoids were cultured in at least 4 wells per mouse, with one mouse per group, across 3 repeats.

Stem cell transcription factor array analysis

IEC-6 cells were treated with TNF (100 ng/mL) or PBS as a control for 48 h. Nuclear protein was then extracted using the NE-PERTM Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China). TNF-induced transcription factors were analyzed using the Stem Cell TF Activation Profiling Plate Array (Signosis, Santa Clara, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the nuclear extract was mixed with biotin-labeled probes and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Free probes were removed, and the bound probes were collected using isolation columns. The purified probes were denatured at 98 °C for 5 min and then chilled on ice. Hybridization with the labeled probes was performed in a plate coated with complementary oligonucleotides at 42 °C overnight. After washing and blocking, the captured probes were incubated with streptavidin-horseradish peroxidase conjugates, and luminescence signals were detected using a Varioskan LUX microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China).

Immunofluorescent (IF) staining

IF staining was performed as previously described [34]. IEC-6 cells cultured on tissue culture coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Following fixation, cells were blocked with 5% normal goat serum (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS at room temperature for 1 h. The cells were then incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table S1) at 4 °C overnight, followed by a 1-hour incubation with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature. The Dclk1 antibody used for IF staining detects both the Dclk1-S and Dclk1-L isoforms. Nuclei were counterstained using Hoechst 33342 (Beyotime) or DAPI and mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium (Sangon). Images were captured using laser scanning confocal microscopy (Nikon, Shanghai, China). The number of positive cells was enumerated, and the immunofluorescent intensity or positive area was measured using ImageJ.

For organoid staining, organoids were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature before being subjected to frozen sectioning. For paraffin-embedded tissue samples, tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Epitope retrieval was performed by boiling the tissue sections in 0.1 mM glycine buffer (pH 9.0) for 10 min. The remaining staining procedures were identical to those used for cell staining.

Fluorescent-activated cell sorting (FACS)

Cells were fixed in 4% formaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min and then permeabilized with methanol on ice for 10 min. The cells were incubated with the primary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. After washing with PBS containing 0.5% bovine serum albumin, the cells were incubated with a fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibody for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the cells were washed and analyzed using a DxFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Suzhou, China).

Western blotting

Whole cell lysates were separated on the sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, Shanghai, China). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) and incubated with the primary antibodies as listed in Supplementary Table S1. Membranes were washed with TBST three times and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody. Signals were generated using the enhanced chemiluminescence (Biosharp, Hefei, China) and captured by Odyssey Fc system (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). RNA was reversely transcribed to cDNA using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was carried out using AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme) with Gapdh gene as an internal control. Primers were listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Alcian blue staining

Frozen sections of organoids were stained with Alcian blue solution (pH 2.5) for 30 min at room temperature. Following the staining, the sections were rinsed with running tap water and subsequently washed with two changes of distilled water. The nuclei were then counterstained with Nuclear Fast Red Solution for 5 min, followed by a 2-minute rinse in running tap water and two additional changes of distilled water. Finally, the sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted for analysis.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) libraries were constructed using the 10K Perseus™ Single-Cell RNA Library Kit (10K Genomics, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of 1.3 × 105 single cells were suspended in PBS containing 0.01% bovine serum albumin and loaded onto the 10K Perseus™ system. After quality checks, individual libraries were diluted to 4 nM and pooled for sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000™, utilizing 150 bp paired-end reads. Following data normalization, differentially expressed genes were identified and analyzed using CellCosmo software version 1.0.2. Cells were subsequently clustered and annotated with SingleR version 2.4.1, and the resulting data were visualized using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). The differentiation trajectory was constructed through pseudotime analysis using Monocle 3.

Bioinformatics analysis

Gene expression correlation in human CRC was analyzed in 308 colon adenocarcinomas (COAD) and 94 rectum adenocarcinomas (READ) using the OncoDB online tool (https://oncodb.org) [35]. The survival curve was analyzed in 356 CRC patients using the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis 2 (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/). Correlation of DCLK1 expression with inflammation was analyzed in 620 samples using a user-friendly online interface (https://www.aclbi.com/static/index.html#/functional-analysis). All datasets were downloaded from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov), extracted in TPM format, and processed using R program. The Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient test was used for correlation analysis. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of scRNA-seq data was conducted using GSEA software 4.3.3 (BROAD Institute).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means with standard deviation (SD). Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between experimental and control groups, with P values < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All data were obtained from a minimum of 3 independent experiments and analyzed using the GraphPad Prism version 9.

Results

Dclk1 expression is upregulated in TNF humanized mice

We previously demonstrated that TNF, as a mediator of the microbiota-induced bystander effect (MIBE), upregulates the expression of CSC markers in mouse colonic epithelial cells [26, 27]. To investigate the role of TNF in the development of colitis-associated cancer, we examined intestinal Dclk1 expression in a human TNF-transgenic mouse model. Intestinal biopsies from 8-week-old TNF-transgenic mice housed in an SPF facility exhibited nearly normal morphology, although hyperplasia and decreases of goblet cells were observed in both the colon and small intestine (Fig. 1A). Quantitative RT-PCR revealed a significant increase in human TNF expression in both the colon and small intestine of TNF-transgenic mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 1B). IF staining for proliferating cell nuclear antigen (Pcna), a proliferation marker, showed an increase in Pcna-positive cells in biopsies of both the small intestine (Fig. 1C and D) and colon (Supplementary Figure S1A and B), indicating increased proliferation of intestinal epithelial cells in human TNF overexpressed mice. Additionally, qRT-PCR indicated significantly elevated Dclk1 expression in both the colon and small intestine of TNF-transgenic mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 1E). IF staining corroborated a significant increase in the number of Dclk1+ cells for small intestinal biopsies (Fig. 1F and G), although no such increase was observed in colon biopsies from TNF-transgenic mice (Supplementary Figure S1C and D). These findings indicate that overexpression of human TNF enhances proliferation and Dclk1 expression, leading to greater susceptibility to CA-CRC in mice.

TNF induces Dclk1 expression in intestinal epithelial cells

To explore the impact of DCLK1 on inflammation and CRC in human subjects, we analyzed the association of DCLK1 expression with inflammation and CRC using the TCGA database. We found that DCLK1 expression was positively correlated with inflammatory response signaling (Fig. 2A; r = 0.57, P < 0.0001) and TNF expression in human CRC (Supplementary Figure S2A and B). Additionally, higher DCLK1 expression was associated with decreased disease-free survival in CRC patients (Fig. 2B). To confirm these findings, we treated IEC-6 rat small intestinal epithelial cells with TNF and assessed Dclk1 expression. Quantitative RT-PCR revealed that Dclk1 was minimally expressed in untreated IEC-6 cells but significantly increased when cells were exposed to TNF for 24 to 72 h (Figs. 2C). Western blot analysis demonstrated predominant expression of the short Dclk1 isoform (Dclk1-S) and minimal expression of the long isoform (Dclk1-L) in untreated IEC-6 cells (Fig. 2D). Additionally, Dclk1-S levels were significantly upregulated in response to TNF treatment, while Dclk1-L levels exhibited only minor changes. Considering that DCLK1-S is the primary isoform expressed in human CRC and a marker of CSCs [30], we focused exclusively on Dclk1-S in this study. FACS analysis and immunofluorescent staining also revealed a significant increase in the number of Dclk1-positive cells in TNF-treated IEC-6 cells compared to untreated controls (Figs. 2E, F, and G). Notably, TNF-induced Dclk1 was blocked by anti-Tnfrsf1a antibody (Fig. 2G), suggesting that Dclk1 expression may be regulated by TNF signaling during inflammation and CRC.

TNF induces Dclk1 via Tnfrsf1a

To investigate the pathways involved in TNF-induced Dclk1 expression, Dclk1 expression was analyzed in IEC-6 cells with silenced Tnfrsf1a. Quantitative RT-PCR demonstrated that Tnfrsf1a expression was reduced by greater than 50% following transfection with Tnfrsf1a-specific small interfering RNAs compared to controls (Fig. 3A). This resulted in an approximate 50% decrease in Dclk1 expression following TNF treatment (Fig. 3B). Western blots further confirmed reduced Dclk1-S in Tnfrsf1a-silenced cells compared with controls (Fig. 3C). Additionally, Dclk1 expression was analyzed in the small intestinal biopsies from Tnfrsf1a−/− mice treated with TNF via intraperitoneal injection for 3 days, alongside biopsies from WT mice under the same conditions. Immunofluorescent staining revealed a significant increase in the number of Dclk1-positive cells in the small intestinal biopsies from TNF-treated WT mice compared to untreated mice (P < 0.01; Fig. 3D and E). In contrast, no increase was noted in the number of Dclk1-positive cells for Tnfrsf1a−/− mice following TNF treatment compared to untreated controls. Western blots demonstrated Dclk1-S expression in most TNF-treated WT mice with minimal expression in PBS-treated controls (Fig. 3F, upper panels). While no Dclk1-S was detected in PBS-treated Tnfrsf1a−/− mice, moderately increased Dclk1-S levels were detected in two out of four mice following TNF treatment (Fig. 3F, lower panels). However, when normalized to β-actin, Dclk1-S levels exhibited an increasing trend in both TNF-treated WT and Tnfrsf1a−/− mice compared to PBS controls, although these increases did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 3G), possibly due to the small sample size and high inter-individual variability. Finally, analysis of TCGA database demonstrated that DCLK1 expression was positively correlated with TNFRSF1A expression in human CRC (Supplementary Figure S2C and D). These findings suggest that TNF induces Dclk1 expression via Tnf-Tnfrsf1a pathway.

TNF induces Dclk1 expression via activating stem cell transcription factors

To better understand the regulation of TNF-induced Dclk1 expression, we assessed the activation of stem cell transcription factors in IEC-6 cells. Compared to untreated IEC-6 cells, TNF treatment activated stem cell transcription factors, including NFκB, Myc, Oct3/4, PRDM14, Twist-1, and WT-1 (Fig. 4A). All other tested transcription factors remained unchanged (fold change < 1.0). In silico analysis for transcription factor binding sites showed an NFκB binding motif in the promoter sequence of rat Dclk1 gene (Supplementary Figure S3). Therefore, we assessed Dclk1 expression following NFκB inhibitor treatment. Quantitative RT-PCR and Western blots demonstrated reduced Dclk1 expression in IEC-6 cells treated with the NFκB inhibitor Bay-11-7082 compared to untreated IEC-6 cells (Fig. 4B and C). Similarly, the NFκB inhibitor also blocked Dclk1 expression in TNF-treated IEC-6 cells compared to cells treated with TNF alone. Given that Wnt/β-catenin signaling inhibitors can also reduce Dclk1 expression [26] and that c-Myc is a downstream molecule of Wnt/β-catenin signaling [36], we also assessed Dclk1 expression by inhibiting c-Myc. Likewise, TNF-induced Dclk1 expression decreased in IEC-6 cells treated with c-Myc inhibitor 10074-G5 compared to cells treated with TNF alone (Fig. 4B and C). Notably, although no further decrease in Dclk1 expression was detected by qRT-PCR when cells were treated with a combination of Bay-11-7082 and 10074-G5, Dclk1 protein levels were further decreased after treatment with both NFκB and c-Myc inhibitors (Fig. 4C), supporting a synergistic regulation of Dclk1 via both NFκB and Wnt/β-catenin signaling. These findings indicate that Dclk1 is regulated by stem cell transcription factors.

TNF facilitates colonic organoid growth

Colon organoids serve as an ideal ex vivo model for studying stemness [37]. To further explore the contributions of TNF to the intestinal stemness, we analyzed the impact of TNF on organoid growth using the colonic organoid model. Organoids were typically derived from the colon crypts of WT mice (Supplementary Figure S4). Alcian blue and IF staining of these organoids revealed the presence of various colonic epithelial cell lineages, including goblet cells (positive for Alcian blue and Muc2) and ChgA-positive enteroendocrine cells (Fig. 5A). This indicates that, under our baseline conditions, the organoids maintain the ability to differentiate into multiple colonic epithelial cell lineages, thereby recapitulating the complexity of the in vivo colon. We next cultured organoids from human TNF-transgenic mice and compared with the growth of organoids from WT mice. The number and size of organoids derived from colon crypts of TNF-transgenic mice were significantly larger than those from WT mice (Fig. 5B-D and Supplementary Figure S4). Additionally, a rising trend was observed in the number of colonic organoids derived from WT mice treated with exogenous TNF compared to untreated controls, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 5E). Notably, the number and size of organoids derived from Tnfrsf1a−/− mice significantly decreased compared to those from WT mice, regardless of TNF treatment (Fig. 5E and F). Finally, IF staining indicated a significant increase in Dclk1-positive cells in TNF-treated colon organoids from WT mice and in organoids from TNF-transgenic mice (Fig. 5G). These findings suggest that TNF enhances Dclk1 expression, facilitates colon organoid growth, and promotes stemness.

Prolonged exposure to TNF induces reprogramming and stemness

Given that intestinal epithelial cells are subjected to prolonged exposure to inflammatory cytokines during chronic inflammation, we assessed long-term effects of TNF on cell fate. Cell lines were established by consecutively treating IEC-6 cells with TNF, or PBS as control, for 10 rounds (hereinafter referred to as IEC-6TNF and IEC-6Ctrl, respectively, Fig. 6A). Quantitative RT-PCR and Western blot analyses demonstrated a significant increase in Dclk1 expression in IEC-6TNF cells compared to IEC-6Ctrl (Fig. 6B and C).

To investigate whether long-term treatment with TNF induces stemness, we analyzed cell clusters in IEC-6TNF cell line and compared them with IEC-6Ctrl cell line using scRNA-seq. The analysis showed that, compared to IEC-6Ctrl cells, 276 genes were up-regulated and 281 genes were downregulated in IEC-6TNF cells (Fig. 6D). UMAP analysis identified 5 cell clusters in IEC-6Ctrl cell line, including 2 clusters of goblet cells, tuft cells, transient-amplifying (TA) cells, and enterocytes. In contrast, only 3 cell clusters were identified in IEC-6TNF cell line: tuft cells, TA cells, and stem cells (Fig. 6E and F). Notably, a substantial number of stem cells (23%) emerged in IEC-6TNF cell line, while goblet cells and enterocytes diminished, indicating that long-term exposure to TNF induces cellular reprogramming. Gene expression analysis showed a similar pattern for colorectal cancer stem cell markers Dclk1 and Cd44, with substantial expression observed in both stem cells and tuft cells in IEC-6TNF cell line. In contrast, their expression was detected in other clusters, such as goblet cells and enterocytes, within the IEC-6Ctrl cell line (Fig. 6G). Additionally, we examined the expression of Ly6E and Prom1, two other colorectal CSC markers. Ly6E was expressed in stem cell and tuft cell clusters of the IEC-6TNF cell line, whereas Prom1 was not expressed in either the IEC-6TNF or IEC-6Ctrl cell lines (Fig. 6G). Notably, very few cells expressed the intestinal crypt stem cell marker Lgr5 in both the IEC-6TNF and IEC-6Ctrl cell lines (Supplementary Figure S5A).

We next reconstructed the developmental trajectory of the cells. Using Dclk1 as a marker gene, pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed that TA cells represented the initial point of the developmental trajectory in both IEC-6Ctrl and IEC-6TNF cell lines (Fig. 6H). In the IEC-6Ctrl cell line, TA cells sequentially differentiated into tuft cells, goblet cells, and enterocytes (Fig. 6H, upper panel). Conversely, in the IEC-6TNF cell line, the trajectory progressed from TA cells to tuft cells and ultimately to stem cells (Fig. 6H, lower panel), indicating that in TNF-treated cells, stem cells arise from Dclk1-positive tuft cells. Intriguingly, when Lgr5 was employed as a marker gene in the pseudotime trajectory analysis, the initial point shifted to a subset of tuft cells, which then differentiated through 4 paths into TA cells, enterocytes, goblet cells, and more differentiated tuft cells in the IEC-6Ctrl cell line (Supplementary Figure S5B, right panel). In contrast, the developmental trajectory in IEC-6TNF cells, similar to Dclk1 as a marker gene, began with TA cells progressing to tuft cells and ultimately the emergence of stem cells (Supplementary Figure S5B, left panel). These findings suggest that prolonged TNF treatment depletes differentiated cells, such as goblet cells and enterocytes, while promoting the emergence of stem cells through dedifferentiation and reprogramming.

TNF causes EMT and neoplastic transformation

Cell type-specific gene expression analysis revealed distinct gene expression profiles for sub-clusters in the IEC-6TNF cell line compared to the IEC-6Ctrl cell line (Fig. 7A). To investigate the signaling pathways triggered by TNF, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on differentially expressed genes identified through scRNA-seq. A total of 57 pathways were enriched in the rat KEGG gene sets (Supplementary Table S3). Notably, CRC and pluripotency signaling pathways along with TNF and NFκB signaling were significantly enriched (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, when these differentially expressed genes were analyzed for hallmarks in the human Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB), numerous inflammation and CRC-related signaling pathways, including P53, KRAS, and EMT signaling, emerged among the 22 significantly enriched hallmarks (Supplementary Table S4 and Supplementary Figure S6). IF staining corroborated the increased expression of pluripotent transcription factors, including c-Myc and Klf4, in IEC-6TNF cells compared to IEC-6Ctrl cells (Supplementary Figure S7A), as well as in colon biopsies from TNF-transgenic mice compared to WT mice (Supplementary Figure S7B). Furthermore, IF staining revealed a significant decrease in the number of goblet cells (positive for Muc2)—a feature of colon inflammation—in colon biopsies of TNF-transgenic mice compared to those of WT mice (Supplementary Figure S7C). Conversely, the number of ChgA-positive enteroendocrine cells slightly increased in colon biopsies from TNF-transgenic mice compared to those from WT mice, although this change did not reach statistical significance. These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating an increase in ChgA-positive enteroendocrine cells in IBD [38].

The xenograft tumor assay revealed that no tumors developed in NOD/Scid mice injected with IEC-6Ctrl cells (0 out of 6 sites). In contrast, tumors formed in 5 mice injected with IEC-6TNF cells, with tumors observed at 6 out of 10 sites (Fig. 7C), indicating neoplastic transformation. PCR amplification of the rat Xist gene confirmed that a 1,147 bp PCR product was obtained from 3 xenograft tumors and IEC-6 cells, while no such amplicon was detected in the NOD/Scid mouse samples (Fig. 7D), supporting IEC-6 cell origin of these tumors. Notably, a larger fragment was amplified in the remaining 3 tumors, suggesting a potential insertion in the target fragment of these clones. Histological analysis through H&E staining and immunohistochemical staining indicated that one tumor (D23R) was positively stained for both pan-cytokeratin and vimentin, while most tumors exhibited strong vimentin staining—characteristics of sarcoma, indicating the occurrence of EMT in IEC-6TNF cells (Fig. 7E and Supplementary Figure S8). Consistent with the IHC findings, Western blot analysis demonstrated a decrease in E-cadherin, accompanied by significant increases in N-cadherin and vimentin (Fig. 7F) in IEC-6TNF cells compared to IEC-6Ctrl cells, further supporting the occurrence of EMT in IEC-6TNF cells. Finally, immunofluorescent staining indicated a strong expression of Dclk1 in all tumors (Fig. 7E and Supplementary Figure S8). These findings suggest that TNF activates pathways associated with CRC, EMT, and cancer stemness, ultimately driving neoplastic transformation.

Discussion

TNF plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of IBD and colitis-associated CRC [7, 39]. Recent studies demonstrate that TNF is also an important regulator of intestinal homeostasis and tissue regeneration [5, 40]. TNF induces dedifferentiation and reprogramming in pancreatic cancer cells [10]. Additionally, TNF can trigger EMT, thereby contributing to stem cell genesis [41, 42]. The present study elucidates the roles of TNF in regulating Dclk1 expression and colorectal CSC genesis. Our findings reveal that Dclk1, a marker of colorectal CSCs, is significantly upregulated in human TNF-transgenic mice and TNF-treated intestinal epithelial cells, suggesting a pivotal role for TNF in driving the emergence of CSCs. This research builds upon previous work that identified TNF as a mediator of microbiota-induced bystander effects in promoting CSC markers in colonic epithelial cells [8, 26]. The novelty of our research lies in demonstrating the TNF-Tnfrsf1a-NFκB signaling axis as a critical regulator of Dclk1 expression. Furthermore, prolonged exposure to TNF induces dedifferentiation and reprogramming of intestinal epithelial cells, leading to the emergence of stem cells and neoplastic transformation, thereby linking chronic inflammation with CRC.

Previous studies have shown that NFκB coordinates with Wnt signaling, leading to the acquisition of a stem cell-like phenotype through the dedifferentiation of intestinal epithelial cells [18]. In line with these findings, the present study demonstrates that TNF induces Dclk1 expression through the activation of stem cell transcription factors, including NFκB and c-Myc, which are known to play essential roles in stemness and tumorigenesis [43]. Our findings are supported by previous studies showing that Dclk1-positive cells strongly express RelA [44], and that TNF increases breast cancer stem-like cells through the non-canonical NFκB pathway [9]. Intriguingly, recent studies revealed that Dclk1 can activate NFκB signaling in macrophages resulting in inflammatory responses [45]. While it remains unknown whether Dclk1 can activate NFκB in the intestinal epithelial cells, these findings imply a feedback loop in the regulation of Dclk1 by NFκB. Furthermore, inhibition studies indicate a synergistic regulation of Dclk1 expression via both NFκB and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways, which supports previous findings on CRC stemness [18].

Colonic organoid culture serves as an ideal model for studying stem cell characteristics and may have therapeutic potential for intestinal damage. Our results demonstrate that organoids derived from TNF-transgenic mice exhibited significantly increased size and number, indicating enhanced proliferation and differentiation of crypt stem cells in TNF overexpressed mice compared to those in WT mice. Interestingly, while exogenous TNF did not stimulate additional growth in WT-derived organoids—possibly reflecting the lack of influence from short-term TNF treatment on Dclk1-positive cell proliferation—the marked reduction in organoid growth from Tnfrsf1a−/− mice underscores the necessity of TNF signaling in maintaining stemness. Notably, the pronounced increase in Dclk1-positive cells in TNF-treated organoids and those from TNF-transgenic mice show that TNF can promote stemness.

While qRT-PCR showed increased Dclk1 expression in the colon of TNF-transgenic mice, this increase was not reflected in the number of Dclk1-positive cells detected by immunofluorescent staining (Supplementary Fig. 1C and D). This may be attributed to the rapid cycling of colon crypt cells, as TNF expression is significantly higher in the colon than in the small intestine. Additionally, post-transcriptional modification of Dclk1 by miR195 can reduce the number of Dclk1-positive tuft cells in mice [46]. Nevertheless, our colon organoid model demonstrated a significant increase in Dclk1-positive cells in organoids derived from TNF-transgenic mice and in those from WT mice treated with TNF, supporting that TNF promotes stem cell proliferation and stemness.

The emergence of stem cells in the IEC-6TNF cell line following prolonged TNF exposure underscores the role of chronic inflammation in driving cellular reprogramming. IEC-6 cells, derived from adult rat small intestine, exhibit normal epithelial features [32]. Our scRNA-seq results revealed that IEC-6Ctrl were composed of multiple cell types of intestinal epithelium. Interestingly, two subsets of goblet cells were identified in the IEC-6Ctrl cell line. Likewise, while not further subclassified, two distinct clusters of tuft cells were also noted in IEC-6Ctrl cells. These findings are supported by previous studies showing that the signature genes of murine small intestinal epithelial cells are differentially expressed with regional diversity [47]. Importantly, we observed that long-term treatment with TNF led to a marked shift in cellular identity, characterized by a reduction in differentiated cell types and an emergence of stem cell populations. In particular, the reduction of goblet cells is a feature of colitis, and TNF treatment indeed decreases the number of goblet cells [48]. This phenomenon aligns with previous studies that report the impact of chronic inflammation on the plasticity of intestinal epithelial cells [49–51]. Additionally, the identification of Dclk1-positive tuft cells as potential precursors in the transition from differentiated cells to stem cells is particularly noteworthy as tuft cells are known to possess stem-like properties in colitis and CRC [19, 49]. Overall, these findings highlight a role for TNF in shaping the cellular landscape toward a more stem-like phenotype.

EMT is a critical process in cancer progression, metastasis, and the development of CSCs [52]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that TNF can induce EMT by activating key signaling pathways such as MAPK, TGF-β, and CXCL10/CXCR3 [42, 53–55]. Consistent with these findings, we observed that prolonged exposure to TNF induces EMT, as indicated by decreased E-cadherin and increased vimentin levels in IEC-6TNF cells. Importantly, nearly all xenograft tumors derived from the IEC-6TNF cell line exhibit a sarcomatous phenotype rather than carcinomas, further supporting the role of TNF in promoting EMT. Considering that Dclk1 also promotes EMT [24, 56], our findings support the notion that TNF may facilitate EMT and cancer stemness through the induction of Dclk1 expression.

As aforementioned, Dclk1 is expressed in two distinct isoforms: Dclk1-L and Dclk1-S. A limitation of this study is that the antibody used for immunofluorescence staining and the qRT-PCR primers could not distinguish between the two isoforms. We observed that IEC-6 cells primarily express the Dclk1-S isoform, with minimal Dclk1-L expression, and that Dclk1-S was significantly upregulated by TNF treatment. Presumably, our immunofluorescence staining and qRT-PCR predominantly detected the Dclk1-S isoform. Furthermore, we found no significant difference in the number of Dclk1-positive cells in intestinal biopsies from TNF-treated Tnfrsf1a−/− mice compared to controls (Figs. 3D and E). This may be explained by the compensatory activation of the Tnfrsf1b pathway, although this was not directly investigated. Similarly, Western blot analysis revealed no significant difference in Dclk1 levels between TNF-treated and control mice (Figs. 3F and G), possibly due to our limited sample size. This highlights the need for further studies with a larger cohort. Additionally, systemic inflammation resulting from the injection procedure also cannot be completely ruled out. Another limitation is our focus on Dclk1 without evaluating other stem cell markers such as Lgr5 and Cd44 in TNF-treated IEC-6 cells. Previous studies by our group have shown that TNF can induce Cd44 expression in mouse colonic epithelial cells [26]. Additionally, our primary objective was to investigate the regulatory role of TNF on Dclk1 expression and its potential contribution to carcinogenesis; therefore, we limited our scope to Dclk1. While exploring the regulation of other stem cell-associated genes is an intriguing area, it is beyond the scope of the present study and could be addressed in future research.

Despite these limitations, a comprehensive investigation was conducted using various models, including genetically modified mouse models, organoid cultures, and IEC-6 rat intestinal epithelial cells to demonstrate the roles of TNF in regulating Dclk1 expression and cellular reprogramming. Importantly, the use of the TNF humanized mouse model preliminarily translates our results to human physiology. Future studies may include patient-derived organoid models to better mimic human CRC biology. Finally, given that higher DCLK1 expression correlates with poorer disease-free survival in CRC patients, the regulation of DCLK1 via the TNF-Tnfrsf1a-NFκB axis presents an attractive target for CRC intervention.

Conclusions

TNF regulates Dclk1 expression via the TNF-Tnfrsf1a-NFκB axis. Prolonged exposure of intestinal epithelial cells to TNF induces cellular reprogramming, leading to the emergence of CSCs and neoplastic transformation. These findings illuminate the mechanisms by which TNF influences the origin of intestinal CSCs and contributes to CRC, underscoring the pivotal role of TNF in shaping the cellular landscape of CRC. Furthermore, this study bridges the gap in our understanding of how chronic inflammation drives the development of colorectal cancer, potentially paving the way for new therapeutic strategies.
