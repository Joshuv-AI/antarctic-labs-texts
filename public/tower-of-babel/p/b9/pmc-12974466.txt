Thirty years of glacier grounding line retreat in Antarctica
Authors: Eric Rignot, Bernd Scheuchl, Jean Baptiste Barre, Virginia Brancato, Laurane Charrier, Hanning Chen, Enrico Ciraci, Andy Dinh, Sam Herreid, Seongsu Jeong, Xin Li, Thomas Mitchell, Yara Mohajerani, Sina Shamsian, Valentyn Tolpekin, Isabella Velicogna, Michael Wollersheim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThree decades of satellite synthetic aperture radar interferometry show that about 77% of Antarctica’s grounding line has remained stable. Grounding-line retreat is concentrated in the northeast and southwest Antarctic Peninsula, Wilkes and George V Lands of East Antarctica, and the Bellingshausen Sea, Amundsen Sea, and Getz Ice Shelf sectors of West Antarctica, where retreat ranges from 10 to more than 40 km. The greatest retreat occurs where deep troughs permit warm Circumpolar Deep Water access and where beds deepen inland, indicating sustained mass loss from vulnerable sectors. Slower retreat occurs where warm-water access is limited and beds shoal inland. These continent-scale grounding-zone observations provide an important benchmark for evaluating and improving next-generation ice-sheet models.

The Antarctic Ice Sheet holds an ice volume equivalent to a 57-m increase in global sea level (1). The ice sheet will thicken as a result of increased snowfall precipitation or glacier slowdown, or thin because of increased surface melt, lower precipitation, or faster glacier flow into the Southern Ocean. The transition boundary between glacier ice that rests on the bedrock and glacier ice floating in the ocean is called the grounding line (GL) (2).

The loss of ice mass is measured using time series of time-variable gravity data from the GRACE and GRACE Follow-On missions (3), changes in elevation of the ice surface from radar and laser altimetry missions (4), or calculations of the difference between the net input of mass to the ice sheet, reconstructed by regional climate models, minus the net outflow of ice along the periphery, calculated from radar sounder-derived thickness and satellite-derived velocity data (1). The mapping of the GL provides independent and critical information about the transition in the stress regime between ice friction on bedrock and ice sliding freely on seawater. Ice crossing the GL displaces sea level, whereas ice inland of the GL does not. GL delineation is difficult to perform at any level of precision on the ground but can be done precisely using satellite differential synthetic aperture radar (SAR) interferometry (DInSAR) data (5–8) over large areas, repeatedly. The first application of DInSAR for GL mapping used the European Space Agency Earth Remote Sensing satellite, ERS-1. This capability was augmented over time by additional SAR missions up to the present day, with the advent of time series of nearly continuous, daily data.

The dense time series of DInSAR data indicate that the transition boundary between grounded and floating ice should be represented as an “ice grounding zone” (IGZ) instead of a GL to reflect the fact that the GL migrates at tidal frequencies on the order of one to several ice thicknesses, or multiple kilometers, which is much more than expected from hydrostatic equilibrium combined with uncertainties in bed topography (8–12). We attribute the existence of a long IGZ to interactions between time-dependent pressurized seawater, bed topography, and pressurized subglacial freshwater (11).

Here, we present a comprehensive, SAR-derived, pan-Antarctic multimission (15 satellites) GL and IGZ mapping. It spans three decades, is harmonized, and provides a robust record of GL retreat, advance, or stability (Materials and Methods and SI Appendix, Fig. S1).

Results

The circumpolar length of the ice shelf and the outlet glacier GL is approximately 31,390 km on a 15-m scale in Antarctica, plus 12,768 km of ice cliffs and rocks (revised from ref. 13). We detect retreat along a length of 7,205 km, or 23% of Antarctica. In East Antarctica, we detect retreat on 1,570 km of coastline out of 19,103 km, or 8%, mostly in Wilkes and George VI lands (Fig. 1). In West Antarctica, we detect retreat on 4,728 km out of 7,929 km, or 60%, mostly in the Amundsen and Bellingshausen sea sectors. In the Antarctic Peninsula, we detect retreat on 907 km out of 4,359 km, or 21%, mostly on George VI and Larsen Inlet, A and B ice shelves. We exclude from this analysis part of the coastline that ends in calving cliffs or rocks, i.e., 7,110 km in East Antarctica, 1,354 km in West Antarctica, and 4,303 km in the Antarctic Peninsula, which does not discharge much grounded ice into the ocean. GLs have remained stable over more than 77% of Antarctica, i.e., they did not migrate within ±1 km given uncertainties associated with oceanic tide, atmospheric pressure (12), changes in flow speed during the tidal cycle (14) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), temporal sampling, and horizontal motion due to ice bending stress (6) (Materials and Methods).

The western antarctic peninsula does not have floating sections north of the Wordie Ice Shelf (Fig. 1). At the Wordie Ice Shelf, glacial retreat has been ongoing for decades (15). In 2025, ICEYE data reveal a 10-km-long ice shelf at the mouth of Airy, Seller, and Flemming glaciers (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Prospect and Field Ice Piemond 1 and 2 glaciers also exhibit floating sections of 2 to 5 km, respectively. The area of GL retreat is 124 ± 15 km2 for 1996–2025 (Table 1 and SI Appendix, Table S1). Further south, we detect no GL migration on the glaciers that feed into the Wilkins Ice Shelf despite its partial collapse in 2008, confirming that these glaciers are not dynamically connected to the ice shelf (1). On the George VI Ice Shelf, migration is negligible on its western flank, flowing slowly from Alexander Island, and on the northeastern sector, flowing faster from Graham Land until the latitude of the ERS Glacier. We detect a 3-km retreat on ERS Glacier, 4 km on Envisat Glacier, 7 km on Cryosat Glacier, 2 km on GRACE Glacier, 5 km on Sentinel Glacier, 2 km on Nikitin Glacier, 6 km on Berg Ice Stream, and 6 km on Landsat Glacier from 1994–1996 to 2020 (Fig. 2G), for a combined retreat of 557 ± 169 km2 when scaled to 1996–2025.

On the east coast of the Antarctic Peninsula, starting from the north, we confirm a 3 to 6 km retreat of Boydell and Sjogren glaciers near the Prince Gustav Channel and a 16, 10, and 9 km retreat of Edgeworth, Bombardier, and Dinsmoor glaciers upstream of the former Larsen A Ice Shelf (16) (Fig. 2I). Edgeworth Glacier did not have a floating section in 1996. The glacier retreated 16 km to form a new ice shelf, which subsequently disintegrated, and is now only 5 km long. Further south, at Cape Worsley, several unnamed glaciers that developed into a small (less than 500 m) ice shelf in 1996 retreated by 4 to 9 km by 2025, for a combined loss of 70 ± 5 km2 (Fig. 2H). At Drygalski Glacier, the 1996 GL was close to the ice front (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). In 2025, the glacier has no floating section and essentially calves 5.5 km inland of the 1996 GL. The combined loss for the Larsen A and Inlet area is 261 ± 20 km2.

On the Larsen B Ice Shelf, the retreat is 21 km for Hektoria, 16 km for Green, and 9 km for Evans glaciers (Fig. 2H). In 2025, Hektoria Glacier develops a 2.3 km long ice shelf compared to only 600 to 900 m for Green and Evans glaciers (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Immediately south, Punchbowl, Jorun North, Jorun, and Crane glaciers have retreated 2.6, 2.2, 3.4, and 5.3 km from 1996 to 2025, with a 2025 GL position less than one ice front thickness inland of the 2025 calving front (Fig. 2H and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). The 30-y retreat is less than 1 km for the Mapple, Melville, and Pequod glaciers further south. We then find little retreat on the glaciers feeding the remnant part of Larsen B, e.g. Starbuck, Flask, and Leppard glaciers; no retreat on the Jason Peninsula that separates the Larsen B and Larsen C ice shelves; and, more important, no retreat in the vast sector that drains into the Larsen C and Larsen D ice shelves along the entire remainder of the east coast of the Antarctic Peninsula. Overall, the eastern side of the Peninsula lost 413 ± 20 km2 of grounded ice in 1996–2025 entirely from Larsen B and areas farther north. The total loss is 1,354 ± 225 km2 for the combined east and west Antarctic Peninsula in 1996–2025 (Table 1 and SI Appendix, Table S1).

East Antarctica, starting clockwise on the Filchner-Ronne Ice Shelf at Academy Glacier, does not show GL migration along the Lassiter Coast, and Evans, Carlson, and Rutford ice streams (Fig. 3H). Further east, we have a GL position for the Union Glacier, Hercules Inlet, Institute Ice Stream, Moller Ice Stream, Foundation Ice Stream, Academy Glacier, and Recovery Glaciers only in year 2009. A comparison of these data with the 2019–2020 GL derived from ICESAT-2 laser altimetry data (17) over a set of 6,531 ICESAT-2 points reveals an average separation of 0.86 ± 1.2 km, with a median of 0.48 km. We conclude that the GL did not migrate between 2009 and 2019. On the Slessor and Bailey ice streams, a comparison of GLs from 2009 versus 2021/2025 shows no migration either. Bailey Ice Stream develops an exceptionally long grounding zone length, up to 23 km (SI Appendix, Fig. S5).

The GL is stable on the Brunt Ice Shelf, Stancomb Wills Glacier, Veststraumen Glacier, Endurance Ice Stream, and the entire Riiser-Larsen Ice Shelf. On Stancomb Wills, we detect a slight variation in horizontal velocity during the tidal cycle (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), but no GL retreat. We find no retreat in the Quar, Ekstrom, Jelbard, and Fimbul ice shelves. The sinuous GL of the Jutulstraumen Ice Stream, a major glacier flowing into the Fimbul Ice Shelf, shows the same pinning point at 71.68°S in year 2023 as it did in year 1994, which is synonymous with glacier stability and steady ice shelf thickness (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Similarly, we find no retreat along the entire sector of Queen Maud Land that includes the Vigrid, Nivi, Lazarev, Borchgrevink, and Roi Baudoin ice shelves.

Along the Prince Harald Coast, GL mapping is difficult because there are few ERS data acquisitions and high interferometric phase noise, which we attribute to weather altering the scattering characteristics between SAR acquisitions. However, the few available data from 1996 to 2024 suggest that there is no GL migration. We detect many pinning points on the ice shelf, which imply a shallow water column. We detect no migration for the Shirase Glacier, the largest glacier in the region from 1996 to 2024 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). In Enderby Land, after revising earlier-derived 1996 GL positions, we report no retreat for the Rayner, Thyer, Wilma, and Robert glaciers. Further east, the Amery Ice Shelf GL has a record depth of 2.5 km and 15-km-long seawater intrusions along two deep channels (18). We detect no retreat from 1996 to 2024 along the entire ice shelf GL, which is synonymous with stability and a state of mass balance. We have no multitemporal GL data on the Publication Ice Shelf and the many small floating sections further east. There are no usable SAR observations of West Ice Shelf in 1996. The West Ice Shelf GL was first mapped in 2000–2001. However, we find no GL migration between 2000 and 2024. We detect many pinning points on the ice shelf, suggesting a shallow water column underneath.

Further east, the Shackleton Ice Shelf hosts the Denman Glacier, a major glacier in East Antarctica (Fig. 3A). The Shackleton Ice Shelf has many pinning points, more than previously reported, and does not exhibit GL migration. However, we report an 18 km retreat for Reid, 12 km for Northcliffe, 12 km for Denman, and no retreat on Smith and Apfel glaciers for 1996–2025 (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Long (13 km) seawater intrusions are visible in Denman GL in data acquired in 2021, 2023, and especially 2025. The intrusions extend well beyond the central ridge that stabilizes the glacier, into one of the deepest bedrock trench in East Antarctica (19). The intrusions extend to the lowest bed elevation within that trench. Long seawater intrusions are also present in the Northcliffe and Reid glaciers. The loss of grounded ice in that sector totals 216 ± 43 km2 in 1996–2025.

East of Shackleton, the Conger Ice Shelf, which collapsed in 2022 (20), does not exhibit GL retreat for 1996–2025. We find that its calving cliff in 2025 coincides with the GL position in 1996, indicating that the collapse of the Conger Ice Shelf has had no impact on the dynamics of the continental ice. In Vincennes Bay, we detect retreat for Adams, Anzac, Bond, Underwood glaciers, and a spectacular 26 km on Vanderford Glacier along an overdeepened bed (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S9) (21). In recent years, the retreat of Vanderford has slowed down, which is explained by a rise in bed elevation above the present-day location of the GL. This sector lost 513 ± 50 km2 of grounded ice in 1996–2025, i.e., the ninth largest loss in Antarctica (Table 1).

The Totten Glacier retreated 0.5 km/y from 1996 to 2013 (22). The retreat is now 10 km for the period 1996–2024 along the glacier side, but not at the center, where an ice rise detaches more slowly from the glacier than the surrounding areas (Fig. 3C). The ice shelf is pinned down at a number of locations in the center and on the sides, exhibiting a complex network of islands and corridors along its eastern flank, which we refer to as Totten East (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). We detect a multikilometer retreat on Totten East, which is the slower-moving but broader part of Totten Glacier. In total, Totten Glacier lost 581 ± 87 km2 grounded ice in 1996–2025.

The adjacent Moscow University Ice Shelf also exhibits a sinuous GL with many pinning points. The main glacier splits into two branches that flow at a 90° angle from each other (Fig. 3D). The southern branch, Moscow West, retreated 6 km. The northern, deeper branch, Moscow, retreated 2 to 3 km (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). The combined loss is 660 ± 102 km2 for 1996–2025.

Continuing east, we find no retreat on the Voyeykov Ice Shelf. The next system of the Frost, De Haven, and Holmes glaciers, which calves into Porpoise Bay, is difficult to survey (Fig. 3E) due to intense surface weathering, short floating sections (Frost), pinning points (De Haven), and a steep transition to flotation (Holmes). This sector experienced a series of major calving events in 2007 that removed 2,900 km2 of floating debris after the breakup of multiyear sea ice in Porpoise Bay (23). We report a 6 km retreat for Frost and 8 km on De Haven in 1996–2023, and 10 km on the west flank of Holmes in 1996–2025 (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). The combined loss is 402 ± 72 km2 in 1996–2025.

We detect a 3 to 4 km retreat at the center of Dibble Glacier, for a modest loss of 30 ± 2 km2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). The ice shelf in front of Dibble has many islands, ice rises, and pinning points, hence a presumably shallow water column. We do not have multiyear GL for the small, slow-moving Pourquoi Pas, Commandant Charcot, Francais, Barre, Liotard, Astrolabe, and Zelee glaciers.

The west flank of Mertz Glacier shows a 10-km retreat along a deeper part of the bed for a total loss of 303 ± 8 km2 in 1996–2025 (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). The retreat is less on the eastern flank, probably because of the presence of a ridge in bed elevation at that location. The retreat is 4 km at the center of Ninnis Glacier, less on the sides (Ninnis East and West), for a total loss of 143 ± 55 km2 (Fig. 3F and SI Appendix, Fig. S15). The Cook Ice Shelf retreated 10 km along its fast-flowing core in the west, which we refer to as Cook West Glacier, and which lost its buttressing ice shelf in the 1970s (24). The retreat is only 3 to 5 km on the Cook East Glacier, which is still supported by an extensive ice shelf. The western half of the Cook Ice Shelf experiences heavy rifting at the GL, indicating little resistance to flow from the continent (Fig. 3G and SI Appendix, Fig. S16). The loss for the Cook Ice Shelf is 368 ± 72 km2 for 1996–2025.

The Matusevitch, Lauritzen, Prior, and Rennick glaciers experienced a multikilometer retreat between 1996 and 2019. We find no retreat further east on any of the glaciers in Victoria Land but detect exceptionally long IGZs, up to 13 km on Tucker, Mariner, and Barnett glaciers. Exceptionally long IGZs mean that many observations are required to conclude on GL migration. The scaled loss is 188 ± 2 km2 for Rennick and 188 ± 71 km2 for the Matusevitch, Lauritzen, and Prior glaciers for 1996–2025.

We have DInSAR data only for year 2009 on the Ross Ice Shelf, where East Antarctica stops at the Amundsen Glacier. The Ross Ice Shelf can only be imaged with left-looking SAR missions, i.e., RADARSAT-2 and TerraSAR-X. When we compare a set of 18,104 GL points from ICESat-2 (17) with the 2009 GL from DInSAR (Fig. 3I), we find an average separation of 0.91 ± 2.3 km, with a median of 0.36 km. We conclude that there is no GL migration in 2009–2019 on the Ross Ice Shelf. On Byrd Glacier, DInSAR data from PAZ acquired in 2025 indicate no migration versus the 2011 GL from TerraSAR-X in ref. 25 or the 2009 GL from RADARSAT-2. We conclude that the GL of the Ross Ice Shelf is stable.

The West Antarctic Ice Sheet, clockwise from the Ross, retreats slowly on the glaciers flowing into the Sulzberger Ice Shelf, a shallow, slow-moving ice shelf with many islands and pinning points. The total loss is 184 ± 45 km2. We have few GL data on the Nickerson Ice Shelf, but the existing data suggest GL stability.

East of Nickerson, the retreat resumes with Land (5 km) (SI Appendix, Fig. S17), Hull (14 km) (SI Appendix, Fig. S18), Berry (19 km) (12), Venzke (8 km), DeVicq (7 km) (Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S19) and the entire remainder of the Getz Ice Shelf (4 to 10 km) (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S20). Hull Glacier retreated 14 km to the southeast along an overdeepening bed that has considerably enlarged its floating section (26). The retreat is spectacular for the Venzke and Berry glaciers at the western edge of the Getz Ice Shelf, also along overdeepened beds (12). The retreat is smaller for the DeVicq Glacier but extends across the entire Getz Ice Shelf by several kilometers along the fast-flowing glaciers, with negligible retreat in between the glaciers (SI Appendix, Fig. S20). The loss of grounded ice totals 1,732 ± 351 km2 for East Getz, the largest loss in Antarctica, and 168 ± 28 for DeVicq Glacier, 320 ± 25 for Berry and Venzke glaciers, and 244 ± 49 km2 for Hull/Land for 1996–2025.

The fastest retreat in West Antarctica occurs in the Amundsen Sea sector (27). In Pope, Smith, and Kohler glaciers, the retreat ranges from 10 to a spectacular 43 km in 1992–2025 (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S21). The retreat along Pope and Smith has proceeded along retrograde bed slopes. This sector lost 1,688 ± 133 km2 of grounded ice in 1996–2025. The Haynes Glacier had a negligible floating extension in 1996 but retreated 25 km by 2025 to form a new ice shelf that is 536 ± 35 km2 in size (SI Appendix, Fig. S22). The Haynes Glacier retreat rate averaged 1 km/y in 2019–2025.

The Thwaites Glacier has been retreating along its 120-km long front since 1992 (Fig. 2B and Fig. SI Appendix, Fig. S22). The fast-flowing, deep portion of Thwaites Glacier retreated 26 km along an overdeepened bed (11). The slower-moving, thinner Thwaites Eastern Ice Shelf retreated from 4 to 23 km over a more bumpy bed. The combined loss for Thwaites Glacier is 1,160 ± 62 km2 for 1996–2025. We note that the Thwaites Eastern Ice Shelf is buttressed by an ice rise that has decreased in size from 74 ± 5 km2 in 1996 to 14 ± 2 km2 in 2024, i.e., a factor of 5. The reduction in ice rise area is indicative of ice shelf thinning and reduced underpinning of the ice shelf by the ice rise.

The Pine Island Glacier has retreated rapidly in the 1990s until the mid-2000s, more slowly since, but the retreat has not stopped (Fig. 2A). The Larter and Piglet glaciers, two slower-moving and thinner tributaries, retreated by 3 and 8 km (SI Appendix, Fig. S23). The Piglet Glacier is important because it connects the Pine Island and Thwaites glaciers through an overdeepened bed. In total, the Pine Island Glacier system lost 1,035 ± 108 km2 of grounded ice for 1996–2025.

We detect minimal loss of grounded ice on the Abbot and Cosgrove ice shelves. However, we find a retreat of 6 to 7 km on the Venable Ice Shelf (Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S24), Fox Glacier, and Ferrigno Ice Stream (SI Appendix, Fig. S25). The grounded ice loss is 238 ± 53 km2 for Venable in 1996–2021, 254 ± 28 km2 for Fox in 1996–2021 and 175 ± 18 km2 for Ferrigno in 1992–2021. The coastal sector from Fox to Allison glaciers includes a large fraction of calving cliffs instead of ice shelves. Most calving cliff sectors are stable in Antarctica, but in this sector, the calving cliffs have retreated by several kilometers since 1992. The total loss is 855 ± 152 km2 for 1996–2025.

The loss of grounded ice totals 7,911 ± 982 km2 for West Antarctica, 3,519 ± 632 km2 for East Antarctica, and 1,354 ± 225 km2 for the Antarctic Peninsula, for a combined total of 12,785 ± 1,840 km2. The retreat in the Antarctic Peninsula excludes glaciers that end in calving cliffs, which could add another 100 ± 84 km2 (28). The annual average of GL retreat is 442 ± 64 km2 for the entire Antarctic Ice Sheet.

Discussion

The length of the IGZ is several kilometers, or several ice thicknesses, over multiple tidal cycles. In this study, we update an Antarctic IGZ for the years 2018–2020 (29), which is the envelope of all GL positions for the years 2018–2020. The IGZ product will help to revise the representation of GL in ice sheet models to include a melt IGZ. The inclusion of melt in an IGZ that spans several-ice-thicknesses will increase the projections of sea level rise from Antarctica by up to two according to previous modeling work (30, 31). A long IGZ can induce high melt because pressurized seawater beneath grounded ice at a high entrainment speed melts the ice vigorously. In contrast, a narrow IGZ can induce low melt, as water can get trapped during the tidal cycle (18, 32).

In several areas, new ice shelves reformed during a retreat. The Fox and Ferrigno ice streams develop more extensive floating sections today than in the past (SI Appendix, Fig. S25), indicating that retreating glaciers are not synonymous with reduced-size ice shelves. The same conclusion applies to Edgeworth, Bombardier, and Dinsmoor glaciers in the Antarctic Peninsula, Haynes, Pope, Smith, Kohler glaciers and Venable and Getz Ice Shelf in West Antarctica, and Cook Ice Shelf in East Antarctica, to name a few. The maintenance or reformation of an ice shelf creates a negative feedback to the retreat because an ice shelf offers more resistance to the outflow of continental ice (33). It will be important for ice sheet models in charge of predicting sea level rise to take that negative feedback into account.

Exceptions to the reform of an ice shelf exist. The Drygalski Glacier has now a grounded terminus. Evans and Green glaciers upstream the former Larsen B Ice Shelf float over only half the ice thickness at the terminus. The Punchbowl, Jorum, Mapple, and Melville glaciers have nearly grounded termini. To understand the conditions leading to ice shelf reformation versus grounded terminus, it will be essential to have precision data on the depth of the sea floor, so that we know how much the ice surface deviates from hydrostatic equilibrium and if retrograde slopes are present. In this part of the Antarctic Peninsula, where glacial retreat is rapid and recent, bathymetry is almost nonexistent. Filling that knowledge gap should be an important research priority in understanding the pattern of glacial retreat, with implications for the remainder of Antarctica.

Interpreting the retreat requires connecting the observed changes in GL position with physical processes, boundary conditions, and forcing mechanisms. There is a broad acceptance that the evolution of the Antarctic Ice Sheet mass balance is driven by the dynamics of its glaciers and, to a lesser extent, by changes in snowfall (1, 34). The flow of the glaciers is affected by intrusions of warm waters, mostly of Circumpolar Deep Water (CDW) origin, which melt ice rapidly at the IGZ. Ice melt reduces basal friction as ice loses contact with the bedrock/sediments, allowing glaciers to speed up, which is conducive to more thinning and retreat. If the glaciers retreat along a retrograde bed, i.e., bed elevation drops in the inland direction, the retreat is rapid, as predicted by the marine ice sheet instability theory (33). In contrast, the retreat is expected to be slower along prograde slopes.

A poleward shift of the westerlies has increased CDW intrusions on the continental shelf toward GLs (34), which explains the recent retreat. Oceanographic data from West Antarctica document the presence of vigorous ocean thermal forcing. In East Antarctica, data collected by sea mammals have suggested that mid-depth CDW warmed by 0.8 to 2.0 °C along the continental slope between 1930–1990 and 2010–2018 due to the summer poleward shift of westerlies (35). Further evidence for the presence of warm CDW exists in front of Vanderford Glacier, in Vincennes Bay, Totten Glacier (36) and Denman Glacier (37), which explains their retreat (19). However, there are few data east of 145°E, e.g., in front of the Cook Ice Shelf or the Ninnis Glacier (38). Detailed oceanography surveys in these regions will be essential to document the potential pathways for warm water to reach the GL (36).

In the Antarctic Peninsula, CDW floods the west coast of AP, except for shallower sectors such as the northern sector of George VI Ice Shelf (SI Appendix, Fig. S26), which explains its lack of retreat (39). The CDW detected in the Bellingshausen Sea, along the Belgica trough, connects with the Fox-Ferrigno glacier system to explain its retreat (SI Appendix, Fig. S26). Abbot and Cosgrove Ice Shelves may not be affected by CDW due to a shallower seafloor (26), which explains their lack of retreat (SI Appendix, Fig. S26). CDW is well documented on Pine Island, Thwaites, Pope/Smith/Kohler (40), and the Getz Ice Shelf (41). The retreat of the Hull and Land glaciers suggests the presence of CDW. There are no bathymetry data in front of these glaciers to reveal potential pathways for CDW and few ocean temperature/salinity data to verify the hypothesis of the presence of CDW (SI Appendix, Fig. S27). Further west, CDW may be blocked by a shallower seafloor beneath Nickerson Ice Shelf (SI Appendix, Fig. S26), which would explain its lack of retreat, but bathymetric coverage is sparse in that sector (SI Appendix, Fig. S27).

On the east coast of the Antarctic Peninsula, the ocean is much colder than on the west coast. Many glaciers have undergone retreat through rapid calving consecutive to ice shelf collapses or a sudden loss of thick multiyear ice melange (42). The glaciers redeveloped small floating sections that enabled calving of icebergs through buoyancy mechanisms. As mentioned earlier, bathymetric coverage is limited in this sector, which limits our understanding of glacier evolution. This sector of Antarctica is the only area of retreat that is not conditioned by CDW. The role of various forcings on the progressive collapse of its glaciers and ice shelves, i.e., warmer air temperature, decreased sea ice cover and ice melange, and warmer ocean temperature, have not been fully elucidated.

The absence of retreat in Amery, Ross, Filchner, and Ronne ice shelves is consistent with their broad separation from warm CDW (SI Appendix, Fig. S26). On the Fimbul Ice Shelf, shallow sills limit the access of cold Weddell deep water to the GL (43). Similarly, the ice shelves on Queen Maud Land are fringed by shallow sills and ice rises, which must limit the intrusions of CDW, consistent with their stability (44). The stability of Shirase Glacier is more surprising given the presence of CDW at +0.14 °C at its front and ice shelf melt rates in the range of 7 to 16 m/y (45). We posit that shallow bathymetry must be present in front of the glacier to limit the access of ocean heat into the cavity.

Similarly, the glaciers in Porpoise Bay have thinned (4), but long-term retreat has been modest. We attribute it to the fact that the glaciers are not deeply grounded, develop short floating extensions (Frost), with many pinning points (De Haven), or the GL is stabilized on a ridge (Holmes). Ocean models suggest colder waters as a result of a shallower seafloor, but bathymetric coverage is incomplete in that sector (SI Appendix, Fig. S27). We suspect that Porpoise Bay has shallow seafloor depths, but it may also include deeper, incised channels capable of hosting warm waters, as in the case of Totten Glacier (36).

The GL retreat rates in West Antarctica are among the fastest in the world. Marine-terminating glaciers in Alaska retreated at 0.6 km/y for Columbia Glacier in 1980–2011 (46) and 0.5 km/y for Leconte Glacier in 1994–1998 (47). The Alpine glaciers retreated 39 km2/y in 2000–2014, or 12 times less than Antarctica (48). In Greenland, Jakobshavn retreated 0.6 km/y from 1996 to 2016 (49). The retreat of GL in West Antarctica is 2 to 3 times higher, demonstrating a high sensitivity to climate change. Antarctic glaciers retreat at 0.3 to 0.6 km/y on prograde slopes versus 1 to 3.6 km/y on retrograde slopes, i.e., a factor 6. More recently, glacier calving fronts in the Antarctic Peninsula have retreated even faster, up to 8 km in a single year upstream Larsen B Ice Shelf (42).

The pin points detected on many ice shelves are synonymous with stability because they provide resistance to flow (33) and imply shallow water columns, making them less favorable for intrusions of subsurface ocean heat. Ice shelves stabilized in this manner include all the ice shelves in Queen Maud Land, Fimbul, Riiser-Larsen, Stancomb-Wills, West, Shackleton, and Voyeykov ice shelves, and Dibble Glacier, to name a few.

GL retreat has been deduced from ice thinning in the past (50). The loss of grounded Antarctic ice has been estimated at 1,463 km2, or 243 km2 per year for the period 2010–2016. Although we cannot compare our results over the exact same time period, our rate of ungrounding of 442 ± 64 km2 per year is twice as high and also different in terms of spatial distribution. The retreat may have been smaller in 2010–2016 compared to the 1990s and 2000s. In addition, GLs are located in areas that deviate by many meters from hydrostatic equilibrium, introducing uncertainties in thicknesses of up to 100 m (51) and uncertainties in flotation. The break in surface slope used to infer the position of the GL is more reliable on slow moving ice, but less so on fast moving and crevassed glaciers (5). We do not detect migration or advance over 77% of Antarctica, in contrast to an estimated spectrum of advances and retreats inferred from short-term changes in ice surface elevation (50). We conclude that inferring GL positions and changes in GL positions from altimetry data is not as robust as previously thought.

GL migration and ice sheet mass balance are interrelated. Sectors exhibiting GL retreat should not be in a state of mass balance. In contrast, sectors with stable GL should be in a state of mass balance. Indeed, we find that the sectors that exhibit GL retreat are all a mass deficit in ref. 1. We do not find an area stated to be in a state of negative mass balance where the GL is stable. Similarly, we do not detect the retreat of GL in all the East Antarctic sectors estimated to be in a state of mass balance in ref. 1. This agreement contrasts with time series of altimetry data that report thickening in parts of East Antarctica (4), especially along the coast. This discrepancy confirms that the relationship between GL migration and changes in ice elevation is more complex than assumed.

Residual uncertainties in mapping GL retreat in Antarctica are caused by a sparse historical record, especially for the Ross, Filchner/Ronne, and Amery ice shelves and in Queen Mary and Princess Elizabeth Lands. In addition, many current SAR missions have long repeat passes, making it difficult to detect GLs, especially for the fast moving glaciers which are most important for the overall ice sheet mass balance. However, this gap has been closing in recent years with the emergence of SAR constellations capable of fast repeat cycles, e.g., ICEYE, CSK, and RCM.

Future retreat is likely to continue along overdeepened beds filled with CDW, e.g. Ferrigno Ice Stream, Pine Island, Piglet, Smith West, Berry glaciers, in West Antarctica; and especially Denman, Cook Ice Shelf, Ninnis in East Antarctica, to name a few. Further south, if warm water could eventually reach them, the Bailey, Slessor, and Recovery ice streams would be highly vulnerable to retreat because they drain a vast marine-based sector along retrograde slopes.

Although the theory of marine ice sheet instability predicts stable GL on prograde slopes, we find that many glaciers continue to retreat along prograde slopes. Examples include Thwaites Glacier currently temporarily held by Mouginot Ridge (11), Kohler, Pope, Haynes (27), Hull glaciers, Moscow University Ice Shelf, and Totten Glacier (36). We conclude that the presence of prograde slopes does not imply GL stability and lack of retreat. It will be important for ice sheet models in charge of projecting sea level rise to take these observations of retreat along prograde slopes into account.

The ongoing retreat of West Antarctica glaciers documents a process of marine ice sheet in which the retreat rates reach a few kilometers per year (10). The more modest but significant retreat of glaciers in Wilkes and George V lands, in East Antarctica, confirms that this sector is an important participant in the future of the continent (1). The poleward shift in westerlies has already conditioned an increase in ocean temperature and ice shelf melt rates that will continue to affect these sectors of East and West Antarctica for decades to come (39, 41, 52).

Materials and Methods

SAR Missions.

We use interferometric synthetic-aperture radar (InSAR) observations from several missions. The European Space Agency (ESA) Earth Remote Sensing ERS-1 collected repeat-pass interferometry at the C-band frequency (5.3 GHz) in 1992 and 1994 (3 d repeat). ERS-1 and ERS-2 acquired 1-d repeat data in October 1995 to May 1996. ERS-1 acquired 3-d repeat data at the end of its mission in 2000. ERS-2 acquired 3-day repeat data at the end of its mission in 2011. ESA launched Sentinel-1a (S1a) in 2014, followed by Sentinel-1b (S1b) in 2016 which acquired SAR data at the C-band frequency (5.4 GHz) with a 6 to 12 d repeat until S1b ceased operations in 2021. S1c, launched in December 2024, collected data at a one day repeat with S1a for one month over selected tracks. The Japanese Space Agency (JAXA) launched the ALOS PALSAR in 2006 to acquire data at the L-band frequency (1.27 GHz) at a 44 d repeat until 2011. In 2014, JAXA launched ALOS-2 PALSAR-2 (1.24 GHz) at a 14-d repeat cycle. The Canadian Space Agency (CSA) launched RADARSAT-1 in 1995 to collect data at a 24-d repeat cycle at the C-band frequency (5.4 GHz) until 2013; RADARSAT-2 in 2007 with the same repeat cycle with a left (i.e. south) looking capability; and the RADARSAT Constellation Mission (RCM) in 2019 at a 4-d repeat cycle which acquired data in Antarctica starting in 2023. The Agenzia Spaziale Italiana (ASI) launched the CSK2 and CSK3 satellite part of the CosmoSkyMed Constellation in 2007 and 2008, which acquired 1-d repeat track data at the X-band frequency (9.60 GHz) from 2015 to 2021 after which we no longer have access to data. ICEYE has been launching satellites since 2020 that operate at the X-band frequency (9.65 GHz). Several assets have the capability to collect one-day ground track repeat data. For each SAR, we form interferometric pairs at the nominal repeat cycle and difference consecutive pairs to obtain a differential interferogram, or DInSAR, that measures the differential displacement between two consecutive InSAR pairs.

SAR Interferometry measures the line of sight displacement of the ice surface over a repeat cycle, combined with topography. We remove the topographic signal using a digital elevation model of Antarctica. In a differential interferogram, or DInSAR, where we difference two InSAR pairs spanning the same time interval, the steady component of the glacier horizontal motion cancels out, leaving only the motion associated with tidal flexure of the ice, subsidence/uplift caused by changes in subglacial water pressure, short-term changes in horizontal flow, horizontal motion of the ice associated with bending, or data noise. When the glacier accelerates, the assumption of steady state breaks down, but the pattern of deformation will resemble that of horizontal motion, not of tidal flexure, which can be detected (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). To mitigate the effect of changes in horizontal motion, we use nearest neighbor InSAR pairs.

GL detection is not performed using the wrapped interferometric phases (or 360° change in phase) (2). We do not detect the hinge line using an automatic fitting of the deformation with an elastic plate model because this approach tends to misplace the GL too far inland, by about one ice thickness. Instead, we detect the first interferometric fringe in the seaward direction. For S1, we use a Machine Learning (ML) algorithm to map the GL (8) in 2018–2020, followed by a manual filtering, with a precision similar to that of a human expert (8). For the time period 2018–2020, we compile all GL picks into an IGZ envelope and report the inland and seaward position of the IGZ.

At the C-band frequency, we get one fringe of deformation (360° phase cycle) for every 28 mm of line of sight displacement (wavelength λ divided by two). This ratio decreases by a factor 4 at the L-band frequency but increases by a factor 2 at the X-band, i.e., for the same tidal signal, we get 4 times less fringes at the L-band and 2 times more at the X-band. The operating frequency of the radar does not influence the detection of GL, which is based on a differential motion of the ice surface, but it affects the signal noise ratio and the precision of detection. Precision is highest at X-band and lowest at L-band.

Retreat rates are characterized by two numbers. First, we quantify the maximum retreat along a center flow line between the most retreated position in the 1990s and the most retreated position in the last acquisition (2021 to 2025). Additionally, we calculate the area of retreat between the most retreated GL position in the 1990s and the most retreated position in the last acquisition.

The error in GL positioning is ±500 m. We pick the GL position manually within 100 m, but we do not correct it for tides, changes in atmospheric pressure or Inverse Barometer Effect (IBE), and horizontal motion associated with bending stress (6). Corrections for tide and IBE require a detailed knowledge of the surface and basal slopes, which is not always available. The correction also assumes that ice is in hydrostatic equilibrium, which is an approximation. For ice bending stress effects, the Euler–Bernoulli beam displacement theory shows that the vertical displacement is w(x)=A(t)[1−exp−βx(cos(βx)+sin(βx))], for an infinite plate grounded at x=0 and forced by a tidal amplitude, A(t); where β is the flexural wavenumber. The horizontal displacement is u(x)=−HβA(t)exp−βxsin(βx), where H is ice thickness. For small x values, w(x)≈2A(t)β2x and u(x)≈−Hβ2A(t)x. The line of sight displacement measured with InSAR is δr=−w(x)cos(α)+u(x)sin(α), where α is the incidence angle of the radar illumination away from vertical. Ignoring the horizontal motion, u(x), caused by bending when analyzing the displacement, δr, results in an apparent misplacement of the hinge line by a quantity δx=tan(α)u(x)/w(x)=−(H/2) tan(α). The negative sign indicates that the hinge line is systematically shifted inland, independent of the tide A(t), by half the ice thickness times by the tangent of the incidence angle. Here, α varies from 23.5° (ERS-1/2) to 44° (RADARSAT), with the bulk of values at 35°, hence δx≈ 0.2 to 0.5 times H, with the bulk at 1/3. For GL thickness ranging from 600 to 1,500 m, the bias δx ranges from 100 to 700 m. We do not apply this thickness-dependent correction which would require detailed knowledge of ice thickness at every point along the coastline and fitting the results with a three-dimensional elastic plate model to detect the hinge line. Instead, we simply assume that δx=±500 m is the nominal error for GL picking.

The error in area of GL retreat is the length, L, of the rectangular polygon that defines the area of retreat multiplied by δx (Table 1). GL positions that vary by less than 1 km in thirty years are assumed to be stable. To calculate the area of retreat, we convert the EPSG 4326 (latitude, longitude, WGS84) GL positions into EPSG 6932 (x, y, WGS 84, NSIDC EASE-Grid 2.0 South, a Lambert Azimuthal Equal Area centered at South Pole). We scale the result in time to a common reference period of 1996–2025 (observations vary from 1992–1996 to 2020–2025).

Each GL element has attributes of sensor name, orbit numbers (3 to 4), dates (3 to 4), Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) in seconds (center time of reference scene), track number, filename of the interferogram (internal use only), calendar year, and glacier/subregion name. ICEYE does not keep an official track number. We calculate a pseudo track number based on the orbit number and the repeat cycle (15 orbits per cycle); the orbit number is the difference in time (days) between acquisition and launch divided by 15 orbits per day. The reference time makes it possible to correct the data for tide and IBE a posteriori, if needed.
