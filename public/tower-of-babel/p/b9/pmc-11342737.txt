Hospital case fatality and mortality related to Chagas disease in Brazil over two decades
Authors: Eliana Amorim de Souza, Marly Marques da Cruz, Anderson Fuentes Ferreira, Andrea Silvestre de Sousa, Ronir Raggio Luiz, Swamy Lima Palmeira, Alejandro Ostermayer Luquetti, Jorg Heukelbach, Alberto Novaes Ramos
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo analyse hospital case fatality and mortality related to Chagas disease (CD) in Brazil, 2000–2019.MethodThis is a mixed ecological study with spatial and temporal trends, based on national population data from the Brazilian Ministry of Health - hospital admissions (HA) and death certificates (DC). Records with CD as a primary or secondary cause of death in HA and/or as an underlying or associated cause of death in DC were evaluated. Temporal trends were analysed by Joinpoint regression and the spatial distribution of age- and gender-adjusted rates, spatial moving averages, and standardized morbidity ratios.ResultsThere were a total of 4,376 HA due to CD resulting in death in Brazil, with a hospital case fatality rate of 0.11/100,000 inhabitants. The Southeast region had the highest rate (63.9%, n = 2,796; 0.17/100,000 inhabitants). The general trend for this indicator in Brazil is upwards (average annual percentage change [AAPC] 7.5; 95% confidence interval [CI] 5.3 to 9.9), with increases in the North, Northeast and Southeast regions. During the same period 122,275 deaths from CD were registered in DC, with a mortality rate of 3.14/100,000 inhabitants. The highest risk of CD-related death was found among men (relative risk [RR] 1.27) and Afro-Brazilians (RR 1.63). There was a downward trend in CD mortality in the country (AAPC − 0.7%, 95%CI -0.9 to -0.5), with an increase in the Northeast region (AAPC 1.1%, 95%CI 0.6 to 1.6). Municipalities with a very high Brazilian Deprivation Index tended to show an increase in mortality (AAPC 2.1%, 95%CI 1.6 to 2.7), while the others showed a decrease.ConclusionHospital case fatality and mortality due to CD are a relevant public health problem in Brazil. Differences related to gender, ethnicity, and social vulnerability reinforce the need for comprehensive care, and to ensure equity in access to health in the country. Municipalities, states, and regions with indicators that reveal higher morbidity and mortality need to be prioritized.Keywords: Chagas disease, Hospitalization, Mortality, Case fatality, Spatio-temporal analysis

Introduction

Worldwide, about 8 million people, mainly in Latin America, are infected by Trypanosoma cruzi, most of them are in the chronic phase of Chagas disease (CD) [1]. Although considered an infectious disease with a potentially curable acute phase, CD’s greatest burden of morbidity and mortality lies in its complications, mainly cardiac or digestive, requiring lifelong healthcare [2]. The analysis of the Disability-Adjusted Life Year (DALY) indicator for heart disease in young and economically productive adults in endemic countries in Latin America underscores the relevance of CD as a public health problem, leading to a high social cost, especially in populations of greater social vulnerability [3].

Similar to other neglected tropical diseases (NTDs), poverty is one of the social determinants that increases not only the risk of T. cruzi infection (approximately 40,000 new cases annually worldwide), but also the progression of the disease due to critical limitations in access to health care, leading to disability, reduced quality of life, and preventable deaths [1, 4]. The importance of contextual factors, such as primary health care coverage, in the outcome of worse clinical prognosis is also emphasized [4–6]. Only 10% of people with CD have access to diagnosis and less than 1% of those who need antiparasitic treatment are treated [2, 7]. Approximately 10,000 CD-related deaths occur annually worldwide [1]. This scenario has been substantially aggravated since 2020, due to the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic and the impact of Covid-19, leading to increased poverty worldwide and critical restrictions on access to national healthcare systems [1, 8, 9]. This limitation to comprehensive care occurs at all levels of care, from primary health care (PHC) to medium and high complexity care, including reference hospitals [10].

In Brazil, CD represents one of the four leading causes of death from infectious diseases, being the leading NTD in terms of morbidity and mortality burden. It is estimated that between 1.9 and 4.6 million people are infected with T. cruzi [3, 11]. There is a clear need to expand access to diagnosis, treatment, and care of Chagas disease in the Unified Health System (In Portuguese: Sistema Único de Saúde [SUS]), based on the integration of health care and surveillance activities in municipalities of the country [1, 2, 12].

Despite its relevance, chronic CD was included as a notifiable disease in the country only in 2020 [13], limiting its tracking to acute phase events. Beyond population estimates, analyses of databases such as the Mortality Information System (In Portuguese: Sistema de Informação sobre Mortalidade [SIM]) and the SUS Hospital Information System (In Portuguese: Sistema de Informações Hospitalares [SIH-SUS]) have been relevant, even with their limitations [14], as they enable direct and indirect assessments of different dimensions of access to health in the SUS care network [15]. In the case of SIH-SUS, by integrating records of Hospital Admissions (HA) from the public hospital network and those affiliated with SUS throughout the country, there are perspectives for epidemiological and operational analyses. In addition to research on public hospital care and health surveillance, it opens up opportunities for health economic analyses, as well as supplementing mortality estimates and disease detection [15, 16].

The hospital care network plays a central role in the management of more severe cases or complications in the acute or chronic phase, or in the presence of more complex comorbidities, such as decompensated non-infectious chronic-degenerative diseases or HIV infection [2, 17]. Just in 2018, 609 Hospital Admission Authorisations (HAA) were registered with a primary diagnosis of CD, 294 (48.3%) with cardiac involvement and 205 (33.7%) with involvement of the digestive system [18], but likely underestimating the actual numbers [16].

However, the historical regional inequality of access to hospital beds in Brazilian municipalities [15, 19] has led to a lack of opportunity for care for more complex cases. Equity, the guiding principle of the SUS, should imply the absence of barriers, particularly in contexts of greater vulnerability [20, 21]. Thus, significant gaps persist in in Brazil, a problem that is exacerbated by the country’s vast territorial dimension associated with the concentration of services in municipalities with larger populations and higher gross domestic product [19].

Comparative analyses of deaths recorded between information systems increase the evidence to guide public management in decision-making for health action planning, including health surveillance based on hospital morbidity/mortality in the municipality or state level. Thus, this study seeks to analyse hospital case fatality and mortality related to CD in Brazil from 2000 to 2019, to generate evidence that contributes to the strengthening of agendas based on the guidelines of universality, equity and comprehensive care in the SUS.

Methods

Study design

This is a mixed ecological study with a national population base, focusing on the outcome of death from CD based on analyses of temporal trends and spatial distribution in the period from 2000 to 2019. The study aimed to exclude the Covid-19 pandemic period to avoid any effects related to the reduction of coverage in the SUS care network that could mask patterns and trends [9]. Secondary databases from SIH-SUS were analysed, using variables contained in the HAA, and SIM, from the Death Certificate (DC). The analyses included identifying hospitalizations due to CD that resulted in death, representing hospital case fatality. To compose a comparative baseline assessment, the SIM database was used to verify the occurrence of deaths whose underlying or associated cause was associated with CD for the same period.

Study area

The study was conducted in Brazil, the largest country in territorial and population extension in South America, with an area of 8.5 million km² and an estimated population of 213.3 million inhabitants (population density of 24.9 inhabitants per km²) in 2021. Brazil is a federal republic composed of 26 states, 5,570 municipalities and the Federal District, integrating the five regions of the country (North, Northeast, Southeast, South and Central-West) (Fig. 1), with great social, environmental, economic and cultural diversity, as well as in the organisation of public health policies and services network [22].

Study area: Brazilian States and Regions

Data from the 2010 Brazilian census indicate that Brazil has a Gini index of 0.60, expressing critical inequalities, particularly in the North and Northeast regions (Gini of 0.62). Brazilian per capita income in 2010 was USD 481.13 per month, being lower in the Northeast (USD 286.30) and North (USD 311.76) regions. Life expectancy at birth was 74 years in 2010, being lowest in the Northeast (72 years) and North (73 years) regions; the other regions have life expectancy close to or above 76 years [23].

The 2019 Continuous National Household Sample Survey (In Portuguese: Pesquisa Nacional por Amostra de Domicílios Contínua [PNAD]) showed highest proportions of the population in the age groups 25 to 39 years (23.1%) and 40 to 59 years (26.2%). The North (22.5%) and Northeast (19.9%) regions had the highest proportions in the population aged 5 to 17 (young people), while the South (17.4%) and Southeast (17.1%) regions had the highest proportions of older population (60 years or older) [24], which has repercussions on the distribution of general hospitals [25].

Data sources

Data referring to HAA and DC from 2000 to 2019 were obtained from the Department of Informatics of the Unified Health System of Brazil (In Portuguese: Departamento de Informática do Sistema Único de Saúde do Brasil [DATASUS]) of the Ministry of Health (MoH) from the SIH-SUS and SIM databases [26]. The two systems represent decentralised platforms in all municipalities of Brazil, whose databases are publicly available via DATASUS/MoH using as unit of analysis HA and deaths, respectively.

SIH-SUS was implemented in 1991 in Brazil, providing a set of variables related to HA events occurring in public or contracted units of the SUS (75% of HA in the country) with eminently administrative and financial objectives [19]. The HAA is the standardised instrument for registration in the SIH-SUS, based on medical records at hospital discharge, containing a list of diagnoses that led to HA, coded according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems in its tenth revision (ICD-10). As there is no distinction/personal identification, the same person may have one or more HA records in the database. In this study, all HA with CD as primary or secondary cause were included.

On the other hand, the SIM started in the country in 1975, digitally in 1979, and it became decentralised after the SUS implementation as a strategic tool for health management. [27].

The selection of HAA and DC was based on ICD-10, in which CD was recorded, respectively, as primary or secondary causes of hospitalisation followed by death, and as underlying or associated cause of death. The following codes were used: B57 - Chagas disease; B57.0 - Acute Chagas disease with heart involvement; B57.1 - Acute Chagas disease without heart involvement; B57.2 - Chagas disease (chronic) with heart involvement; B57.3 - Chagas disease (chronic) with digestive system involvement; B57.4 - Chagas disease (chronic) with nervous system involvement, B57.5 - Chagas disease (chronic) with other organ involvement, K231 - Megaesophagus due to Chagas disease and K931 - Megacolon in Chagas disease [28]. Both systems also have individualised sociodemographic data.

General population data were obtained from the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (In Portuguese: Instituto Brasileiro de Geografia e Estatística [IBGE]) by DATASUS, with population censuses reference of the country of 2000 and 2010, in addition to population estimates by projections for the intercensal periods (2001–2009 and 2011–2019) made available by municipality, containing demographic information such as age and gender [29].

Data from SIH-SUS and SIM were downloaded in “.dbc” format by state and year and later combined and saved in “.dfb” format by TabWin® 4.15 software (DATASUS/MoH). Stata® software, version 11.2 (Statacorp, College Station, Texas, EUA), was used for database management, record selection and statistical analyses [30].

Descriptive analysis

The analysis included the calculation of the following indicators: 1- crude rate of HA due to CD; 2- hospital case fatality rate due to CD; and 3- mortality rate due to CD with deaths registered in the DC (taking as reference the average of the period in relation to the population of 2010, per 100,000 inhabitants).

Simple and relative frequencies were also calculated, including null values, for the following variables: gender (male and female); ethnicity (Caucasian, Afro-Brazilian/Afro-descendant, Asian-descendant, Mixed/Pardo Brazilians], and indigenous [Amerindians]); region of residence (North, Northeast, Southeast, South and Central-West); type of municipality compatible with the National Health Survey (In Portuguese: Pesquisa Nacional de Saúde [PNS]) [31] and stratification index of priority territories for chronic CD surveillance (Very low, Low, Medium, High, Very high) [12]; and the Brazilian Index of Deprivation (In Portuguese: Índice Brasileiro de Privação [IBP]) with reference to the year 2010 (Very low, Low, Medium, High, Very high) [32].

With the use of PNS, the aim was to better characterise the territory from a regionalised integration perspective, providing an alternative to the limited analysis in five macro-regions, making it possible to evaluate municipalities in different categories according to size and level of aggregation: large and aggregated (Metropolitan Region [MR], Integrated Development Region of the Federal District and Surrounding Area [IDRS] or Agglomerates [AGGL]); medium and aggregated (MR, IDRS or AGGL); small and aggregated (MR, IDRS or AGGL); large and isolated; medium and isolated; small and isolated.

Additionally, for the characterisation of the territory from a local (municipal) perspective, the definition by population size was used: “small”, with a population of less than 50,000 inhabitants and a demographic density of less than 80 inhabitants/km², “medium” with a population between 50,000 and 100,000 inhabitants and a demographic density greater than or equal to 80 inhabitants/km², and “large” with a population greater than 100,000 inhabitants, regardless of their demographic density.

The IBP was used as an additional measure to indicate levels of material deprivation or socioeconomic position in different geographical areas of Brazil (in the case of this study, the municipality). Its calculation is based on data on income (proportion of households with per capita income below ½ minimum wage), schooling (proportion of people with no schooling aged seven years or more) and household conditions (proportion of households with inadequate access to basic sanitation and without piped water, rubbish collection, toilet and bathroom), with reference to the 2010 Census [32, 33].

The Brazilian Ministry of Health index for stratifying priority areas for chronic CD surveillance was also included. It consists of three sub-indices. The first one refers to indicators related to chronic CD, including self-reported registrations for CD in the Primary Care Information System, CD mortality rate in the age group up to 49 years and CD mortality rate after 50 years of age. The second sub-index pertains to sentinel indicators, including heart failure hospitalisation rate [ICD-10: I50.0, I50.1 or I50.9] and sudden cardiac death rate [ICD-10: I46.1]). Finally, indicators related to access, including estimated population coverage by PHC teams, ratio of medium-complexity outpatient procedures related to CD and resident population, ratio of medium-complexity outpatient procedures related to CD and resident population up to 49 years, the ratio of medium-complexity outpatient procedures related to CD and resident population aged 50 years and over, the proportion of hospitalizations for heart failure with IgG serology for T. cruzi [12].

For a better representation of the time series, a two (2)-point moving average was used, thus reducing variations and smoothing year-to-year rates, for both hospital case fatality and mortality from CD. Age- and gender-adjusted rates were calculated year by year for the analysed indicators for regions and states. For the states, those with the highest rates were presented. Data for the Brazilian regions were presented using graphs to represent the time series.

To recognize possible differences related to demographic variables, considering the analysed indicators, the relative risk (RR) using Pearson’s chi-square test (χ2).

Temporal analysis

For the analysis of temporal trends, Poisson regression by join points was used, with statistical significance determined by the Monte Carlo permutation method to verify the best-fit line in each segment. Subsequently, annual percentage change (APC) and the average annual percentage change (AAPC) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) were validated and tested. Based on these two parameters, patterns of temporal trends were established, including reduction trends (negative APC/AAPC and statistical significance), growth trends (positive APC/AAPC and statistical significance), or absence of trends (APC/AAPC equal to zero and without statistical significance) [34]. Joinpoint Regression Program Regression® software version 4.4.2 was used for this analysis.

Spatial analysis

To analyse the spatial distribution of morbidity and mortality indicators (municipalities with hospital case fatality from CD and deaths from the disease reported in DC), adjustments were made by age and gender using the direct method, considering the age-gender structure of the 2010 Brazilian census (per 100,000 inhabitants), in the periods 2000–2003, 2004–2007, 2008–2011, 2012–2015, and 2016–2019.

The natural breaks method by Jenks was used to define the spatial classes of municipalities related to the indicators under analysis, adjusted for age and gender as well as the spatial moving average (per 100,000 inhabitants). The unit of spatial analysis was the municipality of residence (n = 5,570) and unknown municipalities and/or those with invalid coding were excluded.

Ethical considerations

The project was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Instituto Nacional de Infectologia Evandro Chagas - Fundação Oswaldo Cruz (INI/Fiocruz), number 4.090.954, CAAE: 229955020.5.1001.5262. Since this research exclusively utilizes public secondary data, available through free access to official websites of the Ministry of Health and with anonymized identification data of hospitalizations and/or deaths, the principles of Resolution no. 466 of December 2012, of the National Health Council of Brazil, with research involving human beings, were obeyed [35].

Results

Mortality and case fatality rates

The results were presented in an integrated manner, with a particular focus on CD case fatality and mortality rates in hospital institutions based on the SIH-SUS analysis. In addition, these results were contextualized with the general CD-specific mortality data contained in the SIM for a broader analysis.

During the period 2000–2019, a total of 228,900,767 HA was recorded in Brazil via SIH-SUS. In 59,419 (0.01%) cases, CD was mentioned as a primary or secondary cause. Of this total, 92.6% (n = 55,043) were discharged from the hospital, 7.4% (n = 4,376) died during hospitalization and 3.0% (n = 1,050) were transferred to other hospital units. In this same period, 122,275/22,663,091 (0.54%) deaths from CD were recorded (via SIM), a difference of 96.4% (n = 117,899) between this death record and those captured via SIH-SUS.

A higher proportion of males was hospitalized and died during HI (52.6%; n = 2,302). We observed a high proportion of HA with missing data for ethnicity (52.3%; n = 2,287). There was a higher proportion of HA in CD cases located in the Southeast region (63.9%; n = 2,796); living in large and aggregated municipalities (34.9%; n = 1,526), with a high index of the stratification of priority territories for chronic CD surveillance (41.8%, n = 1,827), and in municipalities with low IBP (30.4%; n = 1,332).

Noteworthy the risk ratio for HA events was higher in males (RR 1.15), inhabitants of the Southeast region (RR 5.54), and of those living in a large and isolated municipality (RR 1.67), with a stratification index of priority territories for chronic CD surveillance very high (RR 11.67), in addition to municipalities with low IBP (RR 1.16).

Among the records of deaths related to CD (via SIM), there was a higher proportion of males (55.0%; n = 67,232); Caucasians (44.1%, n = 53,937); inhabitants in the Southeast region (51.0%; n = 62,308); people living in small and isolated municipalities (31.2%; n = 38,183), stratification index of priority territories for chronic CD surveillance very high (43.5%, n = 53,202); and in municipalities with low (24%; n = 29,396) or medium (21.8%; n = 26,650) IBP.

There was also a higher risk of death from CD in males (RR 1.27), Afro-Brazilians (RR 1.63), individuals living in the Central-West region (RR 15.51), living in a small and aggregated municipality (RR 1.73), with a stratification index of priority territories for chronic CD surveillance that were very high (RR 37.21), and municipalities with low IBP (RR 1.48) (Table 1).

Temporal trend

The trend for hospital case fatality for CD in the study period shows different inflection points, with a significant increase at the end, during the period 2011–2019 (APC 14.5; 95%CI 10.3 to 20.8). For the full period, there was a significant upward trend (AAPC 7.5; 95%CI 5.3 to 9.9). There was a significant increase in men (AAPC 6.8; 95%CI 4.7 to 9.2) and more markedly in women (AAPC 8.4; 95%CI 6.0 to 11.8).

No trends in hospital case fatality were identified for the South (AAPC 5.2, 95%CI -3.0 to 17.0) and Central-West regions (AAPC − 0.3, 95%CI -3.2 to 2.9), while the trend was upward for the others. No trends were identified for medium and aggregated municipalities (AAPC − 3.5, 95%CI -9.1 to 2.1) and small and aggregated municipalities (AAPC 2.5, 95%CI -1.7 to 9.0). For the remaining categories there was an increasing trend. All categories of IBP showed a definite upward trend, with emphasis on those with medium IBP (AAPC 8.6; 95%CI 4.0 to 17.3).

For CD mortality, there was a single temporal trend of reduction (AAPC − 0.7%, 95%CI -0.9 to -0.5), a pattern maintained in males (AAPC − 1.3, 95%CI -1.5 to -1.1), since among women there was no trend (AAPC 0.0, 95%CI -0.2 to 0.2). All ethnicity categories showed a reduction, with the exception of Afro-Brazilians (AAPC 0.3, 95%CI -0.1 to 0.7) and indigenous populations (AAPC − 0.3, 95%CI -3.8 to 3.6), which showed no defined trends.

The Northeast region (AAPC 1.1, 95%CI 0.6 to 1.6) was the only one with an increase in the analysed period, while the North region (AAPC 0.5, 95%CI -0.8 to 2.5) did not show a defined trend. The others showed a downward trend. Large and aggregated municipalities (AAPC − 1.1, 95%CI -1.4 to -0.7) and large and isolated municipalities (AAPC − 1.1, 95%CI -1.3 to -0.9) showed a downward trend, while the other categories had no defined trend. Municipalities with very high IBP showed an increasing trend (AAPC 2.1, 95%CI 1.6 to 2.7), while the other categories showed a decreasing trend, particularly in municipalities with very low IBP (AAPC − 1.9, 95%CI -2.2 to -1.5).

For the stratification index of priority territories for chronic CD surveillance, no trend was found for hospital case fatality in the very low (AAPC − 3.2, 95%CI -7.7 to 1.1), the others showed an increasing trend. In mortality, reduction trends were observed in those with high (AAPC − 0.8, 95%CI -1.1 to -0.6) and very high (AAPC − 0.6, 95%CI -0.8 to -0.4), while the others did not show a trend for the entire period (Table 2).

Spatial distribution

There were heterogeneous spatial distribution patterns in the Brazilian territory. For the hospital case fatality rate, there was a concentration of high rates in municipalities in the states of Goiás, Minas Gerais, São Paulo, Bahia and Pernambuco, particularly in the periods 2000–2003 and 2016–2019 (Fig. 3). The highest CD mortality rates were found in municipalities in the north and east of the state of Goiás, north and west of Minas Gerais, east of the state of Bahia and south of the state of Piauí, indicating a more focused pattern of occurrence, with little variation over the periods evaluated (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The analysis of two extensive national databases, over a period of two decades, one containing nearly 230 million HA records, and another with almost 23 million of deaths, generated significant evidence to CD in Brazil. The findings of this study reinforce the relevance of CD as a chronic infectious condition for public health, both for its mortality burden and for the existence of limited access to health care of affected people, in the present study indirectly translated from HA.

Approximately 7% of the almost 60,000 HA due to CD in the country died, i.e., almost 5,000 people in total. This aspect may not reflect the potential severity of CD in more advanced stages. These cases may not have access to hospital care (e.g., sudden death), but may have compensated cardiac involvement or procedures for implantation of pacemakers and other cardiac devices [36]. In addition, chronic digestive forms of the disease are also associated with hospital death [16]. On the other hand, it is undeniable that the deaths occurred during the almost 3,000 HA annually due to CD corresponds to less than 3% of those recorded in the SIM. The significant difference between the number of deaths officially declared in the SIM and those recorded in AIH, in addition to the shortcomings in the completion and coverage of the SIH-SUS, may indicate differing access to hospital care, justifying the possible underestimation [37].

Existing barriers throughout the clinical course of the disease may limit access to timely diagnosis and treatment in the SUS, particularly in PHC. This condition may generate more complex clinical demands for higher levels of technological density and cost due to the chronic form of the disease complications, particularly chronic chagasic heart disease or digestive system diseases. [10, 16, 38, 39].

Over the two decades analysed, there was an average of more than 6,000 deaths each year, with an unimpressive downward trend for disease mortality, in addition to the absence of a defined trend for hospital case fatality. These findings reaffirm the relevance of CD as one of the diseases generating highest burden of mortality and disability in Brazil [8] and the leading cause of non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy in Latin America [1, 2, 7, 37, 40]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to improve early diagnosis of T. cruzi infection, etiological treatment and comprehensive and quality clinical management as essential measures to achieve a better quality of life [2]. These needs are amplified when considering that in endemic areas, sudden death is an event frequently associated with CD, a fact that may influence the occurrence of death without care at hospital level [36, 38, 41]. Clearly, CD early diagnosis and treatment will improve survival rates.

There is also a need for systematic evaluations of access to hospital care in Brazil, with CD [42, 43] bringing into perspective a disease strongly associated with poverty [8]. The dimensions of vulnerability associated with CD in this study included a higher risk of death among males. This finding is also verified when analysing the notification of acute cases in Brazil from 2007 to 2019 (74.5% male) [18] and in records of T. cruzi and HIV co-infection cases (69.9%). Additionally, in a study of a historical series of CD deaths in Brazil, there was an independent association between mortality and male gender [44]. In addition to biological conditions associated with a higher risk of disease progression in males, which justifies its inclusion in various risk scores for cardiovascular events, these findings can also be explained by historical and cultural gender conditions in the country that have repercussions on differences in self-care and access to health services. In addition, the way of health services is organised, with little target at this population, increases their institutional vulnerability, including the differentiated geographical distribution [45].

The identification of an increased risk of CD mortality among African-descendant people, as well as the absence of a downward trend in death in these individuals, as opposed to that recognised among Caucasians, is likely to indicate a condition of socially constituted vulnerability. Other studies that assessed the risk of death from neglected tropical diseases in Brazil from 2000 to 2011 and specific mortality from CD between 1999 and 2007 confirmed a higher risk ratio for African-descendant people [44, 46]. However, there was limited completeness of these data in the databases analysed, which may have compromised the analysis, with probable underestimation, reinforcing local contexts of greater endemicity.

Although not a specific objective of this study, it is recognized that racial issues in Brazil often reflect a condition of underlying social inequality. This is constituted from unequal political, economic, and social relations, which are intensified in the current political-institutional context. [47].

There was a tendency for mortality to increase in areas characterised as very high deprivation (high IBP). On the other hand, the lower the municipality’s IBP, the stronger the downward trend in mortality, reinforcing other evidence indicating an inverse relationship between socioeconomic status, endemicity and mortality [48, 49]. It is possible that the real scenario is even more critical, given the greater coverage of death registration in the Census when compared to the SIM in poorer municipalities, with greater deprivation of material goods (higher values of the IBP) and less developed (lower values of the Human Development Index), in addition to being located in the Northeast region of the country [50]. These results reaffirm the link between CD and social and regional vulnerability and inequalities [8, 17, 49].

Another finding that confirms the existence of possible barriers to access to hospital care for CD refers to the recognition of a higher risk of case fatality in this environment in isolated or aggregated large municipalities, places that have a better structured hospital care network, both public and private. In addition, mortality from CD showed a higher risk in small and aggregated municipalities, places with more limited care networks, where affected people need to travel long distances to guarantee the right to access to health [16].

The distance to achieve “territorial justice” is still considerable, since the allocation of resources does not necessarily follow the real needs of the population. The territorial distribution of health resources of greater technological complexity (specialised health professionals, hospital beds and equipment etc.) is unequal, aggravated in a context of crisis and restrictions with a likely worsening of regional inequalities, underfunding and problems related to the quality of care [15, 43, 51]. Given the neglected nature and social vulnerability associated with CD, it is assumed that the majority of people affected by the disease do not use private facilities for HA events [15], although this condition should not be overlooked. However, the fact that the SUS uses the private network as a complement in different parts of the country minimizes this loss of information. Furthermore, the database of more than 10 million annual hospitalizations is believed to underestimate HA events given the monthly SUS funding cap applied to each hospital service [15, 19]. Isolated municipalities are not homogeneous, that is, they are distended due to their social, economic characteristics, with different health policies [52], on the other hand, large urban centres are characterised by accelerated and unplanned population growth, leading to poverty, unemployment and inadequate housing. Despite this, urban centres, with population agglomeration, characterised as metropolitan regions or even large isolated cities, have an influence on nearby cities, providing health services in addition to logistical infrastructure [53].

The spatial analysis identified amplified risks in the territory for hospital case fatality and mortality from CD, particularly in the Southeast region of the country, with emphasis on the state of São Paulo. This aspect may be related not only to the densification of hospital structures, but also to the migratory process intensified in the second half of the 20th century, either in search of work and income, or due to the need the need for access to health services [2, 54]. Part of the people affected are still living in a rural or rural-urban context in specific areas, beyond the peripheries of large cities [16]. However, the evaluation of the temporal trends of these indicators revealed a trend of increasing hospital case fatality in the Southeast region and mortality in the North and Northeast regions, with emphasis on the state of Tocantins. These findings may reaffirm disparities in access to healthcare, with barriers mainly in the hospital setting in regions with lower social and human development. Specifically for the North Region, its relevance in terms of acute cases of the disease is also noteworthy, reflecting a clear change in the epidemiological profile of CD in Brazil, with the majority of these cases in the acute phase being registered in the Amazon region. It is important to emphasize that these acute cases are mainly due to oral transmission [18]. The space-time distribution, using municipalities as the unit of analysis, shows high hospital case fatality and mortality in areas that include municipalities in the states of Goiás, Minas Gerais and Bahia, classic areas of vector transmission of CD [2, 18, 55]. In the Federal District, given its location, its history of constitution and the expressive network of health services, the highest rates of hospital case fatality and one of the highest in terms of mortality in the last two decades are explained. The findings of this study reinforce the need for greater integration between surveillance and health care actions in the SUS network to qualify decision-making at different levels of government in these regions [54].

In 2020, the Brazilian MoH proposed a model for prioritising municipalities for CD based on multicriteria analysis, resulting from the evolution of the disease, giving relevance to chronic phase events, in addition to operational data on access to health services [12, 18]. A total of 2,949 (53%) municipalities were classified as medium, high and very high priority for CD, corroborating the results presented in this study (high and very high vulnerability municipality with higher occurrence and risk for hospital case fatality and death in general, in addition to an increasing trend for both indicators). The region with the highest proportion of priority municipalities was the Southeast (36.1%), highlighting the contribution of municipalities in the states of Minas Gerais and São Paulo, with 74.3% and 65.4% respectively [12, 16]. The composition of this index may have underestimated the impact of the chronic digestive form, with under-representation of the Central-West region in its analysis [2].

The limitations of the study are largely related to the databases used. Despite the advances, there is still evidence of different performances in the country to capture all deaths that occurred in reality, in addition to the quality of filling and investigation of DC [46, 49]. Although it is recognised that from 2005 onwards the MoH, with a view to improving the coverage of the system, began a process to reduce the number of deaths with ill-defined causes, based on the development of new computerised tools and training and/or qualification courses for new coders, challenges persist in terms of the quality of the records [27]. For HA, there are even more critical issues related to coverage, but also to the quality of filling in the form due to inaccuracy in defining the reason for hospitalisation, a fact that requires greater investment in information and education actions for health professionals so that they understand the importance of properly filling in all available variables, as well as sending them to the MoH. This aspect is central in view of the epidemiological relevance, in addition to the administrative and financial aspects.

For both deaths and HA, we sought to increase the sensitivity of the criteria for capturing the events of interest by incorporating associated causes of death (with multiple cause analysis) and all events diagnosed during hospitalisation, including secondary events, particularly since 2015 with updates to the SIH-SUS. It is essential to recognize that hospital mortality data are theoretically included in the mortality database. However, the inability to identify and link these two databases represents a significant limitation of the study.

In addition, the extensive historical series presented, as well as the large size of the databases analysed, have the potential to generate direct and indirect information to support decision-making and the reformulation of strategies that contribute to expanding access to health in the pursuit of equity.

Until 2019, epidemiological surveillance of CD did not include the chronic phase of the disease as an event of interest based on compulsory notification in the Notifiable Diseases Information System (SINAN). In this perspective, we reiterate the relevance of undertaking future analyses of other databases, in an integrated manner, such as SIH-SUS and SIM, capable of providing complementary information related to the death outcome. These aspects should be integrated into the strategies for developing the Brazilian response to CD [56].

Conclusions

The analysis of hospital case fatality and mortality related to CD reaffirmed that CD is still a significant cause of death in Brazil. Equity to health care access in the country is crucial in SUS, considering populations of greater social and institutional vulnerability. Significant differences related to gender and social vulnerability call for more comprehensive care in the primary health care system. The barriers to access all levels of health care, particularly hospital care, need to be tackled. Specific municipalities, states and regions, with indicators reflecting low human and social development, should be prioritised.
