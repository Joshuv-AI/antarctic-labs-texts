Presenilin mutations disrupt iron homeostasis to promote ferroptosis mediated neurodegeneration in Caenorhabditis elegans
Authors: Kerry C. Ryan, Jocelyn T. Laboy, Rabia Ashraf, Zahra Ashkavand, Isabel Jayasinghe, Kenneth R. Norman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Iron is a vital trace element involved in numerous physiological processes, but it becomes toxic when present in excess. Disruption of iron balance in the brain has been linked to the development of neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's disease (AD), though the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. Familial forms of AD are primarily caused by mutations in presenilin, which are known to disturb cellular calcium homeostasis. However, the role of iron in presenilin-related neurodegeneration has not been fully explored. Using C. elegans as a model organism, we investigated the function of SEL-12, the worm ortholog of presenilin, and found that loss of SEL-12 leads to elevated iron levels and increased expression of FTN-2/ferritin, an iron-sequestering protein. Notably, reducing mitochondrial calcium in sel-12 mutants prevented this iron accumulation, indicating that elevated mitochondrial calcium drives increased cellular iron levels. This iron overload depends on mitochondrial superoxide production, which occurs alongside heightened mitochondrial calcium, suggesting that oxidative stress contributes to iron dysregulation. The resulting iron imbalance causes mitochondrial and lysosomal dysfunction, ultimately impairing neuronal and behavioral function. Supporting the involvement of iron, sel-12 mutants exhibit elevated lipid peroxidation, and inhibition of ferroptosis restores neuronal function. Together, these findings reveal a novel role for presenilin in regulating iron homeostasis and identify a mechanism linking calcium signaling disruption to iron dyshomeostasis and neurodegeneration.

Introduction

Maintaining intracellular iron equilibrium is critical for healthy cellular function. Iron is involved in several essential biological processes, including DNA synthesis and repair, mitochondrial respiration and energy production, and neurotransmitter metabolism [1,2]. These cellular processes are mediated by several iron-containing enzymes, which depend on iron's ability to cycle between two redox states, ferrous (Fe2+) and ferric (Fe3+) iron. The ability of iron to cycle between its Fe2+ and Fe3+ oxidation states enables it to catalyze many reactions and is indispensable for life. However, the reactive nature of Fe2+, renders this metal ion potentially dangerous for cells. Indeed, alterations in iron homeostasis can lead to Fe2+ overload, which can act with hydrogen peroxide in the Haber-Weiss and Fenton reactions to produce highly reactive hydroxyl radicals that can damage proteins, nucleic acids and membrane lipids to promote cell death [3,4]. Thus, iron levels and iron distribution within the cell must be tightly regulated. Its active Fe2+ form is sequestered by specialized proteins, such as ferritin, to prevent inappropriate oxidation and damage to cellular components [5].

The ability to maintain iron homeostasis and minimize Fe2+ toxicity declines with age. As iron homeostatic mechanisms become less robust, iron gradually accumulates within certain regions of the brain [6]. Several neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer's disease (AD), have been associated with increased neuronal iron. However, the cellular factors that contribute to iron dyshomeostasis during aging and neurodegeneration, such as AD, remain unclear [1,7]. Additionally, whether iron is a central causal agent of AD or a consequence of other pathologies is not well established.

Familial Alzheimer's disease (fAD) is an inherited form of AD that presents earlier in life but whose symptoms and etiology are closely similar to the more common sporadic form of AD [8,9]. Thus, understanding the pathology underlying fAD should also provide valuable insight into sporadic AD. fAD is primarily caused by mutations in the presenilin-encoding genes, PSEN1 and PSEN2, with the majority of cases attributed to PSEN1 mutations [10]. Presenilin proteins are phylogenetically conserved in diverse multicellular organisms from plants to humans that predominantly localize to endomembrane structures [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. We and others have shown that presenilins have a conserved function to support neuronal survival in the aging nervous system [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]]. Importantly, presenilin expression, particularly presenilin-1, is reported to decline during aging [[21], [22], [23], [24]]. To gain insight into the function of presenilins, we employ C. elegans as a model system to study the function of the highly conserved presenilin-1 ortholog, SEL-12, whose features and function recapitulate many of those established for presenilin in mammalian models [15,17,[25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30]]. SEL-12, like its orthologs, serves as the catalytic component of the gamma-secretase complex, but also, like human presenilin, it has important gamma-secretase-independent roles vital for cellular function [15,17,25,[31], [32], [33], [34]]. Among these, SEL-12/presenilin regulates intracellular calcium signaling, and disruption of SEL-12/presenilin function results in elevated release of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) calcium and mitochondrial calcium uptake [17,25,[27], [28], [29],[35], [36], [37], [38]]. These observations are consistent with reports in sporadic AD models and AD patients demonstrating dysregulation of calcium signaling [[39], [40], [41]]. However, it remains unclear whether iron homeostasis is perturbed in fAD. It is speculated that presenilin dysfunction may sensitize neurons to further oxidative damage facilitated by iron [42], but there is no clear evidence indicating presenilin is directly involved in iron homeostasis.

Since fAD recapitulates many aspects of sporadic AD pathology, and because iron dyshomeostasis is observed in AD models and patients, here we asked whether presenilin dysfunction alters iron homeostasis in C. elegans. Additionally, given our previous discovery of a crucial link between mitochondrial calcium dysregulation and neurodegeneration in sel-12/presenilin mutants, we investigated the potential role of calcium signaling in iron homeostasis. Ultimately, we found that sel-12 mutations result in elevated total iron and Fe2+ concentrations, and this elevation is dependent on aberrantly increased mitochondrial calcium uptake in sel-12 mutants. Furthermore, iron chelation improves several indicators of neuronal health, including axonal and mitochondrial morphology, lysosome health, and nervous system function. Consistent with elevated Fe2+, sel-12 mutants show higher levels of lipid peroxidation and inhibition of ferroptosis restores neuronal function in sel-12 mutants. Altogether, our study identifies a role for SEL-12/presenilin in iron homeostasis and unveils a novel link between mitochondrial calcium signaling, iron homeostasis, ferroptosis and neuronal health.

Results

C. elegans presenilin mutants show elevated iron levels

To investigate the impact presenilin mutations have on iron homeostasis, we utilized a green fluorescent protein (GFP) expression reporter for the iron responsive gene ftn-2 that encodes a C. elegans homolog of the iron-storage protein ferritin [43,44]. This reporter consists of the ftn-2 promoter (ftn-2p) fused to a nuclear localized GFP-histone fusion protein. We introduced the ftn-2p::GFP-histone (GFP-his) reporter into several strains that harbor mutations in the C. elegans gene encoding the presenilin ortholog, sel-12. These include two putative null alleles, sel-12(ty11) and sel-12(ok2078) and a missense mutation, sel-12(ar131), that disrupts a conserved amino acid that has been identified in human PSEN1 in a family of fAD patients [45]. Analyses of Day 1 adult animals revealed that the sel-12 mutants exhibited significantly higher GFP-his expression compared to wild type animals (Fig. 1A and B). These data suggest that disruption of presenilin function results in elevated iron levels. To further investigate the impact presenilin mutations may have on iron homeostasis, we determined levels of total iron in whole animal lysates. Consistent with the high expression of the iron responsive ferritin reporter, we found that iron levels were elevated in sel-12 mutants (Fig. 1C). As a control, we compared total iron between wild type and smf-3(ok1035) mutants, which carry a null mutation in the smf-3 locus that encodes the C. elegans ortholog of the divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1), which is important for iron import [44]. Similar with previous findings, smf-3 null mutants showed a significant reduction in iron levels (Fig. 1C) [44]. We also specifically measured ferrous iron (Fe2+) levels, as Fe2+ is the redox active form of iron, and excessive levels of Fe2+ are responsible for iron-induced oxidative damage and lipid peroxidation [46]. Compared to wild type animals, sel-12 mutants showed significantly higher Fe2+ levels (Fig. 1D). Importantly, we found that introducing the smf-3 null mutation into the sel-12 mutant background significantly rescues the elevated iron and Fe2+ levels, and the ftn-2p::GFP-his reporter activity in sel-12 mutants (Fig. 1C, D, E). Taken together, these results indicate that sel-12 mutants have higher iron levels, including the highly reactive Fe2+.

Iron homeostasis is dependent on SEL-12/presenilin regulation of mitochondrial calcium but not gamma-secretase function

Presenilin forms the catalytic core of the gamma-secretase complex, which is an aspartyl protease required for the cleavage of several type I transmembrane proteins, including Notch and the amyloid precursor protein [47]. This catalytic activity of presenilin is reliant on two phylogenetically conserved aspartic acid residues [48]. While we have previously demonstrated that the neuronal defects associated with sel-12 mutants are independent of gamma-secretase function [17,25], it is unclear if its proteolytic function is required for iron homeostasis. Therefore, to determine if presenilin catalytic activity is required for iron homeostasis, we examined GFP expression for the iron responsive gene ftn-2p::GFP-his reporter in sel-12(D226A) mutants, which carry a point mutation in one of the critical aspartic acid sites required for gamma-secretase activity and has been shown to disrupt SEL-12 protease function [15,25]. Compared to wild type animals, sel-12(D226A) mutants did not show a significant difference in ftn-2p::GFP-his fluorescence (Fig. 2A). Additionally, we did not detect higher iron or Fe2+ levels in sel-12(D226A) mutant lysates (Fig. 2B and C). These data suggest that the aspartyl protease function of presenilin is not involved in iron homeostasis. Previous studies have reported that presenilins have several crucial gamma-secretase-independent functions that influence neuronal health, including calcium signaling [11,15,17,27,32,33,38]. Previously, we discovered that presenilin mutations in C. elegans result in elevated mitochondrial calcium, which in turn promotes oxidative stress and neurodegeneration [15,17,25,29]. Additionally, it was found that reducing mitochondrial calcium uptake in sel-12 mutants rescues their neuronal dysfunction [17]. Similarly, in AD mouse models, it has been shown that an increase in mitochondrial calcium precedes other neuronal defects, and that reducing of mitochondrial calcium uptake prevents neurodegeneration in these models [39,40]. Since we have found evidence that disruption of iron homeostasis following presenilin loss is gamma-secretase-independent (Fig. 2), we asked if this disruption results from mitochondrial calcium dysregulation. To examine the influence mitochondrial calcium has on cellular iron levels in sel-12 mutants, we utilized a null mutation in the mitochondrial calcium uniporter, mcu-1, which we and others have found reduces mitochondrial calcium uptake [17,49,50]. Consistent with previous studies [17,25,27], we found elevated mitochondrial matrix calcium levels in the nervous system and hypodermis of sel-12 mutants, which is suppressed by the loss of mcu-1 (Supplemental Fig. 1A and B). Next, we analyzed ftn-2p::GFP-his reporter expression in these animals and found that mcu-1; sel-12 double mutants had reduced expression compared to sel-12 alone (Fig. 2A). Additionally, mcu-1; sel-12 double mutant lysates also exhibited reduced total iron (Fig. 2B) and Fe2+ levels (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, we explored the role of the inositol 1,4,5 trisphosphate receptor (IP3R), a major ER calcium release channel, that mediates ER to mitochondrial calcium signaling and has been implicated in calcium dysregulation in AD [35,[51], [52], [53], [54]]. We found that depletion of itr-1, which encodes the C. elegans ortholog of the IP3R, using RNA interference reduces mitochondrial matrix calcium levels, as previously shown [27] and also reduces the expression of the ftn-2p::GFP-his reporter in sel-12 mutants (Supplemental Fig. 1C and D). These data indicate that reducing mitochondrial calcium uptake or ER calcium release restores cellular iron homeostasis in sel-12 mutants and suggests that mitochondrial calcium dysregulation is responsible for elevated iron levels.

Scavenging mitochondrial superoxide radicals restores iron homeostasis in sel-12 mutants

We and others have found that mutations in presenilins result in increased mitochondrial generated superoxide and oxidative stress [15,17,28,29,55]. Moreover, we demonstrated that mitochondrial superoxide production and oxidative stress are suppressed by reducing mitochondrial calcium uptake [15,17,29]. Of note, iron plays a critical role in mitochondrial metabolism and is necessary for heme and iron-sulfur cluster cofactor synthesis. These iron-containing proteins are extremely susceptible to mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS), and their dysfunction can lead to the release of iron from the mitochondria [1]. In line with this, there is evidence that mitochondrial ROS generation can increase Fe2+ levels [56]. Thus, to further investigate the mechanism connecting mitochondrial calcium and iron dyshomeostasis, we asked whether increased mitochondrial superoxide generation in sel-12 mutants disrupts iron homeostasis in sel-12 mutants. To accomplish this, we treated sel-12 mutants with the mitochondrial-targeted antioxidant MitoTEMPO. Our previous studies have shown that treatment with MitoTEMPO reduces mitochondrial oxidative stress and restores neuronal function in sel-12 mutants [15,17,29]. Consistent with these studies, using a mitochondrial redox biosensor, roGFP1 [29,57], we found that sel-12 mutants show increased mitochondrial oxidation compared to wild type animals and sel-12 mutants treated with MitoTEMPO (Fig. 3A). Utilizing the iron responsive ferritin reporter, ftn-2p::GFP-his, we found that sel-12 mutants treated with MitoTEMPO have significantly reduced reporter expression compared to sel-12 mutants treated with triphenyl-phosphonium chloride (TPP) control (Fig. 3B). Moreover, analysis of worm lysates showed that treatment of sel-12 mutants with MitoTEMPO reduced overall iron and Fe2+ levels (Fig. 3C and D). Increased ferrous iron promotes neurodegeneration in sel-12 mutants

To determine the functional consequence of iron elevation in sel-12 mutants, we examined the health of the mechanosensory neurons, which exhibit stereotypical behavior and age-related neurodegeneration [[58], [59], [60]]. Consistent with presenilin promoting neuronal health, these neurons show premature degeneration and dysfunction in sel-12 mutants [17]. Indeed, analysis of the axon structure of the posterior lateral mechanosensory (PLM) neurons using a GFP reporter driven under a mechanosensory-specific promoter [61], we found that sel-12 mutants have a significant increase in axonal lesions (Fig. 4A and B). To determine whether iron influences the presence of axonal lesions, we treated sel-12 mutants with the ferrous iron (Fe2+) ion chelator, 2,2’ bipyridine (BP). We found that iron chelation significantly reduced the formation of axonal lesions (Fig. 4A and B), suggesting that the elevated levels of iron contribute to neuronal structural defects in sel-12 mutants.Fig. 4Iron chelation rescues neurodegeneration phenotypes in sel-12 mutants. Along with neuronal structure, we assessed the function of the mechanosensory neurons. Animals with healthy mechanosensory neurons can sense soft touch, such as with an eyebrow hair, which will trigger escape behavior from this stimulus [62]. Soft touch on the anterior half of the body will cause it to reverse direction and move backward; conversely, soft touch on the posterior half will direct it to move forward. Mechanosensory neurons display age-associated decline in the response rate to soft touch [59], which occurs prematurely in sel-12 mutants [17](Fig. 4C). We found that treating sel-12 mutants with BP to chelate Fe2+ rescued the defect in their response rate (Fig. 4C). Fe2+ chelation also improved the touch response of other sel-12 mutants, including a sel-12 deletion mutant [sel-12(ok2078)] and a sel-12 allele [sel-12(ar131)] that contains a point mutation conserved with a human fAD mutation (Fig. 4C). Additionally, we genetically reduced iron levels in sel-12 mutants by crossing in the smf-3 (encodes DMT1 ortholog) null mutant [44] into the sel-12 background (Fig. 1C–E). The soft touch response of smf-3; sel-12 mutants was indistinguishable from that of wild type animals (Fig. 4D). Consistent with the neuronal structural defects (Fig. 4A and B), these data suggest that iron elevation causes neurodegeneration phenotypes in sel-12 mutants.

Ferrous iron elevation disrupts mitochondrial networks and lysosomal function in sel-12 mutants

Alzheimer's disease, along with other neurodegenerative diseases, is strongly associated with mitochondrial and lysosomal dysfunction [[63], [64], [65]]. Consistently, sel-12 mutants show disrupted mitochondrial networks, and lysosomal morphology and functional defects [17,25,[27], [28], [29],66]. Therefore, we wished to determine whether elevated iron levels contribute to mitochondrial or lysosomal dysfunction in sel-12 mutants. To determine whether iron influences these phenotypes, we used BP to chelate Fe2+ in sel-12 mutants and examined the average length of mitochondrial networks in the hypodermis through the use of a Dendra2 marker that localizes to the mitochondrial matrix in hypodermal cells [67]. Consistent with previous studies, we found that sel-12 mutants showed reduced size and length of mitochondrial networks (Fig. 5A and B). In contrast, Fe2+ chelation in sel-12 mutants showed improved mitochondrial morphology as indicated by average mitochondrial network length (Fig. 5A and B). Additionally, utilizing a mitochondrial matrix localized GFP [68], we examined mitochondria in the mechanosensory neurons. Similar to what is observed in the hypodermis, mitochondria in the mechanosensory neurons of sel-12 mutants display a fragmented mitochondrial network [17] (Fig. 5C and D). Similar to our hypodermal mitochondrial analyses, Fe2+ chelation with BP in sel-12 mutants prevents the fragmentation of the mitochondrial network in mechanosensory neurons (Fig. 5C and D). Next, we examined lysosomal morphology and function in sel-12 mutants. Previous studies in human neurons as well as C. elegans have found that mutations in presenilin lead to enlarged and dysfunctional lysosomes [27,69,70]. To determine whether iron influences lysosomal morphology, we examined transgenic animals expressing a lysosomal nuclease, NUC-1, tagged with mCherry [71]. This reporter is an established marker for lysosomal morphology [27,72]. Employing this reporter, we confirmed, as previously described, that lysosomes in the hypodermis of sel-12 mutants were enlarged compared to wild type worms (Fig. 6A and B). In contrast, sel-12 mutants treated with BP to chelate Fe2+ did not display enlarged lysosomes and instead had lysosome morphologies similar to wild type animals (Fig. 6A and B). Additionally, we examined lysosome acidification as an indicator of lysosome function and health. To accomplish this, we utilized transgenic animals where the lysosomal nuclease, NUC-1, is tagged with a pH-sensitive fluorescent marker, pHTomato, that exhibits increased fluorescence intensity with increasing pH [27,72]. sel-12 mutants displayed significantly higher pHTomato fluorescence intensity, indicating a relatively higher pH compared to wild type animals (Fig. 6C and D). Contrarily, sel-12 mutants treated with BP showed reduced fluorescence intensity similar to wild type animals (Fig. 6C and D), indicating that lysosome dysfunction in sel-12 mutants is caused by elevated ferrous iron levels. sel-12 mutant neuronal dysfunction is mediated by ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is an iron- and ROS-dependent form of cell death and has been implicated in AD as well as in other neurodegenerative disease [[73], [74], [75], [76], [77], [78]]. The hallmarks of ferroptosis include alterations to mitochondrial morphology and function, such as mitochondrial fragmentation, calcium overload and elevated ROS production, and lipid peroxidation [3,[79], [80], [81], [82]]. Since several of these ferroptosis hallmarks are observed in sel-12 mutants (e.g., altered mitochondrial morphology, increased mitochondrial calcium and superoxide production), we hypothesized that the neuronal dysfunction associated with sel-12/presenilin mutants is promoted by ferroptosis and hence, investigated whether ferroptosis plays a role in sel-12 neurodegeneration. As ferroptosis is mediated by the accumulation of redox reactive Fe2+, which promotes lipid peroxidation [3], we first asked if there is any evidence of lipid peroxidation in sel-12 mutants. To accomplish this, we determined levels of the toxic lipid peroxidation end product malondialdehyde (MDA) in wild type and sel-12 mutants [[83], [84], [85], [86]]. Compared to wild type animals, we found that sel-12 mutants had significantly higher levels of MDA in animal lysates (Fig. 7A), suggesting an increase in lipid peroxidation in the sel-12 mutants. Since we found that reduction of mitochondrial calcium uptake reduces iron levels in sel-12 mutants and has been previously shown to prevent oxidative stress and neurodegeneration in sel-12 mutants, we examined MDA levels in mcu-1; sel-12 double mutants. In addition to iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation, ferroptosis is also associated with the reduction of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) activity, leading to depletion of glutathione (GSH) and an elevated oxidized glutathione to glutathione (GSSG/GSH) ratio. To investigate glutathione status, we determined whole GSH levels in animal lysates. We found that the GSH levels in sel-12 mutants were significantly reduced compared to wild type animals (Fig. 7B). Moreover, we found that mcu-1; sel-12 double mutants showed increased levels of GSH that was indistinguishable from wild type animals. Furthermore, we utilized a Grx1-roGFP2 GSSG/GSH ratiometric biosensor to determine the in vivo GSSG/GSH ratio in live animals [87,88]. We found that sel-12 mutants have an elevated ratio of oxidized to reduced Grx1-roGFP fluorescence compared to wild type animals that is rescued in the mcu-1; sel-12 double mutants (Fig. 7C). These data indicate that the disruption in GSH levels in sel-12 mutants is mediated by aberrant mitochondrial calcium uptake.

Lastly, to test the hypothesis that ferroptosis is promoting neurodegeneration in sel-12 mutants, we utilized a potent and selective ferroptosis inhibitor, ferrostatin-1, and assayed neuronal function in sel-12 mutants. Ferrostatin-1 is a synthetic small molecule antioxidant that scavenges lipid alkoxyl radicals in lipid bilayers to prevent lipid peroxidation and inhibits ferroptosis [89,90]. Strikingly, while ferrostatin-1 did not impact wild type behavioral response to soft touch, its treatment restored neuronal function and rescued soft touch response in sel-12 mutants (Fig. 7D). Thus, taken together, these results suggest that ferroptosis is contributing to neuronal and behavioral dysfunction in sel-12 mutants.

Discussion

Iron's ability to cycle between its two redox states, Fe2+ and Fe3+, allows it to participate in reduction-oxidation mediated signaling events, making it vital for many cellular processes. However, iron's reactivity also makes it highly toxic if it is not tightly regulated [91,92]. The cell has evolved many regulatory proteins to safely traffic and sequester the oxidizing Fe2+ to prevent free radical production, oxidizing reactions, and protein misfolding. However, the efficiency of these homeostatic mechanisms declines with age [93,94], leading to excess ROS production. Neurons are particularly vulnerable to oxidative damage [95,96]. Dysregulation of neuronal iron levels has been observed in many neurodegenerative diseases, including AD, but the underlying cause is unclear [1,7,97,98]. fAD, caused primarily by mutations in the gene encoding the phylogenetically conserved presenilin-1 protein, results in an early-onset form of AD whose pathology reproduces the features of sporadic AD [8,9]. However, it is unknown whether presenilin itself influences iron homeostasis. Here, using C. elegans to study presenilin function, we identified a link between calcium signaling dysregulation and iron dyshomeostasis. We found that aberrant increased mitochondrial calcium uptake in sel-12 mutants increased total iron and Fe2+ concentration. Elevated iron levels in turn cause extensive neuronal and cellular damage by disturbing lysosomal function, neuronal structure, mitochondrial morphology, and animal behavior. Along with elevated Fe2+, sel-12 mutants exhibit hallmarks of ferroptosis. Indeed, inhibition of ferroptosis using the potent and selective ferroptosis inhibitor, ferrostatin-1, restored neuronal function in sel-12 mutants. Our study demonstrates a new and essential role for SEL-12/presenilin in iron homeostasis, linking SEL-12/presenilin regulation of mitochondrial calcium signaling to iron homeostasis, ultimately governing neuronal health. These data provide novel insight into the mechanism by which neuronal death may occur in fAD and AD more broadly.

The function of presenilin as the catalytic core of the gamma-secretase has been extensively studied [99,100]. While Notch and the amyloid precursor protein are the best-known substrates, there are more than 100 other proteins subjected to cleavage by the gamma-secretase [101,102]. Nevertheless, we found that a sel-12 missense mutation that abolishes its proteolytic activity showed no evidence of disrupted iron homeostasis. Instead, we found that the critical activity of SEL-12/presenilin to regulate iron homeostasis is dependent on its role in mediating mitochondrial calcium levels. Previous studies have shown that loss of C. elegans and mammalian presenilin function is important for regulating organelle interactions, namely ER and mitochondrial contacts [11,27,103]. Disruption of presenilin function leads to an increase in ER-mitochondrial contacts, which in turn can elevate ER-mitochondrial calcium signaling. Deregulated mitochondrial calcium uptake promotes calcium overload and mitochondrial dysfunction [104,105]. Indeed, mitochondrial calcium overload has been reported to occur prior to neuronal defects in mouse models of AD. Accordingly, reducing mitochondrial calcium uptake prevents neurodegeneration in these models [[39], [40], [41]]. Likewise, we have found in C. elegans that reducing mitochondrial calcium uptake or ER calcium release improves sel-12 mutant neuronal dysfunction [17].

Elevated calcium uptake into the mitochondria has been shown to induce mitochondrial generation of ROS such as superoxide [106,107]. Indeed, our group and others have found that presenilin mutations cause a rise in mitochondrial oxidative stress [15,17,29,55,108,109]. Importantly, mitochondria are necessary for heme and iron-sulfur cluster cofactor synthesis and iron-containing proteins play a crucial role in mitochondrial metabolism. However, these iron-containing proteins are highly vulnerable to mitochondrial ROS, and their impairment can result in the release of iron from the mitochondria [1,56]. Here, we show evidence that treatment of sel-12 mutants with a mitochondrial-targeted antioxidant scavenger reduces elevated iron levels. These data suggest that increased mitochondrial ROS generated by deregulated mitochondrial calcium uptake drives the elevated iron levels observed in sel-12 mutants.

Although elevated iron levels have been reported in AD brains [1,7], it remains unclear whether this increase contribute to neurodegeneration. Since we have found increased iron levels in sel-12/presenilin mutants, we tested whether iron chelation could improve neuronal dysfunction in sel-12 mutants. We found that Fe2+ chelation improved both neuronal morphology and function, suggesting that elevated Fe2+ is involved in promoting neuronal dysfunction. Likewise, we found that reducing iron uptake in the sel-12 mutants by introducing a null mutation in the smf-3 gene, which encodes DMT1, into the sel-12 mutant background restored neuronal function. Moreover, consistent with observations in AD models and patients [63,110,111], mitochondrial and lysosomal dysfunction are also observed in sel-12 mutants [15,17,27]. We found that chelation of Fe2+ improved both mitochondrial and lysosomal morphology, as well as lysosomal function. These data suggest that loss of iron homeostasis in sel-12 mutants impairs organelle and neuronal health and represents a critical pathological event that occurs to disrupt nervous system function.

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of cell death that is caused by increased lipid peroxidation and has been implicated in several neurodegenerative diseases including AD [[73], [74], [75], [76], [77], [78]]. sel-12 mutants exhibit many hallmarks of ferroptosis, which includes alterations in mitochondrial morphology, mitochondrial calcium overload, elevated mitochondrial ROS production, and disrupted mitochondrial membrane potential [15,17,29,66]. Here, we found evidence that sel-12 mutants show an elevation in lipid peroxidation, and we demonstrate that neuronal function is restored in sel-12 mutants treated with the ferroptosis inhibitor, ferrostatin-1. Thus, our data implicates ferroptosis, at least in part, in driving neuronal dysfunction in sel-12 mutants. These data are in line with a previous study that showed that mutations that activate skn-1, which encodes the C. elegans Nrf2 (Nuclear-erythroid Factor 2 related Factor 2) transcription factor that promotes a cascade of genes involved in preventing ferroptosis [112,113], could suppress neuronal dysfunction associated with sel-12/presenilin mutations [29]. Importantly, a recent in vitro study found that loss of presenilin function significantly sensitizes multiple human cell types to ferroptosis [114]. Moreover, it was recently demonstrated that inhibiting mitochondrial calcium uniporter function protects neurons against ferroptosis [115].

Altogether, this study identifies a role for SEL-12/presenilin in iron homeostasis. Our data show that loss of SEL-12/presenilin sets off a chain of events by which iron imbalance, mediated by mitochondrial calcium dysregulation and mitochondrial ROS, causes further ROS generation. Destabilization of these homeostatic mechanisms results in dysfunction of both mitochondria and lysosomes, organelles especially crucial for neuronal function. Collective oxidative damage through lipid peroxidation culminates in ferroptosis and neurodegeneration. Our study reveals a mechanism by which mitochondrial calcium leads to iron dyshomeostasis in presenilin mutants and uncovers a link between SEL-12/presenilin, iron levels, and mitochondrial function.

Materials and methods

Caenorhabditis elegans strains and maintenance

C. elegans strains were maintained at 20 °C on nematode growth media (NGM) plates seeded with the OP50 E. coli strain, as previously described [116]. Age synchronization was achieved by bleaching gravid worms to obtain eggs, which were then rocked in M9 buffer at 20 °C for 24–48 h to allow eggs to hatch. Synchronized L1 larvae were grown to adulthood on NGM plates seeded with OP50. All experiments were performed on day 1 adults. The wild type strain used was the N2 Bristol strain. The following transgenic or mutant strains were used in this study: mcu-1(tm6026) IV, smf-3(ok1035) IV, sel-12(ar131,ok2078, tak10, ty11) X, jrIs2 [rpl-17p::Grx1-roGFP2], jsIs609 X [mec-4p::MLS::GFP], juSi271 I [col-19p::mito:dendra2], qaEx6901 [ftn-2p::pes-10::GFP:his], qxIs257 [ced-1p::nuc-1::mCherry], qxIs750 [hsp::nuc-1::pHTomato], takEx767 [semo-1p::2xMLS::GCaMP6f:SL2:wrmScarlet], takEx773 [mec-7p::2xMLS::GCaMP6f:SL2:wrmScarlet], zhsEx17 [mec-4p::MLS:roGFP], and zdIs5 [mec-4p::GFP]. Genotypes were determined by visible phenotypes and PCR. sel-12(ty11) mutants were used as the canonical sel-12 loss of function mutant unless otherwise indicated.

Primers for mcu-1(tm6026) detection are as follows: External forward 5′ ACAAATGAGGAATGGCCGATGC 3′, external reverse 5′ CTGACCAGTTTGCACTCCCATCG 3′ and internal forward 5′ TCGATTATGTCGCCGTGTATGG 3′. Primers used for smf-3(ok1035) detection are as follows: External forward 5′ CCCAAAGTGATCGGAACCTA 3′, external reverse 5′ GGGATTATTTGGACCCGACT 3′ and internal forward 5′ CCTTCACCTTCTTGTTCCTGG 3′.

RNA interference

The feeding method was used to deliver dsRNA to target gene products [117]. NGM plates were seeded with the HT115 E. coli strain that express dsRNA to control vector (ev) or itr-1 [27]. RNAi bacterial strains were confirmed by PCR and sequencing. Synchronized L1 animals were grown on RNAi bacteria until day 1 adults.

Mechanosensation assay

Responsiveness to soft touch was evaluated using an eyebrow hair glued to a Pasteur pipet blinded to genotype, as previously described [17,62]. Alternating touches were applied at the anterior region (between the head and vulva) and the posterior region (between the vulva and tail). Five touches were performed to each region for a total of 10 touches per animal. A positive response was recorded when the worm reversed its direction and maintained its new trajectory. The percentage positive responses were recorded per animal. Behavior analyses were carried out on three independent replicates with ≥7 animals per genotype per replicate.

ftn-2p/ferritin reporter assay

Transgenic animals expressing qaEx6901 [ftn-2p::pes-10::GFP:his] were used as a transcriptional reporter of the iron responsive gene ftn-2, which encodes a C. elegans ortholog of ferritin [44]. Day 1 adults were mounted on 2 % agarose pads with 0.5 M levamisole and imaged using a 60× oil objective lens on a Nikon Eclipse microscope. GFP fluorescence was excited using 488 nm light source at 1 % power on a Lumencor Spectra Light Engine and emitted light collected via a 525/25 m band pass filter (Chroma). The GFP epifluorescence images were captured with a Teledyne Prime BSI sCMOS camera with a consistent exposure time using Nikon Elements (Version AR 5.42.03) software. Fluorescence intensity in captured images was measured using Fiji (Version 1.54f) or Bitplane Imaris (10.0) software. Background fluorescence was removed by subtracting signal from a region adjacent to the specimen. Animal preparation and imaging was done on at least three independent replicates with ≥7 animals per genotype per replicate. Sample preparation, image capture and analyses were done blind to genotype.

Analysis of neuronal morphology

Mechanosensory neuron structure was examined in zdIs5 [mec-4p::GFP] expressing animals [61]. The worms were immobilized in 0.5 M levamisole on 2 % agarose pads and imaged using the 60× oil objective lens on a Nikon Eclipse microscope. The GFP epifluorescence images were captured with a Teledyne Prime BSI sCMOS camera using Nikon Elements (Version AR 5.42.03) software. Imaging was done on 4 independent replicates with greater than 15 animals per genotype for each replicate. The images were compiled using Fiji software. A PLM axon from individual animals were scored for either the presence or absence of lesions sprouting off the axon. Sample preparation, image capture and analyses were done blind to genotype.

Analysis of mitochondrial network

Organization of the mitochondrial network in the hypodermis and the PLM mechanosensory neurons was examined in animals expressing col-19p::mito:dendra2 (juSi271) and mec-4p::MLS::GFP(jsIs609), respectively [67,68]. Animals were immobilized in 0.5 M levamisole on 2 % agarose pads, then imaged using a 60 × oil objective on a Nikon A1R confocal microscope with NIS Elements software, with z stacks taken at 0.25 slice intervals. Images of hypodermis mitochondria were taken at the midbody of the animal. Mitochondrial length was quantified in hypodermis images using Bitplane Imaris (10.0) software. Average volume of the mitochondrial network fragments in the PLM soma was also quantified using Bitplane Imaris (10.0) software. Animal preparation and imaging was done on three independent replicates with ≥5 animals per genotype per replicate. Sample preparation, image capture and analyses were done blind to genotype.

Quantification of lysosome volume and acidity

Lysosomes in the hypodermis were imaged in transgenic animals expressing ced-1p::nuc-1::mCherry (qxIs257), where the ubiquitous ced-1 promoter drives expression of the lysosomal luminal marker, NUC-1:CHERRY [118]. Animals expressing hsp::nuc-1::pHTomato (qxIs750) were used to measure relative pH across strains [118]. The heat shock promoter drives expression of pHTomato, a pH-sensitive fluorescent protein whose fluorescence intensity increases with rising pH. Animals were heat shocked at 37 °C for 30 min to induce expression of the construct, then incubated at 20 °C for 24 h and imaged [72]. Animals were immobilized in 0.5 M levamisole on 2 % agarose pads. For all lysosome images, an A1R Nikon laser-scanning confocal microscope with NIS Elements software was used to capture all images at 60× magnification, with z-stacks acquired at 0.25 μm slice intervals. For all pHTomato:CHERRY strains, pinhole size, laser power and gain were kept constant. Each image was taken at the midbody of the worm to capture a selection of lysosomes. Volume of NUC-1:CHERRY-expressing strains and fluorescence intensity of pHTomato:CHERRY-expressing strains was quantified using Bitplane Imaris (10.0) software. Animal preparation and imaging was done on three independent replicates with ≥5 animals per genotype per replicate. Sample preparation, image capture and analyses were done blind to genotype.

Mitochondrial matrix calcium measurements

Animals expressing mitochondrial localized calcium biosensor GCaMP6f in the touch response neurons (takEx773 [mec-7p::2xMLS::GCaMP6f:SL2:wrmScarlet]) or hypodermal cells (takEx767 [semo-1p::2xMLS::GCaMP6f:SL2:wrmScarlet]) were immobilized in 0.5 M levamisole on 2 % agarose pads. Epifluorescence images were captured using a 60 × oil objective lens on a Nikon Eclipse microscope equipped with a Lumencor Spectra Light Engine and Teledyne Prime BSI sCMOS camera using Nikon Elements (Version AR 5.42.03) software. GCaMP fluorescence was excited using 475/28 nm light source on a Lumencor Spectra Light Engine and emitted light collected via a ET525/20m band pass filter (Chroma). wrmScarlet fluorescence was excited using 575/25 nm light source on a Lumencor Spectra Light Engine and emitted light collected via a ET595/33m band pass filter (Chroma). Identical light intensities and exposure times were used for all specimens. Fluorescence intensity of captured images was measured using Fiji (Version 1.54f) software. Background fluorescence was removed by subtracting signal from a region adjacent to the specimen for both GCaMP and wrmScarlet images. Animal preparation and imaging was done on at least three independent replicates with ≥7 animals per genotype per replicate. Sample preparation, image capture and analyses were done blind to genotype.

Mitochondrial redox measurements

Animals expressing mitochondrial localized redox biosensor roGFP1 in the touch response neurons (zhsEx17 [mec-4p::MLS-roGFP][29,57]) were immobilized in 0.5 M levamisole on 2 % agarose pads, then imaged using a 60 × oil objective on a Nikon Eclipse microscope equipped with a Lumencor Spectra Light Engine and Teledyne Prime BSI sCMOS camera using Nikon Elements (Version AR 5.42.03) software. For all specimens, identical exposure times and light intensities were used to capture roGFP fluorescence. Oxidized roGFP fluorescence was excited using 390/22 nm light source on a Lumencor Spectra Light Engine and emitted light collected via a ET525/20m band pass filter (Chroma). Reduced roGFP fluorescence was excited using 475/28 nm light source on a Lumencor Spectra Light Engine and emitted light collected via a ET525/20m band pass filter (Chroma). Fluorescence intensity of captured images was measured using Fiji (Version 1.54f) software. Background fluorescence was removed by subtracting signal from a region adjacent to the specimen for both 390/22 nm and 475/28 nm collected images. The ratio of 390/475 nm intensity was calculated to define redox state. Animal preparation and imaging was done on at least three independent replicates with ≥7 animals per genotype per replicate. Sample preparation, image capture and analyses were done blind to genotype.

GSSG-GSH ratio measurements

Animals expressing GSSG-GSH redox biosensor Grx1-roGFP2 (jrIs2 [rpl-17p::Grx1-roGFP2][87]) were immobilized in 0.5 M levamisole on 2 % agarose pads, then imaged using a 60 × oil objective on a Nikon Eclipse microscope equipped with Lumencor Spectra Light Engine and Teledyne Prime BSI sCMOS camera using Nikon Elements (Version AR 5.42.03) software. To avoid intestinal autofluorescence, all images were taken in the tail region to capture the posterior ganglion. For all specimens, identical exposure times and light intensities were used to capture Grx1-roGFP2 fluorescence. Oxidized Grx1-roGFP2 fluorescence was excited using 390/22 nm light source on a Lumencor Spectra Light Engine and emitted light collected via a ET525/20m band pass filter (Chroma). Reduced Grx1-roGFP2 fluorescence was excited using 475/28 nm light source on a Lumencor Spectra Light Engine and emitted light collected via a ET525/20m band pass filter (Chroma). Fluorescence intensity of captured images was measured using Fiji (Version 1.54f) software. Background fluorescence was removed by subtracting signal from a region adjacent to the specimen for both 390/22 nm and 475/28 nm collected images. The ratio of 390/475 nm intensity was calculated to define redox state. Animal preparation and imaging was done on at least three independent replicates with ≥6 animals per genotype per replicate. Sample preparation, image capture and analyses were done blind to genotype.

Drug treatments

For mitoTEMPO experiments, either 500 μM of (2-(2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidin-1-oxyl-4-ylamino) -2-oxoethyl) triphenylphosphonium chloride (mitoTEMPO) (Sigma-Aldrich Cat# SML0737) or 500 μM triphenyl-phosphonium chloride (TPP) (Sigma-Aldrich Cat# 11726LE) was added to NGM plates. For 2,2′ bipyridyl (BP) analyses, 25 μM of BP (Sigma-Aldrich Cat# D216305) was added to molten NGM agar. For the ferrostatin-1 assays, ferrostatin-1 (Sigma-Aldrich Cat# SML0583) was dissolved in DMSO and added to molten NGM agar at a concentration of 10 μM. At the L1 stage, animals were moved to drug supplemented plates and assayed as Day 1 adults.

Iron assay

Iron quantification was performed following the manufacturer's instruction using the Iron Assay Kit (Sigma, Cat# MAK025). >5000 synchronized day 1 adult were collected from 100 mm plates in M9 and washed to remove excess bacteria. Animals were frozen in liquid nitrogen then lysed by sonication on ice in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors (Roche). the supernatants were collected after centrifugation at 10 rcf at 4 °C for 15 min. The BCA assay was used to determine the protein concentration of each sample. 4 vol of Iron Assay buffer is added the supernatants and Fe2+ was determined by measuring absorbance of the colorimetric product at 593 nm on a Biotek Synergy 2 plate reader. Total iron was determined by addition of the iron reducer buffer to reduce Fe3+ to Fe2+ as per manufacturer's instructions and the colorimetric product at 593 nm on a Biotek Synergy 2 plate reader. Iron concentration was calculated from the standard curve and normalized by level of protein concentration in the samples. All samples and standards were assayed in duplicate, and the experiment was independently repeated with fresh samples greater than or equal to three times.

Lipid peroxidation analysis

Quantification of lipid peroxidation was measured using the TBARS Assay Kit (Cayman Chemicals, Cat# 10009055) according to the manufacturer's instructions. >5000 synchronized day 1 adult animals were collected from 100 mm plates in M9 and washed in M9 to remove bacteria, then snaped frozen in liquid nitrogen and lysed in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors (Roche) via sonication on ice. The supernatants were collected after centrifugation at 10 rcf and 4 °C for 15 min. The BCA assay was used to determine the protein concentration of each sample. Absorbance of the colorimetric product was measured at 535 nm on a Biotek Synergy 2 plate reader. Absorbance values were calculated from the standard curve and normalized by level of protein concentration in the sample. All samples and standards were assayed in duplicate, and the experiment was repeated with fresh samples three times.

GSH analysis

Quantification of GSH was measured using the reactivity of 5,5’ – dithio-bis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DNTB) with GSH as previously described [119]. In brief, >5000 synchronized day 1 adult were collected from 100 mm plates in M9 and washed in M9 to remove bacteria, then snaped frozen in liquid nitrogen and lysed in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors (Roche) via sonication on ice. Samples are centrifuged at 10 rcf at 4 °C for 15 min. Supernatant is collected into fresh chilled tubes and kept on ice. Using a GSH assay kit (SigmaAldrich MAK440), samples were mixed with DNTB and the byproduct of this reaction 5′-thio-2-nitrobenxoic acid (TNB) was measured at 410 nm on a Biotek Synergy 2 plate reader. GSH concentration was calculated from the standard curve (SigmaAldrich MAK440) and normalized by level of protein concentration in all samples. The samples and standards were assayed in duplicate, and the experiment was independently repeated with fresh samples three times.

Quantification and statistical analysis

A one-way analysis of variance with a Tukey post hoc analysis was used to compare parametric data with equal variance. Normality of the data was determined using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Non-parametric data was analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn's post hoc analysis. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered significant. All statistical analyses were performed using Graph Pad Prism (10.6.1) software.
