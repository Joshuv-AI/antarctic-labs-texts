How to Boost Research Productivity: An American Council of Educators in Plastic Surgery Cross-sectional Survey
Authors: Shannon Su, Ambika Menon, Christopher Stewart, Hannah Jones, Paul Ghareeb
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Understanding barriers to research is crucial for optimizing the productivity and impact of busy physician-scientists and is an increasingly important metric for students and physicians to advance their careers. The purpose of this study was to identify common barriers to research productivity.Methods:A survey was distributed to members of the American Council of Educators in Plastic Surgery. The questions focused on demographics, research productivity, and barriers to research. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed, with significance defined as a P value less than 0.05.Results:A total of 109 people responded to the survey: 42 medical students, 19 plastic surgery residents or fellows, 7 junior attendings, and 41 senior attendings. Among people who had more than 10 completed projects, 53.8% also had fewer than 5 incomplete projects. A comparison between successful individuals and the remainder of the cohort found a difference in the percentage of people whose programs had dedicated research staff, provided institutional support for research, and offered travel reimbursement. Predictive factors for success included being a post-graduate year1–3 (odds ratio [OR] 13.3, P = 0.006), junior attending (OR 10.5, P = 0.026), or senior attending (OR 7.8, P = 0.001); having dedicated research staff (OR = 6.1, P = 0.0019); and receiving travel reimbursement (OR 3.1, P = 0.032).Conclusions:Programs that support research productivity with dedicated research staff and adequate travel reimbursement are likely to have research success. In addition, individuals with high academic productivity are more likely to have the highest number of unsuccessful projects.

Takeaways

Question: What are the common barriers to research productivity that are specific to medical students, residents, and attending surgeons in plastic surgery?

Findings: Individuals who were more successful in research had more failed projects, were more likely to be senior or junior attendings, and were more likely to work at an institution that provides travel reimbursement and dedicated research staff.

Meaning: Individuals with high academic productivity are likely to have the highest number of successful and unsuccessful projects. Programs can support research productivity with dedicated research staff and adequate travel reimbursement to promote success.

INTRODUCTION

Research productivity is an important factor at every level when seeking a career in academic plastic surgery. Research achievement is an increasingly important metric for medical students seeking to match into plastic surgery, especially with recent changes in standardized testing and medical school grading scales that have removed objective metrics used to evaluate students. Research is required in many surgical residency programs, often without dedicated research time being available to residents. Additionally, research productivity is often a metric by which fellowship programs evaluate applicants.1,2 Most notably, research is a key component for career advancement and promotion in academia.3 With the increasing importance of research in plastic surgery, understanding the barriers to research is crucial to optimizing the productivity and impact of busy surgeon-scientists.

The purpose of this study was to identify common barriers to research productivity that are specific to medical students, residents, and attending surgeons in plastic surgery to offer guidance for plastic surgeons and institutions trying to maximize research productivity. Our hypothesis was that specific institutional and individual factors would predict academic success and productivity.

METHODS

A survey was distributed to all members of the American Council of Educators in Plastic Surgery via email to investigate factors influencing research productivity. The survey was also distributed via email to medical students at the senior author’s institution. Respondents were de-identified to maintain anonymity and were grouped based on career level (medical student, resident/fellow, junior attending, senior attending). Junior attendings are defined as surgeons who are less than 5 years into practice following the final year of their training; senior attendings are defined as those who are more than 5 years into practice following the final year of their training. Those who elected to participate in the survey answered a total of 43 questions. The questions focused on demographics, career level, details of research productivity, and factors that may have affected completion, such as institutional research support, prior degrees obtained, access to research staff, or travel reimbursement (document 1). All statistical analysis was performed using MATLAB. Univariate analysis was performed with Student t test and Pearson χ2 test of independence. Multivariate analysis was performed using logistic regression. Statistical significance was defined as a P value less than 0.05.

RESULTS

A total of 109 people responded to the survey (9.1% response rate); 42 were medical students, 19 were plastic surgery residents or fellows, 7 were junior attendings, and 41 were senior attendings (Table 1). Of the 19 residents and fellows, 14 had research as a requirement for their program. Among the 48 attendings, 42 worked at academic institutions. Overall, 39 people had more than 10 completed projects, and 31 had more than 5 incomplete projects, with the senior attendings being responsible for most successes and failures. In the group of people who had more than 10 completed projects, 53.8% also had more than 5 incomplete projects (Table 2). The most common stage at which a project failed was during writing of the manuscript (n = 43), followed by awaiting institutional review board approval (n = 33). Thirty people spent 16–40 hours and 22 people spent 8–16 hours on projects that did not reach completion in the last 5 years.

When comparing high productivity individuals to the remainder of the cohort, there was a statistically significant difference in the percentage of people whose programs had dedicated research staff, provided institutional support for research, and offered travel reimbursement. There was no statistically significant difference in whether programs had dedicated research meetings, access to a dedicated statistician, availability of PhD for collaboration, or ease of navigating the institutional review board. Surprisingly, there was no difference in the proportion of people with mentors (Table 2). Among people who had more than 5 failed projects, there was only a statistically significant difference in the percentage of people whose programs provided travel reimbursement. No other statistically significant differences were found (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

Research remains an integral part of plastic surgery training and academic plastic surgery. There is a statistically significant difference in the number of publications between students who match at top-tier programs and those at lower tier programs, with top-tier programs selecting students with more publications.4–7 There is also a statistically significant relationship between resident productivity and institutional tier, which may reflect resident selection preferences or institutional research support. In a cross-sectional survey of 851 plastic surgeons nationwide, Klifto et al8 found that the number of last-author publications was an independent predictor of promotion both within an institution and between institutions. Furthermore, the H-index was an independent predictor of promotion when advancing to higher tiers of institutions at the associate professor level and when advancing at both top- and lower tier institutions.8 The purpose of our study was to determine whether there are any factors influencing success in research to provide insight into becoming more successful physician-scientists.

The results of this study provide important insight into the reasons for success and failure in academic plastic surgery. Our results suggest that there are various factors that contribute to success, with one of the most prevalent factors identified being institutional travel reimbursement. Although travel reimbursement may not be directly related to research productivity, it likely acts as a surrogate marker for overall institutional budget allocation toward research endeavors. As such, institutions that invest in their research program likely reap more returns than those that do not.

The majority of current literature addresses whether research is an important factor when applying to plastic surgery residency as a medical student, which has been extensively studied. There is a positive correlation between research productivity as a medical student and the probability of matching. However, few studies exist that provide insight into the reasons people have success in general, whether at the student, resident, or attending level. In a survey study distributed to 1 cohort of internal medicine residents, respondents cited insufficient time (79%), inadequate research skills (45%), and lack of a research curriculum (44%) as barriers to completing research. Furthermore, few residents believed that various research skills, such as research design, abstract writing, and data analysis, were thoroughly taught at their institution. Residents reported various suggestions for programs to support their research, most notably including providing adequate protected time, having a research curriculum, and making funding available.9 More research is needed to identify and eliminate barriers encountered by surgeons when conducting research (Tables 5, 6).

Furthermore, little is known about the decision-making process within residency program leadership. The issue of research productivity becomes increasingly important when considering the sex, racial, and ethnic disparities in academic output. One study of more than 31,000 US medical students found that women, Black students, and Hispanic students had significantly fewer publications than men, White students, and Asian students.10 Many medical students take 1 or more years during medical school to complete funded or unfunded clinical research fellowships to gain a competitive edge in the match. Students from lower income backgrounds likely have fewer financial resources to attend national conferences or take unpaid research opportunities. Mellia et al11 demonstrated a significant difference in total publications and first-author publications between students who completed medical school in 4 years versus those who took a nontraditional route. However, they did not find a difference between groups of applicants who matched at the top 20–ranked plastic surgery programs. More work is needed to understand and improve upon these differences to level the playing field for applicants.

There are a few lessons that students, trainees, and plastic surgeons can take from these results. Our study found that individuals who were more successful also had more failed projects, alluding to the reality that not every project started will reach completion. In addition, career level played a significant role in the probability of success. This may be due to the fact that senior attendings can lead more projects at any given moment due to differences in the time commitment of a principal investigator versus a resident performing chart review, thus increasing their chances of success. In addition, senior attendings with greater research experience are likely better able to design studies with a higher likelihood of success than more junior individuals. For those aiming to be more successful in research, it is important to strongly consider institutional values regarding research when selecting a position. Plastic surgeons may use the results of this study to negotiate for increased financial support for research, including travel funding, when searching for jobs. In addition, individuals should aim to create as many projects as they are able to or interested in, as the number of projects initiated likely leads to greater downstream productivity.

For plastic surgeons who are able to enact change at an institutional level and are hoping to increase the research productivity of their group, one might consider allotting a greater budget for research, including reimbursement for travel and hiring dedicated research staff. The creation of a medical student clinical research fellowship at academic institutions may be beneficial for all parties involved. Carney et al12 found a greater than 4-fold increase in published articles in the 8-year period following the initiation of a research fellowship position compared with the 8 years prior. Furthermore, all 8 of their fellows matched into either integrated or independent research positions, which highlights the impact on both the institution and the students involved.12

Numerous limitations exist in our study. Our study had a small sample size, so multivariate modeling was limited given the small number of positive samples within our sample. Additionally, sampling bias was evident, as most participants were medical students and attendings, likely due to the limited free time or lack of interest among individuals at the resident level. Finally, reporting bias is a risk due to the nature of the study.

CONCLUSIONS

Research remains an integral component of evidence-based medicine, scientific discovery, and career advancement in academic plastic surgery. This study identifies several factors that may help future physician-scientists increase research productivity while reducing inefficiency. Individuals with high academic productivity are more likely to have the highest number of unsuccessful projects as well. Programs that support research initiatives with dedicated research staff and adequate travel reimbursement are likely to experience more success.
