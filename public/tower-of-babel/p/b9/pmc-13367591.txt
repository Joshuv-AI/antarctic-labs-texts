Effectiveness of community based HIV screening through targeted intervention project among female sex workers in East Delhi: A mixed methods study
Authors: Nazish Rasheed, Sanjeev K. Rasania, Anita S. Acharya, Ananya R. Laskar, Shibajee Debbarma, Shubham Vivekanand Chawale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Community-based screening (CBS) for (HIV) Human Immunodeficiency Virus is a critical strategy to enhance testing coverage and early diagnosis among high-risk groups (HRGs). Female sex workers (FSWs) face unique structural and interpersonal barriers to accessing HIV testing services. This study assessed the effectiveness and operational challenges of CBS implementation among FSWs through targeted intervention (TI) projects in East Delhi.Methods:A mixed-methods design was employed, combining quantitative record-based analysis with qualitative inquiry. Quantitative data were extracted from two randomly selected TI sites for April 2021–March 2022 to assess CBS indicators. Qualitative data were obtained from eight in-depth interviews with TI staff and eight focus group discussions with FSWs. Data were analysed descriptively and thematically to identify patterns and triangulate findings.Results:A total of 4582 beneficiaries were screened through CBS, of which 19.3% were first-time testers. Partner testing uptake was minimal (17 partners screened). Qualitative data revealed CBS was preferred over Integrated Counselling and Testing Centres due to convenience, confidentiality, and minimal income loss. Barriers included stockouts, logistical delays, inadequate remuneration for TI staff, and stigma at facility-based testing centres.Conclusion:CBS significantly improved HIV testing coverage and accessibility among FSWs in East Delhi. However, gaps in linkage to ART and supply chain disruptions limit its impact. Strengthening logistics, enhancing staff incentives, and integrating mobile confirmatory testing can further optimize CBS implementation.

Introduction

The burden of HIV in terms of prevalence in India was estimated as 0.20% in the year 2022. People living with HIV (PLHIV) in India were estimated to be 24.67 lakhs in 2022.[1] Although there is a declining trend of HIV burden from 2010 to 2021, the epidemic of HIV in India is more concentrated in geographic regions and among high-risk groups (HRGs). Also, despite the declining trend, stigma, poor accessibility, and social barriers continue to hinder HIV testing uptake among FSWs.

The majority of PLHIV are from eight states (Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Uttar Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, Telangana, Bihar, and Gujarat). High-risk groups (HRGs) for HIV include female sex workers (FSW), men sex with men (MSM), Hijra (H)/Transgender (TG), injectable drug users (IDU) and bridge populations such as migrants and long-distance truckers.[2] These groups are provided with a number of preventive services through targeted intervention (TI) sites.[3]

Currently, around 1443 TI sites are running through non-government organisations or community-based organisations. TI sites provide a package of prevention, support and linkage services to HRGs through drop-incentre (DIC) and outreach-based service delivery model which includes screening for and treatment of sexually transmitted infections, distribution of free barriers contraceptives, behavior change communication, creating an enabling environment with community involvement and participation, linkage to integrated counselling and testing services (CTS) for confirmation of HIV, linkages with care and support services for HIV-positive HRGs, community mobilization, ownership building and specifically for IDUs, free distribution of sterile needles and syringes, abscess prevention and management, and opioid substitution therapy and linkages with detoxification/rehabilitation services. This is built on the peer-led approach in partnership with non-governmental organisations (NGO)/community-based organisations (CBOs) along with the State AIDS Control Society (SACS), and Technical Support Unit.[4]

Community-based screening (CBS) for HIV is an effective intervention tool to reach people, especially HRGs who seldom use clinical services.[5] It is carried out through various approaches such as screening by an ancillary health care provider, screening by targeted intervention-Integrated Counselling and Testing Centres (TI-ICTC), mobile HIV CTS (HCTS), HCTS at the workplace, or HCTS by TI-ICTC. Currently, Delhi SACS (DSACS) is implementing CBS through 73 TI partners (31 for FSWs, 11 for MSM, six for TGs, 10 for IDUs, 12 for Migrants, and three for truckers).[6] As per National AIDS Control Organisation (NACO), all HRGs should be tested for HIV at least once in every 6 months. Despite so much efforts, HRGs faced substantial structural and interpersonal barriers to accessing services, and hence increasing the risk of HIV acquisition.[7] The National AIDS Control Programme (NACP) also faces various challenges of increasing the coverage for HIV testing, including care and treatment services among HRGs.[8] NACO acknowledged that there is need of conducting operational research among TI sites to understand the effectiveness and challenges faced in the implementation of CBS. This study was conducted to study the overall effectiveness of CBS in early diagnosis of HIV among FSWs.

Community-based HIV screening reflects the core values of primary care by promoting accessible, people-centred, continuous, and community-focused health services. Family physicians and primary care providers are often the initial point of contact for marginalized and HRGs, including FSWs, and are central to facilitating timely HIV testing, counselling, referral, and ongoing care. By examining the performance and implementation challenges of community-based HIV screening within TI projects, this study offers context-specific evidence to support primary care–driven outreach models, shared service delivery, and community participation. The findings are particularly relevant for family medicine and primary care practitioners engaged in public health initiatives, urban primary healthcare settings, and integrated HIV prevention and treatment services.

Methodology

This convergent parallel mixed methods study was conducted at TI sites integrating quantitative and qualitative components. FSWs were registered under these TI sites. In the year 2020–21, there were four TI sites run by non-governmental organisations in the East District of Delhi. Two TI sites were selected randomly from all the above four sites.

Quantitative data were collected using a set of indicators framed to estimate the screening yield and service utilization for CBS in one year. Screening indicators were total screening for HIV conducted through CBS in one year; number of community mobilization activities conducted in one year; first-time testers among screened; number symptomatic and number reactive among those screened; and number of partners and spouses agreed and actually screened for HIV. Service utilization indicators were the number of commodities distributed annually; number of FSWs availing commodities (condom) annually and; number of test kits utilized annually. For qualitative component, eight in-depth interviews (IDIs) were conducted with TI staff, including the Project Director, Program Manager, Counsellor, Medical Officer, Peer Educator, and Outreach Worker. Eight focus group discussions (FGDs) were held with six to eight FSWs each to capture perceptions, experiences, and barriers to CBS participation. Audio-recorded data were transcribed verbatim, translated, and analysed using thematic content analysis. Triangulation of data sources was applied to ensure validity.

Quantitative data were entered, cleaned, and analysed using Microsoft Excel.

The time period for data collection was year 2021.

Ethical approval

Obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee.

Informed consent was obtained, and confidentiality was maintained throughout.

Results

FSWs registered were 4008 in both TI sites (TI site 1- 486, TI site 2- 3522). The total number of community-based mobilization activities conducted in one year was 228. Screening of HIV/AIDS through CBS was done for 4582 beneficiaries (4008 were FSWs), out of which 2600 were from TI site 1 and 1982 TI site 2. First-time CBS testing was conducted on 19.3% beneficiaries (889/4582). Total partners and spouses who agreed and screened through CBS were 17, all of whom were from TI site 1. A total of 109 micro-plans were planned and available in both sites [Table 1]. One screen positive FSW (TI site 2) was diagnosed with a confirmatory test at ICTC no partner/spouse came up positive in the screening test. Both sites utilized 4651 CBS testing kits in one year (2620 from TI site 1 and 2031 from TI site 2). The total number of commodities distributed in one year was 7,40,508, out of which 54% (4,00,350) were distributed in TI site 1 and the remaining from TI site 2 (46%) [Table 2].

The qualitative component highlighted CBS as a convenient, stigma-free, and community-trusted approach. FSWs preferred CBS over facility-based testing due to reduced time loss and confidentiality. Staff reported improved rapport and engagement with network operators, facilitating new enrolments. However, challenges included logistic constraints, intermittent test kit shortages, and delays in salary disbursement. Fear of discrimination and limited ART linkage were recurrent themes.

HRGs highlighted that CBS has improved accessibility for FSWs by bringing testing services to their doorstep. This eliminated the need to visit ICTCs, which were perceived as time-consuming and economically disadvantageous.

But, one of the Program Manager at the TI revealed:

“There are some issues in CBS because our female sex workers keep changing their locations.”

The Program Manager at one of the TI reported that there was a preference of CBS testing over facility-based testing by HRGs:

“For some ICTC visits we try to persuade them, but they don’t go quickly. They mostly prefer CBS. Because at that time we feel it takes only 10–15 minutes, whereas if they go to the ICTC, it takes half to one hour for the travel and the process. So they feel that their clients get affected, their source of income decreases, and their time gets wasted. That’s why they tell us to mostly do CBS for them, so that your work also continues and they can also manage their clients.”

An IDI with the counsellor at the TI site revealed:

“We can say that since Community based testing has been introduced in TIs, it has benefited a lot because the female sex workers who were our community sisters were not ready to go to the ICTC because of either client load or their drinking habit, they wake up mostly by 12:00 noon. So many times with ICTC, it used to happen that… as their official time to remain working is only till noon, they would then stop by 1:00 pm and did not do testing thereafter. These sisters get up late because they have to work at night. The benefit now is that we inform them beforehand, because even the program has now become network based, we see every network, who has how many dues. We shortlist those are due for HIV testing, and those who are not able to go to hospital, who are not able to go to ICTC. We then shortlist them, and we inform the network-based operator who have these sisters that their testing is due, call them today, get their HIV test done. So she then tells us to come on a particular day and time. Then, our staff goes there - counselor, ORW, go there and get their CBS done and tested. It has happened that there is no longer any dropout.”

FGD with FSWs at the TI site revealed their apprehensions with getting tested in a hospital setting at ICTC-

“we are afraid when we go to the hospital because somebody may think that they have HIV that is why they have come to get tested”.

Outreach workers and peer educators frequently mentioned that time constraints and competing economic priorities among FSWs posed a major challenge in ensuring consistent participation in CBS activities. This underlines the need for flexible scheduling, frequent follow-ups, and incentivization strategies to improve participation in CBS among FSWs.

Discussion

HIV burden in India is more concentrated in nature and higher burden in HRGs. CBS, although it is an effective tool, has limited availability. This study highlights the functioning of TI sites with respect to CBS among FSWs. FSWs are disproportionately affected by HIV due to a range of structural, social, and behavioral factors. The total number of beneficiaries screened through CBS (N = 4582) exceeded the registered FSW (n = 4008) population by 14.3%, reflecting inclusion of non-FSW beneficiaries such as partners. This shows FSWs are more willing to go for community-based testing rather than visiting the ICTC. This could be due to less stigma faced and more accessibility of CBS. A study by Goldenberg[9] also showed similar findings and suggested the need to scale up community-based tailored services for HIV/STI testing. Systematic review done by Atuhaire et al.[10] also reported that there is a significant impact of the community interventions on HIV testing. Although the improvement will not be sustained if not delivered continuously.

Early diagnosis of HIV is crucial for effective treatment and reducing the spread of the virus. CBS facilitates early diagnosis by making testing more routine and accessible. Early diagnosis is vital for initiating antiretroviral therapy (ART) promptly, which can improve health outcomes and reduce HIV transmission rates. This study estimates that 19.4% of the tests were done among FSWs as first-time testing in one year. Peer-led mobilization and flexible scheduling contributed to higher participation. Linkage of HIV-positive individuals to appropriate care and treatment is a crucial component of effective HIV management. In this study, only one FSW was found reactive among all the screened in one year, which was referred for confirmation of HIV and further linkage with the ART centre. Testing of spouses/partners of FSWs is also an important indicator of the effectiveness of the screening programme for HIV. In this study, only 17 spouses/partners agreed and tested for HIV through CBS. The reason for this could be due to the stigma attached. This study was conducted in an urban area and in a metropolitan city; the findings observed may not be representative and extrapolated to other TI sites across the country. Also, the discrepancy in data among the sites could be due to differences in data quality management.

Despite its success, several operational challenges were identified, including inadequate logistics, stockouts, and delayed ART linkage. The transition of FSWs to network-based or virtual sex work necessitates innovative outreach strategies. TI staff faced demotivation due to salary delays and inadequate recognition. Strengthening human resource policies and logistical management under NACP-V is essential for sustained program success. Integrating CBS within a broader community empowerment framework strengthens program sustainability and fosters lasting behavioral change by promoting local ownership and reducing reliance on external support systems. Evidence indicates that empowerment-oriented interventions among FSWs can substantially enhance HIV testing participation, improve adherence to treatment, and build confidence in practicing safer sexual behaviors.[1112]

Moreover, the use of digital and mobile health technologies offers valuable support to CBS by facilitating confidential self-testing, providing reminders, and ensuring follow-up engagement—thereby overcoming challenges related to mobility and privacy among HRGs.[13] Innovative peer-led and technology-enabled CBS strategies have been shown to extend the reach of testing services to difficult-to-access populations, including those involved in online or network-based sex work.[14] Addressing stigma and discriminatory practices through ongoing sensitization and training of healthcare providers remains crucial for improving linkage to care and timely initiation of ART.[15]

This study was limited to two urban TIs, which may affect generalizability. Perspectives of unregistered FSWs were not captured. Quantitative data relied on TI records, which may have reporting biases.

Conclusion

CBS for HIV among FSWs is a highly effective strategy for increasing HIV testing rates, achieving early diagnosis, and improving linkage to care. The study’s findings support the expansion of CBS initiatives as a crucial component of comprehensive HIV prevention and care strategies targeting FSWs. Continued investment in CBS, along with the efforts to address the associated challenges, will be vital in mitigating the impact of HIV among this vulnerable population and advancing global health goals. The recommendations include maintaining an uninterrupted supply of HIV testing kits, condoms, and essential STI medications while improving coordination between CBS and ART services, including the use of mobile confirmation units; and offering flexible ICTC operating hours that suit the work schedules of FSWs, along with ensuring regular training and timely salary revisions for TI staff. Additionally, integrating CBS with digital tools for better tracking and follow-up is encouraged.
