Evaluation of the utility of surgical simulation software in medical postgraduate education
Authors: Bilali Aizezi, Cheng Zhong, Xuanwei Wang, Weiyi Diao, Quan Wang, Miaoda Shen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe use of medical simulation software in medical institutions is growing to address teaching challenges. The study aims to assess the effectiveness of the Touch Surgery app, a free medical simulation software, as a supplement to the teaching methodology of unified surgery for medical students.MethodsTwenty-three postgraduate students, divided into three groups based on residency training years, and five senior orthopedic surgeons took part in the study. Each participant completed three modules within the Large External Fixators module using the Touch Surgery app. The results achieved by orthopedic specialists and postgraduates in the test module were recorded separately. The orthopedic specialists' first test module scores were used as a baseline. A Likert survey assessed the usefulness and content accuracy of the app. Statistical analysis included analysis of variance and 2-tailed t-test.ResultsThe first and second scores achieved by the postgraduates in the three groups were significantly different from the baseline of the orthopedic specialists, but the mean scores of all postgraduate's groups increased with simulator usage for learning and by the third attempt they were at the same level as the orthopedic specialists. However, no significant performance differences were observed between postgraduates with different training years. Postgraduates expressed a positive opinion of the usefulness and content of Touch Surgery in the questionnaire and their intention to continue using it in their future studies.ConclusionsThis study shows that the use of Touch Surgery improves postgraduates' performance and ultimately enables them to reach the level of orthopedic specialists in simulated surgery, and received positive ratings for the software's experience and content. Touch Surgery can be a valuable complement to traditional surgical teaching methods, bridging the gap between theoretical learning from textbooks and guided rehearsals of surgical steps and skills.Keywords: Large external fixator, Touch surgery, Orthopedic simulation, Surgical app, Medical education

Introduction

Surgery is a critical part of medical practice that captures the interest of many medical students during their clinical apprenticeships and internships. However, traditional textbooks tend to be limiting when it comes to teaching surgical techniques. Due to the nature of surgical skills, which require visual and motor learning, numerous intricate surgical procedures are often excluded from textbooks. The mastery of these skills can only be attained through clinical practice [1]. The practice of medicine in China is fraught with challenges, including a strained dynamic between physicians and patients, heightened patient vigilance, a notable degree of clinical risk, and limited opportunities for hands-on training among medical students. Such realities have a profound impact on the acquisition of clinical skills among students, particularly in light of the primary challenge of mastering basic skills within a safe and non-clinical context. To address this pressing issue, medical simulation technology has emerged as a crucial tool for enhancing clinical medicine education. Medical simulation teaching involves the utilization of a diverse array of technical modalities to simulate clinical scenarios, thereby providing a safe and repeatable learning environment for medical students to help them improve various clinical competencies. Medical simulation teaching offers a wealth of standardized and uniform learning and training materials, effectively addressing the challenges of insufficient clinical teaching resources and evaluation standardization. By expediting the learning curve of students’ clinical practice, medical simulation teaching ultimately enhances the quality and safety of medical care [2]. As a result, the American College of Surgeons Accreditation and Education Institute (ACS-AEI) has adopted the resounding slogan of “Using simulation to improve patient safety” in recent years [3]. The Royal College of Trauma and Orthopedic Surgeons of England (RCSEng) believes that simulation training will become increasingly important in the next five years and requires surgeons to use surgical simulators for training [4]. However, in the past, simulation teaching was constrained by limitations in machine and space availability, thereby restricting the scope of virtual reality training to lumpectomy-related procedures. The high cost of training and equipment acquisition further limited audience participation to only those surgeons who could afford such resources, thereby creating a barrier to entry for many [5,6].

Touch Surgery is a revolutionary smartphone-based medical simulation that uses Cognitive Task Analysis (CTA) to deconstruct the key steps and surgical decisions of a surgical operation [7]. The approach, combined with surgical animations, creates an interactive, immersive Virtual Reality (VR) and Cognitive Task Stimulator and Rehearsal (CTSR) tool, enabling students to train on demand in any setting at any time. This innovative technology provides a safe environment for students to learn and grow while allowing room for mistakes. It provides 3D surgical simulation animations covering numerous surgical departments, including orthopedics and hepatobiliary surgery [8,9].

The utilization of a large external fixator is a fundamental practice used in orthopedic surgery to provide stable fixation for bones, thereby enabling the correction of fractures, deformities, and other conditions. The external fixator is typically composed of metal pins, rods, and rings that are attached to the bones and stabilized by a framework of metal struts. These devices can be utilized to manage a diverse range of medical conditions, including but not limited to complex fractures, limb deformities, and non-unions. They allow a superior mean of correcting conditions compared to alternative treatment modalities, affording greater precision and control. In contrast to internal fixators, external fixators enable physical therapy to be conducted during the recovery period, while also minimizing the risk of complications such as infection. Overall, large external fixators are a versatile and effective tool in orthopedic surgery and widely used in clinical practice to improve patient outcomes.

The main objective of this study was to verify whether the adoption and application of surgical cognitive simulation exercises can improve the acquisition of technically challenging surgical skills among medical students. Additionally, the secondary objective was to analyse the users’ evaluation of the content and experience of using the Touch Surgery simulation software.

Methods

Touch surgery application

Touch Surgery is a digital surgical simulation platform that allows healthcare professionals to learn and practice various surgical procedures through virtual simulations. Touch Surgery uses cognitive task analysis (CTA) to deconstruct surgical provides procedures into key steps and operative decisions. The platform provides step-by-step guidance and realistic haptic feedback to help improve surgical skills and techniques, as shown in Fig. 1. These steps combined with surgical animations create an interactive and immersive virtual reality (VR) cognitive task simulation and rehearsal (CTSR) tool [8]. It is designed for both surgical trainees and experienced surgeons to help them improve their surgical performance and prepare for complex procedures. The “Tibia Shaft Fractures, large external fixator: modular frame” (LEF) of Touch Surgery consists of five independent parts certified by two authoritative experts [10].

Touch surgery user interface.

Study participants

Five orthopedic specialists and twenty-three postgraduates of different grades in the Standardized Training of Residents in the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University were randomly recruited into the project. The Department of Orthopedics of the First Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine is one of the first orthopedic residency training bases in Zhejiang Province, and also serves as one of the rotating departments of our surgical base, training about 150 residents each year, with rich experience in residency training. Postgraduates include students from orthopedics and other departments. The twenty-three postgraduates were divided into three groups according to the number of years of Standardized Training of Residents they had received. Inclusion criteria of these three groups included that they must be residents in the Standardized Training of Residents and have never been exposed to Touch Survey. Inclusion criteria for orthopedic specialists was that they must be able to perform large external fixators as a lead surgeon and have never been exposed to Touch Surgery. Exclusion criteria for all groups was dropping out of the study.

Study procedure

Prior to the start of the experiment, the purpose and procedure (Fig. 2) of the trial was explained to all participants. Then, all participants were given sufficient time and comprehensive information to familiarize themselves with the basics of Touch Surgery in order to exclude the effects of proficiency with smart devices, but were not allowed to use the Large External Fixator module in advance. After familiarizing themselves with the basic use of the software, the five orthopedic specialists only had to complete the Large External Fixator Test Module once and record their scores as baseline. For three groups of postgraduates, the Large External Fixator Learn Module and Large External Fixator Test Module should be completed by all participants three times in a row. For each cycle, scores produced by the Touch Surgery app were recorded.

Flowchart.

Post-questionnaire

After three cycles have been completed, the Touch Surgery app's face validity, user happiness with the app, and the content validity of the surgical simulations were all evaluated using a five-point Likert questionnaire containing 14 items given to all postgraduates. Each score was defined from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The participants' replies to the post-questionnaire, which included three dimensions—the face validity of the software (type A questions), user satisfaction (type B questions), and the content validity of the surgical simulation (type C questions)—were also recorded [3].

Statistical analysis

All records were entered into a database, and differences across groups were examined using an analysis of variance and descriptive statistics, as well as within groups using a 2-tailed Student t-test. As the difference in postgraduates' professions in this experiment, in addition to grade level, may have had an impact on postgraduates' proficiency in LEF, a two-way ANOVA was also used to analyse the effect of grade and specialty of postgraduates on performance in the first LEF test module. Cronbach's alpha reliability analysis was used to analyse the reliability of the questionnaire. A result was considered statistically significant when a two-tailed p-value <0.05 [11,12].

Results

Demographics

Five orthopedic specialists and twenty-three postgraduates were initially recruited to the study without any drop-outs. The twenty-three postgraduates were divided into three groups according to the number of years of inpatient training they had received. 3 of the postgraduates were from the orthopedic department and the rest were from other departments. The First-year of residency group (n = 9) included 2 orthopedic graduate students. The Second-year of residency group (n = 6) had no orthopedic graduate students. The Third-year of residency (n = 8) included 1 orthopedic graduate student.

From Table 1 we can see that the mean scores of all postgraduate students who completed the LEF test module for the first time were significantly different from the mean scores of the five orthopedic specialists (p < 0.05). After completing two rounds of the LEF learning and testing module, the postgraduate students scored much higher than they did the first time, but there was still a significant difference between the scores of the orthopedic specialists (p < 0.05). This significant difference was eliminated when the postgraduate students completed three rounds of learning and testing (p > 0.05). The initial huge gap between postgraduates and orthopedic specialists can be eliminated after at least 3 rounds of study and testing, and postgraduates can achieve the same level of performance as orthopedic specialists in simulated surgery.

Discussion

The current state of medical education in China is generally considered to be moving rapidly in a positive direction. However, there is room for improvement in certain areas, such as enhancing hands-on training experiences, improving the use of technology in medical education, Protection of patients’ safety and addressing the shortage of healthcare professionals in certain regions. The COVID-19 pandemic has highlighted the need for effective and flexible medical education.

Based on the above, the medical simulation software represented by Touch Surgery can provide valuable insights and inspiration for medical education in China, as the benefits of Touch Surgery have been proven by many people. Analog [14] and electronic [15,16], simulation techniques have both been shown in the literature on general surgery to increase surgical skill in randomized controlled trials. Similar studies have shown that simulator training is effective in the fields of Gynecology [17], Orthopedics [18], Anesthesiology [19], and Endovascular Surgery [20]. There is also a randomized controlled trial directly demonstrated that simulator training resulted in improved operating room effectiveness in inexperienced trainees [21]. In addition, in low-income countries such as Rwanda [2] and Ghana [22], simulation teaching software such as Touch Surgery has also been shown in studies to have a positive effect on improving the quality of medical education and improving the skills of medical staff.

In our trial, we recruited five orthopedic specialists and twenty-three postgraduate students of various years of residency to evaluate whether the medical simulator Touch Surgery could be a useful addition to the unified surgical teaching method for medical students. At the very beginning, postgraduates and orthopedic specialists showed a significant difference in performance in simulated surgery, however postgraduates can eliminate this difference through repetition to achieve the same level of performance as the orthopedic specialists.

In addition, all five orthopedic specialists achieved a perfect score of 100 on the second completion of the LEF test module. It is possible that cultural and linguistic differences contributed to their failure to score 100 on the first attempt.

We divided the participants into 3 groups according to the number of years of residency because we initially expected that there would be a gap in performance on the LEF module between students with different years of residency. In a trial conducted among medical undergraduates, residents and surgeons, participants' performance on the simulator was significantly correlated with their level of experience [3]. However, our trial data showed that the different years of residency and specialisms did not have a significant effect on the mean initial performance or mean final performance of the participants on LEF test module. We believe that such a result is due to two reasons. Firstly, it may be that the skills training of our hospital's resident trainees has not been done properly, and the operational skills that should be mastered by different grades are not well stratified. Secondly, it is possible that the gap in years on which our groupings are based is too small compared to other published trials, resulting in no gap between the different groupings. It may be a coincidence that at the second attempt of the LEF module, the third-year residents scored significantly lower than the other two groups, as we could find no other plausible explanation.

When analysing the within-group differences after we had determined that the initial levels of the postgraduates in the three groups were comparable, it was evident that the scores of all participants increased with more attempts. This result proves that simulation training has a positive effect on the participants' professional knowledge and operational level, and that, within certain limits, this positive enhancement effect rises with the number of training sessions. Based on Gallagher et al.’s theory that surgical training is made up of psychomotor and cognitive elements, training on the simulator enhances the trainee's cognition and makes them more focused on the psychomotor aspects associated with surgical training, thus improving the trainee's overall performance in surgery [23].

In the questionnaires we collected, the average score for all questions was greater than 4, except for one question which had an average score of 3.9. This means that the simulation software, as represented by Touch Surgery, was well received by the participants in terms of face validity and content validity, and that they were satisfied with the software and expressed a strong desire to continue using the simulator in the future. We analysed our questionnaire using Cronbach's alpha reliability analysis, which scored above 0.7 on all three dimensions, which means that the reliability of our questionnaire is good and the conclusions drawn above are more representative.

There are some shortcomings in our experiment. First, the differences among participants recruited to our trial were too small and the sample size was small, which resulted in no significant differences between the scores of our three groups. Secondly, our trial did not directly demonstrate that the effects of participants’ training on the simulator translated directly to their performance in the operating room. Thirdly, the experiment could have been improved if it had been compared with other learning methods. In future studies, we need larger sample sizes and better randomized controlled trials to assess the value of simulators in real-world teaching environments in China.

Conclusion

In this study, we used a cognitive task analysis simulation, Touch Surgery, to analyse the efficacy of simulation-based training on the acquisition of skills related to large external fixator learning in a cohort of five orthopedic specialists and twenty-three postgraduate medical students at various stages of residency training. We found that simulation training had a positive impact on the performance of the residents. Simulation-based teaching serves as a valuable supplement to the traditional surgical education of medical students, given the limited opportunities for hands-on learning in the operating theatre. By simulating a range of real-life surgical scenarios, simulation-based training affords trainees the opportunity to practice and rehearse cases multiple times. Our study highlights the need for more research in this area of teaching in China.
