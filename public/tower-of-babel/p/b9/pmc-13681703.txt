The RLK7–MPK3/6–LEAFY PETIOLE signaling axis mediates growth inhibition during PIP peptide-induced immunity in Arabidopsis
Authors: Uyen Thu Nguyen, Jin Sun Kim, Na Young Kang, Byeong Wook Jeon, Minsoo Kim, Ambrose Teru Patrick, Eunkyoo Oh, Sung Haeng Lee, Pil Joon Seo, Jeong-Il Kim, Jungmook Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) activate plant immunity through plasma membrane-localized receptor-like kinases (RLKs) and receptor-like proteins. The PAMP flg22 binds FLAGELLIN SENSING 2 (FLS2), inducing the expression of defense-related genes, including those encoding PAMP-INDUCED PEPTIDEs (PIPs), which enhance pathogen resistance via RLK7. However, the mechanisms underlying PAMP- and PIP-mediated growth–defense trade-off remain unclear. Here, we show that PIP1 and PIP2 suppress lateral root (LR) development and seedling growth through the RLK7–MPK3/6–LEAFY PETIOLE (LEP) signaling cascade, with LEP acting as a key node that inhibits LR formation and seedling growth. Exogenous application of PIP1/2 peptides and overexpression of prePIP1 and prePIP2 inhibit LR development, whereas mutations in prePIP1 or prePIP2 enhance primary root growth and accelerate LR develomental kinetics. Genetic analyses, coimmunoprecipitation, peptide-binding and kinase assays, rapamycin-induced dimerization, and a BIR3 chimera system collectively revealed that PIP1/2 recognition promotes the interaction of RLK7 with SOMATIC EMBRYOGENESIS RECEPTOR KINASE coreceptors, triggering MPK3/6-dependent phosphorylation events and inhibiting LR development. The transcription factor LEP acts downstream of RLK7–MPK6 signaling to inhibit LR development. Loss of LEP function alleviates PIP-induced inhibition of seedling growth and LR formation without compromising immune responses. These findings suggest that PIP1/2 peptides mediate the trade-off between growth and defense via the RLK7–MPK6–LEP signaling axis. The flg22 peptide suppresses LR formation and seedling growth in part through the PIP1/2–RLK7–MPK6–LEP signaling pathway, which bifurcates from immune responses downstream of MPK3/6. This study establishes a genetic framework for optimizing crop resilience by decoupling immune activation from growth suppression.

Introduction

Plants possess an innate immune system comprising pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) and effector-triggered immunity (ETI), which monitor biotic danger signals and defend against pathogen attack (Albert et al., 2020). Plants recognize microbial molecules, known as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), and plant-derived damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) via cell surface-localized receptor-like kinases (RLKs) and receptor-like proteins (RLPs) (Albert et al., 2020). RLKs and RLPs thus function as pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that initiate PTI (Couto and Zipfel, 2016). RLKs comprise an extracellular domain responsible for ligand binding, a single-pass transmembrane domain, and a cytoplasmic kinase domain (CKD), whereas RLPs share a similar structural configuration with RLKs but lack the kinase domain (Hohmann et al., 2017; Huang and Joosten, 2025). FLAGELLIN-SENSING 2 (FLS2) is a well-characterized leucine-rich repeat (LRR)-RLK that recognizes PAMPs (Couto and Zipfel, 2016; Huang and Joosten, 2025). FLS2 perceives bacterial flagellin by binding the epitope flg22, which acts as a molecular glue to form a complex between FLS2 and its coreceptor BRI1-ASSOCIATED KINASE 1 (BAK1), also known as SOMATIC EMBRYOGENESIS RECEPTOR KINASE 3 (SERK3), an LRR–RLK (Sun et al., 2013). This heterodimerization brings the CKDs of FLS2 and BAK1 into close proximity, thereby triggering downstream signaling cascades and activating plant immunity against bacterial infection (Zipfel et al., 2004; Chinchilla et al., 2007; Heese et al., 2007; Schulze et al., 2010; Roux et al., 2011). The flg22 peptide triggers reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts, Ca2+-dependent signaling, and activation of mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), ultimately inducing the expression of more than a thousand genes (Boller and Felix, 2009; Dubiella et al., 2013; Boudsocq et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2024).

The flg22 peptide also induces the expression of precursor genes that encode secreted peptides, including plant elicitor peptides (PEPs) and PAMP-induced peptides (PIPs), both of which activate components of the innate immune response (Huffaker et al., 2006; Huffaker and Ryan, 2007; Ryan et al., 2007; Hou et al., 2014). Among the three PIP and eight PIP-like (PIPL) genes in the Arabidopsis thaliana genome, PrePIP1 expression is upregulated by pathogens and elicitors such as methyl salicylate, methyl jasmonate, and flg22 (Hou et al., 2014). Exogenous application of PIP1 and PIP2 peptides to wild-type (WT) Arabidopsis plants and overexpression of prePIP1 and prePIP2 elicit immune responses, including the expression of marker genes (including FLG22-INDUCED RECEPTOR KINASE 1 (FRK1), WRKY30, WRKY33, and WRKY53), stomatal closure, ROS bursts, callose deposition, pathogen resistance, and inhibition of primary root growth (Hou et al., 2014). Moreover, the PIP1–RLK7 and PEP1–PEP RECEPTOR1 (PEPR1) signaling pathways act cooperatively to amplify FLS2-initiated immunity (Hou et al., 2014).

Plant defense responses against pathogens and insect herbivores are generally accompanied by growth suppression, reflecting a trade-off in which resources are diverted from growth to defense to optimize plant fitness—a phenomenon known as the growth–defense trade-off (Huot et al., 2014; Züst and Agrawal, 2017). For example, treatment with PAMPs, such as flg22, results in marked growth inhibition as resources are reallocated to support defense (Gomez-Gomez et al., 1999; Navarro et al., 2004, 2006; Chen et al., 2007; Chinchilla et al., 2007). Overexpression of PrePIP1 and PrePIP2 in Arabidopsis or exogenous application of peptides inhibits root and overall plant growth and activates defense responses (Hou et al., 2014). Understanding the molecular mechanisms by which plants balance growth and defense is critical for developing strategies to enhance plant performance and fitness. Crosstalk between defense signaling networks and phytohormone pathways plays a major role in regulating the growth–defense trade-off (Huot et al., 2014). However, the mechanisms underlying the modulation of the growth–defense trade-off mediated by PAMPs, such as flg22, and PIPs remain unclear. Additionally, whether growth inhibition is essential for effective immune responses remains unknown.

In this study, we demonstrate that PIP1/2 suppress lateral root (LR) development through the RLK7–MPK6–LEAFY PETIOLE (LEP) signaling cascade. Furthermore, we show that PIP1/2 promote the association of RLK7 with SERK coreceptors, triggering phosphorylation events that inhibit LR development mediated by MPK3/6 and LEP. Loss of LEP function alleviates flg22- and PIP1/2-induced inhibition of LR development and seedling growth without compromising immune responses. Additionally, we show that flg22-induced growth inhibition is mediated through the PIP1/2–RLK7–MPK6–LEP signaling pathway. Moreover, LEP-induced growth inhibition may be separable from immune responses downstream of MPK3/6, suggesting that immune activation can be decoupled from growth suppression to optimize crop resilience.

PIP1 and PIP2 peptides suppress lateral root development through the RLK7 receptor

Reporter lines expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) or β-glucuronidase (GUS) under the control of the prePIP1 promoter showed reporter activity in leaves, primary roots, and LRs (Hou et al., 2014). To characterize the expression pattern of PIP2, we generated transgenic lines carrying a prePIP2 promoter–GUS construct. Strong GUS staining was detected in leaves and roots, including LRs and LR primordia (LRPs) at all developmental stages—from stage I to emergence—suggesting a role for PIP2 in LR development (Supplemental Figure S1). In silico analysis further showed that prePIP2 expression was upregulated in response to various pathogens (Supplemental Figure S2). To investigate the functional role of PIP1/2 in LR development, we treated wild-type (WT) plants with increasing concentrations of PIP1 or PIP2 peptides, ranging from 1 nM to 10 μM. Although primary root growth was moderately inhibited, LR number and density (LR number per unit primary root length) were significantly reduced, indicating that PIP1 and PIP2 strongly suppress LR formation (Supplemental Figure S3).

We then generated pip1 and pip2 loss-of-function mutants using the CRISPR–Cas9 system, as T-DNA insertion lines were unavailable due to the small gene sizes. Two independent, transgene-free knockout alleles were obtained for each gene (Supplemental Figure S7). These mutants exhibited slightly longer primary roots but showed no significant difference in LR density compared with WT plants (Figure 1E). To further investigate PIP function in LR development, we performed a root-bending assay (Jeon et al., 2016). After 3 days of vertical growth, seedlings were subjected to 90° gravistimulation, and newly formed LRPs on the convex side were quantified at 24 and 48 h after gravitropic induction. The relative distribution of LRPs across developmental stages I to VIII was then determined according to Malamy and Benfey (1997). Overexpression of PIP1 or PIP2 delayed LR development, whereas the pip1 and pip2 loss-of-function mutants showed accelerated LR progression (Figures 1F and Supplemental Figure S8), suggesting that PIP1 and PIP2 inhibit LR formation by delaying early LR developmental progression.

To determine whether RLK7 functions as the receptor for PIP1 and PIP2 in suppressing LR formation, we grew WT and three rlk7 mutants (which carried distinct mutant alleles, rlk7-1, rlk7-3, and rlk7-4) vertically for 7 days on medium supplemented with or without 100 nM PIP1 or PIP2 peptides and analyzed their LR phenotypes. All three mutants exhibited no detectable response to exogenous PIP1 or PIP2 treatment with respect to LR formation (Figure S9). Therefore, the rlk7-1 allele was used for subsequent experiments. Analysis of LRP distribution revealed that PIP1/2 peptides suppressed LRP development at stage I, whereas this suppression was completely abolished in rlk7-1. This finding indicates that RLK7 is essential for PIP1 and PIP2 perception during stage I of LR development, corresponding to the initiation stage (Figures 2 and Supplemental Figure S10). To investigate the spatial expression patterns of RLK7 and PIP2, we generated dual-reporter transgenic Arabidopsis lines harboring ProRLK7:SVnls2×GFP and ProPIP2:SVntdTomato, with both GFP and Tomato reporter proteins targeted to the nucleus. These findings suggest that PIP2 is locally expressed during early LR development and may directly interact with RLK7. RLK7 interacts with the BAK1 coreceptor in response to PIP1 and PIP2 peptides, triggering phosphorylation events that inhibit LR development

RLK7:HA protein transiently expressed in tobacco leaves binds radioactively labeled 125I-PIP1 (Hou et al., 2014). Building on this observation, we performed microscale thermophoresis (MST) assays to quantify the binding affinities of RLK7 for PIP1 and PIP2. MST measures the movement of fluorescently labeled molecules along a temperature gradient, which is influenced by changes in size, charge, hydration shell, or conformation—parameters that are altered upon ligand binding (Wienken et al., 2010; Seidel et al., 2013; Jerabek-Willemsen et al., 2014). To assess ligand binding, we expressed and purified the extracellular LRR domain of RLK7 (RLK7LRR:6×His) in insect cells using affinity and gel-filtration chromatography (Supplemental Figure S12). MST analysis revealed that PIP1 and PIP2 bind directly to fluorescently labeled RLK7LRR:6×His (Figures 3A and 3B; and Supplemental Figure S13), with dissociation constants (Kd) of 6.27 ± 0.65 μM and 17.47 ± 0.42 μM, respectively (Figure 3C). To determine whether the binding of PIP1 and PIP2 to RLK7 promotes recruitment of the coreceptor BAK1, we performed coimmunoprecipitation assays in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves transiently expressing RLK7LRR:7×Myc and BAK1LRR:3×HA after treatment with PIP1, PIP2, or the unrelated control peptide CEP1. Both PIP1 and PIP2 induced interaction between RLK7LRR and BAK1LRR, whereas C-TERMINALLY ENCODED PEPTIDE 1 (CEP1), which binds CEP RECEPTOR 1 (CEPR1) (Tabata et al., 2014), did not (Figure 3D). This indicates that PIP1/2 binding specifically triggers ligand-dependent formation of the RLK7–BAK1 complex. To test whether receptor–CKD association is sufficient to activate RLK7-mediated inhibition of LR development, we used a rapamycin (Rap)-induced dimerization (RiD) system, which has previously been shown to activate signaling by inducing heterodimerization of receptor and coreceptor CKDs (Kim et al., 2021; Jeon et al., 2024). Rap treatment of RiD–RLK7/BAK1 transgenic plants significantly reduced LR density (Figure 3E and Supplemental Figures S14 and S15). Coimmunoprecipitation analysis confirmed that RLK7CKD interacts with BAK1CKD upon Rap treatment (Supplemental Figure S14C). Similarly, Rap-induced dimerization between RLK7CKD and the CKDs of SERK1, SERK2, and SERK4 reduced LR density (Supplemental Figures S14, S16, S17, and S18).

As an independent approach, we employed an estradiol-inducible BAK1-INTERACTING RLK 3 (BIR3) chimeric receptor system, in which the extracellular LRR domain of BIR3 is fused to RLK7CKD (ectoBIR3:RLK7). Upon estradiol treatment, this fusion receptor constitutively recruits endogenous SERKs, thereby activating RLK7 signaling independently of ligand binding (Hohmann et al., 2020; Jeon et al., 2024). Induction of ectoBIR3:RLK7 expression led to a significant reduction in LR density (Figure 3F and Supplemental Figure S19). Both the RiD and BIR3 chimeric receptor systems inhibited primary root and leaf growth, suggesting that RLK7 signaling broadly suppresses plant growth and LR formation. Additionally, the serk1-1 serk4-1, serk1-1 serk2-1(±), and higher-order serk1-1 serk2-1 serk4-1, serk1-1 serk2-1 bak1-4, and serk1-1 bak1-4 bkk1-1 mutants all exhibited LR inhibition in response to PIP1/2 peptides comparable to that observed in WT (Supplemental Figure S20). However, the serk1-1 serk2-1 bak1-4 mutant displayed reduced LR formation compared with WT even without peptide treatment, indicating that SERK coreceptors contribute to basal LR development.

Because RLK7 is an RLK that contains conserved serine/threonine kinase motifs (Walker 1994; Liu et al., 2007; Pitorre et al., 2010; Chowdhury and Mubassir, 2022), we used anti-pThr antibodies to examine PIP-induced phosphorylation of RLK7. We found that PIP1 and PIP2, but not CEP1, induced phosphorylation of RLK7 in Pro35S:RLK7:3×HA transgenic Arabidopsis plants (Figure 3G), consistent with previous reports that CEP1 is perceived by CEPR1/2 rather than RLK7 (Tabata et al., 2014; Rzemieniewski et al., 2024). Rap treatment activated MPK3/6 in the RiD–RLK7/BAK1 line (Supplemental Figure S15D), suggesting that Rap-induced association of RLK7 and BAK1 is sufficient to activate downstream MAPK signaling. Treatment with PIP1 or PIP2 enhanced MPK3/6 activation in WT within 5 min, and this activation persisted for 30 min; By contrast, this response was greatly attenuated in rlk7-1 (Figure 3H and 3I), further supporting RLK7 as the receptor for PIP1/2 peptides. To examine the role of MAPKs in PIP1/2-mediated suppression of LR formation, we analyzed LR phenotypes of the mkk4, mkk5, mpk3, and mpk6 mutants after PIP1/2 treatment. Among these, only the mpk6 mutant exhibited marked insensitivity to PIP1/2-induced inhibition of LRP and LR formation, whereas WT and the other mutants remained similarly responsive (Figures 3J and 3K). These findings suggest that MPK6 is a key downstream component of the RLK7 signaling pathway that mediates LR inhibition in response to PIP1/2. Further analysis of LRP distribution revealed that in mpk6, early-stage LRPs (stages I and II) were reduced by PIP1/2 to a degree similar to that observed in WT. However, later-stage LRPs (stages III to emergence) were maintained at higher densities than in WT after PIP1/2 treatment (Figures 3L and S21). This finding indicates that MPK6 primarily contributes to PIP1/2-mediated suppression of later-stage LRP development, whereas additional kinases or regulatory components may regulate earlier stages. Additionally, mpk6 exhibited higher LRP density at stages II and IV than WT under normal conditions (Figure 3L), suggesting that MPK6 is also necessary for proper progression of LRP development from early to late stages.

LEP acts downstream of RLK7 and MPK3/6 in response to PIP1 and PIP2

Next, we aimed to identify key transcription factors that act downstream of the PIP1/2–RLK7–MPK3/6 signaling module to suppress LR formation and contribute to growth–defense trade-offs. PUCHI, an APETALA2/ethylene-responsive element-binding protein (AP2/EREBP)-type transcription factor, inhibits LR initiation and disrupts LRP formation in response to the PIPL3 peptide via the RLK7 receptor and to the ROOT MERISTEM GROWTH FACTOR 1 (RGF1) peptide via the RGF1 INSENSITIVE 1 receptor (Goh et al., 2019; Jourquin et al., 2022; Jeon et al., 2023). To examine whether PUCHI suppresses LR formation in response to PIP1/2 peptides via RLK7, we performed quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT–qPCR) analysis and found that PIP1/2-induced PUCHI expression depends on RLK7 (Supplemental Figures S22A and S22B). However, the puchi-1 mutant exhibited LR inhibition in response to PIP1/2 peptides similar to that of WT (Supplemental Figure S22C), suggesting that PUCHI is not a key regulator mediating this suppression via the PIP1/2–RLK7 pathway.

Phylogenetic analysis identified LEP as the gene most closely related to PUCHI (Supplemental Figure S23). In ProLEP:GUS plants, treatment with PIP1/2 peptides enhanced GUS expression during LR development, indicating that PIP1/2 induce LEP expression in developing LRs (Figure 4A). RT–qPCR analysis revealed that PIP1/2-induced LEP expression was completely abolished in rlk7-1 but upregulated in WT (Figure 4B). This PIP1/2-induced LEP expression was suppressed in the mpk3 and mpk6 mutants, indicating that LEP expression depends on RLK7 and MPK3/6 (Figure 4C). Importantly, the lep mutants exhibited partial insensitivity to PIP1/2-mediated inhibition of root growth and LR formation (Figures 4D–4F and Supplemental Figure S24). The puchi lep double mutants exhibited PIP1/2 insensitivity similar to that of the lep single mutants (Supplemental Figures S25, S26, and S27). Collectively, these findings suggest that LEP, rather than PUCHI, functions as a key transcription factor acting downstream of the PIP1/2–RLK7–MPK6 signaling module in controlling LR development. LEP expression suppresses LR development

To examine the overlap in RLK7 and LEP expression, we performed confocal imaging across various LR developmental stages in transgenic Arabidopsis lines harboring both ProRLK7:SVnls2×GFP and ProLEPntdTomato reporters (Figure 5A). The auxin–ARF7/19–LBD signaling module plays a central role in promoting LR initiation and development (Okushima et al., 2007; Lee et al., 2009, 2013, 2015; Goh et al., 2012; Lavenus et al., 2013; Porco et al., 2016). To determine whether LEP suppresses LR development by inhibiting this auxin signaling pathway, we first examined auxin-induced expression of LBD16 and LBD18 in ProRLK7:LEP and Pro35S:LEP plants relative to WT and the lep-1 mutant. No significant differences in auxin-induced gene expression were detected among these lines (Supplemental Figure S32A). Similarly, auxin-stimulated enhancement of LR formation was comparable across all genotypes (Supplemental Figures S32B and S32C). Furthermore, transient expression assays using WT, lep, and Pro35S:LEP protoplasts revealed similar expression levels of direct target genes of LBD16 and LBD18 (Supplemental Figure S33). The lep mutation restores PIP1/2- and flg22-mediated growth inhibition while maintaining resistance to pathogen

LEP was initially identified through activation tagging (Graaff et al., 2000). The dominant T-DNA-tagged mutant exhibited a leafy petiole phenotype, with the petiole transformed into a leaf blade, resulting in the loss of a petiole. Reverse transcription PCR (RT–PCR) analysis showed that LEP expression is restricted to young shoots (Graaff et al., 2000). RNA in situ hybridization using an LEP antisense probe revealed that LEP is expressed throughout leaf primordia, with the strongest expression in the youngest primordia, and that expression gradually decreases in older primordia (Graaff et al., 2000). Given that LEP is expressed in developing leaves and its misexpression affects leaf development, we hypothesized that it may function downstream of the PIP1/2–RLK7 signaling pathway to regulate plant growth as part of the growth–defense trade-off. To examine whether loss of LEP function alleviates PIP1/2-induced growth inhibition, we treated 4-day-old WT and lep-1 seedlings with mock or PIP1 and PIP2 peptides in liquid medium for 9, 10, and 12 days and measured the fresh weight of both treated and untreated plants. WT seedlings treated with 5 μM PIP1 or PIP2 peptides exhibited significantly reduced fresh weight, whereas the lep-1 mutant remained insensitive to PIP1/2 treatment (Figure 6A and 6B). However, the expression of defense-related marker genes, including FRK1, WRKY33, and WRKY53, was similarly upregulated in response to PIP1/2 treatment in both WT and lep-1 plants (Figure 6C). Similarly, flg22-induced inhibition of seedling growth was partially restored in both the pip1 and pip2 mutants compared with WT, whereas the expression of marker genes was comparable between WT and the pip mutants in response to flg22 treatment (Figures 6D and 6E; Supplemental Figure S34). These findings suggest that flg22-triggered growth inhibition is partially mediated through the PIP1/2–RLK7–LEP signaling pathway. Furthermore, PIP1/2- and flg22-induced inhibition of the virulent hemibiotrophic bacterium Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000 was comparable between WT and lep-1 plants (Figure 6F). Similarly, MAPK activity and the ROS burst were not significantly affected by the lep mutation compared with WT (Figures 6G and 6H). flg22 suppresses LR formation and seedling growth through the PIP1/2–RLK7–MPK6–LEP signaling module

To determine whether flg22 inhibits LR formation and plant growth through the PIP1/2–RLK7–MPK6–LEP signaling module, we examined LR phenotypes and seedling growth in the rlk7-1, pip1-2, pip2-2, mpk6, and lep-1 mutants and their corresponding WT plants in the presence or absence of flg22 treatment. All five mutants exhibited reduced sensitivity to flg22-mediated inhibition of LR formation compared with WT (Figures 7A and Supplemental Figure S35). Consistent with this finding, flg22-induced inhibition of seedling growth observed in WT was suppressed in the rlk7-1 and mpk6 mutants (Figure 7B and Supplemental Figure S36). Moreover, flg22 induced GUS expression in the leaves, primary roots, and LRP of ProPIP1:GUS and ProPIP2:GUS transgenic plants, as well as in the primary roots and LRP of ProLEP:GUS plants (Supplemental Figures S37 and S38). These findings indicate that flg22 inhibits LR formation and seedling growth through the PIP1/2–RLK7–MPK6–LEP signaling module. In serk triple mutants, seedling growth was reduced in response to flg22 compared with WT, whereas the serk1-1(±) serk2-1 bak1-4 mutant was less sensitive compared with the serk1-1 bak1-4(±) bkk1-1 mutant (Figure 7B and Supplemental Figure S36), indicating that SERK coreceptors, including SERK2 and BAK1, are required for normal plant growth and flg22-induced growth inhibition. Discussion

Upon perception of PAMPs, plants activate immune responses that are often accompanied by growth inhibition. For instance, flg22—a bacterial PAMP—induces miR393, which targets transcripts encoding the F-box auxin receptors TIR1, AFB2, and AFB3 for degradation, thereby downregulating their expression and inhibiting growth (Navarro et al., 2006). Similarly, salicylic acid, a key hormone in defense responses, suppresses plant growth by downregulating TIR1/AFB expression (Wang et al., 2007). In addition, salicylic acid-mediated repression of gibberellin biosynthesis and signaling enhances the stability of DELLA proteins, which in turn inhibits brassinosteroid (BR) signaling (Galego-Bartolomé et al., 2012). Conversely, upon activation by BRs, the key transcription factor BZR1 promotes the expression of WRKY transcription factors, which act as negative regulators of early immune responses, thereby suppressing plant immunity (Lozano-Durán et al., 2013). This BZR1-mediated immune suppression is particularly relevant during rapid growth, highlighting its role in balancing the trade-off between growth and immune defense (Lozano-Durán et al., 2013). BR signaling also promotes the expression of HBI1, a positive regulator of both BR biosynthesis and BR-mediated growth, further repressing the expression of immune response genes (Lozano-Durán and Zipfel, 2015). Jasmonate, another defense-related hormone, also inhibits plant growth by interfering with the auxin pathway (Sun et al., 2011). In addition, jasmonate antagonizes gibberellin biosynthesis and signaling, contributing to growth repression during immune responses (Navarro et al., 2008; Hou et al., 2010; Wild et al., 2012; Yang et al., 2012; Heinrich et al., 2013). Collectively, these findings emphasize the critical role of hormonal crosstalk in regulating the balance between plant growth and defense.

PEPs are induced by PAMPs, such as flg22 and elf18, as well as by other elicitors and abiotic stresses such as high salinity, and are perceived by their cognate receptors PEPR1/2. These peptide–receptor pairs mediate immune responses and contribute to salt stress tolerance (Huffaker et al., 2006; Huffaker and Ryan, 2007; Nakaminami et al., 2018). The transcription factor SALT TOLERANCE ZINC FINGER (STZ), which acts downstream of PEP1 in Arabidopsis, has been identified as a potential nexus linking stress responses with proliferative cell-cycle regulation in roots, suggesting that STZ may coordinate the balance between growth and defense (Dhar et al., 2025).

In this study, we identified the AP2/ERF transcription factor LEP as a key regulatory node that connects the PIP1/2–RLK7/SERKs–MPK3/6 signaling cascade with LR development. LEP mutations alleviated PIP1/2-induced growth inhibition without compromising immune responses, indicating that LEP selectively mediates growth repression independently of immune signaling. In response to flg22, LEP mutations partially mitigated growth suppression while preserving defense activation, further supporting a role for LEP in modulating growth–defense trade-offs. Moreover, mutations in PIP1, PIP2, or RLK7 attenuated flg22-induced growth inhibition and LEP expression, suggesting that part of flg22-mediated growth suppression operates through the PIP1/2–RLK7–LEP pathway. In parallel, flg22-mediated suppression of auxin signaling, which occurs independently of this pathway, also contributes to growth inhibition and PTI (Navarro et al., 2006). A previous study reported that lep-1 hypocotyls were shorter than those of WT plants under light and dark conditions, suggesting that LEP is involved in seedling development (Ward et al., 2006). The lep-1 mutant was less responsive to gibberellin (GA) and more sensitive to the GA biosynthesis inhibitor paclobutrazol, indicating that LEP also acts as a positive regulator of GA-induced germination (Ward et al., 2006).

Overexpression of PIP1 and PIP2 driven by either the LBD16 or CaMV 35S promoter markedly suppressed LR development from the earliest stages (Figures 1A–1D). Consistently, exogenous application of synthetic PIP1 or PIP2 peptides inhibited LR formation in WT plants (Supplemental Figure S3A). In contrast, the pip1 and pip2 single mutants exhibited slight increases in primary root length but no significant difference in LR density compared with WT plants (Figures 1E and 1F; Supplemental Figure S8), likely reflecting functional redundancy among the three PIP and eight PIP-like genes present in the Arabidopsis genome (Hou et al., 2014). Nonetheless, root-bending assays further demonstrated that PIP1 and PIP2 suppress early LR initiation (Figures 1F and Supplemental Figure S8), supporting the broader view that immune activation antagonizes growth and developmental progression.

In the lep mutant, LRP densities at stages I–II were increased in response to PIP1/2 peptides, whereas LEP overexpression reduced LRP densities at stages I–IV. These results indicate that LEP negatively regulates early LR development, likely by modulating LR initiation in response to PIP1/2. The rlk7, pip1, pip2, mpk6, and lep mutants were largely insensitive to flg22 with respect to LR development compared with WT. Flg22-induced seedling growth inhibition was reduced in rlk7, mpk3, mpk6, and the serk triple mutant (Figure 7B), and flg22-induced immune gene expression was diminished in rlk7, pip1, pip2, mpk6, and lep (Figure 7C and Supplemental Figure S40). Notably, the lep mutant showed attenuated flg22-mediated growth suppression despite normal immune gene expression and MAPK activation and retained disease resistance (Figure 6). Together, these findings suggest that LEP-mediated inhibition of LR development and plant growth is separable from immune responses, including immune gene regulation and disease resilience beyond MPK3/6. Consistent with this concept, an Arabidopsis mutant lacking five JASMONATE-ZIM DOMAIN transcriptional repressors and phytochrome B exhibits both robust growth and enhanced resistance to insect herbivory, demonstrating that jasmonate-mediated growth–defense trade-offs can be genetically uncoupled (Campos et al., 2016).

The mpk3 and mpk6 mutants exhibited significantly reduced LEP expression in response to PIP1/2 peptides compared with WT plants (Figure 4C). However, mpk3 showed PIP1/2-induced suppression of LR formation similar to WT, whereas mpk6 was insensitive to PIP1/2 peptides with respect to LR formation (Figure 3J). These results indicate that although MPK3/6 are involved in PIP1/2-induced LEP expression, additional regulatory components are required, together with LEP, to modulate LR development. Because mutations in MPK3 or MPK6 reduced the expression of LEP and immune response genes, MPK3/6 may directly control the activities of transcription factors that act upstream of LEP and immune response genes. Identifying common and distinct upstream transcription factors will be essential for fully understanding the regulatory networks that integrate plant growth regulation and immunity in response to PIP1/2 peptides.

BAK1 is a key component of PTI, acting as a co-receptor by forming heterodimeric complexes with various LRR–RLKs, including FLS2 (Chinchilla et al., 2007; Heese et al., 2007). Upon perception of flg22 by FLS2, BAK1 rapidly associates with FLS2 to initiate downstream signaling (Heese et al., 2007). Flg22-induced immune responses are diminished in bak1-3 and bak1-4 mutants (Roux et al., 2011). Similarly, PIP1-induced ROS production and inhibition of primary root growth are reduced in bak1-4 compared with WT plants, indicating that PIP1–RLK7 signaling is partly dependent on BAK1 (Hou et al., 2014). However, all tested serk double and triple mutants—including bak1—exhibited LR inhibition in response to PIP1/2 comparable to that in WT plants (Figure S19). Gain-of-function experiments using the RiD and BIR3 chimera systems demonstrated that interactions between RLK7 and four different SERK family members, including BAK1, trigger LR inhibition (Figuress 3E and 3F). Furthermore, coimmunoprecipitation assays revealed that PIP1 and PIP2 induced interactions between RLK7LRR and BAK1LRR (Figure 3D). These findings indicate that PIP1/2-induced RLK7–SERK complex formation may play distinct roles in regulating immune responses, LR development, and broader aspects of plant growth. As quadruple serk mutants are not viable, it remains unclear whether SERK family co-receptors function redundantly to mediate RLK7-dependent LR suppression in response to PIP1 and PIP2.

MST analysis revealed that the dissociation constants (Kd) of PIP1 and PIP2 for RLK7LRR were 6.27 ± 0.65 and 17.47 ± 0.42 μM, respectively (Figure 3C). These values are lower than the reported Kd values for CLE9/10 binding to HAESA-LIKE 1 and IDA binding to HAESA (∼40 μM) (Santiago et al., 2016; Qian et al., 2018), suggesting that PIP1 and PIP2 bind RLK7 with relatively high affinity. A previous study reported a Kd of ∼0.5 μM for PIP1 binding to the RLK7 ectodomain using isothermal titration calorimetry (Rzemieniewski et al., 2024). Although direct comparisons across different methods are not straightforward, these data collectively support strong interactions between PIP peptides and RLK7.

To gain structural insight, we used AlphaFold 3 (Abramson et al., 2024) to predict the three-dimensional structures of PIP1–RLK7LRR and PIP2–RLK7LRR complexes. Both peptides were predicted to bind the concave surface of the RLK7LRR domain, forming two hydrogen bonds with the receptor (Supplemental Figure S42). However, PIP1 exhibited a more favorable binding configuration than PIP2. Notably, the hydroxyproline residue in PIP2 did not participate in hydrogen bonding with the receptor, whereas the corresponding residue in PIP1 formed a hydrogen bond. The predicted template modeling (pTM) scores for the PIP1–RLK7 and PIP2–RLK7 complexes were 0.91 and 0.89, respectively, indicating high overall confidence in the predicted folds. Although the interface pTM (ipTM) scores were relatively low (0.64 and 0.54, respectively; Supplemental Figure S41), these values suggest a moderate level of confidence in the relative positioning of the peptide and receptor within the complex. These structural predictions are consistent with the lower Kd value of PIP1 compared with PIP2, supporting stronger binding of PIP1 to RLK7LRR. However, structural studies at atomic resolution using X-ray crystallography are required to validate these models and reveal the precise molecular interactions. Despite its lower binding affinity, exogenous PIP2 treatment induced stronger phenotypic effects on LR inhibition, seedling growth inhibition, and resistance to Pst DC3000 compared with PIP1. This observation suggests that peptide binding affinity to LRR–RLK receptors does not necessarily correlate with phenotypic output, highlighting the complexity of downstream signaling processes.

The PIP1–RLK7 signaling module has been shown to contribute to plant resistance to the bacterial strain Pst DC3118 by promoting stomatal closure through activation of the kinase OST1 and the S-type anion channel SLAC1 in guard cell plasma membranes (Shen et al., 2020). In addition, RLK7 is involved in normal germination timing and tolerance to oxidative stress (Pitorre et al., 2010), and the PIP3–RLK7 pathway plays a crucial role in salt tolerance, with MPK3/6 acting downstream (Zhou et al., 2022). Together with our present findings, these studies suggest that RLK7 functions as a central hub for integrating multiple biotic and abiotic stress signals. Given that RLK7 recognizes several related yet distinct peptides—including PIP1, PIP2, PIP3, and PIPL3— future studies should elucidate how these ligands specify distinct physiological outputs through the same receptor.

Our findings demonstrate that PIP1/2 peptides suppress plant growth through the action of LEP, establishing a genetic framework for decoupling immune activation from growth inhibition. This decoupling may offer a promising strategy for enhancing crop resilience by harnessing defense peptides without compromising plant growth and development. Comparative analysis of intracellular immune receptors—specifically nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat (NLR) receptors—across 187 plant species with trait data revealed a negative correlation between immune-repertoire size and growth in wild species (Giolai and Laine, 2024). In contrast, this growth–defense trade-off was not evident in domesticated crops (Giolai and Laine, 2024), suggesting that the fitness cost traditionally associated with immune activation may not be a necessary constraint in agricultural settings.

Substantial efforts have been made to optimize growth–defense trade-offs to develop cultivars that combine high yield potential with robust resistance to biotic stressors. In rice, epigenetic regulation of the antagonistic NLR receptors PigmR and PigmS confers durable resistance to the blast fungus Magnaporthe oryzae without yield penalties (Deng et al., 2017). In addition, incorporating pathogen-responsive upstream open reading frame (uORF)-mediated translational control into an autoactivated immune receptor in Arabidopsis and the master immune regulator AtNPR1 in rice has been shown to confer broad-spectrum disease resistance without compromising plant fitness (Xu et al., 2017). More recently, precision genome editing has been used to introduce edited mildew resistance locus O (MLO) alleles in elite wheat varieties, generating lines with robust and durable disease resistance while maintaining high productivity (Li et al., 2022). These studies, together with our present findings, demonstrate that growth–defense trade-offs in plants can be strategically uncoupled through transcriptional, translational, epigenetic, and genome-editing approaches, offering powerful avenues for breeding crop varieties with both high productivity and durable disease resistance.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

All A. thaliana plant materials used in this study were of the Columbia (Col-0) ecotype. The rlk7-1 (CS860338), mkk4 (SALK_058307), mkk5 (SALK_047797C), mpk3 (SALK_151594), mpk6 (SALK_073907), puchi-1, lep-1 (WiscDsLox461-464A4) (Ward et al., 2006), lep-2 (SALK_060863), serk1-1 serk4-1 (CS71781), serk1-1 serk2-1 (CS71779), serk1-1 serk2-1 serk4-1 (CS71790), serk1-1 serk2-1 bak1-4 (CS71788), and serk1-1 bak1-4 bkk1-1 mutants were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (ABRC). The puchi-1 mutant was described previously (Kang et al., 2013). The puchi-1 lep-1 and puchi-1 lep-2 double mutants were generated by crossing puchi-1 with lep-1 and lep-2, respectively. Arabidopsis plants were grown in a growth room under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod at 23°C, as described previously (Park et al., 2002).

Plasmid construction and generation of transgenic Arabidopsis plants

To generate transgenic Arabidopsis plants overexpressing PIP1 or PIP2, their full-length coding regions were amplified from Arabidopsis genomic DNA and subcloned into the pDONR/Zeo vector using Gateway BP recombination (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The resulting entry clones were recombined into either the pB7WG vector (VIB, Ghent, Belgium) under the LBD16 promoter or the binary vector pX-YFP-GW (Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) under the 35S promoter using Gateway LR recombination (Invitrogen), yielding the ProLBD16:PIP1, ProLBD16:PIP2, Pro35S:PIP1, and Pro35S:PIP2 constructs. The full-length coding sequence of LEP was subcloned into the pDONR/Zeo vector as described for PIP1/2. The entry clone was then introduced into the pX-YFP-GW vector under the 35S promoter using the LR reaction, producing the Pro35S:LEP construct. The ProRLK7:LEP construct was generated using Golden Gate modular cloning (MoClo) (Engler et al., 2014). The RLK7 promoter region, corresponding to 1,840 bp upstream of the start codon, was amplified with A and B sequences at the 5′- and 3′-ends, respectively, and the LEP coding region was amplified with B and C sequences at the 5′- and 3′-ends, respectively. These PCR fragments were subcloned into the pGEM-T Easy vector to generate Level 0 modules. Similarly, the NOS terminator was amplified with C and G sequences at the 5′- and 3′-ends, respectively, and inserted into the pGG vector as another Level 0 module. The resulting Level 0 modules were subsequently assembled into the pBm7GG destination vector using T4 ligase (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), generating the final ProRLK7:LEP construct. To generate promoter:GUS reporter lines, the promoter regions of PIP2 and LEP, encompassing 2,002 and 2,388 bp upstream of the AUG initiation codon, respectively, were amplified from Arabidopsis genomic DNA and subcloned into the pDONR/Zeo vector. The resulting entry clones were recombined into the pBWG533 vector via the LR reaction, producing the ProPIP2:GUS and ProLEP:GUS constructs. Rap-inducible dimerization vectors were constructed using the Golden Gate cloning method, as previously described (Kim et al., 2021). The coding sequence corresponding to the kinase domain of RLK7, fused with an N-terminal myristoylation sequence, was amplified using specific primer pairs and inserted into the previously generated Level 2 vectors pRiD-MCS/BAK1, pRiD-MCS/SERK1, pRiD-MCS/SERK2, and pRiD-MCS/SERK4. To generate the estradiol-inducible pER8-BIR3-RLK7 construct, fragments encoding the BIR3 extracellular domain and the RLK7 kinase domain were amplified using specific primer pairs and assembled into the pENTRY3C entry vector (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The resulting BIR3-RLK7 fragment was subsequently transferred into the estradiol-inducible pER8GW vector using LR Clonase enzyme (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For confocal microscopy, the promoter regions of RLK7, PIP1, PIP2, and LEP were amplified from Arabidopsis genomic DNA using primers containing A and B sequences at the 5′-and 3′-ends, respectively. The GFP and RFP promoter reporter constructs were generated using the Golden Gate cloning method. The DNA fragments encoding SVnls2×GFP and SVntdTomato were obtained from the pGIIM_DR5V2_ntdTomato_DR5_n3GFP vector (Addgene) using primers containing B and C sequences at the 5′- and 3′-ends, respectively. The NOS terminator was amplified from the pDOE-09 vector (Gookin and Assmann, 2014) using primers containing C and G sequences at the 5′- and 3′-ends, respectively. The ProRLK7 and SVnls2×GFP fragments were cloned into the pEN-L4-AG-R1 vector (VIB), whereas ProPIP1, ProPIP2, and ProLEP, each fused to SVntdTomato, were inserted into the pEN-L1-AG-L2 vector. Subsequently, the combinations of pEN-L4-A-ProRLK7:SVnls2×GFP-G-R1 with either pEN-L1-A-ProPIP1:SVntdTomato-G-L2, pEN-L1-A-ProPIP2:SVntdTomato-G-L2, or pEN-L1-A-ProLEP:SVntdTomato-G-L2 were assembled into the binary vector pBm42GW,3 (VIB), yielding the ProRLK7:SVnls2×GFP-ProPIP1:SVntdTomato, ProRLK7:SVnls2×GFP-ProPIP2:SVntdTomato, and ProRLK7:SVnls2×GFP-ProLEP:SVntdTomato constructs. The RLK7 coding region was subcloned into the pDONR/Zeo entry vector using the Gateway BP reaction and subsequently transferred into the pGWB514 destination vector using the LR recombination reaction, yielding the Pro35S:RLK7:3×HA construct. All constructs were verified by DNA sequencing. Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 was used to introduce the constructs into Arabidopsis plants. Homozygous T3 transgenic lines were derived from the T1 transformants. Primers and PCR conditions are provided in Supplemental Table S1.

Generation of the pip1 and pip2 mutants using the CRISPR–Cas9 system

To generate the pip1 and pip2 mutants, guide RNAs (gRNAs) were designed using Cas-Designer from the CRISPR-RGEN Tools website (http://www.rgenome.net/cas-designer). The pCBC-DT1T2 vector (a gift from Qi-Jun Chen; Addgene plasmid #50590; http://n2t.net/addgene:50590; RRID 50590) was used as a template to construct expression cassettes containing multiple gRNA target sites. PCR amplification was carried out using pCBC-DT1T2 and gene-specific primers. The purified PCR products were combined with the binary vector pJY-RpCas9 (Yun et al., 2019), AarI restriction enzyme (Thermo Scientific), and T4 DNA ligase (New England Biolabs), and assembled by Golden Gate cloning in a thermocycler under the following conditions: 5 h at 37°C, 5 min at 50°C, and 10 min at 80°C (Xing et al., 2014). The resulting constructs were transformed into E. coli cells, and transformants were selected on medium containing 100 μg/ml spectinomycin. Plasmid DNA was isolated and verified by Sanger sequencing. Confirmed constructs were introduced into Arabidopsis plants via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. Transgenic plants were selected on ½ Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with 30 μg/ml hygromycin and subsequently transferred to soil. To detect mutations, PCR amplicons were treated with the Guide-it Mutation Detection Kit (Guide-it Resolvase, TaKaRa Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) to assess heteroduplex formation, and putative mutants were validated by Sanger sequencing. Homozygous, T-DNA-free lines were isolated and propagated. Mutations in PIP1 and PIP2 were further confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Primers and PCR conditions are provided in Supplemental Table S1.

Chemical synthesis of the PIP1, PIP2, and flg22 peptides

The PIP1 (RLASG-HyP-SPRGPGH), PIP2 (RFVKHSG-HyP-SPSGPGH), and flg22 (QRLSTGSRINSAKDDAAGLQIA) peptides were chemically synthesized by Peptron Inc. (Daejeon, South Korea) and dissolved in double-distilled water to prepare 5 mM stock concentrations.

Root phenotype analysis

Plants were grown vertically on ½ MS plates at 23°C under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod. Primary root lengths were measured from scanned root images using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). LR numbers were quantified using a Leica DE/MZ 7.5 microscope (Leica Microsystems CMS GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). LRP numbers were counted using a Leica DM2500 microscope at 20× magnification. LR developmental kinetics were assessed as described previously (Lee and Kim, 2013). A 90° gravitropic stimulus was applied to seedlings at 3 days after germination (DAG), and the numbers of LRPs at stages I–VIII, according to Malamy and Benfey (1997), were counted on the convex side at 24 and 48 h post-gravitropic induction (pgi).

Histochemical GUS assay and microscopy

Homozygous GUS reporter T3 transgenic plants were grown vertically on ½ MS plates for 7 DAG in the presence or absence of 1 μM PIP1 or PIP2 peptide and then stained for GUS activity using a 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl β-D-glucuronic acid (X-GlcA; Duchefa Biochemie, Haarlem, Netherlands) solution containing 0.1 M Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4, pH 7.0, 0.5 mM K3[Fe(CN)6], 0.5 mM K4[Fe(CN)6], 10 mM EDTA, 1 mg/ml X-GlcA, 25% MeOH, and 0.06% Triton X-100. The samples were vacuum-infiltrated in the X-GlcA solution for 5 min and incubated overnight at 37°C in the dark. For whole-mount visualization, seedlings were rinsed with 100% EtOH every 1 h at room temperature for 8 h and then mounted in 80% glycerol. Samples were observed using a Leica 10445930 microscope for whole seedlings and a Leica DM2500 microscope at 10× magnification for primary roots, LRs, and root tips or at 20× magnification for LRPs.

Confocal microscopy

T2 fluorescent reporter lines showing a 3:1 segregation ratio were grown vertically on ½ MS plates containing Basta (DL-phosphinothricin; Duchefa Biochemie, Haarlem, Netherlands; 10 μg/ml) for selection until 4 DAG. The selected plants were subsequently transferred to ½ MS plates without Basta and grown for 1 additional day. Confocal microscopy was performed as previously described (Jeon et al., 2023).

Coimmunoprecipitation assays

The RLK7LRR:7×Myc-BAK1LRR:3×HA construct was cloned using the Golden Gate cloning method. The Pro35S DNA fragment was amplified with primers containing “A” and “B” sequences at the 5′- and 3′-ends, respectively. The RLK7LRR and BAK1LRR coding sequences were obtained from Arabidopsis cDNA using primers containing “B” and “C” sequences at the 5′- and 3′-ends, respectively. The 7×Myc and 3×HA tag sequences were amplified from pGWB20 and pGWB514 vectors, respectively, using primers containing “C” and “E” sequences at the 5′- and 3′-ends, respectively. The NOS terminator sequence was amplified from the pDOE-09 vector (Gookin and Assmann, 2014) using primers containing “E” and “G” sequences at the 5′- and 3′-ends, respectively. The Pro35S, RLK7LRR, and 7×Myc DNA fragments were cloned into the pEN-L4-AG-R1 vector (VIB), whereas the Pro35S, BAK1LRR, and 3×HA fragments were cloned into the pEN-L1-AG-L2 vector. Subsequently, the pEN-L4-A-Pro35S: RLK7LRR:7×Myc-G-R1 and pEN-L1-A-Pro35S: BAK1LRR:3×HA-G-L2 constructs were assembled into the pBm42GW,3 plant binary vector (VIB), yielding the Pro35S: RLK7LRR:7×Myc-Pro35S:BAK1LRR:3×HA construct. This plasmid was introduced into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101, and A. tumefaciens harboring the construct was infiltrated into the abaxial epidermis of N. benthamiana leaves using a needleless syringe. The plants were incubated in the dark for 24 h at 23°C, and the leaves were then treated with 10 μM PIP1, PIP2, or CEP1 peptide for 4 h. Total protein was extracted from leaves using a buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 10 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA, 50 μM MG132, and one tablet of protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The monoclonal anti-HA–agarose antibody produced in mouse (A2095, Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was washed three times with 1× phosphate-buffered saline solution, and 5 mg of extracted protein was incubated with the beads for 2 h at 4°C on a rotator. Subsequently, the beads were washed five times with washing buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM EDTA, 50 μM MG132, and one tablet of protease inhibitor cocktail [Roche]) and analyzed by immunoblotting using anti-Myc antibodies at a dilution of 1:5,000 (M5546, Sigma–Aldrich) for RLK7LRR:10×Myc and anti-HA antibodies at a dilution of 1:1,000 (sc-7392, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for BAK1LRR:3×HA as primary antibodies, and m-IgGκ BP-HRP (sc-516102, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) as the secondary antibody at a dilution of 1:4,000.

Phosphorylation assays

Pro35S:RLK7:3×HA plants were grown vertically on ½ MS plates for 7 DAG and treated with 10 μM PIP1, PIP2, or CEP1 for 10 min. Total protein was extracted using an extraction buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 10 mM DTT, 1 mM EDTA, 50 μM MG132, one tablet of protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche), and one tablet of phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche). The extracted proteins were incubated with anti-HA-agarose antibodies for 2 h at 4°C, washed five times with washing buffer, and analyzed by immunoblotting using anti-HA antibodies at a dilution of 1:2,000 (sc-7392, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and anti-phospho-Thr/Tyr antibodies at a dilution of 1:1,000 (9381, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) as primary antibodies, and m-IgGκ BP-HRP (sc-516102, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) as the secondary antibody at a dilution of 1:4,000. For MPK3/6 kinase assays, phospho-p44/42 MAPK (Erk1/2) (Thr202/Tyr204) monoclonal antibodies (#4370, Cell Signaling Technology) were used at a 1:1,000 dilution.

ROS burst assay

Plants were grown under short-day conditions (8-h light/16-h dark) at 23°C. Twelve 4-mm-diameter leaf discs from 5-week-old plants were incubated overnight in sterile water. The discs were then transferred to a luminol/peroxidase solution (20 μM luminol L-102 [Wako Chemicals, Richmond, VA, USA] and 1 μg/ml horseradish peroxidase [Sigma-Aldrich]) containing mock, 5 μM PIP1, 5 μM PIP2, or 5 μM flg22. Luminescence was measured at 2-min intervals using a Synergy HTX Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA) and expressed as relative light units.

RNA isolation, RT–PCR, and RT–qPCR

Arabidopsis plants were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. For RT–PCR analysis, total RNA was isolated using the Plant Total RNA Purification Mini Kit (FavorPrep, Favorgen Biotech Corp., Ping-Tung, Taiwan), and RT–PCR was conducted using the AccessQuick RT–PCR System (Promega). For RT–qPCR analysis, total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Real-time RT–qPCR was conducted using a QuantiTect SYBR Green RT–PCR Kit (Qiagen) on a CFX96 qPCR Detection System equipped with a C1000 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). All RT–qPCR analyses were conducted in three biological replicates and subjected to statistical analysis. Relative gene expression levels were calculated as previously described (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). Primers and PCR conditions are provided in Supplemental Table S1.

Transient expression assays in Arabidopsis protoplasts

The full-length LEP coding sequence was cloned into the Pro35S:GD:LBD16 vector (Lee et al., 2013) by replacing the LBD16 DNA fragment using the SalI (N-terminal) and NotI (C-terminal) sites, yielding the Pro35S:GD:LEP construct. The ProGal4(3x):LUC plasmid was used as the reporter plasmid (Lee et al., 2013). The Pro35S:GUS vector was used as a transfection control (Lee et al., 2008). The Pro35S:Ω:LBD16, Pro35S:Ω:LBD18, Pro35Smini:LUC, ProFAD-BD:LUC, and ProEXP14:LUC plasmids were obtained from a previous study (Lee et al., 2013). Protoplast transfection was performed as previously described (Jeon et al., 2016). Briefly, protoplasts isolated from the mesophyll cells of rosette leaves of 3- to 4-week-old Arabidopsis plants were transfected with plasmid DNA and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 16 h. Protein extraction and measurement of LUC activity were performed using the dual-LUC reporter assay system (Promega, E1910). GUS activity was determined using 1 mM 4-methylumbelliferyl- β-D-glucuronide in GUS extraction buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.0, 5 mM DTT, 1 mM Na2EDTA) with a Synergy H1 Hybrid Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (BioTek Instruments). Each transfection was assayed in duplicate for LUC and GUS activities, and LUC activity was normalized to GUS activity. All transfection assays were performed in triplicate and subjected to statistical analysis. Primers and PCR conditions are provided in Supplemental Table S1.

Measurements of plant fresh weight

Plants were grown for 4 days on ½ MS plates that contained 1% sucrose at 23°C and then transferred to 24-well cell culture plates (SPL Life Sciences, Pocheon, Korea) containing liquid ½ MS medium supplemented with 1% sucrose. Plants were grown for the indicated durations with or without peptide treatment. After surface moisture was removed from the plants, fresh weight was measured.

Bacterial growth assays

To monitor the growth of Pst DC3000 in plants, leaves of 4- to 5-week-old Arabidopsis plants grown under short-day conditions (8-h light/16-h dark) were infiltrated with a bacterial suspension containing 1 × 106 colony-forming units/ml using a needleless syringe. Leaves were pretreated by infiltration with mock solution or PIP1, PIP2, or flg22 peptide solutions 18 h before bacterial inoculation. Three days after inoculation, four leaf discs were collected from each sample, ground in 1 ml of 10 mM MgCl2, serially diluted from 10−1 to 10−6, and 5 μl of each dilution was spotted on King’s B medium containing rifampicin to determine bacterial titers. Plates were incubated at 28°C for 48 h before bacterial colonies were counted.

Expression and purification of the RLK7 ectodomain

The LRR domain of RLK7 (residues 666–996), fused to a 6×His tag at its C-terminus, was cloned into a modified pAcGP64A vector containing a GP64 signal peptide for secretion of the recombinant protein. For protein expression, Trichoplusia ni Tnao38 cells were infected with the RLK7 virus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 3. The cells were incubated at 28°C for 1 day and then at 21°C for 2 days. The culture medium was harvested, and the secreted proteins were purified using TALON metal-affinity resin (Clontech, Mountain View, CA, USA). After elution and concentration, the proteins were further purified by size-exclusion chromatography on a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 pg column (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) using MST buffer containing 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, and 0.05% Tween-20. The purified proteins were concentrated using Amicon Ultra concentrators (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) with a 30-kDa molecular weight cutoff.

MST assay

MST experiments were performed according to the protocol of NanoTemper Technologies using a Monolith NT.115 (blue/red) instrument (NanoTemper Technologies, Munich, Germany). Serial dilutions of PIP1 and PIP2 were prepared in MST buffer. The RLK7LRR proteins were labeled with RED–Tris–NTA 2nd Generation dye using the Monolith His-Tag Labeling Kit (NanoTemper Technologies). Labeled RLK7LRR proteins (100 nM) were mixed with 100 nM dye solution prepared in MST buffer and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. The samples were centrifuged at 15,000× g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was used for MST assays. MST assays were performed under the following conditions: MST power, 40%; LED power, 20%–80%; temperature, 23°C; and MST trace setting, 5 s power off, 30 s power on, and 5 s power off. Samples were incubated in the dark for 30 min at room temperature before measurement. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data were analyzed using MO.Affinity Analysis (v2.3, NanoTemper) with T-Jump evaluation. Normalized fluorescence data were fitted to a sigmoidal curve by nonlinear regression using GraphPad Prism (v8, San Diego, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel and IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (v27, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Graphs were generated using Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism (v9.4.1).
