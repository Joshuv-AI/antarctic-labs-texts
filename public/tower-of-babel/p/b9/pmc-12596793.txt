Occiput-to-C4 fixation skipping C3 in a pediatric Down syndrome patient with atlantoaxial subluxation and basilar invagination: A case report
Authors: Ahmed Hafez Mousa, Badr E. Hafiz, Mohammed Aref
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Atlantoaxial instability (AAI) and basilar invagination are potentially life-threatening complications in children with Down syndrome. When symptomatic, they may lead to progressive myelopathy, gait disturbance, and eventual quadriparesis. Surgical fixation is indicated in cases of neurological deterioration or radiographic evidence of significant instability.Case Description: A 6-year-old female presented with Down syndrome and a history of complex congenital heart disease who presented with 10 months of progressive quadriparesis. Imaging revealed atlantoaxial subluxation with basilar invagination resulting in cervicomedullary compression. Surgery included an occiput-to-C4 posterior fusion, skipping C3 due to anatomical hypoplasia, performed under neuromonitoring and utilizing intraoperative imaging. Postoperative studies confirmed optimal location of instrumentation, and the patient exhibits continued neurological improvement.Conclusion: Early recognition and timely surgical intervention are critical to manage children with Down syndrome and cervical instability.

INTRODUCTION

Atlantoaxial instability (AAI) is a well-documented complication in individuals with Down syndrome, with a radiographic prevalence of 10–30%, with only 1% becoming symptomatic.[3] AAI develops due to ligamentous laxity, odontoid hypoplasia, and abnormal atlantoaxial articulation.[6] Basilar invagination may further exacerbate neurological compromise in these patients.[1] Early identification and appropriate management of AAI are essential to preventing irreversible spinal cord damage. Surgical fixation becomes necessary in symptomatic patients or for those who demonstrate progressive instability accompanied by spinal cord compression and/or myelopathy.[1,3] Here, a 6-year-old female with Down syndrome, congenital cardiac anomalies, and progressive quadriparesis due to AAI and basilar invagination, was successfully managed with posterior occiput-to-C4 fusion, skipping C3 due to anatomical hypoplasia.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 6-year-old girl with a known diagnosis of Down syndrome developed a progressive quadriparesis over 18 months. Her medical history included an atrioventricular septal defect, tetralogy of Fallot, and patent ductus arteriosus. She had previously undergone multiple cardiac procedures [Table 1]. Over the next 10 months, she became completely bedridden, with 2/5 power in the upper and lower extremities, hyperreflexia +4, and right-sided clonus +2. Cervical X-rays revealed atlantoaxial subluxation [Figure 1]. The computed tomography (CT) and CT angiography of the cervical spine confirmed basilar invagination with C1–C2 instability and significant narrowing of the foramen magnum with upper cervical cord buckling [Figures 2 and 3].

Surgery: Occiput-C4 instrumented fusion

Surgery included occiput-C4 instrumented fusion. C3 was excluded from fixation as it had small and rudimentary C3 lateral masses. Utilizing intraoperative neural monitoring, the posterior arch of C1 was removed along with the C2-C4 laminae; the lateral masses of C1 and C4 were prepared for instrumentation. Lateral mass screws were inserted bilaterally at C1 (and C4 pedicle screws [18 mm]), which were placed bilaterally in C2. Four screws were secured to the occipital plate. Contoured rods were applied and fixed with set screws. The posterior arch of C1 was removed for decompression. Neuromonitoring remained stable throughout. Fusion was augmented with decorticated bone and morselized autograft, as shown in the intraoperative fluoroscopic image [Figure 4] and postoperative X-rays and CT scan with 3D reconstruction [Figure 5].

Postoperative course

Postoperatively, she was extubated within 24 h and maintained in a Philadelphia collar. Motor function in the lower extremities remained at the 2-3/5 level.

She was discharged 1 week later with 3/5 motor function in the lower and upper extremities, still having hyperreflexia and right side clonus as preoperative assessment and sensation is preserved to painful stimuli with normal sphincter function. Follow-up 6 weeks postoperatively, her neurological examination reveals −4/5 motor function in the lower extremities, both hyperreflexia (from +4 to +3) and right side clonus (from +2 to +1) are improved in comparison to her preoperative assessment and sensation is preserved to painful stimuli with normal sphincter function. The follow-up radiographs [Figure 6] have documented that spine stabilization achieved with the rods and screws are intact and projecting in place and not yet fused. Follow-up MRI will be arranged in further follow-up appointment.

DISCUSSION

AAI in patients with Down syndrome is often underrecognized until clinical symptoms emerge, including weakness, spasticity, or, in severe cases, quadriparesis and respiratory compromise.[4] Neurological deterioration is typically attributed to ligamentous laxity, odontoid dysplasia, and congenital vertebral anomalies contributing to instability.[3,5] Here, our patient presented with AAI and basilar invagination, leading to cervicomedullary compression. Surgical stabilization is the gold standard for patients with progressive neurological deficits and/or significant radiographic abnormalities/instability.[2,4] We chose to perform an occiput-C4 instrumented fusion to decompress the cord and provide stability at the craniovertebral junction. Postoperatively, the child demonstrated early signs of neurological improvement in term of motor power in both upper and lower limbs (from 2/5 to 3/5) prior the discharge and in the first follow-up, he shows further improvement within the 6-week postoperative period. We recognize that the failure to operate earlier likely contributed to her more severe and permanent postoperative neurological deficits. CONCLUSION

This case highlights the importance of recognizing AAI in children with Down syndrome. A high index of suspicion, supported by multimodal imaging such as MRI and CT, is essential for the timely diagnosis of progressive cord compression and/or spinal instability, conditions that, if left unrecognized, may result in irreversible neurological deficits but can often be effectively managed with early surgical stabilization.
