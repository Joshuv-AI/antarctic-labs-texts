Efficacy and safety of a novel pulsed field ablation system in paroxysmal atrial fibrillation
Authors: Qiming Liu, Hanxiong Liu, Xiaoshu Cheng, Zhihui Zhang, Chenyang Jiang, Zhiyu Ling, Yan Wang, Xuping Li, Zhen Zhang, Qi Chen, Wenzhi Luo, Zhenjiang Liu, Duan Luo, Jianhua Yu, Guoshu Yang, Qinmei Xiong, Yan Luo, Jinzhu Hu, Juying Zhang, Shenghua Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulsed field ablation (PFA) is an emerging innovative technology for treating paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (PAF), featured by its superior speed, accuracy, and reduced risk to adjacent tissues. The NOVA-AF study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of a novel PFA system equipped with two PFA catheters: a circular PFA catheter and a linear PFA catheter integrating contact force-sensing capability, in patients with symptomatic PAF.MethodsThis prospective, multicenter, single-arm study enrolled 161 patients with symptomatic PAF from seven medical centers in China. The composite primary effectiveness endpoint was defined as freedom from atrial tachyarrhythmia recurrence, device failure, multiple ablations during the blanking period, or antiarrhythmic drug failure during the evaluation period. The primary safety endpoint was the incidence of major adverse events (MAEs) related to the devices or procedures within three months of the procedure.ResultsAll the ablated lesions (100%) were acutely electrically isolated. No patients underwent repeat ablation during the 90-day blanking period. The 12-month Kaplan–Meier estimates for primary effectiveness success and clinical success (absence of symptomatic atrial fibrillation recurrence) were 83.0% (95% confidence interval, 76.2%–88.0%) and 94.9%, respectively. No MAEs occurred.ConclusionThis novel PFA system is effective and safe for the ablation treatment of patients with symptomatic PAF.RegistryChinese Clinical Trial Registry (www.chictr.org.cn), TRN: ChiCTR2400091941, Registration date: Nov 6 2024 (retrospectively registered).Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10840-026-02284-z.

Introduction

Over the past few years, pulsed field ablation (PFA), an innovative treatment modality, has emerged as a promising technique, providing a novel ablation choice in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) [1]. It offers a unique, non-thermal, and non-freezing energy that differs from conventional ablation techniques. PFA releases intermittent high-intensity electric fields in microsecond or nanosecond pulses, creating electroporation in cell membranes and increasing membrane permeability. This technology leverages differential pulsed electric field thresholds of various tissues to achieve selective ablation. The selective approach protects adjacent critical tissues, such as the esophagus and phrenic nerve, thereby minimizing the risk of collateral damage during the procedure [2].

Current research involving PFA predominantly focuses on the pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) using circular ablation catheters, as PVI is the cornerstone of AF therapy [3, 4]. However, recurrence is often associated with the reconnection of the pulmonary veins (PVs) owing to non-transmural ablation, the presence of non-PV triggers, and the progression of maintaining mechanisms [5]. The ablation of these areas necessitates precise positioning of the electrode. To meet the clinical challenges, the PFA system was developed based on the following considerations: (1) A linear-structured catheter for PFA might provide more precise positioning. (2) Moreover, incorporating contact force (CF)-sensing capability might allow for more accurate monitoring of the contact between the electrode and myocardium, thereby ensuring appropriate force and improving catheter stability. This novel PFA system is thus additionally equipped with a linear-structured PFA catheter incorporating CF-sensing capacity to address the aforementioned obstacles.

The NOVA-AF study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of this novel PFA system including a circular PFA catheter and a linear PFA catheter with CF-sensing capacity in patients with symptomatic paroxysmal AF (PAF).

Methods

Study design

The NOVA-AF study (https://www.chictr.org.cn/; registration number: ChiCTR2400091941) was a prospective multicenter single-arm study. Participants were recruited from seven medical centers in China. This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and the relevant national regulations. Every center followed a unified clinical trial protocol reviewed and approved by the ethics committees before implementation (see Online Resource 1 for details). All amendments to the protocol during the trial were submitted for ethical approval before execution.

Study participants

Patients (aged 18–80 years) with symptomatic PAF were enrolled if they planned to undergo ablation procedures and agreed to participate in this trial, with a left atrial (LA) diameter ≤ 5.0 cm and left ventricular ejection fraction ≥ 40%. Online Resource 2 provides the details of the full inclusion and exclusion criteria. All participants signed informed consent, underwent PFA, and were followed up for 12 months after the initial ablation.

PFA system

The PFA system consists of a three-dimensional (3D) cardiac electrophysiology mapping system (hereinafter referred to as the “3D system”), PFA generator, magnetic-electrical navigation circular pulsed field ablation catheter (the “circular PFA catheter”), and magnetic-electrical navigation CF-sensing pulsed field ablation catheter (the “CF-sensing PFA catheter”). The distal end of the circular PFA catheter is ring-shaped with a diameter of 15–30 mm and equipped with 7–22 electrodes. The fixed spacing between electrodes is 4 mm. Notably, the loop diameter exhibits proportional expansion with increasing electrodes, allowing clinicians to select appropriate dimensions based on PV anatomical measurements. The CF-sensing PFA catheter maintains a standardized configuration with 4 electrodes, featuring electrode spacing ranging from 2 to 3 mm. All four study devices were independently developed and manufactured by APT Medical Inc. (Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) which received approval from the National Medical Products Administration in China in December 2024.

PFA procedure

General anesthesia, deep sedation, or local anesthesia was administered based on the patient’s condition. Heparin was administered intravenously during the procedure to maintain a target activated clotting time of ≥ 300 s. The circular and CF-sensing PFA catheters were introduced and connected to the 3D system (HT-9000Pro) and PFA generator (PFG-micro1). The circular PFA catheter was used to create a 3D model of the LA.

Induction and ablation procedure

All patients underwent a standard induction and ablation protocol (see Online Resource 3 for details). The primary ablation strategy was circumferential PVI, performed using both a circular PFA catheter and a CF-sensing PFA catheter. Then, spontaneous or induced non-pulmonary vein triggers were identified and subsequently ablated. Right atrial cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI) linear ablation was required in cases with documented typical atrial flutter (AFL). Similarly, linear or focal ablation was delivered to eliminate documented inducible atrial tachycardia (AT). During the ablation procedure, the circular PFA catheter emitted a biphasic voltage of 800–1800 V, while the CF-sensing PFA catheter delivered a biphasic voltage output of 1200–1800 V. Both PFA catheters convey a pulse width of 5 µs (Fig. 1).

Consideration of ablation procedure

For the PV, which is a cylindrical vessel, a strip–shaped injury area can be formed after one discharge, and isolation in PVs can be achieved after 3–5 discharges, with the typical lesion depth of 2.4–3.7 mm caused by the circular PFA catheter, which is derived from dose–response testing.

However, the circular PFA catheter, designed for PV ostia, often has poor antral contact due to its oval shape, limiting effective ablation in the antrum—a common site of reconnection [6]. The left atrial appendage (LAA), anterior to the left PVs, is separated by the thick (~ 5 mm) PV-LAA ridge [7], often resulting in non-transmural ablation, which is also a common site of PV reconnection [8, 9]. In contrast, the CF-sensing PFA catheter’s linear design enables stable, point-by-point ablation with focused energy delivery and deeper lesions (5.0–5.5 mm), ensuring transmural ablation in challenging areas like the antrum and LAA ridge.

Studies indicated that up to 20% of AF was triggered by ectopic foci outside the PVs [10, 11]. This has spurred the development of strategies to identify and target non-PV triggers, including the superior vena cava (SVC), coronary sinus, and other areas [12]. Besides, AF sometimes coexists with other atrial arrhythmias, notably AFL [13–15]. In the context of AF ablation, targeting and ablating previously documented or inducible sustained AT or AFL is beneficial, because it reduces the probability of subsequent arrhythmia recurrence [15–19]. Meanwhile, the use of a CF-sensing PFA catheter enables the ablation of non-PV triggers and other atrial arrhythmias combined.

Follow-up

The blanking period refers to the period within 90 days after the initial operation, and the evaluation period refers to the period ranging from the end of blanking period to 12 months after the initial operation. All patients underwent follow-up visits at 7 days, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months after the procedure. Recurrence of AF, AFL, or AT was evaluated using a standard 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) or 24-h Holter monitoring at 3, 6, and 12 months. At the 12-month follow-up, 24-h Holter monitoring was imperative. Additional unscheduled visit, ECG and/or Holter monitoring were performed to exclude recurrence, when a patient exhibited suspicious symptoms or signs.

Study endpoints

The primary effectiveness success was defined as a composite outcome comprising freedom from the following four events: (1) Recurrence: During the evaluation period, continuous AF, AFL, or AT was recorded on the 24-h Holter (≥ 30 s) or standard 12-lead ECG (≥ 10 s). (2) Device failure: The PFA devices failed to complete the procedure during the initial or blanking period operations. (3) Multiple ablations: During the blanking period, two or more ablation procedures were performed for AF, AFL, or AT. (4) Antiarrhythmic drug (AAD) failure: Patients took Class I or III AADs during the evaluation period. Transient AAD use during the blanking period would not be classified as a primary effectiveness failure.

The secondary effectiveness endpoint was the acute success rate. Acute success was defined as the confirmation of entrance block in all PVs. An additional effectiveness endpoint was the clinical success rate. The latter was defined as no recurrence of continuous AF recorded on the 24-h Holter (≥ 30 s) or standard 12-lead ECG (≥ 10 s) with symptoms, such as palpitations, fatigue, chest tightness, or chest pain, during the evaluation period.

The primary safety endpoint was the incidence of major adverse events (MAEs) related to the devices or procedures that occurred intraoperatively or within 3 months after the procedure. MAEs included death, atrio-esophageal fistula, cardiac perforation/tamponade, myocardial infarction, stroke, thromboembolism, air embolism, transient ischemic attack, phrenic nerve injury, pneumothorax, atrioventricular block, PV stenosis, major vascular access complications, and severe muscle spasms during discharge.

Statistical analysis

Simulations were performed to estimate the power for achieving the primary safety and effectiveness endpoints. The safety goal was set at 14% based on a precedent accepted by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, derived from a pivotal clinical trial for a comparable radiofrequency (RF) ablation device [20]. While the 2017 HRS/EHRA/ECAS/APHRS/SOLAECE expert consensus recommended a performance goal for the primary effectiveness endpoint of 50% [21], the performance goal in this study was set at 55%, reflecting the maturation of RF ablation techniques and the corresponding improvement in effectiveness results over time, and this threshold exceeds the 50% goal used in other pivotal trials (PULSED AF, ADMIRE, INSPIRE) [4, 22, 23]. We assumed 6% of patients would have a primary safety endpoint event and 68% of patients would remain free from primary effectiveness failure. After accounting for a 15% attrition rate, a total sample size of 140 participants was calculated to provide 80% power for each primary endpoint analysis, using an exact binomial test at a one-sided α of 0.025.

Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as numbers (%). Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival analysis was conducted to assess primary effectiveness and clinical success. The 95% confidence interval (CI) for the primary effectiveness endpoint was calculated using the log–log transformation, while the primary safety endpoint was assessed using the exact binomial test.

To identify risk factors associated with the primary effectiveness failure, univariable and multivariable logistic regression models were fitted. First, univariable logistic regression was used to assess the associations of demographic characteristics, baseline medical history, and procedural data with the primary effectiveness endpoint. Continuous variables were considered for categorization on the basis of clinically meaningful groups or by their distribution. Variables showing a statistical significance of P < 0.20 in the univariable analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. In cases of severe multicollinearity, only one variable was selected for the multivariable model.

The sponsor was not involved in adjudicating effectiveness or safety events. Data analysis was performed by an independent third-party statistical center (West China School of Public Health, Sichuan University) using SAS software (version 9.4, SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Follow-up

Among 161 patients, 158 (98.1%) successfully completed their follow-up. The data on ECG and Holter examination compliance are detailed in Online Resource 4. Eight patients exceeded their designated 12-month follow-up window, with a maximum of 452 days. Two patients withdrew from the study and one died of sepsis secondary to a respiratory infection associated with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

Effectiveness endpoints

PVI was achieved in all patients with an acute success rate of 100%. Acute electrical isolation was also achieved in all ablation targets outside the PVs. Nineteen patients who underwent further ablation for other atrial arrhythmias were successfully treated with the CF-sensing PFA catheters (Table 2).

The outcomes for the primary effectiveness endpoint are shown in Table 3. A total of 28 patients (17.4%) experienced atrial arrhythmia recurrence, including 6 (3.7%) with recurrent AFL; potential mechanisms are summarized in Online Resource 5. Two patients (1.2%) did not experience recurrence but were considered failures due to the use of Class I AADs during the evaluation period. The 12-month KM estimates for primary effectiveness success and clinical success were 83.0% (95% CI, 76.2%–88.0%) (Fig. 2) and 94.9% (95% CI, 90.1%–97.4%) (Fig. 3), respectively.

Risk factors associated with primary effectiveness outcome

Logistic regression identified predictors of the primary effectiveness failure (Online Resource 6). In the multivariable model, the odds ratio for patients with concomitant atrial arrhythmia was 4.03 (95% CI, 1.40–11.59; P = 0.010); for left atrial diameter > 38 mm, 2.42 (1.02–5.75; P = 0.045); for general versus local anesthesia, 0.49 (0.20–1.22; P = 0.13); and for congenital heart disease, 2.31 (0.23–22.76; P = 0.48).

Primary safety endpoint

No MAEs related to the devices or procedures occurred intraoperatively or within 3 months postoperatively (95% CI, 0%–2.3%).

Other safety event

One patient experienced coronary spasm during the ablation of CTI without prophylactically administered nitroglycerin, which was potentially associated with the CF-sensing PFA catheter. Intraoperative ECG monitoring of the patient revealed inferior wall ST-segment elevation, and emergency coronary angiography revealed a spasm in the midsection of the right coronary artery. After diltiazem administration intravenously, the coronary spasm was effectively relieved, with the ST segment returning to the isoelectric line. The patient had no sequelae during subsequent follow-up. Apart from this single case, none of the remaining 12 patients who underwent CTI ablation experienced coronary spasm, and prophylactic nitroglycerin was not administered in any of these patients.

Itemized lists of all reported adverse events and serious adverse events are provided in Online Resource 7 and Online Resource 8, respectively.

Discussion

The NOVA-AF clinical trial is a multicenter, prospective, single-arm study evaluating the clinical efficacy and safety of a novel PFA system in patients with symptomatic PAF. The key findings were as follows: (i) The circular and linear PFA catheters were guided by the 3D mapping system, allowing nine procedures to be performed without fluoroscopy. (ii) The combined use of two PFA catheters enabled the independent completion of all ablations in symptomatic PAF patients without using catheters of other energy modalities. Specifically, the linear CF-sensing PFA catheter was innovatively utilized for point-by-point ablation, targeting the PV antrum, ectopic trigger foci outside the PVs, and other atrial arrhythmias combined. (iii) 100% acute PVI rate was achieved for all patients. (iv) No patients underwent repeat ablation during the blanking period. (v) The 12-month KM estimate for primary effectiveness success was 83.0%. (vi) The 12-month KM estimate for clinical success was 94.9%, significantly reducing the recurrence of symptomatic AF. (vii) The incidence of MAEs was 0%, with no instance of PV stenosis, atrio-esophageal fistula, and phrenic nerve injury.

Effectiveness

The 12-month KM estimate for the primary effectiveness success of this NOVA-AF study was 83.0%, with a lower bound of 95% CI at 76.2%, surpassing the pre-specified effectiveness goal of 55%. The results of this pivotal Chinese study demonstrated that the effectiveness of the PFA system was comparable to those observed in other recent clinical trials. The PULSED AF, INSPIRE, and ESPFA-CN21 trials reported 12-month primary effectiveness success rates of 58.7%–70.9% [4, 23, 24]. Our trial showed a higher primary effectiveness success rate of 83.0%. The aforementioned trials allowed for one repeat ablation during the blanking period. In our clinical trial, no patients underwent repeat ablation during this period, whereas in the PULSED AF and ESPFA-CN21 trials, 4 and 10 patients underwent repeat ablation, respectively. Furthermore, administering patients with previously failed Class I/III AADs at a dose lower than the highest ineffective historical dose for AF during the evaluation period was not considered a failure in the PULSED AF and INSPIRE trials. When applying the same definition of primary effectiveness success for PAF trials, the randomized ADVENT pivotal trial, which featured more intensive rhythm monitoring during follow-up, reported a KM estimate for 12-month single-procedure success off Class I/III AADs of 73.3% [25]. While our 12-month effectiveness outcome appears favorable, it should be interpreted within the context of a single-arm design, which limits direct statistical comparison with other PFA systems.

In the NOVA-AF study, the PFA catheters included circular and CF-sensing PFA catheters. The combined use of two PFA catheters enabled the independent completion of all ablations in patients with PAF, and ablation catheters with other energy sources were not needed to perform the accurate ablation on dot- or line-targets. In contrast, other PFA trials, including the PULSED AF, INSPIRE, and ADVENT pivotal trials, employed RF energy other than pulsed electric field for lesions outside the PVs. In our trial, the CF-sensing PFA catheter was used to reinforce ablation in the PV antrum, particularly in areas not adequately covered by the circular PFA catheter, thus addressing the ridges in the antrum. This strategy was helpful to reduce the recurrence rate of AF by minimizing PV reconnections. This point is highlighted by an expert consensus [26], which suggests that > 80% of AF recurrence cases involved PV reconnection even with the use of larger and more comprehensive spherical catheters during repeat ablation procedures. It was also used to ablate ectopic trigger foci, such as those in the SVC, which accounted for 10.6% (17/161) of the cases. Additionally, the CF-sensing PFA catheter targeted concomitant atrial arrhythmias, including AFL, AT, and premature atrial contraction, which accounted for 11.8% (19/161) of the cases. All regions mentioned above were successfully isolated. Subgroup analysis identified both concomitant atrial arrhythmias and left atrial diameter > 38 mm as independent predictors of primary effectiveness failure, underscoring the prognostic value of the underlying arrhythmogenic substrate. Notably, despite a higher prevalence of concomitant atrial arrhythmias in our trial compared to the ESPFA-CN21 trial (11.8% vs. 5.3% [3]), the overall success rate was not compromised.

Influence of CF values

During ablation, the mean CF value across all patients was 6.8 ± 2.2 g, with the majority (60.2%, 97 of 161) demonstrating CF values between 5 and 10 g. Under CF guidance, our trial achieved this long-term success without MAEs. Compared to commercial CF-guided RF ablation catheters, PFA at a CF of 5–10 g maintained efficacy while avoiding over-ablation complications, especially no pericardial tamponade occurred. Compared to other commercial PFA catheters without CF (PULSED AF, ESPFA-CN21, and ADVENT trials), NOVA-AF’s higher effectiveness suggested that PFA may need an appropriate CF to achieve transmural injury, especially in thick tissues. For instance, the ridge between the superior and inferior pulmonary veins has a thickness ranging from 3 to 6 mm [27], while the mitral isthmus ranges from 2.5 to 8 mm [28]. This specific CF application may therefore further enhance long-term effectiveness.

Efficiency

The procedure time (119.7 ± 48.3 min) and LA dwell time (65.5 ± 27.9 min) in this study were comparable to those reported in other PFA studies for PAF (70–134 min and 34–65 min, respectively) [4, 23, 25, 29, 30]. Nevertheless, the procedure time was shorter than the weighted mean durations reported in a meta-analysis of 1582 PAF patients (161.8 min for cryoballoon and 165.9 min for RF ablation) [31]. In our study, LA dwell time was defined as the time from insertion to removal of the two PFA catheters, encompassing both 3D modeling and ablation. By contrast, other studies reported the LA dwell time of only a single PFA catheter, even when RF ablation catheters were used concurrently. Additionally, some procedures in these studies were performed without a 3D mapping system, and their LA dwell time calculations therefore excluded the time required for 3D modeling [4, 23, 25, 29]. Importantly, the safety of our ablation strategy was underscored by the absence of perioperative thromboembolic events. Furthermore, the fluoroscopy time (12.7 ± 7.0 min, including nine fluoroscopy-free procedures) was within the 8–26 min range reported in other PFA studies [4, 23, 25, 29, 30]. This low reliance on fluoroscopy was attributed to the seamless integration of the 3D system with the PFA generator and both catheters, a combination that facilitated accurate model reconstruction, precise voltage mapping, and execution of PFA procedures under 3D guidance.

Safety

No MAEs occurred in this study, with an upper bound of 95% CI at 2.3%, indicating that the safety outcomes were favorable compared to the pre‑specified safety goal of 14%. Furthermore, no instances of stroke, PV stenosis, atrio-esophageal fistula, phrenic nerve injury, or thromboembolism were observed throughout the trial. These findings are consistent with the major safety event rates reported in other recent multicenter clinical trials of PFA, which ranged from 0% to 2.5% [23, 30, 32, 33]. In this trial, no study device-related pericardial tamponade was observed, which may be attributed to the CF guidance provided by the CF-sensing PFA catheter.

A coronary artery spasm occurred when the CF-sensing PFA catheter was used to ablate the CTI, possibly related to pulsed field energy. In a specialized study of similar linear-structured PFA catheter focusing on coronary spasm, among 33 patients with AF undergoing CTI ablation, 16 (48%) experienced this complication [34]. Preclinical reports suggested that PFA lesions should be performed more than 6.5 mm away from a coronary vessel to avoid vasospasm. Human anatomical data indicate that the minimal distance between the right coronary artery and tricuspid isthmus is 4.2 ± 2.1 mm, suggesting that PFA in the tricuspid isthmus carries a heightened risk of inducing right coronary spasm [35]. Local coronary spasms resulting from PFA are likely caused by the direct contraction of the vascular smooth muscle adjacent to the coronary artery, triggered by the intraoperative high-voltage pulsed electric field, or by vasoconstriction due to the release of specific mediators [36].

To date, the largest retrospective PFA study, MANIFEST-17K, reported a coronary spasm incidence of 0.14% (25/17,642) [37]. This rate is comparable to traditional cryoablation and RF ablation energies, which had an incidence of 0.19% in a meta-analysis involving 22,232 AF patients [38].

Studies on the PFA of the tricuspid isthmus have demonstrated that the prophylactic administration of nitroglycerin effectively mitigated the risk of severe coronary spasms [39]. Therefore, when ablating the CTI, prophylactic administration of nitroglycerin can be considered to prevent coronary artery spasm.

Limitations

The NOVA-AF study lacks a control arm. Although this study design aligns with most pre-market PFA trials [4, 22, 23, 29], future randomized trials with comparators (conventional RF, cryoablation, or other PFA systems) are needed for effectiveness assessments of the current system. Geographic generalizability warrants consideration as this study was conducted exclusively in an Asian population. Nevertheless, fundamental pathophysiological consistency of AF, standardized ablation practices, and harmonized healthcare settings support the transferability of outcomes to other populations. It is noted that the study population was restricted to patients with PAF and largely preserved cardiac structure and function; therefore, the generalizability of these findings to patients with persistent AF, advanced atrial remodeling, or significant heart failure remains uncertain. Variability in follow-up intensity may impact effectiveness reporting. Our study did not include continuous, implantable, or transtelephonic monitoring, which might result in less intensive rhythm surveillance that could miss isolated recurrence events. However, compared to the PULSED AF and ADVENT studies, our study achieved higher rhythm monitoring compliance during the follow-up period. The 12-month Holter monitoring compliance rate was 100% in our study, versus 87.2% in the PULSED AF study and 82.7% in the ADVENT study [40, 41]. Additionally, our monitoring protocol was similar to the ESPFA-CN21 trial, and symptom-triggered arrhythmia monitoring was performed for suspected recurrences. Furthermore, a stringent recurrence criterion (any ≥ 10 s ECG-documented atrial arrhythmia) was applied to mitigate potential underdetection. Besides, extended follow-up beyond the conventional 12 months is needed to assess the long-term durability of electrical isolation at ablated sites and late arrhythmia recurrence.

Conclusions

The NOVA-AF study demonstrated the efficacy and safety of the novel PFA system. The combined use of circular and linear CF-sensing PFA catheters allows for independent completion of all ablations in patients with PAF, particularly in regions outside the PVs. The PFA system achieved 100% acute PVI without PFA-related atrio-esophageal fistula, phrenic nerve injury, PV stenosis, or pericardial tamponade within one year after the procedure. The observation of a coronary spasm suggests that caution is necessary when applying pulsed field energy to the tricuspid isthmus, and prophylactic administration of nitroglycerin is a measure to consider. Furthermore, the 12-month success rate off Class I/III AADs was highly promising, with notable freedom from atrial arrhythmias.
