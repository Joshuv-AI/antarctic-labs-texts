Ruptured and trapped: balloon dissection of left main coronary artery successfully managed with bailout stenting—a case report
Authors: Ravikumar Aluri, Sujani Vinnakota, Lavanya Narra, Shyam Sundar Reddy P, Ajit Kumar Patnaik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAbrupt left main coronary artery (LMCA) closure from ruptured balloon during percutaneous coronary intervention is rare and can be catastrophic. A comprehensive knowledge of the various bailout techniques in such situations can be life-saving. We describe a case of abrupt left main vessel closure leading to haemodynamic instability and the techniques employed for a prompt and successful revascularization.Case summaryA 74-year-old gentleman presented with effort angina. His trans-radial coronary angiogram revealed significant LMCA stenosis with triple vessel disease. While attempting to prepare the left circumflex (LCx) artery lesion with balloon dilatation, balloon rupture led to retrograde LMCA dissection with abrupt vessel closure and haemodynamic instability. Subsequently, bailout stenting from LMCA to left anterior descending artery was done followed by TAP stenting (T And small Protrusion) to LCx, thereby compressing the remanent balloon fragment against the vessel wall of the LCx (crush technique) and achieving TIMI III (Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction) flow.DiscussionThis case highlights the catastrophic complication of abrupt LMCA closure with remanent balloon fragment that may occur with balloon rupture. Recognizing the various factors that lead to balloon rupture is important. Establishing LMCA perfusion while maintaining haemodynamics should be the primary goal in patients with acute LMCA occlusion. Bailout stenting decreases the time lapse and subsequent prolonged ischaemia with worsening left ventricular function compared to coronary artery bypass grafting.

Introduction

Acute left main coronary artery (LMCA) dissection during percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is a rare but catastrophic event accounting to 0.07%.1 Iatrogenic dissections are mostly due to inappropriate guide catheter manipulation or rarely due to guide wire and balloon complications. The coronary blood flow to a large area of myocardium becomes compromised leading to haemodynamic instability. Rapidly achieving vessel patency while stabilizing the haemodynamics remains the primary goal. Treatment options include PCI with bailout stenting and coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). Percutaneous coronary intervention is often the preferred modality of management in patients with unstable haemodynamics as the time lapse in preparing for CABG can prolong the duration of ischaemia and further worsen left ventricular (LV) function.2 We present a rare case of iatrogenic LMCA dissection due to balloon rupture and retained balloon fragment that lead to haemodynamic instability and was expeditiously managed with bailout stenting and crushing the remnant balloon fragment against the vessel wall.

Case presentation

A 74-year-old gentleman, a known type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and hypertensive (HTN) with significant past history of coronary artery disease, was admitted with effort angina, NYHA class III (New York Heart Association). His coronary angiogram done 2 years ago revealed single vessel disease with 60% stenosis of proximal right coronary artery, and he was on medical management with antianginals for the same.

His electrocardiogram (ECG) showed sinus rhythm with ST depressions and T wave inversions in lateral leads (LV strain pattern). Transthoracic 2-D echocardiography (TTE) revealed no regional wall motion abnormality with normal LV systolic function with LV ejection fraction of 64%.

Coronary angiography was done which revealed significant LMCA stenosis and triple vessel disease. The LMCA had ostio-proximal 70% stenosis and pressure damping was noted with 5 Fr diagnostic catheter. The left anterior descending artery (LAD) was calcific with 80% stenosis in the mid segment. Left circumflex (LCx) had ostial 90% calcific stenosis. Right coronary artery was non-dominant with proximal 90% stenosis (Figure 1).

Coronary angiogram showing significant ostial left main coronary artery, ostial left circumflex, and mid left anterior descending artery calcific disease.

The left main artery was engaged with extra-back up guide catheter (EBU 3.5, 6 Fr, Medtronic Minneapolis). The LAD and LCx were both wired with Versaturn guide wires (Abbott). Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) imaging was done to LAD. However, the IVUS catheter could not cross the proximal LCx lesion. Patient was planned for LMCA bifurcation stenting (provisional strategy) with one drug-eluting stent (DES) from LMCA to LCx and another DES to mid LAD. Lesion preparation of LAD and LMCA was done with intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) balloon (3.5 × 12 mm; Shockwave C2 + balloon) which was inflated for eight cycles. Intravascular lithotripsy balloon could not cross the LCx lesion. Thus, pre-dilatation was done to ostio-proximal LCx with 3.5 × 13 mm Aperta (scoring balloon) and dog-boning was noted. Further pre-dilatation was done with 4.0 × 12 mm NC Euphora balloon (Medtronic). While inflating to 14 atmospheres, the balloon ruptured in the proximal LCx. While attempting to remove the balloon system, the mid shaft of the balloon body fractured and part of the balloon body with distal balloon marker remained embedded in the LCx lesion and was associated with a type F dissection of the LMCA. Patient had ventricular tachycardia with sudden cardiac arrest and was electrically cardioverted, resuscitated, and intubated.

In the chaos, the entire system got disengaged and had to be re-engaged with EBU guiding catheter (EBU 3.5, 6 Fr, Medtronic Minneapolis) and immediate bailout stenting was done from Ostial LMCA to proximal LAD with a 3.5 × 18 mm everolimus-eluting stent (XIENCE Sierra; Abbott) and TIMI III flow was established in LAD (Figure 2). With micro catheter support (Finecross, Terumo), the LCx lesion was crossed with a fielder XT-A guide wire (Asahi Intecc Medical) and later exchanged to versaturn (Abbott) guide wire. After adequate strut dilatation, TIMI III flow was restored in LCx. However, a remnant of balloon body with distal marker was noted entrapped in the proximal LCx on angiogram (Figure 3).

Left anterior descending artery re-wired and flow established with bailout stenting from left main coronary artery to left anterior descending artery. Balloon marker of the entrapped balloon can be seen.

Balloon shaft fracture with entrapped balloon fragment in the proximal left circumflex and Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction III flow restored after serial balloon dilatation.

Intravascular ultrasound imaging showed the presence of the balloon marker with a shadow artefact in the proximal LCx (Figure 4). A 4.0 × 12 mm everolimus-eluting stent (XIENCE Sierra; Abbott) was deployed in the ostio-proximal LCx (TAP technique—T And small Protrusion), ‘crushing’ the trapped balloon fragment against the arterial wall, as confirmed with imaging. A 3.25 × 28 mm everolimus-eluting stent (XIENCE Sierra; Abbott) was deployed in the mid LAD overlapping the proximal LAD stent. Final angiography and IVUS imaging confirmed well-apposed stents and no residual stenosis (Figure 5). The patient was extubated the next day and discharged 2 days later with stable haemodynamics. He remains asymptomatic 6 months after the coronary intervention.

Discussion

Abrupt closure of left main artery during PCI is a rare and life-threatening complication. The major mechanisms for iatrogenic left main coronary dissections include mechanical factors like inappropriate catheter selection, undue manipulation of guiding catheter, vigorous contrast medium injection, using stiffer coronary guidewires or other devices, and balloon rupture. Significant atherosclerosis with calcification, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypertension, and age are the associated risk factors.3

Coronary dissection secondary to balloon rupture is a rare event and is usually due to inflation pressures more than nominal reaching burst pressures, significant over-sizing of the balloon, presence of calcium spurs, complex coronary lesions including in-stent restenosis, and rarely due to the use of reused balloons during the procedure.

Balloon rupture can lead to intimal tears forming intramural haematoma, coronary dissection, perforation, distal embolization, and acute occlusion of the coronary artery as in our case.4 A sudden drop in pressure of the inflating device with blood in the system should alert the operator that the balloon has ruptured. Occasionally, the vessel may be opacified by leaking contrast. The balloon should be deflated immediately and removed gently to avoid entrapment of device material. Immediate contrast injection should be performed to screen for complications.5,6 Maintaining wire position within the vessel is crucial and enables immediate bailout stenting, thus promptly restoring the coronary flow.

National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute classification is widely used to classify coronary artery dissections. Clinically, types A and B are classified as uncomplicated, whereas types C through F represent complicated dissection and need to be managed rapidly.7 Nevertheless, the decision to treat a dissection depends on the clinical and haemodynamic parameters in relation to distal perfusion.

Rapidly achieving vessel patency while stabilizing the haemodynamics remains the primary goal in the management of complicated dissections. Treatment options are PCI with bailout stenting and CABG for complicated dissections and conservative management in case of type A and type B dissections. Percutaneous coronary intervention is often the preferred modality of management in patients with unstable haemodynamics as the time lapse in preparing for CABG can prolong the duration of ischaemia and further worsen LV function.2

In our case, the occlusive dissection resulted from balloon rupture, though it was inflated to nominal pressures only. The presence of underlying calcium might be the culprit. Our case was further complicated by entrapped balloon body fragment in the ostio-proximal LCx as confirmed by IVUS imaging.

Various retrieval techniques for entrapped balloon such as balloon trapping, mini-star, and snaring have been described.8 In our case, the entrapped balloon fragment was jailed by the LMCA-LAD stent and was difficult to retrieve. Hence, we crushed the entrapped balloon fragment against the vessel wall by deploying a stent in the LCx. TIMI III flow was achieved and the adequacy of stent expansion was confirmed with IVUS.

Conclusion

Bailout PCI of the unprotected LMCA for occlusive dissection promptly restores coronary blood flow and is life-saving. Balloon rupture is best prevented by avoiding high inflation pressures, using a slightly undersized balloon in complex lesions, using high pressure non-compliant balloons, and carefully preparing the target coronary vessel before high-pressure balloon inflations. Operators must be familiar with catheter-based methods for retrieval of the entrapped balloon. Intravascular imaging is indispensable in case of complications and confirms adequate stent expansion and stent strut apposition.
