Introduction

Tumor‐to‐tumor metastasis is a rare pathological phenomenon in which one neoplasm serves as the host for metastasis from another distinct primary tumor. Since its first description in 1902, approximately 200 cases have been documented in the English literature [1, 2]. Lung carcinoma is the most common donor tumor, whereas renal cell carcinoma (RCC) and meningioma represent the most frequent malignant and benign recipient tumors, respectively [2, 3]. Although the precise mechanisms underlying tumor‐to‐tumor metastasis remain unclear, this phenomenon is generally explained by the mechanical theory and the seed‐and‐soil hypothesis [4].

Herein, we report a unique and biologically intriguing case in which the LCNEC component of a combined lung carcinoma metastasized selectively to clear cell RCC despite the synchronous presence of a meningioma, providing new insight into the microenvironmental determinants of tumor‐to‐tumor metastasis.

Case Presentation

A 65‐year‐old man with a history of meningioma resection 2 years earlier presented with radiological evidence of recurrence and underwent neurosurgical resection. Preoperative computed tomography (CT) revealed a 21 × 20 mm nodule in the right lung and a 38 × 35 mm enhancing mass in the lower pole of the left kidney (Figure 1). Lung cancer–related tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), squamous cell carcinoma antigen (SCC), neuron‐specific enolase (NSE), and cytokeratin fragment (CYFRA), were all within normal ranges.

Computed tomography findings of the pulmonary and renal tumors. (A) 21 × 20 mm nodule in the right lung. (B, C) 38 × 35 mm enhancing mass in the lower pole of the left kidney on (B) early phase and (C) delayed phase imaging. No specific radiological findings suggestive of tumor‐to‐tumor metastasis were identified preoperatively.

Three months after resection of the recurrent meningioma, the patient underwent video‐assisted thoracic surgery (VATS). Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining revealed areas of adenocarcinoma as well as regions of poorly differentiated carcinoma (Figure 2). The poorly differentiated component exhibited solid nests of tumor cells with frequent peripheral palisading of tumor cell nuclei and a high mitotic rate, raising suspicion for large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (LCNEC). Immunohistochemical analysis of this component demonstrated positivity for CD56 and synaptophysin, indicating neuroendocrine differentiation. Based on these findings, the lung tumor was diagnosed as combined LCNEC and adenocarcinoma (pT1b). One month later, partial nephrectomy was performed for the left renal mass. The postoperative course was uneventful. No specific radiological or gross pathological findings suggestive of tumor‐to‐tumor metastasis were identified preoperatively.

Pathological Findings

Grossly, the renal tumor measured 45 mm in diameter and appeared yellow‐brown and well circumscribed, with peripheral hemorrhagic and necrotic changes (Figure 3).

Histopathological findings of clear cell renal cell carcinoma with an intratumoral metastatic focus. (A) Gross pathological specimen of the renal tumor. (B) Low‐magnification hematoxylin and eosin staining showing metastatic focus (red box) and surrounding clear cell RCC (yellow box). (C) High‐magnification image of typical clear cell RCC. (D) High‐magnification image of the metastatic focus lacking definitive adenocarcinoma morphology. (E) High‐magnification image of the metastatic focus showing solid nest formation and peripheral palisading (Original magnification: ×40 [B], ×200 [C–E]).

Microscopically, the lesion demonstrated classic features of clear cell RCC, including clear cytoplasm, delicate vasculature, and prominent nucleoli (Figure 3). Within the clear cell RCC, a discrete metastatic focus was identified. HE staining did not demonstrate definitive adenocarcinoma morphology in this area. However, the metastatic focus showed morphological features similar to those of the LCNEC component of the primary lung tumor, including solid nest formation and peripheral palisading. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positivity for CD56, synaptophysin, and TTF‐1 in the metastatic focus, whereas the surrounding clear cell RCC was negative for these markers (Figure 4). The primary lung tumor exhibited an identical immunohistochemical profile.

Immunohistochemical findings supporting metastasis of the LCNEC component to clear cell RCC. Low‐magnification images showing positivity for CD56 (A), synaptophysin (B), and TTF‐1 (C) in the metastatic focus (red boxes), whereas the surrounding clear cell RCC was negative for these markers. High‐magnification images of CD56 (D), synaptophysin (E), and TTF‐1 (F) staining in the metastatic focus (Original magnification: ×100 [A–C], ×200 [D–F]).

Collectively, the morphological and immunohistochemical findings strongly suggested that the metastatic focus originated from the LCNEC component of the combined lung carcinoma and metastasized to the clear cell RCC.

Discussion

Tumor‐to‐tumor metastasis is a relatively rare pathological phenomenon, with approximately 200 cases reported in the English literature to date [1, 2]. The diagnostic criteria proposed by Campbell et al. [5] include: (1) the presence of at least two primary neoplasms; (2) the recipient tumor being a true neoplasm; and (3) the metastatic tumor demonstrating established growth within the recipient tumor rather than representing contiguous spread (“collision tumor”) or intravascular tumor emboli. These criteria also exclude metastasis to lymphoid tissues that already contain lymphoreticular malignancies. Our patient met all of these criteria.

Lung carcinoma has been reported as the most frequent donor tumor in tumor‐to‐tumor metastasis, followed by breast and prostate cancers [2, 3, 6]. RCC represents the most common malignant recipient tumor, whereas meningioma is the most frequent benign recipient tumor [2, 3] (Figure S1). The present case is particularly noteworthy because metastatic involvement occurred selectively within clear cell RCC despite the simultaneous presence of a meningioma, another well‐established recipient tumor.

Two major hypotheses attempt to explain this phenomenon. The mechanical theory posits that tumors with rich vascularity and high blood flow more readily trap circulating metastatic cells [7, 8]. Clear cell RCC is highly vascularized and receives a large proportion of renal arterial blood flow, making it susceptible to metastatic implantation.

The seed‐and‐soil theory, first proposed by Paget and later expanded by Hart [4], suggests that metastatic tumor cells (“seeds”) proliferate preferentially in a favorable microenvironment (“soil”). Clear cell RCC contains abundant lipid and glycogen, which may provide metabolic advantages to metastatic tumor cells [4, 9].

Although meningiomas are also vascularized and historically recognized as benign recipient tumors, they possess characteristics that may hinder metastatic colonization, including slow growth, low metabolic activity, and dense collagenous stroma [10]. Such features may make meningiomas less permissive than RCC for metastatic cell implantation.

In the present case, metastatic involvement was observed exclusively within the clear cell RCC despite the coexistence of a meningioma. Moreover, metastasis originated specifically from the LCNEC component of the combined lung carcinoma, with no evidence of adenocarcinoma metastasis. This highly selective pattern of tumor‐to‐tumor metastasis is exceptionally rare. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case demonstrating selective metastasis of the LCNEC component of a combined lung carcinoma to clear cell RCC while sparing a synchronous meningioma.

Conclusion

This case represents an exceptionally rare example of selective tumor‐to‐tumor metastasis, in which the LCNEC component of a combined lung carcinoma metastasized exclusively to clear cell RCC despite the presence of a concurrent meningioma, highlighting the microenvironmental advantages of clear cell RCC in metastatic implantation and contributing to a better understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying tumor‐to‐tumor metastasis.
