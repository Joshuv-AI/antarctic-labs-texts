ABBV-744 induces autophagy in gastric cancer cells by regulating PI3K/AKT/mTOR/p70S6k and MAPK signaling pathways
Authors: Kun Wang, Jiatong Tang, Shengxian Fan, Haochen Su, Ranran Yu, Yixuan Zhang, Hao Wu, Ying Lv, Shu Zhang, Xiaoping Zou
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The mortality rates of gastric cancer remain high due to limited therapeutic strategies. As a highly selective inhibitor of the BD2 domain of BET family proteins, ABBV-744 has potent chemotherapeutic activity against various human solid tumors. However, whether ABBV-744 has potential anti-tumor effects in gastric cancer remain largely unknown. In this study, we evaluated the effect of ABBV-744 on gastric cancer cells and explored the possible underlying mechanisms. We found that ABBV-744 inhibited the growth of gastric cancer cells and patient-derived tumor organoids in a dose-dependent manner. Cellular experiments revealed that ABBV-744 induced mitochondria damage, reactive oxygen species accumulation, cell cycle arrest and apoptotic cell death in gastric cancer cells. Transcriptomic analysis using RNA-sequencing data identified autophagy as a crucial pathway involved in the cell death caused by ABBV-744. Mechanically, further studies showed that ABBV-744 induced autophagy flux in gastric cancer cells by inactivating PI3K/AKT/mTOR/p70S6k and activating the MAPK signaling pathways. In vivo mouse xenograft studies demonstrated that ABBV-744 significantly suppressed the growth of gastric cancer cells via inducing autophagy. Taken together, our results suggest that ABBV-744 is a novel drug candidate for gastric cancer.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is one of the most common malignant tumors in the world, and according to the latest global cancer data, it ranks fifth in incidence and fourth in mortality worldwide [1], posing a serious threat to public health. Early stage gastric cancer is often easily overlooked due to the lack of effective specific diagnostic markers and usually no obvious symptoms, so most patients are already in advanced stage when diagnosed [2]. In the past few years, although many advances have been made in diagnostic methods and surgical procedures [3], various treatments including radiation chemotherapy [4,5] and immunotherapy [6,7] have been improved, the results of comprehensive clinical treatment for gastric cancer are still unsatisfactory [8]. Therefore, it is important to study the potential molecular mechanisms of malignant progression of gastric cancer and develop more effective treatment strategies to improve the survival outcome.

In recent years, "epigenetic" as a genetic way dispensable of DNA coding information, making the same DNA sequence present in different forms of gene expression [9], plays an important role in the occurrence and development of tumors [10,11]. Epigenetic disorders, especially transcriptional dysregulation, may be an important driver of tumorigenesis and development [12]. In eukaryotic cells, acetylation modification of histones is considered to be the most active post-translational modification, which regulates gene transcription by affecting the interaction between DNA and histones [13]. This is essential for the regulation of chromatin structure and transcription [14]. In addition, acetylated lysine can recruit other chromatin modifiers to specifically recognize acetylated lysine, and these modifiers contain specific structural domains, of which bromodomain is more common.

As regulators of gene transcription, BRD-containing proteins, particularly the bromodomain and extra-terminal (BET) family, mediate gene transcription by acting at the initiation and extension stages of transcription. These proteins are overexpressed in a variety of tumors and have been identified as regulators of key oncogene expression [15]. An increasing number of studies have reported that upregulation of BET proteins leads to abnormal transcriptional regulation [16], which favors tumorigenesis and progression. And the downregulation of BET protein expression and inactivation of its function represent possible mechanisms for the antitumor effects of BET inhibitors [17,18]. BET inhibitors, which inhibit tumorigenesis and progression by blocking the interaction between BET proteins and histone acetylation of lysines [19], [20], [21]. The development of gastric cancer is not only associated with genetic and environmental factors, but also with alterations in epigenetic factors [19]. Therefore, targeting epigenetic modification processes may provide a new approach for the treatment of gastric cancer [20].

ABBV-744 is a highly selective BET inhibitor of BD2 [22], and the antitumor effects of ABBV-744 have been demonstrated in previous studies. For instance, it has been shown to have tumor growth inhibitory effects in hematologic malignancies [23,24] and prostate cancer [25]. Also, ABBV-744 exhibits lower cytotoxicity compared to dual bromodomain BET inhibitors such as ABBV-075 [25]. However, whether ABBV-744 has antitumor effects on gastric cancer remains largely unknown. Thus, in this study, we explored the inhibitory effect of ABBV-744 on gastric cancer cells and therefore conducted an in-depth study of the potential molecular mechanisms that may exist.

Autophagy is an important process of material transformation and metabolism mediated by lysosomes in eukaryotic cells, and autophagic dysfunction has been reported to be associated with a variety of diseases, including immune, metabolic, and neurological disorders, and is closely linked to tumorigenesis [26]. Many studies have linked autophagy with the development and therapeutic response of several cancers, including gastric cancer [27], and it is believed to be involved in key mechanisms related to anti-cancer therapy. Therefore, targeting autophagy may provide new opportunities for anticancer drug development. However, the role of ABBV-744 in autophagy has not been reported yet, and further studies are needed to determine whether ABBV-744 can affect the growth of gastric cancer cells by regulating autophagy.

In this study, we first screened the gastric cancer cell lines AGS and HGC-27 as the target of our subsequent experiments based on the previous studies of our group [28]. We aimed to assess the autophagy induced by ABBV-744 in human gastric cancer cells. We showed that ABBV-744 exerted antitumor effects in human gastric cancer cell lines by inactivating PI3K/Akt/mTOR/p70S6k/S6 signaling pathway and activating MAPK signaling pathway. In conclusion, we identified ABBV-744 as a promising therapeutic agent against human gastric cancer in the upcoming future.

Results

ABBV-744 inhibits proliferation and promotes apoptosis in gastric cancer cell lines

The half maximum inhibitory concentration (IC50) of ABBV-744 was determined in individual gastric cancer cell line, which was 7.4 μM and 3.5 μM for AGS at 48h and 72h, and 4.8μM and 2.3 μM for HGC-27 at 48h and 72h separately. Then according to the IC50 values we have measured, we selected ABBV-744 at concentrations of 2 μM and 5 μM for our further experiments. In addition, consistent findings were reached in long-term plate clonogenic cell survival assay that ABBV-744 significantly inhibited the formation of AGS and HGC-27 cell colonies after treatment (Fig. 1C), suggesting that ABBV-744 induced a long-term inhibition of GC cell growth. Also, we monitored the changes of cell morphology by light microscopy after ABBV-744 treatment for 48h. Additionally, to further evaluate whether ABBV-744 could induce apoptosis in GC cells, we performed flow cytometry assays. The results by Annexin V/Propidium iodide double staining showed that the percentage of apoptotic cells was significantly increased in a dose-dependent manner in GC cells treated with ABBV-744 compared to the untreated control (Fig. 1D). In addition, western blotting showed that the expression of cleaved caspase 3, cleaved PARP, Bax and cytochrome C were upregulated after ABBV-744 treatment, while Bcl-2 and Bcl-xl were down-regulated (Fig. 1E). Simultaneously, consistent with the above results, the immunofluorescence staining demonstrated a significant up-regulation of cleaved caspase 3 expression in gastric cancer cells (AGS and HGC-27) treated with ABBV-744 (Fig. 1F & 1G). Taken together, these data indicate that ABBV-744 inhibits the viability of human gastric cancer cells and promotes gastric cancer cell apoptosis in a concentration-dependent manner.

ABBV-744 induces cellular ROS accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction

In order to verify whether ABBV-744 can damage mitochondrial homeostasis in gastric cancer cells, we measured the effect of ABBV-744 on mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) in AGS and HGC-27 cells using JC-1 fluorescent probe. The proportion of mitochondrial depolarization was measured according to the relative proportion of red-green fluorescence. We incubated the fluorescent probe JC-1 with the treated gastric cancer cells and observed the changes of MMP through flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 2A & 2B) and confocal immunofluorescence (Fig. 2C), respectively. The consistent results indicated that the MMP of the cells treated with ABBV-744 was decreased, as compared with the control group. With the increasing concentration, the ratio of JC-1 monomer (green fluorescence) was gradually increased (Fig. 2C). The above data showed that treatment with different concentrations of ABBV-744 could reduce MMP and disrupt mitochondrial homeostasis. In addition, the release of cytochrome C from mitochondria into the cytoplasm was enhanced after ABBV-744 stimulation, which was confirmed by the western blotting results (Fig. 1E). MMP is maintained by mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, then we detected the ATP changes. Subsequently, we verified whether DNA damage occurred in gastric cancer cells upon ABBV-744 treatment. Then we analyzed the expression of γ-H2AX (a biomarker of DNA breakage and a precondition for DNA damage recognition) by western blotting, demonstrating that γ-H2AX levels were upregulated after exposure to ABBV-744 (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that the intensity of γ-H2AX positive cells increased significantly with increasing concentrations of ABBV-744 (Fig. 4F). To confirm these results, we also measured changes in DSB in GC cells by single-cell electrophoresis (comet assay), which is a very sensitive method for detecting DNA breaks. The results showed that the length of the comet tail in the treatment group was significantly longer than that in the control group, thus indicating the occurrence of DNA damage (Fig. S2). We also measured the expression of other markers associated with DNA damage by western blotting. The results showed that the expression of DNA-PKS and phosphorylated forms of ATM and ATR increased after ABBV-744 exposure (Fig. 4G). In conclusion, these data suggest that ABBV-744 treatment can lead to a DNA damage response and G1-phase cell cycle arrest in gastric cancer cells, and ultimately inhibits GC cell proliferation.

The role of autophagy in ABBV-744-induced cell death in gastric cancer

To further elucidate the molecular mechanism of the anticancer effect of ABBV-744 in gastric cancer cells, we used the pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK (Z-VAD) to validate the mechanism of cell death. The results showed that the inhibited cell viability induced by ABBV-744 could be partially reversed by Z-VAD-FMK (Fig. S3), implying that there may be other mechanisms involved. To verify whether other molecular mechanisms participate in the cell death process, we then performed transcriptomic RNA sequencing in AGS and HGC-27 cells after ABBV-744 and DMSO treatment. Based on the sequencing results, we identified 5386 up-regulated genes and 5775 down-regulated genes in the ABBV-744 treated gastric cancer cells based on a false discovery rate of 0.05 (Fig. 5A & 5B). KEGG enrichment analysis also indicated that autophagy pathway was highly enriched upon ABBV-744 treatment (Fig. 5C). Moreover, many of the enriched pathways listed through KEGG were associated with autophagy, including PI3K-Akt, MAPK, lysosomal, FoxO pathways, phagosome pathways, and mTOR signaling pathways (Fig. 5C). Considering that LC3B plays a vital role in the formation of autophagosomes and that p62 serves as a degradation substrate for autophagy, we determined the expression levels of these autophagy-related marker proteins in GC cells after treatment with ABBV-744 by western blotting. The results showed that ABBV-744 enhanced the LC3B levels and down-regulated P62 levels (Fig. 5D). Then in order to observe the changes of autophagic flow more clearly and intuitively, we observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging that the number of autophagic vesicles with vacuolated structures was significantly increased in ABBV-744 treated cells compared to the control group (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, to provide additional evidence that ABBV-744 regulates autophagy, we utilized two autophagy inhibitors, 3- methyladenine (3-MA) and Chloroquine (CQ). Western blotting results showed that ABBV-744-induced changes of LC3-II and P62 levels were partially reversed by 3-MA treatment (Fig. 5F). In contrast, CQ increased the protein levels of LC3-II and P62 induced by ABBV-744 (Fig. 5F), which was also verified by immunofluorescence staining in terms of LC3-II (Fig. 6A & 6B). These results indicated that ABBV could induce autophagy in gastric cancer cells, and the autophagic flow was relatively unobstructed. Therefore, our data indicate that ABBV-744 may be a new autophagy inducer for gastric cancer cells. ABBV-744 induces autophagy by regulating the PI3K/Akt/mTOR and p38/ERK/JNK MAPK signaling pathways

The RNA sequencing results provided definitive evidence for the enrichment of specific signaling pathways. Next, to further elucidate the potential mechanism of ABBV-744-induced autophagy, we analyzed the PI3K/AKT/mTOR/p70s6k/S6 signaling pathway [29,30] and MAPK signaling pathway [31], both of which have been reported to be related to autophagy.

As shown by the western blotting in Fig. 7E, the phosphorylation levels of Akt and mTOR as well as PI3K were significantly reduced in a concentration-dependent manner after treatment with ABBV-744 in gastric cancer cells, while the expression of the total proteins was not affected. These data suggest that ABBV-744 initiates the occurrence of autophagy by inhibiting the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway. Moreover, the phosphorylation of mTOR and its downstream proteins, including p70s6k, S6 and 4EBP1 expression levels were also reduced, suggesting that this signaling pathway was suppressed by ABBV-744. We also validated the MAPK signaling pathway and found that ABBV-744 significantly enhanced the phosphorylation of extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) and p38 in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 7F), instead of affecting the expression levels of total proteins in AGS and HGC-27 cells when compared with controls. Taken together, the above data indicate that exposure to ABBV-744 significantly inhibits the mTOR/p70s6k/S6 signaling pathway to initiate the autophagic flux, and activates the p38/ERK/JNK signaling pathway to induce autophagic cell death.

ABBV-744 inhibits the proliferation of gastric cancer organoids in vitro

Previously we have established gastric cancer organoid model banks derived from patients using surgically resected gastric cancer tissues. To validate the anticancer effects of ABBV-744 in clinically relevant models, we selected three organoids with different Lauren classification (GC14 for intestinal type, GC04 for mixed type and GC11 for diffuse type) for further experiments. The organoids were embedded in growth factor reducing (GFR) matrix gel and cultured in complete medium containing different concentrations of ABBV-744 for 14 days. Notably, bright-field images showed that ABBV-744 treatment significantly reduced the number and size of gastric cancer organoids. Cell viability was significantly decreased in a dose-dependent and time-dependent manner as assessed by Cell Titer-Glo 3D reagent. ABBV-744 effectively inhibits the proliferation of gastric cancer in vivo

To further validate our above experimental results in vivo, we successfully constructed a subcutaneous tumor model in nude mice by injecting AGS cells subcutaneously. Then the nude mice were randomly divided into four groups, the control group, the ABBV-744 group, the CQ group, the ABBV-744 and CQ combination group. After 21 days administration of ABBV-744 and CQ, the mice were euthanized and subcutaneous tumor tissues were collected. Compared with the control group, the tumor volume in the ABBV-744 and CQ groups were smaller, while the volume of the ABBV-744 and CQ combination group was even smaller than any other group (Fig. 9A–9C). The measured weight of the subcutaneous tumor was consistent with the changes in tumor volumes (Fig. 9D). To assess the toxicity of ABBV-744 in vivo, we measured the body weight of the mice all through the administration, and found that there was no significant change in body weight between the four groups (Fig. 9E). Besides, no obvious changes were observed in blood routine test, liver function, and kidney function (Fig. 9F). Moreover, the heart, liver, kidney and lung tissues of the mice were obtained respectively. HE staining was subsequently performed and we didn't observe any significant pathological changes after drug treatment (Fig. 9G), suggesting that ABBV-744 was relatively safe in vivo. Additionally, the IHC staining demonstrated a significant decrease in the protein levels of Ki67 in the ABBV-744 treated group and combination group compared with the control group (Fig. 9H). Furthermore, western blotting and IHC staining revealed that the LC3B expression was upregulated in the ABBV-744 group compared to the control group, and significantly even higher in the ABBV-744 and CQ combination group (Fig. 9H & 9I). Discussion

Many studies have been carried out to solve the problems caused by gastric cancer in human health [32]. At present, there are various methods for anti-tumors, and it has been confirmed that by inhibiting the proliferation of tumor cells and/or inducing apoptosis. may desirable to inhibit tumor growth. As a highly selective BET inhibitor of BD2, ABBV-744 has been shown to exert antitumor effects in leukemia [18] and prostate cancer [19]. However, its role in gastric cancer is still blank.

It is well known that mitochondria, as the energy stations of cells, is able to coordinate most of the metabolism and various physiological processes, and their functional integrity is of great significance to the proliferation and growth of cancer cells [33,34]. The function of mitochondria is related to the proliferation and apoptosis of tumor cells through some specific mechanisms. JC-1 probe was used to detect the mitochondrial function on gastric cancer cells. The results were evaluated by the changes of membrane potential and ATP. Once mitochondrial dysfunction occurs, a large amount of reactive oxygen species leakage arises from the electron transport chain, leading to increased ROS release, and the excess ROS release can also activate oxidative stress, which is a common mechanism for chemotherapeutic drug-induced cancer cell death.

We also know that oxidative stress and apoptosis processes are usually accompanied by organelle damage, which requires scavenger-like processes such as autophagy to clear them. To further explore the potential molecular mechanism of ABBV-744 anti-tumor effects, we performed RNA-seq for enrichment analysis.

Autophagy is a highly conservative process in eukaryotic cells and plays an important role in the occurrence and development of tumors [35]. The PI3K/Akt and MAPK signaling pathways play a key role in the development of many human cancers, which considered to be the main autophagy regulatory pathways and have been commonly studied [36,37]. Previous studies have shown that mTOR activation is regulated by a cascade of PI3K/Akt and MAPK subfamily responses, which are closely associated with growth factors, nutrients, energy signaling and so on. In mammalian cells, the p70 ribosomal S6 protein kinase (p70S6K) is located downstream of mTOR and is a major effector of phosphorylated mTOR and is involved in the regulation of autophagy.

Mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) is a serine/threonine protein kinase that responds to many extracellular stresses. The MAPK signaling pathway plays a key role in a variety of biological processes including cell proliferation, differentiation, and cell death. Due to the important role of the MAPK pathway in cells, its dysregulation often leads to disease, which associated with the development and progression of several cancers, but the role of PI3K/Akt and MAPK in ABBV-744-induced gastric cancer cells is not yet fully defined.In our study, we confirmed that ABBV-744 could induce autophagy by activating the MAPK pathway and inhibiting the PI3K-Akt-mTOR pathway. With the combination of autophagy inhibitor CQ, the autophagy-related protein LC3 was significantly increased and the cell viability was significantly decreased, suggesting that autophagy is involved and plays a protective role.

In this study, we used a variety of experimental methods to detect the effect of ABBV-744 on gastric cancer, we found that ABBV-744 inhibited the growth of gastric cancer cell lines. Moreover, we confirmed that ABBV-744 decreased the weight and volume in vivo model. In addition, we obtained the same conclusion that ABBV-744 has a significant inhibitory effect on the gastric cancer organoid model. However, there are still shortcomings in this study, for it failed to clearly clarify the mechanism and relationship between autophagy and apoptosis, which needs to be further studied. but we present for the first time a potential role of ABBV-744 in gastric cancer treatment, providing a possible promising therapeutic approach.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cell culture conditions

Human gastric cancer cell lines, AGS and HGC-27, were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). According to ATCC's instructions, cells were cultured in the suggested medium. Both kinds of cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Bio-Channel), which supplemented with 10 % FBS (fetal bovine serum, Bio-Channel) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin mixture. All cell lines were maintained in a cell culture incubator (Thermo Fisher) at 37°C, so that cells were incubated in a humid air environment containing 5 % CO2 at all times. The number of cell lines used in this study was kept within 10 generations of each other. After the cell abundance reached about 80 % confluence, they were used for experiments.

Reagents

ABBV-744 (HY-112090) was purchased from MCE(MedChemExpress), dissolved in DMSO until the final concentration was 10μM, and stored -80°C, then kept at 4°C before being diluted to different concentrations with medium. Other drugs used in the experiments were also purchased from Medchem Express (MCE, United States), as follows: N-acetylcysteine (NAC) (HY-B0215), Chloroquine (CQ) (HY-17589), 3-Methyladenine (3MA) (HY-19312), Bafilomycin A1 (Baf-A1) (HY-100558), SP600125 (HY-12041) and Rapamycin (HY-10219). Then dissolve those to appropriate concentrations according to the corresponding instructions. DMSO was used as a control vehicle.

Cellular proliferation assay

The gastric cancer cell lines AGS and HGC-27 cells were inoculated on 96-well plates at a density of 3×103 cells/well and cultured in 100μL 1640 medium containing 10 %FBS. After the cells overnight growth, they were treated with different concentrations of ABBV-744 or other drugs. Then, the cells were incubated with 100μl culture medium containing 10μl CCK-8 at 37℃ for about 1 h at the specified time (24 h, 48 h, 72 h, etc), and cell counting kit -8 (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions. The cell viability of each group was calculated by measuring and recording the absorbance at 450 nm by a microplate reader (SparkTM10M, tecan, shanghai).

Clonogenic cell survival assay

AGS and HGC-27 cells were digested with trypsin into cell suspensions and inoculated in 6-well plates at a density of 1000 cells/well. The cells were then treated with different concentrations of ABBV-744. After incubation in an incubator (Thermo Fisher, United States) for approximately 2 weeks, the cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA), stained with 0.5 % crystal violet (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) prior to observation, and then photographed under EVOS M7000.The experiment was repeated at least three times. Colonies were counted using Image J and then normalized to the number of colonies in the control group.

Cell morphology observation

AGS and HGC-27 cells were plated at 15 × 104 per well in 6-well plates and grown overnight, then treated with different concentrations of ABBV-744. DMSO was used as a control vehicle. Cell morphological changes were then observed under a microscope (Leica, Germany) and photographed.

Apoptosis evaluation

AGS and HGC-27 cells were plated into 6-well plates and grown overnight before adding different concentrations of ABBV-744. Cells were collected at specified times and washed with PBS, following the manufacturer's instructions for the Annexin V-fluorescein Isothiocyanate (FITC)/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Finally, the cells in each group were analyzed by flow cytometry, and the apoptosis rates of cells in each group were analyzed by Flow Jo software.

Cell cycle assay

AGS and HGC-27 cells were added to 6-well plates, and exposed to varying concentrations of ABBV-744 after overnight growth. Cells were collected at specific time, washed with cold PBS, and fixed overnight at 4℃ with 70 % iced ethanol. They were then incubated for 30 min in the dark with propidium iodide (PI) at room temperature the following day, following the manufacturer's instructions for cell cycle kits (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Finally, the distribution of cell cycles in each group was measured by flow cytometry, and the data were analyzed by Flow Jo software. All experiments were repeated at least three times.

Measurement of intracellular ROS production

AGS and HGC-27 cells were plated into 6-well plates and after overnight growth, different concentrations of ABBV-744 were added to the plates. Following the manufacturer's reactive oxygen species assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) instructions, the cells were harvested at indicated times and stained with the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA at 37°C for 30 min. Then the ROS levels in each group were measured by flow cytometry. And analyze the data using Flow Jo software. All experiments were repeated at least three times.

Evaluation of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP)

AGS and HGC-27 cells were implanted into 6-well plates and cultured overnight, and the cells were treated with different concentrations of ABBV-744. The mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was used according to the manufacturer's manual instructions. The collected cells were washed with PBS, incubated with JC-1 at 37°C for 30 min and then washed twice with JC-1 buffer (1×). Then analyzed for MMP changes in cells of each group by flow cytometry.

Immunofluorescence analysis

AGS and HGC-27 cells were inoculated on slides in 24-well plates at a density of 3×10 4 cells/well, incubated overnight, then cells were treated with ABBV-744 for subsequent processing within the specified time periods. The cells were fixed at room temperature with 4 %PFA for approximately 15 min. Cells were then infiltrated with 0.5 %Triton X-100 for 20 minutes and washed with PBS. Next, after blocking with 5 % BSA (bovine serum albumin) for 1 h at room temperature, the cells were cultivated overnight at 4℃ with the corresponding primary antibodies, LC3B(1:2000, cell signaling technology,#3868), Cleaved Caspase-3 (1:500, cell signaling technology, #9664). The next day, the cells were washed three times for 5 min in PBST. The cells were then incubated with the secondary antibody goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor ® 488 (1:500, Abcam, ab150077) at room temperature for 1h in the dark.The cells were then washed in PBS, incubated with DAPI (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 20 min and stored in darkness at 4℃. Finally, we observed and photographed the images with a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany).

Comet assay

Cells were cultured overnight before treating with DMSO or ABBV-744 for 48 h. Perform single-cell gel electrophoresis under alkaline conditions according to the manufacturer's instructions (KeyGEN BioTECH). Comet images were captured with a fluorescence inverted microscope (Olympus, BX43, Japan).

Measurement of intracellular ATP levels

To measure intracellular ATP levels, cells were seeded in 6-well plates (15 × 104 cells per well) and then treated with different concentrations of ABBV-744.The collected cells were measured for intracellular ATP levels according to the instructions in the manufacturer's ATP assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

RNA sequencing

Gastric cancer cells were incubated overnight in 6-well plates treated with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) or ABBV-744, and the cells were collected and treated as before.

Protein extraction and western blotting analysis

AGS and HGC-27 cells were collected and lysed on ice for 30 min with RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) which containing a mixture of protease and phosphate inhibitor. After centrifugation at 12,000 X g for 20 min at 4℃, that supernatant was collected. Then, we used the BCA Assay (Beyotime, China) method to determine the protein quantity and further lysed with 5x loading buffer. The collected protein lysates were separated by 8-15 % SDS-PAGE gel using an electrophoresis apparatus (Bio-Rad, USA) at a constant voltage of 90 V. The protein was then transferred to PVDF (polyvinylidene fluoride) membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Non-specific binding was locked at room temperature for 2 h with TBST containing 5 % skimmed milk. The membranes were then incubated overnight with the corresponding primary antibodies at 4℃ as described. On the next day, the membranes were washed three times with TBST and incubated for 2 h at room temperature with the appropriate secondary antibodies. Finally, after washing again with TBST, the signals generated by the western blots were visualized using a chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon, Shanghai, China). All experiments were repeated at least three times. Details of all antibodies are listed follows.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM)

TEM images can visualize the formation of autophagosomes, so we used TEM to determine the occurrence of autophagy. We collected ABBV-744-treated gastric cancer cells and followed the manufacturer's instructions. The ultrastructure of autophagy in different treatment groups was observed by TEM (Hitachi, H-7650, Japan). As shown, a large number of autophagosome vacuoles accumulated in the cells after treatment with ABBV-744 compared to the untreated group. Therefore, this study suggests that ABBV-744 can induce the formation of autophagosomes in human gastric cancer cells.

Autophagy flux determination

AGS and HGC-27 cells were inoculated on climbing plates in 24-well plates at a density of 3×10 4 cells/well, cultured overnight, and then treated with ABBV-744 to transfect adenovirus mRFP-GFP-LC3 (Hanheng Biology, Shanghai, China) into gastric cancer cells within the specified time according to the instructions of the manufacturer. Then stained with DAPI (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 15 min at room temperature. Finally, the images were observed and taken by fluorescence microscopy (Leica, Germany).

RNA extraction, reverse transcription and quantitative real-time PCR

AGS and HGC-27 cells were plated into 6-well plates at a density of 15×104 cells/well. The total RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer's instructions using the RNAiso plus reagent (Takara, Dalian, China).We used PrimeScript RT Reagent (TaKaRa, Japan) to reverse transcribe RNA to cDNA. Following the manufacturer's instructions, the PCR cycle conditions were 40 cycle controls of 10s at 95℃ and 30s at 60℃. The relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. We repeated all reactions three times independently to ensure that all data were repeatable. GAPDH was used as an internal control for RNA. Human gastric cancer organoid model

On the basis of the previous article [38], we established human gastric tissue model to simulate the microenvironment of the human body. To investigate the effect on GC, diluted concentrations of ABBV-744 were added to the organoid model. Every 4 days, we replace fresh medium. Finally, we observed the growth of the human gastric cancer organoid model daily using a microscope.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three times independently. Statistical analysis was carried out using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA,USA). Differences between the two groups were analyzed by Student t-test. Differences in three or more groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. Results are presented with mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), unless otherwise noted. P<0.05 was considered as a statically significant difference.
