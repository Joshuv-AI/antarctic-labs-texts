DDR1 drives cervical cancer progression and immune evasion: a bioinformatics analysis with experimental verification
Authors: Yunting Zhou, Xiuting Guo, Jingyi Han, Wenming Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer remains a major threat to women’s health worldwide. Discoidin domain receptor 1 (DDR1) drives immune evasion in a variety of cancers, but its expression pattern, clinical significance, and immunoregulatory mechanisms in cervical cancer have not been clarified.MethodsDDR1 expression profiles were resolved based on TCGA and GEPIA2 databases; DDR1-related pathways were enriched by GO/KEGG/GSEA; immunohistochemistry was performed in 40 cases of cervical cancer and 20 cases of normal tissues to assess the association of the DDR1 protein with clinicopathological features and survival prognosis for clinical validation. In addition, the biological role of DDR1 in cervical cancer was detected by Western blot, CCK8 and transwell.ResultsDDR1 was significantly overexpressed in cervical cancer and correlated with advanced FIGO stage and poor overall survival; DDR1 can promote the proliferation and migration of cervical cancer cells, and at the same time affect immune escape by reshaping the tumor microenvironment and metabolic reprogramming.ConclusionDDR1 is able to remodel the immunosuppressive microenvironment. Targeting DDR1 may overcome immune escape and provide a new therapeutic strategy for cervical cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15099-4.Keywords: DDR1, Cervical cancer, Tumor microenvironment, Immunoevasion, Prognosis

Introduction

Globally, cervical cancer (CC) represents a prevalent female reproductive system cancer with significant public health implications [1]. The widespread availability of human papillomavirus (HPV) screening technology in the past has allowed some cases of cervical cancer to be detected at an early stage and treated accordingly, which has improved the survival rate of early-stage patients to some extent [2]. However, in recent years, epidemiology data have shown that survival rates for cervical cancer have not shown significant improvements [1]. Currently, the clinical management of early-stage CC includes surgery and radiotherapy [3]. However, patients with late disease stages and those with recurrent or metastatic cervical malignancies continue to experience poor survival outcomes due to limited therapeutic efficacy. This challenge has led to the investigation of immune checkpoint inhibitors and adoptive cell therapies as potential therapeutic breakthroughs [4]. It is a rapidly progressing field aimed at modifying and recruiting the immune system to target cancer cells more effectively and specifically, offering hope for improved cervical cancer treatment and survival. This is evidenced by the approval of PD-1 blocking antibodies for recurrent or metastatic disease [5]. Although immunotherapy has made some breakthroughs, there are still some patients with cervical adenocarcinoma who have a high PD-L1-negative rate and thus are not sensitive to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors [6]. High cost limiting accessibility and complex mechanisms of resistance all suggest that there are limitations of the existing therapeutic tools in controlling the disease progression and decreasing the morbidity and mortality. Furthermore, the physical barriers of the tumor microenvironment (TME) —notably the tightly packed collagen fibers in the extracellular matrix (ECM) —can impede the infiltration of cytotoxic T cells (CD8 + TILs) into the tumor core, leading to immune escape and treatment resistance. Therefore, in-depth investigation of the molecular mechanisms of CC development, analyzing the regulatory mechanisms of the ECM barrier, and developing targeting strategies are of great clinical significance for improving the prognosis and reducing the mortality rate of cervical cancer patients.

Discoidin domain receptor 1 (DDR1) is a special type of receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) that was first discovered by Johnson and others in 1993 while studying proteins in breast cancer cells [7]. The receptor is defined by three segregated domains: an extracellular ligand-binding module, a membrane-spanning helix, and an intracellular tyrosine kinase catalytic core. It can be activated by microenvironmental collagen (Col) or its fragments and has attracted much attention in oncology research. DDR1 exerts pleiotropic effects in oncogenesis by reprogramming metabolic pathways during matrix remodeling events, modulating ECM topology, and reshaping TME cellular landscapes—mechanisms that collectively foster therapeutic resistance and immune evasion. Numerous studies indicate that DDR1 expression is elevated in various tumors, such as hepatocellular carcinoma [8], colon cancer [9], head and neck cancer [10] and ovarian cancer [11]. Its main ligand is collagen, but matrix-metalloproteinase cleaved Col I (cCol I), which exhibits distinct biological functions from intact Collagen I (iCol I) in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [12]. cCol I activates DDR1-NF-κB-p62-NRF2 signaling to drive tumor progression, whereas iCol I induces DDR1 destabilization via proteasomal degradation, thereby suppressing PDAC expansion and dissemination [12]. In breast cancer models, the cleavage of DDR1 collagen-binding outer domain proteins can lead to the recombination of collagen fibrils in the tumor microenvironment, thereby driving immune evasion and promoting tumor progression [13]. Not only is DDR1 overexpressed in all subtypes of breast cancer, but DDR1 mutations correlate with adverse clinical outcomes specifically in hormone receptor-positive cohorts [14]. In gastric cancer, DDR1 mediates cytoskeletal reorganization through activation of the RhoA/ROCK1 signaling pathway, which in turn drives metastasis [15]. Although the immune evasion function of DDR1 has been elucidated in breast cancer, there are significant knowledge gaps regarding its role in cervical cancer: the location and clinical significance of DDR1 in CC lack validation, and its involvement in immunoregulatory mechanisms is unexplored.

To better understand whether DDR1 is involved in cervical carcinogenesis and progression, we used the TCGA database for mRNA level analysis of its possible involvement in biological processes. In addition, validation was performed in 40 clinical samples and cells. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed significantly elevated DDR1 protein levels in cervical carcinoma specimens compared to normal controls, and the DDR1 expression was connected to FIGO stage and histologic grading. Quantitative research in cells revealed that DDR1 protein levels were markedly decreased in cervical epithelial cells relative to malignant CC cells. These findings collectively position DDR1 as a promising molecular biomarker for prognostic stratification and immunotherapy response prediction in CC management.

Materials and methods

Data sources

DDR1 transcriptome data in normal and tumor tissues were obtained from GEPIA2 [16] (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#general) and TCGA (https://tcga-data.nci.nih.gov/tcga/). GEPIA2 integrates RNA-seq data from the TCGA tumor projects and the GTEx (Genotype-Tissue Expression) project as normal controls. Transcriptome RNA-seq data of 309 cases of cervical cancer (3 normal samples and 306 tumor samples) were obtained from the TCGA database level 3.

Analysis of immune cell infiltration and immune checkpoints

To evaluate immune infiltration patterns linked to DDR1 expression, computational analyses were conducted using R-based bioinformatics. The ESTIMATE algorithm quantifies the heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment through three different indicators: matrix score, immune score, and tumor purity. The CIBERSORT calculation was applied to estimate the Tumor-Infiltrating Immune Cells (TIC) abundance profile in all samples.

Differential expression gene (DEG) functional enrichment analysis

Differential expression analysis between cervical cancer and normal samples was performed using the DESeq2 package in R, with HTSeq-counts as input. The Benjamini-Hochberg method was applied to adjust p-values for multiple testing and control the false discovery rate (FDR). Genes with |log2FoldChange| >1 and an adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Based on the median expression level of DDR1, samples were stratified into DDR1-high and DDR1-low groups. Subsequently, a set of R software packages was used for functional annotation, including enrichment for visualization, org.hs.egg.db for gene ontology mapping, and ggplot2 for graphical representation. Gene ontology (GO) analysis systematically evaluated three functional domains, while the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment provided supplementary mechanistic insights. We completed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) with the “clusterProfiler” and “enrichplot” software tools.

Collection of clinical tissue specimens

All samples were obtained from paraffin-embedded tissue specimens that had been surgically resected or biopsy-preserved at the Department of Pathology, Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University, between June 2017 and September 2018. Only patients with complete clinical and pathological records were included in the study. Based on the predefined inclusion criteria, a total of 40 patients diagnosed with cervical cancer and 20 patients with histologically normal cervix (samples obtained from individuals undergoing benign hysterectomy) were selected. The average age of the participants was 50.23 ± 7.87 years, with an age range of 33 to 70 years. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) no prior chemotherapy treatment before surgery; (2) absence of comorbidities or secondary malignant tumors; (3) availability of complete follow-up data until August 2024; and (4) histopathological confirmation of cervical cancer in all selected cases.

Immunohistochemistry

Cervical cancer and normal tissue specimens were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 h, followed by standard dehydration, clearing, and paraffin embedding. Sections of 4 μm thickness were prepared from the paraffin-embedded blocks, mounted on slides, and baked at 60 °C, and then hydrate them with graded ethanol. For antigen retrieval, the slides were heated in a boiling sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 10 min and then allowed to cool naturally. Incubate with 3% H₂O₂ to inhibit endogenous peroxidase. After PBS washing, the sections were blocked with normal serum for 30 min, and then incubated overnight with rabbit anti-DDR1 antibody (1:3000, Huabio, HA500141) at 4 °C. The next day, the goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L/HRP antibody (1:500, abcam, ab6702) was incubated at 37 ° C for 20 min. Antigen-antibody complexes were visualized using a DAB substrate kit. The sections were then counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated through a graded alcohol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral gum. Immunohistochemical results were evaluated by two independent investigators who were unaware of the clinical data. Positive staining was interpreted as follows: we scored the intensity of cell staining as follows: no staining as 0, light yellow as 1, tan as 2, and brown as 3. The percentage of positive cells in the sum of cells was measured as shown below: less than 5% was 0, 5%−25% was 1, 26%−50% was 2, 51%−75% was 3, and more than 75% was 4. The final semi-quantitative result was obtained by multiplying the score for the intensity of cell staining and the percentage of positive cells to the total number of cells, with a product equal to or greater than 4 being considered positive.

Cells and cell culture

CC cell lines C33A, SiHa, CaSki, HeLa and culture medium were purchased from Procell (Wuhan). Normal cervical cell line H8 was purchased from ATCC, USA. Complete medium was prepared from fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin-streptomycin double antibody and basal medium. All cells were added to the corresponding complete medium and incubated in a cell culture incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

RNA interference and cell transfection

si-DDR1-s1(5’-GCAGCAGCAGTAGAGATGAGT-3’), si-DDR1-s2(GCAGTGATGGAGATGGGGCT) and si-NC (5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3’) were synthesized by genepharma (Shanghai, China). Transfection was conducted using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. At 48 h posttransfection, the transfection efficiencies in the knockdown groups were determined via western blotting.

CCK8 proliferation assay

The treated cells were seeded into 96-well plates and allowed to adhere for 2–4 h. Thereafter, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent, followed by incubation for 1–2 h. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured on day 1. Subsequent measurements were taken at 24-hour intervals on days 2, 3, 4, and 5.

Transwell migration assay

Transfected cells were harvested by trypsinization, centrifuged, and resuspended in serum-free medium. A total of 8.0 × 10⁴ cells in 200 µL of serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber of a transwell insert. The lower chamber was filled with 500 µL of complete medium containing 20%FBS. After incubation for 24–48 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere, the non-migrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were carefully removed with a cotton swab. The migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, washed gently with PBS, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min. After air-drying, the membranes were imaged under an inverted light microscope.

RT-qPCR

Sample total RNA was extracted by TRIzol reagent (Beyotime, R0016) according to the instructions, and after passing the test for concentration and purity, the reverse transcription reaction was carried out using the Nearshore Protein Reverse Transcription Kit (Novoprotein, E046-01 A). Subsequently, amplification was performed using NovoScript® Multiplex Probe One Step qRT-PCR 5×SuperMix (novoprotein, E395-01 A). The expression of target genes was normalized with GAPDH, and the relative expression levels of the genes were calculated according to the 2 −ΔΔCT method. Primer sequence: GAPDH Forward 5′-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3′ Reverse 5′-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3′ DDR1 Forward 5′-AAGGAGTTCTCCCGGAGCTA-3′ Reverse 5′-TGCCTGAGATCACCTCCTGA-3′.

Western blot (WB)

Proteins were extracted using RIPA (containing protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors) and then separated by 10% gradient gel electrophoresis. The proteins were then transferred onto PVDF membranes, sealed at room temperature with 5% skimmed milk for 1 h, and incubated with anti-GAPDH antibody (1:5000, Proteintech) and anti-DDR1 antibody (1:1000). Incubate at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the secondary antibody was incubated at room temperature for 1 h and detected using a sensitive ECL luminescent reagent (Meilunbio, MA0186).

Statistical analysis

We used R (4.3.2) and SPSS (27.0) software for all statistical analyses. Relationships between DDR1 expression and clinicopathologic parameters were examined using the χ2 test. The inter-group statistical comparison was conducted using the non-parametric Wilcoxon test. Pairwise associations are evaluated by the Spearman rank correlation coefficient (ρ). Scatter plots of correlations between clinicopathology and DDR1 expression and survival curves were analyzed and visualized using the R language. All experiments were performed at least in three times and p < 0.05 was regarded as significance (ns: no significance, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001). Images were processed using Adobe Illustrator collage (2024).

Results

DDR1 is significantly expressed in various tumors

The GEPIA2 database and TCGA database were analyzed for pan-cancer expression of DDR1; the results indicated that DDR1 has been observed in multiple carcinomas, including cervical cancer, colorectal adenocarcinoma, glioblastoma multiforme, and lower-grade glioma. In contrast, DDR1 mRNA level was lowly expressed in acute myeloid leukemia and cutaneous melanoma (Fig. 1A, B). Then, data obtained from the TCGA dataset compared the differential genes between cancerous and normal cervical tissues, and the results of volcano plots and heat maps showed that DDR1 mRNA was up-regulated in CC (Fig. 1C, D). Among the DEGs in CESC, 2669 upregulated genes were represented by red dots, while 1688 downregulated genes were indicated by blue dots.

The relationship between DDR1 mRNA expression and the tumor immune microenvironment

To interrogate DDR1-mediated immunomodulatory effects in cervical carcinoma, we utilized ESTIMATE to examine the disparities in stromal scores, immunological scores, and ESTIMATE scores between patients with high and low DDR1 expression levels. Comparative stratification based on DDR1 transcriptional levels revealed DDR1-low tumors exhibited significantly elevated immune landscape scores and composite ESTIMATE indices, accompanied by reduced tumor cellularity versus DDR1-high counterparts (Fig. 2A). The CIBERSORT technique was chosen to assess the proportion of TICs in CC. (Fig. 2B). The results suggested that there were more resting-state memory CD4 + T cells in the DDR1 high-expression group; conversely, tumors with low DDR1 exhibited enhanced cytotoxic CD8 + T lymphocyte infiltration (Fig. 2C, D). Subsequently, the analysis of DDR1-related immune checkpoint molecules was conducted. Notably, LAG3 expression was decreased and CD47 expression was elevated in the DDR1 high expression group. Enrichment analysis of DDR1 differential genes in GO, KEGG and GSEA

All cervical cancer samples were divided into two groups based on whether they had high or low levels of DDR1 expression, with a total of 518 different genes identified in these groups, including 198 that were increased and 320 that were decreased. Functional enrichment analysis using GO revealed that DDR1 was associated with processes such as keratinization of intermediate filamentous tissues and differentiation of epidermal cells; in the cellular components, the DDR1 differential proteins were mainly present in the Golgi lumen and keratinized envelope sites. Additionally, the analysis of molecular functions showed that DDR1 is involved in activities like hormone action, growth factor action, and serine-type peptidase action (Fig. 3A). KEGG analysis revealed the enrichment of pathways such as DNA adducts of chemical carcinogens, exogenous drug metabolism of cytochrome P450, and neuroactive ligand-receptor interactions (Fig. 3B). Further analysis was performed by GSEA. A total of 11 pathways were enriched to be altered. Highly expressed samples were enriched in pathways including KRAS signaling and mitosis (Fig. 3C), while lowly expressed samples were mainly enriched in graft rejection, DNA repair, interferon-gamma response, MYC target genes, and oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 3D).

DDR1 expression and clinical verification in 40 patients

We utilized cervical tissues from 40 patients with cervical cancer and 20 patients with normal cervixes to explore differences in DDR1 protein expression levels. Immunohistochemical results showed positive DDR1 protein expression in 32 patients with cervical cancer (80%) and 1 patient with a normal cervix (5%) (Fig. 4A); statistical associations between DDR1 protein levels and clinicopathological parameters were evaluated using Pearson’s chi-square test. As summarized in Table 1, DDR1 was significantly correlated with the advanced histopathological differentiation grade (p = 0.041) and the progression of FIGO stage (p = 0.025), but not significantly correlated with the age at diagnosis, deep matrix infiltration and lymph node metastasis status (all p > 0.05). Quantitative associations between IHC staining intensity and key clinicopathological parameters were systematically evaluated. The immunohistochemical staining score of DDR1 was significantly correlated with the stage, myometrial invasion, and histological grade of cervical cancer patients. The scatter plot only showed the meaningful parts (Fig. 4B). Survival analysis showed that the OS and PFS of DDR1 expression-positive patients were significantly shorter than those of DDR1 expression-negative patients, and the difference was statistically significant (p < 0.05, Fig. 4C).

DDR1 promotes the proliferation and migration ability of Hela cells

RT-qPCR and WB were applied to detect the expression of DDR1 in four types of CC cells, C33A, HeLa, CaSki, and SiHa, and normal cervical cells, H8. The findings indicated that the mRNA and protein expression levels of DDR1 were markedly increased in CC cells compared to normal cervical cells (Fig. 5A, B). Subsequently, DDR1 was successfully knocked down in the Hela cell line (Fig. 5C, D). The CCK-8 proliferation assay revealed that reduced DDR1 expression markedly inhibited the proliferation of HeLa cells (Fig. 5E). Meanwhile, the transwell assay evaluated the effect of DDR1 on the migration ability of cervical cancer cells (Fig. 5F, G). The research results indicated that DDR1 knockdown significantly inhibited the migration ability of cells. In addition, WB results showed that DDR1 expression was negatively associated with the immune checkpoint LAG3 but positively correlated with CD47 expression (Fig. 5H-J). These findings underscore a critical role for DDR1 in the progression of cervical cancer.

Discussion

In low- and middle-income countries, CC still accounts for high morbidity and mortality among women’s cancers [17]. Although cervical cancer screening programs have achieved significant results and immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy has shown clinical benefit in a few patients, the overall prognosis is still unsatisfactory [18]. The current treatment faces a triple bottleneck: the pathogenesis has not been fully elucidated, targeted therapeutic strategies are limited, and the recurrence and metastasis challenges are prominent. Abnormal remodeling of the extracellular matrix in the TME has been confirmed in recent years to be a central factor driving immune escape and therapeutic resistance [19, 20]. DDR1 is able to interact with Col, a major structural component of the ECM, hence influencing the migratory and invasive capabilities of tumor cells as well as the extent of T cell infiltration [21, 22]. However, the specific molecular mechanisms by which DDR1 affects cervical cancer progression remain unknown.

Recent reports have shown that DDR1 expression is elevated in several tumor tissues and promotes tumor progression. Pan-cancer analysis and existing studies have shown that DDR1 upregulation in ovarian cancer [11], non-small cell lung cancer [23], breast cancer [24], gastric cancer [15], pancreatic cancer [12] and other tumors. Mechanistically, DDR1 promotes cancer the advancement of cancer by affecting cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and TME [22]. It has been demonstrated that inhibition of DDR1 expression reduces bone metastasis in a lung cancer model [25], as well as proliferation, invasion and chemoresistance through DDR1 and its downstream signaling pathways [26]. The small-molecule inhibitor 7rh-benzamide significantly impedes xenograft growth in gastric cancer models [27]. According to our analysis of TCGA data, DDR1 expression was remarkably higher in CC compared to normal cervical tissues. However, a notable limitation of the TCGA-based analysis in this study on cervical cancer is only three normal cases were used as controls. Compared with hundreds of tumor samples, this may affect the reliability of differential expression analysis (such as DEGs), and it cannot effectively identify those genes that already have certain expression variations in normal tissues. Fortunately, our immunohistochemical staining and WB results confirmed its high expression in cervical cancer tissues and cells. DDR1, mainly considered a membrane-bound receptor that localizes in the cell membrane, but we found that it also exists in the cytoplasmic nucleus. This conclusion parallels prior research in the kidney, in which DDR1 migrated to the nucleus to generate pro-fibrotic chemicals [28]. A recent study also showed that DDR1 could localize to the membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus of normal and cancerous prostate epithelial cells [29]. By analyzing the relationship between DDR1 expression and various clinicopathological parameters of cervical cancer, we found that positive expression of DDR1 protein was associated with histological grading and FIGO stage of CC, and we also noted that positive expression of DDR1 was related to a shortened OS and PFS. Although the robust associations observed in our cohort are compelling, the relatively small sample size must be considered a limitation that could impact the generalizability of our results. Future large-scale investigations are therefore necessary to confirm the clinical significance of DDR1. In the present study, we analyzed the expression of DDR1 in normal cervical cells and cervical cancer cells at both the mRNA and protein levels utilizing RT-qPCR and WB. The results suggested that the expression of both was markedly elevated in CC cells compared with normal cervical cells. These suggest that the high expression of DDR1 has a certain relationship with the occurrence and development of CC. Crucially, the functional validation—where DDR1 knockdown suppressed proliferation and migration—offers compelling evidence that DDR1 is a functional mediator of tumor progression and immune evasion in cervical cancer.

The composition of immune cell infiltrates within TME critically regulates both oncogenesis and clinical outcomes [30]. Comparative analysis of immunophenotypes between DDR1-high and DDR1-low cohorts revealed increased proportions of quiescent CD4 + memory T cells in the DDR1-high group. Mechanistically, enhanced CD4 + central memory T cell quiescence coupled with reduced activated effector subsets correlates with unfavorable prognoses—a pattern concordant with gastric cancer studies demonstrating survival benefits from CD4 + memory T cell activation [31], and our experimental data demonstrate that elevated DDR1 expression may impair patient survival outcomes by modulating functional transformation of CD4 + memory T cell subsets in the TME. Distinct from conventional cytotoxic modalities (surgery, chemo/radiotherapy), immunotherapy leverages immune system modulation via targeted blockade of inhibitory checkpoints [32]. Dominant ICI targets include the PD-1/PD-L1 axis and CTLA-4, with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 agents now constituting first-line options for advanced CC [33, 34]. Expanding beyond classical checkpoints, emerging therapeutic targets such as CD47, TIM-3, and LAG-3 are undergoing clinical validation [35–38]. Scholars are conducting preclinical or clinical studies on inhibitors targeting these proteins [39–41]. We found that DDR1-overexpressing cervical cancers showed a unique pattern of immune checkpoint regulation: CD47 was elevated and LAG3 was decreased. The CD47-SIRPα axis is thought to be a tumor-phagocytosis checkpoint signaling, which transmits “don’t eat me” signals to macrophages and promotes cancer immune evasion, and we speculated that this process might also occur. We speculate that this process may also occur in cervical cancer [42].

To further explore the possible biological effects exerted by DDR1, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analysis on DEG of its high and low expression groups. The GO findings suggested that the high expression group may have abnormal keratinization and intermediate filament backbone reorganization, and may also promote migratory invasion by inhibiting metalloproteinases and thus affecting collagen degradation, accumulation [43, 44], and immune response [45, 46]. DDR1 facilitates immunological rejection in triple-negative breast cancer by aiding in the proper arrangement of collagen fibers and establishing of physical barriers [13]. Antibody knockdown or blockade of DDR1 effectively increases immune cell infiltration and triggers potent antitumor effects [47]. While KEGG analysis and GSEA results reveal that DDR1 might stimulate tumor development and drug resistance through mechanisms such as metabolic enzyme reprogramming, hormone regulation, and DNA damage response [21]. Studies have shown that high expression of DDR1 can simultaneously activate β-cell-related pathways and inhibit oxidative phosphorylation, a phenomenon that reveals its mediated metabolic reprogramming properties [48]. Vella et al. demonstrated that the silencing induction of DDR1 affects the speed of mitochondrial ATP production and the down-regulation of key proteins related to glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, significantly reducing total ATP production [49]. Emerging evidence further highlights the metabolic coupling between T cell activation states and glucose utilization and glycolysis [50], and glucose metabolism promotes the development and differentiation [51]. Thus, this metabolic remodeling not only provides tumor cells with biomolecules required for rapid proliferation, but the resulting microenvironmental alterations may also synergistically mediate immune escape by inhibiting T cells.

In summary, our research findings demonstrate that DDR1 facilitates cancer growth through its interactions with immune cells, immunological checkpoints, and metabolic pathways. However, this study still has some limitations. First, our analyzed data came from public databases; the model involved multiple metabolic pathways, and was not validated with clinical samples. In our future work, we will continue to investigate the reasons for the elevated expression in cervical cancer so as to gain a deeper understanding of the pathogenesis of cervical cancer and to provide promising biomarkers and new therapeutic strategies for the diagnosis and treatment of cervical cancer.
