Microencapsulated Alkane Wax Melting: Measured by 1H NMR Relaxometry and Diffusometry
Authors: Matthew C Young, Madison L Nelson, Matthew E Skuntz, Sarah L Codd, Ryan Anderson, Joseph D Seymour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPhase change materials (PCMs) are useful for energy storage. Large latent heats make n‐alkane waxes effective PCMs. Octadecane and eicosane were studied as both unencapsulated and microencapsulated with NMR relaxometry and diffusometry throughout melting. Each wax was stepped degree‐wise through its melting point. At each temperature point, NMR T2 relaxation, T1−T2 correlated relaxation, and PGStE measurements as a function of molecular migration time Δ were acquired. These measurements detected molecular‐level disordering due to microencapsulation. NMR relaxometry measurements on the solid waxes measured a major intermediate mobility domain in the microencapsulated waxes. This pseudo interface domain in the solid microencapsulated waxes is consistent with the disruption of long‐range order, increasing heterogeneity and extending the melting point range. NMR diffusometry measured diffusion coefficients D in the unencapsulated wax melts, matching values established in the literature. D as a function of 1/T was fit for Arrhenius activation energies Ea as a function of Δ for each stage of melting. D values are reported for each solid wax sample, which are not measurements of only molecular diffusion but are consistent with magnetization transport via spin diffusion.Keywords: 1H, alkane wax, diffusion, NMR, PCM, phase change materials, relaxation

Introduction and Background

Phase change materials (PCMs) are useful for thermal energy storage [1, 2, 3]. With large latent heats, n‐alkane waxes are effective PCMs [2]. Below the melting point, these waxes are solid. At temperatures above the melting point, these waxes melt, absorbing surrounding thermal energy [2]. When melted waxes freeze, they release energy to their surroundings [2]. These waxes are often encased in micron‐scale acrylic shells [1, 2]. The shell seals in liquid wax for reusability and can protect the wax from environmental contamination [2]. Some specific applications include heat regulation in automotive engines [2]. and batteries [3], capturing solar energy for cooking [4]. and improving thermal efficiency of buildings [2, 5].

The thermal properties of n‐alkane waxes are well studied [6, 7, 8], and NMR relaxometry has long been used to measure molecular dynamics in alkane phase transitions [9, 10]. The impact of microencapsulation has been studied with differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) [1, 11], but further understanding of the impact of microencapsulation on melting can improve design as PCMs. Changes in phase transition properties with molecular restriction due to the curvature in pores, the Gibbs–Thomson effect, are well‐established for liquid–solid phase transitions [12]. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) techniques based on cryoporometry have been developed using the unfrozen liquid signal to characterize pore size [13, 14, 15]. Recently, microencapsulated alkanes have been used as sensors to observe heat transfer in a PCM packed bed [16], T2 relaxation has been correlated to temperature in melting microencapsulated eicosane PCMs [17], and liquid alkane mixtures have been observed to exhibit different diffusion behaviors based on confinement [18]. These works demonstrate the utility in studying PCMs noninvasively using NMR as a complement to DSC studies.

To measure the impact of microencapsulation induced restriction on molecular motion in melting, temperature‐controlled NMR relaxometry and diffusometry are used to quantify rotational and translational dynamics of unencapsulated and microencapsulated n‐alkanes, n=18 octadecane and n=20 eicosane. The impact of microencapsulation on structural rearrangements, such as solid–solid (premelt) rotator phase transitions during melting, is elucidated. The data contribute unique molecular‐scale insight into DSC observations of the melting behavior of unencapsulated and microencapsulated alkane wax PCMs.

Theory

NMR Relaxometry

T1 and T2 relaxation processes induce NMR signal attenuation. In NMR, the magnetic moments of H atoms within a sample align with an applied magnetic field B0. The net macroscopic magnetization M of an ensemble of atoms aligns with and precesses around B0 at the Larmor frequency ω0=γB0, where γ is the H gyromagnetic ratio. Radio frequency (RF) pulses at ω0 excite spin energy level transitions that rotate M out of alignment with B0. The spin system dissipates this excess energy to its surroundings, returning M to thermal equilibrium, in a process termed T1 relaxation. The RF pulse synchronizes the precession frequency of H atoms in a coherence induced detectable transverse magnetization, which decays by dipole‐induced local magnetic field fluctuations dephasing the spin precession by T2 relaxation.

T1 and T2 relaxation reflect molecular dynamics of a system. Their rates depend on molecular rotational correlation times τc, which mediate dipolar interactions, and ω0 [19, 20]. The Bloembergen–Purcell–Pound theory of relaxation [21, 22]. provides a relation between T1 and T2 with atomic properties making up the second moment or variance of the frequency distribution M2 [23, 24], ω0, and τc [19, 20, 25].

Field cycling NMR applications probe changes to T1 as a function of ω0 to measure alkane molecular dynamics [10, 26, 27, 28, 29]. Alkanes have evolving τc during phase transitions, impacting T1 and T2 [9, 10]. A solid alkane wax is a rotationally restricted semicrystalline lattice with long τc, the motionally broadened NMR relaxation regime [10]. in which T1 is the order of 1 s and T2 is of order 10−4 s. Alkanes undergo solid–solid rotator transitions in the intermediate NMR relaxation regime near the onset of melting, with enhanced rotational mobility shortening τc. Here T1 minimizes and T2 slows as the ordered crystal lattice becomes more disordered [6, 7]. While the models of Equations (1) and (2) are too simple to capture the complex molecular dynamics in alkanes, which include collective translational and uncorrelated rotational motions oriented along various axes of the crystal domain and crystal interfacial structure near the melting point, it provides connection to the average molecular motion correlation time [30]. Liquid state melted alkanes are most mobile with short τc causing T1 and T2 convergence at the order of seconds, the motionally narrowed NMR relaxation regime [19, 20].

NMR Diffusometry

Pulsed gradient spin echo (PGSE) NMR measures molecular self‐diffusion in homogeneous liquids [20, 31]. The pulse sequence RF and magnetic gradient field pulses encode displacement of molecules containing H species, with a gradient pair separated by a displacement time Δ [31]. PGSE measures an effective diffusion coefficient D in saturated porous media [20, 32]. Repeated measurements over a range of Δ times reveal the system's diffusive behavior D(Δ) [20, 32, 33, 34]. Comparison with bulk fluid diffusion dynamics measures pore size and tortuosity α in two time‐limiting regimes [33]. In the limit t→∞,D(t)=⟨z2(t)⟩/2t approaches 0 for isolated pores and D0/α for interconnected pores [34], where D0 is the coefficient of free molecular self‐diffusion of the liquid. Pulsed gradient stimulated echo (PGStE) can be advantageous over PGSE when T2 is short and T1 is long [20, 35]. PGSE stores magnetization in the transverse plane during Δ, resulting in T2 relaxation limits on displacement time Δ. PGStE NMR stores magnetization longitudinally during Δ, where T1 controls signal relaxation, a useful capability for solid‐like materials, large molecule liquids or viscous fluids [20, 35]. where T1≫T2. PGStE NMR measures total molecular motion within liquid systems including coherent displacements over Δ due to velocity, while double PGStE (DPGStE) refocuses phase shifts due to coherent displacements to measure pure molecular self‐diffusion [36, 37]. The echo attenuation from a PGStE experiment is due to total motion in the system [20]. E(g)=exp(iγδg·vΔ−γ2g2δ2D(Δ−δ/3)), where E(g)=S(g,t)/S(0,t) is echo amplitude, δ is gradient pulse length and g is gradient amplitude. The iγδg·vΔ term is the mean phase shift due to velocity induced coherent motion. The γ2g2δ2D(Δ−δ/3) term represents the variance or second cumulant magnetization phase fluctuations caused by stochastic motion during time Δ [20]. DPGStE NMR combines back‐to‐back PGStE sequences, which refocuses constant bulk motion, producing an echo signal dependent only on random diffusive motions in the echo attenuation E(g)=exp(−γ2g2δ2D(Δ−δ/3)). The use of a stimulated echo in solid‐like systems, such as soft waxes and polymer melts, introduces signal attenuation due to longitudinal magnetization spin diffusion during storage period which is proportional to Δ [38]. This signal attenuation due to exchange of H magnetization between domains of differing dipolar coupling, for example, strong dipolar coupled short T2 crystalline and weaker dipolar coupled longer T2 amorphous domains, results in a larger measured D(Δ) than in the absence of spin diffusion.

Materials

Microencapsulated octadecane and eicosane from Microtek (Westminster, CO, USA) were studied. These are ∼3mm beads filled with 10‐ to 20‐ μm diameter particles containing octadecane C18H36 or eicosane C20H42. Reference 99% pure octadecane wax (Lot MKCM5285), with melting point range 299.15–302.15 K, and eicosane wax (Lot MKBJ7060V), with melting point range 308.15–310.15K, were acquired from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Methods

Experimental Setup

Each sample was loaded into a 5‐mm NMR tube and inserted into a 250‐MHz Bruker (Billerica, MA, USA) superconducting magnet paired to a Bruker Avance III spectrometer and custom 5‐mm high‐power RF probe with diff30 gradient coil. Each sample was temperature‐controlled using the Bruker VTU to step through a temperature range in increments of 1K. At each fixed temperature step, the sample equilibrated for 30min, a protocol established by observing FIDs over time at a range of temperatures, before relaxometry and diffusometry scans were run. Unencapsulated octadecane was measured each degree from 293 to 308K and microencapsulated octadecane from 288 to 308K. Unencapsulated and microencapsulated eicosane were measured each degree from 293 to 313K.

Relaxation Data Acquisition and Analysis

T2 relaxation measurements were made with a Carr–Purcell Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) pulse sequence [39, 40]. with 60,000 echoes spaced 24 μs apart ( τ=12μs), 1‐ μs dwell time for a 0.5‐MHz sweep width, 30‐s interexperiment delay, and 32 averages. RF pulses were 100 W and applied for 3.24–3.41 μs for 90° pulses and 6.48–6.82 μs for 180° pulses, dependent on the sample.

T1−T2 correlation measurements were made on all samples with a combined inversion recovery and CPMG pulse sequence [20, 27, 41]. The inversion recovery component was acquired over 32 pseudo log spaced steps between 0.001 and 50 s. The CPMG component was set up identically to the 1D T2 measurements.

NMR T2 and T1−T2 voltage signals were processed by a MATLAB (Natick, MA, USA) based Tikhonov‐regularized nonnegative least squares fit to a Fredholm integral of the first kind [41, 42, 43], often referred to in NMR literature as an inverse Laplace transform or ILT [44]. The ILT fits a probability distribution of relaxation rates with the integral of total probability normalized to unity. A regularization smoothing parameter was selected to minimize the residual without overfitting and generating pearling artifacts. Each signal decay was analyzed with this value to reduce smoothing bias. P(T2) distributions (Figure 2) and a subset of T1−T2 correlation distributions (Figure 3) are shown.

Diffusometry Data Acquisition and Analysis

PGStE measurements were made with a spoiler recovery delay of 5 s, 64 linearly ramped g steps to a maximum of 5–10 T/m, 2 ms δ, and 16 averages. Echoes were acquired with dwell times of 1 or 2 μs over 4096 or 8192 points, producing sweep widths between 0.25−0.5 MHz. Measurements were repeated at displacement observation times Δ=10, 50, 75, 100, 400, and 1000ms. RF pulses were 100 W for between 3.24 and 3.41 μs for 90° and 6.48–6.82 μs for 180°, depending on the sample.

The unencapsulated waxes were measured with DPGStE NMR because they exhibited convective PGStE behavior, while the microencapsulated waxes did not indicate convection over the measured temperature range. The extra pulses used in the DPGStE sequence lengthened the minimum Δ time to ∼12.5ms. Therefore, these scans were set up with identical parameters to PGStE experiments except the shortest Δ time measured was 13ms rather than 10ms.

Monoexponential fits to NMR diffusometry echo decays estimate diffusion coefficients D [20, 31]. Results and Discussion

NMR Relaxometry

It is well‐established by XRD data that n‐alkanes form lamellar structures of triclinic crystal domains which undergo rotator and defect transitions with changes in temperature near the melting point [48, 49]. In Figure 1 a cartoon of typical structures is shown, including regions of interfacial methylene −CH2− groups which have greater mobility than central −CH2− groups as demonstrated in molecular dynamics simulations where this interface extends two −CH2− groups from the highly mobile methyl −CH3 end groups [30]. Thus, in the discussion below, the T2 populations do not directly correlate to the number of −CH2− and −CH3 groups on a molecule.

Cartoon depicting the different H populations in (a) unencapsulated bulk in a crystalline phase, (b) unencapsulated bulk in a rotator phase, and (c) microencapsulated n‐alkane waxes. The highly rotationally mobile −CH3 end groups are yellow, the pseudo interfaces of −CH2− are red, and the restricted crystalline ordered lattice atoms are blue. Domains of oriented crystals exhibit greater long‐range order when unencapsulated. The steric hindrance of the microencapsulated wax prevents it from forming solid lattice crystalline domains, leading to larger yellow and red populations.

The intermediate population now makes up 79.9% of the signal and has increased in T2 to 53.0ms. At 308K, both of these populations persist at 41.6 and 424ms, accounting for 2.2% and 97.8% of the signal originating from wall restricted mobility and bulk liquid restricted mobility populations.

The primary peaks in the solid phase again reflect the −CH2− protons in the ordered crystalline domains and the most mobile −CH3 protons, with the −CH2− relaxation time nearly identical to the solid unencapsulated octadecane and the most mobile relaxation time reduced significantly from the smaller chain alkane. At 300K, the low and high mobility populations are at 0.368 and 376ms and account for 34.7% and 61.7% of the signal. The intermediate mobility populations at 7.67 and 42.6ms make up 0.4% and 3.3% of the signal. At 304K, the low and high mobility populations at 0.634 and 369ms and make up 12.7% and 93.1% of the signal. The intermediate populations have merged by 304K at 36.4ms and account for 4.2% of the signal. At 307K, the low mobility population disappears and the remaining intermediate and high mobility populations at 51.4 and 551ms make up 3.2% and 96.8% of the signal. In the initial liquefaction at 308K, the beginning of the melting range, the intermediate mobility population also disappears, leaving a sole highly mobile population of a pore liquid at 655ms which continues to increase in T2 to 720.ms by 313K due to the increase in rotational diffusion with T.

Microencapsulated eicosane at 293K (Figures 2d and S1d and Table S1) has a low mobility and high mobility population at 0.362 and 304ms, accounting for 44.9% and 36.1% of the signal, and two smaller populations at 7.74 and 47.7ms accounting for 5.2% and 14.5% of the signal. As in octadecane, eicosane microencapsulation does not impact the mobility of the most ordered crystalline region spins but does generate stronger dipolar coupling due to lower rotational mobility of the most mobile spins; with negligible change of the smallest T2 and significant decrease of the longest T2. At 300K, the low and high mobility populations increase in T2 to 0.438 and 351ms and account for 32.9% and 31.4% of the signal. The intermediate mobility populations merged by 299K and here at 300K make up 36.2% of the signal at 44.2ms. At 304K the low, intermediate, and high mobility populations at 0.584, 45.2, and 383ms make up 20.7%, 52.8%, and 27.0% of the signal. At 312K, the low mobility population disappears, leaving the intermediate and high mobility populations at 64.0 and 576ms, which make up 11.4% and 88.6% of the signal, representing the breakdown in molecular ordering of noncrystalline domains. The intermediate mobility population shrinks and shifts to shorter T2 of 51.1ms at 313K and makes up 5.62% of the signal, while the highly mobile population at 313K increases to 603ms T2 and makes up 94.4% of the signal. This behavior again reflects particle surface interactions between the alkane‐core and the shell and demonstrates general restriction relative to the unencapsulated wax.

To summarize, NMR T2 relaxation distributions in Figure 2 compare unencapsulated (a) octadecane and (c) eicosane with microencapsulated (b) octadecane and (d) eicosane across melting. Prior to melting, the unencapsulated waxes have one population below 1ms T2 and another above 100ms. The short T2 peak represents H along the alkane backbone in crystalline ordered domains, the intermediate peaks the more mobile protons in the interfacial domain −CH2− groups and the long T2 peak corresponds to the rotationally mobile −CH3 end groups (Figure 1). The magnetization in these distinct dipolar coupled domains undergoes exchange resulting in complex multiexponential signal decay [50]. Heating to the melting point breaks down the crystalline structure, seen through the restricted motion population shifting to longer T2. To this point, the mobile population remains steady. Above the melting point, the restricted peak decays away. As alkane chains are liberated from the lattice, the restricted peak shrinks while the mobile peak grows proportionally. Increasing disorder of the alkane chains allows interior and terminal H atoms to rotate more rapidly, shortening τc and increasing T2 (Equation (2)). Further heating beyond melting causes the mobile peak to display liquid‐like mobility, as T2 approaches 1 s. This single population also narrows as τc decreases across the system, characteristic of liquids and low molecular weight polymer melts.

The melting encapsulated wax trends parallel the unencapsulated waxes with an additional peak between 10 and 100 ms, representing a pseudo interface of H atoms on −CH2− groups near the rotationally mobile −CH3 end groups, with intermediate mobility. This is due to more disorder in microdomains. Microencapsulation sterically hinders the formation of large continuous crystalline domains, resulting in a substantial population of more mobile −CH2− spins and diminished restricted lattice peak. Unlike the unencapsulated waxes, the lattice population does not decay away during melting. As the lattice weakens, mobility of this population increases to that of the pseudo interfaces. For microencapsulated octadecane, the −CH2− pseudo interface peak grows and merges with the −CH3 end group peak. The pseudo interface for microencapsulated eicosane never reaches the mobility of the free ends, as these populations remain separate. Microencapsulation limits the liquid like mobility of the melted waxes, with an enhanced effect on longer chains [18]. Overall, the microencapsulated waxes melt over a broader range than when unencapsulated. Microencapsulation favors intermediate mobilities expanding the melting point range.

T1−T2 distributions (Figure 3) reflect the favorability of intermediate mobilities by microencapsulation. The −CH2− alkane backbone in the ordered crystal lattice is at T2<1ms and T1>1 s and the rotationally mobile −CH3 end groups are at T2>100ms and T1<1 s for the unencapsulated waxes. Even in the solid phase, the −CH3 end groups are liquid‐like with similar T1 and T2. Only the highly mobile population exists in the unencapsulated wax melt. The solid microencapsulated waxes have the same pseudo interface population as in the T2 distributions. The alkane backbone disappears in the microencapsulated wax melts, while the pseudo interface peak grows indicating the breakdown of the crystalline domains faster than when unencapsulated, due to less long‐range multidomain ordering. Above the melting point, the pseudo interface peak disappears, leaving a lone mobile peak at T2=480ms and T1=800ms, which is shorter T2 than the 800ms for the unencapsulated waxes. This result again demonstrates the propensity for more complex intermediate molecular mobilities throughout melting in microencapsulated alkanes.

Averaged T1/T2 provides another analysis of how microencapsulation limits high and low mobility populations (Figure 4). In the solid unencapsulated waxes, T1/T2 of the highly restricted −CH2− lattice population (blue) is about 105, while the high mobility −CH3 end groups (yellow) have a liquid‐like T1/T2 of ∼1. Eicosane shows the emergence of a highly mobile pseudo interface prior to melting. In the liquid state wax melts, all H spins exhibit liquid‐like T1/T2∼1 behavior. The red pseudo interface in the microencapsulated waxes has T1/T2 of ∼10 still indicating more amorphous, less ordered, solid‐like behavior. Prior to the melting point, the crystal lattice domain population increases mobility with decreasing molecular order increasing the proportion of amorphous −CH2− pseudo interfaces, which remain until above the melting point range. Emergence and persistence of a region of −CH2− spins in a less ordered pseudo interface indicates that microencapsulation decreases long‐range crystalline microdomain ordering in solid and melted alkanes, broadening the melting point range.

NMR Diffusometry

Diffusion coefficients D(Δ)/D0 plotted in Figure 5a show that unencapsulated wax in the liquid phase, at 2K above the unencapsulated wax melting point, have a slight Δ displacement time dependence with D∼2×10−10m2/s, consistent with values reported in Von Meerwall et al. [47]. In contrast, the microencapsulated liquid wax shows a classic decrease in D(Δ)/D0 with increasing Δ due to the restricted diffusion in the core shell particle [33, 34, 51]. Fits using Equation (4) give a particle size V/S∼3.4μm for microencapsulated eicosane and 3.1μm for microencapsulated octadecane, which for spherical pores scales as r=3V/S resulting in 10.1μm for the eicosane and 9.4μm for the octadecane. These values are in good agreement with the high end of the 5‐ to 10‐ μm manufacturer specified particle radius. At 3K below each respective unencapsulated wax's melting point (Figure 5b), the microencapsulated waxes again show the classical D(Δ)/D0 decrease of a liquid with a transition from the short displacement time, with Δ1/2 scaling behavior, to a constant value at large Δ [33, 34]. The unencapsulated wax diffusivity D(Δ)/D0, in the solid phase, decreases nearly linearly with increasing diffusion time Δ1/2. This observed behavior is consistent with diffusion of longitudinal magnetization during the magnetization storage time of the stimulated echo sequence, that is, spin diffusion [10, 50, 52].

An Arrhenius type plot of D for Δ=400ms is shown in Figure 5c (see Figure S2 for data from all Δ times). In the unencapsulated wax, the larger molecular weight eicosane has a broader 1/T ( K−1) transition region from solid to liquid than octadecane, indicative of the difference in activation energy (Figure 6) and temperature during melting from a more crystalline state (Figures 5c and S2). In the microencapsulated waxes, the liquid to solid transition is broader in 1/T than in bulk, consistent with increased melting ranges observed in DSC measurements of microencapsulated wax relative to bulk [53]. The smaller D of the encapsulated waxes in the liquid phase is due to the restriction of the core shell as indicated above. The lower diffusivity in the melting and solid regimes indicates the intermediate T2 relaxation time spin populations substantially impact the diffusion. Near the melting point, molecular mobility of the interfacial −CH2− groups is enhanced and extends further into the molecule, increasing the intermediate T2 component. This increases longitudinal magnetization transport between the crystalline and less ordered dipolar coupled domains during the PGStE magnetization storage time [30, 38, 50]. This is consistent with the solid phase spin diffusion measured scaling as D∼1/T2, with the intermediate larger T2 dominating the spin diffusion [54].

A novel aspect of the diffusion time dependent NMR data is exemplified in the activation energy calculated for the liquid, transition, and solid regimes (Figure 6) from linear fits on the Arrhenius plots (Figure S2). To our knowledge, no such data have previously been generated. Increasing Δ generates a longer diffusion length scale ld∼(DΔ)1/2. In the liquid state (Figure 6a), the unencapsulated waxes show near constant Ea in agreement with other published values [47], while the decrease of Ea in the microencapsulated systems is an artifact of the restricted translational motion when molecules diffuse longer distances. In the melt transition (Figure 6b), Ea increases with spin diffusion over longer distances due to spatial variations in the ordered crystalline and disordered amorphous mesodomains. In the solid phase (Figure 6c), Ea is fairly constant with varying diffusion lengths, indicating fixed crystalline and amorphous domain length scales. Increases in Ea near the melt transition reflect the complex molecular dynamics present, including collective translational motions which impart a time dependent dynamics [30]. Since Ea in classical theory is the energy barrier for kinetic energy to overcome intermolecular forces, the time dependence measured reflects the increased energy of multiple structural and orientational rearrangements in time Δ.

Conclusion

NMR relaxometry and diffusometry on unencapsulated and microencapsulated C18 and C20 n‐alkane waxes provide unique insight into molecular‐scale transport processes and structure during melting. NMR T2 and T1−T2 relaxation measurements are sensitive to structural modifications in the solid waxes, revealing a major intermediate relaxation domain exclusive to the microencapsulated waxes. These relaxometry data demonstrate how microencapsulation alters the range of molecular mobilities compared to unencapsulated waxes, favoring intermediate mobilities. These intermediate mobilities are related to more amorphous domains in the microencapsulated wax, relative to the unencapsulated wax. Diffusion data from PGStE on the microencapsulated waxes estimate radius ( S/V) in the liquid phase, informing hindered diffusion within particles. The diffusion data provide a new means to calculate activation energies from Arrhenius plots which depend on the diffusion displacement observation length scale. This novel result has potential to further characterize the impact of spatial variations of structural crystalline and amorphous domains on melting processes and latent heat.
