A protective human antibody against respiratory syncytial virus by targeting a prefusion epitope across sites IV and V of the viral fusion glycoprotein
Authors: Lianpan Dai, Jian Song, Lili Xu, Zhao Gao, Senyu Xu, Yan Chai, Liang Wang, Mi Yang, Tong Ma, Qihui Wang, Sushan Cao, Junming Yie, Gang Zou, Zhengde Xie, Jim Zhen Wu, George Fu Gao
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is one of the leading pathogens that cause lower respiratory tract infections in infants and the elderly. Passive immunoprophylaxis with monoclonal antibody (mAb) has been approved to prevent morbidity and mortality from RSV infection in infants. Here we report the isolation of two neutralizing mAbs against RSV from convalescent children by prefusion form of fusion (F) glycoprotein as bait. One mAb RV11 exhibited good potency in neutralization of RSV strains from both A and B subtypes in cell-based assay, and protected mice from RSV infection in vivo. An RV11 escape mutant was identified, which contains an S443P mutation in F protein. Crystal structure showed the RV11 bound to a conserved prefusion epitope across the antigenic sites IV and V of the F glycoprotein. RV11 showed a strong synergistic effect when combined with two RSV antivirals, an F-targeting small molecular inhibitor ziresovir and a site Ø neutralizing mAb D25 (the parental mAb for nirsevimab). The study extended our knowledge to the neutralizing and protective epitopes of RSV, and the mAb RV11 deserves further development for clinical translation.Keywords: Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) antibody, RSV monoclonal antibody, RSV fusion glycoprotein, RSV epitope, palivizumab, RSV prophylaxis

Introduction

Human respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), a member of Pneumoviridae, is a globally prevalent cause of lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) in all age groups. Human RSV has two major antigenic subtypes (A and B) circulating over the world [1]. RSV infection can cause severe illness, such as bronchiolitis and pneumonia, and contributes substantial morbidity and mortality in premature infants, infants less than six month of age, and children with chronic lung diseases, congenital heart diseases, or other underlying conditions considered as risk factors [[2], [3], [4]]. For the elderly beyond 65 years of age, RSV infection causes high number of hospitalizations and deaths annually, like influenza [5], with the huge burden of disease.

Passive administration of monoclonal antibody (mAb) has been approved for high-risk infants in prevention of serious LRTI diseases caused by RSV infection. Palivizumab (Synagis®) is an available humanized mAb against RSV infection and has been used prophylactically in premature infants or children with high-risk factors since 1998 [6]. However, limited efficacy, monthly dosing requirement, and high cost restricted its clinical application and wide usage. Development of new generation of RSV mAbs with higher potency, longer half-life, and single-dose administration to cover the whole RSV season is a global priority. A leading candidate is nirsevimab (formerly MEDI8897), derived from a human mAb D25, with affinity maturation to further increase the neutralizing activity and YTE mutation to extend the half-life [7,8]. A single dose of nirsevimab showed approximately 70% of efficacy in prevention of medically attended RSV-associated LRTI in preterm infants from two phase three clinical trials [9,10]. It was recently approved by European Medicines Agency (EMA) and US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the prevention of RSV LRTI disease in newborns and infants during their first RSV season [11]. Clesrovimab (formerly MK-1654), derived from the parental antibody RB1, is another candidate in late stage of clinical trials [5,12].

RSV has two major glycoproteins (G and F) mediating viral entry. G glycoprotein is a putative attachment protein and F glycoprotein is responsible for the fusion of virus with host cell membrane [13]. F protein is highly conserved among various RSV strains, therefore, is a favorable vaccine target. The prefusion F (pre-F) protein is metastable and prone to undergo a conformational transition to a more stable post-fusion F (post-F) structure [14]. Most of potent neutralizing antibodies bind to epitopes exclusively presented on pre-F [13,15]. Therefore, Kwong and others designed to stabilize the F protein in its prefusion conformation to elicit robust antibody responses [14]. This strategy has been widely used in current RSV vaccine and prophylaxis antibody development [5,16]. There are six well-characterized antigenic sites (Ø, I, II, III, IV, and V) [13,15] in F protein. Sites Ø (the binding site of nirsevimab [17]) and V display exclusively on pre-F, representing two major sites recognized by potent neutralizing antibodies, while sites I, II (the binding site of palivizumab [18,19]), III, and IV (the binding site of clesrovimab [12]) presented on both pre- and post-F conformations. A clinically efficacious RSV mAb should be of potent neutralizing activity targeting a conserved epitope. Previous studies showed that site IV is one of the most conserved regions in F protein across subtypes and strains, suggesting an attractive site for mAb against RSV [20,21].

Here, we describe the isolation of two neutralizing RSV mAbs (RV8 and RV11) from convalescent children using pre-F protein as the bait. One mAb RV11 bound exclusively to pre-F and neutralized RSV strains from both A and B subtypes and was 7-to-58-fold more potent in vitro than palivizumab. In addition, RV11 conferred mice protection against RSV infection in the lung, with 6.3-fold more potency in vivo than palivizumab. Furthermore, a mapping experiment for antibody-resistant mutation identified a serine-to-proline mutation at site 443 of F protein allowing virus escape of RV11 neutralization. The co–crystal structure of RV11 bound to pre-F protein revealed a novel but conserved epitope across sites IV and V in pre-F protein. Finally, RV11 showed strong synergistic effect when combined with F-targeting small molecule antiviral, ziresovir, and the site Ø mAb, D25.

Results

Isolation of RSV mAbs from Memory B Cells of Children Convalescents

We used the purified RSV pre-F glycoprotein, DS-Cav1 [14], as a bait to sort specific memory B cells from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of five children convalescents. The variable regions of both the heavy and light chains (VH and VL) were amplified from the singularized memory B cells and cloned into human IgG1 backbone. The mAbs were expressed and tested for binding and neutralization of RSV. We identified two mAbs, namely RV8 and RV11, for further characterization based on their viral neutralizing activity. The germline gene usage of RV8 and RV11 was analyzed and determined by the ImMunoGeneTics information system (Table 1). RV8 and RV11 use IGVH3-30∗01 and IGVH3-30∗03, respectively, in VH with somatic hypermutation rates of 2.8% and 4.6%, respectively. RV8 uses the VL of κ with germline gene of IGKV1-6∗01, while RV11 uses the VL of λ with germline gene of IGLV1-47∗01.

In vitro Binding and Neutralizing Activity of RSV mAbs

We tested the binding activity of antigen-binding fragment (Fab) of mAb (RV8 and RV11) to both the pre-F (DS-Cav1) and post-F (F glycoprotein with deletion of fusion peptide [22]) proteins via surface plasmon resonance (SPR). Fab of palivizumab was tested as a positive control. We found that, like palivizumab, RV8 bound to both pre- and post-F, with the binding affinity of 40 nM and 18 nM, respectively. In contrast, RV11 bound exclusively to the pre-F with an affinity of 8.4 nM (Figure 1A).

We next measured the neutralizing activity of these mAbs against various RSV strains. Both RV8 and RV11 neutralized both A (Long strain) and B (9320 strain) subtypes of RSV. Compared to palivizumab, RV8 showed a similar 50% effective concentration (EC50) (841.8 ng/mL versus 898.8 ng/mL) to neutralize RSV A, but a 7.6-fold higher activity (183.2 ng/mL versus 1400.0) to neutralize RSV B (Figure 1B and 1C). In contrast, RV11 showed much stronger neutralizing activity and equipotently neutralized both the RSV A and RSV B strains, with EC50 of 56.3 ng/mL and 32.2 ng/mL, respectively, which were 16.0- and 43.5-fold of potency increase compared to palivizumab.

RV11 was further evaluated for its neutralization activity to a panel of RSV clinical isolates, 10 from subtype A (including the Long strain as the reference) and 4 from subtype B (including the 9320 strain as the reference) (Table S2). Palivizumab was tested alongside as a control. We found that RV11 neutralized the RSV A and B strains with a mean EC50 of 25.1 ng/mL (16.0–31.7 ng/mL) (Figure 1D) and 49.6 ng/mL (24.7–66.7 ng/mL) (Figure 1E), respectively, demonstrating its broad anti-RSV capability. Compared to palivizumab, RV11 was 7.1- and 58.0-fold more potent in neutralizing these RSV A and B clinical strains (Figure 1D and 1E).

In vivo Protection Efficacy of mAb RV11 in Mice

Next, we performed a head-to-head comparison of RV11 and palivizumab to evaluate the prophylactic efficacy in a murine RSV viral challenge model. A cohort of 6-8-week-old female mice (n = 9 per group) were randomized into thirteen groups. They were administered intramuscularly with weight-based doses of either RV11 or palivizumab in a dose escalation manner from 0.03 to 8.00 mg/kg (Figure 2A). Phosphate buffer solution (PBS) was given as vehicle control. One day after antibody or PBS administration, mice were challenged intranasally with 1 x 105 plaque-forming unit (pfu) of RSV (A2 strain). Five days post viral challenge, mice were euthanized and the viral loads in the lung were measured. Mice in control group showed an average of 104.5 pfu RSV per gram of lung tissue. Both RV11 and palivizumab showed inhibition of lower respiratory infections by RSV in the lung in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 2A). A dose of more than 0.89 mg/kg of RV11 or 2.67 mg/kg of palivizumab completely prevented viral infection in mice. The EC90 value of RV11 was 1.5 μg/mL (serum level of mAb), which is 6.3-fold more efficacious than palivizumab (9.5 μg/mL) (Figure 2B). This demonstrated that RV11 is a potent neutralizing antibody to prevent RSV A2 infection in the lung in this viral challenge study.

Identification of RV11-escape Mutants

To investigate which mutants may lead to escape of RV11 neutralization, triplicates of RSV A (Long strain) were grown on HEp-2 cells in the presence of serial concentrations of mAb. After 5 rounds of blind passaging, RV11-escape mutants were selected under a final antibody pressure of 1.8 μg/mL from each triplicate (Figure 3A). The mutants were sequenced for the F gene. Notably, all three selected variants (namely Sel 1, 2, and 3) contained the same serine-to-proline mutation at residue 443 (S443P) of F glycoprotein (Figure 3B), suggesting that the region around S443 is critical for RV11 binding. We further tested the neutralizing activity of RV11 to these 3 variants. As expected, all Sel 1–3 showed escape of RV11 neutralization, with no detectable activity at the highest testing concentration of 10 μg/mL (Figure 3C).

Structural Analysis of RV11 Bound to RSV Glycoprotein

To explore the molecular basis of RV11 bound to RSV F glycoprotein, we expressed the pre-F glycoprotein (sc9-10 DS-Cav1 [22]) and complexed it with single-chain fragment variable (scFv) of RV11. The crystal structure of RV11 bound to pre-F was determined at a resolution of 2.9 Å (Table S1). Overall, three RV11 scFv engage around the waist of an RSV pre-F trimer across two adjacent F protomers (Figure 4A). RV11 recognizes epitope at the junction region of pre-F, with its VH contacting site IV residues from one protomer and its VL mainly contacting site V residues from the neighboring protomer (Figure 4B and 4C).

Regarding the binding epitope, the interactive residues are mainly within β-sheets of β17-β19, β17/β18 loop, and β22 of the site IV from one F protomer (Figure 4D, Table 2). The rest residues that contribute to the antibody interactions are from the neighboring protomer within α2/α3 loop and β3-β4 hairpin of site V. Regarding the paratope, CDR2, CDR3 and FR3 from VH and CDR1, CDR3 and FR3 from VL participate in the epitope recognition (Figure 4D, Table 2). Notably, residue S443 from β19 of F glycoprotein forms two hydrogen bonds with residues D112 and Y114 from CDR3 of RV11 via its hydroxyl oxygen (Figure 4D, Table 2). A proline mutation at residue 443 is expected to lose these H-bonds, which explains why the resistant mutants with S443P in F protein evaded neutralization by RV11 as described above.

RV11 has the similar binding mode to pre-F as RB1, the parent mAb of clesrovimab [12], however, is more tilted toward site V (Figure 4E). Compared with RB1, RV11 contacts more residues (E163 and G184) on the site V of pre-F and forms three more hydrogen bonds with E161 and N183 (Figure 4E). This may explain why RV11 binds exclusively to pre-F, whereas RB1 binds to both pre- and post-F.

RSV F Glycoprotein Amino-acid Sequence Variation Analysis for RV11-Binding Site

By Shannon entropy analyses of all amino acid sequences of RSV F-protein from GISAID up to May 2023, we found the RV11 binding site was highly conserved (>98.3%) at all positions of F glycoprotein of strains from both RSV A and B subtypes (Figure 5, Table S3). In contrast, the binding site for either nirsevimab or palivizumab has a higher substitution rate. The nirsevimab binding site contains the high polymorphism rate at residue 206 (57.7% I206M) and 209 (57.1% Q209R) among current circulating RSV B strains, some of which were shown to reduce the virus sensitivity to mAb neutralization [23] (Figure 5B, Table S3). The palivizumab binding site contains the polymorphism at residue 276 (15.9% S276N) among RSV A strains (Figure 5A, Table S3). Of note, the RV11-escape S443P mutation is not found, and the residue S443 is highly conserved (>99.0%) until May 2023.

Antiviral Activities and Cytotoxic Effects of Two-Drug Combinations

Small molecule fusion inhibitor ziresovir [24,25] (an antiviral currently in New Drug Application in China) and mAb D25 (the parental mAb of nirsevimab) targeting site Ø of Pre-F18 were chosen to test their combined antiviral activities against RSV in combination with RV11. We measured the anti-RSV activities of different drug pairs in a checkerboard format using a cytopathic effect (CPE) assay. To assess whether the observed antiviral activities of the drug combinations were synergistic, antagonistic, or simply additive, the experimental data were analyzed with the MacSynergy II program. This program calculated the theoretical additive interactions of the drugs based on the Bliss independence theory [26] and displayed synergy and antagonism as peaks above or below a predicted additive plane in a three-dimensional graph. When ziresovir was combined with RV11, the volumes of synergy were extremely high (466.98 μM2%), indicating a strong synergistic effect (Figure 6A and 6C). Similarly, when D25 was combined with RV11, strong synergistic effect was also observed (Figure 6B and 6C). The corresponding mean volumes of synergy and antagonism at the 95% confidence intervals (CI) are summarized in Figure 5, Figure 6C. Of note, all combination studies included concurrent evaluations of cell viability, and none of the synergistic combinations showed synergistic cytotoxicity within the range of drug concentrations examined. The extent of the synergy volume of two combinations indicates that these effects are probably important and would be translated to a better anti-RSV effect in vivo.

Discussion

Palivizumab as the first generation of mAb to prevent RSV LRTI has been used clinically for more than 20 years in high-risk infants. Palivizumab targets epitope at antigenic site II on both pre- and post-F, in which its binding to post-F conformation is regarded to be ineffective, and thus only exhibited a 55% efficacy in prevention of hospitalization as a result of RSV infection in high-risk infants [27]. A new generation of RSV mAbs with higher specificity to pre-F conformation with higher potency and extended half-life are sought. Potent mAbs have been identified to mainly target epitopes on pre-F conformation, represented by site Ø and V [13,15]. The newly approved RSV mAb, nirsevimab, recognizes the epitope on site Ø. The site Ø has the disadvantage of being highly glycosylated which could potentially interfere with antibody binding in vivo, whereas site IV does not. Recent studies have shown that potent mAbs can recognize site IV presented on both pre- and post-F, such as clesrovimab [12], and 3M3 [28]. Site IV is presented on both pre- and post-F, spanning residues from amino acid 422 to 468. Here, we report the discovery of RV11 as a novel and potent mAb that binds exclusively to pre-F, with its epitope compassing both site IV and V. RV11 is equipotent (EC50 < 50 ng/mL) to neutralize both RSV A and B subtype strains and showed superior in vitro activity (7.1–58.0 folds elevation against A and B subtype, respectively) and in vivo efficacy (6.3-fold elevation against A subtype) compared to palivizumab.

Previous studies found the sites III, IV and V are relatively conserved on F glycoprotein among RSV A strains, and site IV is the most conserved region among RSV B strains [20,21]. A previous site V mAb, suptavumab, failed to meet the primary endpoint in premature infants in clinical trials due to resistance to antibody by RSV B strains [29]. This highlights the importance of epitope conservation for RSV mAb. RV11 binds mainly to the conserved site IV. Variants resistant to RV11 neutralization contain a single S443P mutation in F glycoprotein from three independent selects, implying a narrow resistant spectrum for RV11. S443P mutation is also shared in variants resistant to RB1, the parental mAb of clesrovimab [12]. Therefore, for RV11 clinical development, S443P mutation of F protein deserves further surveillance during clinical studies.

Compared with the other antibodies that bind to site IV (RB1 and 101F), RV11 interacts with F glycoprotein at different angles. 101F binds to epitope exclusively on site IV which was presented on both pre-F (residues 391–399) and post-F (residues 398–406) [[30], [31], [32]]. RB1 contacts mainly on residues 426–447 of site IV presented on both pre-and post-F, but has interactions with two site V residues (E161 and S182) from the adjacent F protomer [12]. In contrast, RV11 engages more amino acids on both site IV (residues 426–447 and 464–470) and V (residues E161, E163, S182, and N183). The neutralization mechanism of RV11 may derive from its cross-linking of two protomers that inhibit conformational change required for virus-host membrane fusion during pre-to post–F transition.

There are limitations in this study. First, the in vivo protection of RV11 was only tested for RSV A subtype. It is important to test the antibody protection against RSV B subtype in vivo, even though RV11 is equipotent to neutralize both RSV A and B strains in vitro. Secondly, identification of variants resisted to RV11 neutralization was not performed for RSV B subtype strain. Both limitations should be addressed during the further development of RV11.

Development of RSV mAbs with different targets and mechanisms of action would be beneficial for RSV prophylaxis in face of the continuous viral mutation and appearance of drug resistance. RV11 showed strong synergistic effect when combined with ziresovir, a small molecule drug in late stage of clinical trial, and D25, the parental site Ø mAb of nirsevimab. Therefore, the discovery of RV11 mAb provides a potential alternative to other known RSV mAbs or small molecules with a unique binding mode to RSV F glycoprotein for prophylaxis and treatment of RSV infection.

Materials and Methods

Blood Samples, Cells, Viruses, and Compounds

Blood samples were obtained from the convalescent with RSV infection. HEK293T and HEp-2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco modified Eagle medium (DMEM; HyClone) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco; Thermo Scientific) and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (PS; HyClone) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. RSV_A2 was kindly provided by Jianwei Wang (Key Laboratory of Respiratory Disease Pathogenomics, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences & Peking Union Medical College, Beijing, China), which was purchased from ATCC (VR-1540). RSV-A Long strain was directly purchased from ATCC (VR-26). RSV-B9320 was kindly provided by Wenbo Xu (National Institute for Viral Disease Control and Prevention, Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, Beijing, China), which was purchased from ATCC (VR-955). All clinical isolates of RSV tested in this study were from Beijing Children's hospital. All strains of RSV were propagated in HEp-2 cells in DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS and 2.5% HEPES and preserved in liquid nitrogen before used for antiviral activity assays and combination studies. The compound ziresovir was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide, and the monoclonal antibodies RV8, RV11, palivizumab, and D25 were dissolved in PBS for antiviral experiments.

Generation and Purification of RSV F Proteins

Three constructs of RSV F proteins were the same as those previously reported and derived from A2 strain (accession no. P03420) with three substitutions (P102A, I379V, and M447V) [17,22,31]. Specifically, the DS-Cav1 contains RSV F residues 1–513 with four mutations (S155C/S290C/S190F/V207L), fused with a C-terminal T4 fibritin trimerization motif, thrombin site, 6x His-tag, and Strep-tag-II. The sc9-10 DS-Cav1 was generated from DS-Cav1 with deleted fusion peptide (N104-L144 is substituted by a GS linker). Post-F contains RSV F residues 1–513 with truncated fusion peptide (F137–S146), fused with a C-terminal T4 fibritin trimerization motif, thrombin site, 6x His-tag, and Strep-tag-II. All constructs were synthesized after codon optimization and subcloned into a mammalian expression vector pCAGGS.

RSV F proteins were expressed by transient transfection of HEK293T cells. After 4 days, the supernatant was collected, and purified by nickel-affinity chromatography (HisTrap HP column, GE Healthcare) and then size-exclusion chromatography (Superdex 200 column, GE Healthcare).

PBMCs Isolation

Freshly isolated EDTA anticoagulated blood was diluted with PBS solution and layered carefully on Ficoll-Hypaque density gradients. After centrifuging at 1000 g for 20 min at room temperature, interphase cell layer was carefully transferred into new 15 mL tubes. The 15 mL tube was filled with 10 mL PBS, and centrifuged at 800 g for 15 min. The supernatant was discarded completely and the PBMCs in pellet were resuspend the with 1 mL of PBS.

Isolation of Antigen-Binding Single Memory B Cells

PBMCs were stained and cell-sorted as previously reported [33]. Briefly, PBMCs were incubated with His-tagged DS-Cav1 at 400 nM for 30 min and mixed with multiple antibodies (anti-CD3, anti-CD16, anti-CD235a, anti-CD19, anti-CD38, anti-CD27 and anti-His). The memory B cells binding to DS-Cav1 were sorted into a 96-well (Polymerase Chain Reaction) PCR plate by BD FACSAria ΙΙΙ flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), one cell per well.

Amplification and Sequencing of Ig Variable Region of B-Cell Receptor

The antigen-specific memory B cells were lysed and used as templates to amplify VH and VL following a reported method with similar primers [34]. Firstly, the complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized by reverse transcription following the manufacturer's instructions (Invitrogen), using a mixture of primers specific for IgA, IgD, IgE, IgG, IgM, Igκ, and Igλ constant regions. After cDNA synthesis, VH, Vκ and Vλ genes were amplified separately by a two-round nested PCR. In the first-round PCR (PCRa), 2 μL cDNA was used as the template, 1 μL product of which was used to further amplify variable genes in the second-round PCR (PCRb). Samples of VH, Vκ, and Vλ chain PCR products were analyzed on 1.2% agarose gels. Bands around 400 bp were cut, purified by the Gel Extraction Micro Kit (CWBIO) and sequenced. Sequences were analyzed using the IMGT information system [35] to identify variable region gene segments and somatic mutations.

mAb Generation and Purification

These verified VH, Vκ and Vλ were separately linked to the constant region by overlapping PCR as previously reported [33]. These constant regions included the IgG1 constant region (GeneBank accession no. BC041037) for the heavy chain, the Ig kappa constant region (GeneBank accession no. BC073791) for the κ chain, and the Ig lambda constant region (GeneBank accession no. BC073769) for the λ chain. The Ig leader sequence (METDTLLLWVLLLWVPGSTGD) was appended to the 5′ end of the variable region by PCR. The overlapping product containing the leader sequence, variable region, and constant region was ligated into the KT351861 plasmid for further expression, using HindIII and NotI restriction sites for cloning.

The monoclonal antibodies were expressed in HEK293T cells by transient transfection. The supernatant containing monoclonal antibodies was collected and passed through a HiTrap Protein A FF column (5 mL, GE Healthcare) after centrifugation. The antibodies were eluted from the column using 0.1 M Glycine, pH 3.0, and neutralized immediately with 1 M Tris–HCl, pH 8.5. The collected antibodies were further purified by gel filtration (Superdex 200 column, GE Healthcare). For the antigen-binding affinity and crystallization study, purified antibodies were digested with the immobilized papain (Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The monoclonal antibodies were first buffered in 20 mM Na3PO4, 10 mM EDTA (pH 7.0), and then digested in buffer containing 20 mM Na3PO4, 10 mM EDTA, 10 μM cysteine-HCl (pH 7.0) at 37 °C overnight. The fragments of Fab were collected in the flow-through fraction from the HiTrap Protein A FF column (5 mL, GE Healthcare) and further purified by gel filtration (Superdex 200 column, GE Healthcare).

The RV11 scFv form was constructed as VL-(GGGGS)4-VH form and cloned into the pET21a expression vector (Invitrogen) through NheI and XhoI restriction sites. After overexpression in Escherichia coli as inclusion bodies, the RV11 scFv was sequentially dissolved in 8 M urea and diluted against a refolding buffer (100 mM Tris, pH 8.0; 400 mM l-arginine; 5 mM EDTA-Na; 5 mM glutathione; 0.5 mM glutathione disulfide). After 12 h of slow stirring at 4 °C, the refolded RV11 scFv was then concentrated and changed to a 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) and 150 mM NaCl buffer, and further analysed by gel filtration (Superdex 200 column, GE Healthcare).

Surface SPR Analysis

The interaction between purified antibodies with DS-Cav1 or post-F was tested by surface plasmon resonance analysis using a BIAcore 3000 system (GE Healthcare) carried out at 25 °C. All proteins used in this experiment were exchanged into the HBS-EP buffer, 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl and 0.005% (v/v)Tween 20. In brief, DS-Cav1 and post-F were separately immobilized to the flow cells (Fc) 2 and 4 on a CM5 biosensor chip (GE Healthcare), according to the manufacturer's amine-coupling chemistry protocol (GE Healthcare). The fragments of Fab were 2-fold serially diluted to certain concentrations (125nM–1.9 nM) and then injected over the immobilized RSV F proteins, beginning with the lowest concentration. Fc 1 was covalently coupled in the same way and used as the negative control. After recording the binding responses, the equilibrium dissociation constants (binding affinity, Kd) for each mAb interaction were calculated by software BIAevaluation Version 4.1 using the 1:1 Langmuir binding model (GE Healthcare).

Neutralization Assay

A flow cytometry-based neutralization assay with HEp-2 cells was performed to test the neutralizing activity of each RSV mAb using a previously reported protocol [33] with some modifications (Figure 1B and 1C). In brief, HEp-2 (ATCC cat. no: CCL-23) cells were seeded in 24-well plates (2 × 105 cells/well) 16 h before the infection experiment. The antibodies were serially diluted and incubated with RSV (final titer 1.2 × 104 TCID50/100 μL for 1h. Then, the mixtures were used to infect HEp-2 cells. After 48 h, cells were harvested, fixed, and permeabilized followed by being stained with primary antibody (palivizumab) at 2 μg/mL and anti-hIgG/FITC. After washing, the percentages of infected cells were measured using a BD FACSCalibur. Sigmoidal neutralization curves were plotted with GraphPad Prism 5.

A CPE assay with HEp-2 cells was carried out (Figures 1D, 1E). HEp-2 cells were seeded to the 96-well plate (Corning cat. no: 3917) at 5 × 103 cells/well in 50 μL for RSV-A or 24-well plate at 1.5 × 105 cells/well in 1 mL for RSV-B. The plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight. Antibodies were diluted in DMEM containing 2% FBS (heat inactivated) starting at indicated concentration followed by 3-fold serial dilutions. Antibodies dilutions were mixed with 2500 pfu of RSV A strain in 50 μL or 100 pfu RSV-B strain in 200 μL and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C.

For RSV-A, following the 1 h incubation, the mixture was added to the plate and incubated at 37 °C for 4 days. After 4 days of incubation, 50 μL CellTiter Glo (Promega cat. no: G7572) was added to every well and incubated for 10 min at room temperature. Then the luminescence was measured by microplate reader (Tecan M200 pro). Finally, neutralizing titers were analyzed by non-linear regression curve fit in GraphPad Prism.

For RSV-B, after the 1 h incubation, the mixture was added to the plate and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Then the mixture was discarded and 1 mL DMEM containing 2% FBS and 0.8% methylcellulose medium was added. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 6 days. Afterward, the cells were washed and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde PBS for 15min. Then cells were permeabilized by 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS for 20 min and incubated with 200 μL of 1:2000-diluted mouse anti-RSV F monoclonal antibody (Fitzgerald, MA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. The cells were washed twice and incubated with 1:6000-diluted HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG antibody (Bethyl, TX, USA) for 1 h. Then plaque was counted by adding TrueBlue Peroxidase Substrate (KPL, MD, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Finally, neutralizing titers were analyzed by non-linear regression curve fit in GraphPad Prism.

Prophylactic in vivo BALB/c Challenge Study

Eight, 6–8-week old, female BALB/c mice per dose group were passively immunized intraperitoneally with RV11 or palivizumab. For each experiment, antibody dilution was started at 8 mg/kg and was serially diluted 3-fold to 0.033 mg/kg [specifically, a total of six antibody dose groups (8 animals/group) were tested]. Animals were administered with antibodies in a volume of 100 μL on day 1 under isoflurane anesthesia. A control group consisting of naive animals was also included. Serum samples were collected to determine serum antibody concentration followed by intranasally inoculating animals with 105 pfu of RSV A2 (ATCC cat. no: VR-1540) virus in 0.1 mL volume on day 2, approximately 24 ± 2 h post antibody injection, under anesthesia. Four days post inoculation, animals were sacrificed by CO2 inhalation, and left lung lobes were used for virus titration.

RV11 Escape Mutant Selection

HEp-2 cells were seeded to 24-well plate at 1.5 × 105 cells/well. RSV-A long strain was incubated with RV11 at a final concentration of 450 ng/mL at 37 °C for 1 h in DMEM. Two hundred microliter of mixture was added to the wells at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.5. After 1 h incubation at 37 °C, the cells were washed with DMEM; fresh media containing 450 ng/mL of RV11 was added. Virus growth under antibody selection proceeded at 37 °C for 96 h. Then 100 μL of the culture fluid was mixed with 100 μL of fresh medium containing RV11 for 1 h; the mixture was added to HEp-2 cells to establish next round of infection. After five passages (RV11 concentration is shown in table as below) the resistance phenotype was confirmed, and viral RNAs were extracted from culture fluids using RNeasy kits (Qiagen). The extracted RNAs were amplified by reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) using SuperScript III onestep RT-PCR kits (Invitrogen). The F gene was then directly sequenced from the gel-purified PCR products. As a negative control, RSV-A Long was cultured in the absence of antibody for five rounds; the resulting virus was subjected to sequencing. Passage 1: RV11 concentration 450 ng/mL; Passage 2: RV11 concentration 900 ng/mL; Passage 3: RV11 concentration 900 ng/mL; Passage 4: RV11 concentration 1800 ng/mL; Passage 5: RV11 concentration 1800 ng/mL.

Crystallization, Data Collection, and Structure Determination

For the RSV-pre-F/RV11 complex, RSV-pre-F was mixed with RV11-scFv at a stoichiometry of 1:2 and incubated on ice for 2 h. The mixture was then subjected to gel filtration. The survival peak containing the indicated scFv and pre F was pooled, and buffered in 20 mM Tris and 50 mM NaCl (pH 8.0). It was concentrated to 10 or 5 mg/mL for crystallization.

To obtain RSV-pre-F-RV11 complex, the inclusion bodies of RSV-pre-F and RV11-scFv were diluted into refolding buffer containing 100 mM Tris (pH8.0), 400 mM L-Arg HCl, 2 mM EDTA, 5 mM reduced glutathione, 0.5 mM oxidized glutathione. After 12 h of slow stirring at 4 °C, the refolded RSV-pre-F/RV11-scFv complex was concentrated and further purified in an ÄKTA Pure System (GE Healthcare) by gel filtration on a HiLoad™ Superdex™ 200 preparation grade column (GE Healthcare). The survival peak containing the indicated RSV-pre-F/RV11-scFv was pooled. The indicated complex was further buffered in 20 mM Tris, 50 mM NaCl (pH 8.0), and concentrated to 10 or 5 mg/mL for crystallization. Crystallization trials were performed in sitting drops of 600 nL. All crystals were obtained by mixing equal volumes of protein and reservoir solution, using a Mosquito LCP robot (TTP LabTech). Diffraction-quality crystals of RSV-pre-F/RV11-scFv were obtained at 18 °C in an optimum condition based on PEGRx™2 #12, consisting of 0.15 M dl-Malic acid pH 6.2, 0.1 M Imidazole pH 7.0, 27% (v/v) polyethylene glycol monomethyl ether 550.

All diffraction data were collected at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility and processed with HKL2000 [36]. The RSV-pre-F/RV11-scFv dataset was collected at BL19U1. The complex structure was solved by the molecular replacement module of Phaser [37] from the CCP4 [38] program suite, with the previously reported structure of pre-F from RSV (PDB 5K6C), RSV-pre-F/RV11-scFv as the search model. Restrained rigid-body refinement with Phenix and manual model building with Coot [39] were then performed. Further rounds of refinement were then done with phenix. refine [40]. During model building and refinement, the program of MolProbity [41] was used to validate the stereochemistry of the structure. Structural data were analyzed by PyMOL [42]and the figures were generated using Chimera [43].

Combination Studies

The antiviral effects of the drug combinations on RSV replication were determined by a CPE-based assay. For each drug combination, the two drugs were prepared separately by serial 2-fold dilution and were mixed in 96-well plates (without using the edges of the plates) to create a 6-by-9 matrix of single and combined diluted drugs. The drug serial dilutions spanned a range of concentrations near each drug's EC50 so that equivalent antiviral activities were compared. Cell control wells containing medium only and virus control wells without compound treatment were included in each plate. The cytotoxicity of each combination was assessed in parallel by determination of the viability of uninfected cells using Cell-Titer-Glo reagent (Promega). To assess the antiviral effects of different drug combinations, volumes of synergy or antagonism were determined by using the MacSynergy II program developed by Prichard and Shipman. This program is based on the Bliss independent model. The synergy/antagonism volumes (μM2%) of drug combinations at 95% confidence were defined as follows: values between −25 and +25 indicate additivity and values between +25 and +50 or between −25 and −50 indicate minor but significant synergy or antagonism, respectively, whereas values between +50 and +100 or between −50 and −100 are interpreted as moderate synergism or antagonism, respectively. Values greater than +100 or less than −100 indicate strong synergistic or antagonistic activity, respectively.
