A protocol to establish low-cost floating treatment wetlands for large-scale wastewater reclamation
Authors: Muhammad Arslan, Samina Iqbal, Ejazul Islam, Mohamed Gamal El-Din, Muhammad Afzal
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryFloating treatment wetlands (FTWs) consist of buoyant rafts that support the growth of macrophytes on waterbodies. The long-term performance of these rafts depends on their buoyancy and resistance to weathering. Here, we present a protocol for establishing low-cost FTWs for large-scale wastewater reclamation by integrating traditional ecological knowledge with modern engineering principles. We describe steps for setting up a plant nursery, designing and establishing the FTWs, vegetating units, and building the FTW island.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Afzal et al.1Subject areas: Plant sciences, Biotechnology and bioengineering, Environmental sciences

Before you begin

Floating Treatment Wetlands (FTWs), also known as hydroponic root mats, represent an ecotechnological approach aimed at enhancing water quality through the utilization of purification mechanisms akin to those found in natural wetlands.2 One of the fundamental elements of FTWs is the presence of buoyant rafts that serve as platforms for large aquatic plants or macrophytes.1 These plants play a crucial role in the phytouptake of pollutants while fostering diverse microbial communities that facilitate various biogeochemical processes leading to the transformation of complex organic compounds into simpler inorganic substances.3 The buoyancy of these rafts is primarily attributed to the gases produced by microbial biofilms on the root surfaces and the presence of aerenchyma, which are air spaces within the root tissue of select plant species.4 By integrating ecological engineering principles that reinforce the innate buoyancy factor, these characteristics significantly enhance the long-term durability of the rafts.

This protocol outlines a thorough process for establishing economically viable and environmentally sustainable FTWs, primarily focusing on the rafts building. These systems can seamlessly integrate into both shallow and deep waste stabilization ponds, pit lakes, or lagoons. A water depth of 0.8–1 m is essential for optimal root growth; however, increased depth enhances the remediation capabilities of FTWs by extending pollutant exposure to roots and microbial biofilm. The ideal depth is determined by factors such as wastewater type, treatment goals, and inflow variations. For instance, shallow depths are preferable for fine particle removal due to effective plant-microbe interplay, while deeper waters better address coarse solids through sedimentary processes.5 To ensure optimal efficiency, it is advisable to allocate 20% of the overall pond surface for the installation of FTW. However, this proportion can be adjusted depending on the wastewater characteristics and hydraulic retention time (HRT). For instance, in the case of wastewater rich in crude oil content, we expanded the coverage to 33% of the pit area to meet the required standards (a batch system).6 These parameters are adaptable and subject to alterations, contingent upon the specific type of wastewater and the prevailing local climate conditions. For a deeper grasp of the system’s hydraulics, refer to the earlier study by Nivala et al.7

When selecting plants, it’s crucial to consider their tolerance to varying water conditions, such as pollutants, salinity, and pH, which can be achieved by choosing species naturally found in wetlands or marshy areas. The root systems of these plants play a vital role in water purification by offering a surface for beneficial bacteria, so plants with robust and dense root systems are preferable. A balanced mix of fast-growing species (e.g., Phragmites australis),8 which can quickly cover the water surface and prevent algal blooms, and slower-growing species (e.g., Juncus effusus),9 which offer long-term stability, is ideal. In anaerobic environments, the use of helophytes is advocated due to their capacity to convey atmospheric oxygen to the plant rhizosphere.10 This creates a redox cone around the root surface, facilitating efficient and accelerated degradation of organic matter.10 Incorporating aesthetically pleasing plants like roses can enhance the visual appeal of the system, though they may not offer significant scientific benefits for water treatment. It’s imperative to avoid non-native or invasive species that could disrupt the ecosystem by outcompeting local plants. Consulting with local experts, such as botanists or wetland specialists, as well as traditional ecological knowledge (TEK), can offer valuable guidance in plant selection for specific regions or applications.11

The unit raft measures 1.8 m in length, 1.2 m in width, and 1.2 cm in thickness, yielding a mat area of 2.2 m2. To assemble a 1000 m2 FTW, one needs to interconnect approximately 455 of these unit rafts.

Step-by-step method details

The timeline outlined in this protocol for the various steps is dependent on the area of the FTW. However, the timeline provided here is an approximate a week for an FTW area approximately 1000 m2.

Part 1: Setting up a plant nursery

Timing: ∼3–4 months

The establishment of a nursery is pivotal for ensuring a consistent supply of macrophytes that have undergone optimal growth. By providing a controlled environment, the nursery encourages robust root development, shields juvenile plants from potential predators, and promotes the cultivation of particularly efficacious species. Moreover, the nursery is a cost-effective approach due to bulk cultivation and acts as an experimental platform for trialing new plant species or cultivation techniques.

It is essential to have wetland plants nurturing in a nursery under optimal conditions and preparing them for placement in the FTWs (Figures 1A–1C). This requires cultivation of plants in specialized containers such as plastic pots equipped with drainage holes at the base. These plants undergo a growth period of approximately two months. To achieve this, obtain the initial propagules which include seedlings, rhizomes, and cuttings, from locales exposed to wastewater, such as drains and ponds, as well as from established nurseries. Then, use the pots manifesting optimal plant growth for the assembly of floating wetlands. Conversely, rejuvenate the pots that do not exhibit satisfactory growth by introducing fresh seedlings, cuttings, and rhizomes. Within this cultivation framework, the rhizomes are integrated into the pots.

Expected outcomes

The strategic assembly of raft units allows formulation of an FTW system. Within the initial months post-implementation, emergent vegetation will both proliferate and demonstrate adaptive responses to the prevailing environmental conditions and specific wastewater constituents (Figure 5). Evaluating FTW systems, it becomes evident that attributes such as durability and continued operation stand as pivotal benchmarks. Data presented in Table 1 provide empirical evidence of the rafts' sustained structural integrity for a period exceeding 10 years. This extended lifespan not only highlights the robustness of the FTWs but also provides insights into potential replacement or maintenance timelines, which are minimal. Notably, each study listed in Table 1 remains operational, reinforcing the consistent performance and reliability of the FTWs across diverse research settings. Further, cost of indigenously developed FTWs is significantly lower compared to commercially purchased rafts. Finally, implementation of FTWs results in a significant enhancement of all measured water quality indicators and a decrease in heavy metal concentrations in the effluent as compared to the influent. This includes significant attenuation of chemical oxygen demand (COD), biochemical oxygen demand (BOD), and total dissolved solids (TDS).1,3 Figure 5 depicts a representative FTW system installed in a waste stabilization pond in Faisalabad, Pakistan.

Limitations

Field-scale systems often experience suboptimal performance in the initial year, but over time, successful outcomes have been observed in variety of studies treating wastewater from multiple origins, e.g., industrial, sewage, domestic. Also, FTW systems can treat wastewater up to the secondary level, which has implications for irrigation or potential discharge into natural streams and cannot be used for domestic purposes. Nevertheless, we earlier found that FTW efficiently reclaimed 60 million cubic meters of wastewater annually, meeting local environmental quality standards, at a cost of US$0.00026 per cubic meter.1

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Impeded plant growth following FTW installation.

Potential solution

During the initial weeks following installation, plant die-back is commonly anticipated due to alterations in environmental conditions, predominantly tied to the characteristics of the wastewater. This phenomenon is particularly prevalent in cases involving industrial wastewater. In such scenarios, it’s crucial to assess the concentration of essential plant nutrients - Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P), and Potassium (K) - in the wastewater. If these nutrients are found to be insufficient or out of balance, it becomes necessary to supplement the system with fertilizers to facilitate successful plant growth. Once the plants are well-established, further fertilizer additions are typically not required in subsequent stages.

Problem 2

Fluctuating water levels due to seasonal variations (related to Step 15).

Potential solution

If FTW is positioned in a waste stabilization pond or lagoon with water levels subject to seasonal shifts (e.g., due to freshet or rainfall events), it becomes essential to extend the length of the metal wire attached to the anchor by a few meters. This modification enables the FTW structure to adapt to the varying water levels.

Problem 3

Reduced wetland efficiency during late-stage plant senescence and extensive biomass.

Potential solution

Typically, FTW performance declines after a few months of robust plant growth, attributed to senescence, plant maturation (phytouptake saturation), and prolonged pollutant exposure. By implementing a biannual pruning regimen, specifically in the months of February and August, we can ensure that the plants remain healthy and do not overcrowd the wetland. This not only aids in maintaining optimal water flow but also ensures that the plants can effectively uptake nutrients and pollutants. Furthermore, the periodic manual removal of deceased plant matter prevents the accumulation of decaying biomass, which could otherwise hinder the wetland’s performance.
