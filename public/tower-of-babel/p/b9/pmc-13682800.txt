A cog in the Weil: A case report of severe leptospirosis
Authors: Anam Azhar, Iman Ali, Maha Azhar, Namitha Govinda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe nonspecific presentation of leptospirosis and delays in serologic confirmation often make its diagnosis challenging. The presence of comorbidities or potential drug toxicities, which can mimic or obscure clinical findings, further adds to this diagnostic difficulty. Here, we present the case of a 46-year-old male with well-controlled HIV infection on antiretroviral therapy, who presented with a four-day history of fever, malaise, and jaundice. The patient is a pilot with recent travel to the United Kingdom and prior travel throughout North America, Central America, and the Caribbean. On admission, he was febrile and tachycardic, with laboratory studies demonstrating elevated creatinine and transaminases. Broad-spectrum antibiotics were initiated and subsequently de-escalated to doxycycline, while antiretroviral therapy was temporarily held. A renal biopsy demonstrated acute tubulointerstitial nephritis. The patient improved clinically with doxycycline, with normalization of liver and kidney function by the time of discharge. Post-discharge serologic testing returned positive for Leptospira IgM antibodies, confirming the diagnosis of Weil’s disease. This case underscores the diagnostic challenges of leptospirosis, particularly in patients without clear exposure histories, and highlights the importance of early empiric therapy when clinical suspicion is high.

1. Introduction

Leptospirosis is a bacterial zoonotic infection caused by the pathogenic Leptospira species and is recognized as the most prevalent zoonotic disease worldwide.[1] Humans typically acquire the infection through exposure to water or soil contaminated with the urine of infected animals.[1] In the United States, approximately 100–150 cases are reported annually.[2] In recent years, most cases have occurred in Hawaii and Puerto Rico.[2] The majority of cases are associated with recreational activities such as swimming and water sports. Clinical manifestations range from mild to severe, life-threatening diseases. Most cases are mild and present with nonspecific symptoms such as fever, headache, myalgia, and subconjunctival suffusion. The severe form, known as Weil’s disease, is characterized by jaundice, renal dysfunction, and hemorrhagic manifestations.[3]

The diagnosis of leptospirosis can be challenging due to its nonspecific presentation and delays in serologic confirmation. Comorbidities or potential drug toxicities that mimic or obscure clinical findings further complicate diagnosis. We present a case of Weil’s disease in an HIV-positive patient on antiretroviral treatment. The patient presented with fever, jaundice and renal dysfunction; initial differentiation was obscured by prior nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) intake and suspected drug-mediated hepatorenal toxicity. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion and initiating empiric therapy when diagnostic uncertainty exists.

2. Case presentation

A 46-year-old male with a past medical history of well-controlled HIV, managed with Dovato (a combination of dolutegravir and lamivudine), presented to the emergency department in September 2025 with body aches, chills, and progressive jaundice. He had recently returned from a four-day trip to the United Kingdom, during which he visited an area near Manchester.

Shortly after returning to the United States, the patient developed vomiting and a headache and was evaluated at an urgent care center where he tested negative for COVID-19 and influenza. He was given intramuscular NSAIDs and prescribed oral NSAIDs and metoclopramide upon discharge. Over the following days, his symptoms worsened, and he noted progressive jaundice, prompting him to present to the emergency department.

The patient works as a pilot and travels regularly for work. In the past six months, he has flown throughout the United States, Canada, Central America, and the Caribbean. He does not have any pets and denied recent contact with animals. He also denied swimming in lakes, rivers, or other open bodies of water, and had no recent history of hiking or zoo exposure. He denied right upper quadrant pain, cough, shortness of breath, urinary changes, visual disturbances, neck stiffness, weakness, paresthesias, or focal neurological deficits. He was diagnosed with HIV 10 years ago and has remained compliant with antiretroviral therapy. His most recent viral load, obtained one month prior, was undetectable. He transitioned from a three-drug regimen containing abacavir to Dovato two months earlier. He is a former smoker with a one pack-year history.

On presentation, his vital signs revealed a temperature of 101.8 ºF, pulse of 131 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute, blood pressure of 134/86 mmHg, and oxygen saturation of 97% on room air. Physical examination showed jaundice with conjunctival icterus but no conjunctival hemorrhage, neck stiffness, abdominal tenderness, or calf tenderness. Murphy’s sign was negative. Cardiopulmonary and neurological examinations were unremarkable.

An electrocardiogram revealed sinus tachycardia, and chest radiography showed no pulmonary infiltrates. Laboratory evaluation revealed lymphopenia, markedly elevated creatinine at 5.46 mg/dL on admission (with a normal baseline), AST 535 U/L, ALT 438 U/L, alkaline phosphatase 206 U/L, total bilirubin 8.2 mg/dL, direct bilirubin 6.2 mg/dL, and C-reactive protein 18.83 mg/dL. Urinalysis was bland, and an extensive immunologic panel was negative. Blood cultures showed no growth.

Given the symptoms of fever, jaundice, and acute renal dysfunction, initial differential diagnoses included sepsis, drug-induced liver or renal injury from antiretroviral therapy or NSAIDs, and leptospirosis. The patient was empirically started on piperacillin-tazobactam, cefepime, and doxycycline. His antiretroviral therapy was temporarily held due to concern for a potential drug reaction. Cefepime and piperacillin-tazobactam were discontinued after blood and urine cultures did not grow any organisms. Doxycycline monotherapy was continued for seven days to empirically treat vector-borne and atypical organisms.

The patient’s fever resolved within 24 hours of hospitalization, and his clinical status gradually improved. His hepatic and renal function did not significantly improve during the first three days of hospitalization. On day two, a renal biopsy demonstrated acute tubulointerstitial nephritis with patchy interstitial inflammation containing scattered neutrophils and occasional eosinophils (Figure 1). There was no evidence of segmental sclerosis or immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritis.

The patient’s hepatic and renal dysfunction and general symptoms improved after seven days of inpatient treatment. He was discharged with close nephrology follow-up. In the days following discharge, serologic testing returned positive for Leptospira IgM antibody, confirming the diagnosis of Weil’s disease. The patient resumed Dovato under the care of his HIV specialist without recurrence of symptoms.

Written informed consent for the publication of case details and related laboratory test result was obtained from the patient.

3. Discussion

Leptospirosis is a bacterial zoonotic infection caused by the pathogen Leptospira and is considered the most widespread zoonotic disease globally.[1] It is transmitted to humans through contact with the urine of infected animals, either directly or via contaminated soil and water. The disease is most prevalent in tropical and subtropical regions, where heavy rainfall and flooding facilitate transmission. A study published in Eurosurveillance in 2025 identified a positive association between higher temperatures and increased leptospirosis incidence, suggesting that climate change may contribute to future increases in disease burden.[4]

According to the National Notifiable Diseases Surveillance System, no cases of leptospirosis were reported in Texas from 2014 to 2020.[2] Most cases in the United States are associated with recreational activities and occur from July to October[2]; this case presented in August.

Walking barefoot is a recognized risk factor for leptospirosis, as it can lead to contact with infected water, mud, or urine.[5] After the diagnosis was confirmed serologically, a more detailed discussion with the patient was conducted to identify a possible source of infection. The patient denied any known exposure to animals, natural water bodies, or soil. However, he reported that he had recently placed a new bird feeder in his driveway and noticed increased animal droppings in the area. He occasionally walked barefoot in his driveway to retrieve packages, which may represent the most plausible source of infection in the absence of any other clear exposure.

Most cases of leptospirosis are mild and present with fever, myalgia (particularly in the calves), headache, conjunctival suffusion, and nausea. Weil’s disease represents the severe systemic form of leptospirosis, characterized by jaundice, acute kidney injury, hemorrhage, and multi-organ dysfunction.[3] The severe manifestations correspond to the vasculitic and immune-mediated phase of infection. This patient’s presentation with fever, jaundice, and renal impairment was consistent with Weil’s disease.

Leptospira organisms have an affinity for endothelial cells, hepatocytes, and renal tubular epithelial cells, particularly within the proximal tubule, which provides a favorable environment for bacterial survival.[1] Renal involvement results from both direct cytotoxic effects and immune-mediated injury, leading to tubulointerstitial nephritis and, in some cases, subsequent fibrosis. Classically, leptospiral nephritis presents as non-oliguric, hypokalemic acute kidney injury.[1] In this case, renal biopsy demonstrated acute tubulointerstitial nephritis with scattered neutrophils and eosinophils. The presence of eosinophils despite peripheral eosinopenia is characteristic of leptospirosis. Approximately 5% fibrosis was noted; however, renal function improved with antibiotic therapy, and the patient did not progress to chronic kidney disease, as can occur in some cases of Weil’s disease. Additionally, he maintained adequate urine production throughout his illness.

Hepatic involvement in leptospirosis results from direct damage to hepatocytes and Kupffer cells, followed by an inflammatory immune response leading to hepatitis.[1] This patient’s liver enzymes and bilirubin levels gradually improved with antibiotic therapy. Pulmonary involvement can occur in severe cases, manifesting as alveolar hemorrhage, but was absent in this case.

The patient developed transient hypotension and tachycardia approximately ten hours after starting antibiotics, which resolved without vasopressors. This episode was consistent with a Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction, a self-limiting inflammatory response that can occur following antibiotic treatment of spirochetal infections, including leptospirosis.[2]

Serological confirmation in this patient was delayed because the specimen was processed at an outside laboratory. Drug reaction to NSAIDs or antiretroviral therapy was considered in the differential diagnosis. An extensive infectious workup was also performed, including testing for typhus fever, West Nile virus, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, and syphilis; all were negative. Empiric treatment with doxycycline was initiated based on high clinical suspicion. In the United States, most cases are confirmed by positive Immunoglobulin M, Microscopic Agglutination Test, Immunohistochemistry, culture, or Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) testing.[2] In many cases, there is a delay in serological confirmation. Next-generation sequencing and quantitative PCR of urine and blood can be used to reduce this delay. Although not performed in this case, these methods could have expedited diagnosis.

This case highlights the diagnostic challenges of leptospirosis, particularly in the absence of an obvious exposure, and emphasizes the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in patients presenting with fever, jaundice, and renal dysfunction. Early empiric antibiotic therapy should be initiated when clinical suspicion is high, even before laboratory confirmation, to reduce morbidity and prevent progression to severe disease.

Leptospirosis remains an important tropical disease that should be considered in patients presenting with classical signs and symptoms, even in the absence of a clear exposure. This case illustrates the broad spectrum of disease manifestations, including severe forms such as Weil’s disease, and highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by overlapping presentations and delayed serologic confirmation. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion and initiate empiric antibiotic therapy promptly when leptospirosis is suspected, as early treatment can significantly improve outcomes.
