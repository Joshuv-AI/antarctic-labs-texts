Notes on the life cycle of a new marine ciliate, Metafolliculina songi n. sp. (Protozoa, Ciliophora, Heterotrichea), with description of its infraciliature and phylogeny
Authors: Tingting Ye, Mingzhen Ma, Jemin Park, Mann Kyoon Shin, Yaohan Jiang, Wen Song, Lifang Li, Yong Chi, Xiangrui Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With a total of 33 genera, the family Folliculinidae Dons, 1914 represents the most genus-rich lineage within the class Heterotrichea. Its members are characterized by a transparent lorica, conspicuous peristomial lobes, and a dimorphic life cycle, which distinguishes them from other heterotrichs. Although approximately 80 nominal species have been recognized within the family, most aspects of their morphology, life cycle, and molecular information are still unknown. In the present study, a new species, Metafolliculina songi n. sp., collected from a wetland in China, is investigated using morphological and molecular methods. The complete life cycle and morphology of this species are documented, along with detailed ecological information and cultivation protocols. Notably, this folliculinid exhibits unique vulnerabilities: its short, wide neck and lack of a closure mechanism make it susceptible to predation by the ciliate Loxophyllum salinum and infestation by rotifers. Phylogenetic analysis based on small subunit ribosomal DNA (SSU rDNA) sequences confirms the monophyletic nature of the family Folliculinidae. However, M. songi n. sp. unexpectedly formed a sister relationship with Metafolliculina + Eufolliculina, suggesting evolutionary relationships within this family require further investigation through additional data.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s42995-026-00376-3.

Introduction

Ciliate protozoa are among the most complex and morphologically diverse unicellular eukaryotes. These organisms are found in a wide range of habitats and play a vital role in the microbial food web by facilitating the transfer of energy and nutrients (Chi et al. 2024; Dolan et al. 2022; Heidelberg et al. 2013; Lynn 2008; Ma et al. 2025; Rondello Bonatti et al. 2023; Song et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2022, 2024; Zhang et al. 2025a). Members of the class Heterotrichea Stein, 1859, a large omnivorous group, have been widely utilized as model organisms in various fields such as regenerative biology, environmental ecology, and toxicology. Consequently, research on this group has consistently garnered widespread attention (Campello-Nunes et al. 2018; Chi et al. 2022; Fernandes et al. 2016; Iida 2025; Jin et al. 2021; Lobban et al. 2007; Perrotta et al. 2016; Shimada et al. 2024; Wancura et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2025a, b; Zheng et al. 2025b; Zhou et al. 2024).

The family Folliculinidae Dons, 1914 comprises the largest number of genera (33) within Heterotrichea. Its members live solidly attached to the surface of various substrates and mostly graze on bacteria and microalgae (Chi et al. 2021; Hadži 1951; Lynn 2008; Ye et al. 2021a, b, 2022). They are mainly characterized by a transparent lorica and a conspicuous pair of peristomial lobes. These sessile, loricate ciliates are distinguished from other heterotrichs by their dimorphic life cycle, which alternates between two distinct forms, i.e., the mouthless, motile swarmer and sessile trophont. Although about 80 nominal species have been documented from marine and freshwater habitats, their morphology and life cycle remain largely unexplored. To date, the morphological characters of only nine species have been studied using silver staining and electron microscopy (Hadži 1938, 1951; Kahl 1932; Luo et al. 2019; Mulisch et al. 1993; Mulisch and Patterson 1987a, b; Ye et al. 2021a, b). Detailed life cycle observations using light or electron microscopy, have been recorded for only six species, namely Metafolliculina andrewsi, Metafolliculina producta, Folliculina ampulla, Eufolliculina uhligi, Lagotia minor, and Ampullofolliculina lageniformis (Andrews 1920, 1923, 1949; Fauré-Fremiet 1936; Mulisch et al. 1981, 1993; Mulisch and Hausmann 1983, 1988; Mulisch and Patterson 1983, 1987a, b; Uhlig 1973a, b; Villeneuve-Brachon 1940; Ye et al. 2021a). In addition, SSU rDNA sequences are available in GenBank for only six species (Chi et al. 2021; Hammerschmidt et al. 1996; Luo et al. 2019; Miao et al. 2009; Ye et al. 2021a, b).

This study describes a new species of Metafolliculina, M. songi n. sp., collected from a wetland in China, with detailed observations of its morphology and life cycle based on both living specimens and protargol-stained preparations. This is only the second study worldwide to use protargol staining to investigate the metamorphosis of a folliculinid. Furthermore, detailed ecological information and cultivation methods are supplied. Phylogenetic analysis based on SSU rDNA sequences is also conducted to reveal evolutionary relationships within the folliculinids. Lastly, the global distribution of six currently recognized Metafolliculina species are summarized based on previous and current research.

Materials and methods

Sample collection, cultivation, observation, and identification

Specimens were collected between 22 September 2020 and 10 January 2021 from a wetland (29°46′45″N, 121°57′41″E) connected with the East China Sea on Meishan Island, where the water temperature ranged from 24 to 30 °C and salinity was 4 PSU (Fig. 1A–D). The sampling site was characterized by a dense population of Portulaca oleracea Linnaeus, 1753, with submerged stems and leaves providing a substrate for the attachment of M. songi n. sp. Glass slides and transparent sealing bags were under water at the sampling site and served as artificial substrates for the colonization and regular collection of M. songi n. sp. Living cells (trophonts and swarmers) and loricae were observed at 40–400 × magnification using an inverted microscope (Nexcope NIB400). Trophonts were squeezed out of their loricae using an eyebrow brush under a stereo microscope (VIHENT VHL-745) at 10–50 × magnification, and placed on a glass slide for observation using bright field and differential interference contrast (DIC) microscopy (Leica DM2500) at 400–1000 × magnification.

The ciliary pattern and nuclear apparatus were revealed by protargol staining (Wilbert 1975). Counts, measurements, and drawings of stained specimens were performed at 1000 × magnification. Terminology follows Hadži (1951), Luo et al. (2019), and Ye et al. (2022).

DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and gene sequencing

Trophont cells were squeezed out of loricae, rinsed five times with filtered in situ water (0.22 μm pore size membrane, Millipore, USA) to eliminate any potential contaminants, and transferred to 1.5 mL microfuge tubes with a minimum volume of water. Genomic DNA was extracted using a DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The universal eukaryotic primers 18SF (5’-AAC CTG GTT GAT CCT GCC AGT-3’) and 18SR (5’-TGA TCC TTC TGC AGG TTC ACC TAC-3’) were used to amplify the SSU rDNA (Medlin et al. 1988). The cycling parameters of PCR were as follows: one cycle of initial denaturation at 98 °C at 30 s, followed by 35 cycles of amplification (98 °C, 10 s; 56 °C, 20 s; 72 °C, 100 s), and a final extension at 72 °C for 120 s. To minimize the possibility of PCR amplification errors, Q5 Hot Start High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (NEB Co., Ltd., M0493, Beijing, China) was employed. PCR products were checked using agarose gel and sequenced by TSINGKE (Hangzhou, China). SeqMan (DNASTAR) was used to assemble the sequences fragments into contigs.

Phylogenetic analyses

Phylogenetic analyses of SSU rDNA sequences were performed using a dataset comprising one newly obtained sequence and 65 sequences of representative heterotrichs obtained from GenBank. Five karyorelictean sequences were used as the outgroup. Sequence alignment was performed using the MAFFT algorithm with default parameters on the GUIDANCE2 web server (Penn et al. 2010a, b). Alignments were trimmed using BioEdit v.7.1.3.0 (Hall 1999). The final alignment constructed for phylogenetic analyses contained a total of 1561 positions. Maximum likelihood (ML) analyses were performed on the CIPRES Science Gateway using RAxML-HPC2 on XSEDE v.8.2.11 (Stamatakis et al. 2008). The GTR + I + G model was selected as the optimal choice according to the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) by ModelTest v.3.4 (Posada and Crandall 1998). A total of 1000 bootstrap replicates were used to support the best ML tree. Bayesian inference (BI) analysis was carried out with MrBayes 3.2.7 (Ronquist et al. 2012) on the CIPRES Science Gateway, with the GTR + I + G model selected by MrModelTest v.2.3 (Nylander 2004). The analysis was run for 10,000,000 generations, with trees sampled every 100 generations. The first 25% of sampled trees were discarded as burn-in. Tree topologies were visualized using MEGA v.6.0 (Tamura et al. 2011) and TreeView v.1.6.6 (Page 1996). The systematic classification mainly followed Chi et al. (2021), Hadži (1951), Gao et al. (2016), and Shazib et al. (2014).

Topology testing

To test whether Metafolliculina is monophyletic, phylogenetic relationships within the genus were evaluated using the approximately unbiased (AU) test (Shimodaira and Goldman 2002). A constrained ML tree was constructed in PAUP v.4.0 (Swofford 2002) with predefined constraints, which was subsequently compared to the optimal unconstrained ML topology via CONSEL (Shimodaira and Hasegawa 2001). Notably, internal relationships both within the constrained clades and among the remaining taxa were left unspecified.

Diagnosis: Lorica transparent, colorless or light green; flask recumbent and pyriform when viewed from above, about 140–330 × 105–150 μm in size, ratio of length to width about 2:1; neck short, about 110–160 × 65–80 μm in size, obliquely raised at an angle of 30–60° relative to basal plate, with a conspicuous spiral ridge making 5–7 turns; fully extended trophont about 670 × 40–60 μm; peristomial lobes slender, about equal in size and shape, very flexible; posterior portion of trophont gradually narrowed; holdfast organelle small and button-shaped; cortical granules elliptical, dark greenish, forming 1 or 2 irregular rows between adjacent somatic kineties; pigment granules globular, blackish green; macronucleus moniliform, comprising 9–19 nodules; 330–834 adoral membranelles; paroral membrane composed of dikinetids; 51–76 somatic kineties; brackish water habitat.

Type locality: A subtropical brackish wetland (29°46′45″N, 121°57′41″E) on Meishan Island, Ningbo, China, near the East China Sea; water temperature 24–30 °C and salinity 4 PSU; with numerous Portulaca oleracea individuals.

Dedication: The species-group name is in honor of Prof. Weibo Song, Ocean University of China, who has contributed tremendously to the research of marine ciliates in China.

Type slides: A protargol slide with the holotype specimen marked with an inked circle, and 17 slides with paratype specimens showing different stages of life cycle, have been deposited in the Laboratory of Protozoology, Ocean University of China, with the registration numbers: YTT-20200922–01 and YTT-20200922-(02, 03, 04…18).

General morphology and ciliary pattern: Lorica transparent, wall thick (Figs. 1E, 2D, 3L), colorless or light green; flask recumbent, pyriform when viewed from above, about 140–330 × 105–150 μm in size, ratio of length to width about 2:1; neck short, about 110–160 × 65–80 μm in size, obliquely raised at an angle of 30–60° relative to basal plate, with a conspicuous spiral ridge that makes 5–7 turns (each turn spaced about 8 μm apart), lorica opening with a narrow brim which curves slightly outwards (Figs. 2A–D, 3B, I, J). In some loricae, the neck extends beyond the original location of the aperture, forming a few transparent and slightly sculptured new parts, aperture with a narrow brim (Figs. 2G, 3H). Basal plate transparent (Fig. 3K). Lorica and basal plate of mature individuals and newly formed neck usually covered by layers of biofilm with a mass of flocculent debris and microalgae attached (Fig. 3H).

Trophont in vivo about 670 × 40–60 μm when fully extended, about 150–170 × 55–65 μm when contracted (Figs. 2A, C, D, 3B, 4A). Peristomial lobes slender (short in a minority of individuals), flexible, equal in shape and size (about 100–340 × 20–30 μm), connected by a pellicular flap extending from the ventral side of right lobe (Figs. 2A, D, 3B, C), lobes of some individuals with pointed tips of variable size (Fig. 3E). When contracted, lobes form conspicuous wavy folds in mature individuals (Figs. 2C, 3A, F). Posterior part of cell gradually narrowed, with small individuals slim, and holdfast organelle button-shaped (Figs. 2A, C, D, 3K, L). Cortical granules dark greenish, oval to elliptical, about 0.8–1.0 × 0.9–1.0 μm, forming one or two irregular rows between adjacent somatic kineties, arranged sparsely (Figs. 2H, 3G). Pigment granules blackish green, globular, about 0.3 μm in diameter, forming several irregular rows between adjacent somatic kineties, packed tightly (Figs. 2H, 3G). Cytoplasm colorless, usually containing numerous microalgae, along with a limited number of vacuoles (Figs. 2C, D, 3A, B, F, K). Macronucleus moniliform, comprising 9–19 nodules, not easily detectable in vivo, conspicuous in protargol-stained specimens (Figs. 2L, 3Q). Micronucleus not observed. Contractile vacuole absent.

Adoral zone of membranelles (AZM) conspicuous, composed of about 330–834 membranelles (Table 1), cilia about 12–20 μm long in vivo (Figs. 2D, J–L, 3A, C–E, P, Q). AZM commences on the ventral side at the base of right lobe, regularly distributed along the margins of the peristomial lobes, spiraling into the buccal cavity along the edge of the left lobe, making about one and a half turns within the buccal cavity (Figs. 2J, 3P). Paroral membrane, parallel to AZM, not easily detectable in vivo, conspicuous in protargol-stained specimens, single-rowed, composed of pairs of kinetosomes, terminating above the buccal cavity (Fig. 2J). About 51–76 somatic kineties (Table 1), cilia about 7 μm long in vivo (Figs. 2K, L, 3Q).

Swarmers about 345–470 × 45–50 μm in size, dark green, vermiform, globular when contracted (Figs. 4D, 6D). Membranellar spiral comprising 66–81 membranelles, cilia about 14 μm long in vivo (Fig. 6D, E). Pellicle flexible with dark greenish cortical granules and blackish-green pigment granules forming about six rows between adjacent kineties (Fig. 2I). Granules in membranellar spiral darker and more densely distributed than in other regions. Densely packed pigment granules forming an elliptical black pigment patch in the glabrous area near the distal end of the membranellar spiral (Fig. 6C, D). Macronucleus moniliform, composed of about nine nodules. Somatic kineties bipolar, 58–77 in number, cilia about 10 μm long in vivo (Figs. 5F, 6E).

The life cycle of Metafolliculina songi n. sp.

The main events of cell division are illustrated schematically in Figs. 4 and 5 and are shown photographically in Fig. 6. It has been observed that cells preparing for reproduction tend to be larger in size. During the initial stage of division, the trophont retracts into the lorica, and then the peristomial lobes shorten, become irregular in shape and finally get resorbed (Figs. 4B, 5A, B). Simultaneously, two new oral primordia emerge in the mid and posterior regions of the cell (Figs. 4B, 5A). Concurrently, macronuclear nodules begin to fuse into a spheroidal structure. The two oral primordia, which differ in size, develop separately. The smaller oral primordium of the proter migrates to the anterior part of the cell, while the oral primordium of the opisthe extends diagonally from the holdfast organelle towards the cell’s equator, where the division furrow forms. It is evident that half of the band-like macronuclear nodules are allocated to the proter (Figs. 5B, C, 6A). Subsequently, the oral primordium of the proter reaches the anterior end, and the membranellar spiral forms, leaving an elliptical area devoid of cilia (Fig. 5D). Simultaneously, the peristomial lobes of the opisthe start their growth, while the division furrow continues to develop and cell becomes increasingly constricted in this region (Fig. 5D). Prior to the completion of cell division, the peristomial lobes are distinguishable, and the buccal spiral reaches its final position at the base of the left lobe. The proter remains attached to the dorsal base of the left lobe of the opisthe until the latter completes stomatogenesis (Fig. 5E). After division, the swarmer quickly leaves the lorica, undergoing a rapid differentiation process (Fig. 4C-E2). Each lobe of opisthe develops a pointed tip at its anterior end (Figs. 4L, 6B), which will be completely absorbed within about 10 h.

In a small number of exceptional cases, the swarmer does not leave the lorica after division. This phenomenon may arise when, during the late stage of division, the daughter cells separate prematurely, resulting in the under-development of their membranellar spiral and peristomial lobes. In such cases, they remain in the lorica, thereby facilitating continued growth. During this period, the peristomial lobes of the opisthe can undergo complete development, resulting in their extension out of the lorica for the purpose of food acquisition (Fig. 4C).

After leaving of the lorica, the swarmer locates a suitable surface for settlement and, within 3 min of settling, rapidly flattens its body and begins secreting material to form a new lorica (Fig. 4E1, E2). During the secretion process, the swarmer remains flattened on the surface to determine the width of the flask, and its posterior portion becomes slightly depressed (Fig. 4E2). Consequently, the swarmer undergoes a process of shrinkage, resulting in the formation of the ampulla region of the flask (Fig. 4F). The anterior end of the cell becomes cylindrical and extends upwards to form the lorica neck and spiral ridge. Then the anterior extremity of the cell undergoes significantly broadening, resulting in a trumpet-like configuration, concurrent with the formation of the lorica brim (Figs. 4G, 6I). The membranelles of the membranellar spiral loosen and are gradually absorbed. Simultaneously, the primordium of the AZM emerges in the left middle part of the cell and extends towards both ends, with no paroral cilia observed at this stage (Figs. 5G, H, 6F). About 15–30 min later, the brim of the neck is formed, indicating that the lorica is fully constituted.

The cell then retracts into the newly formed lorica and continues the process of metamorphosis. The trumpet-shaped structure is gradually absorbed (Figs. 4H, 6J). The oral primordium extends diagonally across the kineties, and the somatic cilia along its border undergo degradation (Fig. 5H). A few minutes later, the primordium of the AZM forms an anterior hook, which undergoes expansion in the lateral and anterior directions of the cell (Figs. 4I, 5I). The arrangement of adoral cilia into membranelles is initiated, and progresses from anterior to posterior, as illustrated in Fig. 5I. Following a period of 20–40 min, the membranelles are fully formed, and the AZM is clearly differentiated into an anterior narrow peristomial part of the adoral ciliature. This part later surrounds the peristomial lobes, while a broader posterior buccal part forms the ciliature around the buccal cavity and the cytostome (Figs. 5J, 6H, K). An obvious pointed tip appears at the anterior end of each peristomial lobe, and the shape of these tips continually changes during differentiation (Figs. 4J, K, 6L). Hereafter, the buccal end of the AZM forms a left-handed spiral which invaginates into the cell, and the paroral cilia disappear as gullet formation begins (Figs. 4K, 5K, 6G). Then the interstices between adjacent membranelles increase as the AZM elongates. The AZM exhibits a deep invagination at the proximal end, and makes about 1.5 turns to circumscribe the future buccal cavity, although it has not yet attained its ultimate position at the base of the right lobe (Figs. 5L, 6N). The completion of the peristomial lobes takes around 20–40 min, during which the pointed tips of the lobes persist (Fig. 6N). At this stage, the cell resembles the trophont, but the holdfast organelle has not yet formed. During the differentiation of certain individuals, the edges of the lobes develop and protrude, forming folds that gradually grow and deform (Fig. 6M). About 10 h later, the anterior ends and edges of the lobes become smooth, the holdfast organelle forms, and the trophic cell extends its lobes out of the lorica and commences feeding.

Initially, the new lorica is thin and transparent, but it gradually thickens and acquires a green hue as it accrues a substantial quantity of detritus and microalgae on its surface. In certain instances, the neck undergoes repeated extension (Figs. 2G, 3H). Additionally, observations revealed a high incidence of loricae inhabited by rotifers, with some individuals observed depositing eggs within the confines of the lorica (Figs. 2E, 3N, O). Furthermore, a considerable number of Loxophyllum salinum Wu et al., 2013 individuals were observed preying upon the folliculinids within their loricae (Figs. 2F, 3M).

(1683 bp, 46.82%, PV920936) (Fig. 7A). The maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) analyses based on the SSU rDNA sequences generated phylogenetic trees with similar topologies. Consequently, only the ML tree with support values from both analyses is presented in Fig. 8A, while the BI tree is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1 to display where its topology differs from that of the ML tree. The newly obtained sequence of M. songi n. sp. together with 12 sequences sourced from the GenBank database form a well-supported clade corresponding to the family Folliculinidae (ML 85%, BI 1.00). The family is sister to Maristentor dinoferus, the only member of the family Maristentoridae, also with strong support (ML 98%, BI 1.00). Within the family Folliculinidae, the 12 GenBank sequences, representing eight species, exhibit 25–74 unmatched nucleotides compared to M. songi n. sp. (Fig. 7).

The family Folliculinidae is divided into two subclades: subclades I and II. Subclade I includes Metafolliculina and Eufolliculina. In the ML tree (Fig. 8A), M. songi n. sp. is the first branch to emerge, with the other three sequences of Metafolliculina demonstrating strong support for their grouping (ML 96%) and subsequently forming a cluster with two sequences of Eufolliculina (ML 100%). In contrast, in the BI tree (Supplementary Fig. S1), M. songi n. sp., the other three sequences of Metafolliculina, and the two sequences of Eufolliculina diverged simultaneously, giving rise to three distinct branches (BI 1.00). In contrast, the hypothesis that Metafolliculina is monophyletic is not rejected by the AU test (P = 0.278). Subclade II comprises three genera: Folliculina, Ampullofolliculina, and Diafolliculina. The four sequences of Ampullofolliculina lageniformis and Diafolliculina longilobata are closely related, forming a sister group to the two sequences of Folliculina with strong support (ML 98%, BI 1.00) (Fig. 8A).

Discussion

Comments on Metafolliculina songi n. sp.

The genus Metafolliculina was established by Dons (1924), who later revised its diagnosis in 1934 as: “mit Rosenkranzkern, Wohnraum schmal, Hals sehr lang, mit kräftiger Spiralverdickung” [Translation: “macronucleus moniliform, with a narrow flask-like structure and very long neck, which is strongly spiral-covered”] (Dons 1934). Ye et al. (2022) conducted a comprehensive review of the family Folliculinidae and further revised the diagnosis of Metafolliculina as follows: flask single-chambered, recumbent or vertical; neck long and sculptured, with prominent spiral or annular ridges; without an closure device; peristomal lobes long and slender, equal in size; holdfast organelle slim; macronucleus moniliform. Prior to the present study, Metafolliculina comprises five valid species, namely M. nordgardi, M. ballerina Mulisch et al., 1986, M. elongata (Das, 1949) Hadži, 1951, M. andrewsi Hadži, 1938, and M. producta (Wright, 1859) Dons, 1934.

The morphological features of our isolate closely correspond to the diagnoses of Metafolliculina supplied by Dons (1934) and Ye et al. (2022), confirming its placement within this genus. However, it should be recognized as a new species based on its clear morphological distinguishability from congeners. Specifically, Metafolliculina nordgardi and M. ballerina differ from M. songi n. sp. in possessing an upright (vs. recumbent) flask (Dons 1924; Mulisch et al. 1986) (Fig. 8A–C; Supplementary Table S1). Metafolliculina andrewsi has more ciliary rows (ca. 100 vs. 51–76) and macronuclear nodules (up to 30 vs. 9–19) than M. songi n. sp. (Andrews 1952; Hadži 1938) (Fig. 8A, E; Supplementary Table S1). Additionally, M. elongata has a longer lorica neck (1000–1500 μm vs. 140–330 μm) and a larger trophont size in vivo (2000–3000 μm vs. 650–690 μm) than M. songi n. sp. (Das 1949) (Fig. 8A, D; Supplementary Table S1). The neck of M. producta is also longer than that of M. songi n. sp. (100–600 μm vs. 110–160 μm) (Andrews 1921; Das 1949; Dons 1934; Fauré-Fremiet 1936; Hu et al. 2019; Ji et al. 2004; Ye et al. 2021a). Moreover, M. producta also differs from M. songi n. sp. in having a slender and taut (vs. robust) posterior portion when fully extended, along with smooth (vs. wavy) peristomial lobes when contracted (Ye et al. 2021a) (Fig. 8A, F; Supplementary Table S1). Behaviorally, M. producta is sensitive to light and susceptible to damage under strong illumination (Ye et al. 2021a), whereas M. songi n. sp. shows no such phenomena. Genetically, the SSU rDNA sequences of these two species differ by 25 nucleotides (Fig. 7B).

Phylogeny of Metafolliculina songi n. sp.

The class Heterotrichea comprises ten families, among which Folliculinidae contains the largest diversity, with 33 genera and 81 valid species (Chi et al. 2021; Ye et al. 2022). Nevertheless, molecular data are currently available for only five genera, namely Metafolliculina, Eufolliculina, Folliculina, Diafolliculina, and Ampullofolliculina (Fig. 7; Supplementary Fig. S1). Previous phylogenetic studies consistently recovered Metafolliculina and Eufolliculina as sister groups that form an independent, well-supported branch within Folliculinidae (Chi et al. 2020; Hao et al. 2022; Ye et al. 2021a, b, 2022). In contrast, after incorporating the sequence of M. songi n. sp., the sister relationship of these two genera is no longer supported. The newly added Metafolliculina sequence does not cluster with its congeners but instead falls outside the previously recognized Metafolliculina + Eufolliculina clade, rendering Metafolliculina non-monophyletic in the present analyses.

Morphologically, Metafolliculina and Eufolliculina share several diagnostic characters, including highly flexible peristomial lobes, slim holdfast organelle, moniliform macronucleus, sculptured neck, and the absence of closure devices, which may contribute to the blurred boundaries between the two genera. More importantly, molecular data are available for only seven of the 81 recognized folliculinid species, severely limiting the establishment of a robust phylogenetic framework for the family. In addition, the result of the AU test does not reject the monophyly of Metafolliculina (P = 0.278), further indicating that the current SSU rDNA tree topology should be interpreted with caution. Therefore, future studies necessitate additional molecular data from broader taxon sampling to better clarify the phylogenetic relationships within Folliculinidae.

Geographical records of the genus Metafolliculina

The current global record patterns of Metafolliculina species are based primarily on historical accounts, which rely heavily on morphological descriptions and recognizable illustrations (Das 1949; Dons 1924, 1934; Hadži 1938, 1951; Ji et al. 2004; Mulisch et al. 1986; Wright 1859; Ye et al. 2021a). The known distribution records of the six Metafolliculina species are limited, with occurrences reported from only three countries and one sea. Notably, Metafolliculina nordgardi, M. ballerina, M. elongata, and M. songi n. sp. appear to be extremely rare, with each species documented only once. Specifically, M. nordgardi was discovered attached to Rocinela sp. collected from a coastal region of Norway. Metafolliculina songi n. sp. was found attached to natural and artificial substrates in a brackish coastal wetland in Ningbo, China. Metafolliculina ballerina and M. elongata were found only once each in the United Kingdom (UK): M. ballerina attached to Chlamys shells from a coastal port in Northern Ireland, and M. elongata on the tests of Ciona intestinalis and various algae from Cullercoats on the NE coast of England (Das 1949; Dons 1924; Mulisch et al. 1986). In contrast, M. andrewsi and M. producta exhibit comparatively wider distributions. Metafolliculina andrewsi has been recorded from collections in the UK (on dead Pecten shells and the test of Ascidiella aspersa from Plymouth and Cullercoats) and the Mediterranean Sea (attached to Zoobotryum pellucidum from the port of Seget near Split). Nevertheless, the current understanding of the geographical distribution of Metafolliculina remains limited, relying primarily on sporadic collections. Research to date has been confined to Europe, Asia, and the Mediterranean, leaving vast regions such as the Americas, Africa, Oceania, and Antarctica entirely unexplored. Therefore, expanding surveys to these undersampled areas is crucial to accurately delineate the full geographic range of Metafolliculina and uncover the drivers of its distribution.
