Risk factors for HIV-associated neurocognitive impairment and frailty in aging people with HIV in Latin America: a systematic review
Authors: Marleny Nolasco, Shriya S Pokala, Laura M Saavedra Forero, Julio Cuevas, Jacqueline M Koble, Jamie L Conklin, Monica M Diaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs people living with HIV (PWH) in Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC) have longer life expectancy due to improved access to antiretroviral therapy (ART), complications such as HIV-associated neurocognitive impairment (NCI; including HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder [HAND]) and frailty are increasingly more prevalent. We aimed to identify the risk factors for neurocognitive impairment and frailty among aging PWH in LAC.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review following PRISMA guidelines. We searched five databases that reported on NCI, HAND or frailty in PWH in LAC. Eligible studies included observational studies, clinical trials or case series. Data were extracted on study characteristics, diagnostic tools utilized, prevalence estimates (when available) and associated risk factors of each condition of interest.ResultsA total of 83 studies met inclusion criteria: 71 focused on NCI (including HAND) and 14 on frailty (2 on both NCI and frailty). NCI and HAND prevalence ranged from 11% to 89%, with most studies using brief cognitive screening tools (NCI) rather than formal neuropsychological testing (HAND). Risk factors for both NCI included older age, lower education, female sex, low CD4 counts and psychosocial factors. ART use did not consistently protect against NCI. Only a small subset of studies employed complete neuropsychological test batteries, imaging or biomarker analysis. Frailty studies, primarily from Brazil, reported prevalence ranging from 8% to 50%. Frailty risk factors included older age, female sex, depression, low CD4 counts, malnutrition, and unemployment. Some studies reported no or inconsistent associations with traditional HIV characteristics.ConclusionsNCI and frailty are common and underrecognized conditions affecting aging PWH in LAC. Region-specific diagnostic approaches and targeted interventions are urgently needed to improve cognitive and functional health outcomes in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04767-y.Keywords: HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder, Frailty, Aging, HIV, Latin America, Caribbean, Sarcopenia

Background

In 2023, there were 2.3 million people in Latin America and 340,000 people in the Caribbean living with HIV [1]. Despite current preventative measures, new HIV infections are steadily increasing in Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC). Globally, the population of people living with HIV (PWH) is aging with 8.1 million aged 50 years or older in 2022 and continuing to increase. In LAC, projections suggest that PWH aged 45 years or older will increase from 38% in 2020 to approximately 50% by 2030 [2]. However, addressing comorbid conditions associated with aging with HIV is still needed given aging of PWH in the region.

Access to antiretroviral therapy (ART) has allowed people living with HIV (PWH) to live longer. AIDS-related deaths have decreased significantly by 6% between 2010 and 2023 in LAC, respectively. [3] PWH in LAC on ART are reaching the same life expectancy of the general population in the respective region [3], which may lead to substantial health challenges, including neurocognitive impairment (NCI) and HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder (HAND) or frailty, two common complications of the accelerated aging process in HIV.

Frailty has multiple definitions. The most common definition of frailty phenotype defines frailty with criteria that include unintentional weight loss, weakness, exhaustion, slowness and low activity, focusing mostly on the physical domain [4]. In contrast, the frailty index/deficit-accumulation model considers frailty as the accumulation of deficits across physical, cognitive, emotional, social and functional domains. [5] It was originally investigated in people over the age of 65, but frailty is known to begin at earlier ages in PWH, highlighting the need to begin frailty assessments in earlier ages in PWH [5, 6]. However, differing definitions of frailty make it difficult to compare frailty across studies. Frailty has been associated with a greater risk of developing other medical comorbidities, which can impair quality of life and increase morbidity and mortality [6]. However, little is known of the risk factors impacting frailty in PWH in LAC.

In PWH, aging is often accompanied by a range of manifestations beyond frailty alone, including sarcopenia, characterized by loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength [7]. Sarcopenia has been shown to be closely related to frailty, disability, and worse outcomes in HIV. In men living with HIV [8], sarcopenia was independently associated with increased odds of frailty and highlighting the importance to investigate sarcopenia and frailty.

NCI is a known complication of HIV that may develop as PWH age and can be screened for using brief cognitive screening tools. HAND is a type of NCI that can be diagnosed with formal neuropsychological testing using published criteria [9]. HAND can vary in severity manifesting as three distinct subtypes: asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment (ANI; neuropsychological testing reveals cognitive impairment in at least two cognitive domains, but independent activities of daily living [IADLs] remain intact), mild neurocognitive disorder (MND; neuropsychological test impairment in at least two cognitive domains plus mild but detectable impairment in IADLs) or HIV-associated dementia (HAD; performance ≥ 2 standard deviations below demographically-appropriate normative means in at least two cognitive domains with marked impairment in IADLs), based on a battery of neuropsychological tests and not by brief cognitive screening tests [9]. HAND is typically characterized by impairment in executive functioning, speed of information processing and fine motor skills. Despite the impact of HAND, no studies have synthesized the risk factors for neurocognitive impairment across all of LAC.

As the prevalence of HIV continues to increase in LA, as well as life expectancy of PWH, with increased access to treatment in LAC, understanding the prevalence of neurocognitive impairment and frailty is crucial in the region to better tailor healthcare services for this population. Therefore, in this systematic review, we aimed to (1) identify the most common risk factors for NCI (when screening tool was applied) and HAND (when formal neuropsychological testing was applied), and (2) to identify the most common risk factorws of frailty among PWH in LAC.

Methods

We followed the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statements [10]. We did not register the protocol prior to completing our search.

Data source and searches

We searched the following six databases from their dates of inception through the last search date of June 15, 2023: APA PsycInfo (EBSCOhost), Embase (Elsevier), Global Health (EBSCOhost), Latin America and the Caribbean Literature on Health Sciences (World Health Organization), PubMed (National Library of Medicine), and Scopus (Elsevier) and SciELO.

The search strategy included a combination of keywords and subject headings on three main concepts: LAC, HIV, and NCI/HAND/frailty/sarcopenia. We considered NCI and HAND together. We analyzed sarcopenia as a separate entity from frailty given the impact that sarcopenia is known to have on frailty. While all databases must have included LAC and HIV concepts, the search results could have included either NCI or HAND or frailty or sarcopenia terms. No language or date limits were applied to the search; however, conference proceedings were removed from the Embase search. The complete, reproducible search strategy for all databases is available in the supplementary files.

Study selection

We used Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) for screening and data extraction. Four independent screeners (MMD, SSP, JC, and LMS) performed a title/abstract review, and MMD resolved any conflicts between two reviewers. All studies that met inclusion criteria at the title/abstract screening were then reviewed in full text by four independent screeners (MMD, SSP, JC, and LMS) with MMD to resolve conflicts between two reviewers.

Studies were included if they met the following criteria (i) discussed NCI or HAND or HIV-associated dementia, sarcopenia or frailty (we included any study that addressed frailty or sarcopenia irrespective of frailty or sarcopenia definition utilized in the paper); (ii) included people living with HIV; (iii) took place in a LAC country; and (iv) was an observational research study (including qualitative studies) or case series (>5 patients) or a clinical trial.

Eligible LAC countries included (i) Central American countries: Costa Rica, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, Nicaragua, and Panama; (ii) Spanish-speaking or Latin Caribbean countries: Cuba, Puerto Rico, and Dominican Republic; (iii) South American Southern Cone countries: Argentina, Chile, Paraguay, and Uruguay; (iv) South American Andean countries: Bolivia, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, and Venezuela; (v) Mexico; and (vi) Brazil. We excluded studies that were (i) not in English, Spanish or Portuguese; (ii) case reports, conference abstracts, opinion/commentary articles; or (iii) did not mention risk factors for NCI/HAND/HIV-associated dementia, sarcopenia or frailty.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Four reviewers extracted data independently (MMD, SSP, JC, and LMS). The data was compared, and any conflicts were resolved by reviewer MMD. The data that were extracted from each eligible paper included study characteristics, country, sample size, HIV characteristics (i.e., recent absolute CD4 count, duration of HIV infection, HIV plasma viral load), reported prevalence of the condition (if the prevalence was reported; prevalence was not required as an inclusion criteria) and risk factors that were or were not related to the condition were noted. Because the included studies were heterogeneous in design, measures and reporting, a formal meta-analysis was not feasible. Instead, we adopted a structured synthesis approach: for each outcome domain (frailty, neurocognitive impairment, HAND) we counted the number of studies reporting a positive association, no significant association, or negative association with the risk factor measured (Table 1).

Among the 148 participants studied, a notable 69.6% (103 patients) exhibited cognitive impairment as assessed by the International HIV Dementia Scale (IHDS). This significant prevalence underscores the commonality and impact of cognitive challenges within this patient population.

The study identified a significant association between female sex and higher rates of cognitive impairment. Females had an increased risk compared to males with an odds ratio (OR) of 3.5 before adjustment (95% CI 1.5‚Äì8; p < 0.01) and an OR of 2.8 after adjustment for educational status and illicit drug use (95% CI 1.2‚Äì6.8; p = 0.02). This finding highlights a gender-specific vulnerability within the HIV-infected population.

Education level significantly influenced cognitive outcomes. Patients who had completed secondary education had a much lower risk of cognitive impairment, with an adjusted OR of 0.1 (95% CI 0.1‚Äì0.4; p < 0.01). Furthermore, full years of schooling were positively correlated with higher IHDS scores, indicating that education could be a protective factor against cognitive decline.

Variables such as age, employment status, alcohol and tobacco use, syphilis diagnosis, time since HIV diagnosis, recent CD4+ cell counts, and viral load did not show significant associations with cognitive impairment in this study. This suggests that factors traditionally considered in HIV-associated cognitive decline may not always predict cognitive outcomes, emphasizing the need for a broader understanding of other potential influences.

Results

The search returned 2,165 results, and after removing duplicates, 1,399 studies were screened. Of these, 1,115 studies were excluded based on the title and abstract review, and 284 full texts were assessed for eligibility. Of those, 197 studies were excluded. A total of 83 articles met the inclusion criteria and were included in this study (Fig. 1). Two of the articles were in Portuguese, 11 were in Spanish, and the remaining were in English. All studies that were included in this systematic review were found to be of low risk of bias.

NCI or HAND

71 studies reported NCI or HAND risk factors in LAC countries (Table 1). Supplemental Table 1 summarizes the NCI risk factors, prevalence (when reported), demographics reported in the studies and main points of the studies when cognitive screening tool was used, while Supplemental Table II summarizes those of HAND when formal NP testing was used. The majority of the studies took place in Brazil and Puerto Rico with a total of 32 studies and 17 studies being from these countries, respectively (Fig. 2). Supplemental Fig. I demonstrates the number of studies included on NCI risk factors and Supplemental Fig. II includes those on HAND. A majority [79] of the articles were published in English. 9 were published in Spanish and only 2 in Portuguese. The earliest published article comes from 2003, while the most recent was published in 2023. The total sample size ranged from 8 to 501 participants. The mean age of these samples ranged from 21.8 to 71 years. The percentage of females was 100% in 15 studies [33, 60–67, 69–71, 75, 76] and 100% males in two of the studies [45, 46].

Neurocognitive impairment and HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder articles identified per country

Of the NCI/HAND studies, there were only 9 studies that focused on aging with populations older than 40 years of age (4 studies) or 50 years of age (5 studies). In those studies that reported NCI or HAND prevalence, NCI prevalence ranged widely from 11% to 89.3% with 21 of the studies reporting cognitive deficits in over half of participants (Supplemental Table II) [17, 54]. The most common diagnostic tools used to screen for HIV-associated NCI were International HIV Dementia Scale (IHDS), Mini Mental State Exam (MMSE), and European AIDS Clinical Society (EACS) cognitive impairment screening questionnaire. In those studies that reported HAND prevalence, HAND prevalence ranged from 4.6% to 77% (Supplemental Table I) [76, 80]. ANI was most commonly identified in five articles, followed by MND in four articles and HAD in eight articles. These individuals most experienced deficits in attention, working memory, psychomotor speed, executive function, and memory.

The most commonly reported risk factors of NCI or HAND were older age, lower education level, being female, low CD4+ counts, and biomarkers of inflammation and neuronal injury (Table 1). Notably, low nadir and absolute CD4 counts were frequently associated with neurocognitive impairment, highlighting the importance of maintaining a normal CD4 count to preserve neurocognitive health among PWH in 6 studies [41, 46, 49, 81–83]. Depression also was significantly associated with neurocognitive impairment in 7 studies [15, 17, 39, 48, 57, 77, 84]. However, there was no significant association between depressive symptoms and cognitive performance in 3 studies [46, 85], highlighting the heterogeneity of neurocognitive impairment risk factors across the region. For NCI only, the most commonly reported risk factors were CD4 count, gender, education level, and other biomarkers of inflammation (Supplemental Table I). For HAND only, the most commonly reported risk factors were also CD4 count, gender, education level, along with other comorbidities and co-infections (Supplemental Table II).

Seven studies explored biomarkers and pathophysiological mechanisms of NCI or HAND (Table 1). Elevations of inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-2, neuronal injury markers (cathepsin B, cystatin B, neurofilament light protein), and insulin signaling pathways (soluble insulin receptor, IRS-1) were studied [60–63, 65, 69, 72], suggesting potential biological mechanisms underlying NCI or HAND in this region. However, few of these biomarkers demonstrated consistent predictive risk associated with NCI or HAND. Four of the studies utilized neuroimaging, including MRI and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) to study PWH with NCI [20, 23, 86, 87]. Two studies compared PWH with neurological symptoms to those without neurological symptoms. Both studies found that individuals with neurological symptoms had significantly higher HIV viral loads in both plasma and CSF, lower CD4+ counts and were less likely to have received antiretroviral treatment or HIV outpatient care [20, 74]. Another study compared PWH without neurological symptoms to HIV-negative healthy controls, reporting no significant cortical atrophy or major structural brain changes among the HIV-positive group [86]. However, subtle differences in white matter tract integrity were observed [86]. Additionally, one study focused on patients with HIV-associated dementia only and found widespread cortical atrophy, particularly in the frontal regions, along with ventriculomegaly [87]. These patients exhibited deficits in attention, working memory, and executive function, and frequently displayed psychiatric symptoms [87].

Social determinants of health were found to be significant risk factors for NCI or HAND prevalence and worse severity of HAND. Eleven studies highlighted the role of low education, female sex, older age, and socioeconomic vulnerability, such as unemployment and limited healthcare access, as important contextual factors influencing cognitive outcomes [11, 13, 15, 31, 34, 37, 54, 77, 81, 82, 84].

However, 16 studies found no statistically significant risk factors that increased the risk of NCI or HAND [15, 19, 21, 24, 25, 28, 29, 58, 59, 69, 75, 76, 79, 84, 86, 88, 89]. For example, studies found no significant associations between NCI and APOE ε4 genotype [19], ART adherence [21], CSF viral load [21], or traditional demographic variables like age, education or depressive symptoms [59, 76, 79]. There was no consistent pattern in the relationship between ART and NCI nor HAND. Even in samples where ART adherence was high and viral loads were lower, ART adherence was not protective of NCI nor HAND. However, one study did find improvements in specific cognitive domains, such as psychomotor speed, after the initiation of ART [58]. Similarly, biomarkers, such as CSF neurofilament light protein (NfL) and β2-microglobulin, showed limited diagnostic utility or inconsistent correlations with neurocognitive impairment [26, 27].

Frailty or sarcopenia

A total of 15 studies assessed the prevalence and risk factors of frailty or sarcopenia (Fig. 3; Table 2). Of these 15, the majority [60] took place in Brazil. All but two article was in English [98, 99]. The date of publication ranged from 2010 to 2023, with 7 of them published on or after 2020 [80, 90, 92, 93, 100–102]. Sample sizes for these studies ranged from 11, [103] to 501, [55] participants. The mean age across these studies ranged from 41 to 64 [102, 103]. These studies varied in sex distribution with the percent female ranging from 0% to 55% [98, 103].

Studies reporting frailty risk factors among people with HIV in Latin America

Frailty and sarcopenia among PWH were assessed using a range of tools, including handgrip strength [80, 103], calf circumference [100], bone densitometry via Dual-Energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DEXA) scans [98, 99], and clinical frailty or sarcopenia diagnostic criteria [55, 92, 93, 95, 102]. Three studies included comparisons between PWH and HIV-negative control participants [95, 99, 101], and four studies compared subgroups of PWH based on frailty status, comorbidities or cognitive impairment [55, 56, 92, 102].

Across studies, 11 reported a positive association with one or more risk factors, 3 reported null findings, and 1 reported a negative or inverse finding. Across sarcopenia‐related studies (n=8) 5 reported positive associations, 2 were null, and none reported negative associations. Common risk factors that increased the risk of frailty included older age, female sex, low current or nadir CD4 count, unemployment, depression, malnutrition, and multimorbidity. Specific findings included associations between frailty and low recent absolute CD4 count, low nadir CD4 count or detectable HIV viral load [93], as well as lower current absolute CD4 counts among frail or prefrail PWH compared to non-frail PWH [55]. Lower nadir CD4 count was also associated with MND and prefrailty [56], while a higher CD4/CD8 ratio was protective against low handgrip strength [80]. Depression was associated with both frailty and pre-frailty in two studies [90, 93], while pain and reduced quality of life were linked to frailty [97]. Social determinants including low income, unemployment or being widowed/divorced were also significantly associated with frailty in four studies [80, 90, 93, 97]. Biomarkers, such as vitamin D deficiency and altered bone mineral density, were reported in two studies [98, 99], and vertebral fractures and sarcopenia were more prevalent in PWH compared with HIV-negative controls [95, 100].

Notably, several studies reported no significant risk factors or unexpected findings. Sarcopenia was not associated with typical HIV-related characteristics. Only age, diabetes, and malnutrition were found to be significantly associated with sarcopenia [102]. No significant associations were found between CD4 count or viral load and bone mass density alterations [99]. Additionally, while one study found PWH were initially weaker than HIV-negative controls, a resistance training program improved their strength without affecting body composition or bone mass [103].

Discussion

This study synthesized the peer-reviewed literature on the risk factors for NCI, HAND and frailty among aging PWH in LAC, which has not been previously described across LAC countries. Our findings highlight the significant burden of NCI, HAND and frailty in this region, and adverse multifactorial risk profiles for both NCI/HAND and frailty in the region highlighted in our study.

The high variability in reported NCI/HAND prevalence (11–89%) is consistent with global patterns [104], ranging between 20–50% in virally suppressed cohorts, but highlights methodological heterogeneity across studies. Most studies utilized brief cognitive screening tools, such as the IHDS or MMSE, which are known to have limited sensitivity and specificity for detecting milder forms of NCI and does not allow for diagnosis of HAND [105, 106]. Few studies utilized comprehensive neuropsychological batteries nor functional assessments (Table 1). The gold standard for HAND diagnosis based on the established Frascati criteria is a complete neuropsychological battery [9, 107]. Therefore, without the application of a complete neuropsychological battery evaluating seven cognitive domains typically involved in HAND, the diagnosis of HAND is difficult to make reliably [108]. Furthermore, several studies lacked standardized criteria for HAND diagnosis, limiting comparisons across studies and across the region. Recently, strategies to harmonize and standardize criteria for diagnosis of HAND have been proposed and are currently being developed [109, 110].

Consistent with literature from high-income countries, common risk factors for NCI and HAND included older age, female sex, low educational attainment and lower nadir or current absolute CD4 counts in our study [55, 56, 80, 93]. However, expected protective factors such as ART adherence and viral suppression did not consistently correlate with lower NCI prevalence, highlighting the persistence of NCI or HAND despite effective systemic HIV control. Several studies identified inflammatory and neuronal injury biomarkers (i.e. cathepsin B, NfL) were associated with NCI or HAND [62], though findings were inconsistent and often based on small samples. These findings suggest that NCI or HAND may not be reliably associated or predicted by any single clinical or biological factor and instead likely reflects a multifactorial pathophysiologic process leading to cognitive impairment (Supplemental Tables I and II).

Frailty prevalence also varied widely, up to 50% in one cohort, and was consistently associated with aging, low absolute CD4 counts, malnutrition, depression or unemployment across several studies. Notably, the definition of frailty that were utilized were heterogeneous (although the majority applied the Fried criteria) limiting comparisons across studies and highlighting the importance of harmonizing the definition of frailty utilized for PWH. These findings suggest that frailty may be caused by a multifactorial process, including physical decline, immune dysfunction and/or adverse psychosocial factors. However, only a small number of studies examined frailty using validated geriatric assessment tools, such as the Fried criteria [4]. Moreover, no study analyzed longitudinal outcomes of frailty, and all studies were cross-sectional limiting the prognostic information that can be ascertained from these studies.

Frailty risk in LAC may be influenced by specific regional factors, such as diet, environmental exposures, adverse social determinants of health or physical health, which were not studied in any study we found. These factors need further investigation in LAC to develop interventions to help reduce these risk factors at a societal level. Social determinants of health, including poverty and limited healthcare access, were found to be important risk factors for both neurocognitive impairment and frailty. Our findings highlight the need for comprehensive HIV care models that integrate cognitive and frailty screening, psychosocial support and targeted geriatric care in HIV or infectious diseases clinics embedded within Ministries of Health of LAC.

Our study has limitations worth noting. First, our review is limited by heterogeneity across studies in diagnostic criteria of both NCI, HAND and frailty and by various study designs. Many studies used convenience samples from urban tertiary centers, limiting generalizability to more rural areas across LAC. Few included longitudinal follow-up or validated biomarkers, and only a subset considered mental health comorbidities or structural risk factors. An important limitation is the absence of a universally accepted definition of frailty. The choice of frailty construct (phenotype vs index) may affect prevalence and risk factor associations. Therefore, harmonizing the definitions of frailty utilized in HIV would help compare across studies. Next, we did not complete a meta-analysis given the heterogeneity of the methodology utilized in each study. However, our study presents a summary of the peer-reviewed literature that has investigated neurocognitive impairment and frailty risk factors across the LAC region, an area that has not been studied across the region.

Conclusions

There is a critical need for standardized, culturally appropriate diagnostic protocols for NCI screening and HAND and frailty diagnosis in LAC. Future research should incorporate longitudinal designs, validated complete neuropsychological batteries and frailty indices that may be incorporated into the primary care setting. Integrating assessments of mental health and social determinants into HIV aging research in LAC will be essential to inform interventions and public health initiatives in the region to help mitigate NCI/HAND and frailty incidence as PWH age.
