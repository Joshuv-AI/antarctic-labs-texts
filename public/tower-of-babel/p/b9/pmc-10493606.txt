Hub genes in adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction based on weighted gene co-expression network analysis and immunohistochemistry
Authors: Zhiyong Lai, Zhongyuan Bai, Shuzhe Yang, Rui Zhang, Yanfeng Xi, Jun Xu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlights•We determined the CD93, TRIM28, SLC3A2, CBX4, PATL1 as hub genes of AEG by WGCNA.•What's more, we verified the CD93, TRIM28, SLC3A2, CBX4, PATL1, IGFBP7 in AEG tissue and normal tissue by immunohistochemical analysis.•The results indicated that the expression of CD93 is closely related to tumor T staging and tumor diameter, suggesting that CD93 plays an important role in the growth and development of tumor blood vessels.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a common malignant tumor of the digestive tract with the fifth-highest incidence and the third leading cause of cancer death worldwide [1]. According to the National Central Cancer Registry, there were 697,100 new GC cases and 498,000 GC deaths in China in 2015 alone [2]. Although the overall incidence of GC worldwide has decreased in recent decades, the incidence of adenocarcinoma of the esophagogastric junction (AEG, a GC subtype) has increased significantly in Western and East Asian countries [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]. In China, especially with a high incidence of traditional esophageal cancer, new AEG cases account for about 50% of all GC cases [6].

According to Siewert's classification, AEG can be classified into Types I, II, and III. Regardless of the anatomical location of the tumor, the three types of AEG are similar in epidemiology, morphology, and other aspects; however, there are significant differences in pathogenesis [4,9,10]. It is not clear whether this classification reflects the pathogenesis. Next-generation sequencing was adopted in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and other projects to analyze different cancer types' genomic and RNA profile changes, including GC [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]. A whole-exome sequencing study showed that Siewert Types I and II/III display distinct driver genes, mutational spectra, and recurrently disrupted pathways [16]. The goal of cancer genome mapping and tumor RNA profiling is precision medicine, including adjusting treatments for individual patients [17].

Genomic sequencing and transcriptome RNA sequencing have the potential to achieve precision medicine for cancer patients. To understand the potential race and geographic differences and the applicability of RNA-seq to Chinese populations, we used RNA sequencing technology to characterize clinically actionable driver events in 22 patients with AEG in Shanxi Province of China.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis and immunohistochemistry (WGCNA) assesses correlations between specific genes across a range of samples, generating similarity-based gene networks that allow for a global assessment of gene expression patterns, and helps us identify hub genes [18], [19], [20], [21]. The WGCNA approach has not been applied to AEG RNA-seq-based gene expression data. Therefore, in this study, our goal was to build co-expression modules from AEG RNA-seq data. We identified co-expressed gene modules specific to clinical tumor markers using this approach. The Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment, and co-expression network analyses were performed for the modules of interest. The hub genes were recognized, which helped identify candidate hub genes' potential biological functions. The expression of hub genes was verified using immunohistochemistry, and the correlations between expression and clinicopathological features and outcomes were analyzed. These findings might provide a set of genes that indicate malignant potential and AEG outcomes and verify hub genes' expression and clinical functions.

Methods

RNA-seq data of AEG samples

Twenty-two AEG tumor tissue samples and paired normal gastric mucosa samples were obtained from the Department of General Surgery, Shanxi Cancer Hospital (Table S1). All tissue donors included provided written informed consent. The Shanxi Medical University (China) Human Ethics Committee approved the study. Methods were carried out following guidelines.

The samples were quick-frozen in liquid nitrogen after resection and were stored at –80 °C until RNA extraction. RNA integrity was assessed via rRNA bands using 1.0% formaldehyde denaturing gel electrophoresis. Only RNA samples with 1.8 < A260/A280 < 2.0 and 28S/18S rRNA > 2 were used for subsequent experiments. AEG diagnoses were confirmed pathologically. First, we measured expression levels of RNA transcripts using RNA-seq analysis from 22 paired normal and cancerous AEG tissues. Each sample was sequenced (200 ×) on the Illumina HiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA). RNA-seq was conducted in Shenzhen, China's BGI gene Technology Corporation laboratory.

WGCNA of AEG RNA-seq data

The RNA co-expression networks were constructed using the R language package's WGCNA program kit [18]. First, the square Euclidean distance of each sample was calculated by the function adjacency method, and the connectivity of the total sample network calculated by the distance was standardized by the function scale [22]. Cluster analysis was performed using FLASH CLUST software. We chose β = 11 as the soft-thresholding power to build a scale-free network, resulting in a scale-free topology index of 0.8. Novel Bioinformatics Ltd., Co supported the NovelBio Cloud Analysis Platform.

Identifying gene modules associated with tumor markers

The relationship between gene module characteristics and serum tumor marker measurements was used to estimate the relationship between modules and tumor markers to identify modules highly correlated with specific phenotypes. The gene significance was determined by the absolute value of the correlation between the RNA expression profile and each trait, and the module membership (MM) was determined by the correlation between the RNA expression profile and the eigengene of each module [23]. The correlations between module eigengenes and clinical tumor markers (CEA, CA199, CA242, AFP, CA724, CA50, SCC, TPA, TPS, and VEGF) were calculated using Pearson's correlation tests.

KEGG pathway and GO analysis

To investigate the biological functions and pathways of genes in modules, the GO and KEGG pathways were annotated and visualized using the cluster analyzer in R language [24]. The differentially-expressed RNAs with P < 0.05 and fold-change > 2 were used to annotate the GO terms and KEGG pathway analyses using DAVID. The GO items were divided into biological processes, cell components, and molecular functions. Biological processes are the most important in diseases. The differentially-expressed genes were subjected to pathway analysis based on the KEGG database. The significant GO terms and pathways were identified using Fisher's exact tests, and the results were filtered for enriched GO terms and pathways using a false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected P < 0.01. The KEGG pathway and GO analyses were performed using the NovelBio Cloud Analysis Platform.

The hub genes in modules

In WGCNA, the expression value of the whole gene in the module is generally standardized, and the values are assigned values between -1 and 1, which facilitates the comparison between modules. A module engine (ME) represents the value of a module's characteristic genes [18]. For each module, there is only one ME. ME is the first principal component (PC1) calculated and analyzed based on all module gene expression data and assigned values between -1 and 1. The gene expression of modules is also standardized and assigned values between -1 and 1. KME (also known as MM) represents the relevance between gene expression and module ME and is assigned values between -1 and 1. The closer the absolute value of KME is to 1, the stronger the correlation between the gene and the module, and the closer the gene is to the module's core. Thus, the KME and its p-value can be screening indexes to identify a hub gene [21]. The calculated saliency of the hub gene with high KME was consistent with the saliency of the module. In blue and brown modules, we selected these hub genes with MM values greater than 0.8. This set used hub genes with fold-change (tumor vs. normal, up- and downregulated) of more than 120% to construct a gene co-expression network of the module visualized using Cytoscape software.

Immunohistochemistry verifies hub genes’ proteins

From January 2015 to September 2015, 116 patients who were pathologically diagnosed with AEG at Shanxi Cancer Hospital were selected. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis of the tumor and paired normal tissues was performed. A tissue microarray was prepared from all tissues. Blocks were cut into 3-μm sections used for hematoxylin and eosin staining and IHC. Hub gene protein expression in 116 AEG cases was evaluated using IHC. The slides were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies against CD93 polyclonal antibody (1:400; Invitrogen; America), KAP1 (TRIM28) polyclonal antibody (1:1000; Abcam; England), CD98 (SLC3A2) polyclonal antibody (1:150; Abcam; England), CBX4 monoclonal antibody (1:200; Abcam; England), PATL1 polyclonal antibody (1:3000; Lifespan; America), IGFBP7 monoclonal antibody (1:200; Abcam; England). SP immunohistochemical staining was performed on the Roche VENTANA BenchMark XT platform. The immunohistochemical results were assigned to two experienced pathologists for interpretation. The improved Sinicrope method was used to determine immunohistochemical results: 0 points for no staining, 1 point for light yellow, 2 points for yellow or orange, and 3 points for brown. The percentage of positive cells was based on the following criteria: 0–5% was 0 points, 5–25% was 1 point, 26%–50% was 2 points, 51%–75% was 3 points, and > 75% was 4 points. The immunohistochemistry scores were calculated by multiplying the staining intensity and the percentage of positive cells. 0 and 1 scores: negative (–); 2 and 3 scores: weakly positive (+); 4 scores: moderately positive (++); > 5 scores: strongly positive (+++).

Statistical analysis

The Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated for the module genes, and significantly related genes were selected to construct the co-expression network [25]. In the co-expression network, the degree is a major index of centrality; it reflects the significance of a gene. In short, the degree is the number of links between nodes. To understand network properties, k-cores were introduced to simplify graph topology analysis. The k-core is all genes connected to at least k other genes in the subnetwork [26]. This strategy aims to identify the core hub genes in modules.

SPSS27.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used for statistical analysis. The rank-sum test was used to analyze differences in hub gene expression between tumor tissue and paired normal tissue. The χ2 test was used to analyze the relationship between hub gene expression and clinicopathological features of AEG. The Kaplan-Meier test was used for univariate survival analysis. P-values < 0.05 indicated that the difference was statistically significant. The figures were drawn using R 4.3.0 and SPSS27.0 software.

Results

The RNA expression profiles for AEG

Cancer and normal samples were subjected to massive transcriptome RNA sequencing, and we obtained excellent sequencing depth, with an average coverage of approximately 200-fold of the human transcriptome. Next, we identified differentially-expressed RNAs between AEG tissues and normal gastric mucosa. The RNA expression profile was standardized by fragments per kilobase of exon per million fragments mapped. We detected 63,677 mRNA or non-coding RNA transcripts. To study these RNA-seq data and identify RNA transcript levels that differed between the two groups, more stringent filtering criteria were used (fold-change ≥ 2 or fold-change ≤ 0.5, FDR < 0.05). This analysis identified 3,311 mRNAs and 2,013 non-coding RNAs as differentially-expressed in AEG tumor tissue samples versus paired normal gastric mucosa samples (Fig. 1 A, B, C). The GO and KEGG enrichment analysis was performed to explore the biological functions of those differentially-expressed genes using Cluster Profiler software. A cutoff of 0.05 for the adjusted p-value was used. We selected β = 11 as the soft-thresholding power to build a scale-free network, resulting in a scale-free topology index of 0.8 (Fig. 2 B and C). Modules with clinical significance and preservation

Modules with RNA expression profile patterns and interactions with modules associated with clinical traits were identified based on the correlation between module eigengenes and clinical traits. To evaluate the clinical significance of each module, correlations between module eigengenes index and commonly-used clinical tumor marker traits (CEA, CA199, CA242, AFP, CA724, CA50, SCC, TPA, TPS, and VEGF) were calculated and analyzed (Table S2). To analyze gene-weighted co-expression, tumor markers were used as priority correlation analysis indicators. This is a reasonable strategy because tumor markers are highly reliable as numerical indicators and continuous variables when logical grouping is used for association analysis.

Six modules mostly positively correlated with clinical tumor markers, and these modules were colored purple, cyan, blue, grey60, brown, and turquoise (Figs. 3 A, B, C). The purple module was positively correlated with CEA (p < 0.05). The cyan module was positively correlated to CA199 and CA242 (p < 0.05). The blue module was positively correlated with CA724, SCC, and TPS (p < 0.05). The grey60 module was positively correlated with CA199, CA242, CA724, CA50, and VEGF (p < 0.05). The brown module was positively correlated with TPA (p < 0.05). The functional enrichment analysis in gene modules of interest

The blue and brown modules were particularly interesting. The blue module contained 325 differentially-expressed RNAs (tumor vs. normal, FDR < 0.05). These were mapped to the GO database to determine their potential biological functions. The bioinformatics results suggested that the top five enriched biological processes were the extracellular matrix organization, inflammatory response, collagen catabolic process, extracellular matrix disassembly, and cell adhesion. Differentially-expressed RNAs in the blue module were also involved in immune response, angiogenesis, positive regulation of cell proliferation, and vascular endothelial growth factor production, all related to tumorigenesis (Fig. 3D).

KEGG pathway analysis indicated that these differentially-expressed RNAs in the blue module participated in extracellular matrix-receptor interaction, the TNF signaling pathway, the NF-kappa B signaling pathway, focal adhesion, the Jak-STAT signaling pathway, and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, all of which are tumorigenesis-related signal pathways (Fig. 3E).

The brown module includes 1,207 differentially-expressed RNAs (tumor vs. normal, FDR < 0.05). Mapping these RNAs to the GO database showed cell cycle functions, cell division, and DNA replication enrichments. RNAs in this group were also involved in chromosome segregation, microtubule-based movement, and ATP catabolic process, all related to tumor cell division and replication (Fig. 3F). KEGG pathway analysis suggested that these genes participated in the p53 signaling pathway, viral carcinogenesis, pathways in cancer, and ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, all of which are tumorigenesis-related signal pathways (Fig. 3G).

In fact, we conducted GO analysis and KEGG cell signaling pathway analysis for all gene modules of interest, and the results suggested that only blue and brown gene modules were strongly correlated with tumor development. Other gene modules, such as the cyan, grey60, purple, turquoise gene modules, did not show strong associations with tumorigenesis in the GO and KEGG pathway analyses (Fig. S1- S4).

Module visualization and hub gene identification

In the blue and brown modules, 200 RNA molecules (Fig. 3H) and 157 RNA molecules (Fig. 3I) were differentially-expressed (tumor vs. normal; fold-change ≥ 1.2, fold-change ≤ 0.83; FDR < 0.05) and analyzed for inclusion in a co-expression network. Within the co-expression networks, two potentially related RNAs are connected by a string. This connection indicates a correlation between different RNAs and a potential regulatory relationship (Figs. 3H and I). The co-expression network analysis showed that one RNA might correlate with one to dozens of RNAs, suggesting an interaction between critical RNAs in AEG. CD93 expression was directly related to the remaining 199 genes in the blue module, indicating a high degree of relativity. This finding suggests CD93 could be a hub gene for AEG (Fig. 3H).

The co-expression network structure of the brown module RNAs was utterly different from that of the blue module. The solid lines represent the positive interactions between RNA molecules for each pair of RNAs, while the dashed lines represent the negative correlation interactions. High structural cohesion levels are presented in red (Fig. 3I).

Identification of candidate hub genes

The intra-module connectivity of each RNA was determined by summing the connection strengths with other module RNA molecules, and the RNAs with high intramodular connectivity are initially assumed to be intramodular hub genes [27]. CD93 was highly expressed in AEG cases in RNA-seq data as the hub gene with the highest connectivity in the blue module. These findings suggest CD93 may play an essential biological role in tumorigenesis in AEG. Therefore, CD93 can potentially be a hub gene, and further study is warranted.TRIM28, SLC3A2, CBX4, PATL1, and ZNF473 were the top hub genes with high KME values in the brown module (Table S3). Based on the statistical analysis, the functions of these genes were potentially consistent with the brown module. These five genes are also at the center of the network, indicating that they are likely to play a crucial role in the gene co-expression network.

Immunohistochemical validation of hub genes

We validated the clinical significance of hub genes using immunohistochemistry. Protein expression of all hub genes in tumor tissues was significantly higher than in normal paired tissues (Fig. 4 and S5-9). CD93 expression was concentrated in vascular endothelial cells and was strongly positive in 87 cases (75.0%) in AEG tissues and negative in 86 cases (74.1%) in normal tissues (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4). TRIM28 and CBX4 were in the tumor nucleus . The expression of TRIM28 in AEG was strongly positive in 103 cases (88.8%); in normal tissues, it was weakly positive in 60 cases (51.7%) (P < 0.001) (Fig. S5). CBX4 expression in AEG was strongly positive in 69 cases (59.4%); in normal tissues, it was negative in 97 cases (83.6%) (P < 0.001) (Fig. S6). SLC3A2, PATL1, and IGFBP7 were in the cytoplasm. SLC3A2 expression in AEG was strongly positive in 69 cases (59.4%); in normal tissues, it was negative in 62 cases (55.4%) (P < 0.001) (Fig. S7). PATL1 expression in AEG was strongly positive in 54 cases (46.6%); in normal tissues, it was negative in 89 cases (84.5%) (P < 0.001) (Fig. S8). The relationship between hub gene expressions and clinicopathological features of AEG

We included 116 patients pathologically diagnosed with AEG in Shanxi Cance Hospital from January 2015 to September 2015. There were 98 males and 18 females, 61 cases (52.6%) of poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma, 56 cases (48.2%) with tumor maximum diameter > 5 cm, 50 cases (43.5%) at T3, 53 cases (46.1%) at T4, 26 cases (22.4%) at N2, 22 cases (19.0%) at N3, and seven cases (6.0%) with M1. There were 35 cases (30.2%) with preoperative CEA > 3 µg/L and 31 cases (26.7%) of CA199 > 20 U/mL (Table 2). Statistical analysis showed that the T stage of 116 patients with AEG was significantly higher in the group with high CD93 expression (50.5% of T4) than in the group with low CD93 expression (25.0%) (χ2 = 14.860, P = 0.002). The maximum tumor diameter >5 cm group with high CD93 expression (44.8%) was significantly higher than that with low CD93 expression (3.4%) (χ2 = 7.783, P = 0.005) (Table 2). SLC3A2 high expression and low differentiation adenocarcinoma (42.2%) was significantly higher than the SLC3A2 low expression group (10.3%) (P = 0.021). The preoperative CA199 > 20 U/mL group in PATL1 high expression group (25.0%) was significantly higher than the PATL1 low expression group (1.7%) (P = 0.012). The group with high expression of IGFBP7 and CA199 > 20 U/mL (25.9%) was significantly higher than the low expression of PATL1 (0.9%) (P = 0.007). Correlation analysis of hub gene and survival

Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to evaluate the prognostic significance of hub gene expression. The RNA data of Kaplan-Meier Plotter and survival information were subjected to survival analysis, the results showed that patients with lower expression of CD93, TRIM28, SLC3A2, CBX4, PATL1, and ZNF473 had longer overall survival (Tumor vs Normal, FDR<0.05) [28] (Fig. S11). However, our immunohistochemical experiments have not found the similary results (Fig. S12). This may be due to the small sample size. The sample size should be expanded later to clarify the relationship between the indicators and outcomes. No significant difference was found in other indicators.

Discussion

The present study explored the transcriptomic landscape of RNAs in AEG and sought the most significant mRNA biomarker in AEG pathogenesis. RNA sequencing technology was used to characterize clinically actionable driver events in 22 patients with AEG. The high‐throughput platform provides a tool for medical oncology with broad clinical application prospects. However, utilizing a substantial number of genes in clinical applications is challenging.

Our study constructed a global RNA co-expression network to identify several candidate hub genes in AEG carcinogenesis. We built 25 co-expression gene modules for 40,523 RNA transcripts from 22 human AEG samples using WGCNA. Compared with other methods, WGCNA has many advantages since this strategy focuses on the relations between co-expression gene modules and clinical traits, providing convincing results of bioinformatics analysis [29]. WGCNA is based on a power-law distribution and constructing weighted networks using soft-thresholding. It can detect modules, identify hub genes, and recognize candidate genes or modules relating to external information.

RNA molecules in the same module are supposed to be functionally related. Therefore, this study aimed to identify tumorigenesis-related modules and central hub genes that may be biomarkers for AEG detection or treatment [27]. Using WGCNA, we identified six gene modules that relate to commonly-used tumor markers. We assume that the gene module, expressed closely with tumor markers, enriches shared functional annotations. It may provide the best-predicted candidate gene set to be the basis for a specific biological process. The structure of AEG RNA transcriptomes was analyzed by WGCNA method, and co-expression modules were constructed. The correlation between modular characteristic gene index and common clinical tumor markers was calculated. We selected two modules, blue and brown, as our main research modules, and conducted GO and KEGG analysis on them. According to the co-expression network, CD93 was identified as the hub gene of blue module and TRIM28, SLC3A2, CBX4, PATL1, and ZNF473 were identified as the hub gene of brown module. Then, hub genes were verified by immunohistochemistry and the correlation between hub genes and clinical clinicopathological features of AEG was found.

According to a correlation study by module-trait associations (Fig. 3 A, B, C), modules purple, cyan, blue, grey60, brown, and turquoise were positively correlated with clinical tumor markers. We next performed GO and KEGG pathways analyses to obtain insight into the potential functions of the genes in modules. Genes in the blue and brown modules were involved in immune response, angiogenesis, positive regulation of cell proliferation, chromosome segregation, microtubule-based movement, ATP catabolic process, and vascular endothelial growth factor production; these activities are related to cancer (Fig. 3 D, E, F, G). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis suggested these genes participate in extracellular matrix-receptor interaction, the Jak-STAT signaling pathway, focal adhesion, the TNF signaling pathway, the p53 signaling pathway, viral carcinogenesis, ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, the NF-kappa B signaling pathway, and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. These are all cancer-related signal pathways (Fig. 3 D, E, F, G). WGCNA and correlation analysis by module-trait associations allowed identifying the blue and brown modules as candidate hub gene sets.

CD93 is a cell surface glycoprotein initially identified as a myeloid cell-specific marker. It is now thought to participate in intercellular adhesion and apoptotic cell clearance [30,31]. Previous studies showed that CD93 protein was highly expressed in tumor tissue and tumor-related vascular endothelial cells but not in non-proliferating endothelial cells [32]. CD93 overexpression enhances the malignancy of nasopharyngeal carcinoma and glioma [33,34]. Abnormal tumor angiogenesis is critical for tumor growth and metastasis and is crucial for tumor immunotherapy. According to a meta-analysis of breast, kidney, and head and neck cancers, CD93 was positively correlated with tumor angiogenesis, and CD93 was one of the top 20 core genes of primary tumor angiogenesis [35].

Research on the mechanism by which CD93 regulates tumorigenesis is scarce. Its molecular mechanism research focuses on vascular endothelial cell adhesion, vascular regeneration, and promoting tumor growth. Yi et al. found that CD93 was related to tumor blood vessel generation; the interaction between CD93 and IGFBP7 promotes disordered tumor blood vessels, and blocking the CD93 pathway led to blood vessels normalization, increasing tumor perfusion, improving the therapeutic effect of chemotherapeutic agents, and improving immune cell infiltration [36]. Another study showed that CD93 binding to MMRN2 in tumor vascular endothelial cells promoted endothelial cell proliferation, activation, and migration by activating β-integrin and increasing the phosphorylation of focal adhesion kinase [37]. We speculated that during AEG, proteins should bind to CD93, promoting tumor occurrence and development. Compared with other tumor vascular markers, such as CD34, CD93 found in this study can better judge the role of tumor cells in the generation of vascular endothelial cells in AEG, and it CD93 was closely associated with clinicopathological features [38].In our study, Our research team invited professional pathologists to analyze the immunohistochemical pathological sections of 116 clinical tissue samples, and the results indicated that the expression level of CD93 in vascular endothelium of tumor tissue was indeed higher than that in normal tissue under the same vascular density conditions and immunohistochemistry scores were statistically significant and its expression level correlated with the T stage and maximum tumor diameter. This result suggests that CD93 is critical for tumor vascular development. A relationship between CD93 and AEG has not been reported previously; however, our bioinformatics analysis and immunohistochemistry analysis suggests that CD93 may be essential for AEG tumorigenesis and can be a potential biomarker. Nevertheless, to date, no study has revealed the regulatory mechanism of CD93 in tumor vascular endothelial cells; the molecular regulation mechanism of upstream and downstream of CD93 is worthy of further study.

Although TRIM28, SLC3A2, CBX4, PATL1, and ZNF473 have not been reported in AEG studies, our findings suggest that these hub genes may be associated with cancer cell proliferation. The enrichment analysis confirmed that the hub genes in the brown module were related to tumor cell division, replication, the p53 signaling pathway, viral carcinogenesis, pathways in cancer, and ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis. The expression of TRIM28, SLC3A2, CBX4, and PATL1 was identified using immunohistochemistry, suggesting that the expression of these genes in AEG cells was significantly upregulated. SLC3A2 expression might be correlated with preoperative CA199. The expression of CBX4 and SLC3A2 may be related to AEG outcomes. Therefore, the top hub genes in the brown module (TRIM28, SLC3A2, CBX4, PATL1, and ZNF473) may be biomarkers; future work should investigate the molecular mechanisms of these genes in AEG and other cancers.

There are several limitations to this study including that this study only verified the expression of AEG hub genes in tumors, and there was still a lack of research on their mechanism. Moreover, the validation method was a retrospective study, and Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT) experiments should be added to verify the significance of hub genes in AEG in the later period.

Conclusions

Using WGCNA and immunohistochemistry analysis, we identified CD93, TRIM28, SLC3A2, CBX4, PATL1, and ZNF473 as candidate hub genes in AEG. For most of these genes, this was the first association with AEG. These genes were expressed at a high level, suggesting that they may contribute to AEG tumorigenesis. Several clinicopathological features correlated with hub gene expression. The expression of CD93 is closely related to tumor T staging, suggesting that CD93 plays an important role in the growth and development of tumor blood vessels. Nevertheless, the specific mechanism of hub genes requires further study to provide insight into AEG, with the ultimate goal of personalized therapy.
