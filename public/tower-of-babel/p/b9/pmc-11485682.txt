Smoking and serological response to influenza vaccine
Authors: Won Suk Choi, Mary Patricia Nowalk, Krissy Moehling Geffel, Michael Susick, Sean Saul, Chyongchiou Jeng Lin, Ted M Ross, Richard K Zimmerman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCigarette smoking confers additional risk from influenza. This study assessed the effect of smoking on humoral immune response to influenza vaccine. Adults ≥50 y of age were enrolled during the 2011–2016 influenza vaccination seasons in an observational prospective study. Non-fasting whole blood samples for hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) assays were obtained from participants at pre- and 28 d post-clinically administered, trivalent influenza vaccination. Among 273 participants, 133 subjects self-reported as never smokers, 87 as ex-smokers, and 53 as current smokers. Postvaccination geometric mean HAI titers were significantly higher among smokers for A/H1N1 (p = .031) and A/H3N2 (p = .001). Relative to never smokers, smoking was independently related to seroconversion to A/H1N1, A/H3N2 and B. The adjusted odd ratios (ORs) were 5.2 [95% confidence interval (CI), 2.3, 11.5] for seroconversion to A/H1N1, 5.4 (95% CI, 2.4, 12.1) for A/H3N2, and 2.7 (95% CI, 1.3, 5.7) for B. Smoking was also independently related to seroprotection to A/H1N1, A/H3N2 and B. The ORs were 3.6 (95% CI, 1.6, 8.08) for seroprotection to A/H1N1 in smokers, 2.7 (95% CI, 1.14, 6.5) for A/H3N2, and 2.5 (95% CI, 1.1, 5.7) for B. Although the mechanism is unclear, smokers showed a better immune response to influenza vaccination than never smokers and ex-smokers. The results can be used to emphasize the value of influenza vaccination for smokers.KEYWORDS: Influenza, vaccine, immunogenicity, hemagglutination inhibition tests, smoking

Introduction

The relationship between cigarette smoking and immune function is complex, especially with regard to influenza vaccination and infection. Cigarette smoking with its thousands of inhaled gaseous toxins and aerosolized particulates affects the lungs’ immune response to respiratory pathogens by several mechanisms.1 Smoke and its components directly disrupt the integrity of the respiratory epithelium and induce its production of pro-inflammatory mediators.1 Similarly, smoking stimulates the growth of alveolar macrophages and their production of pro-inflammatory mediators or may skew the inflammatory mediatory profile.1 A significant positive relationship had been reported between smoking and antibody response to respiratory viruses.2 Conversely, there are numerous other effects of cigarette smoking on the many components of the immune system that increase susceptibility to respiratory infections.

The relationship between cigarette smoking and immune function is no less complex with regard to influenza vaccination and infection. Thus, studies have examined the relationship between cigarette smoking and the humoral immune response to influenza virus using hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) assay as an indicator of vaccine immunogenicity.3 These studies have demonstrated mixed results. Although cigarette smoking has been found to be associated with lower immunoglobulin levels,4 studies in both the general population5,6 and among participants with COPD7 have found that post-influenza vaccination antibody response was higher in smokers8 than nonsmokers or not different between the groups. However, studies that examined antibody titers post vaccination reported more rapid decline in titers among smokers than nonsmokers,9 and among those with low prevaccination titers, HAI titer response to inactivated vaccine was significantly lower among smokers than nonsmokers at 50 weeks post vaccination.9

Many of these studies of antibody response to influenza virus used vaccine formulations that have not been in use for many years and have not considered ex-smokers as a separate group. This study examined the antibody response, specifically hemagglutinin inhibition titers to the 2011–2012, 2012–2013, 2013–2014, 2014–2015 and 2015–2016 seasonal influenza vaccines in a group of adults, comparing responses among smokers, ex-smokers and never smokers.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a post hoc analysis of a series of observational prospective studies of adults ≥50 y of age who were recruited from three family medicine practices and the University of Pittsburgh community during the 2011–2012 through 2015–2016 influenza seasons (September–November each year). We used nonprobability convenience sampling to enroll 110 adults in 2011. Each subsequent year, the original 2011 cohort was recontacted for the enrollment, and additional participants were enrolled to meet recruitment sample size goals. Each year, the study questions regarding influenza vaccine formulation varied; however, smoking history was asked each year as part of the enrollment survey.

Inclusion criteria were self-report of prior season receipt of influenza vaccine, no known egg allergies or Guillain-Barré syndrome, age 50–80 y and willing to receive the current season’s influenza vaccine. Participants were not eligible if they reported being on dialysis, having active cancer other than skin cancer, having a solid organ transplant or use of an immunosuppressant medication, such as oral prednisone, hydroxychloroquine, methotrexate, rituximab, etc., experiencing cognitive impairment or were receiving hospice care. The University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board approved this study. Participants provided written informed consent prior to study initiation.

Data collection

Baseline demographics included sex, race, self-reported age, smoking status and socioeconomic status (SES) measured by the MacArthur Scale of Subjective Social Status (scored 0 low to 9 high).

Non-fasting whole blood samples were obtained on participants pre- and 28 (19–35) d post-influenza vaccination using serum tubes with clot activator and silicone coated interior additive (BD Vacutainer, REF 367,820) and held at room temperature until centrifugation to separate serum. Aliquoted serum samples were frozen at −80◦C until assayed.

After the blood draw at the prevaccination (Day 0) visit, all participants received an injection of the seasonal trivalent influenza vaccine containing the influenza strains shown in Table 1. Only immune response to TIV was included in this analysis. Although licensed and available, quadrivalent influenza vaccine was not widely in use and all study-provided vaccine was TIV.

Following Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) standardized protocols, serum was tested in HAI assays against each vaccine strain included in the annual influenza vaccines, measuring the ability of antibodies to inhibit 100% agglutination of hemagglutinin to 0.8% turkey erythrocytes in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Lampire Biologicals, Pipersville, PA, USA). Prior to testing, serum was treated with receptor-destroying enzyme (RDE) (Denka Seiken, Co, Japan) adding three parts of RDE to one part of serum with overnight incubation at 37°C. RDE-treated serum was serially diluted in PBS two-fold using V-shaped 96-well bottom microtiter plates. RDE was inactivated by incubation at 56°C for 30–45 min and then cooled to room temperature before being diluted with 1× PBS or 0.85% NaCl to a final concentration of 1:10. Twenty-five (25) μl of each vaccine strain adjusted to ~8 HAI units/50 μl was added to each well. HAI titers were the reciprocal dilution of the last well that contained inhibited agglutination; all tests were conducted in duplicate. Positive and negative serum controls were included in each plate.

Statistical analyses

All analytical procedures were performed using SAS® 9.3 (Cary, NC) and SPSS v 20.0 (Cary, NC). The analytic cohort included 273 adults enrolled from 2011 to 2015. Because this was a cohort study with replacement, not all participants were included each year. All data for each participant for each year of participation were included in the analyses. Participants were divided into three groups based on smoking status, never smoker, ex-smoker (smoked >100 cigarettes in the past but not currently smoking), and current smoker.

Due to the skewness of the HAI titers, they were log2 transformed. Outcome measures were seroprotection (HAI titer ≥ 1:40), seroconversion (4-fold rise in Day 28 HAI titer post vaccination given a Day 0 ≥ 10), log2 geometric mean titers (GMTs), and GMT ratios (GMTRs) equal to Day 28 GMT divided by Day 0 GMT. These outcome measures are reported for A/H1N1, A/H3N2 and the B strain that was contained in the trivalent vaccine for each year.

GMTs and GMTRs were computed by first calculating the means and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for each time point by smoking status. Summary statistics of demographics and immunological response (seroconversion, seroprotection, GMTs) were conducted by smoking status using chi-square tests for categorical variables and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables. Turkey’s multiple comparison test was used for the post hoc analysis for the ANOVA. Proportions were reported for categorical variables, and means, standard deviations and ranges were reported for continuous variables. Logistic regression (seroconversion and seroprotection at Day 28) modeling was used for each vaccine strain and by each outcome to assess the association of smoking status with immunological response to influenza vaccination. Baseline HAI titer and age (continuous), year, and race (white/nonwhite) were added as adjustment covariates to the model based on a significant relationship with immunologic responses in the univariate analysis. An analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) model was used to compare the GMTs and GMTRs with baseline HAI titer and age as covariates. Statistical significance of two-sided tests was set at type I error (alpha) less than or equal to 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 273 adults participated in the study for at least one season from 2011 to 2015. New enrollees comprised 46% of participants during 2012–2013 decreasing to virtually none in the latter 3 y (Appendix Table A1). These 273 participants contributed 691 observations of immune response to influenza vaccine. The majority of participants (133) were never smokers, 87 were ex-smokers, and 53 were current smokers (Table 2). There was no statistically significant difference in sex or subjective social status across smoking groups. Among the three groups, smokers were younger (59 y for smokers vs. 62 y for never smokers and 65 y for ex-smokers, p = .005) and less likely to be white (35% for smokers vs. 61% for never smokers and 56% for ex-smokers, p = .008). Vaccines received by participants ages 50–64 y were exclusively standard dose influenza vaccine. Participants ages ≥65 y received high-dose influenza vaccine during the 2012–2013, 2014–2015, and 2015–2016 seasons and standard dose vaccine during the 2011–2012 and 2013–2014 seasons (Appendix Table A2). Current smokers did not receive high-dose vaccine more frequently than nonsmokers (X2 = 1.02; p = .311).

Geometric mean titer (GMT) and GMT ratio

There was no significant difference in the prevaccination GMTs by smoking status for any of the three vaccine strains (Table 3). Postvaccination GMTs were significantly higher in current smokers for A/H1N1 (p = .031) and A/H3N2 (p = .001). Postvaccination GMTs for influenza B did not differ across smoking groups (p = .117). GMTR for A/H1N1 was also significantly higher among current smokers (p = .012) but did not differ for A/H3N2 (p = .487) and B (p = .073). When ANCOVA was used to compare the postvaccination GMTs and GMTRs with prevaccination GMT and age as covariates, the postvaccination GMTs for A/H1N1 and A/H3N2 were higher among smokers (p = .001 for both; data not shown) with no differences for GMTRs.

Seroconversion and seroprotection rates by smoking status

Seroconversion to A/H1N1 and A/H3N2 was significantly higher in current smokers compared to ex-smokers and never smokers (p = .001; Figure 1), with a linear trend across exposure groups. Differences across smoking groups for seroconversion to influenza B were not statistically significant. Logistic regression models for the outcome of seroconversion for each strain showed that current smoking status was independently related to seroconversion to A/H1N1, A/H3N2 and B (Figure 2), adjusting for baseline titer, age, year and race. Postvaccination seroprotection against A/H1N1 and B was highest in current smokers compared with ex-smokers or never smokers. Postvaccination seroprotection against A/H3N2 was also highest in current smokers, but the difference was statistically marginal (81.1% vs. 64.4% vs. 63.2%, p = .051). Logistic regression models for the outcome of seroprotection for each strain showed that current smoking status was independently related to seroprotection to A/H1N1, A/H3N2 and B with ORs ranging from 2.5 to 3.6, adjusting for baseline titer, age, year and race (Figure 2).

Discussion

Many factors have been shown to influence immune response to the presence of influenza antigens including age, underlying disease, and previous exposure to the antigen, whether from infection or vaccination,14–17 and cigarette smoking. One study performed decades ago showed no difference in HAI antibody responses between smokers and nonsmokers,18 while another found better immune response to influenza vaccine among smokers than nonsmokers,6 with a third study reporting higher initial HAI titers among smokers than nonsmokers, with a larger and more rapid decline over time.8 In our study using newer formulations of influenza vaccine, smokers showed a better immune response after influenza vaccination than never smokers, with an intermediate response for ex-smokers. Furthermore, multivariable analysis that adjusted for factors known to affect immune response showed that smoking was independently related to vaccine HAI titers.

The reasons for these findings are not clear. It is possible that cigarette smoking acts as an adjuvant, causing a heightened immune response to vaccination. It is also possible that physiological changes due to cigarette smoking cause the immune system to be primed for response to vaccine antigens, as evidenced by the intermediate immune response to influenza vaccine observed among ex-smokers in this study.

In the United States, all persons ≥6 months of age are recommended to receive annual influenza vaccine.19 Yet, as a group at high risk for influenza morbidity and mortality, the annual influenza vaccination rate for smokers is low.20–22

The relationship between cigarette smoke and influenza morbidity and mortality is also complex. Cigarette smoke increases the risk of respiratory infections and is a leading cause of chronic respiratory diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.23 The presence of chronic respiratory disease increases the risk of serious complications of influenza.24 Current and former smokers are at greater risk of death from influenza than never smokers25 and may be at increased risk of influenza-associated hospitalization.26,27 Given the difficulty of quitting, influenza vaccination may help to protect these high-risk individuals. Our findings should serve as encouragement to providers to strongly recommend influenza vaccination to their patients who smoke.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. The analytical sample consisted of an observational cohort of adults whose data were included for each year in which they participated in the study and confounding may have occurred, such as by unmeasured chronic medical conditions. Knowledge of high-risk conditions that may have affected the immune response was limited and sample size was too small to perform subgroup analyses. However, immunocompromising conditions and medications were exclusion criteria for this study, so it is unlikely that differences in the distribution of other high-risk conditions would have affected the outcomes.

There were changes to one or more of the influenza strains included in the vaccine most influenza seasons and with waning of immune response over time, it seems to be reasonable to include participants each year. In the future, a larger study that uses advanced statistical techniques such as hierarchical modeling of titers should be performed.

Long-term (for example, 6-months post vaccination) immune response was not measured. In most seasons, a 6-month postvaccination measurement would have been after the peak of influenza season and in some cases after influenza stopped circulating, thus negating the need for antibody protection against influenza at that time.

After the first season when participants self-reported respiratory illnesses, influenza incidence was not assessed following vaccination. Thus, few influenza cases were reported during that season (2011–2022) as to discourage further pursuit of these data in subsequent years. The primary independent variable, smoking status, was assessed in a general way, using self-report of current smoking vs. ever or never having smoked ≥100 cigarettes over a lifetime. While this method does not allow for a lifetime smoking exposure and thereby a measure of the degree of exposure to cigarettes’ toxicity, it is sufficient to evaluate the association between current smoking and serological response to influenza vaccine.

Conclusions

Immune responses to both influenza vaccine and cigarette smoking are multifactorial and complex. Response to influenza vaccine has been shown to be associated with a number of factors, including aging and its corresponding immunosenescence, inflammation resulting from chronic disease, as well as cigarette smoking. Given that smokers are at high risk of influenza infection, the results of this study can be used to support influenza vaccine recommendations by providers and public health policymakers. Further research on other immune responses to influenza vaccine such as cell mediated immunity is warranted.
