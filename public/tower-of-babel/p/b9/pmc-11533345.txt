Explainable spatio-temporal graph evolution learning with applications to dynamic brain network analysis during development
Authors: Longyun Chen, Chen Qiao, Kai Ren, Gang Qu, Vince D Calhoun, Julia M Stephen, Tony W Wilson, Yu-Ping Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractModeling dynamic interactions among network components is crucial to uncovering the evolution mechanisms of complex networks. Recently, spatio-temporal graph learning methods have achieved noteworthy results in characterizing the dynamic changes of inter-node relations (INRs). However, challenges remain: The spatial neighborhood of an INR is underexploited, and the spatio-temporal dependencies in INRs’ dynamic changes are overlooked, ignoring the influence of historical states and local information. In addition, the model’s explainability has been understudied. To address these issues, we propose an explainable spatio-temporal graph evolution learning (ESTGEL) model to model the dynamic evolution of INRs. Specifically, an edge attention module is proposed to utilize the spatial neighborhood of an INR at multi-level, i.e., a hierarchy of nested subgraphs derived from decomposing the initial node-relation graph. Subsequently, a dynamic relation learning module is proposed to capture the spatio-temporal dependencies of INRs. The INRs are then used as adjacent information to improve the node representation, resulting in comprehensive delineation of dynamic evolution of the network. Finally, the approach is validated with real data on brain development study. Experimental results on dynamic brain networks analysis reveal that brain functional networks transition from dispersed to more convergent and modular structures throughout development. Significant changes are observed in the dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) associated with functions including emotional control, decision-making, and language processing.Keywords: Spatio-temporal dependencies, Explainability, Dynamic functional connectivity, Brain development

Introduction

Exploring dynamic evolution of complex networks is crucial to comprehending the evolution mechanism of networks. The dynamic evolution of networks includes the dynamic changes of both component entities and their relations, which usually show spatio-temporal dependencies. The entity states and inter-entity relations at any given moment are affected by both historical states and their spatial neighborhood information. Precisely characterizing the interactions among entities is of great significance for exploring dynamic evolution of complex networks.

Complex networks are often modeled as graph structures, with entities being represented as nodes and their relations as edges (Dorogovtsev and Mendes, 2003; Fortunato, 2011). Traditional machine learning methods for dynamic inter-node relations (INRs) learning usually use shallow models. These models rely on various optimization objective functions and incorporate prior constraints such as smoothness and sparsity. Monti et al. proposed the smooth incremental graphical lasso estimation algorithm to infer dynamic INRs, using the graphical lasso penalty for sparsity and the fused lasso penalty for temporal homogeneity (Monti et al., 2014). Li et al. estimated dynamic INRs by recursive least squares and eliminated spurious connections by the ultra-weighted-lasso (Li et al., 2020). Maleki Balajoo et al. used a kernel-reweighted logistic regression to estimate multivariate relations, modeling temporal dependencies by means of kernel weights and using l1-norm to ensure the sparsity of neighborhoods (Balajoo et al., 2020). Natali et al. expressed time-varying relations learning as a composite optimization problem, based on the regularization on data fidelity, sparsity, smoothness, and other requirements (Natali et al., 2022). These linear models learn shallow relations, failing to characterize nonlinear relations.

Deep learning techniques have received widespread attention for their ability to hierarchically extract abstract features. Researchers often combine recurrent neural networks (RNNs) or temporal convolutional networks (TCNs) with graph neural networks (GNNs) to explore the dynamic evolution of complex networks, based on the learning of INRs to capture spatio-temporal information (Li et al., 2017; Qi et al., 2019; Cao et al., 2022). In many tasks, INRs are not provided as prior knowledge or the prior graph structure is incomplete and should be discovered from data. Wu et al. introduced an adaptive dependency matrix to learn hidden INRs and overcome the limitations of a prior graph structure (Wu et al., 2019). Bai et al. utilized the node embedding vectors to adaptively learn INRs (Bai et al., 2020). Wu et al. proposed a graph learning layer that employs node embedding vectors to learn directed INRs (Wu et al., 2020). However, these methods aim to seek fixed INRs, ignoring the dynamic changes of INRs. To characterize dynamic INRs, Xu et al. extracted dynamic adjacency matrices from different traffic features to accommodate the dynamic changes in spatial relations of urban traffic (Xu et al., 2023a). Li et al. used node embedding vectors to learn the long-term pattern of INRs, and time-varying node representations to reflect the dynamic pattern of INRs (Li et al., 2023). Sriramulu et al. combined statistical structure modeling with neural networks and introduced causal semantics to determine the temporal dependencies between multivariate time series (Sriramulu et al., 2023).

For fMRI sequence, it can be regarded as graph-structured data, where nodes are defined as brain regions of interest (ROIs), and edges are defined as functional connectivity (FC) obtained by calculating pairwise correlations between time series of different brain regions (Zhao et al., 2022; Kim and Ye, 2020; Qu et al., 2021). Spatio-temporal graph deep learning methods are used to capture the spatio-temporal dependencies of brain functional networks (Dvornek et al., 2018; Gadgil et al., 2020; Bedel et al., 2023). Azevedo et al. (2022a), Huang et al. (2022) characterized brain functional networks as static, which rely on neighborhood information fixed in the time course to enhance dynamic information in node representations. Previous studies have shown that FC varies over time and is associated with cognitive activities (Allen et al., 2014). To model dynamic brain functional networks, Liu et al. (2023), Wang et al. (2023a), Jiang et al. (2022) used a sliding time window approach to obtain dynamic fMRI subsequences, and computed brain networks under different subsequences to obtain dynamic neighborhood information for enhanced node representations. Sivgin et al. (2024), Kim et al. (2021a) generated independent dynamic brain functional networks in different time windows and captured temporal dependencies in fMRI sequences in parallel based on the transformer model to enhance dynamic information in node representations. The above methods independently generate FC at different time points, and do not consider the spatio-temporal dependencies of FC’s dynamic changes, which are not conducive to reveal the real dynamic functional connectivity.

The black box nature makes it challenging to understand the decision behavior of deep models and limits the explainability of models. The main goal of spatio-temporal graph deep learning models is to capture and understand complex spatio-temporal patterns in observed data to enable an informed decision-making process. To adapt the demand of explainability, gradient-based methods (Baldassarre and Azizpour, 2019; Simonyan et al., 2013), perturbation-based methods (Ying et al., 2019; Dos Santos et al., 2023), and gradient-weighted class mapping methods (Selvaraju et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2023b) have been proposed. Wang et al. (2023b) used GradCam to identify significant hyperedges in hypergraphs as biomarkers for brain network analysis to enhance the explainability of the model. Bedel and Çukur (2023) proposed a diffusion-driven counterfactual method that performs diffusion-based resampling of fMRI samples to change the decision of a downstream classifier, and then computed the minimum difference between the original and counterfactual samples for explanation. Ellis et al. (2022) used 1-D convolution networks to handle dynamic functional connectivity and combined Monte Carlo normalization with layer-wise relevance propagation to gain insight into the uncertainties associated with explanations. Tang et al. (2023) used structural distillation methods based on graph information bottleneck principle to identify influential spatio-temporal subgraphs that have a significant impact on the prediction results. Tang et al. (2022) combined contrast learning with a collection of hierarchical symbolic graphs to distinguish between positive and negative edge weights in the graph, leading to more detailed insights into brain connectivity patterns. For dynamic brain functional network analysis, constructing explainable spatio-temporal deep learning model to identify dynamic functional connections with significant differences is important for revealing the complex evolution process of dynamic brain functional networks.

Existing studies, which independently generate INRs at different time points, do not pay sufficient attention to the spatio-temporal dependencies of the data during the learning of INRs. To address the above issues, we propose an explainable spatio-temporal graph evolution learning (ESTGEL) model. It considers the multi-level neighborhood information and the spatio-temporal dependencies to explore the dynamic evolution of complex networks. Firstly, to utilize the multi-level neighborhood information of INRs, an edge attention module is proposed based on neighborhood information differences. Specifically, the graph decomposition strategy is used to decompose the initial node-relation graph into a sequence of nested subgraphs. The edge attention aggregation layer aggregates the edge neighborhood information within the subgraphs and defines the attention scores based on the variations observed in edge neighborhood information. Subsequently, to characterize the evolution of INRs, a dynamic relation learning module is introduced. The neighborhood information of INRs is combined to describe the retention of historical and current relation information. Finally, a dynamic node learning module is proposed to refine the representations of nodes.

To validate the proposed model ESTGEL, it is applied to the analysis of dynamic brain functional networks using fMRI data from the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort (PNC). The temporal associations between brain regions are defined as dynamic functional connectivity (dFC), which quantify the change of the correlational patterns over time between regions of interest (ROIs) within the cerebral cortex. ESTGEL is used to explore the dynamic evolution mechanism of brain functional networks and their developmental variations.

Explainability

The explainability of the model can help better understand what critical features can successfully predict the result, and thus providing an insight on the underlying mechanisms.

The task of ESTGEL is to reveal the dynamic evolution process of complex network and thus to discover the underlying evolutionary mechanisms of the data. The outputs of ESTGEL, i.e., the learned node representation vectors and connection representation matrices at each time step, can thereby explore the spatio-temporal evolution characteristics of the data, which are exactly the revealed explainability. The age classification based on the selected INRs by ESTGEL, which reveal the group differences on the changes of dynamic functional connectivity between children and young adults, are further used to validate the effectiveness of these INRs and the proposed ESTGEL.

Experimental results and analysis

Data acquisition and preprocessing

The testing of ESTGEL utilizes data from the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort (PNC). PNC is a large-scale collaborative research project between the Brain Behavior Laboratory at the University of Pennsylvania and the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia. The PNC dataset involves rs-fMRI data from 874 healthy developing adolescents aged 103 to 272 months and contains 193 children aged 103 to 144 months and 204 young adults aged 216 to 272 months (Satterthwaite et al., 2014). During the resting state scan, subjects were instructed to remain awake, keep their eyes open, focus on the displayed crosshairs, and remain still. Standard brain imaging preprocessing was performed on the acquired data using statistical parametric mapping 12 (SPM12) including motion correction, spatial normalization to Montreal Neurological Institute standard space, and spatial smoothing using a 3 mm full width half maximal Gaussian kernel. Next, the effect of head movement was eliminated using regression (Satterthwaite et al., 2013). A band-pass filter was applied to the functional time series within a frequency range of 0.01 Hz to 0.15 Hz. Finally, according to the definition of brain regions by Power et al. (2011), the data containing 21384 voxels were mapped to 264 ROIs. For each subject, a 264 × 124 matrix was obtained.

To evaluate the dynamic functional networks of the brain, a sliding-window technique (Shakil et al., 2016) is used to traverse the time-courses with window length w and step length g. The whole time series signal containing 124 time points is divided into T subsequences. The functional connection is obtained by calculating the PCC between the two ROIs for each subsequence. Experimental setup

To avoid the deviation of the final results caused by data selection, we have validated the model using 10-fold cross-validation. The grid search is used to select the optimal hyperparameters (Abas, 2020). After grid search, the hyperparameters are set as follows: the window length and step size of the sliding time window are w=8 and g=4, and the number of subsequences T=30. The graph decomposition strategy uses the iteration number K=11, S=264K=24, and in EAA layer Le=12. In DRL module Lr=Lz=Lh=12. The effect of iteration times K of graph decomposition on the classification results are shown in Fig. 3(b). To illustrate the robustness of node importance score, the Gaussian noise of different intensities are added to the initial adjacency matrix, and the experimental results under different noises are shown in Fig. 4. The model is trained with a maximal epoch of 50, a learning rate of 1 × 10−4, a weight decay of 1 × 10−2, and a dropout rate of 0.5.

Brain functional network analysis

To analyze the dFC on the characterization of brain functions, 264 ROIs are parceled corresponding to 13 resting-state networks (RSNs). They include sensory/somatomotor network (SSN), cingulo-opercular task control network (COTCN), auditory network (AN), default mode network (DMN), memory retrieval network (MRN), visual network (VN), frontoparietal task control network (FPTCN), salience network (SN), subcortical network (SCN), ventral attention network (VAN), dorsal attention network (DAN), cerebellar network (CN), and uncertain network (UN). The 12 RSNs are mainly related to movement, memory, language, vision, cognition, and other brain functions (Power et al., 2011). The method for mapping the functional connection matrix between 264 brain regions to the functional connection matrix between 13 RSNs is shown in Appendix A.

To better understand the changes in brain functional connectivity during brain development, the hypothesis testing method in Xu et al. (2023b) is used to test whether the change in dFC is significant. The details of the hypothesis test methods can be found in Appendix C.

By hypothesis testing, 2339 functional connections with significant differences are selected from the original 34716 functional connections, including 129 significantly enhanced functional connections and 2210 significantly weakened functional connections. Discussion

The ESTGEL is applied to rs-fMRI data to learn the dFC changes and differences between children and young adults. The dFC with significant differences are mainly distributed in SSN, COTCN, VN, FPTCN, SN, DMN, and UN, which are primarily responsible for information processing, alertness, attention, and cognition.

SSN plays a key role in sensory information processing and motor control, which includes brain regions such as postcentral gyrus and medial frontal gyrus (Londei et al., 2009). COTCN and FPTCN are important networks involved in executive functions, involving working memory, inhibition, and set-switching tasks (Hausman et al., 2022). COTCN mainly includes cingulate gyrus, superior frontal gyrus and insula. FPTCN is mainly composed of middle frontal gyrus, inferior frontal gyrus, and middle temporal gyrus. VN is related to visual information processing, which includes brain regions such as middle temporal gyrus, lingual gyrus, cuneus, and inferior occipital gyrus (Qiao et al., 2021b). SN includes brain areas such as paracentral lobule and insula, which are involved in social cognitive functions such as emotional understanding and social interaction (Menon and Toga, 2015). DMN is mainly active in the resting state, focusing on internally focused tasks such as self-introspection and thinking about one’s past and future (Buckner et al., 2008). DMN includes posterior cingulate gyrus, middle temporal gyrus, middle frontal gyrus, and anterior cingulate (Power et al., 2011).

Our research indicates that the number of functional connections that are significantly weakened during development exceeds the number of functional connections that are enhanced. This may be because children’s functional networks are not specialized enough, showing more functional connections between lower levels of functional organization (Jolles et al., 2011). As individuals age, discrete areas of the brain become integrated into functional networks to flexibly support a variety of cognitive functions (Stevens et al., 2009; Fair et al., 2009).

Meanwhile, we can observe a large number of differential functional connections within DMN, and between DMN and other RSNs during brain development. This suggests a broad and intricate connectivity between DMN and other RSNs, aligning with findings from previous studies (Cai et al., 2018). The differential functional connections related to DMN are shown in Fig. 10. The diminished functional connections within DMN predominantly occur in the anterior cingulate cortex, medial frontal gyrus, and superior frontal gyrus. Conversely, the strengthened functional connections in DMN are largely focused in the superior frontal gyrus and middle temporal gyrus. The anterior cingulate cortex performs multiple cognitive and affective functions such as error monitoring, attention regulation, pain perception, etc (Bush et al., 2000). The medial frontal gyrus plays an important role in social cognition, helping individuals understand and interpret the actions, emotions, and intentions of others (Talati and Hirsch, 2005). The superior frontal gyrus is involved in the decision-making process, assisting in evaluating options, selecting appropriate behaviors, and helping to monitor and regulate performance on cognitive tasks (Goldberg et al., 2006). The middle temporal gyrus is involved in tasks such as speech processing and grammatical comprehension, including the decoding of auditory information and the grammatical organization of language (Acheson and Hagoort, 2013).

The weakened and enhanced dFC within DMN and between DMN and other RSNs. (a): Weakened functional connections distribution. (b): Enhanced functional connections distribution.

Compared to children, the significantly weakened functional connections in young adults primarily concentrate within DMN and between DMN and other RSNs like VN, FPTCN, and SCN. As age increases, the participation coefficients of the attention area of prefrontal cortex, front-parietal area and DMN decreased to varying degrees, indicating that the internal connectivity of these networks is strong and the network tends to be isolated (Lopez et al., 2020), this may explain the observed weakened functional connectivity between DMN and FPTCN. It can be observed that the functional connectivity within SCN and between SCN and multiple networks such as SSN, COTCN and AN weakens during brain development. Previous studies have found that functional connectivity within subcortical areas and between subcortical areas and cortical areas diminishes with age (Cerliani et al., 2015; Langen et al., 2018).

The enhanced functional connections found in young adults are consistent with previous research. Enhanced functional connectivity within SSN and between SSN and other RSNs may be related to stronger perceptual and motor abilities in young adults. Research shows that COTCN is related to higher-order abilities such as temporarily preserving limited-capacity information, switching attention between tasks, and suppressing impulses unrelated to goals, FPTCN is also involved in the function of preserving limited-capacity information (Hausman et al., 2022). The functional connectivity related to COTCN and FPTCN increases significantly with brain development, which is consistent with the fact that young adults have stronger abilities in tasks such as problem decision-making and activity organization.

In summary, brain functional networks change significantly during brain development, involving various complex functions such as emotional processing, decision-making, and language processing. In resting state, brain functional network of young adults appears more concentrated and efficient, while for children it exhibits a decentralized pattern.

Limitations and future work

The limitation of ESTGEL is that the analysis of the directed relations between nodes is not considered. For fMRI data analysis, effective connectivity (EC) reflects the directed relations between brain regions. Compared with functional connectivity, EC not only depicts the topology and correlation of brain networks but also represents the causal relations between brain regions. Learning effective connectivity between brain regions helps to understand how the brain works and deepens understanding of the complexity of human brain function (Ji et al., 2021). In addition, the fMRI signals of a single paradigm only reflect the brain state from a specific aspect. Exploring the effective connectivity analysis methods of multi-paradigm fusion and complementarity is helpful to understanding the working mechanism of brain networks under different states (Ji et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2023).

Future work can extend the brain region representation enhancement technique to learn the embedding vector with brain region causal information and generate brain effective connectivity networks through variational graph auto-encoders (Kipf and Welling, 2016b) and other techniques. To explore brain effective connectivity in different states, multi-paradigm information fusion technology can be used to integrate information from different fMRI paradigms.

Conclusion

In this paper, we have proposed ESTGEL to reveal the underlying evolutionary mechanisms of complex networks. Through the exploitation of multi-level spatial neighborhood configurations of INRs, it improve the characterization of INRs. Furthermore, by quantifying the spatio-temporal dependencies inherent in INRs, ESTGEL portrays their dynamic fluctuations. The experimental results on real fMRI data from PNC showed that ESTGEL achieves better classification performance. These results indicate that in the resting state, the brain functional networks in children and young adults exhibit consistent and mild fluctuations, maintaining a particular dFC state for extended periods. However, the dFC state of children switches more frequently. During brain development, the functional connections within DMN and between DMN and other RSNs change significantly. These functional connections are more concentrated in brain regions such as the anterior cingulate cortex, the superior frontal gyrus, and the middle temporal gyrus, and are associated with functions including language processing and emotional regulation.
