Ethical considerations of AI integration in palliative care: a qualitative study
Authors: Mengying Qiu, Yijin Wu, Lan Zeng, Fengying Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNurses are integral to delivering humanistic care in palliative care settings. While Artificial Intelligence (AI) boasts significant potential for palliative care practices, its integration raises critical ethical concerns such as AI’s impact on patient autonomy and its risks of depersonalized care that warrant further exploration.MethodsA qualitative study using reflexive thematic analysis was conducted to explore nurses’ perspectives on the potential impact of AI integration in palliative care, with a focus on ethical considerations and the prospective actions needed for implementation. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 20 registered nurses experienced in palliative care, recruited from four hospitals across two urban centers in Sichuan Province, China.ResultsNurses perceive AI integration as ethically transformative rather than merely technical, raising concerns regarding autonomy, trust, justice, and cultural alignment. The thematic analysis identified five major ethical considerations regarding AI integration in palliative care from nurses’ perspectives: (1) ethical challenges to patients’ autonomy and dignity; (2) AI-driven reconstruction of trust dynamics; (3) decision making conflicts in palliative care; (4) difficulties in achieving equity in AI-enabled health services; and (5) adaptation to cultural sensitivity.ConclusionsThe inclusion of AI ethics and dynamic moral decision‑making modules in nursing education is suggested to enhance nurses’ moral sensitivity to deliver equitable care, and strengthen their digital literacy and ethical awareness in AI‑mediated environments. Nurses may assume an ethical responsibility to reduce the risks of cultural bias in AI tools, and to advocate the integration of multicultural perspectives into AI design, training and evaluation, thereby advancing the development of more culturally responsive technologies. Keywords: Artificial Intelligence, Palliative care, Nurses, Medical ethics, Qualitative study

Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) refers to the simulation of human intelligence within machines, which are designed to perform tasks that typically require human cognition, such as problem-solving, decision-making, and pattern recognition [1]. By utilizing accessible information, AI-based support systems can contribute to clinical decision-making, thereby facilitating evidence-based, enhanced medical care [2]. AI technologies have also been widely used in nursing in recent years, including predictive modeling, natural language processing, computer vision, speech recognition, or planning or scheduling, with some combining two or more technologies [3].

Palliative care is instrumental in enhancing the quality of life for patients with life-limiting illnesses and their family caregivers [4–6]. Conventionally, palliative care practice has been predominantly guided by the four bioethical tenets of principlism, which focuses on respect for autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice [7]. Autonomy is the cornerstone of end-of-life decision-making [7, 8], while beneficence and non-maleficence underpin clinical judgments regarding symptom alleviation and the avoidance of burdensome interventions [9]. Justice, in turn, encompasses equitable access to palliative care services and extends to the equitable provision of digital and AI-enabled palliative care [10, 11]. Yet, the principlism presumption of patients as independent and rational decision-makers is often misaligned with emotional vulnerability, interpersonal dependency, and intricate relational networks that characterize palliative care contexts [7, 12]. This limitation of principlism has spurred the integration and evolution of relational ethics as a complementary ethical perspective.

Relational ethics treats nursing as an inherently relational practice, emphasizing that patients, families, and professionals are in a network of relationships that shape their values and choices [10, 13]. From this theoretical perspective, autonomy is reconceptualized as relational autonomy, sustained in clinical practice through reciprocal supportive relationships [14]. Meanwhile, dignity is regarded as an intrinsic human value, inextricably linked to patients’ subjective perceptions of “death with dignity” within their unique relational contexts of end-of-life care [15]. This study thus adopts an integrated ethical lens: we take the core ethical tenets of principlism as foundational analytical categories, and draw on relational ethics to examine how these principles are interpreted, enacted, and potentially challenged within the complex interpersonal and nursing care dynamics of palliative care settings.

AI offers potential advantages for healthcare services. However, its integration in palliative care poses substantive issues that must be scrutinized under this comprehensive ethical framework. Based on this framework, this study will focus on the following core ethical dimensions: (1) privacy and data governance [16, 17]; (2) algorithmic bias and justice [18–20]; (3) transparency, accountability and explainability [20–22]; (4) respect for patients’ relational autonomy and shared decision-making integrity [23, 24]; and (5) the impact of AI on the evolving provider-patient relationship [10, 14].

Prior research has examined the ethical dimensions of palliative care predominantly from a physician’s perspective [15, 25–28]. Studies reveal that physicians frequently encounter dilemmas related to truth-telling, particularly concerning how, when, and what prognostic information should be disclosed to patients [25, 27–29]. Decisions regarding the limitation of life-sustaining treatments, such as artificial hydration or nutrition, also pose recurrent ethical challenges [25, 26]. Furthermore, physicians often face difficulties in navigating conflicts between patients’ preferences and the wishes of their family members [27, 28].

Although palliative care necessitates collaboration within a multidisciplinary team where physicians, nurses, and other professionals assume distinct roles, nurses’ perspectives are indispensable when considering the ethical implications of AI integration. Nurses typically have the most frequent contact and interaction with patients and their families [30]. Their professional ethics prioritize patient-centered care, with a focus on patient autonomy and dignity [31]. Nurses’ prolonged presence enables them to gain a more comprehensive understanding of patients’ physical and psychological conditions, family dynamics, and personal values, thereby facilitating nuanced trade-off analyses between the selection of clinical interventions and the maintenance of patients’ quality of life [32, 33]. This provides a basis for them to better balance the relationship between technology applications and patients’ needs than other members of the palliative care multidisciplinary team. However, a scoping review of AI deployment in nursing contexts found that 36.6% of the 93 included articles omitted ethical discussion entirely, while none explicitly addressed the ethical implications [3]. One of the most prominent concerns is, the use of large-scale data is not morally neutral, as it raises ethical questions related to patient privacy and autonomy, possible harm, and justice [16, 17].

To summarize, the ethical dimensions of AI integration in palliative care remain unaddressed from the nurse’s perspective. Ensuring that the proper use of AI enhances rather than diminishes the quality of care requires that nurses’ professional judgment and ethical responsibilities be adequately taken into consideration. Accordingly, this research aims to explore the ethical considerations of using AI in palliative care from the nurse’s perspective with a qualitative research method, to probe into nurses’ personal feelings, experiences, and perceptions, revealing values, and moral judgments that are difficult to capture in quantitative research.

Methods

Study design

Semi-structured interviews were conducted to gather in-depth insights, and the qualitative research method of thematic analysis was applied to interpret the interview transcripts. The findings were reported following the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [34].

Setting and sampling

Registered nurses with experience of palliative care were recruited at four hospitals in two cities of Sichuan Province of China, with each hospital having a palliative care unit or center. Only one of the four hospitals integrated its palliative care services into the oncology department. The remaining three hospitals operated separate, dedicated palliative care centers and the three centers provided care for patients with various life-limiting illnesses, not only for those with cancer. The interviews were undertaken at locations where were quiet and free from interruptions. Purposive sampling was used to recruit registered nurses. Inclusion criteria included: more than one year of working experience in palliative care; informed consent obtained; utilized or received training in AI technology applications in their work or studies. Exclusion criteria included: less than one year of working experience in palliative care; not yet utilized or received training in AI technology applications in their work or studies; withdrawal during the interview period. In the inclusion criteria, the AI technology applications included but were not limited to intelligent triage robot, AI-driven drug delivery system, rehabilitation assistive robot, integrated bed-mounted transfer robot, AI-powered mobility assistance exoskeleton, therapeutic companion robot, AI-assisted communication applications and large language models.

Data collection

MQ, LZ and FZ initiated the recruitment by contacting head nurses from multiple hospitals, who did not participate the interview but subsequently facilitated the recruitment of potential participants. The research team and the selected participants were not acquainted prior to the recruitment. The interviews were conducted face to face by MQ in Mandarin Chinese between November and December 2024. MQ, skilled in qualitative research methods, explained to the participants the purpose, methods, risks and benefits of the study prior to the interviews.

Participants who were willing to be interviewed were required to sign a written informed consent form. Each participant was presented with a gift after the completion of the interview. The interviews were conducted based on a guide designed for the study. The preliminary set of interview guide questions underwent a pilot test involving three participants. Upon extensive and thorough discussions with the research team and external experts, the pilot interview guide was revised to ensure it would align with the overall research objectives. The pilot interview guide was reviewed by two external experts: a senior researcher who specialized in qualitative research in nursing and another senior researcher who specialized in nursing ethics. Both experts were course leaders in their specialized fields, with extensive experience in clinical practice, teaching, and research. Their extensive experience ensured that the interview guide was clinically relevant, ethically sensitive, and methodologically sound.

Table 1 lists the adjusted interview guide questions for each participant. The questions were rephrased as open-ended inquiries to enable participants to engage in a thorough exploration of ethical concerns brought by AI. The interviews were conducted until sufficiency of information was achieved [35], indicating no emergence of new data. Finally, a total of 20 participants completed the interviews. The interviews varied in length from 40 to 68 min, with an average of 55 min.

Data analysis

The data of interviews were audio recorded with the participants’ consent. All interviews were conducted and audio-recorded in Mandarin Chinese, and transcribed verbatim in Mandarin Chinese by MQ. In accordance with Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis [35], a reflexive approach was employed for qualitative analysis of the interview transcripts. All interview transcripts were saved and analyzed in Microsoft Word and Excel. Instead of using QDA software, which may prioritize efficiency and standardization, we conducted manual thematic analysis. This manual approach would maintain interpretative depth, avoid the risk of framing software-embedded assumptions and promote researchers’ flexible data engagement, aligning with reflexive qualitative research principles [35].

The data analysis process composed of the following six stages: (1) In the initial phase of data familiarization, all researchers immersed themselves in the interview data through repeated readings of the transcripts while returning to the audio data by careful listening to gain a general understanding of the meaningful units [35]; (2) Then, during the initial codes generation process, based on the aims of the study, each researcher independently identified significant units of meaning as open codes [35]; (3) Subsequently, all researchers developed candidate themes by merging the initial codes through looking for common or important units [35]. Analysis did not proceed until new codes were established; (4) During the fourth stage, all researchers examined the candidate themes to reduce overlaps and discard deviant cases which did not accord with candidate themes [35]; (5) Thereafter, all researchers refined the analysis by clarifying the boundaries of each theme, giving definitions and names that enhanced the thematic development [35]; (6) In the final stage of analysis, themes were checked through writing up [35]. The six stages were conducted iteratively by all researchers to achieve a more profound grasp of the research phenomenon. In cases of disagreement between two researchers, a third researcher was consulted to facilitate discussion and reach a consensus.

The processes of data familiarization, coding, and the development of initial themes, were conducted in Mandarin to ensure the integrity of participants’ meanings. Regular meetings were held in Mandarin to refine codes and build consensus on the emerging thematic structure. After the final themes and sub-themes were established, the specific interview excerpts selected to illustrate them were translated into English for reporting. This translation was performed by MQ and verified by other team members to confirm conceptual accuracy and preserve the original context and nuance.

Rigor

Field notes were utilized to enhance the trustworthiness of the study. Immediately after each interview was conducted and transcribed, the interviewer MQ documented non-verbal cues of the participants and maintained ongoing tracking of preliminary analytical insights. These field notes, derived from the transcripts, were subsequently shared with participants for member checking, particularly to validate terminology that emerged during the interviews. Several participants also contributed additional insights following the interviews, which facilitated the final analysis and ensured the accuracy of the study.

Reflexivity was systematically integrated throughout the research process to improve the methodological trustworthiness. The research team comprised researchers with complementary expertise spanning qualitative research, clinical nursing, and nursing ethics, who brought diverse professional and cultural backgrounds to the study. We acknowledged that these positions could shape interpretations, particularly concerning pragmatic feasibility and normative ethical reasoning. Thus, we took the following steps to operationalize reflexivity.

We held regular meetings during the coding and thematic development phases to explicitly discuss how our individual perspectives might influence code generation and theme construction. The researchers, MQ and YW, with qualitative research background consistently guided the team to remain anchored in the participants’ narratives, cautioning against premature theoretical abstraction. This perspective ensured faithfulness to the empirical data. The researcher, LZ, with a clinical nursing background interpreted data through the lens of practical feasibility, workflow integration, and direct provider-patient interactions. The researcher, FZ, specializing in nursing ethics consistently foregrounded normative frameworks and philosophical concepts. To mitigate biases arising from diverse research perspectives, the research team continuously cross-checked coding outcomes against the original interview data, proactively identified contrasting cases to test and challenge existing interpretations, and fully deliberated within the team to integrate multiple viewpoints.

To ensure the rigor, reliability, and auditability of the study, particularly in the absence of QDA software, we adopted a systematic strategy that combined team collaboration with comprehensive procedural documentation. We adopted shared Microsoft Word documents for documenting interview transcripts and research team reflective journals, alongside collaborative Microsoft Excel spreadsheets to structure, refine and finalize our coding frameworks. During regular team meetings, we conducted line-by-line review and coding calibration of the raw data, ensuring that all analytical interpretations remained firmly grounded in the participants’ original narratives. We engaged in ongoing peer debriefing and sought out disconfirming evidence to test our preliminary themes. Concurrently, we maintained an audit trail noting all analytical decisions and processes, ranging from initial coding, theme generation and refinement, key points of team deliberations, to researcher reflexive notes.

Ethical issues

This study has been approved by Ethics Committee on Biomedical Research, West China Hospital of Sichuan University [2024 Review (No.2360)]. All methods were performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were informed of the study’s objectives and its voluntary nature prior to their involvement. Additionally, they were informed about the study procedures, the fact that interviews would be audio-recorded, and that their participation would have no impact on their personal circumstances. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before the interviews. For confidentiality, numbers rather than names were used to identify participants in the interview records (e.g., Participant 1 = P1), and all self-identifying information was obscured in the interview transcripts.

The qualitative analysis yielded five overarching themes: (1) ethical challenges to patients’ autonomy and dignity; (2) AI-driven reconstruction of trust dynamics; (3) decision making conflicts in palliative care; (4) difficulties in achieving equity in AI-enabled health services; and (5) adaptation to cultural sensitivity. Each theme is explored in full detail in the sections that follow.

Theme 1: ethical challenges to patients’ autonomy and dignity

This theme focuses on how the integration of AI challenges autonomy and dignity, the core ethical cornerstones of patient-centered care, by reshaping patient information and nurse-patient care relationships. Privacy and security represent key ethical issues pertaining to patients’ autonomy and control over their own data. The findings reveal that AI, as a novel mediating force, significantly undermines the stability of traditional empathetic practices. Potential absence of human empathy impairs the preservation of dignity in palliative care. Through continuous data processing, AI redefines the boundaries of privacy and information autonomy at the end of life.

Subtheme 1: privacy and security concerns in palliative care

Participants expressed concerns that the continuous collection and processing of patients’ highly sensitive health information, such as prognostic predictions, symptom trajectories, and psychological states, by AI systems, may erode the traditional boundary of provider-patient confidentiality. This concern goes beyond the scope of conventional data security and touches upon patients’ autonomy in control of their own information and their fundamental need for privacy in the vulnerable phase of end-of-life. The involvement of AI is deemed as a potential monitoring mechanism. It may cause patients to lose a sense of control, thus directly conflicting with the core goal of maintaining their dignity.

In palliative care settings where oncology patients are treated, their emotional vulnerability warrants stringent privacy protection to preserve dignity. The AI system’s immanent defects risk breaching informational privacy, bringing security concerns to family caregivers, as one participant stated, “AI may have some instability, for example, the network may be unstable. Something may suddenly give away. In addition, it may obtain some private data of patients, and then family caregivers may not fully agree to use AI” [P2]. Participants claimed that the risks brought by AI such as privacy leak were uncontrollable, which may lead to further commercialization of medical information. One participant shared her concern of illicit exploitation of personal datasets, “With improper management, it is possible that the patient’s data may be illegally obtained or misused, causing serious consequences for patients and hospitals” [P4]. Participants observed that unauthorized disclosure of personal health data may engender severe psychological outcomes for the affected patients, as one participant analyzed, “What they care most about is that they don’t want others to know what diseases they have, especially patients with AIDS or syphilis… But if such information is given away, it may be really hard for patients to accept that, especially if their relatives and friends also know about it” [P20].

Subtheme 2: potential absence of humanistic care in human-machine interaction

This subtheme explores the potential threat that AI application poses to the core of palliative care. Participants pointed out that the excessive involvement of algorithmic models in care may subtly simplify complex end-of-life care into a series of technical issues and data inputs. This would compress the space for care practices based on empathy and relational connections, thereby undermining the maintenance of dignity. The tension between algorithmic rationality and care empathy may not only weaken the emotional support and humanistic contact felt by patients, but also undermine the professional identity and meaning of work of nurses themselves, ultimately endangering the ethical cornerstone of palliative care.

Participants reported AI involvement would potentially bring a lack of humanistic care. They held the view that one of the most critical deficiencies of AI was its coldness, “…But it’s not as warm as humans when we directly take care of patients… after all, it’s a machine”, as Participant 15 noted. Participants reflected that the ability to empathize was key to humanistic care, which is unique to human beings and something that AI lacks, “Because people have the ability to empathize, but for machines, their ability to empathize may not be that good” [P11]. One participant thus drew the conclusion that nurses’ humanistic care would be indispensable, “…After giving them painkillers that may have side effects, I’ll provide them with relevant drug guidance to relieve their negative emotions. I think these are things that machines can’t do” [P13].

Theme 2: AI-driven reconstruction of trust dynamics

This theme focuses on the analysis of relational trust and elucidates how the integration of AI reshapes the traditional person-centered trust dynamics in palliative care. Rather than merely acting as a novel technological tool, AI becomes embedded within existing ethical relational frameworks, thereby triggering a multidimensional reconstruction of the targets, foundations and directional flow of trust.

Subtheme 1: reconstruction of human-machine trust

This subtheme reveals a contradictory and emerging trust relationship between health care providers and AI systems. Participants demonstrated a general confidence in AI’s capacity to process complex clinical data, acknowledging its potential to enhance efficiency and achieve a degree of objectivity that surpasses human cognition in pattern recognition and risk prediction. However, this trust is highly conditional, relying entirely on the reliability of results, which may be subject to algorithmic limitations, data input inaccuracies, or hardware malfunctions. Therefore, the reconstruction of human-machine trust is not a simple acceptance of technology but a dynamic ethical process that requires continuous negotiation between worship of efficiency and humanistic prudence.

One of the advantages of AI in palliative care lies in its capacity to generate precise monitoring metrics, displaying a higher degree of accuracy than the subjective assessments derived from human experiential judgment, on which occasions participants would trust machines, as one participant pointed out, “Then we will have a better understanding of the patient’s current vital signs…, if it is home care or in some elderly care institutions, relying solely on our own experience may lead to a misjudgment of the patient’s condition” [P5].

However, participants also displayed hesitancy in completely trusting AI technological systems. Similar to human beings, machines are not immune to errors, whether due to algorithmic limitations, data input inaccuracies, or hardware malfunctions. Such errors may have substantial impact on their quality of care, undermining nurses’ trust in AI systems over time. One participant described the possible collapse of technological trust in case of an AI’s malfunction, “There is a problem here. If it’s like this, then everyone will lose trust in this machine. People who have experienced this incident will definitely not trust this machine any more” [P3].

Subtheme 2: reconstruction of human-human trust

This subtheme explores how AI mediates and complicates the traditional trust dynamics between providers and patients. When AI-generated recommendations or assessments are introduced, they may inadvertently become a source of friction for trust. In the provider-patient relationship, if providers completely ignore the objective information provided by AI, they may be questioned by patients about the modernity and scientific validity of their decisions; if providers over-rely on or blindly present AI conclusions, it may also diminish patients’ trust in their professional independent judgment.

The integration of AI technologies into palliative care settings not only “alleviates the temporal and physical burdens on nursing professionals” [P9, P14], but also elevates the precision and efficiency of clinical interventions. Given the benefits of AI that saves more energy and time, nurses are afforded enhanced capacity to communicate with patients to refine treatment plans, thereby improving the patients’ satisfaction, as one participant concluded, “We will have more time, for example, to communicate and chat with patients. In this way, the patients’ satisfaction may be enhanced” [P20]. This would contribute to the reinforcement of patients’ trust in nurses.

However, despite AI’s comprehensive data, its nursing knowledge is limited. In the meantime, participants indicated that there may be a tendency towards the overuse of AI in the future. This could potentially lead patients to trust machines rather than healthcare professionals, ultimately posing a challenge to nurse-patient trust. One participant expressed her concern of patients’ over-reliance on the internet-based medical information generated by AI, “If the advice given by the nurse is different from that of the AI, then the patient may question the professional literacy of the nurse. And then they may have even less trust in the nurse” [P2]. Another participant described the trust crisis, “…When you communicate with them face to face, it shows that you value them highly. But once you say, ‘Hey, talk to this machine yourself,’ they might think, ‘Oh, you are not taking this conversation seriously’. There could be some misunderstandings regarding treatment” [P17]. In response to such crisis, Participant 19 suggested, “It requires you to have very strong professional knowledge.”

Theme 3: decision making conflicts in palliative care

Theme 3 focuses on the beneficence and moral agency of care ethics, examining how AI has affected ethical dilemmas in decision-making. Results reveal that such conflicts not only persist among traditional decision-makers, including patients, their families and medical teams, but are also redefined and exacerbated by the integration of AI. By delivering data, predictions and recommendations, AI fails to resolve conventional decision-making challenges; instead, it often complicates the matter. These conflicts have become the subject of critical scrutiny, thereby challenging the foundational principle of ethical decision-making that focuses on patients’ best interests.

Subtheme 1: decision making conflicts among family caregivers as surrogates

This subtheme reveals how the involvement of AI reshapes and intensifies existing conflicts in surrogate decision-making within families. When AI provides quantitative assessments regarding prognosis, treatment benefits, or quality of life, these seemingly objective data may be interpreted differently by family members with opposing viewpoints, thereby reinforcing or justifying their original disagreements. Therefore, in this context, AI is not an arbiter but a new battlefield within families, making it more difficult to reach a unified surrogate decision.

In traditional palliative care settings, the contradictions within family members’ stances regarding medical decisions are one source of nurses’ ethical dilemma, as one participant recalled, “In a family with multiple children, one family member may think that active treatment is necessary, while another may believe that due to the patient’s old age, it is not suitable to continue treatment” [P15], which was often the case and remained as a large obstacle to the participant’s subsequent work.

Unlike traditional care contexts, where nurses’ guidance is based on clinical expertise and patient values, AI is introduced as a third party that complicates their responsibility to advocate for the patient while respecting family autonomy. Participants described their complicated ethical role when the patient’s multiple immediate family members as surrogates had varied viewpoints about treatment. The participants observed that AI recommendations may support the decision of one immediate family member but clash with the decision of another immediate family member. However, in participants’ clinical practice, consent for major medical procedures usually requires the signature of only one immediate family member, thus bringing a dilemma for both clinicians and nurses. One participant recalled such confusion of family conflicts in decision making, “Whose opinion should I follow?” (P3).

By adding algorithmic recommendations as a contested source of authority, AI can create new disagreements, which may be consistent with physicians’ recommendations, but contradictory to all family surrogates’ consensus. One participant showed concern about decision making conflicts, “Should we choose this decision, or are there other options? It’s not certain that the decision made by AI is the optimal plan for the patient.” (P15). AI transforms the traditional ethical landscape by adding layers of technological mediation and interpretive disagreement to the already complicated dynamics of family surrogate choice, thus increasing nurses’ epistemic burden on mediating conflicts.

Subtheme 2: conflicts between AI algorithm decision making and patient autonomy

This subtheme focuses on the fundamental tension between the logic of AI algorithms and the principle of patient autonomy. The core of this conflict lies in the incommensurability between algorithmic rationality and value rationality. The recommendations generated by AI based on patients’ data and probabilistic models may directly conflict with individual patients’ unique values, interpretations of the meaning of life, and subjective preferences in their current situations.

When an AI recommendation, such as discontinuing a certain treatment, is put forward, it may subconsciously constitute a powerful normative pressure, placing the patient in an ethical dilemma between following scientific advice and being faithful to one’s own will. One participant described, “…It may be about the decision-making between autonomy and algorithms. For example, in an intelligent system, which is likely based on big data analysis, it will recommend some treatment plans for patients. However, this may conflict with the patient’s personal wishes…” [P4]. In palliative care settings, when the family caregivers’ wish is to use AI covertly, which may be without the patient’s consent, it may trigger apprehension among patients. One participant commented on the crux of the lack of informed consent when using AI, “Some patients may be unaware of AI’s role in their care, and they don’t understand what’s going on. They may have misgivings like this: ‘Is it doing something randomly to me? Is this intended just to obtain some data?’… etc.” [P15].

Subtheme 3: dilemma of end-of-life decision making ownership

This subtheme presents the dilemma that occurs in the blurred boundaries of end-of-life decision-making power among patients, families, medical teams, and algorithmic systems after the involvement of AI. With the introduction of AI, the caring process could become more chaotic, growing into a tug-of-war between multiple aspects, such as human relationships, professionalism, filial piety, and technical objectivity. The achievement of consensus has partially shifted from value negotiation to a competition over technical credibility, thereby exacerbating the ethical uncertainty inherent in palliative care itself.

Participants stated that AI could offer personalized care based on the patients’ data, optimizing treatment plans. However, end-of-life phases of terminally-ill patients are often characterized by contingencies, prompting providers to constantly adjust plans to accommodate their ever-changing care needs, even in the presence of advance care planning. On these occasions, one participant revealed the necessity of capturing changes of patients’ physiological data, a task at which AI demonstrates particular proficiency, “Patients may suddenly change their decisions in the terminal stage, and it is necessary to clearly record the state of their conscious awareness” [P10]. The decisions at end-of-life stage, according to the participants, have primarily relied on the opinions of family members in previous cases. With AI that may give options counter to the wishes of patients’ family, participants would still respect the family’s opinions rather than AI-based decisions, as one participant expressed, “Look, when a patient crashes…Say, if the AI alerts us the patient’s BP is tanking and recommends starting pressors, but if the family already signed a DNR saying no blood pressure meds, we’d override that AI recommendation immediately…” [P14]. Nonetheless, participants expressed that their professional compliance would not be determined solely by patient or family decisions, but would be mediated by their own clinical judgment and integrated with medical advice from clinicians and AI. One participant indicated, “…For example, if the patient was previously reluctant to use but now needs or wants to use it, we’ll communicate with the doctor and inform the patient. After informing the doctor, if the doctor decides to use it and the patient is also willing to use, then, of course it should be used appropriately” [P19].

Theme 4: difficulties in achieving equity in AI-enabled health services

This theme reveals a core ethical dilemma in justice and distributive equity: AI technologies designed to advance the quality of palliative care may not only fail to mitigate existing inequalities but also exacerbate structural inequities due to their accessibility and operational logic. Stratification in palliative care payment and disparities in regional economic development will lead to imbalanced access to AI-based palliative care services, which in turn perpetuates inequities in health outcomes. Therefore, justice and distributive fairness transcend the mere application of technology and are deeply embedded in the socioeconomic structures.

Subtheme 1: inadequate coverage of health insurance

This subtheme focuses on the inequalities of AI-enabled services caused by the payment system. Most current medical insurance policies regard AI-enabled palliative care services as non-essential items, unlikely to be reimbursed. Due to the limited coverage of palliative care services under health insurance schemes, patients are often required to bear major non-reimbursable costs, including AI-powered services. Consequently, if AI is introduced, the target population for palliative care may remain more demographically limited, and then such services would only be available in the self-paying market. As one participant observed, “Within palliative care, the legal framework about the introduction to AI is not so clear. And there’s no way AI is getting insurance coverage anytime soon. So realistically, only the well-off can afford it right now” [P2].

For a large number of patients and their families, the economic threshold constitutes an insurmountable gap during the end-of-life stage, already a period of enormous financial pressure. Vulnerable groups with lower incomes thus face obstacles to accessing these services. This not only creates opportunity inequality based on payment capacity but, more profoundly, challenges the core ethics of palliative care as a basic right that every life should equally enjoy the opportunity to use technological means to alleviate suffering and maintain dignity at the end of life, rather than making it a symbol of social and economic status. One participant analyzed, “…Well, if a patient’s family is in good financial conditions, he may think it doesn’t matter… But for patients with average or even lower economic conditions, it may be a heavier burden for them” [P11].

In addition to patients, participants concurred that the implementation of AI would also impose substantial financial burdens on healthcare institutions. To date, the funding for AI-related services within palliative care settings remains solely borne by the institutions themselves, as is exemplified by one participant, who explicitly noted that, “…All these AI helpers, we have to provide them for free because health insurance won’t cover them. Now, health insurance only reimburses standard medical procedures, but not AI. So we’re basically giving away these advanced tools for free” [P14]. Thus, to decrease financial burdens on patients and healthcare institutions, participants suggested that AI-driven palliative care be included in health insurance reimbursement schemes. As one participant argued, “If you were to promote it, it should be better if health insurance is involved” [P8].

Subtheme 2: imbalanced accessibility in AI-enabled medical services

This subtheme reveals the systematic accessibility breakdown caused by disparities in geography, institutions, and digital literacy. Advanced AI-enabled healthcare services are primarily provided at tertiary hospitals and high-end private facilities in a few major cities, while patients in most grassroots medical institutions, community settings, and rural areas are left without access. This digital divide is particularly stark and inequitable in the context of palliative care. Furthermore, differences in nurses’ attitudes towards improving their own AI literacy may further influence the utilization efficiency of AI-enabled services.

Considering regional economic imbalance and educational disparities, the promotion of AI in palliative care settings is confronted with additional challenges of its accessibility. One participant pointed out the digital health equity issue within rural areas, “Well, those with a relatively high level of cultural knowledge will be able to understand these AI things and at least have heard of them to some extent. However, for those in rural areas, especially in remote areas, they may not have heard of this kind of technology at all…” [P12]. Barriers to digital health equity are not only restricted to rural regions but also permeate urban areas. In China, the regionally uneven economies have brought on a tilted distribution of healthcare resources, including AI-enabled medical services, in favor of metropolises among healthcare institutions. One participant claimed, “If you can apply it to palliative care on a large scale, not all regions can afford it. Perhaps it will be piloted as soon as possible. Beijing, Sichuan, Shanghai, and Guangzhou may pilot its use, and it will be initially used in large hospitals…” [P5].

In addition to patients’ uneven affordability, which constitutes an objective factor, participants also identified disparities in nurses’ learning autonomy for improving their digital literacy as a key subjective factor underlying unequal access to technology. Participants expressed the urgent necessity for further education on digital literacy to facilitate the integration of AI into palliative care. However, their apprehension about learning AI techniques, particularly the fear of operating complex machines, may diminish their autonomy in adopting such technologies, thereby potentially widening disparities in technology access. As one participant indicated such possible challenges for female nurses, “…Well, as I said just now, among nurses, there may be those who are intelligent and with high education levels, of course, they’re willing to learn, but there may also be those with relatively lower education levels who are reluctant to learn these new things” [P4].

Theme 5: adaptation to cultural sensitivity

This theme examines the profound adaptive dilemmas triggered by AI integration in cross-cultural palliative care settings. As AI technologies have increasingly permeated the field of palliative care, cultural sensitivity has become an indispensable issue. A significant ethical friction exists between the universal data models underpinning AI algorithms and the unique value systems embedded in clinical practices. When algorithmic recommendations representing modernity and objectivity encounter local customs shaped by family-centric values and religious traditions, the application of AI technology ceases to be a simple issue of tool adaptation. Instead, it will transform into a cultural and ethical practice that demands earnest deliberation to conform to various value systems.

Subtheme 1: constraints of family inter-generational culture

This subtheme concludes how the intervention of AI technology disrupts the traditional family inter-generational ethics. In current practice, family members, especially adult children, often choose to conceal part of the illness from older relatives diagnosed with severe diseases, out of filial piety. However, AI-driven information transparency tools and remote communication platforms may unwittingly bypass the family’s role, presenting medical information directly or indirectly to patients. This forces families to renegotiate their information management strategies and confront the emotional responsibilities and ethical pressures associated with telling the truth.

In palliative care, patients’ right to know about their condition is often restricted by inter-generational culture, especially for older patients. Despite concerns that patients may be unable to accept their end-of-life condition, many family caregivers choose not to inform them. “Such information gap between the patient and the family members can lead to misunderstandings” [P10], one participant remarked how the inter-generation information gap caused trouble for her work. Family caregivers choose not to inform the nurses of the patients’ condition, which is left for the nurses to infer. One participant related her experience of figuring out the allusion to cancer when encountering a terminally-ill patient with enterostomy, “I asked the patient’s family caregiver what the primary disease was. As soon as he uttered a rather oblique reference ‘it was just a polyp’, I would surely understand what it meant but the patient was completely unaware of it” [P9].

Nonetheless, participants believed that in the long run, as the younger generation who grew up in a digital culture enters old age, their open attitude to information may be more aligned with the logic of AI transparency. This could promote an inter-generational paradigm shift in the ethical consensus among generations regarding the priority of benevolence over truth-telling. Another participant expressed such an optimistic view, believing this inter-generational cultural constraint will change as AI rapidly progresses: “…As AI develops in the aging society, therefore, severe elderly care and some aspects such as end-of-life care may play an important role in the future, …the younger generations now have a high degree of acceptance of these aspects, which is promising” [P17].

Subtheme 2: priority for local religious practices

This subtheme centers on presenting the potential fundamental conflicts between standardized clinical recommendations generated by AI and the needs of localized religious practices and spiritual care. Participants strongly believe that during the end-of-life stage, which is highly driven by meaning and hope, cultural appropriateness often takes precedence over technology optimality.

Participants consistently mentioned since AI technology has just been introduced to palliative care in China, it needs to be localized. One nurse expressed the importance of observing local customs, “Also, our department has set up palliative care wards which accommodate people with various beliefs, such as Buddhism, and so on. For those people with special needs, when they have requirements, such as conducting religious rituals, we will arrange for them to stay in these palliative care wards” [P14]. Participants noted it was their routine to conduct cultural adjustment of medical technology, which also holds true for the emerging AI technology. One participant stated, “We need to take individual circumstances into account to provide personalized nursing plans for patient from an ethnic minority group, in the era of AI” [P3]. One participant insisted local religious practices prioritize over technology, offering her solution, “If the patient needs to use AI, but using the device might conflict with some of his religious beliefs, then we would try to achieve a balance by doing some adjustments to using the device…” [P18].

Discussion

With a qualitative approach to semi-structured interview data, this study offers important insights into how nurses navigate implications in the implementation of AI-enabled health services in palliative care settings. The thematic analysis exposes the multifaceted nature of nurses’ outlooks, reflecting the complexity of ethical challenges in the integration of AI in palliative care.

The study confirms findings from prior qualitative studies that have found palliative care is constrained by systemic resource limitations and justice challenges [36–38]. Justice and distributive equity have been widely recognized as core ethical requirements for AI-assisted palliative care [11, 18, 36]. Yet, access to end-of-life care technologies is universally shaped by structural factors including socioeconomic deprivation, racial and immigrant inequities, and gender disparities [39–41]. Global digital health inequities further exacerbate these challenges across three dimensions: infrastructure, skills, and health outcomes [42]. In this context, this study takes the Chinese context as a case study and advances two novel insights with universal implications. First, it uncovers a mutually reinforcing vicious cycle between technological accessibility and digital literacy. Second, while previous studies have focused on the digital divide in palliative care at the patient level [43, 44], this study stresses the crucial role of nurses’ agency in bridging the digital divide at the provider level. Specifically, disparities in nurses’ willingness to learn and adopt AI technologies may engender a form of subjective inequity within the workforce, which impairs the effective utilization of AI technologies, a mechanism that has not been fully explored in the existing literature.

Prior qualitative research has shown that ethical decision-making is inherently relational and the principle of autonomy often yields to family dynamics and cultural contexts [23, 24]. The finding on priority for family decision making is in line with previous research which shows that in China, Spain, aboriginal and migrant communities, families expect to lead decisions or request non-disclosure to the patient, challenging Western individual autonomy models [45–48]. Research has revealed that nursing ethics and relational autonomy provide a powerful framework to keep palliative care human [10, 14]. However, AI can weaken relational autonomy, which depends on dialogue and ethical reasons, not mere output. It may reintroduce a technological paternalism that can marginalize patients’ subject positions and weaken shared decision-making [22, 49]. Patients, after all, fear loss of “human touch” and decision control with AI involvement, and are more comfortable with AI in back-office tasks [50].

In the clinical domain of palliative care, where humanistic care is the core tenet and nurse-patient trust serves as the foundational cornerstone, the integration of medical AI is not a mere technical addition. Instead, it has triggered a series of profound ethical dilemmas, with opacity, bias, threats to autonomy or dignity, and ambiguous accountability being the most salient [20–22]. Specifically, the opacity of AI algorithms poses a fundamental challenge to trust-building in palliative care. Palliative care encompasses highly individualized scenarios such as symptom assessment, prognostic judgment, and end-of-life decision-making, where the establishment of trust between nurses and patients and their families is often predicated on the clear elucidation of clinical reasoning [51–53].

However, the decision-making processes of most medical AI systems lack interpretability [54, 55]. Nurses are unable to accurately explain to patients the algorithmic logic underpinning AI-generated care recommendations and symptom prediction outcomes, and patients may harbor doubts about clinical protocols due to their inability to comprehend the technical rationale. Such information asymmetry gradually erodes patients’ trust in both nurses and AI-assisted care, undermining the transparent communication essential to palliative care. Meanwhile, the opacity of algorithmic bias further exacerbates this trust crisis. Training datasets for medical AI are frequently plagued by representational biases in study populations [56]. Without diverse palliative care cohorts, AI systems are likely to misjudge the needs of marginalized groups. This may reinforce the existing inequalities and elicit resistance among patients from marginalized groups toward AI-assisted care, thereby prompting them to question the professional judgment and impartial stance of nurses.

This tension ultimately points to a fundamental question: when the “rational” recommendations of algorithms diverge from the “optimal” choices by patients and their families in specific contexts, which form of rationality ought to hold moral authority? This demands a critical examination of the algorithm’s role in end-of-life care. It should not serve as a substitute for decision-making authority, but merely function as an informational tool that requires interpretation and context-specific consideration by human caregivers. This requires a shift in ethical cognition from traditional “relational ethics” to a new form of “technology-mediated relational ethics.” Unlike traditional relational ethics, which centers primarily on direct interpersonal interactions and emotional bonds, technology-mediated relational ethics emphasizes that, in contexts where AI systems are involved, ethical relationships are constructed and mediated through technology. This means that trust, responsibility, and care in nursing practice are enacted not only between humans but also through the interaction with and interpretive use of technology. Thus, nurses may assume an essential ethical role as critical interpreters to bridge the gap between patients and AI systems to sustain relational trust that focuses on patient interests.

Palliative care increasingly unfolds in multicultural settings, where ethical issues of autonomy, truth-telling, family roles, and justice are deeply shaped by culture and religion. The study supports the conclusion that in East Asian cultures, serious diagnoses and prognosis are often informed to the family first, and non-disclosure is rationalized to shield the patient from hopelessness and accelerated death [57, 58]. Globally, palliative care is framed as a human right, yet access and cultural fit remain unequal, especially for migrants, Indigenous peoples, and individuals with low-income [46, 59, 60].

Autonomy at the end of life is best understood as relational and culturally situated, not purely individual. Confucian and other collectivist cultural contexts prioritize family-centered decision-making, filial piety, and death taboos, frequently marginalizing direct communication of care preferences and personalized advance care planning [61, 62]. Cross-cultural analyses criticize mainstream autonomy as Western and ethnocentric, neglecting values like family harmony and community loyalty [61–63]. Relational autonomy is proposed to legitimize family involvement while guarding against coercion, collusion, and overtreatment driven by external pressure [63–65].

In an innovative extension of previous findings, we further contend that in the digital age, the intervention of AI would significantly exacerbate the difficulty of localization in the process of adaptation to cultural sensitivity. When AI-enabled palliative care services are applied to people from different cultural and religious backgrounds, the standardized logic and potential implicit biases inherent in the algorithm may inadvertently reinforce existing cultural biases. For example, “one‑size‑fits‑all” prognostic or communication may push early end‑of‑life planning on some groups while missing others whose cultural norms differ from the training data [18, 19]. Language and communication tools that are not culturally and linguistically tailored can misinterpret emotions, goals, or consent, especially for underrepresented racial groups [18, 66, 67]. Underrepresented racial or ethnic groups are often missing or misrepresented in EHRs and trials, so big data learn dominant patterns and may misjudge specific needs for these groups in palliative and hospice care [67–69]. In sum, if the data do not adequately cover diverse cultural values, disease trajectories or social contexts, its “rational” recommendations may systematically marginalize the values or needs of ethnic minorities. This creates a fundamental tension between algorithmic rationality based on statistical laws and care ethics that emphasize individual uniqueness.

In navigating this tension, nurses may serve as cultural and ethical mediators who critically interpret AI recommendations rather than accepting them as authoritative. Employing their cultural competence and situational awareness, they are responsible for evaluating whether such advice aligns with patients’ values and preferences within unique cultural and familial contexts. When discrepancies arise, nurses may assume an ethical responsibility to reject or adapt standardized AI suggestions, and instead advocate for and implement care plans that conform to patients’ cultural or religious practices. By engaging in inquiry, observation, and the cultivation of trusting relationships with patients and families, nurses can capture unstructured cultural information, integrate such insights into care delivery, and thereby address the limitations of standardized AI models.

Implications

Designing and utilizing AI tools with explicit attention to culture, equity, and human relationships is essential to avoid reinforcing inequities at the end of life. Nurses may assume an ethical responsibility to reduce the risks of cultural bias in AI tools, and to push for the integration of multicultural perspectives into AI design, training and evaluation, thereby advancing the development of more culturally responsive technologies. AI tools should not compress humanistic nurse-patient encounters into impersonal data transactions but instead enhance the relational essence of palliative care. Healthcare organizations are suggested to explicitly frame AI as a supportive tool rather than a substitutive one. Institutions are suggested to establish clear policies that allow nurses to override AI recommendations without facing sanctions or negative consequences. In an attempt to strengthen nurses’ moral sensitivity to deliver more equitable care, curricula are suggested to include modules on AI ethics and moral decision-making dynamics to enhance nurses’ competencies in digital literacy and ethical awareness in AI-mediated care environments.

Limitations

Interviews were conducted with nurses working in hospitals, and did not involve nurses working in other institutions, which limits the transferability of the findings. To address this limitation, future studies should consider diversifying the sample to include nurses from a variety of healthcare settings, such as nursing homes and community care centers. The nurses in the qualitative study were recruited from only Sichuan Province of China, reducing the generalizability of findings. Conducting similar research in wider contexts is essential. Such efforts would allow for a detailed comparison and contrast with the findings of this study, thereby informing future ethical considerations of AI in palliative care.

The demographic characteristics of participants may have influenced the findings to a certain extent. The sample was primarily composed of relatively young nurses with a bachelor’s degree. The education background may have shaped their overall attitudes toward AI, for instance, fostering greater openness and acceptance of digital tools, while also framing their understanding of ethical issues through the theoretical frameworks emphasized in their formal education. Additionally, participants had a moderate average length of clinical experience in palliative care. This means that certain ethical judgments formed through long-term practice by highly experienced palliative care specialists are underrepresented. For example, senior nurses may hold more sophisticated understandings of relational autonomy and clinical authority than those reflected in the present findings.

Accordingly, a rigorous purposive sampling strategy is suggested in future investigations to recruit a more diverse sample of nurses in terms of age, educational background, and most notably, the depth of experience and level of specialization in palliative care. Such a design would enable an empirical analysis of how these key demographic and professional characteristics systematically moderate ethical orientations toward AI integration in palliative care, thereby yielding a more multi-layered understanding of this issue.

Conclusion

Nurses view the ethical integration of AI in palliative care as contingent upon a careful, culturally informed, and ethically grounded approach that continuously centers on human dignity and relational trust. They emphasize that while AI does not alter the core person‑centered orientation of palliative care nor replace the essential role of the nurse, it significantly transforms the ethical landscape. Ethical reflection should evolve from traditional relational ethics to a technology-mediated form, where ethical relationships are constructed and mediated through technological systems. This necessitates integrating AI design and governance with such a framework to ensure that technology supports, rather than undermines, humanistic and patient-centered palliative care. The inclusion of AI ethics and dynamic moral decision‑making modules in nursing professional education is suggested to enhance nurses’ moral sensitivity to deliver equitable care, and strengthen their digital literacy and ethical awareness in AI‑mediated environments. Nurses may assume an ethical responsibility to reduce the risks of cultural bias in AI tools, and to advocate the integration of multicultural perspectives into AI design, training and evaluation, thereby advancing the development of more culturally responsive technologies.
