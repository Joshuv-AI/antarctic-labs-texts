Dual Labral and Ligamentum Teres Reconstruction Achieves High Functional Outcomes and Arthroplasty‐Free Survivorship at 2 Years
Authors: Roger Quesada‐Jimenez, Krishi Rana, Matthew J Strok, Ady H Kahana‐Rojkind, Alexander K Burt, Etan P Sugarman, Benjamin G Domb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the clinical outcomes and survivorship of hip arthroscopy with simultaneous labral and ligamentum teres (LT) reconstruction for the treatment of irreparable labral and LT tears, with a minimum follow‐up of 2 years.MethodsProspectively collected data were retrospectively reviewed for patients who underwent combined arthroscopic labral and LT reconstruction between 2017 and 2021. Inclusion criteria required completion of preoperative and 2‐year postoperative patient‐reported outcomes, including the modified Harris Hip Score, Non‐Arthritic Hip Score, Hip Outcome Score‐Sports‐Specific Subscale, International Hip Outcome Tool, and visual analog scale. Clinically relevant thresholds—minimal clinically important difference and patient acceptable symptom state—were included in the analysis. Survivorship was defined as the absence of revision surgery or conversion to total hip arthroplasty.ResultsSeven patients underwent hip arthroscopy with concomitant LT and labral reconstruction, all with 2‐year follow‐up. Six patients (85.7%) underwent the procedure as revision surgery. Statistically significant improvements were observed across all evaluated patient reported outcomes measures, with a mean patient satisfaction score of 7.4. All patients (100%) met the minimal clinically important difference for modified Harris Hip Score, Non‐Arthritic Hip Score, and visual analog scale. Additionally, 6 patients (85.7%) achieved minimal clinically important difference for International Hip Outcome Tool and Hip Outcome Score‐Sports‐Specific Subscale. Five patients (71.4%) answered positively to the patient acceptable symptom state anchor question. A 100% arthroplasty‐free survivorship at 2 years was observed; subsequently, at 38 months, 1 patient underwent conversion to total hip arthroplasty.ConclusionsHip arthroscopy with simultaneous labral and LT reconstruction for irreparable tears resulted in significant improvements in functional outcomes and high patient satisfaction. A high proportion of patients achieved clinically meaningful thresholds at a minimum 2‐year follow‐up. Notably, a high arthroplasty‐free survivorship rate was observed at short‐term follow‐up.Level of EvidenceLevel IV, therapeutic retrospective case series.

The acetabular labrum plays a critical role in maintaining hip joint stability. 1 , 2 The labrum does so by increasing acetabular volume, thereby deepening the socket and enhancing resistance to both lateral and vertical translation of the femoral head. 3 , 4 , 5 In cases where the labrum is deemed irreparable, labral reconstruction has been shown to be the most effective option to restore these biomechanical properties, 1 with proven long‐term clinical outcomes in the primary and revision hip arthroscopy settings. 6 , 7

There has been growing recognition of the ligamentum teres (LT) and its contribution to hip joint stability. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Recent studies have revealed that LT lesions are associated with poorer patient‐reported outcomes (PROs), distinct patterns of intra‐articular pathology, and an increased risk of conversion to total hip arthroplasty (THA) at short term. 11 , 12 , 13 This increased awareness has prompted the development of various treatment strategies for patients with LT pathology, including reconstruction techniques utilizing different graft types particularly in the context of hip instability. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Recent literature has shown positive outcomes when addressing labral pathology and LT tears at short‐term follow‐up, reaching similar outcomes to a control group without LT tears. 18 , 19 However, despite these advancements, the literature evaluating outcomes following concomitant labral and LT reconstruction remains limited. 20 , 21

Further research is warranted to determine the efficacy of these combined interventions. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the clinical outcomes and survivorship of hip arthroscopy with simultaneous labral and LT reconstruction for the treatment of irreparable labral and LT tears, with a minimum follow‐up of 2 years. We hypothesized general improvement and high patient satisfaction across the cohort as a result of the concurrent procedures.

METHODS

Patient Selection Criteria

All patients in this study consented to joining the American Hip Institute Hip Preservation Registry. This research received Institutional Review Board approval (IRB ID: 20242189).

Prospectively collected data was retrospectively analyzed for all patients who underwent hip arthroscopy for femoroacetabular impigement (FAI) and irreparable labral tears and LT tears who underwent concomitant arthroscopic LT and labral reconstructions between January 2017 and July 2021. Included patients had completed preoperative and 2‐year postoperative questionnaires for the following PROs: the modified Harris Hip Score (mHHS), Non‐Arthritic Hip Score (NAHS), Hip Outcome Score‐Sports Specific Subscale (HOS‐SSS), International Hip Outcome Tool (iHOT‐12), or visual analog scale (VAS) for pain. 22 , 23 Patients who had a documented endpoint surgery (revision arthroscopy or conversion to arthroplasty) within the study's timeframe were accounted for. Exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with prior hip conditions such as avascular necrosis, Perthes, and hip dysplasia (lateral center‐edge angle <18°), open worker compensation claims, inflammatory joint disease, or unwilling to participate.

Survivorship was defined as the absence of revision surgery or conversion to THA.

Questionnaires were collected in the clinic, via encrypted email, or by telephone interview preoperatively and at 4 months, 7 months, 1 year, and annually thereafter postoperatively.

Radiographic Imaging

Patients in this study received preoperative and postoperative radiographic imaging as part of their diagnosis and treatment standard of care. 24 Lateral acetabular coverage was measured using the lateral center‐edge angle in the supine anteroposterior view. Coverage was also assessed using acetabular inclination in the supine anteroposterior view and the anterior center‐edge angle in the false profile view. 25 Tönnis grade, measured on supine anteroposterior view, was used to assess progression of osteoarthritis. Cam deformities were classified with alpha angle measurements of greater than 55° in the Dunn view. 26 These measurements were performed by a hip preservation fellow (R.Q‐J.) at the institution under the supervision of the senior author (B.G.D.).

Surgical Indications

All patients were examined by the senior author (B.G.D.), and hip arthroscopy was recommended if the history, physical examination, and imaging were consistent with FAI and intra‐articular pathology. The Beighton criteria were used to diagnose ligamentous laxity, with a score of 4 (out of 9) or higher implying increased ligamentous laxity. 27 All patients had a magnetic resonance arthrogram of the hip with positive findings suggestive of LT partial or complete tears and labral tear or calcification. As part of conservative management and diagnostic confirmation, some patients underwent ultrasound‐guided diagnostic and therapeutic injections, with all patients reporting a positive, temporary response. Conservative treatment also included rest, activity modification, nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, and physical therapy. Those who failed to improve after a minimum of 3 months were then indicated for surgery.

Patients were specifically considered for LT reconstruction based on the following criteria: patient presenting with symptoms suggestive of hip instability on provocative examination such as Dial test, Beighton's score ≥4, magnetic resonance imaging suggestive of LT tears, and intraoperative confirmation of full‐thickness tearing of the LT.

Surgical Approach

All surgical procedures were performed by the senior author (B.G.D.). Each patient was positioned in a supine position on a postless traction table. An interportal capsulotomy was used to access the joint. While in the joint, diagnostic arthroscopy was used to initially assess the labrum, intra‐articular cartilage, and LT. Labral tears were classified using the Seldes classification system. 28 Femoral‐head chondral pathologies were noted with the acetabular labrum articular disruption and Outerbridge classifications. 29 Tears of the LT were defined using the Domb and Villar classifications. 30 , 31 With the use of a mechanical burr under fluoroscopic guidance, acetabular osseous deformities were corrected with acetabuloplasty and femoral head‐neck osseous deformities were corrected with femoroplasty. All labral tears in this case series were deemed irreparable and therefore reconstructed using the knotless pull‐though technique with tibialis posterior allograft 32 (Figure 1). If full‐thickness chondral defects were present, microfractures were performed. 33

Intraoperative image showing the completed labral reconstruction. Right hip visualization through anterolateral portal.

The LT tears were treated with reconstruction using a tibialis anterior tendon allograft, according to a previously published technique 34 , 35 (Figure 2). In brief, the LT stump was cleared using a radiofrequency ablation and burr. The femoral tunnel was made using an aiming guide via a distal lateral incision. Under fluoroscopy guidance, a guidewire was inserted from the lateral cortex of the femur exiting in the foveal footprint of the femoral head. An 8 to 10 mm tunnel was reamed over the guidewire. Two knotless suture anchors were placed in the acetabular LT footprint. The graft was then doubled over, achieving a double‐strand diameter of 9 to 10 mm, inserted through the midanterior portal, and the looped end was attached to the acetabular footprint with the knotless suture anchors. Fixation of the graft in the femoral tunnel was performed with the limb in partial traction and 60° to 90° of external rotation to prevent overtensioning of the graft with a bioscrew. The capsulotomy was preferentially repaired or plicated, and if unrepairable, reconstructed with dermal allograft. 36

Postoperative Rehabilitation Protocol

Patients were fitted with a hip abduction brace (allowing 0°‐90° of flexion) and were restricted to 20 pounds of weight‐bearing for 6 weeks. Early movement was encouraged to prevent intra‐articular adhesions, and nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs were administered for 4 weeks to prevent heterotopic ossification. Physical therapy began on postoperative day 1 with restrictions on abduction and flexion.

The brace and crutches were discontinued after 6 weeks, and physical therapy continued with a focus on strengthening the abductor and core muscles while gradually increasing the range of motion. Over 12 to 16 weeks, all patients progressed through a 5‐phase physical therapy protocol, with the final phase focused on sport‐specific training.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using RStudio (Version 4.4.0). Descriptive statistics for continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations, whereas categorical variables were reported as numbers and percentages. Chi‐square and Fisher's exact tests were used to assess differences between categorical variables. The Shapiro‐Wilk test was performed to test for normal distribution, with P > .05 indicating normality. Normally distributed data was analyzed by a paired 2‐tailed t‐test. Nonparametric data was analyzed using an equivalent nonparametric test, such as the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was calculated using the distribution method (0.5 * standard deviation) for each PRO. 37 Patient acceptable symptom state (PASS) achievement was determined based on the percentage of patients who answer positively to the PASS anchor question “Taking into account all the activities you have during your daily life, your level of pain, and also your functional impairment, do you consider that the state of your right/left hip is satisfactory?”.

RESULTS

Patient Demographics

There were 7 eligible patients with combined LT reconstruction and labral reconstruction. All 7 (100%) had a 2‐year follow‐up and were included within the case series (Figure 3). Demographic data is presented in Table 1. The average age and body mass index of the patients were 32.5 years and 25.3 kg/m2, respectively. For 6 patients (85.7%), the procedure was a revision surgery after a failed hip arthroscopy with labral repair. There were 5 (71.4%) females and 2 (28.6%) males, and the mean Beighton score in this population was 4.1. Finally, the average follow‐up time was 44.5 months, ranging from 24 to 55 months.

Survivorship

There was a 100% arthroplasty‐free survivorship at 2‐year follow‐up. Subsequently, 1 patient (14.3%) underwent a conversion to THA 38 months following their procedure.

DISCUSSION

In this study, hip arthroscopy with simultaneous labral and LT reconstructions for irreparable tears yielded significant improvements across all evaluated PROs, accompanied by favorable patient satisfaction. A high proportion of patients achieved the MCID for the mHHS, NAHS, HOS‐SSS, iHOT‐12, and VAS. Additionally, 71.4% of patients responded positively to the PASS anchor question. Notably, at a minimum 2‐year follow‐up, 100% arthroplasty‐free survivorship was observed.

Previous literature has suggested that addressing LT pathology in the same surgical setting may contribute to better functional outcomes and improved long‐term survivorship. 8 , 12 , 13 Perumal et al. reported on 1935 patients who underwent hip arthroscopy for FAI and labral tears, including 323 patients with LT tears. 12 Patients with LT pathology showed significantly worse postoperative scores in iHOT‐12 and HOS‐SSS compared with those without LT involvement. Moreover, the LT group had a 7‐fold increased relative risk of conversion to THA within 2 years. It is important to note that in the commented study, the LT was debrided, and no reconstructions were performed.

Although the literature on LT reconstruction remains limited, O’Donnell et al. described outcomes in 9 hips that underwent arthroscopic isolated LT reconstruction. 17 The study revealed favorable improvements in mHHS at an average follow‐up of 48 months. Importantly, no conversions to THA were reported; however, 3 patients required revision arthroscopy, possibly due to untreated concomitant labral pathology. Similarly, Hammarstedt et al. reported on a patient who underwent LT reconstruction with labral repair and capsular plication, showing positive outcomes at 1 year, including improvements in mHHS, NAHS, HOS‐SSS, activities of daily living (ADL) subscales, and VAS. 34

Consistent with these findings, patients in our cohort experienced meaningful symptom relief and significant improvements across multiple PROs, including mHHS, NAHS, HOS‐SSS, iHOT‐12, and VAS (Table 4). White et al. presented a case report of a 15‐year‐old patient who underwent simultaneous labral and LT reconstruction following a traumatic injury that resulted in hip instability. 38 At 14 months postoperatively, the patient reported improvements in mHHS, lower extremity functional scale (LEFS), and VAS for pain, along with high satisfaction. The patient was able to return to sport participation without the need for reintervention. In the present cohort, we observed similar outcomes with positive functional outcomes and 100% arthroplasty survivorship at 2‐year follow‐up. Further work must be done to ascertain the long‐term durability of this treatment protocol. As of the completion of this study, 1 patient was converted to THA at 38 months postoperatively. Although this rate of conversion is consistent with failure rates in the hip arthroscopy population in general, midterm follow‐up studies are needed.

This case series aims to address a gap in the current literature regarding hip arthroscopy with concomitant labral and LT reconstruction for irreparable tears, with a minimum follow‐up of 2 years. The study incorporates previously validated PROs along with the use of cohort‐specific thresholds such as the MCID and the PASS anchor question. These additions enhance the clinical relevance and interpretability of the outcome data beyond simple mean score improvements.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, this study is retrospective in design and includes a relatively small cohort of patients, which introduces potential bias and limits the generalizability of the results. Additionally, the limited sample size prevented a cohort‐specific calculation of PASS; consequently, we presented the number of patients who answered yes to the PASS anchor question. All procedures were performed by a single high‐volume surgeon at a hip preservation referral institution, which may introduce selection bias, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Third, although the observed improvements in PROs are promising, they cannot be attributed solely to the labral and LT reconstructions; rather, they are likely the result of a comprehensive surgical approach. Finally, due to the limited sample size, a sex‐based subanalysis could not be performed.

CONCLUSIONS

Hip arthroscopy with simultaneous labral and LT reconstruction for irreparable tears resulted in significant improvements in functional outcomes and high patient satisfaction. A high proportion of patients achieved clinically meaningful thresholds at a minimum 2‐year follow‐up. Notably, a high arthroplasty‐free survivorship rate was observed at short‐term follow‐up.
