Atomic engineering of intrinsic permanent magnetism in MnBi
Authors: Uranbaigal Enkhtur, Dorj Odkhuu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The low-temperature phase MnBi has been a focus of rare-earth free permanent magnet research because of its atypical behavior of magnetic anisotropy and coercivity enhancements at an elevated temperature. Using systematic density functional theory, density functional perturbation theory, and Monte Carlo simulations, we investigate the structural stability and intrinsic magnetic properties of MnBi with metal and metalloid substitute elements. We theoretically demonstrate that the low magnetocrystalline anisotropy in the MnBi plane can reorient to a large uniaxial magnetocrystalline anisotropy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_u$$\end{document}), which is associated with Mn relocation at an interstitial site and lattice expansion under thermal conditions. Furthermore, we predict that among 11 substitute elements, only the Bi-site Ge can preserve the MnBi phase stability and simultaneously improve intrinsic permanent magnetism. More specifically, we predict a large \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_u$$\end{document} value of 3.6 MJ/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {m}^3$$\end{document} and Curie temperature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_c$$\end{document} up to 780 K for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {MnBi}_\mathrm{{1\text {-}x}}\hbox {Ge}_\mathrm{{x}}$$\end{document} with x<0.2, which are significantly higher than the corresponding values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-$$\end{document}0.3 MJ/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {m}^3$$\end{document} and 750 K for the MnBi phase. The underlying mechanism for magnetization reversal and large \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_u$$\end{document} is illustrated by an energy-level shift in the strong spin-orbit-coupled Bi 6p orbital states. These results demonstrate the feasibility of a possible enhancement of the intrinsic magnetic performance of the MnBi phase through substitutional doping with nonmagnetic metalloid elements.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20764-6.

Introduction

The permanent magnet is an important component in a wide range of technological applications, ranging from information storage memories to electric motors. The state-of-the-art permanent magnet is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {Nd}_2\hbox {Fe}_{14}$$\end{document}B, which exhibits a high uniaxial magnetocrystalline anisotropy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_u$$\end{document} of 4.3 MJ/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {m}^3$$\end{document}, a saturation magnetization \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _0 M_s$$\end{document} of 1.6 T, and a maximum energy density product \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(BH)_{\text {max}}$$\end{document} of 516 kJ/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {m}^3$$\end{document} at room temperature1. Although \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {Nd}_2\hbox {Fe}_{14}$$\end{document}B has favorable permanent magnetic characteristics, its \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(BH)_{\text {max}}$$\end{document} degrades rapidly with increasing temperature, which is associated with the low Curie temperature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_c$$\end{document} of 588 K1. The addition of Dy or Pr is necessary to ensure \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(BH)_{\text {max}}$$\end{document} and coercivity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_c$$\end{document}) at an elevated temperature2. However, these rare-earth (RE) elements of Nd, Dy, and Pr are expensive and scarce.

There are several groups of ferromagnetic (FM) materials that do not contain RE elements but demonstrate better permanent magnetic performance than typical ferrite permanent magnets, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {BaFe}_{12}\hbox {O}_{19}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {SrFe}_{12}\hbox {O}_{19}$$\end{document}3. One of these groups of materials is MnBi, which exhibits \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _0 M_s$$\end{document} of 0.91 T at room temperature and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_c$$\end{document} of 628 K4,5. MnBi exhibits two phases upon temperature change: the low temperature phase (LTP) and the high temperature phase (HTP)5–9. The MnBi LTP is FM and undergoes a phase transition to HTP at 628 K, which is paramagnetic5. The LTP MnBi exhibits a fairly low magnetocrystalline anisotropy (K) value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-$$\end{document}0.25 to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-$$\end{document}0.155 MJ/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {m}^3$$\end{document} on the ab plane (that is, biaxial anisotropy) at 4.2 K and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_u$$\end{document} of 1.1–1.6 MJ/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {m}^3$$\end{document} at room temperature5–7. Such a low K does not guarantee intrinsic permanent magnetic properties such as the anisotropy field \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_a$$\end{document} and the hardness parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa$$\end{document}. Although MnBi has weaker intrinsic magnetic properties compared to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {Nd}_2\hbox {Fe}_{14}$$\end{document}B, it possesses a positive temperature coefficient in K. Specifically, the coercivity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_c$$\end{document} of MnBi increases with temperature from 100 to 540 K, reaching a maximum value of 2.5 T at 540 K10–13. The possible origin of the positive temperature coefficient is proposed as the thermal expansion of the lattice14,15. Consequently, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(BH)_{\text {max}}$$\end{document} for MnBi surpasses that for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {Nd}_2\hbox {Fe}_{14}$$\end{document}B above 450 K18.

Over the years great efforts have been made to improve K and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_c$$\end{document} of the MnBi phase through atomic doping engineering with transition metal (TM) elements19–25. Experimentally, replacing Mn with Ti, Cr and Fe has been shown to improve \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_c$$\end{document} at room temperature19–23. Similarly, according to density functional theory (DFT) calculations, Fe and Co were predicted to improve \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_s$$\end{document} and K but reduce the value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_c$$\end{document} from 650 K to 275–325 K24,25. Furthermore, simple metals Al, Ga and the metalloid Ge for the Bi site were reported to enhance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_c$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_c$$\end{document} while reducing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_s$$\end{document}26–28,31,32. In addition to these intriguing phenomena, the renewal of research objectives appears to explore the systematic search for the possibility of simultaneous improvements in K, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_s$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_c$$\end{document} of MnBi to consider it as a potential permanent magnet.

In this paper, we perform DFT, density functional perturbation theory (DFPT), and Monte Carlo simulations to explore possible improvements of the structural stability and intrinsic magnetic properties of the LTP MnBi structure by substituting Mn, Bi, and interstitial sites with a series of TM and metalloid elements. First, we elucidate the positive temperature coefficient in K in correlation with Mn relocation at the interstitial site and volume increase. Furthermore, it is predicted that among the 11 substitute elements, the metalloid Ge at the Bi site retains phase stability and simultaneously improves K up to 3.6 MJ/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {m}^3$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_c$$\end{document} to 780 K in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {MnBi}_\mathrm{{1\text {-}x}}\hbox {Ge}_\mathrm{{x}}$$\end{document} (x<0.2), which are -0.2 MJ/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {m}^3$$\end{document} and 750 K, respectively, in MnBi. Computational methods

Structural optimization and thermodynamic stability calculations were performed using the DFT and DFPT approaches within the projector augmented wave (PAW) method33 implemented in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)34 version 5.4.4. Figure 1a displays the primitive unit cell structure of the LTP MnBi phase, which crystallizes in a hexagonal lattice with a space group \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P6_{3}$$\end{document}/mmc. In our computation, we have chosen a 2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}2 supercell structure consisting of 16 Mn atoms at the 2a site and 16 Bi atoms at the 2c site (Wyckoff positions). Both Mn and Bi sites were considered substitutional sites for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {Mn}_\mathrm{{1\text {-}x}}\hbox {BiM}_\mathrm{{x}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {MnBi}_\mathrm{{1\text {-}x}}\hbox {M}_\mathrm{{x}}$$\end{document} (M\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$=$$\end{document}Ti–Ge), with x=0, 0.0625, 0.125, 0.1875, and 0.25. From total energy minimization, M atoms were identified to prefer a uniform distribution over that of the unit cell. An energy cut-off of 500 eV, a 13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}11 k-point mesh, and a force convergence criterion of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{\text {-}4}$$\end{document} eV/Å were imposed for ionic and lattice optimization. This method accurately deals with both the core and the valence electrons and is suitable for magnetic systems. The generalized gradient approximation − PBEsol approach was chosen for the exchange correlation functional36. Results and discussion

In our calculations of MnBi, the low-temperature experimental results are severely underestimated5–8,10. The DFT plus U (DFT+U) approach is adopted to minimize such a discrepancy. Figure 1a–c display the lattice parameters (a and c), the spin magnetic moment of the Mn atom, and the magnetocrystalline anisotropy K for different U parameters. Here, we evaluate the results of lattice parameters optimized via the VASP and compare the magnetic properties obtained from the VASP and WIEN2k techniques. In both approaches, introducing the Hubbard U parameter minimizes discrepancies and yields values that align with experimental data when U=2 eV is used, which is hence applied in this study. In the absence of an external magnetic field, the Heisenberg spin Hamiltonian is expressed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H = -(1/2) \sum _{i\ne j}J_{ij} {\textbf {S}}_i\cdot {\textbf {S}}_j-K_u\sum _{i}({\textbf {S}}_i\cdot {\textbf {e}})^2$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$J_{ij}$$\end{document} is the exchange coupling interaction between two spins \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textbf {S}}_i$$\end{document} (at the i site) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textbf {S}}_j$$\end{document} (at the j site), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\textbf {e}}$$\end{document} is the unit vector along the magnetic easy axis. The calculated exchange interaction parameters of different atomic pairs in MnBi and Mn(Bi,M) (M=Ga, Ge) are shown in Fig. 5a. The positive (negative) exchange parameter reflects the preference for spin-parallel (antiparallel) coupling between the two magnetic moments. In MnBi, the Mn–Mn interactions dominate, while the Mn–Bi interactions are relatively weak. Both Ga and Ge improve the exchange parameters due to the reduced interatomic distances associated with the lesser overlap of 3d wave functions31.

Conclusion

In conclusion, using systematic DFT, DFPT, and Monte Carlo simulations, we have investigated transition metal and metalloid substitutes for their potential impact in improving structural stability, intrinsic permanent magnetic properties, and Curie temperature of the LTP MnBi structure. Our calculations show that among the substitute elements, the Ge atoms reserve the MnBi phase stability and prefer the Bi site, unlike the transition metal atoms occupying the Mn site or the interstitial site. We further find that Ge can improve the value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_u$$\end{document} to approximately 3.6 MJ/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {m}^3$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_c$$\end{document} to 780 K in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {MnBi}_\mathrm{{1\text {-}x}}\hbox {Ge}_\mathrm{{x}}$$\end{document} with x=0.18–0.25, which are significantly higher than the corresponding values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-$$\end{document}0.3 MJ/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {m}^3$$\end{document} and 750 K in pure MnBi. Magnetization reversal and enhanced \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_u$$\end{document} are discussed in connection with an energy level change in the strong spin-orbit-coupled Bi 6p orbital states. These results suggest that Mn-Bi-based compounds with optimal Ge content are potential candidates for rare-earth-free permanent magnets, because of their anisotropy field, hardness parameter, and maximum energy density product comparable to those of the widely investigated permanent magnets.
